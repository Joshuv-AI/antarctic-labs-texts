Targeting MCL‐1 to Overcome Therapeutic Resistance and Improve Cancer Mortality
Authors: Md Mohiuddin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMyeloid cell leukemia‐1 protein (MCL‐1) is a major anti‐apoptotic member of the Bcl‐2 family and is typically overexpressed in a broad range of malignancies, including non‐small‐cell lung cancer, multiple myeloma, acute myeloid leukemia, and various solid tumors. By sequestering pro‐apoptotic effectors, MCL‐1 maintains mitochondrial stability and supports the tumor cell survival during stress, driving disease progression, therapeutic resistance, and ultimately, poor patient outcomes. In the last 10 years, the development of selective small‐molecule inhibitors of MCL‐1 has opened doors to enable the targeting of this mechanism of apoptosis avoidance.DiscussionThis perspective summarizes the current knowledge regarding the dual roles of MCL‐1 in apoptosis and mitochondrial homeostasis regulation. The structural foundations for the design of MCL‐1 inhibitors are revisited, the pharmacological profiles of the leading drugs (S63845, AZD5991, AMG 176) in advanced clinical development are summarized, and emerging strategies, such as combination therapies with inhibitors of anti‐apoptotic proteins such as BCL‐2, PROTAC strategies designed to degrade MCL‐1, and reversible‐binding chemotypes to maximize MCL‐1 inhibition and minimize toxicity, are reviewed. The non‐apoptotic roles of MCL‐1 in immune modulation and metabolism adaptation are also reviewed, along with the development of analytical methods to refine the patient selection process (e.g., BH3 profiling and transcriptomic signatures). The overexpression of MCL‐1 is associated with worse patient survival in ACC, CESC, ESCA, HNSC, LGG, and UVM cancers.ConclusionMCL‐1 is an exciting anti‐cancer target, and its inhibition may sensitize treatment‐resistant tumors to cell death and enhance patient survival. The key to clinical success will be to carefully develop more intensive dosing regimens, rationally combine agents, and develop trial designs that prioritize the evaluation of new agents that maximize antitumor activity without the risk of off‐target toxicities. Continued translational research and adaptive clinical trials are critical to fully realize the therapeutic potential of MCL‐1 inhibition across various cancer contexts.

Introduction

Programmed cell death or apoptosis is a key cellular process that establishes and maintains tissue homeostasis and eliminates damaged or premalignant cells. Within the Bcl‐2 family, myeloid cell leukemia‐1 protein (MCL‐1) has received significant attention as an important apoptotic regulator, given its remarkably high expression, dynamic regulation, and potent cytoprotective activity [1, 2]. While less stable than BCL‐2 and BCL‐XL, the other two family members that are expressed more constitutively, MCL‐1 has a more transient half‐life, with its expression modulated by transcriptional and post‐translational events, facilitating rapid turnover of protein levels to allow cellular responses to factors associated with the cellular environment and genotoxic effects. In cancer, aberrant overexpression of MCL‐1 ultimately subverts apoptotic signaling, thereby allowing malignant cancer cells to escape cell death induced by cytotoxic chemotherapies, previous therapies, radiation exposure, or targeted agents [3, 4, 5].

Elevated levels of MCL‐1 expression have been associated with a range of differential clinical features across tumor types, and in non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC), high MCL‐1 levels positively correlate with an increased metastatic capacity and a decreased progression‐free survival [6, 7]. Multiple myeloma (MM) with MCL‐1 overexpression is associated with a poor prognosis and drug resistance [8], and MCL‐1 dependency leads to poor response rates to BCL‐2 antagonists in patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [9]. It is also important to note the non‐canonical functions of MCL‐1, including the regulation of mitochondrial bioenergetics, calcium handling, and production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), as these phenomena contribute towards cancer metabolism, stemness, and immune escape [10, 11, 12]. Overall, the widespread and numerous roles of MCL‐1 place MCL‐1 at the heart of cellular survival pathways in cancer and thus provide a clear rationale for targeting MCL‐1.

From this perspective, we will provide a comprehensive overview of the mechanistic rationale for targeting MCL‐1, present an overview of pharmacological inhibitors that target MCL‐1, discuss the potential for enhanced apoptosis through combination therapies, and finally touch on safety considerations for translating MCL‐1‐directed strategies into practice.

Despite recent progress, several key questions remain unanswered. The most significant controversy is whether the cardiotoxicity observed with early MCL‐1 inhibitors is due to a true on‐target inhibition of mitochondrial survival pathways in cardiomyocytes or simply an off‐target pharmacological effect [13, 14]. A second complication is the dual role of MCL‐1, whether in apoptotic or non‐apoptotic functions, which makes therapeutic targeting difficult; furthermore, there is a lack of confirmation about possible long‐term systemic inhibition [1, 5, 12]. The most significant translational avenue may be the development of reversible‐binding chemotypes to maximize the inhibition of MCL‐1 while minimizing toxicity, proteolysis‐targeting chimera (PROTAC)‐based degradation strategies, and patient stratification based on biomarkers to identify tumors reflecting true MCL‐1 dependence. All of these open questions reflect the challenges and opportunities that will shape the ongoing development of MCL‐1‐directed therapy.

Biological Roles of MCL‐1 in Cancer

The Canonical Anti‐Apoptotic Function MCL‐1 prevents apoptosis by binding BH3 domains of pro‐apoptotic proteins, blocking mitochondrial membrane permeabilization and cytochrome c release [12, 15, 16, 17]. By directly binding pro‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 family members in complex with cell survival factors, the sequestering of pro‐apoptotic partners preserves mitochondrial integrity, which inhibits caspase activity and induces apoptotic cell death. Furthermore, while MCL‐1 shares several key features with other anti‐apoptotic paralogs, it has also been shown to have unique interactions owing to its binding affinities and kinetics with pro‐apoptotic family members, which can result in selective dependencies on MCL‐1 in some cancer lines. Regulation and Post‐Translational Modifications The accumulation of MCL‐1 is regulated by its phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and degradation by the proteasome [18, 19, 20]. Reversible post‐translational modifications, specifically phosphorylation by kinases such as ERK and GSK‐3β at specific amino acid residues, modulate MCL‐1 interaction with E3 ligases, such as MULE and β‐TRCP, reducing its half‐life [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. For example, it has been shown that phosphorylation of MCL‐1 at Thr163 by ERK promotes stabilization of the protein in response to growth factor signaling, while phosphorylation by GSK‐3β at the same location targets MCL‐1 for turnover by ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis [23]. Kinase dysregulation and hyperstabilization of MCL‐1 in cancer cells can enhance its anti‐apoptotic function. Non‐Apoptotic Cellular Programs for Tumor Growth Recent reports have revealed that MCL‐1 participates in non‐apoptotic cellular processes that support tumor growth. Within the mitochondrial matrix, MCL‐1 interacts with voltage‐dependent anion channels (VDAC) to help modulate calcium homeostasis and enhance oxidative phosphorylation [12, 24, 25]. MCL‐1 promotes energy reprogramming for proliferation under nutrient‐deprived conditions. MCL‐1 regulates mitochondrial functions, including cristae structure, metabolism, and ROS signaling, which can have a profound effect on cell survival and metastatic potential [12, 24]. Beyond the mitochondria, MCL‐1 can modulate the initiation of autophagy, DNA damage responses, and hypoxic stress adaptation in a context‐dependent manner, thereby extending its oncogenic role beyond the regulation of apoptosis [1, 12, 26, 27, 28]. Immune Modulation Recent studies implicate MCL‐1 in the maintenance of features of immune cell survival and function [29, 30]. Overexpression of MCL‐1 in tumor‐infiltrating immune cells can paradoxically promote immunosuppressive microenvironments in tumors, even though it promotes lymphocyte survival. Selective targeting of MCL‐1 can reprogram tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) to increase effector T‐cell persistence and induce tumor cell death, functioning as a dual therapeutic approach to eliminate tumor cells and enhance antitumor immunity [31, 32]. This situation presents as a paradox; MCL‐1 promotes lymphocyte persistence and immune cell survival; however, its overexpression in TAMs and myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) promotes an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [12, 33, 34]. In this regard, inhibiting MCL‐1 not only induces apoptosis in tumor cells but can promote the re‐programming of suppressive immune cell populations to promote T‐cell‐mediated anti‐tumor immune activity.

Structural Insights and Clinical Advances in MCL‐1 Inhibition

Design Principles for BH3 Mimetics Effective MCL‐1 inhibitors must follow the design principles established for BH3‐mimetic molecules that mimic the BH3 helix of pro‐apoptotic proteins, which bind with high affinity to the hydrophobic binding groove within MCL‐1 [11]. Structure‐based drug design focuses on the identification and optimization of important side chains to produce compounds with high potency, selectivity, and favorable pharmacokinetics through optimal lipophilicity and solubility. Clinical Stage Inhibitors The small molecule S63845 binds with high affinity to the BH3‐binding groove of MCL‐1 [35]. S63845 kills MCL‐1‐dependent cancer cells, including MM, leukemia, and lymphoma, by activating the BAX/BAK‐dependent mitochondrial apoptosis pathway [35].The macrocyclic small molecule AZD5991 inhibits MCL‐1, an anti‐apoptotic protein, and is highly potent and selective [30, 36, 37]. In cancer cells, such as myeloma and AML, it induces cell death, including apoptosis, at low micromolar concentrations [30, 36, 37]. AMG176 utilizes a new chemotype, binding with long residence times in cellular and animal models, increasing the utilization of MCL‐1 inhibitors in vivo, and demonstrating significant regressions in tumor size in xenografts [38, 39, 40].According to the TCGA database, high MCL‐1 expression levels were associated with a worse overall survival (OS) relative to low expression levels of MCL‐1 in ACC, CESC, ESCA, HNSC, LGG, and UVM cancers (Figure 1), thus suggesting potential targets for novel therapies.

MCL‐1 Function and Therapeutic Targeting Strategies

Role of MCL‐1 in Regulating Apoptosis and Mitochondrial Function The action of the BCL‐2 anti‐apoptotic family, such as BCL2, BCL‐XL, and MCL‐1, is primarily by directly binding and neutralizing pro‐apoptotic proteins like BAX and BAK. Then, these proteins, as well as the pro‐apoptotic BH3‐only proteins (such as BIM), cannot activate and oligomerize to cause mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization [46, 47]. If permeabilization does not occur, the mitochondrial outer membrane will not release cytochrome c or other pro‐apoptotic factors and thus inhibit the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [47, 48] (Figure 2). Binding Grooves and Mechanisms of BH3 Mimetics/PROTACs MCL‐1 is a pro‐survival protein in the BCL‐2 family and is commonly overexpressed in cancers, which allows for evasion of programmed cell death. Both BH3 mimetics and PROTACs are different pharmacological strategies to target MCL‐1 in an effort to restore apoptosis. BH3 mimetics are small molecules that structurally mimic the BH3 (BCL‐2 homology 3) domain of pro‐apoptotic proteins, such as BIM and PUMA [49, 50]. The binding of BH3 mimetics competes with the binding of pro‐apoptotic proteins, such as BIM and PUMA, to the BH3‐binding groove on MCL‐1. Without a BH3 mimetic, MCL‐1 would otherwise sequester and neutralize these proteins [49, 50]. Therefore, the pro‐apoptotic proteins released from MCL‐1 are now able to facilitate the mitochondria‐mediated pathways for apoptosis. On the other hand, PROTACs are bifunctional molecules designed to hijack a cell′s own protein degradation machinery. PROTACs consist of two ends: an end that binds the target protein (like MCL‐1) and an end that binds an E3 ubiquitin ligase [45, 51]. The PROTAC, now capable of bringing the two proteins close together, then induces the E3 ligase to ubiquitinate MCL‐1 [45, 51, 52]. The poly‐ubiquitinated MCL‐1 is then released from the PROTAC and recognized and degraded by the proteasome (i.e., a protein‐recycling complex in the cell) (Figure 2). Resistance Mechanisms and Rational Combination Strategies Targeting MCL‐1 Resistance mechanisms observed in KRAS‐mutant cancers to targeted therapies frequently involve the action of the anti‐apoptotic proteins MCL‐1 or BCL‐XL, while combination treatment strategies aim to overcome this resistance by disrupting selective key pathways. Utilizing MCL‐1 loss‐of‐function in combination with loss‐of‐function for BCL‐2 or BCL‐XL is an appealing strategy because MCL‐1 is able to sequester pro‐apoptotic proteins (e.g., BIM), thereby preventing them from triggering programmed cell death. Therefore, the single BH3 mimetics that specifically target BCL‐2 or BCL‐XL would not be effective if MCL‐1 is still active and holding BIM or the like. In this case, the BH3 mimetic and an MCL‐1 inhibitor or PROTAC will free BIM to facilitate potent, synergistic apoptosis [53, 54, 55]. Through dual inhibition, trapped pro‐apoptotic proteins are released, and cells are prevented from compensating by switching to another anti‐apoptotic protein to overcome resistance [53, 54, 55]. Targeting both MCL‐1 and MEK inhibitors would be another potential strategy in KRAS‐mutant cancers, as the MEK inhibitors can increase BIM expression levels. However, the overall efficacy is limited by the fact that MCL‐1 is still present and continues to limit BIM activity; therefore, the co‐inhibition of both would alleviate the resistance and restore the apoptotic activity of BIM [56, 57].

MCL‐1 Inhibition: Monotherapy and Synergistic Combination Strategies

Monotherapy Evidence An intriguing study demonstrated in vitro and in vivo that huMcl‐1 and Eµ‐Myc lymphomas were more sensitive to S63845 than Eµ‐Myc lymphomas expressing mouse MCL‐1 [58]. According to another study, AZD5991 binds directly to MCL‐1 and induces rapid apoptosis in cancer cells, including myeloma and AML [59]. In models of hematologic malignancies, AMG176 induces rapid commitment to apoptosis as a potent, selective, and orally bioavailable MCL‐1 inhibitor [38]. Bcl‐2 Family Inhibition Preclinical investigations have shown additivity between MCL‐1 inhibitors and the BCL‐2 antagonist venetoclax, resulting in the rapid priming of mitochondria and apoptosis in AML and MM animal models, both preclinical and clinical, to overcome venetoclax resistance [54, 60, 61]. In vitro, when ABT‐263 (Navitoclax) was combined with S63845/S64315 (MIK655), significantly greater cell killing of melanoma cells (p < 0.05) was observed among multiple assays (including sphere assays) in comparison to single‐agent treatment [62]. The combination‐mediated cell killing was independent of BIM, as well as NOXA [62]. Another study stated that VU661013, a novel, potent, selective MCL‐1 inhibitor that destabilizes the BIM/MCL‐1 interaction, induces apoptosis in AML and is active in venetoclax‐resistant cells and patient‐derived xenografts [9]. Kinase Inhibitor Pairs The MCL‐1 inhibitor AZD5991 enhanced the anti‐leukemic activity of gilteritinib and MRX‐2843 against FLT3‐mutated AML with FMS‐like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3)‐internal tandem duplication (FLT3‐ITD) [63]. An interesting study demonstrated that MEK + MCL‐1 inhibition induces apoptosis and tumor regression in KRAS‐mutant NSCLC models that do not respond well to MEK inhibition alone [56]. Chemotherapy and Targeted Agents Cytarabine and venetoclax showed synergistic effects by enhancing BH3 activity and/or suppressing MCL‐1 to promote apoptosis in preclinical studies [64]. Consistent with these observations, AML patients receiving venetoclax in combination with chemotherapy experienced deeper remission [64]. PARP Inhibition In homologous recombination‐deficient tumors, PARP inhibitors induce DNA lesions that activate intrinsic apoptotic responses, whereas MCL‐1 inhibition amplifies stress to the tumor to induce synthetic lethality [65, 66, 67]. Immunotherapy Synergy Compared with either agent alone, the MCL‐1 inhibitor S64315 combined with anti‐PD‐1 significantly slowed tumor growth [68]. The results of this study indicate that MCL‐1 inhibition enhances immune checkpoint blockade efficacy by targeting MDSCs, thereby promoting anti‐tumor immunity [68].

Safety and Toxicity of MCL‐1 Inhibitors

Cardiotoxicity MCL‐1 maintains cardiomyocyte survival and hematopoietic stress levels, and its deletion in the murine heart results in impaired heart function independent of apoptosis, whereas the clinical dilemma of elevated cardiomyocyte‐specific troponin with AZD5991 demonstrates subclinical myocardial injury [14, 37]. Therefore, strategies to build on the risk of MCL‐1 antagonists in the heart can ensure appropriate dosing strategies, such as employing intermittent dosing and cardiac biomarker management. In the phase 1 study, asymptomatic elevation of troponin I or T was observed in 10.3% of patients treated with AZD5991 [37]. Hematopoietic Toxicity MCL‐1 deletion induces hematopoietic failure through the loss of hematopoietic stem cells, granulocyte‐monocyte progenitor cells, and common myeloid progenitor cells [12]. One MCL‐1 allele was deleted in adult mice, resulting in the depletion of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), which leads to their death within two to 3 weeks [69]. The neutrophil survival is dependent on MCL‐1, with knockout mice exhibiting 80%–90% reductions in numbers of mature neutrophils in the blood, spleen, and peritoneal exudates [30]. In the study of AMG 176, 62% of patients had grade ≥ 3 adverse events, most commonly neutropenia and anemia [37, 70]. Immune Effects Chronic inhibition of MCL‐1 at a systemic level may also deplete the variability of lymphocyte populations, which may impede host immunity [71]. Preclinical immunophenotyping and the addition of solid tumors to clinical trials are appropriate in settings where the effects of MCL‐1 deletion manifest. Off‐Target Manifestations Similarly, to cardiotoxicity, MCL‐1 is a key anti‐apoptotic factor for the liver, contributing to hepatocellular homeostasis, according to previous research [72]. Furthermore, toxicity across preclinical models needs to be further evaluated for level clarity. Additional challenges to consider involve off‐target mitochondrial perturbations and drug delivery. The considerable hydrophobicity of current MCL‐1 inhibitors may limit tissue access, while systemic delivery may induce unwanted mitochondrial stress in non‐malignant tissues. Nanoparticle‐based delivery systems and conjugates specifically designed for tumor targeting are being explored to provide targeted delivery and minimize systemic toxicity. The most common treatment‐related adverse events included nausea, fatigue, diarrhea, and palpitations. This study concluded that tapotoclax was both safe and tolerated in a high‐risk cohort of patients with MDS after HMA failure; instances of cardiac adverse events were self‐resolving without needing to reduce the dose; and there were no patients with clinically significant troponin elevation [73].

Conclusion

MCL‐1 is integral to the healthy variation of apoptosis and cellular physiology of mitochondria, making it a clear oncology target. The development of selective inhibitors, PROTACs, and reversible chemotypes has been shown to validate the feasibility of inhibiting MCL‐1 to induce tumor cell death. The combination of targeted, cytotoxic, and immune‐mediated therapies offers the potential to increase persistence and improve patient‐centric targeting therapies, and despite concerns about the efficacy of such therapies in critical tissues, this approach is currently receiving robust consideration. By developing new biomarkers to maximize precision, patient selection with MCL‐1 affinities should be maximized, with the ultimate goal of thereby reducing cancer deaths. Preclinical and early clinical studies have demonstrated the potential of selective MCL‐1 inhibitors to increase apoptotic opportunity, enhance chemosensitivity, and provide synergy with BCL‐2 inhibitors and other standard‐of‐care therapies—mechanisms that could all improve OS.
