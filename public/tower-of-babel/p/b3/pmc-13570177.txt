Targeting nuclear export and Janus Kinase/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK/STAT) signalling in myelofibrosis: A novel combinatorial strategy that impacts intrinsic and microenvironment‐related pathways
Authors: Trinayan Kashyap, Andrea A. Ellero, Christopher J. Walker, Marie E. Maloof, Min Lu, Lijuan Xia, Ronald Hoffman, Pietro Taverna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMyelofibrosis (MF) is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by splenomegaly, constitutional symptoms, marrow fibrosis, cytopenias and inflammation. Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) inhibitors, such as ruxolitinib, reduce splenomegaly and alleviate symptoms but have limited disease‐modifying activity, and resistance frequently develops. Selinexor, an oral selective exportin (XPO1) inhibitor, restores nuclear retention of tumour suppressors and inhibitors of inflammatory signalling. Dual XPO1/JAK2 inhibition targets complementary downstream pathways, enhancing suppression of MF progenitor cell fitness. We evaluated selinexor plus ruxolitinib in MF models, including JAK2V617F or calreticulin (CALR) exon 9‐mutant MPN cell lines and samples from patients with MF. The combination showed greater anti‐proliferative activity than JAK2 inhibition alone and suppressed colony formation from MF Cluster of Differentiation 34 (CD34)+ cells. Selinexor remained active in a ruxolitinib‐resistant MPN cell line, inducing G1 arrest and apoptosis. Multi‐omic analyses demonstrated increased nuclear retention of p53 and disruption of Nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB) and Janus Kinase/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK/STAT) signalling, complementing and extending ruxolitinib‐targeted pathways. The combination suppressed NF‐κB transcriptional activity and reduced Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha (TNFα), Interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and Monocyte Chemoattractant Protein‐1 (MCP‐1) secretion from MF patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells. These findings highlight that selinexor plus ruxolitinib affects both MF haematopoietic cell–intrinsic and microenvironment‐related pathways, providing a novel disease‐modifying strategy with the potential to improve clinical outcomes in patients with MF.

INTRODUCTION

Myelofibrosis (MF) is a Philadelphia chromosome–negative myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by marrow fibrosis, cytopenias, systemic symptoms, megakaryocytic atypia and splenomegaly. It is primarily driven by mutations in JAK2, CALR or Myeloproliferative Leukemia virus oncogene (MPL), leading to constitutive JAK/STAT activation and clonal expansion. A proinflammatory tumour microenvironment promotes fibrosis, disrupts haematopoiesis and drives extra‐medullary haematopoiesis. Clinical outcomes are further influenced by variant allele frequencies, additional mutations and cytogenetic abnormalities. 1

JAK2 inhibitors are the standard of care for MF, improving splenomegaly and symptoms; however, resistance or intolerance often develops within ~3 years. 2 , 3 Fewer than half of patients achieve SVR35 or TSS50 with ruxolitinib, and durability of response declines early. 4 , 5 , 6 Its impact on disease modification, including fibrosis and mutation burden, is limited. 7 Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only curative option but is not widely feasible and carries significant risk. 2 Thus, there remains a need for therapies that provide sustained and durable benefit in MF. 8 , 9

Selinexor, an oral, selective XPO1 inhibitor, prevents nucleocytoplasmic transport of cargo proteins, resulting in nuclear accumulation of tumour suppressors and RNA‐binding proteins and reduced oncoprotein translation. 10 Overexpression of XPO1 has been observed in many malignancies, both haematological and solid, and has been correlated with poor prognosis. 11 , 12 , 13 The effects of XPO1 inhibition are pleiotropic, since hundreds of proteins are dependent on XPO1 for nuclear export and include induction of cancer cell‐specific apoptosis as well as anti‐inflammatory effects. 11 , 14 , 15

We sought to elucidate the context‐specific molecular pathways affected by selinexor and ruxolitinib combination on MPN cell lines and primary cells from patients with MF.

METHODS

Compounds

Selinexor was obtained from Karyopharm Therapeutics (Newton, MA, USA). Ruxolitinib (#S1378), momelotinib (#S2219) and pacritinib (#S8057) were purchased from Selleckchem.

Cell lines utilized

Human JAK2V617F HEL 92.1.7 (HEL) (#ACC 11) cells were obtained from DSMZ, JAK2V617F UKE‐1 cells from the Coriell Institute and CALR‐mutant Marimo cells from Dr. C. Schaniel. Cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 with 10% FBS, penicillin/streptomycin and 1% Glutamax. ELF‐153 cells (#ACC 693; driver mutation–negative) were obtained from DSMZ and cultured in RPMI/MEM with 20% FBS, Granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF) (10 ng/mL), penicillin/streptomycin and Glutamax. All cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. Ruxolitinib‐resistant HEL cells were generated through stepwise exposure to increasing drug concentrations over 6 weeks, starting at IC10 and were defined by a >15‐fold increase in IC50 compared with parental cells. 16

Normal donors and patient specimens

De‐identified MF patient samples were obtained from the MPN Research Consortium Tissue Bank and the Hematological Malignancy Tissue Bank at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (New York, NY, USA) (Table S2) and from patients with JAKi‐naïve MF participating in the Phase 3 SENTRY trial (NCT04562389). Each patient provided informed written consent, approved by individual Institutional Review Boards in accordance with principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. De‐identified normal donor mobilized peripheral blood CD34+ cells were purchased from All Cells (Emeryville, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were estimated using normalized three‐parameter nonlinear regression unless otherwise noted. Statistical significance for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), transcriptional activation and cytokine assays was assessed using Šídák's multiple comparisons test, and colony forming unit (CFU) assays using Tukey's test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RNA‐seq data were analysed in R using DESeq2 17 and Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) proteomics using Perseus. 18 Gene set enrichment analyses were performed with fgsea, 19 EnrichR 20 or clusterProfiler. 21 Omics data significance was defined by false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05. Protein data are presented as mean ± SEM unless stated otherwise. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v10) or R (v4.3.1).

See Supporting Information for methods on cell viability assay, haematopoietic progenitor cell assays, enumeration of JAK2V617F colonies, TaqMan probes and antibodies, western blotting, cell‐cycle analysis, real‐time reverse transcription qPCR, NF‐κB transcriptional activity assay, THP‐1 cell polarization assay, analysis of cytokines elaborated by MF patient peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) and Ribonucleic Acid (RNA) sequencing and quantitative subcellular proteomics.

RESULTS

Selinexor is effective as a single agent and in combination with JAK2 inhibitors against MPN cell lines

We compared the anti‐proliferative activity of selinexor with three FDA‐approved JAK2 inhibitors (ruxolitinib, momelotinib, pacritinib) across MPN cell lines harbouring JAK2V617F, CALR mutations or no driver mutation (Table 1). 22 , 23 Selinexor showed the greatest potency (IC50: 0.01–1.8 μM) irrespective of mutational status, whereas ruxolitinib was the least potent (IC50: 1–20 μM). Notably, selinexor was active in CALR‐mutant, ruxolitinib‐refractory Marimo cells (IC50 = 0.47 μM), which were modestly sensitive to momelotinib (IC50 ~7 μM) and responsive to pacritinib (IC50 = 0.83 μM). Combinations of selinexor with JAK inhibitors demonstrated greater cytotoxicity than single agents, with the highest Bliss synergy scores observed for selinexor plus ruxolitinib. Selinexor combined with momelotinib showed synergy in HEL cells, while combinations with pacritinib were generally additive (Table S1). Overall, selinexor‐based combinations produced enhanced anti‐proliferative effects at clinically relevant concentrations compared with monotherapies.

Selinexor reduces colony formation by MF patient–derived CD34+ cells, while having minimal effect on clonogenicity of normal donor CD34+ cells

Anti‐clonogenic effects of selinexor and ruxolitinib were evaluated using primary CD34+ cells from five patients with MF (Table S2) and five normal donors (ND). Single‐agent selinexor at concentrations clinically achievable doses (50–200 nM) reduced the total number of haematopoietic colonies cloned from MF patient CD34+ cells while exerting minimal inhibition on CD34+ colonies from normal donors (Figure 1). By contrast, 50 nM ruxolitinib was not selective in the inhibition of colony formation between normal CD34+ cells and those derived from MF patients. Colony formation inhibition was further augmented by the combination of 50 nM ruxolitinib and the different selinexor concentrations (Figure 1). JAK2 genotyping of the CD34+ colonies showed that selinexor, alone and in combination with ruxolitinib, was equally effective in reducing colonies for both JAK2V617F and JAK2 WT subsets (Table S3). Selinexor, alone and in combination with ruxolitinib, was also effective in reducing the colonies from CALR‐mutated CD34+ cells from patients with MF (data not shown).

Haematopoietic colonies cloned from CD34+ cells from patients with MF and normal donors were evaluated in the presence of selinexor, ruxolitinib or both in combination. Selinexor did not significantly decrease the colonies of CD34+ cells from normal donors. *p < 0.05. CFU, colony forming unit; DMSO, dimethyl sulphoxide; RUX, ruxolitinib; SEL, selinexor.

Selinexor, alone and in combination with ruxolitinib, lead to G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in MPN cell lines

UKE1 (JAK2V617F) cells treated with selinexor (250 nM) ± ruxolitinib (100 nM) for 24 h showed significant upregulation of TP53 and its downstream targets (CDKN1A, NOXA1, PUMA), along with reduced BCL2 transcript levels (Figure 2A). This activation of TP53 signalling was associated with time‐dependent G0/G1 arrest and apoptosis, supported by increased PUMA/NOXA1 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression and caspase‐3 cleavage (2.8‐fold SEL; 2.6‐fold selinexor + ruxolitinib) (Figure 2A–C). At the protein level, selinexor ± ruxolitinib increased p53 (1.8‐fold; 1.3‐fold) and decreased CDK4 (0.3‐fold; 0.8‐fold) and CDC25A (0.5‐fold; 0.6‐fold), key regulators of G1/S transition (Figure 2B). Similar selinexor‐driven G0/G1 arrest and apoptosis were observed in triple‐negative ELF‐153 cells (Figure S1). In contrast, ruxolitinib alone had minimal effects on apoptosis or cell cycle–related genes, aside from increased MCL1 (Figure 2B). Overall, selinexor induces robust pro‐apoptotic and anti‐proliferative responses in MPN cell lines, largely independent of ruxolitinib.

Selinexor and ruxolitinib negatively impact NF‐κB and JAK/STAT signalling

Selinexor (250 nM) treatment in ELF‐153 cells induced XPO1 degradation and modulated NF‐κB signalling, increasing inhibitory p65 S468 phosphorylation (1.2‐fold) and decreasing activating p65 S536 phosphorylation (0.3‐fold) (Figure 3A). 24 , 25 Similar effects were observed in HEL and UKE‐1 cells (Figure S2). The combination with ruxolitinib further reduced Inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa B alpha (IκBα) and NF‐κB p65 levels (~0.3‐fold) and increased p53 protein levels (1.6‐fold selinexor; 1.2‐fold selinexor + ruxolitinib) in UKE‐1 cells as well as p53 level in ELF‐153 cells (3.0‐fold; 2.3‐fold) (Figure 3A; Figure S2). Both agents reduced STAT3 (Y705) and STAT5 (Y694) phosphorylation, with greater inhibition observed in combination (phosphoSTAT3 0.7‐fold; phosphoSTAT5 ~0.8‐fold) (Figure 3A). These results suggest enhanced inhibitory impact of the combination of selinexor and ruxolitinib on NF‐κB and JAK/STAT transcriptional activity. 26

Subcellular fractionation revealed greater XPO1 reduction in the cytoplasmic (0.6‐fold) than nuclear fraction (0.3‐fold) following selinexor treatment in ELF‐153 cells. This was accompanied by increased nuclear accumulation of p53 (6.9‐fold; 7.9‐fold), NF‐κB p65 (1.3‐fold; 1.4‐fold) and IκBα (1.9‐fold; 2.6‐fold) with selinexor alone and in combination (Figure 3B), consistent with previous data. 27 Additionally, increased p65 S468 phosphorylation and decreased STAT3/STAT5 phosphorylation were observed in both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments. Overall, these results demonstrate greater modulation of key NF‐κB and JAK/STAT pathway components with the combination compared with either single agent.

The combination of selinexor and ruxolitinib inhibits NF‐κB transcriptional activity and reduces the elaboration of key inflammatory cytokines

To explore the anti‐inflammatory effects, ELF‐153 and UKE‐1 cells were stimulated with TNFα to activate NF‐κB. Selinexor treatment reduced TNFα‐induced NF‐κB transcriptional activity in both ELF‐153 and UKE‐1 cells, indicating that the effect was not dependent on JAK2 mutational status. Although treatment with ruxolitinib alone did not significantly inhibit NF‐κB transcriptional activity in either of the cell lines, the addition of ruxolitinib augmented selinexor's reduction of NF‐κB transcriptional activity (Figure 4A,B).

Next, we evaluated the effect of the compounds on the levels of key pro‐inflammatory cytokines mediated by the NF‐κB pathway. Selinexor treatment of Phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA)‐polarized THP‐1 macrophages resulted in a dose‐dependent reduction of Lipopolysaccharide/Interferon‐gamma (LPS/IFN‐γ)–stimulated elaboration of TNFα, IL‐6 and MCP‐1. The addition of ruxolitinib (10 nM) to selinexor further attenuated cytokine release at each selinexor dose tested (Figure 4C–F).

Selinexor alone and in combination with ruxolitinib suppresses pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion from primary MF cells ex vivo

We evaluated the effects of selinexor and ruxolitinib on production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines by MF patient PBMCs. The patients had diverse molecular and clinical characteristics, summarized in Table S4. Stimulation of primary MF PBMCs with R848 increased cytokine secretion (fold increases of 309.1 ± 907.1 for IL‐6, 4.0 ± 4.0 for TNFα and 2.6 ± 1.9 for MCP‐1). Treatment with selinexor led to median reductions in IL‐6, TNFα and MCP‐1 levels of −28.3% ± 8.8%, −42.8% ± 12.3% and −39.6% ± 26.4% respectively (Figure 5). The addition of ruxolitinib further enhanced the suppression of IL‐6 and MCP‐1 to −33.4 ± 13 and −72.8% ± 22.7%, respectively, while the reduction in TNFα with the combination was −33.9% ± 11.8. By contrast, ruxolitinib alone had a minimal effect on IL‐6 (8.3% ± 19%) and TNFα (3.6% ± 15.6%) levels but did reduce MCP‐1 levels (−30.5% ± 27.9%). Although selinexor and ruxolitinib each demonstrated an overall median suppression of MCP‐1 levels, neither agent achieved statistical significance versus dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO) control, whereas the combination produced a statistically significant reduction. Subgroup analyses revealed that cytokine suppression was not influenced by either the donor's bone marrow fibrosis grade or driver mutation status.

Global and subcellular proteomics elucidate selinexor induced proteomic changes that drive apoptosis and disrupt NF‐κB signalling in MPN cells

Whole‐cell and subcellular proteomic profiling identified 5617/4724 (HEL) and 5226/4940 (ELF‐153) quantifiable proteins in nuclear and whole‐cell fractions respectively. Gene set enrichment analysis of whole‐cell lysates showed that selinexor significantly reduced multiple MF‐relevant pathways (FDR < 0.05), including NF‐κB, toll‐like receptor (TLR), NOD‐like receptor (NLR), TNF, Phosphoinositide 3‐kinase‐AKT Serine/Threonine Kinase (PI3K‐Akt) and chemokine signalling (Figure 6A), whereas ruxolitinib reduced only NLR and chemokine pathways (Figure 6B). Notably, selinexor produced broader and more consistent suppression of NF‐κB–related proteins compared to ruxolitinib (Figure S3A).

Subcellular proteomics revealed that selinexor induced significant nuclear accumulation (FDR < 0.05, absolute fold change >1.4) of 537 and 654 proteins in HEL and ELF‐153 cells, respectively, with fewer proteins decreased in the nucleus (234 and 157) (Figure 6C,D; Tables S5 and S6). Consistent with XPO1 inhibition, many known cargo proteins (e.g. p53, Forkhead box O3 (FOXO3), Checkpoint Kinase 2 (CHEK2), E2F Transcription Factor 4 (E2F4), Interferon Regulatory Factor 3 (IRF3), v‐rel avian reticuloendotheliosis viral oncogene homolog A (RELA)) showed increased nuclear localization (Nuclear Export Signal Database, NESDB). 28 , 29

Overrepresentation analysis of upregulated nuclear proteins in HEL cells demonstrated enrichment of apoptosis, FAS ligand (FasL), NLR, Ataxia‐Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) and p38 mitogen‐activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK) signalling pathways (Figure 6E). The most overrepresented pathways in ELF‐153 nuclear fractions are shown in Figure S3C. 28 , 30

Selinexor and ruxolitinib in combination effectively inhibit ruxolitinib‐resistant JAK2V617F cells

To investigate mechanisms of acquired ruxolitinib resistance and assess combination efficacy, HEL cells were rendered resistant through stepwise drug exposure, achieving a >15‐fold increase in IC50. RNA‐seq identified 2822 differentially expressed genes (FDR‐corrected p < 0.05 and absolute fold change >2), with most upregulated in resistant cells (Table S7). Enriched pathways included those implicated in MF pathobiology, including TNFα signalling via NF‐κB (normalized enrichment score [NES] = 1.85, adjusted p‐value [p adj] <0.01), hypoxia (NES = 2.10, p adj <0.01), cholesterol homeostasis (NES = 2.28, p adj <0.01), inflammatory response (NES = 1.46, p adj <0.05) and IL2‐STAT5 signalling (NES = 1.43, p adj <0.05), while involved DNA repair (NES = −1.93, p adj <0.01), G2M checkpoint (NES = −2.01, p adj <0.01) and MYC (Myelocytomatosis) targets (NES = −2.99, p adj <0.01) were downregulated (Figure S4). Only three new mutations (METTL7BG240E, MYH7F359S, PRR35R328Q; VAF > 0.2) were detected (Table S8), none previously linked to ruxolitinib resistance, indicating the resistance is likely epigenetically mediated. Importantly, selinexor retained single‐agent activity in resistant cells (Table 1) and, in combination with ruxolitinib, demonstrated similar synergistic effects in both resistant and parental HEL cells (Table S1).

DISCUSSION

Ruxolitinib is effective in treating patients with MF but has limited potential to modify the disease course. 31 Combination therapeutic strategies targeting complementary signalling pathways have emerged as an approach to overcome the limitations of ruxolitinib therapy and achieve deeper, more durable clinical responses. 32

The relevance of nuclear cytoplasmic transport in the survival of MPN cells has been previously demonstrated. 9 Our study extends these findings into the molecular mechanisms underlying the combination of XPO1 and JAK2 inhibition on pathways relevant for MF Hematopoietic Progenitor Cells (HPC) fitness using clinically achievable concentrations for the agents. This study demonstrates the pleiotropic nature of the selinexor and ruxolitinib combination in addressing key pathways that promote MF HPC fitness.

Selinexor alone and in combination with ruxolitinib demonstrated significant anti‐proliferative and anti‐inflammatory effects by modulation of MF‐associated pathways, suggesting its potential to overcome JAKi therapeutic resistance, a current unmet clinical need. Selinexor reduced the viability of MPN cell lines with different driver mutational backgrounds, including a CALR mutant ruxolitinib‐resistant cell line and demonstrated more anti‐proliferative effects when combined with currently approved JAK2i. Ex vivo, selinexor and ruxolitinib effectively suppressed colony formation by primary CD34+ cells of patients with ruxolitinib‐refractory MF. Interestingly, selinexor had limited impact on the colony numbers from CD34+ cells of normal donors, whereas ruxolitinib demonstrated comparable toxicity towards CD34+ cells from either patients with MF or normal donors. This finding may suggest a favourable therapeutic profile of selinexor compared with ruxolitinib.

Selinexor retained efficacy against ruxolitinib‐resistant HEL cells, highlighting its potential efficacy in contexts of adaptive and persistent resistance to JAK2i. This observation confirms prior results in a different HEL subline resistant to momelotinib and ruxolitinib. 9 These findings are particularly relevant, given that resistance to JAK2 inhibition frequently emerges through activation of alternative survival and inflammatory signalling pathways rather than loss of JAK/STAT dependence alone. Through XPO1 inhibition, selinexor restores nuclear retention and activity of multiple tumour suppressors and transcriptional regulators, thereby modulating signalling networks distinct from, yet complementary to, JAK2 inhibition. Transcriptomic analysis of ruxolitinib‐resistant HEL cells further indicated that resistance was driven not by new mutations but by upregulation of pro‐inflammatory and survival pathways, including NF‐κB signalling, which contributes to MF disease progression and has been shown to be dysregulated in advanced, high‐molecular risk MF. 33 Previous reports 34 have shown enriched NF‐κB signalling in JAK2V617F haematopoietic stem cells, increased NF‐κB p65 (RelA) phosphorylation/activation in the bone marrow of JAK2V617F mice and inhibition of JAK2V617F HEL, SET‐2 and UKE‐1 cells after RelA knock‐down. Selinexor inhibits NF‐κB activity by reducing IκB‐α degradation and retaining it within the nucleus, thereby blocking the NF‐κB p65‐subunit from binding to DNA. 27 Proteomic analyses in the current study demonstrated that selinexor induced a broader and more pronounced effect than ruxolitinib in modulating both global and subcellular proteomic landscapes, promoting apoptotic signalling and disrupting NF‐κB pathway activity, irrespective of JAK2 mutation status. These results underscore the role of non‐JAK/STAT pathways in MF pathobiology and support combining selinexor with JAK2 inhibitors as a mechanistically complementary strategy to overcome adaptive resistance and achieve broader pathway suppression.

The distinct clinical features of MF have been attributed in part to dysregulated inflammatory cytokine production. Fisher et al. 35 reported that many cytokines elevated in MF exhibited ex vivo hypersensitivity to thrombopoietin (TPO), TLR ligands and/or TNF, while a subset (including TNF and IL‐6) were minimally sensitive to the actions of ruxolitinib. All TPO/TLR/TNF‐sensitive cytokines, however, were sensitive to pharmacological inhibition of NF‐κB signalling. Our results indicate that selinexor effectively suppressed TNFα signalling via NF‐κB more potently than ruxolitinib and that this suppression was further enhanced by the addition of ruxolitinib. Combination therapy downregulated NF‐κB target genes and decreased synthesis of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, IL‐6 and MCP‐1, in MF primary cells. These findings support the hypothesis that selinexor can mitigate the inflammatory tumour microenvironment central to MF pathogenesis while complementing JAK/STAT‐mediated regulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokine production. 36 The plasma levels of TNFα and IL‐6 in MF patients correlated with MPN progression. 37

Ruxolitinib has previously been shown to reduce phosphorylation of STAT3 and STAT5, 38 essential proteins in JAK/STAT signalling. Constitutive activation of STAT3 has been associated with cancer and can be attributed to the large number of cytokines, including IL‐6, for which STAT3 transduces signals. 39 Selinexor blocks STAT3 nuclear export, 40 preventing its cytoplasmic delocalization and subsequent reactivation, 41 as evident by reduced levels of phosphoSTAT3 post‐treatment. These data indicate that combining selinexor with ruxolitinib further enhances STAT3/STAT5 inhibition, which can co‐operate with NF‐κB inhibition in impacting cytokine production. 34

Overall, this study provides evidence that combining selinexor with ruxolitinib can modulate key intrinsic and extrinsic factors driving MF HPC fitness, including the p53, NF‐κB and JAK/STAT pathways, and exhibits broader activity across genetically diverse and treatment‐resistant cell models compared with either agent alone. XPO1 inhibition represents a complementary therapeutic approach to JAK2 inhibition and may serve as a potential disease‐modifying strategy in MF. Selinexor's improved anti‐proliferative and anti‐inflammatory effects in combination with ruxolitinib strengthen the rationale for clinical investigation of this novel paradigm. Clinical data from the phase 1 portion of XPORT‐MF‐034 (SENTRY; NCT04562389) support these preclinical findings. Among JAKi‐naïve patients with MF treated with selinexor plus ruxolitinib, 79% achieved SVR35 at week 24. Many also showed reductions in MPN driver mutational VAFs and circulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines. 42 Selinexor is currently being evaluated in combination with ruxolitinib in the phase 3 portion of the SENTRY trial for JAKi‐naïve patients with MF (NCT04562389).

Supplemental Methods

Figure S1. Selinexor increased p53 transcriptional activity leading G1 cell cycle arrest and subsequent apoptosis in cell line lacking MPN driver mutation. ELF‐153 cells were treated with 250 nM selinexor, 100 nM ruxolitinib, or the combination for 24 h. (A) mRNA levels of TP53 and its related genes were evaluated with real‐time quantitative PCR. (B) Cells were treated for 24, 48, and 72 h, and population of cells at different cell cycle phases was determined using Attune Nxt Acoustic Focusing Cytometer (Invitrogen).

Figure S2. The combination of selinexor and ruxolitinib impacts JAK/STAT and NF‐kB pathways. HEL and UKE‐1 cells were treated with 250 nM of selinexor, 100 nM of ruxolitinib or the combination for 24 h. Protein expression of XPO1, p53, JAK/STAT pathway members and NF‐κB pathway members were evaluated in whole cell lysate. Beta‐actin was used as the loading control for the whole cell lysate.

Figure S3. Differential regulation of proteins involved in NF‐κB pathway after selinexor and ruxolitinib exposure. (A) Heat map of differential expression of proteins involved in the NF‐KB pathway following selinexor (left) or ruxolitinib (right) treatment at equitoxic concentrations in HEL cells (each drug exposure done for 6 h at each drug respective 72 h IC50 concentration). (B) Schematic representation of global and spatial proteomics methodologies. (C) Overrepresentation analysis of proteins significantly enriched in nuclear fractions of ELF‐153 cells following selinexor exposure.

Figure S4. Differentially regulated pathways in ruxolitinib‐resistant compared with HEL parental cells. Bar plot shows significantly differentially regulated pathways in ruxolitinib‐resistant HEL cells compared with parental. Pathways represented in red are increased in resistant lines, and those represented in blue are reduced in resistant lines.
