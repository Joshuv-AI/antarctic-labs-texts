A novel cabozantinib formulation with improved safety and bioavailability: preclinical validation in rats
Authors: Shangwei Qin, Yu Zhang, John Duan, George Kraft, Joanne Bowen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to compare the safety and tolerability of CABOMETYX® (commercial name of the marketed cabozantinib tablets) with a novel cabozantinib formulation, referred to hereafter as HND-039 (HP), in Wistar rats.MethodsThe research comprised several studies: a single-dose pharmacokinetic (PK) study comparing CABOMETYX® (R) to two versions of HP (T1 and T2) formulations; a dose tolerance study investigating various doses of CABOMETYX® in terms of adverse effects; and a safety and tolerability study comparing HP at 5 mg/kg/day to CABOMETYX® at 10 mg/kg/day and 7.5 mg/kg/day. Analytical methods included LC-MS/MS for sample analysis, non-compartmental analysis for pharmacokinetics, Kaplan-Meier plots for survival, and standard statistical tests for safety endpoints.ResultsIn the single-dose PK study, the HP formulation demonstrated area under the plasma concentration-time curve from time zero to the time of the last measurable concentration (AUCt) and maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax) values approximately 50% higher than those of CABOMETYX®, indicating significantly enhanced bioavailability. The dose tolerance study revealed that doses ≥ 12 mg/kg/day of CABOMETYX® resulted in severe adverse effects and high mortality, while doses ≤ 6 mg/kg/day caused minimal adverse reactions. In the safety and tolerability study, HP demonstrated significantly lower incidence of diarrhea and mortality compared to both CABOMETYX® groups. Additionally, rats treated with HP exhibited attenuated body weight loss and a less pronounced reduction in food intake, along with favorable blood pressure measurements and organ weights.ConclusionThe HP exhibits enhanced bioavailability and superior safety and a favorable safety and tolerability profile in preclinical settings. These findings support further clinical development as a potentially safer alternative to CABOMETYX®, that may reduce treatment-limiting adverse events.Keywords: Cabozantinib, Bioavailability, Gastrointestinal toxicity, Preclinical, Pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Cabozantinib (CABOMETYX®) is a multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) approved in the United States and Europe, indicated for the treatment of Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC), Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC), Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (DTC) and Neuroendocrine Tumors (NETs) [1–4].

Adverse events (AEs) associated with cabozantinib are common, including diarrhea, fatigue, hypertension, hand-foot syndrome, weight loss, decreased appetite, stomatitis, and nausea [5]. Clinical trials consistently report high rates of cabozantinib-related adverse events, which frequently necessitate dose modifications. Dose reductions have been required in up to 78% of patients, and treatment discontinuations have occurred in up to 16% [6–8]. Diarrhea is a predominant, dose-limiting AE, affecting over 70% of patients [9]. Other common AEs include fatigue, hypertension, hand-foot syndrome, and decreased appetite, which collectively contribute to significant treatment burden [5, 10].

Gastrointestinal-related adverse events induced by cabozantinib have mechanisms that remain incompletely understood. A potential explanation is that high drug exposure in the gastrointestinal tract leads to epithelial cell damage, which may trigger inflammation or compromise intestinal barrier function, ultimately resulting in adverse effects like diarrhea. Compared to agents that selectively inhibit the Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF) pathway, multi-kinase inhibitors like cabozantinib are associated with more frequent gastrointestinal adverse events. Given the high expression of VEGF and Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor Receptor (VEGFR) in the intestine [11, 12], inhibition of the VEGFR signaling pathway is widely hypothesized to play a primary role in diarrhea development. Additionally, KIT inhibition may contribute to diarrhea via altered motility [9, 13]. In metastatic triple-negative breast cancer patients, cabozantinib treatment has been shown to significantly increase cluster of differentiation 8-positive (CD8+) T lymphocytes and cluster of differentiation 14-positive (CD14+) monocytes, which could also represent a potential inflammatory mechanism underpinning diarrhea development [14].

Given gastrointestinal-related adverse events are the most common cause of dose modification with cabozantinib, they are an ideal target for new strategies to improve drug utility and ultimately patient outcomes. This study introduces a novel cabozantinib formulation (HP) that reduces gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events through enhanced drug release, lower dosing, and minimized GI exposure and retention. Through a series of preclinical pharmacokinetic and toxicity studies, we have demonstrated that the HP formulation exhibited improved safety and tolerability compared to CABOMETYX® at equivalent exposure levels.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Shanghai Medicilon Inc. (China). Each experiment was performed pursuant to its own signed memorandum. The use and care of the animals complied with Regulations for the Administration of Affair Concerning Experimental Animals and the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction and Refinement). Humane endpoints were implemented, and euthanasia via CO2 inhalation was performed at the study conclusion or if animals developed untreatable injuries or illnesses to minimize unnecessary suffering.

Animals

The Wistar rats used in this study were Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF)-grade and weighed approximately 190 g at the time of initial dosing. Prior to dosing, the rats were acclimated for 25 days under controlled environmental conditions:

Temperature: 20–25 °C.

Temperature: 20–25 °C.

Relative humidity: 40–70%.

Relative humidity: 40–70%.

12-hour light/dark cycle.

12-hour light/dark cycle.

Animals were fasted for a defined period before and after dosing as described in Sect. 2.4. Except for scheduled fasting periods, rats had ad libitum access to food and water.

Materials

HP formulations (group T1 and group T2): A cabozantinib capsule dosage form (HP) was prepared by melting the inactive ingredient (polyethylene glycol monostearate (Gelucire® 48/16) for T1 and vitamin E polyethylene glycol succinate (Vitamin E TPGS) for T2) in a water bath (55 ± 5℃) to obtain a solution. Micronized cabozantinib lauryl sulfate was added into the solution to form a uniform dispersion. The dispersion was poured into a size 9 hard gelatin capsule (approximate dimensions: 5.00 mm in length×2.05 mm in diameter) and immediately cooled to solidify.

Cabozantinib reference capsules (CABOMETYX®, group R): Cabozantinib reference capsules were prepared by refilling a size 9 hard gelatin capsule with pulverized commercial CABOMETYX® tablets (60 mg).

CABOMETYX® was purchased from Patheon (France). Cabozantinib lauryl sulfate was purchased from Nanjing Hairun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (China). Polyethylene glycol monostearate was purchased from Gattefosséé SAS (France). Vitamin E polyethylene glycol succinate was purchased from Jiangxi Lianlu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Hard gelatin capsules were purchased from Guangdong Qiangji Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (China). The reference standard was supplied by Nanjing Hairun Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (China). Sample preparation was completed at Hangzhou Handa Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). All other reagents were of analytical grade or higher.

Study design

Study 1: Pharmacokinetic study.

A 24 h pharmacokinetic study was conducted to compare drug absorption between formulations. Eighteen male SPF Wistar rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6). All animals were fasted overnight for at least 10 h prior to dosing. Each rat received a single 2 mg dose of either CABOMETYX®, T1 (HP), or T2 (HP), administered via oral gavage. Specifically, the solid-filled capsule was placed at the tip of a rodent-specific gavage applicator and gently delivered into the stomach. Food was returned to cages 4 h post-dose. Blood samples were collected pre-dose and at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, and 24 h post-dose.

Study 2: CABOMETYX® dose-tolerability study.

A dose-tolerability study was conducted to set the dose range associated with cabozantinib intolerance in rats (rather than identifying the maximum tolerated dose (MTD)). Thirty-two male SPF Wistar rats were randomly divided into four groups (n = 8) and received once-daily oral administration of CABOMETYX® at either 0.6, 1.2, 2.4 or 4.8 mg for 37 consecutive days. All animals were fasted overnight for at least 10 h before receiving cabozantinib, and food was returned to cages 2 h post-dose. The rats in each group were randomly assigned into two cages. Mortality, body weight changes, daily food intake, and diarrhea scores were measured across the observational period [15, 16].

Study 3: Safety and tolerability study.

A safety and tolerability study was conducted to identify the superior cabozantinib formulation by comparing incidence and severity of adverse effects. Thirty female SPF Wistar rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 10) and received either 2 mg CABOMETYX®, 1.5 mg CABOMETYX®, or 1 mg T1 (HP) for 35 consecutive days. Animals were fasted for 4 h pre-dose and received food 2 h post-dose. Mortality, body weight changes, daily food intake, diarrhea scores, and blood pressure (systolic and diastolic) were collected across the duration of the observational period. At cull, small and large intestines were removed and weighed.

Based on known differences in sensitivity to cabozantinib toxicity between sexes in rats, this study utilized male rats for pharmacokinetic assessment while employing the more toxicity-sensitive female rats for safety evaluation. Reference data indicate no significant sex-related differences in key pharmacokinetic parameters; this design was implemented to optimize the sensitivity of toxicity detection.

Sampling and testing

For pharmacokinetic analysis, blood samples (0.2 mL per time point) were collected from the jugular vein. After fixing the animal in a supine position and disinfecting the cervical area, the jugular vein was punctured using a sterile needle attached to a syringe. Blood was gently aspirated via negative pressure. The procedure was performed swiftly by trained personnel to minimize animal stress and discomfort. Samples were placed in tubes containing dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetate (K2-EDTA) as an anticoagulant and kept on ice until centrifugation. Within 1 h of collection, blood samples were centrifuged at 6800 rpm for 6 min under 2–8 °C. Plasma was subsequently isolated and stored frozen at approximately − 80 °C until analysis.

Protein precipitation was performed with 20 µL of plasma sample and 400 µL of MeOH that contained 10 ng/mL of internal standard (IS). The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged at 18,000 g for 7 min. 400 µL supernatant was transferred to each well of a 96 well plates. 1 µL of supernatant was injected for Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry/Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis. Mobile phase A was 0.1% Formic acid in water, and mobile phase B was 0.1% Formic acid in acetonitrile. Flow rate was 0.60 mL/min. Column was ACQUITY UPLC BEH Phenyl 1.7 μm 2.1 × 50 mm, and the sample tray was controlled at 40℃. The validated detection and quantification range for cabozantinib in rat plasma samples is 5 ng/mL to 10,000 ng/mL.

In the safety and tolerability study, body weight was recorded daily, blood pressure was measured every three days, and food intake was monitored and recorded twice daily. The feed hoppers were weighed on a daily basis, and food intake was calculated for individual animals. Diarrhea severity was scored twice daily and assessed as:

0 points: Firm stool.

1 point: Soft stool without perianal fur staining. (mild)

2 points: Wet stool with perianal fur staining. (moderate)

3 points: Very watery stool with staining extending to the legs or abdomen. (severe)

At the end of the study, all animals were euthanized via CO2 inhalation. The entire stomach, full-length small intestine, and entire colon (from the cecum to the anus) were excised. The stomach was opened along the greater curvature, and the intestines were opened along the mesenteric border. The length and weight of the stomach, intestines, and colon were measured before and after rinsing with saline. The evaluation of gross pathological lesions was performed using a semi-quantitative ulcer scoring system, adapted from established methods for assessing gastrointestinal injury in rodent models [17]. An ulcer scoring system was applied to evaluate necrotic lesions in the stomach, intestines, and colon during autopsy. The ulcer scoring system is as follows:

0 points: no lesions (normal stomach)

1 point: hyperemia

2 points: ≥3 distinct hemorrhagic spots (≤ 1 mm diameter)

3 points: 1 to 5 small ulcers (1–3 mm diameter)

4 points: >5 small ulcers

5 points: 1 to 5 small ulcers and 1 to 3 large ulcers (> 3 mm diameter)

6 points: >5 small ulcers and > 3 large ulcers

7 points: Full-thickness perforations

Pharmacokinetic Analysis

A non-compartmental analysis of plasma concentration-time data was performed using Phoenix WinNonlin® 7.0 (Certara, USA). The following pharmacokinetic parameters for cabozantinib were derived:

Cmax and Tmax: the observed maximum plasma concentration and its corresponding time.

AUC0−t: the area under the concentration-time curve from time zero to the last measurable concentration (Clast), calculated using the linear trapezoidal rule.

Statistical analyses

Statistical significance was predefined as a two-sided P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23 and R software. Key statistical tests included:

PK analysis: Analysis of cabozantinib in rats with Welch correction for dose-group comparisons. Log-transformed Cmax and AUC parameters were analyzed using an analysis of variance (ANOVA) model, with treatment as a fixed effect. 90% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the geometric mean ratios (test/reference) of Cmax, AUC0–t, and AUC0–∞.

Safety and tolerability analysis: To estimate the survival functions for each treatment group, time-to-event data were analyzed via the Kaplan-Meier method. Differences between groups were assessed using the two-sided log-rank test. Rats without an event by the end of the study period were censored at the date of last observation. As detailed in Study 3, the time to onset of diarrhea (scores ≥ 1 and ≥ 2) was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves. Given that diarrhea monitoring is conducted twice a day, “day” is used as a time variable and is incrementally increased by half a day, with 0.5 score in the morning of the first day, 1 score in the afternoon of the first day, and so on, until the end of study. Differences among the three groups were assessed for statistical significance [18].

Results

Single-dose pharmacokinetic study

Bioanalytical method development and validation were conducted by the testing institution using LC-MS/MS [19]. The bioanalytical method was validated according to FDA guidance for specificity, accuracy, precision, and lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). Quality controlled reference standards were used to verify analytical results and inter-assay variability. The accuracy of the controlled standards should exceed 66.7% and fall within 80% to 120% of the known value(s).

All rats were included in the PK analysis. Plasma parameters and PK parameters for each group are summarized in Table 1. To obtain these parameters, plasma concentration-time profiles were created following a single oral administration to healthy Wistar rats of CABOMETYX® (group R) or HP (group T1 and group T2) at a dose of 2 mg per rat, which is equivalent to 10 mg/kg/day based on an average body weight of 0.2 kg (Fig. 1). The median time to peak plasma concentration (Tmax) after 2 mg of CABOMETYX® administration was 4.00 h (range: 2.00–8.00), whereas the median Tmax values for 2 mg of T1 and T2 were 2.00 h (range: 1.00–4.00), and 3.00 h (range: 2.00–4.00), respectively. The elimination half-life (t1/2) was comparable across all groups. Drug exposure (AUCt and Cmax) in plasma was higher for both group T1 and group T2 compared to group R. Specifically, when compared to Group R under fasting conditions, group T1 (the proposed commercial formulation) increased 109.03% in Cmax and 54.97% in AUCt, while group T2 increased 120.59% in Cmax and 64.87% in AUCt. The animal study results show that under fasting conditions, group T1 had the potential to enhance bioavailability. Considering that cabozantinib is used for chronic cancer treatment, AUC may serve as a more clinically meaningful exposure metric. Based on the comparative AUC results between T1 and R, it can be concluded that the bioavailability of T1 in rats was at least 50% higher.

Dose tolerance study

After establishing the PK and bioavailability relationship between T1 (HP) and CABOMETYX®, the next step was to determine an appropriate dose schedule of CABOMETYX® for evaluating tolerance and safety in Wistar rats and future comparisons with HP formulations. Ideally, the dose of CABOMETYX® needed to reflect the clinical symptoms experienced by patients without excessive toxicity. Therefore, a dose-ranging toxicity study was conducted. Wistar rats received daily oral administration of CABOMETYX® at 0.6 mg (3 mg/kg/day), 1.2 mg (6 mg/kg/day), 2.4 mg (12 mg/kg/day), and 4.8 mg (24 mg/kg/day) for 37 consecutive days. Throughout the study, parameters such as food consumption, body weight, mortality, and adverse effects were monitored.

Throughout the study, food intake and body weight in the 3 mg/kg/day and 6 mg/kg/day dose groups remained relatively stable. In the 12 mg/kg/day group, food intake was stable during the first 16 days but exhibited a declining transition period around Day 20. Meanwhile, body weight increased during the first 6 days before gradually decreasing thereafter. In contrast, the 24 mg/kg/day group showed a sharp decline in food intake and body weight starting immediately after treatment initiation which worsened progressively (fluctuations in food intake and body weight curves were attributed to inter-individual variability) (Fig. 2a and b). The Kaplan-Meier plot demonstrated significant differences in survival outcomes among the four treatment groups (Fig. 2c).

(a) Food intake, (b) relative body weight change, and (c) survival in rats following treatment with CABOMETYX®. Data for (a) and (b) are presented as mean ± SD. Pairwise ANOVA comparisons for (b) showed statistically significant differences among all groups (p < 0.05). Data for (c) are presented as a Kaplan-Meier plot

Diarrhea was observed in the groups with dose levels ≥ 12 mg/kg/day. Rats in the 12 mg/kg/day group developed persistent, recurrent diarrhea starting from Day 16, while those in the 24 mg/kg/day group showed multiple episodes of continuous mild or moderate diarrhea (Grade 1 and Grade 2 diarrhea) from Day 13. In contrast, rats in the other two dose groups only occasionally presented sporadic, mild diarrhea (Grade 1 diarrhea), suggesting minimal GI toxicity of cabozantinib formulations at ≤ 6 mg/kg/day.

Safety and tolerability study

Based on the findings of Study 2, the dosing regimens for CABOMETYX® were established at 10 mg/kg/day and 7.5 mg/kg/day. Since study 1 demonstrated that HP formulation exhibited ~ 50% higher bioavailability compared to CABOMETYX®, 1 mg (5 mg/kg/day) of HP formulation was predicted to achieve AUC equivalence (or bioequivalence) to 1.5 mg (7.5 mg/kg/day) of CABOMETYX®. Throughout the study, parameters such as food intake, body weight, mortality, diarrhea, and other adverse effects were monitored.

The overall food intake of these three groups began decreasing from Day 3 by around 13 g per day (Fig. 3a). The CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group and the CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group showed a sharper, progressive decline in food consumption immediately after treatment initiation. Notably, the decline in food intake of the HP group was far less pronounced than in the CABOMETYX® groups. The average food intake of the 5 mg/kg/day HP group was significantly higher than that of the CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group (P < 0.001) and the CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group (P < 0.005), while no statistically significant difference was observed in food intake between the two CABOMETYX® groups.

(a) Food intake, (b) relative body weight change, (c) survival, (d) systolic blood pressure, and (e) diastolic blood pressure in rats following treatment with CABOMETYX® or HP (Data for (a), (b), (d) and (e) are presented as mean ± SD and data for (c) are presented as a Kaplan-Meier plot)

Figure 3b shows the weight gain ceased earliest in the CABOMETYX® groups (fluctuations in weight curves were attributed to inter-individual variability), followed by a progressive weight decline. Further statistical comparisons revealed significant differences among the three groups. Specifically, from the 17th day to the end of the experiment, there was a significantly higher body weight in the HP group than both CABOMETYX® groups (P < 0.001). Similarly, from Day 27 to Day 34, the body weight of the Group CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day was significantly lower than that of the CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group (P < 0.001).

Mortality was absent in the HP group but substantial in both CABOMETYX® groups. The CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group reached 80% mortality by Day 35, while the CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group progressed to 70% by the study’s end (Fig. 3c). The mortality rate of the 5 mg/kg/day HP group was thus significantly lower than that of both CABOMETYX groups (P < 0.001).

All groups showed an overall downward trend in systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). The CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group had significantly lower SBP than the HP group (5 mg/kg/day) on days 16, 22, 25, and 28 (P < 0.05), as did the CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group on day 34. Conversely, the HP group exhibited significantly higher DBP than the CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group on days 4 and 28 and had higher DBP than the CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group on days 22 and 28 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3d and e).

Over the 35-day study, diarrhea incidence was highest in the CABOMETYX® 10 mg/kg/day group (28 instances total; 19 score 1, 9 score 2), followed by the CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group (25 instances total; 10 score 1, 14 score 2, 1 score 3), and lowest in the 5 mg/kg/day HP group (10 instances total; 7 score 1, 3 score 2). No significant difference was found between the two CABOMETYX® groups. However, Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that the HP group had a significantly longer time to onset of both mild (score ≥ 1) and moderate (score ≥ 2) diarrhea compared to either CABOMETYX® group (Fig. 4).

Kaplan-Meier plots for time to first diarrhea event in the rat toxicity study comparing HP 5 mg/kg/day with CABOMETYX® groups. (a) Diarrhea Score (≥ 1) for HP Versus CABOMETYX® at 10 and 7.5 mg/kg/day (b) Diarrhea Score (≥ 2) for HP Versus CABOMETYX® at 10 and 7.5 mg/kg/day

Assessment of GI toxicity revealed that stomach and colon weights (after cleaning) in the HP group were significantly higher than those in the CABOMETYX® 7.5 mg/kg/day group (P < 0.05). No other statistically significant differences were observed in the weights of the stomach, intestine, or colon among the three groups, nor in the lengths of the intestine and colon (Fig. 5a).

Organ toxicity endpoints in rats following treatment with CABOMETYX® or HP: (a) stomach, intestine, and colon weights (after cleaning), (b) ulcer score of the intestine and (c) ulcer area of the intestine (all data are presented as mean ± SD)

The 5 mg/kg/day HP group demonstrated significantly lower duodenal ulcer scores and areas than both CABOMETYX® groups (P < 0.05). Although gastric ulcer scores and areas were also lower in the HP group, the differences were not statistically significant. No colonic ulcers were observed in any group (Fig. 5b and c).

Discussion

The HP formulation demonstrated significantly higher bioavailability (≥ 50%) versus CABOMETYX®. In the safety and tolerability study, HP at 5 mg/kg/day outperformed both CABOMETYX® dosages (7.5 and 10 mg/kg/day) in food intake, body weight maintenance, survival rate, diarrhea incidence, and duodenal ulcer scores, indicating better GI tolerability in this preclinical model. These preclinical findings suggest that the HP formulation may enhance cabozantinib bioavailability and improve tolerability at an exposure level expected to be comparable to CABOMETYX®, supporting its potential for clinical development.

i. Integration of PK findings and safety outcomes

The single-dose pharmacokinetic study (Study 1) established that at an equivalent dose (2 mg/rat), the HP formulation achieved approximately 50% higher systemic exposure (AUC) than CABOMETYX®. This PK profile informed the design of the pivotal safety study (Study 3): a lower dose of HP (1 mg/rat) was selected to evaluate its potential in providing systemic exposure and tolerability comparable to higher doses of CABOMETYX® (1.5 and 2 mg/rat). The results confirmed this rationale; HP at 1 mg/rat demonstrated significantly improved GI tolerability (e.g., reduced diarrhea, better weight maintenance) compared to both CABOMETYX® dose groups. This dissociation-comparable or superior safety at a substantially lower absolute dose-strongly supports the hypothesis that the enhanced bioavailability of HP reduces the local GI drug burden, a key driver of cabozantinib’s dose-limiting toxicity [20]. This is of direct clinical relevance, as GI toxicities lead to dose modifications in most patients [6, 7, 9, 14, 21]. By potentially mitigating these dose-dependent adverse effects, the HP formulation could address treatment-limiting toxicities that impair treatment adherence [14, 21].

ii. Translational perspective and imperative for clinical validation

It is critical to state that extrapolating rat PK data and dose ratios directly to human bioequivalence is premature. The potential clinical advantage of HP-maintaining efficacy at a lower, better-tolerated dose remains a hypothesis derived from this integrated preclinical package. These findings provide a strong rationale and a clear framework for clinical testing; human bioequivalence studies should assess whether the exposure advantage observed in rats translates to patients, and subsequent efficacy trials must verify that the lower dose of HP does not compromise antitumor activity. Confirmation in clinical trials would position HP as a valuable strategy to mitigate the high rate of cabozantinib-related dose reductions and interruptions [21]. Further clinical validation would also support its development as a promising alternative to CABOMETYX® that may improve quality of life and long-term treatment success.

iii. Study limitations and ethical considerations

A key limitation is that the projected chronic exposure equivalence between formulations was extrapolated from single-dose PK data (Study 1) and not measured at steady-state during long-term studies. Furthermore, while the tolerability data strongly suggest reduced local GI exposure, this mechanism lacks direct confirmation, as drug concentrations in gastrointestinal tissues were not measured. Future work should monitor drug levels in patients to confirm the improved safety profile stems from reduced local GI exposure rather than lower systemic exposure. Regarding animal welfare, the elevated mortality in the CABOMETYX dose-tolerability study (Study 2) was a consequence of its design objective to characterize the dose-toxicity relationship up to severe levels, not to simulate therapeutic exposure. All procedures adhered to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-approved protocols with strict, humane endpoints.

Conclusion

This study established a preclinical platform for evaluating multi-kinase inhibitor toxicity with a focus on GI impacts. In this rat model, cabozantinib-induced dose-related mucosal injury was shown throughout the GI tract, and this injury was reduced with the HP formulation administered at a 50% lower dose. These preclinical findings suggest that HND-039 is a promising candidate for further clinical development. If confirmed in human trials, its improved GI tolerability and bioavailability might offer substantial patient benefits in chronic cabozantinib therapy.
