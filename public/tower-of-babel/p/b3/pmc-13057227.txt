Spatially divided outlier removal for LiDAR de-noising in adverse weather conditions
Authors: Sangkyu Shin, Jaehan Joo, Hunyoul Lee, Suk Chan Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdverse weather conditions, such as rain and snow, significantly degrade the performance of Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) perception systems, creating a demand for real-time and high-precision denoising algorithms. This paper proposes a novel LiDAR point cloud denoising method, Spatially Divided Outlier Removal (SDOR), which spatially divides the point cloud into multiple sectors and processes them in parallel to reduce execution time. Furthermore, SDOR dynamically adjusts the search radius based on the point density within each sector, enabling more accurate noise removal than conventional distance-based filtering. Experiments on the WADS and Weather-KITTI datasets show that SDOR achieves F1-scores of 90.53% on WADS and 90.24% (Snow) / 91.20% (Fog) on Weather-KITTI, along with error rates as low as 6.17%, 0.61%, and 2.89%, respectively. In addition, SDOR demonstrates the fastest execution times on the Weather-KITTI benchmark, recording 0.219 s in Snow, 0.241 s in Rain, and 0.229 s in Fog. These results highlight that SDOR provides both high denoising accuracy and real-time performance, making it a promising solution for reliable LiDAR perception under diverse adverse weather conditions.Subject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The development of autonomous vehicle technology has received significant attention and investment over the past few years. One of the major challenges faced by such vehicles is the ability to perceive the environment and navigate without human intervention1. To address this issue, autonomous vehicles are equipped with various sensors—cameras, Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), radar, and GPS—to gather rich environmental data. These data are then integrated with advanced algorithms and machine learning techniques to perceive their surroundings and navigate effectively2–4. Among these sensors, LiDAR is considered a critical component for enabling vehicles to accurately sense and understand the environment. In most cases, sensor fusion significantly improves the reliability and efficiency of autonomous vehicles5–11. However, despite the rapid advancements in LiDAR sensing technology and data processing techniques, several challenges, particularly noise and perception errors under adverse weather conditions, remain unresolved.

One of the most critical challenges is the contamination of LiDAR data by noise under adverse weather conditions. Many researchers have observed that the quality of LiDAR data degrades significantly when collected during adverse weather events such as strong winds, rain, snow, and fog12–18. To address this problem, various point cloud de-noising techniques have been proposed. These methods strive to balance precision and recall while still meeting real-time processing requirements under extreme weather conditions19,20.

LiDAR noise removal techniques can be broadly categorized into distance-based filtering methods, intensity-based filtering methods, and deep learning-based methods. Distance-based filtering removes outliers by utilizing the spatial relationships between points. Although it is effective in removing isolated noise, it suffers from high computational cost, making real-time processing challenging. Representative approaches include Radius Outlier Removal (ROR) and Statistical Outlier Removal (SOR)21, which remove noise by applying a radius-based or statistical threshold. However, these filters have limitations in fully considering the non-uniform characteristics of LiDAR data, making it difficult to effectively remove environment-specific noise such as snow. To address this limitation, Dynamic Radius Outlier Removal (DROR)15 and Dynamic Statistical Outlier Removal (DSOR)18 have been proposed, where the search radius is dynamically adjusted according to point-to-point distance variations. However, these methods incur an increased computational complexity due to the need for real-time adjustment of the search radius for each point.

Intensity-based filtering classifies noise in LiDAR point clouds by utilizing reflection intensity information, offering lower computational cost and faster processing speed compared to distance-based methods. A representative method is Low-Intensity Outlier Removal (LIOR)22, which removes noise by setting an intensity threshold based on snow modeling and analysis of light characteristics. Although LIOR has demonstrated higher accuracy and faster computational performance than traditional distance-based filters, its fixed intensity threshold limits its adaptability to varying environmental conditions. To address this limitation, various extended techniques have been proposed. Representative examples include Dynamic Light-Intensity Outlier Removal (DIOR)23, which combines LIOR and DROR, and Low-Intensity Dynamic Radius Outlier Removal (LIDROR)24, which enhances robustness against distance variations based on LIOR. While these techniques improve the performance of the original LIOR, they still suffer from increased computational cost, limiting their applicability for real-time processing, and continue to face challenges in adapting to dynamic environmental conditions.

Deep learning-based filtering techniques classify noise in LiDAR point clouds by leveraging deep learning algorithms that learn from labeled datasets to identify noise. This approach can achieve higher performance than traditional algorithms, especially in complex environments; however, it requires a large amount of labeled data and incurs high computational costs. A representative study is WeatherNet25, which utilizes LiLaNet as a backbone and proposes a deep learning-based denoising method capable of effectively removing LiDAR noise caused by weather conditions such as rain, snow, and fog. Furthermore, 4DenoiseNet26, the first model to consider temporal coherence, was developed to preserve object shapes while removing noise not only in static but also in dynamic environments. Experimental results demonstrated that 4DenoiseNet achieved superior denoising performance compared to the existing WeatherNet. Subsequently, SLiDE20, the first self-supervised learning-based model for LiDAR noise removal, was introduced, enabling training without labels and extending its applicability to supervised learning models. However, further research is still needed to enable real-time application of these techniques.

In parallel with these developments, several recent studies across adjacent sensing and AI domains have explored multimodal fusion and advanced representation learning strategies. For example, adaptive Fourier decomposition has been combined with deep learning for enhanced physiological signal interpretation27, while LiDAR–camera fusion frameworks have been proposed to improve defect detection robustness in industrial inspection28. Hybrid detection architectures integrating template-based priors with attention mechanisms have also been introduced29, and large-scale language-model-driven recommendation frameworks have emerged in other intelligent decision-making tasks30. Although these works target different application domains, they collectively highlight an increasing trend toward combining physics-based modeling, multimodal sensing, and learning-based optimization to improve robustness under challenging conditions.

While the aforementioned distance-based, intensity-based, and deep learning-based approaches each offer unique advantages, they also suffer from limitations such as high computational cost, lack of adaptability to varying environments, or dependence on large labeled datasets.

To address these limitations and to achieve both high denoising accuracy and real-time processing efficiency, we propose a novel noise removal method, named Spatially Divided Outlier Removal (SDOR). The proposed method divides the LiDAR point cloud into multiple independent spatial regions based on vertical and horizontal resolutions, allowing for parallel processing to significantly improve computational efficiency. Within each region, a point is classified as an outlier if the number of neighboring points within a given radius is below a specified threshold. Furthermore, the search radius is dynamically adjusted based on the distance from the sensor and the local density within each region, enabling more precise noise removal by accounting for the non-uniform density characteristics of LiDAR data.

Methods

The proposed SDOR filter is a novel approach designed to effectively remove outlier points from LiDAR point clouds. Unlike traditional filters, SDOR divides the LiDAR data into multiple spatial regions based on horizontal and vertical resolutions, and performs noise filtering in parallel for each region. Furthermore, SDOR dynamically adjusts the search radius according to the point cloud density within each region, compensating for the non-uniformity of point spacing and enhancing noise removal performance. This section details the structure and algorithm of the proposed method.

Dividing the LiDAR data into multiple sectors

Most traditional noise removal filters apply a global filtering operation to the entire point cloud collected by LiDAR. However, this approach results in a significant increase in computational load as the size of the point cloud grows, creating challenges for real-time processing. In this study, the LiDAR point cloud is divided into multiple regions by partitioning the 3D space into horizontal sectors along the x-y plane and vertical sectors along the z-axis. By processing each sector in parallel and then merging the results, the overall efficiency and scalability of noise removal are improved.

Dynamic outlier removal for sector-wise point clouds

Based on the previously derived method, the LiDAR point cloud is divided into horizontal () and vertical () sectors. Dynamic outlier removal is then performed independently within each sector.

The outlier removal process involves setting a dynamic search radius using a density adjustment factor and evaluating the number of neighboring points within this radius to filter out outliers.

The key feature of the proposed method is that the search radius is dynamically adjusted not only based on the distance between the LiDAR sensor and the points, but also considering the density of each sector. This differs from conventional methods such as DROR and DSOR, which set the search radius and threshold solely based on the distance to the LiDAR sensor. by incorporating sector-wise density information, the proposed approach enables a more adaptive identification and removal of outliers.

Neighbor point evaluation and outlier removal

To efficiently perform nearest neighbor searches, a global KD-tree31 is constructed for the entire point cloud rather than creating separate KD-trees for each sector. Using sector-specific KD-trees can lead to information loss at sector boundaries when the search radius overlaps into adjacent sectors, since neighboring points outside the sector would be ignored. By applying a single global KD-tree and then using a sector-wise decision to determine which sectors to filter, this issue is avoided while still enabling parallelized processing.

Experiments

Datasets

In this study, two LiDAR point cloud datasets, WADS18 and Weather-KITTI32, were used to evaluate the performance of the proposed filter under adverse weather conditions.

The WADS dataset is the first publicly available LiDAR dataset that includes labeled snow particles. Each point in WADS is categorized into one of 22 classes, and the dataset consists of 19 scenes, each containing approximately 100 frames with more than 200,000 points per frame. In this work, sequences 13, 15, 22, 23, and 24 from the WADS dataset were utilized to assess the filter performance in real snow environments.

Weather-KITTI, on the other hand, is a synthetic LiDAR dataset derived from SemanticKITTI and designed to simulate various adverse weather conditions. It contains 22 sequences totaling 130,656 frames, segmented into 21 semantic classes, including additional labels for snow (110), fog (111), and rain (112), with semantic annotations for non-weather classes available only in the first ten sequences. In this experiment, sequences 00, 01, and 02 corresponding to the snow scenario were used to evaluate denoising performance under simulated conditions.

In this work, weather-induced noise points (e.g., snow, rain, and fog) were evaluated according to their corresponding semantic labels, while all non-weather classes—such as vehicles, buildings, and other static or dynamic structures—were grouped into broader Object Categories for separate evaluation. This allowed consistent comparison across different adverse-weather scenarios without relying on class-specific characteristics.

Evaluation metrics

To evaluate the performance of the filters, we classified the de-noising results into four categories using a 2×2 confusion matrix. True Positive (TP) refers to the number of points correctly identified as snow noise, while True Negative (TN) denotes the number of points correctly identified as object points. False Positive (FP) represents object points that are incorrectly classified as snow, and False Negative (FN) refers to snow points that are mistakenly classified as object points.

While accuracy is a commonly used metric in classification tasks, the WADS and Weather-KITTI datasets are unbalanced datasets with far more object points than snow noise points. Therefore, accuracy alone is insufficient to evaluate filter performance. In contrast, the F1-score provides a more balanced evaluation by considering both precision and recall32. A higher F1-score indicates a better balance between precision and recall, implying superior filtering performance.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the operational characteristics and performance of the proposed SDOR filter across various adverse-weather conditions, and performed a comprehensive comparison with existing distance-based and intensity-based filters. Throughout all experiments, SDOR consistently maintained high accuracy and F1-score under snow, rain, and fog environments, demonstrating strong robustness to changes in point distribution and environmental factors. This confirms that the sector-wise density-normalized search radius enables stable radius adjustment even in scenes with significant local density variation.

Distance-based filters such as DROR and DSOR showed stable performance overall; however, due to the absence of an explicit mechanism for compensating spatial density fluctuations, they exhibited structural limitations, particularly a degradation in precision when the point distribution became highly uneven. In contrast, SDOR normalizes the local density on horizontal and vertical sectors, allowing it to maintain a balanced precision–recall relationship and achieve consistently higher accuracy and F1-score than DROR and DSOR across diverse environmental conditions.

Intensity-based filters (LIOR, LIDROR, DIOR) operate by identifying inlier points through intensity thresholding. This approach yields very high precision and fast execution in the WADS (snow) environment, where the intensity distribution is relatively consistent. However, in the Weather-KITTI dataset, where intensity variation is substantially larger, the thresholding stage fails to sufficiently discriminate inliers, resulting in a significant increase in points passed to the subsequent neighbor-checking stage and consequently increasing the overall runtime. In contrast, SDOR does not use intensity information and is therefore unaffected by such variations, achieving faster runtime than intensity-based filters under the Weather-KITTI conditions.

The sector-based structure of SDOR also provides major benefits in terms of parallelization. Since all sectors apply identical filtering rules independently, multi-threaded execution naturally aligns with the algorithmic structure. In our experiments, the multi-threaded implementation reduced the execution time by approximately 64% compared to the single-thread version. Nevertheless, when the number of sectors becomes excessively large, each sector becomes too sparse and the increased thread-management overhead negatively impacts real-time performance. Therefore, the selection of an appropriate number of sectors is crucial.

The effect of sector partitioning was further investigated through the Sector Boundary Consistency Analysis. A region around each horizontal sector boundary was defined as a boundary zone and compared to the interior region. The results showed that the F1-score decreased from 92.53% (interior) to 90.70% (boundary), and the accuracy decreased from 95.36% to 92.43%. Precision was lower in the boundary zone, while recall slightly increased, indicating that density discontinuities near sector boundaries directly affect radius adjustment.

Additional visual analyses reinforce these observations. Figure 3 illustrates the F1-score distribution across combinations of horizontal and vertical sector counts. Increasing the vertical sector count from 1 to 2 or 4 more effectively reflects the vertically non-uniform density pattern inherent to LiDAR channels, yielding performance improvements. Figure 5 presents the execution-time heatmap for the same sector configurations, showing that moderate sector counts reduce workload per sector and benefit parallelization, whereas overly fine subdivisions introduce unnecessary overhead. Finally, Fig. 4 visualizes the total execution time as a function of the number of sectors, revealing a U-shaped trend in which runtime initially decreases due to improved parallelism but increases once sparse-sector and overhead effects dominate.

Empirically, the range of to 8 provided the most stable balance between accuracy, runtime, and robustness, with each sector containing several thousand points to ensure reliable density estimation. In this work, we adopted and as the default configuration, as they consistently delivered the strongest overall performance.

Overall, SDOR simultaneously achieves robust performance and real-time capability across diverse adverse-weather conditions, demonstrating clear advantages in stability and scalability compared to conventional distance-based and intensity-based filters. However, several structural limitations remain, including performance variation near sector boundaries, sparse-sector issues when partitioning becomes excessive, and parallelization overhead at high thread counts. Future work may address these challenges through soft-overlap sector designs, hybrid radius-adjustment strategies incorporating intensity cues, and continuous-density-field based compensation suitable for environments with extreme fog or severe point-density fluctuations.

Conclusion

Adverse weather conditions, such as rain and snow, significantly degrade the performance of LiDAR perception systems. To address this issue, we proposed a novel LiDAR point cloud denoising method called SDOR. Unlike conventional approaches that process the entire point cloud at once, the proposed SDOR filter divides the space into multiple sectors and applies parallel processing. Moreover, it dynamically adjusts the search radius based on the point density within each sector, enabling more precise noise removal beyond simple distance-based filtering.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the operational characteristics of the proposed SDOR filter. We first examined how the key parameters of SDOR influence the adaptive radius adjustment and noise removal behavior, followed by an evaluation of how different horizontal and vertical sector partitions affect both accuracy and computational efficiency. We also compared single-thread and multi-thread execution to verify the benefit of SDOR’s parallel processing structure for real-time performance, and performed a sector-boundary analysis to assess the impact of local density discontinuities on filtering stability. Through these investigations, SDOR demonstrated consistent noise removal performance and strong structural preservation across a variety of adverse-weather conditions, surpassing both distance-based filters (ROR, SOR, DROR, DSOR) and intensity-based filters (LIOR, LIDROR, DIOR). These results confirm the practical applicability and scalability of SDOR as an effective real-time LiDAR preprocessing method.

The proposed SDOR method introduces a novel concept of spatial division combined with a parallel processing structure, offering both high denoising accuracy and real-time performance in LiDAR point cloud filtering. These findings highlight SDOR’s strong practical applicability and scalability, as it effectively removes weather-induced noise while maintaining structural integrity, thereby ensuring stable perception performance in autonomous driving systems. This work demonstrates the potential of SDOR as a foundational technology for enhancing the reliability of autonomous vehicles under adverse weather conditions.
