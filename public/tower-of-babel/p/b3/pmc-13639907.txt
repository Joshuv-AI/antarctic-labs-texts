Increasing the Incentive to Serve: The Effects of a Juror Pay Increase in Texas
Authors: Mary R Rose, Max Lisch, Faris A Husain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough low juror pay is commonly offered as a reason why jurors do not show up to jury service, there is remarkably little empirical information on how juror pay affects summons response. This study examines four large‐sized counties in Texas before and after pay increased from as little as $6 a day to $40, analyzing patterns of the percent of available jurors and non‐response (“no show”) across time. Although there was, on average, a larger percentage of available jurors in the years following the pay raise compared to those preceding it, there is little evidence that this increase is attributable to the change in pay, particularly given a trend of having more available jurors that pre‐existed the raise. Additionally, summons response does not appear sensitive to over‐time inflationary pressures. Results suggest that pay may not be the best place to focus attention if the goal is to generate an overall higher summons response rate, although states should ensure that rates are not insultingly low, and we encourage more work on whether targeted increases for low‐income persons have an effect on that population.

Introduction

Each year, a substantial number of jury summonses go out to people who do not end up serving (e.g., Gau 2016; Hannaford‐Agor et al. 2023; Taylor et al. 2007). Although many factors can affect whether a summons leads to an experience with jury service (see Rose et al. 2012), undoubtedly lack of resources is an issue (see Boatright 1998; Eades 2001). For example, a recent survey of Harris County jurors who did not respond to a jury summons found that 39% said that they faced hardships, a category that included lack of pay (Hannaford‐Agor, Moffett, Embry, et al. 2023). A recent pilot program in San Francisco, California reports on low‐income persons who were eligible for a substantial rise in juror pay (from $15 a day to $100 per day); 81% of these individuals said that they could not have served without the financial support the program provided (Lau and Stuhldreher 2022). This is not a new concern. People avoiding summonses to serve as jurors in the early days of the United States was a routine juror‐misconduct issue (King 1996). Because economic hardships are not distributed equally across racial and ethnic groups in U.S. society (see Fukurai et al. 1993), resource barriers not only limit the size of the available pool of jurors but also potentially affect juries' representativeness.

To alleviate the hardship of jury service, one commonly proffered solution is a rise in juror pay. Indeed, prompted by many factors, including empirical data on attrition patterns, in 2006 the state of Texas increased juror pay from a statutory minimum of $6 a day to $40 per day, commencing after the first day of service (the first day, typically spent in jury selection, remained compensated at $6) 1 . In theory, if financial barriers impede service, higher pay should promote it and increase summons responsiveness.

Despite the intuitive appeal of this approach, there is remarkably little empirical data on the effect of juror pay on the response to a summons. Our paper addresses this gap by examining whether summons yield rates increased in several large‐sized counties in Texas following a state‐wide juror pay increase. We look at short‐term effects (a few years following the raise) as well as longer‐term to assess whether effects (if any) continued across time as inflation cut into the value of the increase. In the main, results fail to show a link between the stipend increase or its over‐time change in value and summons response behavior. We discuss the possible reasons for this outcome and implications of the findings.

Increasing Juror Pay in Texas

In the early 2000s, a close collaboration between the Dallas Morning News, Southern Methodist University Law School, and Dallas court officials produced data on summons response in Dallas, finding that, due to multiple sources of attrition, 13,612 summonses in a single month in 2000 yielded just 2214 jurors in Dallas County (16%; Curriden 2001; R. Walters and Curriden 2004; Eades 2001). The research team examined no‐shows in particular, randomly selecting 400 to be interviewed and given immunity from any prosecution for failure to respond; they compared this group to 400 people randomly selected from those who did appear, and examined the composition of both groups against the known demographics of the community (Eades 2001). Results showed large disparities between communities and the jury pool. Dallas jury pools underrepresented the young (ages 18–34, who were 37% of the county but just 8% of the pool); the poor, that is, those making less than $35,000 per year (40% of the county, 13% of the pool); and Hispanics, who were 23% of the county but just 9% of the pool. This latter result is notable because the absolute disparity (14%) between Hispanics in the jury pool versus in the community easily passed even the very conservative standards presented in the jurisprudence on Sixth Amendment fair cross section claims—the so‐called “10% rule” (Eades 2001; see generally Rose et al. 2018)—meaning that, in theory, the disparity could have been successfully litigated across criminal cases affected by the underrepresentation.

The Morning News/SMU study correlated disparities among the poor and among Hispanics specifically to resource constraints. Compared to other groups, low income and Hispanic no‐show respondents were more likely to say that they would receive no wage from their employer if they served: 44% and 33%, respectively; only one in five (20%) of Whites reported this (Eades 2001, 1816). Even among those who did appear, Hispanic and Black jurors were more likely to say they receive no wage: just under 20% for minorities compared to 5% of Whites. Hispanics in the no‐show group were also twice as likely as Whites to report that it would be difficult to take off work to appear.

A leader of the research effort, former Dallas Morning News journalist Mark Curriden, presented these data in Texas legal periodicals (R. Walters and Curriden 2004; R. C. Walters et al. 2005) to argue that the underrepresentation of Hispanics and the poor was the direct result of the very low juror pay provided—which in Dallas County was the minimum amount the state statute allowed, $6 per day, the lowest in the nation. Working with the prominent Texas law firm, Vinson and Elkins, Curriden was in a position to challenge the constitutionality of jury pools in particular cases (R. C. Walters et al. 2005).

Apart from the empirical data and the possible threat of mass challenges to criminal convictions, the Texas Legislature had other reasons to increase juror pay. For example, in 1999 El Paso County decided to use the discretion granted to it in Texas Law (Section 61.001, Government Code) to increase juror pay on its own, up to $40 a day. A judge in El Paso told a subcommittee of the Texas Judicial Council that the county saw a doubling of juror participation (from 23% to 46%) and that court leaders believed the pay increase contributed to this and to the realization of a more diverse jury pool (Subcommittee on Juries 2001). Despite the attribution to the pay increase, it bears mentioning that El Paso also contemporaneously made other changes, such as greater enforcement of summonses and public outreach, which could have also explained the uptick. Whatever the motivation, in the 2005 session, the 79th Texas Legislature approved a bill to more than sextuple juror pay, setting the new statewide minimum to $40 per day of trial service, beginning in January of 2006 2 .

Pay Increases and Jury Participation

The association between the underrepresentation of Hispanics/the poor in Dallas County and the financial hardships reported by these groups provide a reasonable basis for expecting a rise in juror pay to increase juror participation in Texas courts. The pay raise covers more of the costs of serving (e.g., parking, lunch, etc.); also, given a $5.15 minimum federal minimum wage level in 2005 that Texas mimicked, $40 per day came closer to matching a day's wage (since 2009, the minimum wage in Texas has been $7.25). Solely on the basis of rational choice theories of behavior, increasing the “reward” for serving on juries should increase participation.

Nonetheless, empirical findings raise questions about the proposed effects of raising pay on jury participation. From the same study discussed above, among those who appeared for service in Dallas County, about as many Black prospective jurors (17%) as Hispanics (19%) reported no compensation from their employers during jury service. A rational‐choice hypothesis suggests that those who do not receive compensation from employers are most likely to be swayed by increased juror pay as this would help to offset the loss in wages. Nonetheless, despite comparable proportions of Black and Hispanic citizens reporting no employer compensation, only Hispanics were significantly underrepresented in the jury pools—a pattern consistent with some other large counties in Texas (see Rose 2022; Rose and Brinkman 2008; but see Hannaford‐Agor, Moffett, Embry, et al. 2023). As others reviewing the Dallas study have noted (see Subcommittee on Juries 2001), a majority, about 60%, of no‐show jurors claimed that they did, in fact, receive employer compensation for service, suggesting pay concerns were not the primary basis for their behavior. Instead, the modal reason given for non‐appearance was personal or family illness; many others cited childcare constraints (Subcommittee on Juries 2001)—resource challenges that a pay raise does not address.

Research on jurors who appear for service also suggest that juror pay rates may not change participation. Contrary to the assumption that financial compensation is a primary driver, these studies highlight values and normative alignments that are common among jury participants. For example, among jurors who did appear in Dallas County, far more thought that reimbursement for parking would likely increase participation (84%) than an increase in juror pay (52%; Subcommittee on Juries 2001), suggesting that there is something symbolically or actually frustrating about having to pay out‐of‐pocket for parking, and covering this cost is seemingly valued more than a commensurate increase in pay. Further, in a report to the Wichita Bar Association on juror willingness, researchers found that the vast majority of jurors (77%) said that pay was a less important motivator than civic duty (Seamone 2002, 292 in footnote 6).

Broader normative standards also correlate with attitude and behavior regarding jury duty. In a survey, people who said they were more willing to serve on a jury were also more conforming to expected behavior in other realms; for example, they were less likely to say they went to bars and more likely to report that they adhered to recommended intervals for health check‐ups (i.e., seeing a physician once a year and visiting the dentist twice per year; Musick et al. 2015). Compared to the population at large, people selected for jury duty profiled as somewhat more likely to be “authority‐minded” and concerned about wrongdoing in the community (Rose et al. 2017). Taken together, it appears that jury participants hold norms and values that contribute to a felt‐obligation to serve, perspectives that are not likely to be altered by changes in juror pay.

Existing quasi‐experimental evidence on jury pay likewise suggests that the pay increase will not solve the problem of low juror yield. As part of a pilot study, jury pay in three Washington State counties was temporarily raised from $10 to $60 a day. Researchers compared jury summons compliance before and after the pay raise but found a decrease in compliance (Bloeser et al. 2012). Significantly, only a minority of people, both before and after the raise, were aware of what jurors were paid (2012, p. 187). This suggests that for jury pay to have an effect on yield, potential jurors need to be aware of the pay increase and, even then, no evidence suggests that higher pay increases yield. Using the same pilot study, but including the period after the rate returned to $10, court researchers saw no clear pattern across the three counties: One site behaved as economic and rational choice theory would predict (a rise in yield after the increase, and a decrease when pay returned to baseline); one decreased across all three time periods; and, strangely, one increased when pay returned to baseline but not when it was raised (Washington State Center for Court Research 2008). This heterogeneity in trends suggests that other factors were likely more influential over the study period than the change in rates.

Of note, previous studies have relied on relatively short study periods, which make it difficult to disentangle jury pay effects from prevailing trends in jury yield. The data from Washington State demonstrate substantial variability across time in summons response (Washington State Center for Court Research 2008) As the county‐level analyses indicated, yield rates fluctuated across the twelve‐month periods before, during, and after the pay increase. A stronger design would look over a longer period of time to better disentangle pay raise effects from year‐to‐year trends in yield rates. A longer time period also permits attention to inflationary effects, allowing observation of whether the impact of the pay raise decreases over time as the value of a dollar decreases.

Current Study

The current research tackles the dearth of data in this area and examines responses to jury summonses following changes in the monetary incentive. We asked counties to send us information on their summonsing and yield patterns in two different time periods, one of which included the period before the pay increase. We examine changes, if any, across time in the proportion of jurors available for service and the proportion who were “no‐shows” (also sometimes called “Failure to Appear” or FTA jurors; e.g., Eades 2001) both before and after the increase in juror pay. Our study examines whether any changes occur close in time to the increase in juror pay (i.e., after the 2006 implementation) compared to the pre‐reform period; we also have data from more recent years (2015–2019), which allow us to examine whether any increases in juror yield as a result of the pay raise diminish over time due to a decade of inflationary effects. Together, these analyses indicate whether summons response is sensitive to financial changes in the juror stipend amount.

Specifically, we test the following four hypotheses:

If prospective jurors are responsive to the increase in juror pay, the percentage of available jurors in the pool should increase following the 2006 raise compared to the pre‐reform period.

If prospective jurors are responsive to the increase in juror pay, the rate of no‐show's should decrease following the 2006 raise compared to the pre‐reform period.

As the value of the pay increase declines over time due to inflation, the proportion of available jurors should decrease in the later phase of the data (2015–2019), compared to patterns just after the 2006 raise.

As the value of the pay increase declines over time due to inflation, the proportion of no‐show jurors should increase in the later phase of the data (2015–2019), compared to patterns just after the 2006 raise.

Method

Sample

We drew samples at two time points. Our initial sample targeted the 10 most populous counties (population greater than 400,000) in Texas for participation. Larger, more urban areas have some of the highest no‐show rates (e.g., Taylor et al. 2007), making them ideal places for analyses. These areas included: Bexar (San Antonio), Collin (in the Dallas metro area), Dallas, Denton, El Paso, Fort Bend (just outside the Houston metro area), Harris (Houston), Hidalgo (Harlingen/Edinburg), Tarrant (Fort Worth), and Travis (Austin) counties. Counties first heard a request to participate in the study from the Travis County District Clerk at a statewide meeting of jury administrators in 2009, and they also received an invitation to participate; we also followed up via phone and email.

Two counties did not respond (Bexar and Dallas), and features of the data from four other counties (Collin, El Paso, Hidalgo, and Tarrant) made it difficult to use the data either in part (n = 2) or in whole (n = 2). Specifically: Hidalgo County used a two‐stage summons process (a qualification questionnaire is sent, then a summons is sent only to those who responded at the first stage), making response results from the second step incomparable with other counties that used a one‐step system. El Paso experienced stark changes in the numbers reported in their exempt category, suggesting year‐to‐year changes in data collection methods, and we were unable to get a clear explanation for the variability. Consequently, we excluded both Hidalgo and El Paso entirely. Collin County moved to a new jury management system in 2006, meaning that it changed how it calculated and reported data at exactly the time that the pay raise took effect, making the pre‐raise period non‐comparable to the post‐raise period. Tarrant County also changed its reporting and record‐keeping system shortly after the pay raise (2008). We use Collin and Tarrant Counties in sections where their data are relevant, but in the main, we focus on the data from Denton, Fort Bend, Harris, and Travis counties, which, together compose 25% of the state population (per the Census Bureau's American Community Survey).

To supplement these data, we followed up with participating counties in 2020 and requested the five most recent years of summons response data, beginning with 2015. (Because COVID radically transformed how courts operated and whether there would be jury trials, we did not request data from 2020.) All original counties provided usable data, except we do not have follow‐up data for Tarrant County. Our aim with this outreach was to see how and whether summons response behaviors had changed as the pay amount decreased in value. Per the Consumer Price Index (CPI), the initial $40 amount decreased to $33 (in 2006 dollars) by 2015 and to $31.51 by the end of our observation period in 2019. Although we do not expect people to be sensitive to small changes across a single year, the follow‐up data offer an indication of whether there is a detectable drop‐off in the proportion of available jurors, or an increase in no‐show rates, when juror pay is roughly 79%–85% of its original value.

Variables

We asked jury administrators in each county to provide the following information for each year beginning in 2002, running through 2009 or 2010 (depending on when they were contacted), and then again for 2015 through 2019: Total summons sent; total returned as undeliverable; total resulting in disqualification; total resulting in exemptions/excusals; total non‐responses; total available for service. Not all counties were able to provide data that reflected exactly these categories, but for all we were able to directly report or calculate two dependent variables: yearly changes in the percent available for service and the percent who were no‐shows.

Of note, undeliverable rates differed substantially across areas and time. For example, in the first wave of data collection, Travis Country reported an average undeliverable rate of 32% (range: 24%–38%), likely reflecting the large and frequently‐migrating youth population associated with the public university in that county. Fort Bend, Denton, and Harris produced average undeliverable rates of 10%, 17%, and 21% respectively. Undeliverable rates could be affected by some of the same factors that shape juror responses to a summons, particularly differences in how much effort a county makes to locate jurors and keep address lists up to date, as well as population differences in the burdens associated with service which may make people more prone to relocation and address changes. As several jury scholars have noted, a non‐trivial proportion of “no shows” are actually undeliverables that were not returned to the court (e.g., Boatright 1998; Seltzer 1999; Hannaford‐Agor, Moffett, Embry, et al. 2023). We therefore ran our analysis with undeliverables included and excluded from the denominator and observed little change in the pattern of overall results. Where the information was available to us, we netted out undeliverables in the denominator when calculating the proportion available and the proportion of non‐response.

Data Analytic Strategy

We sought to identify patterns in the data before and after the reform to juror pay in multiple ways. First, we ran t‐tests on the pre‐reform period (2002–2005) compared to the years immediately following the reform (2006–2010) for each variable; this would indicate only if any significant increase or decrease occurred, not whether the change in pay is correlated with that change. Summons response could change across time for reasons other than the pay increase, or it could have been increasing before the reform took place.

To look for associations with the pay increase, we use both descriptive and analytic tools. Descriptively, we charted patterns for each county across time to identify whether data are consistent with the hypotheses. Additionally, we used an interrupted time‐series analyses (McDowell et al. 1980). This regression‐based approach indicates if there is a significant change in values across time (e.g., if the slope of a given line differs from zero), as well as whether the average value of the dependent variable in the post‐reform period significantly differs from that for the pre‐reform period; further, by adding in an interaction term between the pre/post dummy variable and time, we can test the possibility that there is a discontinuity or “interruption” in the slope values that corresponds to the introduction of juror pay. This allows us to see if the patterns in the data changed with the introduction of the new policy, using either the short‐term or the longer‐term dataset. We also look at only the post‐reform period to see if we can detect a significantly decreasing slope for available jurors and a significantly increasing slope for nonresponse to test for effects due to inflationary pressures.

Although these initial mean results support the hypothesis that a pay raise increased the proportion of available jurors and decreased no‐shows, the next set of analyses examine year‐over‐year in more detail to test whether this change can be reliably linked to the pay change.

Change Over Time

Figure 1 plots the proportion of available jurors across the years 2002–2009 (for Harris and Denton) and 2002 to 2010 (Travis and Fort Bend). As a simple descriptive matter, the trend lines for each county offer little support for the notion that the year 2006 marked a substantial departure in summons‐response behavior. In all counties, especially Travis, the percentage of available jurors had been trending upward prior to the pay increase. If the increase in juror pay was associated with a commensurate increase in juror participation, we would have expected to see a steepening of the trends beginning in 2006, the year the pay increase went into effect. Instead, county level trends appear comparable before and after the pay increase, providing no evidence in favor of a relationship between juror pay and jury participation.

Longer‐Term Patterns

The final way we assessed the potential pay increase effect was to add data from 2015 to 2019 to the dataset for the counties we described above. (These analyses include Collin County for analysis of trends since 2006.) Our aim was to assess whether inflationary pressures make the $40 per‐day amount less attractive roughly 10 years after it went into effect. This is plausible given that, as noted, the original 2006 pay amount had a value of just over $30 by 2019. As Figure 3 (Available Jurors) demonstrates, all lines suggest a variable, but mostly flat, pattern. Data from Collin County suggest some lessened response over time, but by 2018 and 2019, all counties had rates of available jurors that mirrored those from 10 years prior. A similar pattern appears in Figure 4 (No‐Shows), with some increases across time but recent years stabilizing back down to prior rates. Thus, descriptively, we saw no consistent sensitivity to inflationary pressures in these data.

Results for the time series regression analysis again confirmed our descriptive analysis above. Just examining the post‐reform time period on its own (i.e., removing pre‐reform years), the slope term for time in the analysis of available jurors did not differ from zero, indicating a flat line in the data (t = 0.‐65, p = 0.54). Additionally, using all the years of data did not produce a significant effect for the pay reform (p = 0.38); the interaction term was negative (coefficient = −1.63), pointing to a pattern of decreasing availability, contrary to the hypothesis. Likewise, the analysis of no‐show rates failed to confirm hypotheses: there was neither responsiveness to inflation after the reform (i.e., the slope for time was non‐significant in the post‐reform phase; t = 0.73, p = 0.50), nor a significant pay‐effect in the whole dataset (t = −0.48, p = 0.63; interaction term was non‐significant, t = 0.80, p = 0.43).

Taken together, these regression results find little evidence to support the notion that the juror pay increase positively affected the pool of available jurors in the long term nor significantly reduced no‐show rates. These findings, along with the lack of evidence of short‐term trends described above, falsified all four hypotheses tested, and instead support the claim that the juror pay increase failed to have the intended effect of increasing the pool of people available to serve as jurors and deterring nonresponse.

Discussion

The clear news from the current study is that, across multiple counties in Texas, the proportion of jurors available for service and counties' no‐show rates were quite variable across time. However, a substantial pay increase in 2006 offered little explanation for the variability in either of these rates. In most counties, improvements in the proportion of available jurors and the rate of no‐show's began to emerge ahead of the pay raise. Overall, there was also little indication that the inflationary decrease in the value of the stipend meant that fewer people came to jury duty. Thus, our results offer a decidedly unimpressive picture of the effects of an increase in juror pay, even though pay increased six‐fold.

Although before/after t‐tests suggested some improvements in rates of available jurors and decreases in no‐show rates, this is primarily attributable to positive trends that preceded the pay increase. For example, in Travis County, both the percentage of available jurors and no‐show rates began to improve dramatically in 2003, well before the pay increase. As has been described elsewhere (Rose and Brinkman 2008), in this time period Travis County initiated a system for qualifying and empanelling jurors (i.e., assigning them to specific courtrooms) via the internet, a system dubbed “iJury.” The vast majority of jurors make use of this system, rather than taking a day from work to participate in a separate qualification/empaneling session (for details, see Rose and Brinkman 2008). The iJury system in Travis County is precisely the type of innovation that addresses jurors' strong desire for increased convenience and better management of their time (see Subcommittee on Juries 2001; Hannaford‐Agor, Moffett, Embry, et al. 2023). As the data show here, changes in the rates of available jurors and no‐show's were largely flat in the years following the pay raise; by contrast, in the three years after the launch of iJury in March of 2002 (Rose and Brinkman 2008), non‐response plummeted and availability increased solidly.

Outside of Texas, other methods for increasing jury yield have seen some success. For example, sending reminders to summoned jurors appears to improve response rates (Bowler et al. 2014). Rather than focusing on paying people more to serve, reminders address attrition attributable to busy people losing track of jury summons. This study thus joins published research from Washington State (Bloeser et al. 2012) and other unpublished commission reports (e.g., Subcommittee on Juries 2001) in offering little support for the notion that an increase in pay for all persons summoned will move the needle far in terms of increases in yield.

These data are not well‐suited to answer some other important questions. It is possible, for example, that some sub‐populations (e.g., the poor, some minority groups) are responsive to pay increase, but such effects were not large enough to emerge in the overall data. Lacking any information about the racial/ethnic profile of the jury pool, we could not disentangle the data in this way. Second, we were able to study only the pay increase in Texas as it was implemented. The amount of the increase simply may not have been large enough to activate rational choice in this setting.

Despite such limitations, we see other reasons to urge reformers to think carefully about blanket juror pay increases. Most basically, because it requires legislative action, juror pay is remarkably difficult to increase. In Texas, the large 2006 increase occurred only against a backdrop of several justiciable fair cross‐section challenges (R. C. Walters et al. 2005). Further, the appropriate cost‐of‐living‐adjustment and targets for pay are not straightforward (see Seamone 2002). For example, over four decades ago, Van Dyke (1977) argued that a fair wage for a juror should be $50 a day, a daily rate pegged to the median income at the time. In today's dollars, courts would have to pay jurors over $250 a day to keep up with the inflationary value of that $50 amount, an entirely unrealistic amount politically. Even thinking about juror pay in terms of current median income (which has been fairly flat the last several decades; see Guzman and Kollar 2023), only a small handful of states today exceed the rate proposed in the 1970s, indicating that few areas are keeping up with any increases in median wage (National Center for State Courts 2023).

Rather than a blanket increase for all jurors, we believe counties should consider, as San Francisco has done, ways to fairly compensate people for lengthy trials and most importantly, to consider targeting more substantial increases to the people who need it most (see Lau and Stuhldreher 2022). In Arizona, higher per diem compensation levels are focused on people who do not have income support from their workplace (see Superior Court in Maricopa County 2024). Although, as noted, we could not disentangle data to examine pay effects for poorer individuals, our results make it clear that non‐targeted, wholesale increases in juror pay are not a particularly fruitful vehicle for the goal of increasing overall juror yield and summons‐response rates.

To be clear: even as we do not see that raising juror pay significantly changes summons response and yield rates, we also do not view low juror‐stipend amounts as having no cost. Stipends that are particularly low risk being perceived as offensive, an insult that someone may consider, for example, $10 a day to be sufficient to cover costs associated with service, such as gas or public transportation, parking, and food. Juror pay should always cover reasonable costs to serve so that jurors do not have to pay out of pocket to appear. Further, if future studies find that newer, emerging programs that target low‐income persons or that offset losses to people who receive no salary for service do increase responses, then all jurisdictions should consider these more targeted increases. Our message from this study is that, for purposes of increasing the size of the juror pool, the stipend appears less important than other potential innovations designed make the process of showing up and serving easier for jurors.
