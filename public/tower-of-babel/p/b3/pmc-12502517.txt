BrCER1 intron mutation causing a wax deficient phenotype in Chinese cabbage
Authors: Luyao Zhang, Zifan Zhao, Chong Tan, Yike Zhang, Shuang Zhang, Zhiyong Liu, Jie Ren, Xueling Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCuticular wax is one of the most important substances that cover the aerial parts of most plants and protect them from biotic and abiotic stresses. Plants lacking wax powder on their surface are more popular in terms of appearance and quality. Although most wax deficiency mutants are of high commercial value, most genetic and biochemical studies have been conducted in Arabidopsis, and only a few studies have been conducted in Chinese cabbage (Brassica rapa L. ssp. pekinensis).ResultsIn the present study, we described a glossy leaf mutant from the ethyl methanesulfonate mutagenesis population of Chinese cabbage doubled haploid line ‘FT’. In the M5075 mutant leaves, wax crystals were completely absent and leaf cuticular permeability was increased. Genetic analysis indicated that the glossy phenotype of M5075 is controlled by a single recessive gene. MutMap detected the responsible gene BrCER1 in the 4.76 Mb region on chromosome A09. BrCER1 shares homology with AtCER1 encoding a key enzyme responsible for biosynthesis of cuticular wax. Sequencing revealed the presence of only one nucleotide substitution at the 4th exon boundary, leading to a alternative splicing of BrCER1 in M5075. Our results showed that the mutation of BrCER1 would severely reduce wax crystals, thereby conferring a glossy phenotype on M5075.ConclusionThis study describes the known functions and molecular characterization of Chinese cabbage cuticular wax biosynthesis to elucidate its potential use in crop improvement. It not only provides important information for the functional study of BrCER1, but also provides a basis for the study of Chinese cabbage wax synthesis pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07387-9.Keywords: Chinese cabbage, Cuticular wax, Glossy leaf, Gene mapping, BrCER1

Background

Cuticular wax is a complex mixture of hydrophobic compounds that coat the outer surfaces of most plants, including the aerial parts of leaves, stems, and flowers. This wax layer serves as a vital barrier, protecting plants from a range of biotic and abiotic stressors, including water loss, pathogen invasion, and environmental extremes such as cold, drought, and UV radiation, and is the plant’s first line of defense against environmental threats [1–4]. The cuticle of plant leaves plays a key role in maintaining the integrity of the plant, and its outer surface is covered with a hydrophobic layer composed mainly of long-chain aliphatic molecules collectively known as cuticular waxes [5]. The major constituents of cuticular wax are VLCFAs and their derivatives, including alkanes, aldehydes, ketones, primary and secondary alcohols, and esters, which are mainly involved in the synthesis of seed glycerides, biofilm lipids and sphingolipids, and provide precursors for the biosynthesis of cuticular waxes [6–8]. This layer is important in determining a plant’s survival under harsh conditions due to the composition and structure that constitute the layer. Thus, wax biosynthesis is one of the major processes that have become very significant in the research of plant biology, especially the genetic and biochemical regulation of wax biosynthesis.

Cuticular wax plays an important role in the plant’s resistance to environmental stresses. One of the most striking features of Brassica species is the glaucous appearance of their leaves, imparted by epicuticular wax crystals on the leaf surface. These crystals impart a silvery or bluish color to the plant, a feature that has helped in its protection as well as increasing its beauty [1]. In mutants devoid of these crystals of wax, the leaves possess a shiny green color, which has now become a trait of commercial importance. In such mutants, the lack of wax crystals would give a soft and smooth leaf surface. This is an advantage in some markets for better texture and appearance, and it has gained interest in improving the quality for consumers [9].While the glossy mutants have some advantages commercially, this loss of cuticular wax crystals is costly for the plant in terms of protective capabilities. The cuticular wax acts as a barrier against environmental stressors. Without it, the plant becomes more susceptible to water loss, pathogen invasion, and physical damage [5, 10]. The ability to manipulate the presence or absence of these wax crystals is both a challenge and an opportunity in agricultural breeding. While the glossy trait is commercially advantageous for specific applications, the genetic mechanisms need to be understood to optimize the trade-offs between enhanced visual appeal and reduced stress tolerance. Thus, breeding for the control of the amount of cuticular wax and its crystal structure is important to improve commercial value and environmental toughness of Brassica species.

In Brassica species, the glossy leaf trait has been studied in mutants of Chinese cabbage, cai-tai, non-heading Chinese cabbage, and cabbage [9]. Most of the mutants exhibited reduced or a complete loss of epicuticular wax crystals, contributing to the appearance of the glossy phenotype. Genetic studies on such mutants demonstrated that the character of glossiness was generally controlled by a single recessive gene, and few genes controlling wax biosynthesis accounted for this trait [11]. The mutation of BrKCS4 leads to a reduced total wax content and inhibited the activities of FAE complex, the alkane synthesis and the alcohol synthesis in wdm9 mutant [12]. An SNP of BrBCAT1 confers a glossy phenotype and affects the extension of the fatty acid chain and the formation of alkanes and wax esters of wdm11 mutant in Chinese cabbage [13]. Loss of function of BnA1.CER4 and BnC1.CER4 increases the branched aliphatic wax compounds levels and produces disordered wax crystals of ngl2 mutant in rapeseed [14]. Two wax deficient allelic mutants, wdm4 and wdm8, were in Chinese cabbage, and BrWAX1 (BraA01g015290.3 C) was regarded as the candidate gene through MutMap [15].

ECERIFERUM1 (CER1), encoding a fatty acyl-CoA reductase enzyme involved in VLCFAs synthesis, is involved in the production of stem epicuticular wax. VLCFAs are key precursors for cuticular wax formation, and modifications in their biosynthesis may have profound effects on wax composition and structure. CER1 has been studied almost exclusively for wax biosynthesis in Arabidopsis, it is known to affect the synthesis of both primary and secondary alcohols within the wax mixture [1]. CER1 and CER3 share a common role in the alkane synthesis pathway in Arabidopsis. CER3 encodes a VLCFA reductase that catalyzes the formation of aliphatic aldehydes, whereas CER1 acts as an acyl decarbonylase that catalyzes the formation of alkanes from aliphatic aldehydes [16, 17]. Similar genes have been implicated in wax biosynthesis in Brassica species through study of glossy leaf mutants. These include the FAE complex genes, KCS1, KCS2, KCS6, KCS9, KCS20, responsible for VLCFA elongation, and the genes, such as CER4, MAH1, CER3, and CER2, concerned with VLCFAs modification [1, 2]. Additionally, other transporter genes like DESPERADO/AtWBC11, ABCG transporters, and regulatory genes such as WIN1/SHN1, MYB30, and CFL1 are also involved in wax deposition and overall wax barrier development [18]. BnWIN1 bound directly to the promoters of genes participating in lipid anabolism and regulated wax synthesis accumulation [19]. BnUC1 affected epidermal wax synthesis in B. napus by modulating the expression of genes associated with VLCFA biosynthesis, such as KCS [20].

In this study, we focused on a newly identified glossy mutant in Chinese cabbage named M5075, which carries a severe reduction in wax crystals on the surface of leaves. Using MutMap, we positioned the candidate gene within a 4.76 Mb region on chromosome A09. Subsequent gene prediction and sequencing analysis identified BrCER1 as a very strong candidate gene for the wax deficiency phenotype of M5075. BrCER1 encodes a fatty acyl-CoA reductase, orthologous to CER1 in Arabidopsis, and is required for the synthesis of VLCFAs destined for wax production. This finding adds a new dimension to our knowledge about genetic control over wax formation in Brassica species and may imply the commercial value and improvement in stress tolerance of crops.

Methods

Plant materials

The chinese cabbage doubled haploid (DH) line ‘FT’ was derived from the ‘Futian 50’ cultivar, and the germinated seeds of ‘FT’ were treated with 0.8% ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS) for mutagenesis. Compared to the ‘FT’ wild type, the wax powder deficiency mutant identified in this study exhibited significantly reduced wax crystal deposition, particularly noticeable during bolting and flowering stages. No other phenotypic differences were observed between wild-type and mutant plants. All plants were cultivated in the greenhouses at Shenyang Agricultural University. Seeds were germinated on filter paper in late August, then subjected to 15-day vernalization at 4 °C. After vernalization, the seeds were transplanted into plug trays and grown in the greenhouse. Seedlings were transferred to nutrient pots in the greenhouse 25 days after sowing.

Cryo-SEM analysis

Utilizing cryo-scanning electron microscopy, the epidermal wax morphology of both ‘FT’ and M5075 mutants was examined. Slices of fresh leaves, measuring 1 mm by 3 mm, were attached to the sample disk. Following inserting the sample disk into the transmission device, it was precooled by submerging it in liquid nitrogen. And after the vacuum was pumped, the sample disk was moved to the preparation chamber where the transmission device was attached. Before viewing, sublimate the water in the sample and mist it with gold [12].

Stratum corneum permeability measurement

Whole leaves of the same size were removed from the same location on the wild-type ‘FT’ and mutant M5075, with three replicates. The initial weight was recorded and timing started at the time of leaf removal. The leaves were placed at room temperature (25℃) and weighed every 20 min. After 180 min, the leaves were wrapped in aluminum foil and placed in a 65℃ oven until their weight no longer changed, at which point they were weighed again. The water loss rate was calculated and the water loss curve was plotted as previously described [13].

Candidate gene identification by the mutmap method

A total of 50 plants with wax powder deficiency mutant were selected from the F2 generation, and equal amounts of DNA were extracted and pooled. DNA was extracted from the two parental wild-type ‘FT’ and M5075 mutant using a modified CTAB method, and the DNA samples were sonicated to fragment them for library construction. Sequencing was performed using the IlluminaHiSeq sequencing platform. After obtaining high-quality data, the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) sequence alignment tool was used to precisely align the sequenced results with the reference genome sequence from the Chinese cabbage genome (Brara_Chiifu_V3.5). MutMap method was performed as described previously [21].

SNP genotyping by KASP

KASP was used to detect the co-segregation of SNPs identified using MutMap and to confirm the candidate gene of M5075.A total of 50 F2 plants with wax powder deficiency phenotypes were selected for SNP genotyping. The experiment was conducted as previously described [22]. The primers are listed in Table S1.

Cloning candidate gene in M5075

DNA and RNA were extracted from leaves of wild-type‘FT’and mutant M5075 plants using a modified CTAB method and an RNA extraction kit (Coolaber, China). The extracted total RNA was reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (TIANGEN, China). Specific primers were designed based on the reference genome of Brara_Chiifu_V3.5 to amplify the sequences of BrCER1 (Table S1), and the sequences were aligned using DNAMAN software.

Bioinformatic analysis of BrCER1

The three-dimensional structure was constructed using partial amino acid sequences of BrCER1 using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). The Brassica database BLAST was used to obtain the amino acid sequence of the BrCER1 homologs. For sequence alignment, we uploaded it to DNAMAN V6 (Lynnon BioSoft, Canada).

RNA isolation and relative expression analysis

Through the use of quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), the expression of candidate gene was found in the root, stem, leaf, flower, bud, and silique portions. Total RNA was isolated from ‘FT’ and M5075 using an RNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China). First-strand cDNA was created using FastQuant RT Super Mix (TIANGEN, China). The design of specific primers and qRT-PCR were carried out in accordance with other reports [23].

RNA-Seq

Total mRNA was extracted from ‘FT’ and M5075 using TRIzol (Invitrogen, USA) with three biological replicates. The cDNA Libraries were quality-checked and then sequenced using Illumina Novaseq 6000. Raw data were filtered to remove adaptors and low-quality sequences, and the obtained cleans were aligned to the Brassica reference genome using Hisat2. Transcripts were constructed using StringTi for transcript and gene quantification. DESeq2 was used to identify DEGs, using |log2FC| ≥ 1 and P-value < 0.05 as thresholds. Subsequently, DEGs were mapped to the GO (http://geneontology.org) and KEGG databases (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg) to perform GO enrichment and KEGG enrichment analyses.

Results

Wax is significantly reduced in M5075

Through EMS mutagenesis of the ‘FT’ line, we isolated a mutant plant M5075 that displayed a visually distinct phenotype compared to the wild-type ‘FT’. The wild-type ‘FT’ exhibited a white appearance, with a white powdery substance covering its entire surface, including leaves, stems, buds, and appendages (Fig. 1A). In contrast, the surfaces of these organs in mutant M5075 were glossy and lacked wax coverage (Fig. 1A). Additionally, other phenotypic traits remained unaffected by the mutation.

We then used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine the abundance and shape of wax crystals on the leaf surfaces of the wild-type ‘FT’ and the mutant M5075. We found that the leaves of ‘FT’ were covered with dense rod-shaped wax crystals on both sides, while the number of wax crystals on the surface and back of the leaves of mutant M5075 was significantly reduced, and the existing wax crystals tended to be rod-shaped (Fig. 1B). These results indicated that the reduction in wax crystals on the surface of M5075 led to its glossy phenotype.

Wax deficiency increases the permeability of the stratum corneum of M5075

The wax layer covering the surface of plant leaves can influence the permeability of the cuticle. To investigate whether the wax powder deficiency mutant of Chinese cabbage affects leaf water retention capacity, we measured the water loss rates of the wild-type ‘FT’ and the M5075 mutant at room temperature. The results revealed that the water loss rate of M5075 mutant was higher than that of the wild-type ‘FT’ from the beginning. After 1 h of treatment, this difference became increasingly significant over time (Fig. 1C). Therefore, we concluded that the reduced wax content in the epidermis of the M5075 mutant increased leaf permeability, leading to an increase in water loss rate.

Genetic analysis of M5075

To analyze genetic characteristics, we crossed the wild-type ‘FT’ with the wax powder deficiency mutant M5075 in both directions and found that all plants in the F1 population had wax powder. In the BC1 (F1×‘FT’) population, all plants exhibited phenotypes consistent with the wild-type, while in the BC1 (F1×M5075) population, the wax powder phenotype showed segregation with a ratio of 1.05:1 (χ² = 0.03 < χ²0.05 = 3.84). In the 2,834 F2 population, there were 2,130 plants with wax powder and 704 plants without wax powder, with a segregation ratio of 3.03:1 (χ² = 0.24 < χ²0.05 = 3.84), consistent with the Mendelian segregation ratio (Table 1). Thus, we concluded that the epidermal wax powder deficiency phenotype is controlled by a single recessive gene.

Genetic analysis of M5075

Mapping of the candidate gene of M5075

We used a modified MutMap method to separate candidate genes. We performed resequencing of two parental DNA pools, ‘FT’ and M5075, as well as a bulked pool composed of wax powder deficiency plants. After filtering, a total of 176,452,962 reads for ‘FT’, 149,082,476 reads for M5075 and 279,320,418 reads for the B5075 were obtained; 95.53%, 96.99%, and 96.85% of them, respectively, were mapped to the Brassica rapa genome, and 2,429,579 SNPs were selected. We used the 95th percentile of the SNP-index of all SNPs as the threshold and identified a candidate region of 4.76 Mb (40,400,000–45,156,810 bp) on chromosome A09. Within this candidate region, there were two SNPs with an SNP-index of 1 (SNPs A09:44921553 and A09:45148778) (Fig. 1D; Table S2). We found that BraA09g067710.3.5 C is located in the exon region and is an SNP causing a synonymous mutation, while BraA09g068200.3.5 C is located in the region corresponding to alternative splicing (AS).

To identify the candidate SNP, we performed KASP genotyping using 50 F2 individuals without wax powder. Only SNP A09:45148778 co-segregated with the mutant phenotype of F2 individuals (Table S3). Therefore, BraA09g068200.3.5 C is considered a candidate gene, named BrCER1 for ‘FT’ and Brcer1 for M5075.

Sequence analysis and protein structure analysis

BrCER1 is 4,563 bp in length, with a CDS of 1,887 bp, comprising 10 exons and 9 introns (Fig. 2A). We cloned the full-length DNA sequence of BrCER1 from ‘FT’ and M5075, respectively, and found a nucleotide substitution at the edge of the fourth exon, within the third intron (Fig. S1). This substitution occurs within the critical splicing signal sequence of the intron, altering the sequence characteristics of these recognition sites. To prove our conjecture, we cloned the CDS sequence of BrCER1 from the cDNA of M5075. Base on Sanger sequencing results, four distinct splice variants were identified in the M5075 mutant (Fig. 2A, B).

BrCER1 had 628 amino acids, with a molecular weight of 72.85 kDa and a theoretical pI of 8.42. BrCER1 protein has two conserved structural domains, the Fatty acid hydroxylase domain (129–270 aa) and the WAX2 C-terminal domain (446–618 aa) (Fig. 2C). The mutation site of BrCER1 is located in the fatty acid hydroxylase structural domain (Fig. 2D). Compared to ‘FT’, the protein sequence and 3D structure of Brcer1 was changed, due to variable splicing, resulting in the formation of four different structures (Fig. 2E).

Analysis of BrCER1 expression patterns

To investigate the expression pattern of BrCER1, we performed qRT-PCR analysis on the root, stem, leaf, flower, bud, and silique of ‘FT’. The result showed that BrCER1 was most highly expressed in flower, followed by bud and leaf, and was almost undetectable in root (Fig. 3). This expression pattern aligns with the typical high expression of CER1 family members in aboveground plant organs, consistent with their biological function in epidermal wax synthesis.

RNA-seq of ‘FT’ and M5075

To further clarify the function of BrCER1, we performed RNA-seq analysis using the leaves of ‘FT’ and M5075. A total of 4813 different expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between wild-type ‘FT’ and M5075 plants. Compared to ‘FT’, M5075 contained 2484 upregulated genes and 2329 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A, B). GO enrichment analysis showed that most of the DEGs were enriched in ‘cellular component’ and concentrated on ribosomal structural components (Fig. 4C; Table S4). Among them, GO:0043603 (cellular amide metabolic process), GO:0043604 (ribonucleoprotein complex), and GO:0043043 (peptide metabolic process) may be involved in the wax synthesis. KEGG enrichment analysis showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in map00940 (Phenylpropanoid biosynthesis) (Fig. 4D; Table S5).

To further verify the reliability of the RNA-seq data, we identified eight key candidate genes involved in wax synthesis among the DEGs (Fig. 4E). We performed RT-qPCR analysis of these eight DEGs, and the results showed that the differential expression trends of these genes were consistent with the RNA-seq, verifying the accuracy of the data (Fig. 5). However, the expression of BrCER1 in ‘FT’ was higher than that of M5075, although there was no significant difference (Fig. S2). Discussion

In this study, we reported the identification of BrCER1 as the candidate gene responsible for the wax deficiency phenotype in the M5075 mutant of Chinese cabbage. The M5075 mutant has a glossy green appearance resulting from a significantly reduced amount of cuticular wax compared with the wild-type ‘FT’. Our results indicated that BrCER1, a gene responsible for cuticular wax biosynthesis, has a crucial role in this phenotype. Identification of BrCER1 further elucidates the molecular mechanism of wax biosynthesis in Brassica species and may contribute to a key target for crop improvement in terms of commercial quality and stress resistance.

CER1 encodes an essential enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of VLC alkanes. The biological function of CER1 has been well characterized in Arabidopsis, where it is expressed predominantly in the epidermis of the aerial organs, suggesting that its primary function is closely related to the synthesis of epidermal waxes [24]. In Chinese cabbage, wax powder deficiency caused by a single SNP of BrCER1 has also been reported before [11]. In this study, we identified a Chinese cabbage wax deficiency mutant and localized the candidate gene BrCER1 by MutMap. Mutations in Brcer1 resulted in a decrease in the number of epidermal wax crystals and increased cuticle permeability in mutant leaves, leading to increased water loss. Overexpression of CER1 increases the number of alkanes at odd numbers of carbon atoms but has only a minor modifying effect on the other wax components [24]. CER1 and CER3 functionally interact to form an enzyme complex leading to the synthesis of VLC alkanes [25]. In Arabidopsis, CER1 has been shown to be expressed only in aliphatic organs and reproductive organs [26], and CER1 is highly expressed in floral organs because it participates in the synthesis of cuticular wax in floral organs at various stages [24, 27]. The results of the expression pattern analysis of BrCER1 in this study indicated that its expression level is highest in floral organs, consistent with the findings in Arabidopsis (Fig. 3). CER1 has also been shown to participate in the biosynthesis of epidermal wax alkanes and to play a very important role in crops such as wheat [28], rice [29], cabbage [30], cucumber [31], and citrus [32]. Plant epidermal waxes are associated with VLCFA derivatives and formed a barrier that effectively prevents water loss [33]. Overexpression of MdCER1-1 reduced water loss and increased alkane biosynthesis in apple healing tissue [34]. It has also been shown in Arabidopsis [24, 35] and other cash crops [28, 36]that CER1 is able to enhance plant tolerance by increasing wax alkane content under extreme stress.

The spliceosome is a giant complex consisting of five small ribonucleoproteins, U1, U2, U4, U5 and U6snRNPs, and more than one hundred proteins [37, 38]. Recognition of different splice sites by the spliceosome leads to AS. AS is the process by which a gene generates multiple mRNA variants by removing introns and retaining different combinations of exons of the pre-mRNA, encompassing five types, and AS of the pre-mRNAs can dramatically increase the diversity of proteins and complexity of gene expression [39–41]. In plants, the occurrence of AS affects plant adaptation to the environment [42]. AS regulates mineral nutrient homeostasis in rice [43], and cassava affects the quantity and quality of gene expression through AS under cold stress [44].The regulation of AS of pre-mRNAs by RNA-binding proteins is an important pathway for plant adaptation to abiotic stress [45]. Some genes undergo short truncation mutations due to AS and lose functional structural domains, while some AS does not change the length of the gene but still results in premature termination of protein translation and truncated proteins, leading to the deletion of some key structural domains. CcbHLH1 lose structural domains due to truncation as a result of selective splicing, which ultimately led to the failure of anthocyanins to be synthesized normally [46]. The two types of spliceosomes formed by AS, full-length and truncated, produce two protein isoforms that play completely opposite roles in the regulation of flowering in Brassica juncea [47]. A splice variant, BrrWSD1-2X, was found in waxy varieties of radish, resulting in a much higher ability to synthesize wax than in waxy-free varieties [48]. In this study, BrCER1 produced AS as a result of recognition site mutations, generating four different spliceosomes, two of which exhibited amino acid insertions and deletions, and the other two were short truncation mutations, which caused the truncation of the fatty acid hydroxylase domain and the loss of the WAX2 C-terminal domain. But the molecular mechanism of how they regulate the wax powder deficiency in Chinese cabbage is still unclear, and still needs to be further explored and investigated.

Plant epidermal wax biosynthesis is a complex process mediated by multiple enzymes. Mutations in Brcer1 also affected other enzymes in the alkane synthesis pathway. The transcriptome results showed that the expression of genes involved in the extension of the VLCFA, such as CER1-LIKE2, CER3, KCR1, and KCS2, were up-regulated, in addition to FAR1-2 (Fig. 5), which we also verified by qRT- PCR results. The key enzyme genes involved in the synthesis of ultra-long-chain fatty acids, KCR1, KCS2, PAS2, and CER1-LIKE2, encode enzymes that catalyze the lengthening of the carbon chains of ultra-long-chain fatty acids and provide the basic substrates for wax synthesis [26, 49–51]. CER3, a key gene regulating the decarbonylation pathway (alkane synthesis pathway), encodes a protein that is directly involved in the construction of wax components by catalyzing the decarbonylation of aldehydes to form alkanes [52]. FAR1 and FAR4, which affect the acyl reduction pathway (alcohol synthesis pathway), optimize the ratio of wax components by regulating the reduction of fatty acids to fatty alcohols, which affects the physical properties of wax powder [53, 54]. In other studies, it has also been found that deletion of CER1 caused up-regulation of the expression of genes related to wax synthesis and transport [55]. In rapeseed, the expression of CER1 was much higher in plants with a high content of wax coverage on the leaf surface than in those with a smooth leaf surface, and transcriptome analysis revealed that the expression of other genes related to wax synthesis, such as CER3, CER4, KCS2, and KCR1, was down-regulated in LWC plants [56]. Under well-watered conditions, the expression of genes related to wax synthesis, CER2, MAH1, etc., was reduced in wax powder deficiency mutants [57]. Expression of KCS1, KCR1 and CER3 was lower in broccoli without wax powder than in plants with wax powder [49]. qRT-PCR showed that among the differentially expressed genes involved in the extension of the long-chain fatty acid and alkane formation pathways, only CER1 was significantly down-regulated in the CGL-3 mutant, whereas there was no significant difference in the expression of ACE1, KCS1, and ACBP1 [58]. Expression of WAX2, FAR1, and WSD1, which are involved in wax synthesis, was down-regulated in barley glossy mutants [59]. Our RNA-seq and qRT-PCR results showed that most genes encoded enzymes that catalyze the lengthening of the carbon chains of ultra-long-chain fatty acids expression were upregulated in the wax powder deficiency mutant M5075. Therefore, we hypothesize that the reduction in CER1 caused a feedback effect in the alkane synthesis process, leading to the upregulation of certain genes related to very long-chain fatty acid synthesis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study successfully identified BrCER1 as the candidate gene responsible for the wax deficiency phenotype in M5075, a novel glossy mutant of Chinese cabbage. The combination of genetic mapping, bioinformatics analysis, and gene expression studies provided compelling evidence for the involvement of BrCER1 in the regulation of cuticular wax biosynthesis. The results of this study not only extend our knowledge on the molecular mechanism of wax biosynthesis in Brassica but also give a useful insight into the possibility of genetically manipulating the wax content to improve both the commercial quality and stress tolerance in crops. Future research should be based on further validation through gene editing techniques to authenticate the function of BrCER1 and explore potential applications in crop improvement.
