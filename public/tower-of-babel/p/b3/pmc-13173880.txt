Association of glucose metabolism with advanced cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome and prediction model development in type 2 diabetes mellitus
Authors: Qi Sun, Xinyi Yan, Xiaofeng Li, Yilun Lu, Dongqin Wei, Hai Zhang, Caoxu Zhang, Yu Shi, Yunjuan Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, characterized by progressive dysfunction of the cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic systems, has gained increasing recognition in recent years. However, the relationship between glucose metabolism and advanced CKM stages in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) remains insufficiently explored.ObjectiveTo investigate the association between glucose metabolism and advanced CKM stages, and to develop and validate a prediction model for advanced CKM syndrome in Chinese adults with T2DM.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled 3,410 patients with T2DM, who were classified into CKM stages 1–4. Glucose metabolism was evaluated using fasting and post-load insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-β, disposition index (DI), HOMA-IR, QUICKI, Matsuda index, and ISI. Associations between glucose metabolic indices and advanced CKM stages (stages 3–4) were examined using logistic regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) models. A prediction model for advanced CKM syndrome was subsequently developed and validated by assessing the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration, and decision curve analysis.ResultsAmong the participants, CKM stages 1–4 accounted for 18.6%, 50.8%, 28.8%, and 1.9%, respectively. Advanced CKM stages (3–4) were more prevalent among older and male patients. As CKM stages progressed, markers of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR, fasting insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-β) significantly increased, while β-cell function and insulin sensitivity indices (QUICKI, Matsuda index, ISI) decreased (all p < 0.001). Higher quartiles of fasting insulin, C-peptide, HOMA-IR, and HOMA-β were positively associated with advanced CKM syndrome, while better insulin sensitivity and preserved β-cell function exhibited protective effects. The developed prediction model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.935), strong calibration, and favorable clinical utility.ConclusionsAdvanced CKM syndrome is strongly associated with impaired glucose metabolism in Chinese adults with T2DM. The validated prediction model based on glucose metabolism may facilitate early identification and targeted management of individuals with a high likelihood of advanced CKM syndrome.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02244-8.Keywords: Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Glucose metabolism, Prediction

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major global public health issue, with its prevalence increasing rapidly, particularly in Asia. China, one of the countries bearing the heaviest burden of diabetes, currently has approximately 116.4 million adults affected, according to the latest data [1, 2]. Patients with diabetes frequently present with serious comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), significantly increasing the complexity of disease management and clinical treatment. Given this coexistence of multiple conditions, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced the concept of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome in 2023 to systematically describe the pathological state arising from the interaction between metabolic abnormalities, renal impairment, and cardiovascular pathology [3]. The progression of CKM syndrome typically begins with dysfunctional adiposity (e.g., overweight or obesity), which gradually affects metabolic health, kidney function, and vascular integrity, ultimately leading to a markedly increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and other cardiovascular events. CKM syndrome is further classified into stages: stage 0 (no CKM risk factors), stage 1 (presence of overweight or obesity), stage 2 (presence of metabolic risk factors such as hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, or diabetes), stage 3 (subclinical ASCVD), and stage 4 (clinically diagnosed CVD).

Glucose metabolism is a central physiological process that spans the entire course of life. The regulation of glucose metabolism fundamentally depends on two core components: pancreatic β-cell secretory function and insulin resistance. Pancreatic β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance are both central pathological features in the onset and progression of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). In the early stages of T2DM, insulin resistance in peripheral tissues—especially skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver—impairs glucose uptake and utilization, leading to compensatory hyperinsulinemia as β-cells increase insulin secretion to maintain relatively stable blood glucose levels. However, persistent insulin resistance imposes chronic metabolic stress on β-cells, promoting their dysfunction and eventual exhaustion. As the disease progresses, β-cells lose their ability to compensate, resulting in a progressive decline in insulin secretory capacity and further exacerbation of hyperglycemia [4, 5]. This pathological interplay between β-cell failure and insulin resistance not only contributes directly to the development of diabetes but also plays a crucial role in the onset and progression of chronic complications, particularly diabetic kidney disease and cardiovascular disease. Numerous studies have confirmed that reduced β-cell function, combined with worsening insulin resistance, plays a significant pathogenic role in diabetes-related organ damage, especially in the kidneys and cardiovascular system [6–9]. For example, clinical studies have observed that lower fasting C-peptide levels are significantly associated with increased risks of CKD, heart failure, and coronary artery disease in patients with diabetes [10, 11]. This suggests that β-cell dysfunction is not only a marker of disease progression but also an important predictor of complication severity. Furthermore, in patients with T2DM, both greater severity of insulin secretory defects and insulin resistance are associated with an increased risk of renal impairment and cardiovascular events [12, 13]. Glucose metabolic dysregulation interacts with other metabolic disorders, vascular dysfunction, and inflammatory responses to collectively promote the development of diabetes-related cardiac and renal damage.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to describe the prevalence of different CKM stages in Chinese patients with T2DM, to explore the association between glucose metabolism and advanced CKM syndrome, and to establish a predictive model for identifying individuals at high risk of advanced CKM syndrome.

Methods and materials

Study design and population

This study was a cross-sectional observational study conducted in Qidong City, Jiangsu Province, China. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Standardized Metabolic Disease Management Center at Qidong People’s Hospital. The study utilized de-identified retrospective data, and the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Qidong Hospital of Nantong University due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older who had been clearly diagnosed with type 2 diabetes according to the guidelines of the American Diabetes Association (ADA) [14]. Exclusion criteria included: failure to provide a fasting blood sample; missing or abnormal lipid profile data; missing indicators related to the Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome; diagnosis of type 1 diabetes; pregnancy; acute kidney injury or ongoing dialysis treatment; and the presence of active malignancies or other severe chronic diseases. After initial exclusions, a total of 3,410 participants were included in the baseline analysis. The detailed process of participant selection is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and marital status were collected via standardized questionnaires. Clinical history such as diabetes duration and comorbidities was documented from medical records. Anthropometric measurements included body mass index (BMI), which was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Blood pressure was measured using an automated sphygmomanometer after participants had rested for at least 5 min. Hypertension was defined according to the current guidelines (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications). The duration of diabetes was defined as the time interval (in months) from the date of diagnosis to the time of enrollment. Data on antidiabetic medication use were extracted from electronic medical records and verified through patient interviews when available. The use of metformin, insulin, and other oral or injectable glucose-lowering agents (including DPP-4 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, sulfonylureas, meglitinides, thiazolidinediones, and α-glucosidase inhibitors) was documented. Medication use was further categorized as “yes” or “no” for each drug class, and distribution across CKM stages was statistically compared.

Laboratory measurements

Venous blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of at least 8 h. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was measured using the hexokinase method. Lipid profiles including triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were determined using enzymatic methods. Liver function markers (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]) were measured using automated biochemical analyzers. Free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) were measured using automated chemiluminescence immunoassays. Complete blood counts including red blood cell (RBC), white blood cell (WBC), and platelet (PLT) counts were assessed using an automated hematology analyzer. Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was used to evaluate early renal impairment. All laboratory tests were performed in the clinical laboratory of Qidong People’s Hospital under strict internal quality control procedures.

Glucose metabolism assessment

In accordance with recent clinical and metabolic research, we assessed glucose metabolism by measuring insulin and C-peptide levels both in the fasting state and 2 h after the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). To minimize pharmacological interference, all glucose-lowering medications were withheld for 48 h prior to testing when clinically feasible and blood glucose levels were within a safe range. To obtain a comprehensive evaluation, we also applied a panel of validated indices, including the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), homeostasis model assessment of β-cell function (HOMA-β), quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI), Matsuda index, insulin sensitivity index (ISI), and the disposition index (DI). These indices jointly provide a robust and multidimensional assessment of glucose metabolism under both basal and glucose-stimulated conditions. The calculation formulas for these indices are as follows:

HOMA-IR = [FPG (mmol/L) × FINS (µU/mL)] / 22.5

HOMA-IR = [FPG (mmol/L) × FINS (µU/mL)] / 22.5

HOMA-β = [20 × FINS (µU/mL)] / [FPG (mmol/L) − 3.5]

HOMA-β = [20 × FINS (µU/mL)] / [FPG (mmol/L) − 3.5]

QUICKI = 1 / [log(FPG (mg/dL)) + log(FINS (µU/mL))]

Matsuda Index = 10,000 / sqrt[(FPG(mg/dL) × FINS (µU/mL)) × (2hPG(mg/dL) × 2hINS(µU/mL))]

DI = HOMA-β × [1 / HOMA-IR]

CKM syndrome stages

According to the guidelines issued by the AHA, the CKM syndrome is classified into five stages [3]. Stage 0 includes individuals without any metabolic risk factors (such as overweight/obesity, abdominal obesity, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia [TG < 135 mg/dL], prediabetes, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome), CKD, or subclinical/clinical CVD. Stage 1 includes individuals who are overweight/obese, have abdominal obesity, or prediabetes, but without any other metabolic risk factors or CKD. Stage 2 refers to individuals with one or more metabolic risk factors (e.g., hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, diabetes, or metabolic syndrome) or those at moderate to high risk of CKD. Stage 3 includes individuals with subclinical CVD along with metabolic risk factors or CKD. Stage 4 comprises individuals with a self-reported history of CVD and concurrent metabolic risk factors or CKD. As this study lacks direct measurements of subclinical CVD, an equivalent risk definition is applied, including individuals with CKD in stage G4 or G5 or those with a predcted 10-year CVD risk of ≥ 10% (Chinese CVD risk equation) [15]. CKD staging is based on estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated using the CKD-EPI equation. According to the AHA scientific consensus, CKM stages 3 and 4 are classified as advanced CKM stages. For detailed definitions of each stage, refer to Supplementary Table 1. However, since this study exclusively includes individuals with diabetes, none of the participants meet the criteria for Stage 0, and therefore this stage is not represented in our analysis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using the Chi-square (χ²) test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as means ± standard deviations and analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

To assess the association between glucose metabolism and advanced-stage CKM syndrome (stages 3 and 4), multivariable logistic regression models were constructed. Three models were developed: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, ALT, and AST levels. All covariates underwent collinearity diagnostics to ensure model stability. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the observed associations. Specifically, participants receiving exogenous insulin at baseline were excluded to evaluate whether the associations between glucose metabolism measures and advanced-stage CKM syndrome remained consistent. Additionally, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was applied to explore potential non-linear associations between glucose metabolism and advanced-stage CKM syndrome.

For prediction modeling, the dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) using the createDataPartition function. Feature selection was performed in the training set using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ). The final set of predictors was used to develop a multivariable logistic regression model. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA) in both the training and test sets. A nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized prediction. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1), with two-tailed P-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study population

Among the 3,410 individuals with T2DM, the distribution of Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) stages was as follows: CKM stage 1 (n = 633, 18.6%), CKM stage 2 (n = 1,731, 50.8%), CKM stage 3 (n = 982, 28.8%), and CKM stage 4 (n = 64, 1.9%). Advanced CKM stages were more frequently observed in older participants and males. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, among participants classified as stage 3, 1.32% were identified based on CKD G4/G5, 95.93% were identified based on a predicted 10-year CVD risk, and 2.75% met both criteria. When comparing the three subgroups, significant differences were mainly observed in renal function parameters, such as the UACR (p < 0.001), whereas most cardiometabolic risk factors—including BMI, Glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), lipid profile, and insulin resistance indices—were similar across groups. Participants classified as stage 3 due to CKD G4/G5 tended to be older and had lower DBP compared with those defined solely by cardiovascular disease risk.

Both SBP and BMI increased progressively from CKM stage 1 to stage 3, indicating an escalating cardiometabolic burden. HbA1c levels were highest in CKM stages 1 and 2, with a slight reduction in stage 4. The UACR, a marker of renal impairment, rose significantly with CKM progression and peaked at stage 3.

Insulin resistance progressively worsened across the CKM spectrum. From CKM stage 1 to stage 4, HOMA-IR levels increased steadily, while insulin sensitivity indices—QUICKI and Matsuda index—decreased (all p < 0.001), reflecting a continuous decline in insulin sensitivity. Fasting insulin and C-peptide levels were significantly elevated in CKM stage 4, along with increased HOMA-β, suggesting compensatory β-cell activity in the context of advanced metabolic stress.

Regarding lipid profiles, both HDL-C and LDL-C declined with advancing CKM stage, whereas TG levels were lowest in stage 1 and higher in subsequent stages. Hematological parameters such as WBC and PLT counts exhibited minimal variation across stages, while RBC counts remained relatively stable. No significant trends were observed for thyroid hormone levels or liver enzymes (ALT and AST).

Lifestyle factors including smoking and alcohol consumption did not significantly differ across CKM stages. However, a modest difference in marital status distribution was observed (p < 0.001). Additional details are provided in Table 1.

Moreover, the duration of diabetes increased progressively across CKM stages, with a median of 44.0 (4.0–130.0) months in Stage 1 and 130.5 (42.3–197.8) months in stage 4 (p < 0.001). Overall, 83.9% of participants were receiving glucose-lowering therapy, with metformin (51.2%) and insulin (57.4%) being the most commonly prescribed agents. The use of GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors was relatively low (5.4% and 22.4%, respectively); however, their prescription rates tended to increase in higher CKM stages (p < 0.01 for both). In contrast, sulfonylureas were prescribed to 48.1% of patients and remained relatively consistent across CKM stages (p-value requires confirmation for consistency). Meglitinides, thiazolidinediones, and α-glucosidase inhibitors were less frequently used (2.8%, 2.1%, and 17.5%, respectively). Notably, insulin use increased steadily with CKM progression, from 41.9% in stage 1 to 67.2% in stage 4 (p < 0.001), as presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Association between glucose metabolism and advanced CKM stages

To examine the independent relationship between glucose metabolic indices and advanced CKM stages, we constructed three multivariable logistic regression models. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for sex and age; and Model 3 was fully adjusted, further accounting for potential confounders including smoking, alcohol consumption, marital status, and liver enzymes (ALT and AST). As shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), both fasting insulin and fasting C-peptide levels were significantly associated with advanced CKM stages. A one SD increase in fasting insulin showed a positive but nonsignificant association with advanced CKM stages (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.99–1.20, p = 0.0859). Participants in the highest quartile (Q3) of fasting insulin showed a significantly higher likelihood of advanced CKM stages compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q0) (OR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.66–2.98, p < 0.0001; p for trend < 0.0001). Similarly, fasting C-peptide exhibited a dose–response association (Q3 vs. Q0: OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.36–2.45, p < 0.0001), suggesting that increased β-cell secretory activity may contribute to advanced CKM stages.

Markers of insulin resistance also demonstrated consistent positive associations. Higher HOMA-IR values were associated with an increased likelihood of advanced CKM stages (Q3 vs. Q0: OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.47–2.63, p < 0.0001), whereas insulin sensitivity indices—including QUICKI, Matsuda index, and ISI—were inversely associated with advanced CKM stages (QUICKI Q3 vs. Q0: OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.38–0.68; Matsuda index: OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.39–0.69; ISI: OR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.38–0.68; all p < 0.0001), indicating a potential protective role of enhanced insulin sensitivity.

Postprandial insulin and C-peptide levels (at 120 min) were also associated with advanced CKM stages to some extent. While 120-minute insulin levels showed only borderline significance in the highest quartile (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 0.96–1.72, p = 0.0910), 120-minute C-peptide levels were significantly associated across quartiles, with Q3 exhibiting elevated odds of advanced CKM stages (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.20–2.15, p = 0.0016).

Notably, FPG and 120-minute glucose levels were not significantly associated with advanced CKM stages after multivariable adjustment (all p > 0.3), suggesting that dynamic indices of glucose metabolism may be more informative than absolute glucose levels in predicting advanced CKM syndrome.

Furthermore, the HOMA-β index, a surrogate marker of β-cell function, showed a graded positive association with advanced CKM stages (Q3 vs. Q0: OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.19–2.13, p = 0.0017). In contrast, the DI demonstrated attenuated associations after adjustment and did not show a significant trend.

In sensitivity analyses, the fully adjusted associations were broadly similar. Higher 2-hour glucose was associated with greater odds of advanced CKM stages. In quartile-based analyses, higher fasting insulin and fasting C-peptide, as well as HOMA-IR, were associated with increased odds, whereas insulin sensitivity indices (e.g., QUICKI and the Matsuda index) were inversely associated. Fasting glucose and 2-hour insulin/C-peptide were not materially associated after full adjustment. See Supplementary Table 5 for details.

Exploration of nonlinear relationship

To investigate the potential nonlinear associations between glucose metabolic indices and advanced CKM stages, RCS models were employed. Models were adjusted for relevant confounders, including sex, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, ALT, and AST.

As shown in Fig. 3, fasting insulin and HOMA-IR displayed strong positive nonlinear associations with advanced CKM stages (p for overall < 0.001; p for nonlinear < 0.001), with a noticeable acceleration of the association at higher concentrations. Likewise, fasting C-peptide (p < 0.001 and p = 0.036), 120-minute insulin (p = 0.001 and p = 0.005), and HOMA-β (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001) also exhibited significant nonlinear associations, suggesting that dysregulated insulin secretion and β-cell dysfunction contribute progressively to the development of advanced CKM stages.

Insulin sensitivity indices—including QUICKI, Matsuda index, and ISI—showed robust inverse nonlinear relationships with advanced CKM stages (all p for overall and nonlinearity < 0.001), implying diminishing marginal protection beyond a certain threshold of insulin sensitivity.

Conversely, variables such as FPG (p = 0.916; p for nonlinear = 0.773), 120-minute glucose (p = 0.961; p for nonlinear = 0.863), and DI (p = 0.622; p for nonlinear = 0.861) did not demonstrate meaningful nonlinear trends. This suggests their predictive relevance for advanced CKM stages may be limited or approximately linear.

Development and validation of the prediction model for advanced CKM stages

The entire dataset was randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3 using the createDataPartition function. Specifically, 70% of the samples were allocated to the training set for model development and internal validation, while the remaining 30% were assigned to the test set for external validation of model performance.

To identify the most relevant predictors, LASSO regression analysis was performed in the training set. The optimal value of the regularization parameter λ was determined via 10-fold cross-validation, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The minimum mean squared error (MSE) was achieved at log(λ) = − 6.58 (λ = 0.001), resulting in the selection of 24 nonzero predictors. The selected variables and their corresponding coefficients are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Based on these predictors, a prediction model was constructed, and a nomogram was developed to facilitate individualized estimation (Fig. 4). The predictive performance of the model was further evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. As shown in Fig. 5, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.94 in the training set and 0.935 in the test set, indicating excellent discriminative ability and minimal likelihood of overfitting. Further evaluation of model calibration in the test set was performed using a calibration curve (Supplementary Fig. 3), which demonstrated strong agreement between predicted and observed outcomes (mean absolute error = 0.016). Additionally, decision curve analysis (Supplementary Fig. 4) showed that the model provided a greater net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, supporting its potential clinical utility.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 3,410 Chinese patients with T2DM, we identified a high prevalence of advanced Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) syndrome, with over 30% of patients classified as stage 3 or 4. We found a significant positive association between β-cell dysfunction, insulin resistance, and advanced CKM stages, which remained robust across multivariable logistic regression and RCS analyses. These findings support the emerging view that glucose metabolism deterioration plays a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of advanced CKM syndrome.

Consistent with previous reports, elevated fasting insulin, C-peptide levels, and HOMA-IR were closely associated with advanced CKM stages, underscoring insulin resistance as a central mechanism linking metabolic dysregulation to renal and cardiovascular injury [16, 17]. After adjusting for conventional risk factors, individuals in the highest quartiles of fasting insulin and HOMA-IR showed a 2.2-fold and 2.0-fold increased likelihood of advanced CKM syndrome, respectively. These results align with population-based studies identifying insulin resistance as an independent predictor of CKD and cardiovascular events [18–20].

Importantly, β-cell function—measured by HOMA-β and C-peptide—showed a nonlinear positive association with CKM progression. Higher HOMA-β levels were associated with an increased risk of advanced CKM syndrome, with a tendency toward plateauing at the upper range. This pattern suggests that elevated β-cell activity may reflect a compensatory hyperinsulinemic state under insulin resistance. These findings support the notion that β-cell overcompensation, rather than deficiency alone, may contribute to the progression of CKM syndrome [21, 22]. Mechanistically, β-cell impairment may promote microvascular injury via hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, lipid dysregulation, and endothelial dysfunction, ultimately driving glomerulosclerosis and atherosclerosis [23–25]. Moreover, excessive insulin levels may act synergistically with cortisol, promoting sodium retention and myocardial remodeling, which may underlie the observed complex non-linear trends [26–28].

In contrast, insulin sensitivity indices—such as QUICKI, Matsuda Index, and ISI—were inversely associated with CKM stages and exhibited a plateau in protective effects at higher sensitivity levels, suggesting a possible “saturation threshold” for metabolic benefit [29]. Notably, neither fasting nor 2-hour plasma glucose independently predicted CKM progression, highlighting the superior prognostic utility of dynamic insulin-related indices over static glycemic measures. This advantage likely arises from the fact that dynamic insulin and C-peptide indices capture both insulin resistance and β-cell compensatory responses, which are key metabolic drivers of cardio-kidney disease progression. In contrast, static glucose measurements—such as fasting or 2-hour plasma glucose—mainly reflect the net glycemic outcome at a single time point. During early compensatory stages, glucose levels may remain within the normal range despite substantial insulin resistance, thereby masking the underlying metabolic stress and vascular burden. Incorporating insulin and C-peptide measurements into standardized OGTT or routine metabolic evaluations could enable earlier identification of CKM risk among patients with T2DM.

Furthermore, we developed and validated a prediction model for advanced CKM stages in this population, integrating key β-cell function and insulin resistance indicators. The model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability (AUC = 0.94 in the training set and 0.935 in the test set), robust calibration (mean absolute error = 0.016), and favorable clinical utility as shown by decision curve analysis. These results suggest that such models can provide individualized estimates and enhance early identification of patients at high likelihood of advanced CKM syndrome. The incorporation of a nomogram offers a practical tool for clinical decision-making, supporting targeted interventions and more precise management strategies. The model includes 24 predictors to achieve optimal predictive performance, all of which are routinely collected during standard clinical evaluations, such as anthropometric measurements, basic biochemical assays, and commonly used metabolic and renal indicators. This makes the model feasible for implementation in real-world healthcare settings, as it does not require additional testing or specialized equipment. The routine availability of these parameters facilitates easy integration into existing electronic health record systems, enabling seamless risk stratification during regular patient visits. However, we also recognize that although these 24 variables are commonly collected in many clinical environments, manual entry may be burdensome, particularly in primary care settings with lower levels of digitalization. Among the predictors selected by LASSO, the five variables with the largest absolute coefficients, and thus the greatest contributions to the model’s linear predictor, were age, sex, systolic blood pressur, and smoking status. When complete data are unavailable, these variables may serve as a pragmatic ‘minimum-input’ set to prioritize for risk prediction. To improve the model’s feasibility and usability, future research will focus on developing a simplified version of the predictive model, concentrating on the core predictors that are critical for risk stratification. This would be especially valuable for resource-limited institutions facing challenges with data entry and digitalization.

Clinically, our findings underscore the potential utility of early glucose metabolism assessment in stratifying CKM stages among patients with T2DM. Integrating C-peptide and insulin resistance markers into screening algorithms may improve classification and guide personalized therapeutic approaches. Among potential interventions, sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have demonstrated multifaceted benefits—improving glycemic control, enhancing insulin sensitivity, and alleviating cardiorenal burden—making them a promising option for CKM management. Early insulin therapy may also help maintain glucose metabolic stability by reducing functional stress on β-cells and delaying metabolic decline [30, 31].

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and prospective studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationship between glucose metabolic impairment and CKM progression. Second, CKM stage 3 was defined using a predicted 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% as a surrogate rather than direct measures of subclinical cardiovascular disease. Third, the use of antidiabetic medications may influence glucose metabolism indices. Fourth, the single-center data from Eastern China may limit the generalizability of the findings; because this model was developed in a Chinese population and within a specific healthcare context, external validation in independent cohorts—particularly in non-Chinese and multi-ethnic populations—is required before broader application. Fifth, Stage 4 (clinically diagnosed CVD) comprised only a small proportion of the cohort, which may reduce precision and affect specificity in identifying the highest-risk population. Stages 3–4 were combined as ‘advanced CKM’ to improve statistical stability, but larger Stage 4 samples are needed to better characterize model performance. Finally, future studies should explore CKM-related molecular pathways and validate interventions targeting glucose metabolic disorders for CKM prevention and management across diverse populations.

Conclusion

In summary, this study revealed a high prevalence of advanced CKM syndrome among Chinese patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus. Impaired glucose metabolism was identified as a key factor associated with advanced CKM stages. Moreover, we developed and validated a prediction model that demonstrated excellent discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility in identifying patients with a high likelihood of advanced CKM syndrome. Further prospective and biological studies are needed to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
