Impact of junctional reflux on the effectiveness of endovenous ablation in patients with great saphenous vein insufficiency
Authors: Chandu Vemuri, Kathleen D Gibson, Mikel Sadek, Cassius I Ochoa-Chaar, Antonios P Gasparis, Yana Etkin, Andrea T Obi, Windsor Ting, Shreef Said, Nicholas J Mouawad, Anuj D Mahajan, Shalini Sahoo, John D Sorkin, Tara McDonald, Brajesh K Lal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffect of junctional reflux on the venous clinical severity score in patients with insufficiency of the great saphenous vein (JURY) study proved that venous clinical severity scores (VCSS) are similar in patients with great saphenous vein (GSV) reflux, with or without saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) reflux. Most studies testing the efficacy of endovenous ablation have simply required the presence of truncal reflux for inclusion, and limited data exist on whether junctional reflux impacts benefit. JURY-2 hypothesizes that patients with GSV reflux, with or without SFJ reflux, will derive similar benefit from endovenous ablation.MethodsJURY-2 was a prospective longitudinal multicenter study of patients undergoing GSV ablation. Inclusion criteria: 18 to 80 years age, reflux of ≥0.5 seconds in ≥2 contiguous segments of the GSV above the knee, and C2 or C3 disease. Exclusion criteria: prior deep vein thrombosis; prior vein ablation on the index leg; concomitant obstruction of the iliocaval system; or known renal, hepatic, or cardiac failure. The primary outcome, sample size, and analytic plan were determined pre hoc. The primary outcome was the 3-month postprocedural change in VCSS. The outcome in patients with isolated GSV reflux vs combined GSV and SFJ reflux was considered equivalent if the difference in VCSS change-scores was within the minimum detectable change of ±1.4 points. Change scores were also compared between groups. The multivariable regression model adjusted for baseline VCSS, demographic, clinical, and therapeutic covariates. Statistical analyses were two-tailed, and P < .05 was considered significant.ResultsA total of 252 patients were enrolled; most were female (75.00%) and White (78.57%). Of them, 207 patients (82.14%) had combined SFJ+GSV reflux, whereas 45 (17.86%) had isolated GSV reflux. Thermal ablation was used predominantly in both groups, but more so in the SFJ+GSV reflux group (77.78% vs 57.78%). GSV ablation was successful in nearly all cases (99.51% in the SFJ+GSV reflux group; 100% in the isolated GSV reflux group) with no complications. Both groups demonstrated a significant reduction in postprocedural VCSS (6.17 ± 2.20 to 1.86 ± 2.04). The change score in the SFJ+GSV reflux group was equivalent to the GSV group 3 months after endovenous ablation (a difference of –0.54 between groups, 95% confidence interval: –1.36, +0.27). There was no statistical difference in the VCSS change-scores between the two groups (P = .12).ConclusionsIn this prospective multicenter study with full accounting of important clinical covariates, patients with isolated GSV reflux derived equivalent benefit after endovenous ablation compared with those with combined GSV+SFJ reflux. These data support the clinical recommendation that venous ablation must be guided by symptomatic severity rather than the presence or absence of junctional reflux.Keywords: Ablation, Great saphenous vein, Reflux, Saphenofemoral junction, Venous

Take Home Message: The presence or absence of SFJ reflux should not be a decisive factor in determining the treatment approach for patients with chronic venous insufficiency and great saphenous vein reflux. Venous ablation must be guided by symptomatic severity rather than the presence or absence of junctional reflux. Patients with great saphenous vein reflux who meet clinical criteria for treatment should have equivalent treatment regardless of whether or not they have SFJ reflux.

Great saphenous vein (GSV) incompetence is a major contributor to symptomatic varicose veins and venous insufficiency.1 Endovenous ablation of the refluxing GSV is an established first-line treatment, showing safety and efficacy comparable to open surgery.2 Studies have confirmed that endovenous ablation yields successful vein closure with symptom relief in patients with GSV reflux.3, 4, 5 Although many patients presenting with C2 or C3 disease have combined GSV and saphenofemoral junction (SFJ) reflux, a substantial number have isolated GSV reflux and a competent SFJ. Most clinicians do not consider SFJ involvement a mandatory prerequisite for intervention in symptomatic patients.3,6 In fact, most clinical trials testing the efficacy of venous ablation have required hemodynamically significant truncal reflux for inclusion, not necessarily junctional reflux. Therefore, there is a current lack of reliable supportive evidence for the efficacy of venous ablation in patients with isolated GSV reflux, even though there is no evidence that treatment is ineffective. As a result, patients with symptomatic isolated GSV incompetence are typically not able to receive optimal treatment.

The multicenter JURY study demonstrated that patients with GSV reflux experienced comparable symptom severity, assessed by Venous Clinical Severity Scores (VCSS), regardless of whether SFJ incompetence was present or not.7 The trial questioned the rationale behind current restrictions, because patients meeting clinical severity criteria (eg, significant varicosities or edema) have comparable disability whether or not the SFJ is involved. Although the JURY study identified baseline symptom equivalence, it did not assess whether ablation treatment of the GSV yielded equivalent benefit in the two groups.

JURY-2 aims to determine whether endovenous ablation results in equivalent improvement, measured as a change in the 3-month postprocedural VCSS, in patients with isolated GSV reflux compared with those with reflux involving both the GSV and the SFJ. Results of JURY-2 will help guide vascular specialists and payers to offer evidence-based management of GSV reflux.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed a prospective controlled cohort study of patients undergoing endovenous GSV ablation. Participating centers included Lake Washington Vascular, Bellevue, WA; New York University Langone Health, NY; Northwell Health, NY; Yale University, CT; University of Michigan, MI; Northwell Health Huntington, NY. The study was conducted by, and all analyses were performed by, the Center for Vascular Research, University of Maryland. The sponsor had no access to the data. The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the University of Maryland and participating clinical centers. The paper was prepared based on STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting standards.8

Participants

Patients had to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) 18 to 80 years of age; (2) reflux of ≥0.5 seconds on ultrasound examination in two or more contiguous segments of the GSV above the knee, with or without reflux at the SFJ; (3) undergo endovenous ablation of the target vein; and (4) varicose veins or edema (defined as a C2 or C3 classification on the CEAP [clinical, etiologic, anatomic, pathophysiologic] clinical scoring system). Patients were excluded if they had any of the following: (1) history of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or stigmata of DVT on ultrasound examination; (2) prior vein ablation on the index lower extremity; (3) concomitant obstruction of the iliocaval system; and (4) known renal, hepatic, or congestive heart failure. Participating centers enrolled each consecutive patient as they were scheduled for an ablation procedure. They then used a web-based data capture system to enter study information, ensuring standardized and efficient data collection across all sites. In patients with bilateral disease, the leg undergoing treatment was enrolled, and if both were planned for treatment, only one leg with the more severe C clinical class was enrolled.

Variables

The primary outcome measure was the difference in change in postprocedure VCSS scores between the two groups of patients—those with combined GSV and SFJ reflux vs those with GSV reflux alone, after adjusting for potential confounders. The modified VCSS evaluates 10 hallmarks of chronic venous disease, each on a scale of 0 to 3: pain (none, occasional, daily, and daily limiting), varicose veins (none, few, calf or thigh, and calf and thigh), venous edema (none, foot and ankle, above ankle below knee, and to the knee or above), skin hyperpigmentation (none, perimalleolar, diffuse lower 1/3 of calf, and wider above lower 1/3 of calf), inflammation (none, perimalleolar, diffuse lower 1/3 or calf, and wider above lower 1/3 of calf), induration, number of ulcers (none, 1, 2, and 3), size of ulcers (not applicable = 0, diameter less than 2 cm = 1, diameter 2-6 cm = 2, diameter greater than 6 cm = 3), durations of ulcers (not applicable = 0, less than 3 months = 1, greater than 3 months but less than 1 year = 2, and not healed for greater than 1 year = 3), and compliance with compression therapy (none, intermittent, most days, and fully compliant). Possible VCSS scores range from 0 to 30.9

In addition to VCSS, other data collected included patient demographics such as age (in years), sex, race (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black or African American, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, White, and multiracial), and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino and not Hispanic or Latino). Clinical details collected included body mass index (BMI), known hypercoagulable state, renal failure, hepatic failure, congestive heart failure, and previous deep or superficial venous procedure in the index limb.

Endovenous ablation procedure

Participating investigators were free to select the ablation technique of their choice, provided it included one that was approved by the Food and Drug Administration for GSV ablation. The type of ablation used was recorded as thermal or nonthermal. Additional procedural details were also recorded, including the side treated (left or right), any simultaneous ancillary procedures conducted (microphlebectomy, sclerotherapy, branch ligation, and ablation of the small saphenous vein, anterior accessory saphenous vein, and other axial veins), and complications during or after the procedure.

Sample size estimates

A priori sample size estimation was conducted using PASS 2024 (NCSS, LLC) software to determine the sample size required for an equivalence test comparing VCSS scores between participants with combined GSV+SFJ reflux and those with isolated GSV reflux. We conducted a priori sample size calculation before data collection using the Two-One-Sided t-Tests procedure for a two-sample test of VCSS change-scores, with 80% power and a 95% confidence level. Passman et al10 previously reported a mean VCSS score of 2.83 (standard deviation [SD] = 0.47) in 2907 patients with venous disease. We therefore, conservatively, assumed an SD of 1.0 for our sample size estimates. Marston et al11 reported that the mean interobserver variability of the VCSS scoring system was 1.4 (SD = 1.7). We therefore assigned our boundaries for equivalence for change in VCSS scores at ±1.4. To ensure an adequate sample size, we conservatively estimated our target enrollment based on a smaller equivalence boundary (±1.0). Estimates were computed, assuming unequal distribution between the two groups of up to 1:5. This yielded an estimated sample size of 302 patients. After enrolling the first 100 participants, we performed a preplanned reassessment of our sample size by analyzing the entire cohort, blinded to group identity or treatment received. We calculated the mean and SD of VCSS in our cohort to obtain a more accurate estimate of the sample size needed for the analysis. Based on these updated estimates, we adjusted our target enrollment to 245 patients.

Statistical methods

Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and continuous data as means and SDs or median and interquartile range. A comparison of characteristics of the two groups was performed using the Pearson χ2 test or the Fisher exact test for categorical data and Student t-tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous data.

The primary aim of our study was to determine whether patients with combined SFJ+GSV reflux and those with isolated GSV reflux were equivalent in their postprocedure change in symptom severity as measured by the VCSS score. We began by computing unadjusted (crude) VCSS in the two groups at baseline, at 3 months postprocedure, and for the change over time (post minus pre). To compare VCSS between the two groups of patients, we used Welch’s two-sample t-tests, which account for unequal variances between groups. These tests were conducted separately for baseline scores, postprocedure scores, and change scores. To account for potential confounding variables, we then conducted a multiple linear regression analysis. The dependent variable was the change in VCSS (postprocedure minus preprocedure), and the primary independent variable was patient group (combined SFJ+GSV reflux vs isolated GSV reflux). The model adjusted for baseline VCSS, age, sex, race (Black, Other vs White), ethnicity (Hispanic vs non-Hispanic), BMI, and endovenous ablation type (thermal vs nonthermal). This allowed us to estimate the adjusted difference in symptom improvement between the two groups while holding other covariates constant. Graphical displays of the difference in change scores between the two groups (±95% confidence intervals [CI]) were used to determine if the differences were equivalent. Because the minimum detectable interobserver difference in VCSS is 1.4,11 the responses to endovenous ablation in the two groups were considered equivalent if the 95% CI of the primary outcome (the difference in VCSS change-scores) did not include the a priori equivalence boundaries of +1.4 and –1.4.

In secondary analyses, we computed crude differences in VCSS between patients with vs without SFJ reflux at baseline and 3 months after endovenous ablation. We also sought to define differences in patterns of change in individual items of the VCSS between the two groups of patients. Histograms were used to present comparisons of the mean scores for individual components of the VCSS between the two groups at baseline, postprocedure, and for change over time. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc) and were repeated in R (version 4.2.2; R Core Team [2023]). A P value of ≤.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants

A total of 252 patients were enrolled in the study. The number of patients enrolled by site was: Lake Washington Vascular = 111, Northwell Health = 41, NYU Langone Health = 77, Yale University = 15, University of Michigan = 5, Northwell Health Huntington = 3. A total of 207 (82.14%) patients were in the group with combined SFJ+GSV reflux, whereas 45 (17.86%) were in the group with isolated GSV reflux.

Descriptive information

Table I shows that patients with isolated GSV reflux were older than those with combined SFJ+GSV reflux (mean age 58.58 vs 52.94 years; P = .006). Most patients were female (75.00% overall) and White (78.57% overall), with no difference between groups. The isolated GSV reflux group had a higher proportion of Hispanic or Latino individuals (13.33% vs 6.28%; P < .01). BMI was similar between groups (mean 27.59 kg/m2 overall). Comorbid conditions such as renal failure and hepatic disease occurred in one patient each in the isolated GSV reflux group (2.22% each), and although these were statistically different between groups (P = .03 for both), their frequency was too low to be of clinical importance. The combined SFJ+GSV reflux group had a numerically higher rate of previous superficial venous procedures in the contralateral limb, though the difference did not reach statistical significance (10.63% vs 2.22%, P = .08).

Primary outcome

A fully adjusted analysis found that the estimated difference in change in VCSS score 3 months after endovenous ablation between patients with combined SFJ+GSV reflux vs those with isolated GSV reflux was –0.54, with a 95% CI of –1.34 and +0.26 (Table III). The model adjusted for baseline VCSS score, age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, and ablation technique. Using the minimum reliable detectable difference in VCSS scores of –1.4 and +1.4 as equivalence boundaries,11 the change in VCSS scores in the two groups was equivalent. The model also identified no statistical difference in the change in VCSS scores between the two groups 3 months after endovenous ablation (P = .12).

Secondary analyses

The raw unadjusted VCSS scores of the entire cohort improved 3 months after endovenous ablation (from 6.17 ± 2.20 to 1.86 ± 2.04). At baseline, the mean VCSS scores were lower for patients with combined SFJ+GSV reflux (6.00 ± 2.16) compared with isolated GSV reflux (6.98 ± 2.24, P = .01). Both the combined SFJ+GSV reflux and the isolated GSV reflux groups demonstrated significant improvement in VCSS scores, down to 1.71 ± 1.87 and 2.58 ± 2.60, respectively.

An analysis of individual elements of the VCSS by patient group identified notable differences at baseline, at postablation follow-up, and in changes after ablation. Fig 1 shows that a relatively higher proportion of patients with isolated GSV reflux had pain and edema before the procedure. Fig 2 shows that fewer patients with isolated reflux had pain, varicose veins, and edema after ablation. Finally, Fig 3 shows that within 3 months of the procedure, pain, varicose veins, and edema improved for both groups of patients.

Discussion

Endovenous ablation is equally effective in patients with isolated GSV reflux and in those with combined GSV+SFJ reflux. Patients in both groups benefited from endovenous ablation. After adjusting for patient characteristics, ablation type, and disease severity, the change in VCSS score was equivalent and not significantly different in patients treated for isolated GSV reflux compared with patients with combined GSV and SFJ reflux. These results are consistent with the findings of the JURY study, which set the stage by reporting that disease severity does not correlate with the presence of junctional reflux.7 Patients with isolated GSV reflux experienced a similar severity of symptoms as those with combined GSV+SFJ reflux. JURY-2 findings confirm that in patients with C2 or C3 disease and GSV reflux, the absence of SFJ reflux should not preclude endovenous ablation.

Symptomatic chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) with isolated GSV reflux without junctional reflux is a prevalent yet underserved clinical problem. One study demonstrated that isolated GSV reflux occurred in approximately 30% of patients.12 Labropoulos et al13 reported that in 123 limbs with GSV reflux, 22% presented without reflux in the SFJ. Another series reported that 15.5% of patients undergoing endovenous ablation did not have SFJ reflux on ultrasound examination.14 Contextually, for JURY-2, approximately 18% of patients exhibited isolated GSV reflux without SFJ reflux, and this was a notable enrollment percentage given the challenges in treating patients without junctional reflux in the current climate of payor restrictions. The actual prevalence of symptomatic isolated GSV reflux is likely even higher, because many patients do not receive consideration for ablation and could therefore not be enrolled. The multisociety Appropriate Use Criteria sponsored by the American Venous Forum has advocated for treatment of truncal reflux in symptomatic patients but without a specific requirement for the presence of junctional reflux.15 Despite these recommendations, reimbursement policies remain restrictive, often requiring evidence of reflux at the SFJ as a prerequisite for ablation.16 By one estimate, this may be impacting up to one-third of patients with C2 and C3 disease. The reluctance has been driven primarily by an absence of reliable evidence of the efficacy of endovenous ablation in this subgroup of patients.

The insistence on SFJ reflux as a prerequisite for ablation traces back to the traditional view of CVI pathophysiology. This “descending theory” of reflux proposed that venous valve failure propagated downward. Incompetence of the SFJ led to venous hypertension transmitted distally, sequentially leading to valvular dysfunction in the thigh and calf.17 By this logic, a competent SFJ would protect the limb from significant reflux, and treating isolated distal GSV reflux might be less necessary. However, subsequent studies have challenged this theory.18, 19, 20 The most common origin of primary GSV reflux is in the below-knee segment, not at the SFJ.18 Furthermore, research in vein-wall biology indicates that venous insufficiency originates from intrinsic degenerative changes in the vein-wall and valves, rather than from chronically elevated pressure due to proximal valve loss.21 Therefore, the presence of SFJ reflux is neither necessary for the development of CVI nor a reliable marker of disease severity.

The findings in JURY-2 are generalizable. The overall cohort was consistent with patients typically seen in venous practices, with a mean age of 53.95 years and BMI 27.6 kg/m2, comprising 75% females and 78% White patients. In a retrospective study (N = 1300 patients) conducted in two centers, the mean age of participants was 53 years, 72% were female, and the mean BMI was 23 kg/m2.2,22 Recruitment was consecutive, from a geographically diverse set of clinical centers, and at a variety of practice settings (community and academic). The results are thus broadly representative of patients with C2 and C3 disease.

It is unlikely that a randomized trial will be ethically possible; hence these results offer the most robust evidence on which to base clinical recommendations. The two groups were similar in most baseline and procedural characteristics. Key differences included isolated GSV reflux patients being older (58.58 vs 52.94 years old), having more Hispanics or Latinos (13.33% vs 6.28%), undergoing fewer thermal ablations (57.78% vs 77.78%), and fewer ancillary procedures (20.00% vs 44.44%). These differences were carefully adjusted for in the analytic model.

The results of JURY-2 are reliable due to the rigorous a priori sample size estimation and careful study design. We performed an interim reassessment to ensure adequate statistical power and precision, and ensured that target enrollment was achieved. This approach minimized the risk of a type II error and ensured that our findings were robust. We used conservative assumptions for variances in VCSS score assessments to assign equivalence boundaries.10 Our primary analysis for postprocedural equivalence in VCSS change scores was further substantiated by a sensitivity analysis that confirmed no significant difference in the change scores between the two groups of patients. We minimized the risk of confounding by carefully planning our sample size to permit adjustment for common covariates such as baseline VCSS score, age, sex, race, ethnicity, BMI, and ablation technique.11 We included only patients with GSV reflux (with or without junctional reflux) with at least two contiguous above-knee segments confirmed by ultrasound examination and a clinical score of a CEAP C2-C3. We excluded patients with deep venous reflux, prior DVT, significant comorbidities, or previous venous ablation in the index limb, further strengthening the validity of our results.

Our results are clinically relevant. JURY-2 used the modified VCSS, a validated clinical tool, to assess both the baseline severity of venous disease and clinical outcomes after ablation.10,11 There already exists strong proof for effective symptom relief by endovenous ablation in patients with CVI.23, 24, 25, 26 The postablation drop in VCSS from 6.17 ± 2.20 to 1.86 ± 2.04 in the entire cohort is consistent with published experience. Prior studies have shown a decrease in VCSS ranging from 2.4 to 4.9 at various time points after ablation.2,27,28 Technical success rates exceeded 99% in both groups. Publications assessing longitudinal change in VCSS have found that most patients experienced a significant decrease at 1 month postablation, which was sustained with little change up to 1 year.28,29 Prior publications with VCSS changes similar to the current trial support the choice of a 3-month assessment of VCSS in JURY-2. A single-center comparison of the outcomes of endovenous ablation in patients with junctional reflux compared with isolated saphenous reflux showed more than 90% symptomatic improvement in both groups.14 JURY-2 provides a more accurate assessment of symptomatic relief by measuring the change in VCSS, adjusting for confounders, and reporting an adequately powered, multicenter analysis.

Limitations

Ultrasound protocols were not standardized across sites, including whether examinations were performed in the standing position. Reflux-detection and reproducibility have been shown to vary with patient position. Reflux duration may also differ by hydration status and time of day. However, if the study enrolled patients with minimal disease, it would be biased toward less effective reduction in VCSS, contrary to what was observed. Postprocedure bandaging and elastic compression were not standardized. However, this likely affected both treatment groups, and there is no reason to believe that the variability was biased toward one treatment group over the other. Scoring for VCSS was implemented by many individuals and was subject to variability. However, this was accounted for in the sample size calculation. The choice of ablation (thermal vs nonthermal, with or without ancillary procedures) was not standardized; thus we cannot draw any reliable inferences from the observed differences. The procedures were clearly effective in both groups. Finally, the nonrandomized study design limits causal inference and introduces potential for unmeasured confounding. However, given previously published data, and now, the findings of JURY-2 preclude the conduct of such a trial on ethical grounds.

Conclusions

Patients with isolated GSV reflux derive equivalent and similar benefit from venous ablation compared with those with combined GSV and SFJ reflux. The two successive trials (JURY and JURY-2) provide robust data to inform evidence-based practice and appropriate care of symptomatic C2 and C3 disease, and emphasize that symptoms of CVI and not the presence of junctional reflux should determine treatment of GSV reflux. The two studies validate a novel model of rigorous, prospective research led by an academic society, in this case the American Venous Forum, and bring together its biggest stakeholders, the physicians and their academic institutions, to conduct multicenter investigations.
