Long‐Term Clinical Outcomes of Acute Myocardial Infarction Based on Institutional Experience With IVUS
Authors: Youngjoon Kwon, Namkyun Kim, Do‐Hoon Kim, Byungwon Kim, Jong Sung Park, Yoon Jung Park, Bo Eun Park, Hong Nyun Kim, Jang Hoon Lee, Dong Heon Yang, Hun Sik Park, Yongkeun Cho, Kwang Soo Cha, Seung‐Ho Hur, Jin‐Yong Hwang, Myung Ho Jeong, the KAMIR‐NIH Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntravascular ultrasound (IVUS)‐guided percutaneous coronary intervention improves patient outcomes, yet the impact of a center's IVUS experience on long‐term outcomes remains unclear. We evaluated whether the prognostic association of IVUS‐guided percutaneous coronary intervention in patients with acute myocardial infarction differs based on a center's level of IVUS use.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 9752 patients with acute myocardial infarction treated with second‐generation drug‐eluting stents from the KAMIR‐NIH (Korean Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry–National Institutes of Health). The primary outcome was 3‐year major adverse cardiovascular events, defined as a composite of all‐cause death, myocardial infarction, and coronary revascularization. The secondary outcome was target‐lesion failure, defined as a composite of cardiac death, target‐vessel myocardial infarction, and ischemia‐driven target lesion revascularization. Centers were classified into higher‐ or lower‐IVUS‐use groups on the basis of median institutional usage (10.3%).ResultsIn higher‐use centers, IVUS‐guided percutaneous coronary intervention was associated with lower rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (15.3% versus 18.5%, P=0.016) and target‐lesion failure (6.3% versus 8.3%, P=0.039) in propensity score–matched populations. Multivariate Cox analysis confirmed lower risks of major adverse cardiovascular events (hazard ratio [HR], 0.80 [95% CI, 0.69–0.93]; P=0.003) and target‐lesion failure (HR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.59–0.93]; P=0.01). Conversely, in lower‐use centers, IVUS guidance was not associated with significant differences in major adverse cardiovascular events (15.7% versus 18.6%, P=0.422) or target‐lesion failure (8.9% versus 10.4%, P=0.644).ConclusionsThe association of IVUS‐guided percutaneous coronary intervention with lower adverse event rates was more apparent and statistically demonstrable in centers with higher IVUS use. These findings suggest that institutional experience may amplify the observable impact of IVUS guidance, underscoring the potential value of standardized IVUS implementation in acute myocardial infarction management.Keywords: acute myocardial infarction, center's experience with IVUS, intravascular ultrasound, major adverse cardiovascular events, percutaneous coronary intervention, target‐lesion failureSubject Categories: Percutaneous Coronary Intervention

Numerous clinical studies consistently suggest that intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)‐guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) significantly improves patient outcomes. For instance, the IVUS‐XPL (Impact of Intravascular Ultrasound Guidance on Outcomes of Xience Prime Stents in Long Lesions) multicentered, randomized trial and its 5‐year follow‐up study have shown that the use of IVUS‐guided Everolimus‐eluting stent implantation, compared with angiography‐guided stent implantation, resulted in a significantly lower rate of major adverse cardiac events up to 5 years. 1 , 2 Similarly, the ULTIMATE (Intravascular Ultrasound Guided Drug Eluting Stents Implantation in “All‐Comers” Coronary Lesions) trial and its subsequent 3‐year outcomes have demonstrated that IVUS‐guided drug‐eluting stent implantation significantly improves clinical outcomes in all‐comers, particularly for patients who had an IVUS‐defined optimal procedure compared with angiography guidance. 3 , 4

Beyond randomized trials, observational studies have also supported the clinical benefit of IVUS guidance in high‐risk populations, including patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and those in cardiogenic shock. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Despite these consistent findings, the adoption and use of IVUS remain highly variable across institutions and countries. Differences in institutional IVUS volume may influence the quality and effectiveness of IVUS‐guided PCI, potentially affecting patient outcomes.

Therefore, this study aims to clarify the impact of institutional IVUS expertise on clinical outcomes in AMI, thereby providing further evidence for the value of experience‐based IVUS use.

Method

Study Design and Population

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. We conducted a retrospective analysis of all patients registered in the KAMIR‐NIH (Korean Acute Myocardial Infarction Registry–National Institutes of Health) between November 2011 and December 2015. KAMIR‐NIH is a prospective, open, observational, multicenter online registry of AMI cases in the Republic of Korea. A total of 20 hospitals were enrolled in this registry, and the frequencies of IVUS usage at each hospital are summarized in Table 1. The median frequency of IVUS use across the 20 institutions was 10.3%.

Study overview.

This retrospective cohort study analyzed 9752 patients with AMI from the KAMIR, stratified by IVUS use and imaging guidance strategy during PCI. The final study population (n=9752) was stratified into 2 groups based on the institutional median IVUS use rate (10.3%): higher‐use centers (n=5140) and lower‐use centers (n=4612). Within each group, patients were further categorized according to the imaging modality used during PCI: angiography‐guided PCI or IVUS‐guided PCI (Figure 1).

Procedures and Treatment

AMI, including both ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (MI) and non–ST‐segment–elevation MI, was diagnosed according to established guidelines. 9 , 10 All interventional procedures were performed in accordance with current standard practices. The use of IVUS during PCI was at the discretion of the treating cardiologists in the catheterization laboratory.

Aspirin and P2Y12 inhibitors were administered to all patients. Before the procedure, a loading dose of aspirin (300 mg) and either clopidogrel (300 or 600 mg), prasugrel (60 mg), or ticagrelor (180 mg) was given, following the practices of each center. Guideline‐directed medical therapy, tailored to each patient's clinical status, was prescribed at the discretion of the attending physician. This included antiplatelet agents, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, β blockers, and statins.

Study End Points

The primary outcome was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) at 3 years, defined as the composite of all‐cause deaths, any MI, and any coronary revascularization. The secondary outcome was target‐lesion failure (TLF), defined as the composite end point of all cardiac deaths, target‐vessel MI, and ischemia‐driven target lesion revascularization at 3 years. Target‐vessel MI is defined as a MI marked by myocardial necrosis occurring in the vascular area served by a previously treated target vessel. Target‐lesion revascularization was classified as ischemia driven if revascularization procedures were conducted on the target lesion under specific conditions: either in the case of ≥50% angiographic diameter stenosis accompanied by ischemic symptoms or a positive functional study, or when there was ≥70% stenosis without ischemic symptoms or a positive functional study.

Data Acquisition and Statistical Analysis

At the time of admission, details of patients and procedures were documented. Data were collected prospectively at each hospital, electronically encrypted, and recorded on a web‐based case report form within the National Institutes of Health database. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Kyungpook National University Hospital (No: KNUH‐2022‐01‐011), and all patients provided written informed consent.

Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median (interquartile range), according to their distribution, and were compared between groups using independent 2‐sample t tests with Welch's correction or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using Pearson's χ2 test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. As the patient distribution was not uniform across study groups, propensity score (PS) matching was used to adjust for hidden confounding factors. 11 , 12 PS was derived through logistic regression considering clinically important parameters between the 2 groups (age, sex, body mass index, Killip class, final diagnosis, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, previous MI or cerebrovascular accident, history of smoking, left ventricular ejection fraction, estimated glomerular filtration rate, troponin I, creatine kinase–myocardial band, discharge medication, success of PCI, use of transradial approach, use of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, thrombus aspiration, stent diameter and length, stent type, stent number, number of vessels involved, multivessel disease, culprit vessel, and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association lesion type). We used nearest‐neighbor matching with a caliper size set at 0.2 times the SD of the logit‐transformed PS. 13 To complement the PS matching analysis and preserve statistical power, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) using the estimated PS was additionally performed. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences, with a standardized mean difference <0.1 indicating acceptable balance. 14

We estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for the main outcomes using multivariable and propensity score–matched stratified Cox proportional hazards models with robust (Huber–White) sandwich variance estimators. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and no evidence of violation was observed for the IVUS term in the primary models. In the overall cohort, standard errors were clustered by treating hospital, and in the propensity score–matched and IPTW analyses they were clustered by matched set. The adjusted variables in the multivariable Cox model included age, sex, Killip class, body mass index, current smoking status, left ventricular ejection fraction, dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, previous history of MI, previous history of cerebrovascular accident, estimated glomerular filtration rate, use of β blockers, statins, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, transradial approach, stent type, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor, multivessel disease, stent diameter, stent length, multiple stents, and left main disease. Cumulative clinical events at 3 years were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and outcomes were compared using the log‐rank test. To assess use‐related differences, centers were additionally classified into 3 IVUS‐use categories—low (1.6%–11.6%), intermediate (16.7%–36.3%), and high (51.4%–74.9%)—using K‐means clustering, and outcomes were compared across categories (Table S1; Figure S1). 15 Center‐level IVUS use in our data was not uniformly distributed but showed distinct patterns of low, intermediate, and high use; therefore, we used K‐means clustering to group centers with similar IVUS‐use patterns rather than applying arbitrary quantile cut points to better reflect real‐world practice levels across hospitals. To assess whether the association between IVUS guidance and clinical outcomes differed by institutional IVUS experience, we included interaction terms between IVUS use and the 3‐level center use category in the Cox models; global P values for interaction were obtained from Wald tests and are reported in the corresponding figures. A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The final study population included 9752 patients from the registry, with a median follow‐up of 1099 days. The higher‐use IVUS group comprised 5140 patients (52.7%) from 13 institutions, whereas the lower‐use group included 4612 patients (47.3%) from 7 institutions (Table 1).

In higher‐use centers, 1786 patients (34.7%) underwent IVUS‐guided PCI, while 3354 patients (65.3%) underwent angiography‐guided PCI. Compared with the angiography‐guided group, patients treated with IVUS‐guided PCI were younger (63.46±12.61 versus 62.70±12.23 years, P=0.037) and more often men (75.0% versus 78.2%, P=0.012). They also presented less frequently with ST‐segment–elevation MI (58.5% versus 49.2%, P<0.001) and had a higher initial left ventricular ejection fraction (51.07%±10.15% versus 52.89%±9.76%, P<0.001).

In terms of procedural characteristics, the IVUS‐guided group more frequently underwent transradial PCI (34.2% versus 40.3%, P<0.001) and received glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (19.1% versus 21.6%, P=0.038), while thrombus aspiration was performed less often (29.3% versus 23.6%, P<0.001). Notably, this group frequently used stents with larger diameters (≥3 mm), longer lengths (≥35 mm), and used multiple stent implantations (≥2 stents). Regarding lesion characteristics, the IVUS‐guided group had a higher incidence of left main coronary artery disease (1.3% versus 3.8%, P<0.001) (Table 2).

Clinical Outcomes

In the overall cohort from higher‐use centers, the incidence of MACEs was lower in the IVUS‐guided PCI group than in the angiography‐guided group (15.5% [n=276] versus 17.8% [n=597], P=0.036), primarily driven by a reduction in all‐cause death (6.2% [n=111] versus 8.5% [n=285], P=0.004). The incidence of TLF was lower in the IVUS‐guided PCI group (6.4% [n=115] versus 7.8% [n=263], P=0.075); however, this difference was not statistically significant.

Following propensity score matching, the association of IVUS‐guided PCI with lower event rates persisted. The incidence of MACEs remained significantly lower in the IVUS‐guided group (15.3% [n=256] versus 18.5% [n=309], P=0.016). Notably, the incidence of TLF became statistically lower in the IVUS‐guided group after matching (6.3% [n=106] versus 8.3% [n=138], P=0.039), largely attributed to a reduced rate of target‐vessel MI (0.5% [n=9] versus 1.4% [n=23], P=0.021) (Figure 2).

Among all patients from higher‐use centers, IVUS‐guided PCI was associated with a lower risk of MACEs compared with angiography‐guided PCI (HR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.73–0.98]; P=0.03). This association remained consistent in multivariable analysis (adjusted HR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.69–0.93]; P=0.003), the propensity score–matched cohort (HR, 0.82 [95% CI, 0.69–0.96]; P=0.02), and the IPTW‐adjusted analysis (HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.84–0.98]; P=0.02). Similarly, IVUS‐guided PCI was associated with a lower risk of TLF in the multivariable model (adjusted HR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.59–0.93]; P=0.01), the matched cohort (HR, 0.76 [95% CI, 0.59–0.98]; P=0.04), and the IPTW analysis (HR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.55–0.98]; P=0.04) (Table 4). These findings were corroborated by Kaplan–Meier survival analyses (Figure 3).

Subgroup Analysis

Centers were analyzed by the 3 institutional IVUS‐use categories defined in the Methods section. Within each category, we estimated univariable and multivariable Cox HRs for MACEs and TLF in the overall and propensity score–matched cohorts.

Across both cohorts, all 3 categories showed HRs <1.0 for MACEs and TLF; however, estimates in the low category were not statistically significant in any model. Numerically, HRs declined from low → intermediate → high across analyses, with the high category showing the largest relative reduction. For MACEs in the overall cohort, the high category yielded a univariable HR of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.62–0.84; P<0.05) and multivariable HR of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.66–0.84; P<0.05); in the PS‐matched cohort, the corresponding multivariable HR was 0.48 (95% CI, 0.33–0.69; P<0.05). For TLF in the overall cohort, the high category showed a univariable HR of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.52–0.95; P<0.05) and multivariable HR of 0.71 (95% CI, 0.52–0.97; P<0.05); in the PS‐matched cohort, the multivariable HR was 0.44 (95% CI, 0.22–0.88; P<0.05). Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive and multicentered study to determine the impact of institutional experience on the long‐term clinical outcomes of IVUS‐guided PCI in AMI. To our knowledge, this is the first study to suggest that interinstitutional differences in IVUS use may be associated with long‐term clinical outcomes following PCI in AMI.

Several observational studies and randomized controlled trials have consistently reported that IVUS improves patient outcomes. These improvements, including reductions in MACEs and TLF, are thought to be achieved by optimizing stent deployment across preinterventional, peri‐interventional, and postinterventional phases. 16 , 17 However, these findings generally assume uniformity in IVUS expertise across clinicians and institutions. In real‐world practice, IVUS experience varies considerably between individual clinicians, institutions, and even across different countries. For instance, the penetration rate of IVUS in the United States has been estimated to be <5%, which is markedly different from that in the Republic of Korea. 18 Given this variability, it is inappropriate to assume uniform clinical benefits of IVUS across all practice settings.

Recent studies have explored the clinical implications of this variability, with some suggesting that even less experienced operators within a single center may achieve favorable outcomes with IVUS guidance. 19 Both that study and our study share the finding that greater IVUS experience is associated with better patient clinical outcomes. Superficially, the results of the 2 studies may appear to be conflicting. However, the scope, including the patient population, differs between the previous study and our study, suggesting that the results are complementary rather than contradictory. Specifically, the hospital involved in the previous study is one of the most representative institutions in the country with extensive IVUS experience. On the basis of our study, IVUS‐guided PCI at such hospitals would be expected to be associated with more favorable outcomes. Moreover, integrating both studies' results suggests that the advantages of IVUS might be most pronounced for less experienced operators working at centers with accumulated IVUS expertise.

In addition, recent advancements in intravascular imaging, particularly the integration of artificial intelligence, have expanded the potential utility of IVUS in contemporary clinical practice. Current platforms offer artificial intelligence–based automated analysis tools capable of quantifying critical parameters such as minimal stent area and stent expansion, thereby facilitating real‐time procedural optimization. 20 , 21 These innovations, which were not available during the period when this registry was constructed, may further augment the clinical effectiveness of IVUS when combined with institutional experience. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether these technologies primarily amplify procedural precision in high‐experience centers or serve as compensatory aids in limited‐experience settings. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate their differential impact across varying institutional contexts.

Our finding that in higher‐use centers the IVUS‐guided PCI group showed more favorable outcomes, including lower MACEs and TLF, than the angiography‐guided group is consistent with prior reports. However, reductions in MACEs and TLF in prior research were mainly attributed to decreases in hard end points, such as all‐cause death and cardiac death. These results could not be fully explained solely by stent optimization and the reduction of periprocedural complications through IVUS. In higher‐use centers, the observed association between IVUS‐guided PCI and lower TLF appeared to be driven largely by fewer target‐vessel MIs. These findings suggest that, beyond differences in hard end points, IVUS‐guided PCI at higher‐use centers may be associated with additional advantages, such as fewer target‐vessel MIs.

Despite the novel findings, this study presents several considerations that must be carefully interpreted. First, centers were categorized into higher‐use and lower‐use groups according to the institutional median IVUS use rate (10.3%) across the 20 participating hospitals. This threshold was statistically derived and may not represent a universally applicable definition of institutional IVUS practice. A classification based solely on relative use may incompletely reflect institutional expertise; absolute case volume represents an additional dimension of proficiency. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as implying that all PCI centers must exceed this cutoff to achieve favorable outcomes with IVUS. Moreover, given that this threshold was determined using data from Korean centers with relatively high IVUS adoption, its applicability to other health care systems, particularly in regions where IVUS use remains considerably lower, may be limited. Thus, the generalizability of the findings should be interpreted within the context of regional practice patterns and resource availability.

Second, the number of IVUS‐guided cases in centers with limited IVUS experience was relatively small (n=207), leading to a marked difference in group sizes compared with the angiography‐guided cohort. Although we used multivariable adjustment, propensity score matching, and IPTW to mitigate potential confounding, the limited sample size may have reduced statistical power. Importantly, the direction and absolute magnitude of risk reduction for MACEs were similar between higher‐ and lower‐use centers, indicating a consistent association, but statistical significance was reached only in the former. This suggests that the favorable association of IVUS guidance with outcomes may be present across centers yet becomes more apparent and more reliably detectable in institutions with greater IVUS use. For TLF, the absence of statistical significance is also plausibly explained by the small number of events. These findings, together with the center‐level clustering analyses, suggest that institutional use may influence how prominently the association of IVUS guidance with outcomes is observed. Prospective studies, particularly including lower‐use centers, would help to confirm these patterns.

In the center‐level clustering analysis (low, intermediate, high), point estimates generally decreased from low → intermediate → high for both MACEs and TLF, with the high group showing the most consistent patterns across univariable, multivariable, and PS‐matched models. These exploratory findings are compatible with a graded association and suggest that any potential advantage of IVUS guidance may begin to emerge at moderate levels of institutional use, although a strict threshold cannot be determined.

In addition, proficiency and expertise in medical practice are often determined by the frequency and volume of procedures performed. This study similarly assumed that the proportion of IVUS procedures relative to overall PCI volume correlates with institutional expertise. However, this assumption may not always be applicable in real clinical settings. Absolute case volume may also be a critical factor, as centers with high proportions of IVUS use but small absolute numbers may still have limited overall experience. Conversely, there may be paradoxical situations where centers that have historically performed a high volume of PCI procedures and achieved good outcomes may show relatively lower IVUS use, reflecting their preexisting proficiency and success with PCI even before the widespread implementation of IVUS. Accordingly, proportional use should be regarded as an imperfect surrogate rather than a definitive measure of institutional expertise.

Furthermore, subgroup analysis demonstrated that centers with consistent year‐to‐year IVUS use achieved significantly better clinical outcomes compared with those with more variable use. This finding underscores that not only the overall volume of IVUS procedures but also the consistency of application plays a critical role in maximizing the benefits of IVUS‐guided PCI. Nonetheless, the number of patients in the inconsistent use group was relatively small, and the absence of statistical significance for both MACEs and TLF in this subgroup likely reflects limited statistical power. These results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating, warranting confirmation in larger data sets. Taken together, these findings suggest that institutional‐level standardization, including the development of routine imaging protocols and team‐based training, may further enhance procedural efficacy and long‐term outcomes. Thus, promoting structured and sustained IVUS use across PCI‐capable centers may represent an important step toward optimizing contemporary AMI care.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective and observational study based on the KAMIR‐NIH registry. Although we used propensity score matching, IPTW adjustment, and multivariable analysis for key clinical variables to address the limitations of the study design, residual or unmeasured confounding factors cannot be entirely excluded, and the associations observed should not be interpreted as causal. Furthermore, the decision to use IVUS guidance was made by the operator and was not randomized. Some lower‐use centers were large referral hospitals that may have treated more complex cases, which could have influenced IVUS use and outcomes. In addition, although several lesion‐related variables (eg, culprit vessel, multivessel disease, stent characteristics, American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association lesion type) were included in the propensity score model, detailed lesion‐level characteristics (eg, calcium severity, bifurcation involvement, thrombotic burden) were not captured in the registry, and IVUS may have been selectively applied to higher‐complexity lesions in lower‐use centers, introducing potential selection bias. The direction of this potential bias warrants careful consideration. Selective use of IVUS for more complex lesions, known as confounding by indication, would likely bias the observed association toward the null, thereby underestimating the potential benefit of IVUS guidance in lower‐use centers. This provides important context for interpreting the nonsignificant HR for MACEs observed in this group (HR, 0.69 in the PS‐matched cohort). The lack of statistical significance may thus reflect not only limited power but also residual confounding and does not necessarily indicate the absence of a clinically meaningful association. This interpretation aligns with our overall finding that the association between IVUS guidance and favorable outcomes is more consistently and robustly observed in centers with higher and more routine IVUS use. Moreover, standardized clinical criteria for IVUS‐guided stent optimization were not applied in this study. Additionally, due to the inherent limitations of the registry data, specific IVUS parameters such as minimal stent area and stent expansion were not collected, thereby precluding a detailed assessment of the technical quality of IVUS‐guided PCI. Finally, socioeconomic variables such as patient education, medication adherence, and access to cardiac rehabilitation were not collected in the registry, and since higher‐use centers were often large urban hospitals, such unmeasured factors may have contributed to the observed differences in outcomes.

Conclusions

This comprehensive, nationwide cohort study suggests that IVUS‐guided PCI was associated with lower rates of MACEs and TLF, with the association more consistently observed in centers with higher IVUS use. Recognizing the observational design and limited power in lower‐use centers, these findings suggest that institutional experience may augment the observed association of IVUS guidance with outcomes. These results highlight the potential importance of promoting standardized IVUS protocols and institutional training to optimize the role of IVUS in the management of AMI.
