Wealth, health expenditure and cancer: an econometric analysis for European countries
Authors: Bayram Aydın, Emine İlkin Aydın
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe main purpose of this study is to examine the effects of health expenditures and wealth on cancer treatment success in European countries. Cervical cancer data were used for this purpose.MethodsCervical cancer incidence/death rate (CANCER), Gross Domestic Product per capita (GDPP) and Current health expenditure (HEXP) were included as predictors. Cancer data was obtained from WHO European Data Warehouse while health expenditure and wealth datas were obtained from World Bank Development Indicators. Panel regression models, panel ARDL cointegration analysis and Dumitrescu-Hurlin causality analysis were used to define the existence of a statistical relationship between variables.ResultsThe result obtained in the model shows the existence of a positive relationship between cervical cancer and all independent variables (health expenditures and wealth). While cervical cancer treatment success in the European countries is highly sensitive to health expenditure, the effect of wealth on cervical cancer is very weak. According to the findings of the causality analysis, a unidirectional causal relationship from HEXP to CANCER and a unidirectional causal relationship from GDPP to CANCER was determined in analysis. So, independent variables in the model are the cause of cervical cancer.ConclusionThis study provides important evidence for policy makers to allocate relevant and economic resources to healthcare services to succeed in cervical cancer. The determination that health expenditures have a positive effect on the treatment of cervical cancer provides a clue that more efforts should be made regarding the economic accessibility of health services in european countries.Keywords: Health economics, Wealth, GDP per capita, Cancer, Panel data analysis

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide [1]. Although cervical cancer is a type of cancer that can be diagnosed and prevented early, it continues to be one of the most common cancers in women worldwide. The annual number of new cervical cancer cases is estimated to increase from 570,000 to 700,000 between 2018 and 2030, while the annual number of deaths is estimated to increase from 311,000 to 400,000 [2]. The increasing incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer pose a significant health threat and economic burden to health systems [1]. Delay in diagnosis and treatment of cervical cancer may cause this economic burden to increase [3, 4].

Cervical cancer is one of the most common causes of death, especially in low-income and middle-income countries. It is thought that there is a relationship between the incidence of cervical cancer and the socioeconomic status of a country and its people [5]. The highest cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates are in Sub-Saharan Africa, Central America, and Southeast Asia [6], and the lowest in North America, Australia/New Zealand, and Western Asia [7]. These regional differences in the burden of cervical cancer may be due to inequalities in access to vaccination, screening and treatment services, risk factors such as HIV prevalence, and social-economic determinants such as gender and poverty [8]. Therefore, it is critical to assess the financial and economic burden of cervical cancer globally, to make appropriate health care plans and to develop cost-effective strategies [4, 9]. This situation is also clearly seen when comparing the incidence and mortality rates by region. In regions such as Europe, where income levels and health expenditures are high, mortality-to-incidence ratio is at a low level, as can be seen in the data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (2022) [10] in Fig. 1. In addition, the general incidence rate in such regions is low compared to other regions. However, in regions such as Africa, where income levels and health expenditures are low, both the cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates are high. In addition, when looked at in terms of mortality-to-incidence ratio, African regions are at higher levels than the European continent.

Incidence and mortality for cervical cancer according to region

The World Health Organization (WHO) published a global strategy to accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer as a public health problem in 2020 [11]. The aim of this strategy is to fully vaccinate 90% of girls with HPV vaccine by the age of 15 by 2030; to screen 70% of women with a high-sensitivity test at the age of 35 and 45; and to treat 90% of women diagnosed with cervical cancer [12]. However, competing demands for health services and economic, social and political problems prevent the implementation of cervical cancer prevention programs [13]. Economic factors are an obstacle to cervical cancer screening. Low-middle income resource-limited countries account for 84% of the global population, 90% of the disease burden, and 20% of gross domestic product (GDP), but only 12% of healthcare spending [14]. The difference in annual per capita healthcare spending between high income and low-middle income countries is striking. While the annual health expenditure per capita in the United States in 2019 was 10,921 dollars and 9,666 dollars in Switzerland, these figures are around 21 dollars in the Democratic Republic of Congo, 46 dollars in Bangladesh and 64 dollars in India [15].

Health expenditures have an important place in the development agenda of many countries in the world. Improving society’s health not only improves individual quality of life but also impacts the sustainability of economic growth [16]. For example, a society comprised of healthy individuals can contribute to economic growth by increasing labor productivity and reducing healthcare costs [17]. Healthcare expenditures for cancer treatment have increased significantly over the last 20 years. In a study conducted in European Union countries, it was determined that the total cost of cancer was €199 billion in 2018, health care expenditures for cancer care were €103 billion, and €32 billion of this was spent on cancer drugs [18]. In the United States, approximately $183 billion was spent on cancer-related healthcare in 2015, and this amount is estimated to increase by 34% to $246 billion by 2030 [19]. Such statistics and indicators indicate that health expenditures and wealth may be determining factors in the treatment of cervical cancer.

The determinants of cervical cancer can be situated within theoretical frameworks that link socioeconomic factors to health outcomes. According to health economics theory, national wealth and public health expenditure are expected to enhance population health by improving access to preventive care, early diagnosis, and effective treatment [20]. From a social determinants of health perspective, wealth influences health behaviors, and access to healthcare, all of which are critical in preventing cervical cancer [21]. However, the relationship between wealth, health expenditure, and cervical cancer remains theoretically and empirically contested. Some studies demonstrate that increased health spending and higher GDP per capita are associated with reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality due to improved screening coverage and HPV vaccination programs [22, 23]. Conversely, other research shows weak or non-significant associations, highlighting issues such as resource misallocation, regional disparities in service access, or lagged health benefits from expenditure [24, 25]. This controversy reflects a gap in the literature: few studies have conducted econometric analyses to assess these relationships across European countries. This paper addresses this gap by investigating how variations in wealth and health expenditure influence cervical cancer incidence and mortality across Europe.

While numerous studies have explored the relationship between economic indicators and health outcomes, limited research has specifically examined how national wealth and health expenditure influence cervical cancer incidence and mortality across European countries. Most existing literature either focuses on general cancer outcomes or narrows in on cervical cancer without accounting for the broader socioeconomic context. Moreover, cross-country econometric analyses remain scarce, with many studies confined to single-country case studies or descriptive statistics. This paper aims to fill this gap by employing a panel data econometric approach to analyze how variations in GDP per capita and health expenditure correlate with cervical cancer rates across Europe. By integrating economic variables with health outcomes in a comparative European framework, this study offers a novel contribution that enhances our understanding of the economic determinants of cervical cancer and informs more targeted public health strategies.

Literature review

Theoretical foundations

The starting point for the theoretical analysis is the Grossman model of health demand, which conceptualizes health as a durable capital stock that produces healthy time and thus contributes to both utility and productivity [20]. In this model, individuals and governments invest in health to extend the quantity and quality of life. At the macroeconomic level, greater wealth, often proxied by GDP per capita, enhances a country’s capacity to invest in healthcare infrastructure, human capital, and preventive services all of which are essential in addressing diseases such as cervical cancer.

Building on Grossman’s foundation, Rosenzweig and Schultz (1983) developed the Child Health Production Function, which views health as a result of various inputs including income, parental education, and public services such as healthcare access [26]. Though originally applied to child health in developing countries, this framework has broader implications for understanding how different types of capital interact to produce health outcomes. Public health expenditures, within this model, can be considered inputs that raise the marginal productivity of other health investments, particularly in areas requiring organized screening and prevention programs such as cervical cancer.

Also, the health production function framework further builds on the idea that health is an output resulting from various inputs, including medical care, public health interventions, and socioeconomic factors [26]. In this model, public health expenditure plays a critical role in improving population-level health, particularly for diseases that are preventable or manageable through early intervention. Cervical cancer, for instance, is highly responsive to investments in organized screening (Pap tests, HPV testing), vaccination, and health education. Several studies have shown that sustained health expenditure leads to reductions in cancer incidence and mortality when resources are allocated efficiently [27, 28].

While these economic models suggest a generally positive relationship between income, health spending, and health status, empirical evidence in the context of cervical cancer shows mixed results. Some studies suggest that increased national wealth and public health expenditure are associated with improved access to HPV vaccination, screening programs, and early detection, thereby reducing incidence and mortality [22, 23]. However, other research points to inefficiencies in health spending or inequalities in access, which may dilute or even reverse expected health benefits [24, 25]. These inconsistencies underline the need to empirically test how economic factors influence cervical cancer outcomes in different healthcare and policy contexts.

Cancer treatment is highly costly, often exceeding $10,000 per month. These substantial expenses affect not only patients but also their caregivers and families, due to factors such as increased out-of-pocket costs, the long-term and intensive nature of treatment, and its negative effects on employment and income. This economic burden is directly related to the successful outcome of cancer treatment [29, 30]. Furthermore, increased cancer-related morbidity and mortality can lead to slower workforce development and economic growth [31]. In this context, budget-synthetic health policies should be developed to reduce economic and social outcomes, and treatment costs should be effectively managed [32].

According to the Conceptual Framework in Fig. 2, health outcomes are influenced by multiple factors operating at the individual (patient), organizational (provider), and policy (system) levels. Socioeconomic status (wealth), in particular, is recognized as a key factor affecting health at the individual level, with lower wealth often linked to poorer health outcomes and higher mortality. Exploring this link from a system-level perspective may help to correct the current mismatch between what health systems invest and the outcomes they produce an issue central to both national and local health policy agendas [33].

Conceptual framework for wealth, health expenditure and cancer outcomes relationship [33]

The link between economic conditions, public health investment and disease outcomes is an important research topic for health economics and development studies. This article draws on interdisciplinary theoretical frameworks that link macroeconomic indicators specifically national wealth and health expenditure to public health outcomes, focusing on cervical cancer incidence and mortality across European countries. In this context, health expenditures play a central role, especially for preventable diseases such as cervical cancer.

This study builds upon these theoretical foundations by applying them to the case of cervical cancer across European countries, where health systems vary significantly in terms of structure, funding, and equity. By analyzing the link between wealth, health expenditure, and cervical cancer incidence and mortality using econometric methods, this study seeks to empirically assess the extent to which economic resources are effectively translated into cancer prevention and control.

Empirical literature review

The empirical relationship between economic factors (such as wealth and healthcare expenditures) and cancer outcomes has received increasing attention in global health and health economics research. However, limited studies have examined how macroeconomic conditions affect cancer incidence, survival, and mortality, with results varying depending on cancer type, region, and methodological approach. The literature has not examined the relationship between countries’ investments in their healthcare systems, their overall economic well-being, and the success of cervical cancer treatment, creating a research gap. This study aims to fill this gap by systematically examining the impact of macro-level factors such as healthcare expenditure and wealth on cervical cancer treatment success.

In this section, cervical cancer data were used to examine the effects of healthcare expenditures and wealth on cancer treatment success in European countries. Therefore, the empirical literature section focuses on studies on cervical cancer and key studies that can be used to draw conclusions on this subject.

A growing body of research examines how economic variables such as national wealth, public health expenditure, and economic shocks influence cancer-related outcomes at the population level. Sung et al. (2021) presented comprehensive global cancer statistics, emphasizing that cancer burden, particularly for cervical cancer, is disproportionately high in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to inadequate screening and treatment infrastructure [1]. Similarly, Batouli et al. (2014) highlights how disparities in national healthcare resources such as health expenditure, infrastructure, and physician density are strongly associated with cancer mortality outcomes, with low- and middle-income countries experiencing significantly worse survival rates compared to high-income nations [34]. Maruthappu et al. (2016) also showed that economic crises negatively impact cancer outcomes in OECD countries, particularly when health spending is reduced [35]. Jung et al. (2019) found GDP and health expenditure to be inversely associated with cancer mortality, though effects differed by country and cancer type [36]. Focusing on cervical cancer, Bray et al. (2012) and Vaccarella et al. (2013) emphasized that socioeconomic development plays a crucial role in lowering cervical cancer incidence and mortality [22, 23]. These studies demonstrated that higher the human development ındex (HDI) levels and public investment in organized screening (e.g., Pap smears, HPV vaccination) correlate strongly with improved outcomes. Summary of key studies

Some studies in the literature are centered around the risk factors of cervical cancer. For example, a study by Le et al. (2025) examined the disease burden and main risk factors for cervical cancer in Vietnam between 1990 and 2021 and also focused on projecting potential trends through 2050 [37]. A retrospective study in Mexico examined the relationship between sociodemographic characteristics and survival rates of women with cervical cancer. The study found that women with low income, low education, and inadequate health insurance had lower cervical cancer survival rates [38]. A study by Doubeni et al. (2012) found that individuals living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas had higher mortality rates compared to those living in more advantaged areas [39]. Lortet-Tieulent et al. (2020) evaluated the relationship between national socioeconomic indicators and cancer incidence and mortality rates for all cancers and 27 specific cancer types in 175 countries. The study found that as the socioeconomic development index increases, cancer incidence increases for many cancer types, while mortality rates generally remain stable. The study also explained that this may be due to the greater availability of early diagnosis and effective treatment options for cancer in high‐income countries [40].

Despite this growing literature, few studies have conducted robust econometric analyses focusing solely on European countries. This is a significant gap, given the variation in health systems, wealth distribution, and cancer control policies across Europe. Moreover, while much of the literature identifies broad correlations, fewer studies disentangle the specific impacts of health expenditure and national wealth on cervical cancer outcomes. This study contributes to the empirical literature by applying a panel data econometric approach to analyze the influence of GDP per capita and health expenditure on cervical cancer incidence and mortality across European countries. By doing so, it offers a more detailed understanding of how economic variables affect health outcomes within the specific context of Europe’s diverse healthcare landscapes.

Methods

Data specification and model

This study investigated the impact of health expenditures and wealth on cervical cancer for European countries. For this purpose, cervical cancer data was used as the dependent variable while health expenditures and wealth datas were used as the independent variables in the model. The study covers annual data for the period 2000–2019 for 12 European countries. Other European countries were excluded from the panel due to missing data. The period up to 2019 represents a relatively undisturbed socio-economic environment, which allows us to identify underlying patterns without the confounding effects of recent large-scale disruptions (events such as the post-COVID period, the Ukraine-Russia conflict, etc.). The countries included in the study are Austria, Czechia, Germany, Denmark, Finland, United Kingdom, Hungary, Iceland, Netherlands, Poland, Slovenia and Sweden. All sample countries in our study have a per capita income above $13,205, and according to the World Bank country classifications by income, the countries in the panel are included in the high-income group [41]. In the analysis, cervical cancer data was used in terms of (cervix uteri incidence/death), wealth data was used in terms of (GDP per capita constant 2015 US$) and health expenditure data was used in terms of (Current health expenditure % of GDP). Specifically, the main explanatory variables wealth (measured by GDP per capita) and health expenditure (as a share of GDP or per capita) were selected based on a substantial body of literature that links economic prosperity and investment in healthcare to health outcomes, including cancer prevention and treatment [33, 34]. Cervical cancer data was obtained from WHO European Data Warehouse while health expenditure and wealth datas were obtained from World Bank Development Indicators. Eviews 10 and Stata 17 package programs were used to perform econometric analyses. All data and variables specifications are shown in Table 2.

Results

Diagnosis and preliminary tests for panel data

Panel data analyses reveal observations that are repeated over time. Therefore, in order to choose the correct analysis; cross-sectional dependence, serial correlation and heteroscedasticity problems must be tested before the analysis. According to the results obtained, it is necessary to use the correct robust estimator [46].

When the diagnosis results in Table 4 are examined; according to the results of Friedman’s test for cross-sectional independence, the null hypothesis is accepted at the 1% significance level. The null hypothesis of Friedman’s test states that there is no cross-sectional dependence in the model. This indicates the absence of cross-sectional dependence in the data set of the study. In the case of autocorrelation tests, the null hypothesis accepts the absence of autocorrelation in the model. According to the Wooldridge test results, the F statistic is insignificant at the 5% level, which shows that there is not autocorrelation in the data set. In the Levene, Brown and Forsythe test, the null hypothesis states that there is homoscedasticity in the series, while the alternative hypothesis states that there is heteroscedasticity in the model. According to the results in Table 4, the chi2 statistics in the model is significant at the 1% level, which shows that there is heteroscedasticity in the data set.

In order to determine the appropriate method, it is first necessary to test whether there is a unit effect in the model. LR, LM and F tests are used for this purpose. According to the results shown in Table 5; The null hypothesis that there is no unit effect in the model was rejected in all three tests. The common results obtained from the three tests show that there is a unit effect in the model. The presence of unit effect in the model shows that the Pooled method, called the classical model, is not suitable for this model. It is appropriate to use fixed effects or random effects models for our study. However, the results obtained from the diagnosis tests show that there is deviation from the assumptions in the model. Therefore, estimators that are robust to assumption problems should be used for random effects and fixed effects models. In this context, the Eicker-Huber-White Robust Variance Estimator and the Driscoll-Kraay Standard Error Estimator were used in this study.

In the model of our study, CANCER is dependent; HEXP and GDPP are independent variables. According to the results in Table 6, while the GDPP variable was statistically insignificant at 5% level in the standard random effects model, it became statistically significant when the model was estimated with the Eicker-Huber-White Robust Variance Estimator. The R-Square value is highly significant for panel regression. This value shows that approximately 25% of the variation in CANCER is explained by independent variables. Similar results were found in the fixed-effects model and the Driscoll-Kraay Standard Error Estimator model. Panel ARDL cointegration analysis results and long-run findings

In order to select the appropriate cointegration analysis and unit root tests to be used in the model, the correlation between units should be tested firstly. The Pesaran CD (2004) test, which gives the correlation results between units, is generally used in the literature.

The basic hypothesis of the Pesaran CD (2004) test states that there is no correlation between units in the series, while the alternative hypothesis states that there is correlation between units in the series. When Table 7 is examined, it is observed that the Pesaran CD test probability value is below the 5% significance level for all series. As a result, there is cross-sectional dependence between units in the HEXP, GDPP and CANCER series. In such cases, it is appropriate to use the CIPS unit root test, which takes into account the cross-sectional dependency. It can be interpreted that when GDPP is increased by 1 unit, CANCER will increase by approximately 0.0001 on average. The coefficient obtained shows that the effect of wealth on cervical cancer treatment success is very weak.

As emphasized by Angrist and Pischke (2014), establishing true economic causality requires a reliable identification strategy that isolates exogenous variation in explanatory variables [47]. Accordingly, our findings show that changes in health expenditures and wealth precede and help predict variation in cervical cancer outcomes across European countries, suggesting the possibility of a causal relationship between the parameters.

Discussion

Cervical cancer is a common cancer globally and poses a significant health threat and economic burden on healthcare systems [1]. Although there are not many studies on the subject in the literature, pioneering studies suggest that a screening program can reduce the incidence of cervical cancer. Shen, Long and Wu (2023) [42] used the mortality-to-incidence ratio in their study on cervical cancer and found that health expenditures were associated with this ratio. In another study, Chahoud, Semaan and Rieber (2016) [33] showed in their analysis that health expenditures and GDP per capita were significantly associated with the decrease in Cervical cancer mortality-to-incidence ratio. In this study, as a contribution to these works, it has been statistically proven that health expenditures are effective on cervical cancer. In this context, investment, expenditure and support to be made in health are beneficial for cervical cancer. In addition, welfare and wealth are effective on cervical cancer. Both parameters positively affect the success of cancer treatment.

The findings of our analysis reveal a positive relationship between cervical cancer and both independent variables health expenditure and wealth with health expenditure exhibiting a stronger association than wealth. This suggests that, in the context of European countries, increased investment in healthcare systems has a more direct and significant impact on cervical cancer outcomes than overall national wealth. This result aligns with previous empirical studies emphasizing the crucial role of healthcare spending in cancer prevention and treatment. For example, van den Akker-van Marle et al. (2003) and Vaccarella et al. (2013) demonstrate that enhanced health expenditure correlates with improved cervical cancer screening rates and earlier diagnosis, which are essential for successful treatment and reduced mortality [23, 48]. Similarly, Arbyn et al. (2020) highlight that countries with well-funded public health systems tend to achieve higher coverage of HPV vaccination and organized screening programs, both of which are vital in reducing cervical cancer incidence [49]. The relatively weak effect of wealth may be explained by the fact that GDP per capita is a broader economic measure that does not directly reflect healthcare access or quality; thus, its influence on disease-specific outcomes like cervical cancer is more indirect. This observation is also consistent with findings from research by Jemal et al. (2010), who noted that while wealthier countries generally experience lower cervical cancer mortality, it is the targeted public health interventions often made possible by adequate health budgets that drive these improvements [50]. Furthermore, our results resonate with the socio-economic health model proposed by Wilkinson and Marmot (2003), which posits that health outcomes are shaped more by social determinants and public policy (such as healthcare funding) than by aggregate national wealth alone [51]. However, some divergence from other studies can be noted. For instance, Singh & Jemal (2017) report a stronger association between income level and cervical cancer mortality across global regions, which may be attributed to the inclusion of lower-income countries where economic development directly influences healthcare infrastructure [52]. In contrast, our study focuses solely on European countries, where baseline wealth levels are relatively high and differences in healthcare outcomes are more attributable to how resources are allocated rather than to wealth alone. This nuanced interpretation underscores the importance of health policy choices and targeted health investment, particularly in preventive care, over mere economic prosperity in improving cervical cancer outcomes.

The incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer worldwide vary depending on the development levels of countries and cancer screening programs [42]. In Western developed countries, due to the widespread use of HPV vaccination and screening programs, the incidence of cervical cancer is decreasing, albeit slowly. However, the incidence of cervical cancer in low-middle income developing countries is high due to unequal distribution of health resources, few health financing channels, lack of policy support and many other reasons [53, 54]. In terms of cervical cancer, early diagnosis and treatment can be started by screening precancerous lesions with Pap smear and HPV test [55]. While early detection of cancer allows individuals to receive more simplified treatment, late detection can affect individuals’ quality of life and mortality rates, causing them to deal with more complex treatment methods such as hysterectomy, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [42, 56, 57]. This level of complexity in cancer treatments can be a burden on the economy of countries.

Cancer screening and control programs used by countries should be comprehensive and include surveillance, primary prevention, early diagnosis, treatment and palliative care. Cancer screening control programs have many benefits for countries, both in terms of public health and economy [58]. Ensuring the detection of new cancers and preventing related deaths, and reducing the direct and indirect costs of cancer care can be counted among these benefits [31, 58, 59]. More support for health expenditures in developed and affluent countries, the existence of early cancer screening and HPV vaccination programs, and the availability of high-level treatment options for cancer may affect the low incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer in these countries [42]. In this study examining European countries, it was determined that the welfare level of the countries affected the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer, and that the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer were lower in countries with higher welfare levels.

HDI is a summary measure of average socioeconomic development, life expectancy, education, and gross national income. The HDI is the most widely used measure to distinguish a country’s comprehensive development status [60]. For example, when the literature is examined; A study investigating the trends in the incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer worldwide found inverse correlations between HDI and cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates [61]. In the study conducted by Arbyn et al. (2018) [62], it was determined that countries with low HDI had high rates of incidence and mortality, while countries with very high HDI had low rates of incidence and mortality. These results in the literature are consistent with the findings of this study. In this study, it was statistically determined that economic investment in the health sector was effective in reducing cervical cancer incidence and mortality. In order for countries to reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates, it is very important for them to invest in and disseminate cervical cancer screening programs and to announce the preventive HPV vaccine to the public [63].

HPV vaccine is effective in protecting against infection, preventing the development of precancerous lesions, and reducing the incidence of cervical cancer [64, 65]. It is recommended that in order for HPV vaccines to be highly effective, they should be administered to the target group at the appropriate time, before they encounter infection [41]. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) in the United States recommends routine HPV vaccination for all females and males between the ages of 11–12 and states that it can be administered from the age of 9 [41, 66, 67]. However, HPV vaccination rates of countries vary according to their economic conditions and societies’ attitudes towards vaccination [68]. It has been determined that some European countries, except Sweden, implement the vaccination program at a rate of 80% to reduce the risk of cervical cancer [69]. As of July 2023, it was determined that among the 194 WHO member countries, 131 countries included the HPV vaccine in their national immunization program, 3 countries partially included it, and 60 countries have not yet taken any initiative regarding the HPV vaccine [70]. Religious beliefs and cultural factors have been found to be obstacles to the introduction and inclusion of the HPV vaccine in vaccination programs in Saudi Arabia, Qatar and other countries in the Middle East [71]. As seen in the literature, in order to provide protection against cervical cancer, countries need to raise awareness about the HPV vaccine and include the HPV vaccine in their national vaccination programs.

Although regular screening of women for cervical cancer is very effective in detecting precancerous lesions, it has been determined that approximately 50–90% of women have never had cervical cancer screening in their lives [72, 73]. Poverty, lack of education, and gender inequality significantly impact a woman’s access to cervical cancer prevention and treatment services. In many countries, women do not have access to regular screening services, thus reducing the likelihood of accessing early and effective treatment [74]. Pap smear test, HPV test or both tests can be used in the detection of cervical cancer. Pap smear test is a low-cost screening test used to detect cervical cancer [75]. When looking at the results of some meta-analysis studies conducted worldwide; It was determined that the regular cervical cancer screening rate was 46.52% in Iran [76], 14.79% in Ethiopia [77], 62.3% in High Middle Income Pacific countries [78] and 21.4% in China [79]. Again, a qualitative study in the literature identified the obstacles to women having a Pap smear test as the weakness of the health system, difficulty in accessing health services, low health literacy and socio-cultural factors [72]. Therefore, countries should support the implementation of screening tests such as pap smear by introducing screening tests to the public to prevent cervical cancer, which is a public health problem, and by eliminating the factors that prevent the implementation of the tests.

The differences in cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates around the world are quite significant and it is closely related to the socio-economic development of the country, health infrastructure and the implementation of effective screening and vaccination programs. These differences need to be eliminated and access to early diagnosis and treatment services needs to be facilitated worldwide. Expanding effective screening and vaccination programs, raising awareness, and addressing socioeconomic barriers are essential in cervical cancer treatment success. Consequently, countries allocating adequate budgets for health expenditures and investing in national screening programs (such as Pap smears and HPV testing) can reduce cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is conducted at the macro (country) level using aggregate data, which may mask significant within-country disparities and introduce the risk of ecological fallacy where relationships observed at the group level may not accurately reflect individual-level associations. Second, although the incidence-to-death ratio was used as a proxy for cervical cancer treatment success, this measure does not capture important factors such as screening coverage, HPV vaccination rates, or the stage at diagnosis data which were not uniformly available across countries. Third, while panel data methods and causality tests (including panel ARDL and Dumitrescu-Hurlin) were applied to strengthen the analysis, the potential for omitted variable bias remains, as variables such as behavioral risk factors, health system capacity, and education levels could not be included due to data constraints. Finally, our measure of health expenditure reflects total current spending and does not distinguish between allocations for preventive care, screening programs, or cancer-specific treatment services. This limits the ability to identify which components of expenditure are most effective in reducing cervical cancer mortality. Despite these limitations, the study provides meaningful insights into the macroeconomic determinants of cervical cancer outcomes and highlights the role of health investment in improving cancer care across Europe.

Conclusions

From theoretical perspective, this study contributes to the existing literature by examining the macroeconomic determinants of cervical cancer outcomes across European countries, focusing on the roles of national wealth and healthcare expenditure. Our findings offer a nuanced perspective: while both variables show a positive relationship with cervical cancer outcomes, healthcare expenditure demonstrates a far more substantial and statistically significant impact than overall wealth. This result adds theoretical depth to the health economics and public health literature by reinforcing the idea that structural investments in health systems not just general economic development are central to disease prevention and treatment effectiveness. By focusing on a specific cancer type (cervical cancer) in a relatively homogeneous group of high-income countries, our study fills a gap in the literature unlike studies that often focus on broad health outcomes.

From a policy perspective, our results strongly suggest that strategic allocation of resources toward health-specific spending particularly in preventive services such as cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination is more impactful than relying on general economic growth to improve public health outcomes. European health authorities and decision-makers can leverage this evidence to justify sustained or increased investment in cancer screening programs, awareness campaigns, and healthcare accessibility. The relatively weak association between GDP per capita and cervical cancer outcomes indicates that economic prosperity alone is insufficient to guarantee improved cancer control. Thus, health managers should prioritize efficient spending, ensure equitable access to care, and reinforce early detection systems to maximize outcomes regardless of a country’s wealth level.

While this study provides valuable insights, it also opens several avenues for future research. First, incorporating more granular data such as education levels or healthcare infrastructure quality could enrich the analysis and clarify the mechanisms through which health spending translates into better outcomes. Second, comparative studies across other types of cancers or other world regions could help assess the generalizability of our findings. Finally, applying different econometric methods could better account for time-invariant heterogeneity and dynamic effects over time.
