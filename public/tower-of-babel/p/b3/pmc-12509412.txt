Serum vitamin D and lipid profile differences between breastfed and Formula-fed infants
Authors: Sara Ghazi Zadeh, Hamid Reza Rahimi, Melika Malek, Kiana Rahimi, Maryam Mohammadi-Bajgiran, Gordon A Ferns, Khosrow Adeli, Reza Assaran-Darban, Majid Ghayour‐Mobarhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe serum levels of some biochemical markers reflect an individual’s health status, particularly during infancy. This study aimed to compare the levels of serum vitamin D and lipid profile between two groups of infants: those exclusively breastfed and those fed with infant formula.MethodsThis cross-sectional study enrolled a total of 160 pediatric participants (70 males, 90 females) aged between 3 days and 30 months (average age: 10.11 months) after obtaining informed consent. Serum levels of Vitamin D, LDL, HDL, TG, and COL were analyzed using routine methods on an Alpha Classic AT-Plus analyser.ResultsInfants consuming infant formula were found to have significantly higher levels of serum vitamin D compared to those exclusively breastfed (p < 0.001). Moreover, the levels of various lipid profile factors, with the exception of triglycerides, were significantly higher in breastfed infants compared to formula-fed infants (p < 0.05).ConclusionOur findings indicate that infants in Iran who are exclusively breastfed may receive inadequate amounts of vitamin D, falling below the minimum recommended intake as well as the recommended dietary intake, resulting in an increased susceptibility to vitamin D deficiency, insufficient bone mineralization, and conditions such as rickets. Conversely, infants fed with formula milk exhibited higher serum vitamin D levels than their breastfed counterparts. Moreover, our study highlights the differential serum levels of lipid profile observed between infants consuming infant formula and those consuming breast milk.Keywords: Infant feeding, Breast milk, Formula milk, Metabolic markers, HDL, TG, LDL, Vitamin d, Cholesterol, Lipid profile, Comparative analysis

Introduction

Infant nutrition during the early stages of life plays a critical role in shaping immediate and long-term health outcomes. Breast milk is widely recognized as the optimal source of nutrition due to its unique combination of nutrients and bioactive components that support immune development, digestion, and cognitive growth [1, 2]. However, concerns remain regarding its adequacy in supplying certain micronutrients—most notably, vitamin D and vitamin K [3, 4] .

Vitamin D is essential for bone mineralization, immune modulation, and overall growth. Exclusively breastfed infants may receive less than the minimum recommended daily intake of vitamin D due to the naturally low concentration of this vitamin in human milk [4–7]. Consequently, such infants are at increased risk for vitamin D deficiency, which can lead to insufficient bone mineralization and rickets [8, 9] .

In contrast, most infant formulas are fortified with vitamin D, offering a potential protective effect against such deficiencies [5–15]. Meanwhile, the lipid profile—which includes total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL)—has emerged as an important indicator of early metabolic health. Some studies suggest that lipid levels in infancy may be associated with the risk of developing obesity, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular disease later in life [6, 7, 14] .

Several observational studies have reported favorable associations between higher serum 25(OH)D concentrations and improved lipid profiles, including reductions in total cholesterol (TC) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels, as well as increases in high-density lipoprotein (HDL) [16, 17]. While randomized controlled trials offer mixed results, some evidence, particularly in individuals with type 2 diabetes, suggests that vitamin D supplementation can lead to modest but significant improvements in lipid metabolism [17]. These findings point to a broader systemic role for vitamin D, linking early nutritional interventions such as fortified formula feeding not only to immediate infant health but also to long-term cardiometabolic outcomes.

Notably, the fat composition and energy density of breast milk and infant formula differ considerably [3, 12], yet few studies have systematically compared their effects on serum lipid profiles in infants. Moreover, vitamin D status and lipid metabolism are both influenced by nutritional intake and metabolic activity, but most existing research explores these factors independently. Investigating vitamin D levels and lipid markers together may provide a more integrated view of infant metabolic health, especially during a critical window of growth and development.

Given the limited regional data and differences in infant nutritional practices, this study aimed to determine whether serum vitamin D and lipid profile levels significantly differ between breastfed and formula-fed infants in Iran. Understanding these differences may help inform local pediatric guidelines and early interventions to support optimal metabolic health during infancy.

Methods

A total of approximately 600 infants, aged between 3 days and 30 months, were initially identified through health records from five healthcare centers in Mashhad, Iran. To ensure the inclusion of only healthy participants, infants with known medical conditions, low birth weight, premature birth, or any recent infections were excluded. Detailed interviews with parents and thorough reviews of health records were conducted to confirm exclusive feeding status. Only infants who had been exclusively breastfed or exclusively formula-fed since birth were included in the final analysis.

After applying these strict eligibility criteria, 160 infants (80 breastfed and 80 formula-fed) were enrolled in the study (Fig. 1: Flowchart of participant recruitment and enrollment). All enrolled infants were confirmed to be healthy based on pediatric health records and clinical evaluations. Additionally, all participants were receiving routine iron supplementation (iron drops) in accordance with national pediatric guidelines.

Flowchart of participant selection. A total of 160 healthy infants (80 breastfed, 80 formula-fed) were included following consent and blood sample analysis from 600 participants

Infants with mixed feeding patterns were excluded to maintain group homogeneity. Although older infants who had started complementary foods were included, no formal dietary recall or nutrient intake analysis was conducted regarding their solid food consumption. For breastfeeding mothers, a questionnaire was used to collect information on their weekly intake of protein-rich foods and omega-3 sources. However, the classification of infant feeding groups in this study was based solely on the type of infant feeding, regardless of maternal dietary intake.

The flowchart illustrates the recruitment process of infants for the study. Enrollment took place between January 2021 and March 2021, after obtaining informed consent. Using a cluster sampling method, participants were randomly selected from four healthcare centers (Centers 1, 2, 3, and 5) in Mashhad and its suburbs.

Blood sampling was performed via antecubital venipuncture using V-Tube™ between 8:00 and 10:00 AM. Approximately 4 mL of blood was collected into plain vacuum tubes following a standardized protocol. The samples were centrifuged at 14,563 g for 10 min at room temperature (25 °C) using a Hettich model D-78,532 centrifuge. Serum samples were processed within 4 h of collection and stored at -80 °C until analysis [18, 19].

The analysis of metabolic markers was conducted using commercial kits (Pars Azmon) on an Alpha Classic-AT Plus automatic analyzer [20]. The biochemical markers analyzed included total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and fasting blood glucose (FSG).

The reference intervals and clinical cut-off values for each biochemical marker were defined based on age-specific pediatric reference data, primarily from the CALIPER study and previous Iranian population-based research [2, 4] For instance, vitamin D deficiency was defined as < 20 ng/mL, insufficiency as 20–30 ng/mL, and sufficiency as > 30 ng/mL. Similar age-adjusted thresholds were applied for LDL, HDL, TG, and total cholesterol according to published pediatric guidelines.

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 3.4.1) in accordance with the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines, specifically the EP28A3c guideline. Age and gender partitions were statistically evaluated using the Harris and Boyd method, which estimates the statistical significance of the difference in mean and standard deviation (mean ± standard deviation) between two subgroups. The serum levels of LDL, HDL, TG, COL, and vitamin D were measured using the Alpha Classic AT-Plus automatic analyzer [21, 22] .

Our research, under Ethical Code 971,176, was conducted with the informed consent of the parents. Participant information within the project was recorded with their explicit permission and stored securely in the center’s archives using numerical encryption. This study has been officially registered with the number IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1398.498. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments regarding research involving human subjects.

Results

In this study, a total of 160 healthy infants were analyzed, equally divided between exclusively breastfed and formula-fed groups. The comparison focused on serum levels of vitamin D and lipid profile markers including total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides. Since the data did not follow a normal distribution, p-values were calculated using non-parametric statistical tests to ensure valid group comparisons.

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, female infants, serum HDL levels were slightly higher in breastfed girls (mean: 47.64 mg/dL) compared to formula-fed girls (46.10 mg/dL), while LDL levels were nearly identical (76.24 vs. 76.28 mg/dL). Triglycerides were lower in breastfed girls (117.09 mg/dL) than in formula-fed girls (127.89 mg/dL), and total cholesterol levels showed minimal variation. However, as shown in Table 3 statistically significant difference was observed only in serum vitamin D levels, which were markedly higher in formula-fed girls (30.14 mg/dL) than in their breastfed counterparts (20.31 mg/dL; p = 0.000). Other parameters, including cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and TG, did not demonstrate significant group differences.

In the pooled analysis of both genders (Tables 7 and 8), serum vitamin D consistently showed the greatest disparity, with higher concentrations in formula-fed infants (28.85 ± 11.11 mg/dL) than in breastfed infants (20.32 ± 9.81 mg/dL). Lipid profile differences were less pronounced: triglyceride levels were slightly higher in formula-fed infants (119.27 vs. 117.87 mg/dL), whereas HDL, LDL, and cholesterol were marginally higher in breastfed infants. However, these differences were not statistically significant and fell within acceptable error margins.

Gender appeared to influence some serum markers, particularly HDL and TG. Breastfed boys exhibited notably higher HDL levels, while triglyceride trends varied by sex: TG was higher in formula-fed girls but lower in formula-fed boys compared to their breastfed counterparts. These findings suggest a possible interaction between feeding method and infant sex in shaping early metabolic profiles.

Discussion

Breast milk is known to provide a wide range of essential nutrients for infants, although it lacks sufficient amounts of certain vitamins, particularly vitamins D and K. Several studies have shown that exclusively breastfed infants often receive less than the minimum recommended intake of vitamin D and fall significantly short of established dietary requirements [21–23]. As a result, these infants are at increased risk for vitamin D deficiency, impaired bone mineralization, and related conditions such as rickets [3, 9, 22] .

In the present study, we assessed serum vitamin D levels in two groups of infants: those exclusively breastfed and those exclusively formula-fed. Our findings demonstrated that formula-fed infants had significantly higher levels of vitamin D compared to their breastfed counterparts. This result is consistent with earlier studies conducted in Iran and elsewhere. For example, Badakhshan et al. [4] in a national study in Iran, reported vitamin D insufficiency in a large proportion of exclusively breastfed infants. Similar results have been observed in studies conducted in North America and Europe, where vitamin D-fortified formula contributes to higher serum levels in formula-fed infants [2, 9, 11, 13, 15] .

It is important to consider that reference intervals for vitamin D and lipid profile markers are influenced by various physiological and environmental factors, including age, gender, geographic location, sun exposure, and developmental stage [21–23]. In our study, such differences were also noted between age groups and sexes, which is consistent with previous pediatric population studies.

We also examined serum lipid profiles and found significant differences between feeding groups. Formula-fed infants had generally lower levels of both HDL and LDL cholesterol compared to breastfed infants. This finding aligns with previous reports suggesting that breast milk may influence early lipid metabolism differently from formula milk [21, 22, 24]. Some studies have indicated that breastfed infants tend to exhibit slightly higher cholesterol levels in early life, which may have long-term programming effects on lipid metabolism [25] .

The composition and distribution of human milk lipids are distinct from those found in milk from other mammals or commercial infant formulas. Understanding these differences is crucial, as lipids play a central role in infant growth, brain development, and metabolic programming. Advances in lipidomics and analytical technologies have greatly contributed to characterizing the complex structure of human milk lipids and their biological significance [26, 27] .

Despite these insights, our study has several important limitations. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 160) limits the generalizability of the findings. While our sampling was based on strict eligibility criteria, a larger and more diverse sample would provide more robust estimates.

Second, although we carefully classified infants based on exclusive feeding status, we did not fully control for potential confounding variables, such as parental education, maternal health, or household socioeconomic status—factors known to influence both feeding practices and infant health outcomes [25] .

Third, while we excluded infants with mixed feeding patterns, we included some older infants who had begun consuming complementary foods. However, we did not perform dietary recalls or quantify nutrient intake from solid foods, which may have impacted serum lipid and vitamin D levels in these infants.

Fourth, maternal dietary intake was only partially assessed via a basic questionnaire focusing on protein and omega-3 sources. We did not measure maternal vitamin D intake, supplementation, or sun exposure, which could have influenced breast milk quality and consequently infant serum levels.

Moreover, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits causal inference. Longitudinal studies are necessary to understand whether the observed differences in lipid and vitamin D profiles persist over time or influence long-term health outcomes.

Finally, although we referenced standard pediatric reference ranges (e.g., CALIPER), we did not compare our findings with local or regional biochemical reference intervals. This limits the contextual relevance of the results. Comparing with existing national datasets would enhance the clinical interpretability and applicability of our results in Iranian settings.

In conclusion, this study identified statistically significant differences in serum vitamin D and lipid profile markers between breastfed and formula-fed infants. Vitamin D levels were significantly higher in the formula-fed group (p < 0.001), whereas breastfed infants showed slightly higher HDL and LDL levels. These findings highlight the need for targeted vitamin D supplementation strategies for exclusively breastfed infants and underscore the importance of infant feeding choices on early biochemical health markers [24, 28, 29].

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence on the biochemical disparities between breastfed and formula-fed infants in Iran, particularly with regard to serum vitamin D and lipid profile markers. Our results demonstrate that formula-fed infants exhibit significantly higher serum vitamin D levels than their breastfed counterparts—a finding that reinforces concerns over the insufficiency of vitamin D in exclusive breastfeeding without supplementation. These disparities are clinically meaningful, as vitamin D plays a critical role in bone development and immune regulation, and its deficiency during infancy is linked to long-term skeletal disorders such as rickets.

In contrast, breastfed infants presented marginally higher levels of HDL, LDL, and total cholesterol compared to those fed with infant formula, though most differences remained within clinical error ranges and lacked statistical significance. Notably, triglyceride levels showed a divergent pattern, with higher values in formula-fed girls but lower in formula-fed boys, indicating a possible gender-related interaction with dietary fat metabolism in early life.

Overall, the data suggest a nuanced relationship between infant feeding modality and early metabolic health. While breast milk offers numerous immunological and developmental advantages, it may fall short in delivering adequate amounts of certain micronutrients—most notably vitamin D—underscoring the necessity for routine supplementation in exclusively breastfed infants. Furthermore, early variations in lipid markers may reflect foundational metabolic programming, although their long-term implications require further longitudinal exploration.

Given these findings, public health strategies should emphasize vitamin D fortification or supplementation for breastfed infants and consider integrating tailored nutritional guidance into pediatric care protocols. Future research should expand on these insights with larger, multicenter cohorts and include long-term follow-up to better understand the trajectory of metabolic outcomes influenced by early feeding practices.
