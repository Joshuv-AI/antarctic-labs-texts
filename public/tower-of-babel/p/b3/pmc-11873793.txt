Kidney function and bone mineral density in chronic kidney disease patients
Authors: Dong Hoon Kang, Cheol Ho Park, Hyung Woo Kim, Jung Tak Park, Seung Hyeok Han, Jayoun Kim, Jong Cheol Jeong, Yaeni Kim, Soo Wan Kim, Kook-Hwan Oh, Shin-Wook Kang, Tae-Hyun Yoo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBone mineral density (BMD) predicts fracture risk in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and in the general population. However, few studies have investigated risk factors for bone loss in patients with CKD. The aim of this study was to investigate whether renal function is associated with the rate of BMD decline.MethodsA prospective cohort study included 1006 patients with CKD stages 2–4 between 2011 and 2016. BMD was measured using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry at baseline and 4 years. The eGFR was measured 2–6 times during the 4-year follow-up. We analyzed the decline in bone mineral density according to CKD stage and further compared the rate of BMD decline according to eGFR trajectories at each stage.ResultsAdvanced CKD stage was associated with a faster rate of decline in total hip BMD [stage 2 −0.23, stage 3A −0.39, stage 3B −0.80, stage 4 −1.23% change/year in men (P < .001); stage 2 −0.86, stage 3A −1.19, stage 3B −1.20, stage 4 −1.58% change/year in women (P < .03)]. Two distinct eGFR trajectories (Class 1 stable group; Class 2 rapid decline group) were observed. The rapid decline group showed a trend toward an increased rate of decline in total hip BMD. Subgroup analysis according to eGFR trajectories revealed a significant difference in BMD decline rate between stable and rapid decline groups.ConclusionsAdvanced CKD stage and accelerated decline in renal function were associated with rapid BMD decline in non-dialysis patients with CKD.

INTRODUCTION

Abnormal bone mineral density (BMD) is common in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). One of the main complications of CKD is renal osteodystrophy, a complex disease characterized by imbalances in bone turnover, mineralization, and volume. Renal osteodystrophy is caused by various factors related to CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD) [1, 2].

Historically, decreased bone mineral density has received less attention compared to renal osteodystrophy, and BMD testing was not routinely performed in patients with CKD. The 2009 KDIGO Guideline did not recommend BMD measurement based on cross-sectional studies showing that BMD measurement using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) did not predict fracture risk in patients with CKD [3, 4]. However, growing interest in fracture risk prediction among CKD patients along with prospective studies demonstrating that low BMD predicts fracture risk, led to an update in the 2017 KDIGO Guideline, recommending BMD testing in CKD patients [1, 5, 6].

Factors associated with BMD decline, including age, female sex, smoking, and the use of concomitant medications, are well established in the general population [7]. Many of these risk factors have also been observed in patients with CKD; however, the direct association between renal function and reduced BMD has not been fully elucidated. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between BMD loss and renal function in patients with CKD.

In this study, using a well-structured prospective longitudinal cohort database, the rate of BMD decline according to CKD stage was investigated using BMD measurements during a 4-year follow-up using DXA. Additionally, we determined the trajectories of the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and analyzed their association with the rate of BMD decline and eGFR trajectories.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics statement

The study protocol adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of each participating hospital's Clinical Trial Center. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment in the study.

Study design and population

This study was conducted with participants from the Korean Cohort Study for Outcome in Patients With CKD (KNOW-CKD) who were enrolled between 2011 and 2016. The KNOW-CKD study is an ongoing, prospective cohort study that included CKD G1–G5 non-dialysis patients and aimed to elucidate the natural course, complication profiles, and risk factors of Asian populations with CKD [8]. The study rationale, design, methods, and protocol are detailed elsewhere (NCT01630486; http://www.clinicaltrials.gov).

Among the 2238 patients included in the study, we excluded 1010 who did not undergo BMD measurements at baseline or at the 4-year follow-up. Additionally, 410 participants with CKD stages 1 and 5 were excluded. Finally, 1006 participants were included in this study (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the included patients.

Data collection

Baseline sociodemographic data, comorbidities, and medication history were extracted using an electronic data management system (PhactaX, Seoul, Republic of Korea). Demographic data, including age, sex, smoking history, and CKD etiology, were collected at the initial visit. Blood samples were obtained after fasting overnight, centrifuged within 1 h for serum separation, and sent to the KNOW-CKD central laboratory (Lab Genomics, Seongnam, Republic of Korea). Serum creatinine was measured using an isotope dilution mass spectrometry traceable method, and serum intact parathyroid hormone (intact PTH) levels were measured using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics, Penzberg, Germany). Fibroblast growth factor-23 (FGF-23) (Immutopics, San Clemente, CA, USA) and soluble ɑ-Klotho (Immuno-Biological Laboratories, Fujioka, Japan) were measured by ELISA at baseline, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] levels were measured using a radioimmunoassay kit (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA). The CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration equation was used to determine eGFR [9]. CKD was classified according to the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes guidelines [10]. Serum creatinine was measured 2–6 times during the 4-year follow-up. Blood sampling and BMD testing were conducted during a window period from 3 months before to 3 months after the date of informed consent according to study protocol.

Bone mineral density

We employed a DXA system (Hologic, Marlborough, MA, USA) to assess BMD, which was measured in the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, and total hips. Results were expressed as T-scores (standard deviation from the average BMD value in the healthy young population) and absolute BMD (g/cm2). BMD was measured at baseline and 4 years. For the analysis of the rate of BMD decline, we mainly included total hip BMD because longitudinal spine BMD changes may be affected by degenerative changes, such as aortic calcification and osteophytes. The rate of BMD decline is presented as the percentage change per year. Osteoporosis was defined using the definition from the WHO, which defines osteoporosis based on a decreased BMD T-score ≤−2.5. Osteopenia was defined as T-scores of −1.0–−2.4, while T-scores >−1.0 were defined as normal [11].

Trajectory analysis

For the eGFR trajectory analysis, we used a data set of 1828 patients with CKD stages 2 to 4 before excluding missing values of BMD to minimize bias. We generated eGFR trajectory groups for each CKD stage by using eGFR measured 2–6 times over a 4-year follow-up period. We used STATA to define longitudinal eGFR data as a discrete mixture of two or more trajectories by maximum likelihood [12–14]. A semiparametric group-based modeling strategy was used to analyze eGFR. The population was assumed to be composed of multiple trajectory groups. Thus, the model estimates the probabilities of multiple trajectories simultaneously.

We tested models with different numbers of trajectory groups and potential trajectory forms (linear, quadratic, or cubic) to determine the best model fit using the Bayesian information criterion. As the outcome values were continuous, we used a censored normal model. Based on the best model fit, we identified two eGFR trajectory groups for CKD stage 2–4, which were named according to their visual appearance and clinically meaningful categories: the stable and rapid decline groups. In the final model, both trajectory groups were modeled in quadratic terms. Thereafter, the posterior predicted probabilities of each participant were calculated as a member of a given trajectory group. We assigned participants to the trajectory group with the highest posterior predicted probability. Our final model classified participants into trajectory groups with good discrimination, and the mean probability of final group membership was 94% across the trajectory groups. The parameters used and the estimates of the trajectory model, as well as individual probabilities of each participant, and results of adequacy criteria for each group are presented in the Supplementary materials (Supplementary Tables S3–S6).

Because the group-based trajectory modeling does not provide numerical slopes of eGFR, we additionally calculated the slope of eGFR for each trajectory group using linear regression model for the purpose of numerical comparison.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or median (interquartile range), and categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentage). The distribution of variables was determined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To compare the differences between categorized groups, ANOVA and chi-square tests were used for continuous and categorical variables, as appropriate. The main outcome was the decline in the total hip BMD level according to the CKD stage and eGFR trajectory group for each stage in the longitudinal analysis. The analyses were performed separately according to sex. Change from baseline in bone mineral density and the total hip BMD decline rates (% change per year) were determined using multiple linear regression model conducted separately by sex and by each CKD stage. The 4-year BMD was converted to a percentage of baseline BMD. The terms included year and other various covariates. In the regression model, potential confounding factors were hierarchically adjusted. Model 1 was stratified by center and adjusted for demographic factors (age, BMI, and smoking status). Model 2 was further adjusted for CKD-specific risk factors baseline eGFR, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR), serum albumin levels, and diabetes mellitus]. Model 3 was further adjusted for bone mineral factors [calcium, phosphate, 25(OH) vitamin D, PTH, ALP, FGF-23, and soluble klotho]. BMD decline rates were compared according to CKD stage, which was classified using baseline eGFR and according to the KDIGO guideline [15], and eGFR trajectory groups of each CKD stage. The P-for-interaction by CKD stage was calculated with likelihood ratio test using two models, with one model including variables for time and stage and another model including the interaction term of time and stage.

To confirm the robustness of our findings we performed several sensitivity analyses. First, BMD measured at lumbar spine and femoral neck in addition to the total hip were used to investigate the association between the rate of decline in BMD and renal function. Second, we employed multiple imputation analysis in 1006 participants to account for the missing data [16]. Chained equations were applied to fill in the 196 missing values of the baseline covariates. We also examined effect modification by several key subgroups stratified by age (<50 and ≥50 years), BMI (<24.5 and ≥24.5 kg/m2), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and smoking status (never or former and current). Statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 15.1 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) and R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < .05.

BMD slope is higher in rapid decline group according to eGFR trajectories

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was measured 2–6 times between baseline and year 4. Repeated measurements of eGFR are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Trajectory modeling of eGFR over a period of 4 years revealed two distinct groups for CKD stages 2–4 (Class 1, stable group; Class 2, rapid decline group) (Supplementary Fig. S1). The eGFR slope per year showed significant differences between the trajectory groups (stage 2 1.3 vs. −2.8, stage 3A −0.1 vs. −5.7, stage 3B −0.5 vs. −5.1, stage 4 −2.5 vs. −8.0 ml/min/1.73 m2) (Supplementary Table S7). The baseline characteristics of both groups are presented in Supplementary Table S8.

The BMD slopes for each trajectory group for CKD stages 2–4 are presented in Table 3. Overall, the rapid decline group showed a steeper BMD slope than the stable decline group. Statistical significance was observed in stages 2, 3A, and 3B in men, and stages 3A and 3B in women [stage 2 0.06 vs. −0.36 (P = .020), stage 3A −0.18 vs. −0.67 (P = .009), stage 3B −0.36 vs. −1.10 (P = .031) in men; stage 3A −0.79 vs. −1.67 (P = .006), stage 3B −0.60 vs. −1.52% change/year (P = .001) in women]. In women with CKD stage 4, the P value was significant in Models 1 and 2 but was only marginally significant in Model 3 (P = .080) (Table 3 and Fig. 4).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed according to age (<50 and ≥50 years), BMI (<24.5 and ≥24.5 kg/m2), type 2 diabetes (T2DM), and smoking status (never or former and current). As a well-known risk factor of osteoporosis, age, and BMI showed significant interaction on the rate of decline of BMD, meaning more rapid BMD decline at older age and lower BMI. Other factors showed no significant interaction and a trend toward decreasing BMD slope according to renal function was found in all subgroups (Supplementary Table S9).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed using BMD measured at lumbar spine and femoral neck. Lumbar spine BMD did not show a consistent trend of decreasing BMD slope according to the CKD stage, although rapid decline of BMD was observed at rapid eGFR decline group without significance. However, femoral neck BMD showed a trend similar to total hip results but failed to show significance in the CKD stage. Analysis with the multiple imputation data set resulted in similar results as well as significant P values (Supplementary Table S10).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, we found that decreased renal function was associated with faster BMD loss over a 4-year follow-up period in patients with CKD. Furthermore, using the trajectory model, we identified an accelerated decline in eGFR and rapid loss in BMD. These results were adjusted for various covariates, including BMI, smoking status, baseline eGFR, and bone mineral factors. The present study revealed that both the baseline CKD stage and the eGFR trajectory at each stage were significantly associated with the rate of BMD decline in patients with CKD.

Previous cross-sectional studies have predominantly reported advanced CKD stages and low BMD in patients with CKD [17–19]. Kim et al. conducted a baseline study on the KNOW-CKD cohort and reported the lowest BMD values (lumbar spine −0.64 ± 1.67; total hip −0.49 ± 1.21; femur neck −1.02 ± 1.25) and a high prevalence of osteoporosis in CKD stage 5 [17]. Huang et al. conducted a cross-sectional study of 11 050 patients with CKD in the USA and reported that femoral neck BMD was positively correlated with eGFR [19]. Several studies have tracked longitudinal BMD changes in CKD patients [20, 21]. Malluche et al. conducted a prospective study of 89 hemodialysis patients over 2 years, and reported a 2-year decline of −3.1% in total hip BMD. Although numerous studies on the general population have revealed factors influencing the decline in BMD [22, 23], such data are limited in the CKD population. Previously, BMD measurement was not recommended for patients with CKD owing to the limited evidence supporting the predictive value of low BMD for fracture risk [3, 4]. However, recent research has shown that BMD also predicts fractures in patients with CKD; thus, the KDIGO 2017 guidelines have been revised to recommend BMD measurement [1, 5, 6]. Nonetheless, there is still a lack of research on the factors contributing to BMD decline in patients with CKD. Herein, we used longitudinal data to assess baseline and 4-year BMD measurements, revealing that the rate of BMD decline increased in accordance with CKD stage and increased eGFR decline. In addition to our findings, future studies identifying the factors associated with BMD decline will further benefit patients with CKD by facilitating targeted interventions for fracture prevention.

In our findings, there was a similarity between total hip BMD and femoral neck BMD results but inconsistent results for the lumbar spine BMD. Previous studies have shown an increased extra-osseous calcification and osteophytes in the lumbar spine with increasing CKD stage, leading to underestimation of BMD results and this effect may have been present in our study [24, 25]. This result indicates the importance of assessing total hip and femoral neck BMD in assessing bone mineral density of CKD patients.

Interestingly, a recent longitudinal study from the French NephroTest cohort showed a decrease in BMD only at the proximal radius in 477 patients with CKD stages 1 to 5, and BMD decline was not associated with CKD stage [26]. In this study, total hip BMD showed a trend toward increasing BMD change with increasing CKD stage but the P value was not significant. This may be due to the small number of patients and the fact that the study did not adjust for FGF23 and klotho values, which was stated as a limitation in the paper. Also, the results on proximal radius BMD need to be interpreted with caution. The results showed that there was no difference in baseline BMD of radius according to CKD stage, which is contrary to previous reports [27] and in the other skeletal sites such as total hip and femoral neck in the NephroTest study. The BMD measurement of radius may be inaccurate due to relative location of the proximal site, the dominant hand of the patients, and by relatively common movement artifacts [28, 29]. These factors may contribute to the indifference of baseline BMD and BMD change at the proximal radius according to CKD stage.

Several mechanisms can support our findings. First, both CKD and decreased BMD involve inflammatory processes. It is well known that the inflammatory process is a key mechanism in the progression of CKD [30]. As such, an experimental study showed that inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1,6, increase bone resorption and inhibit bone formation [31]. Another clinical study that demonstrated improved BMD by blocking inflammatory cytokines further supports this mechanism [32].

Second, the relationship between reduced renal function and accelerated BMD loss in CKD patients may be attributed to various substances associated with CKD progression. Experimental studies have shown that indoxyl sulfate (IS), a uremic toxin, affects bone remodeling. An ex vivo study showed that IS induced downregulation of the PTH receptor in cultured osteoblast cells, which reduced bone turnover [33]. Moreover, it can release osteocyte-derived substances that inhibit Wnt signaling, resulting in impaired bone formation [34]. Another substance, osteoprotegerin (OPG), is a decoy protein that interacts with receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK) to inhibit osteoclast activity. A gradual increase in OPG according to the CKD stage and its relationship with BMD decline has been reported [35, 36].

Third, an accelerated decline in renal function could serve as an indicator of worsening comorbidities, such as diabetes. Many studies have shown a relationship between diabetic mellitus (DM) and the decline in BMD. Studies have revealed that the accumulation of oxidative stress, advanced glycation end products, and downregulation of Wnt signaling in diabetes are associated with bone fragility [37, 38].

The strength of this study was the use of a large and well-characterized CKD cohort with detailed covariate data, which enabled comprehensive multivariable adjustment. To make the most of the advantages of the longitudinal study, we used trajectory analysis to assess the eGFR changes.

This study had several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single Asian country, and although we adjusted for the centers, this may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. However, considering the aforementioned mechanism, we believe our results are consistent with those of the general population with CKD. Second, we adjusted for numerous confounding factors but not for all variables such as exercise or nutrition. Third, with regard to CKD-MBD, renal osteodystrophy is the main indicator of bone disease in the CKD population. Thus, a decline in BMD alone cannot be used to determine the fracture risk. The trend of BMD decline could indicate bone disease, but other examinations should be attempted (e.g. bone biopsy) for proper therapeutic intervention. Last, due to the lack of data on fracture incidence in the KNOW-CKD cohort, further studies are warranted to establish a direct link between faster BMD decline and increased fracture risk. The FRAX score is known to predict osteoporotic fracture and hip fracture in patients with non-dialysis CKD [39]. It will be worthy of calculating the FRAX score for estimating the risk of fracture. Unfortunately, our cohort lacked data on the history of fracture and parental fractured hips, which are required to calculate the FRAX score. It would be meaningful to investigate these factors together to calculate the fracture risk in our future studies.

In conclusion, our longitudinal observational study showed that decreased renal function and faster eGFR decline were associated with faster BMD decline in patients with CKD stages 2–4. This study suggests that clinicians should carefully follow-up BMD in patients with advanced CKD stage or with rapid eGFR decline.
