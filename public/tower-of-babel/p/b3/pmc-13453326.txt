Individuals with posttraumatic stress disorder have increased beat-to-beat blood pressure variability
Authors: Melissa J. McGranahan, Jeann L. Sabino-Carvalho, Matias G. Zanuzzi, Jeanie Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Individuals with posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are at elevated risk for cardiovascular disease (CVD). Emerging research suggests that increased blood pressure variability (BPV) reflects abnormalities in autonomic regulation and may be an early indicator of future CVD risk. PTSD is associated with autonomic dysregulation that is related to PTSD symptoms and is associated with increased risk of CVD; however, BPV in PTSD remains unexplored. We hypothesized that individuals with PTSD would exhibit greater short-term BPV compared with controls and that BPV would correlate with psychological symptoms. Fifty-nine participants (PTSD: n = 38; controls: n = 21) completed beat-to-beat blood pressure recordings via finger photoplethysmography for 5–10 min. BPV indices included standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and average real variability (ARV) for systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean blood pressure. PTSD symptoms were assessed via the PTSD checklist, fifth edition. Anxiety symptoms were assessed via the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and depression symptoms were assessed via Beck’s Depression Inventory. Compared with controls, the PTSD group had significantly higher SBP-SD (4.3 ± 1.7 vs. 3.3 ± 1.3 mmHg, P = 0.021), SBP-CV (3.7 ± 1.6 vs. 2.7 ± 1.0 mmHg, P = 0.005), and DBP-CV (4.6 ± 1.4 vs. 3.9 ± 1.4 mmHg, P = 0.045). No group differences were found in ARV measures or cardiac baroreflex sensitivity. BPV was not significantly correlated with PTSD, anxiety, or depression symptoms. In conclusion, individuals with PTSD exhibited greater BPV, but psychological symptoms were not associated with BPV indices.

INTRODUCTION

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a psychiatric condition impacting 8% of civilians and 23% of military personnel (1–3). PTSD symptoms adversely impact personal relationships, occupational functioning, and quality of life (4, 5). Importantly, beyond its psychological and social impact, individuals with PTSD have an elevated risk of developing hypertension and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) (6). Multiple large, well-controlled studies have shown that PTSD is independently associated with increased CVD risk and that this risk increases with worsening PTSD symptoms (7–10). Importantly, PTSD often co-occurs with other psychiatric symptoms, particularly anxiety and depression, which not only worsen overall symptom burden but are independently linked to increased CVD risk (11, 12). Thus, there is a need to identify mechanisms and biomarkers underlying CVD risk in PTSD so that early interventions can be developed and implemented to improve CVD risk in this growing patient population.

Increased fluctuations in blood pressure (BP), termed blood pressure variability (BPV), are a potential early marker of CVD risk (13). BPV can be assessed across several timescales, including very short-term (beat-to-beat), short-term [24-h ambulatory blood pressure (ABPM)], and long-term (visit-to-visit) clinic measures, each of which may reflect distinct physiological mechanisms. Prior work has shown that wider fluctuations in beat-to-beat, 24-h ABPM, and visit-to-visit BP are all associated with increased CVD risk, independent of mean BP (14–16). Compared with short-term and long-term methodologies, very short-term BPV has the advantage of being feasibly obtained within a single clinic visit; BPV indices can be quantified by capturing dynamic oscillations in resting BP over a period of 5–10 min. Beat-to-beat BPV is closely regulated by autonomic function and baroreflexes to maintain arterial BP in a very narrow range (17). Individuals with PTSD have impaired baroreflex sensitivity (BRS) that manifests before the onset of overt hypertension (18, 19). However, whether this BRS dysfunction is associated with an augmented BPV in PTSD remains unexplored.

Therefore, the present study evaluates very short-term BPV at rest in individuals with and without PTSD for comparison. We hypothesized that individuals with PTSD would exhibit greater beat-to-beat BPV indices compared with controls without PTSD. In addition, we hypothesized that higher beat-to-beat BPV is associated with lower cardiac BRS and higher severity of PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms.

PROCEDURES AND METHODS

Ethical Approval

The present study is a secondary analysis of data from two ongoing research protocols approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board (No. 01192022 and No. 54697) and the Atlanta Veterans Affairs Health Care System Research and Development Committee. All participants provided written informed consent before study participation.

Participant Characteristics

A total of 59 individuals were included in the study [n = 38 PTSD and n = 21 controls (CON)]. Individuals with and without a prior documented diagnosis of PTSD in medical records were recruited from the Atlanta Veterans Affairs Healthcare System and the greater Atlanta community. Eligibility was confirmed during an in-person screening visit. Exclusion criteria included: 1) history of stage 2 hypertension, heart, or vascular disease; 2) illicit drug use within the last 6 months; 3) excessive alcohol use (>2 drinks per day); 4) females who were currently pregnant or planning to become pregnant; 5) history of autonomic disorder, 6) current use of clonidine and other antihypertensive agents; and 7) over the age of 65 yr. Participants on prazosin for nightmares were included.

Measures

Heart rate and beat-to-beat blood pressure.

Heart rate was continuously monitored via a three-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) using a Bio amp system (ADInstruments, Ltd., Colorado). Noninvasive beat-to-beat blood pressure was measured using finger photoplethysmography (CNAP Monitor 500; CNSystems, Graz, Austria). Beat-to-beat values from the finger were calibrated against a brachial blood pressure as part of an internal calibration system performed before data collection. Respiratory rate (RR) was continuously monitored via a respiratory belt transducer placed around the chest (Pneumotrace II; ADInstruments). All data were recorded at a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz and stored offline for later analysis (PowerLab, LabChart 7 software; ADInstruments, Ltd., Colorado).

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist Diagnostic and Statistical Manual 5th Edition.

The Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist, Fifth Edition (PCL-5) is a validated 20-item questionnaire designed to assess the severity of PTSD symptoms (20). The PCL-5 asks participants to indicate how much they were bothered by symptoms in the past month. The responses range from “Not at All” (0) to “Extremely” (4). Scores range from 0 to 80. The PTSD symptoms classification is as follows: 0–11 asymptomatic/few symptoms/remitted, 12–23 mild, 24–35 moderate, 36–47 severe, and 48 or higher indicates extreme symptoms. The PCL-5 has good test-retest reliability (r = 0.82) and excellent internal consistency (α = 0.94; 20, 21).

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-Military Version.

The Posttraumatic Stress Disorder Checklist-Military Version (PCL-M) is a validated 17-item questionnaire designed to assess the severity of PTSD symptoms in military veterans (22). The PCL-M asks participants to indicate their symptoms frequency in the past month. Items range from “Not at All” (1) to “Extremely” (5). Scores range from 17 to 85, with higher scores indicating greater PTSD symptom severity. The following symptom classification is as follows: 17–29 asymptomatic/few symptoms/remitted, 30–39 mild, 40–49 moderate, 50–59 severe, and 60 or higher indicates extreme symptoms. The PCL-M has acceptable test-retest reliability (r = 0.70) and good internal consistency (α = 0.80) (23).

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory.

The Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) form is a commonly used psychometrically validated 40-item inventory. The trait anxiety (STAI-Y2) portion of the STAI-Y2 consists of 20 items and participants are asked to read each item and circle a response that indicates how they “generally feel.” Items range from “Almost Never” (1) to “Almost Always” (4). Scores range from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating higher trait anxiety. The STAI has previously been reported to have excellent reliability and validity (24).

Beck Depression Inventory-II.

The Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) is a 21-item inventory. Total scores range from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater depression symptoms. Scores of 0–9 indicate none to minimal, 10–18 mild to moderate depression, 19–29 moderate to severe depression, and 30–63 severe depression symptoms (25).

Protocol

Participants completed two visits at the Human Integrative Physiology Laboratory located at the Atlanta Veterans Affairs Healthcare System. During the first visit, participants completed questionnaires. On the second visit, participants presented after refraining from smoking and consuming caffeine or alcohol for at least 12 h and from exercise for 24 h before the visit. Female participants completed a urine pregnancy test. Menstrual cycle phase was not assessed; therefore, testing occurred across all phases of the menstrual cycle. Following 5 min of seated rest, three resting blood pressure measurements were taken using the oscillometric method, each separated by 1 min. After the seated blood pressure measurements, participants were then placed in a semirecumbent position and a fin-ger cuff was applied to measure continuous beat-to-beat blood pressure. Heart rate was continuously measured via ECG, and a respiratory belt sensor was placed around the chest to monitor RR. All procedures were performed in a temperature-controlled room maintained between 22°C and 24°C. Before data collection, all participants rested for at least 5 min. Following the rest period, a 5–10 min baseline segment was used for analysis.

Data Analysis

Blood pressure variability.

BPV indices were calculated as standard deviation (SD; the square root of the average squared difference from the mean), range (maximum-minimum), interquartile range (IQR; the difference between the 25th and 75th percentiles), coefficient of variation [CV; (SD/mean) × 100], and average real variability (ARV; average of the absolute difference between consecutive BP measurements), as previously described (26). Our a priori focus was on SD, CV, and ARV. These BPV indices have been shown to have good-to-excellent day of test-retest reliability (27). The variability indices were calculated for systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean blood pressure (MBP).

Heart rate variability.

ECG recordings were processed in LabChart to detect R-waves and generate a beat-to-beat heart rate time series. R-R intervals were then expressed in milliseconds using the conversion R-R (ms) = 60,000/heart rate (beats/min). The resulting R-R interval series was exported from LabChart and imported into CardioSeries (v2.7) for heart rate variability (HRV) analyses. Data segments were visually inspected to identify artifacts and ectopic beats, and artifact correction was applied as needed so that only normal-to-normal (NN) intervals were analyzed. Time-domain HRV was quantified using the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) of NN intervals. For frequency-domain HRV, the fast Fourier transformation was used to estimate spectral power. Power was calculated in the low-frequency (LF: 0.04–0.15 Hz) and high-frequency (HF: 0.15–0.40 Hz) bands, and total power was calculated over 0.00–0.40 Hz. LF and HF were also expressed in normalized units, calculated as LFnu = LF/(LF + HF) × 100 and HFnu = HF/(LF + HF) × 100, and the LF/HF ratio was computed as LF/HF.

Cardiac baroreflex sensitivity.

Cardiac baroreflex sensitivity (cBRS) was calculated from beat-to-beat SBP and the time between R-R intervals using the sequence technique (28, 29). The sequence technique identifies three or more consecutive heartbeats in which SBP and R-R intervals change progressively in the same direction (either both increasing or decreasing). Sequences were detected only if the following criteria were met: 1) the R-R interval exceeded 1.0 ms, 2) the change in SBP was >1 mmHg, and 3) the sequence spanned at least three cardiac cycles. Linear regression was applied to each sequence, and only sequences with an R2 ≥ 0.85 were included. Slopes from sequences with decreasing (cBRSdown) and increasing (cBRSup) were calculated, and the average of these slopes (cBRSall) was used to provide an overall estimate of cBRS (CardioSeries v2.7). The number of sequences identified for cBRSdown, cBRSup, and cBRSall is also reported.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Chi-square tests were performed to compare categorical variables. Mann–Whitney U tests were performed for continuous variables when assumptions of normality were violated. When the assumptions of normality were met, independent t tests were used to compare those with PTSD (n = 38) relative to CON (n = 21) on all measures. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ 0.05. Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for all outcomes. Pearson correlations examined associations in two sets: first, between BPV indices that were statistically significant in the primary analyses and PTSD, anxiety, and depression symptoms, and second, between these BPV indices and cBRSall. Due to missing data, sample sizes for the correlation analyses varied as follows: n = 55 for the STAI-Y2 (PTSD n = 37, CON n = 18); n = 51 for the BDI-II (PTSD n = 33, CON n = 18); n = 27 for the PCL-5 (PTSD n = 22, CON n = 5); and n = 31 for the PCL-M (PTSD n = 16, CON n = 15). Given that some individuals in the PTSD group were treated with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRI; n = 17) and prazosin (α1-adrenergic antagonist; n = 9), all significant group differences were further evaluated using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) and the rank-based Quade ANCOVA to control for medication use and determine whether medication differences contributed to the observed effects.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

A total of 59 participants were enrolled: 38 with PTSD (males = 26, females = 12) and 21 CON without PTSD (males = 11, females = 10). Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. There was no difference in baseline physical characteristics, hemodynamics, or RR between the groups. Due to technical issues, RR was obtained for PTSD = 32 and CON = 16. The PCL-5 and PCL-M scores were significantly higher in the PTSD group, as expected. The PTSD group also had significantly higher BDI-II scores compared with the CON group, with average scores indicating moderate to severe depression in PTSD and none to minimal symptoms in controls. The PTSD group also had significantly higher STAI-Y2 scores (95th–97th percentile), indicating higher anxiety compared with the CON group (53rd–54th percentile; P < 0.05; 24). Use of SSRI and alpha blockers was significantly greater in the PTSD versus the CON group (P < 0.05).

Beat-to-Beat Blood Pressure Variability

There were no differences in resting mean beat-to-beat SBP, DBP, and MBP between the PTSD and CON groups (Fig. 1, A–C).

SD.

The PTSD group had greater SBP-SD [t(57) = −2.37, P = 0.021, Cohen’s d = 0.66] and MBP-SD [t(57) = −2.06, P = 0.044, Cohen’s d = 0.50] compared with the CON group (Fig. 2, A and C). There was no statistically significant difference in DBP-SD [t(57) = −1.89, P = 0.064, Cohen’s d = 0.45] between groups. After adjusting for prazosin and SSRI use, the group effect on SBP-SD remained statistically significant [F(1,55) = 4.00, P = 0.050, partial η2 = 0.07], and neither prazosin use (P = 0.39) nor SSRI use (P = 0.59) was associated with SBP variability. For MBP-SD, the group effect was reduced to a non-significant after covariate adjustment [F(1,55) = 2.29, P = 0.136, partial η2 = 0.04], and neither alpha blocker use (P = 0.63) nor SSRI use (P = 0.72) was a significant predictor.

CV.

The PTSD group had greater SBP-CV [t(55.65) = −2.92 P = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 0.75], DBP-CV [t(57) = −2.05, P = 0.045, Cohen’s d = 0.50], and MBP-CV [t(57) = −2.34, P = 0.023, Cohen’s d = 0.63] compared with the CON group (Fig. 2, D–F). After adjusting for prazosin and SSRI use, the group effect on SBP-CV remained statistically significant [F(1,55) = 4.94, P = 0.030, partial η2 = 0.08], indicating robust group differences independent of medication status. For DBP-CV and MBP-CV, the adjusted group effects were reduced to non-significant [F(1,55) = 2.26, P = 0.14, partial η2 = 0.04; and F(1,55) = 3.70, P = 0.059, partial η2 = 0.06, respectively], and neither prazosin use (all P > 0.35) nor SSRI use (all P > 0.49) were significant predictors in any model.

ARV.

There was no difference between the PTSD and CON groups for SBP-ARV [t(57) = −0.62, P = 0.54, Cohen’s d = 0.25], DPB-ARV (U = 427, z = 0.44, P = 0.66, Cohen’s d = 0.16), and MBP-ARV (U = 433, z = 0.54, P = 0.59, Cohen’s d = 0.25; Fig. 2, G–I).

Range and IQR.

The SBP range [t(57) = −1.65, P = 0.11, Cohen’s d = 0.47], DBP range [t(57) = −1.91, P = 0.061, Cohen’s d = 0.54], and MBP range [t(57) = −1.70, P = 0.094, Cohen’s d = 0.48] trended higher in the PTSD group but did not reach statistical significance (Supplemental Table S1). The PTSD group had significantly greater SBP interquartile range (U = 549, z = 2.38, P = 0.018, Cohen’s d = 0.70), and MBP interquartile range (U = 528, z = 2.04, P = 0.041, Cohen’s d = 0.52; see Supplemental Table S1). However, when adjusting for prazosin and SSRI use in a rank-based (Quade) ANCOVA, group differences in IQR metrics were attenuated and no longer statistically significant (all P’s > 0.11). This suggests that medication status and variability in small-sample nonparametric estimates likely reduced power to detect modest group effects.

Heart Rate Variability, Cardiac Baroreflex Sensitivity, and Heart Rate Associations

HRV metrics were not statistically different between PTSD and CON groups for RMSSD (PTSD: 42.23 ± 23.20, CON: 57.67 ± 39.58; U = 305, z = −1.35, P = 0.18, Cohen’s d = 0.52), HF-HRVnu [PTSD: 53.76 ± 17.31, CON: 58.38 ± 18.43; t(56) = −0.96, P = 0.34, Cohen’s d = 0.27], LF-HRVnu [PTSD: 46.24 ± 17.31, CON: 41.62 ± 18.43; t(56) = −0.96, P = 0.34, Cohen’s d = −0.27], or LF/HF (PTSD: 1.60 ± 1.37, CON: 1.19 ± 0.97; U = 470, z = 1.32, P = 0.19, Cohen’s d = −0.34).

cBRS did not differ significantly between PTSD and CON groups for any indices: cBRSdown (PTSD: 20.06 ± 9.00, CON: 28.64 ± 19.33; U = 247, z = −1.55, P = 0.12, Cohen’s d = 0.64), cBRSup (PTSD: 20.79 ± 12.34, CON: 31.67 ± 24.82; U = 249, z = −1.37, P = 0.17, Cohen’s d = 0.61), or cBRSall (PTSD: 20.99 ± 11.61, CON: 31.75 ± 22.23; U = 269, z = −1.69, P = 0.091, Cohen’s d = 0.67). The number of sequences did not differ significantly between PTSD and CON groups for cBRSdown [PTSD: 6.10 ± 4.98, CON: 7.49 ± 6.57; t(56) = −0.84, P = 0.40], cBRSup [PTSD: 4.76 ± 3.11, CON: 6.46 ± 5.35; t(56) = −1.33, P = 0.19], or cBRSall [PTSD: 10.86 ± 7.53, CON: 13.95 ± 10.95; t(56) = −1.15, P = 0.26]. Because cBRS is inherently related to heart rate (30) and several CON participants had very low heart rates, potentially accounting for the extreme cBRS values, we conducted Pearson correlations between resting heart rate and each cBRS metric. Heart rate was not significantly correlated with cBRSup (r = −0.24, P = 0.078) or cBRSdown (r = −0.22, P = 0.093) but showed a significant inverse association with overall baroreflex sensitivity (cBRSall: r = −0.30, P = 0.02). cBRSall was not correlated (all P’s > 0.05) with any significant BPV indices within the PTSD group.

Symptoms and Beat-to-Beat Blood Pressure Variability

The average length of days between the questionnaires (visit 1) and BPV measurements (visit 2) was 18 ± 18 days (range 0–78 days). PCL-5, PCL-M, and BDI-II were not significantly correlated to any BPV indices within the PTSD group (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

We demonstrate that very short-term BPV indices are elevated in individuals with PTSD compared with controls, despite similar resting mean BP between the groups. Specifically, we observed moderate-to-large effect sizes in SBP-SD, SBP-CV, SBP interquartile range, DBP-CV, MBP-SD, and MBP-CV in the PTSD group. Increased BPV was not associated with lower cBRS indices or higher severity of PTSD, anxiety, or depression symptoms. Our findings place PTSD within a broader literature linking higher very short-term beat-to-beat BPV to groups at elevated cardiovascular risk. These results suggest that increased very short-term BPV may represent one physiological pathway through which PTSD is associated with heightened cardiovascular risk, even before the onset of hypertension. Prior studies have found higher very short-term beat-to-beat BPV indices in groups prone to hypertension and CVD, such as Black males (31) and chronic kidney disease (26). In addition, increased beat-to-beat BPV has been shown to be associated with the progression of arterial stiffness (32). In a study of patients with recent stroke, beat-to-beat BPV was predictive of CVD mortality and other major cardiovascular events (16). Although no prior studies had examined beat-to-beat BPV in PTSD, one study from Yoo et al. (33) performed short-term BPV measures using 24-h ABPM and found that SBP-SD was significantly elevated in a sample of women with PTSD. In addition, they found that increased SBP-SD was associated with early muscle sympathetic nerve activity responses during handgrip, suggesting that BPV-SD in PTSD is associated with exaggerated neurocirculatory reactivity (33). Similar to results derived from 24-h ABPM, we demonstrate heightened SBP-SD and SBP-CV using very short-term beat-to-beat BPV in a cohort of males and females with PTSD, independent of prazosin or SSRI use. SBP variability may be more sensitive to beat-to-beat changes in large-artery compliance and wave reflection than DBP or MBP variability. Accordingly, greater arterial stiffness could preferentially amplify SBP fluctuations. Future studies incorporating indices of arterial stiffness are needed to further evaluate the relationship between BPV and arterial stiffness.

Very short-term beat-to-beat BPV is influenced primarily through autonomic regulation, including baroreflex control, sympathetic transduction, and respiratory effects (34). On the contrary, short-term BPV derived from 24-h ABPM reflects circadian and behavioral influences such as posture, activity, and sleep-wake transitions, whereas longer-term visit-to-visit BPV incorporates slower-acting factors including renal function and medication adherence (35). Despite these mechanistic differences, elevated BPV at all three timescales has been suggested to predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes (16, 35, 36). The advantage of beat-to-beat BPV is the clinical feasibility. As opposed to 24-h ABPM and visit-to-visit long-term BPV, beat-to-beat BPV can be easily and noninvasively obtained during a single outpatient visit over a short duration of 5–10 min, without necessitating the use of 24-h ABPM, which may be burdensome, or multiple clinical visits over a long duration of time. Whether increased beat-to-beat BPV may be used as a clinical biomarker for CVD risk in PTSD before the onset of hypertension and CVD remains to be tested; however, it could result in a new clinical tool for detecting increased risk early in PTSD.

The baroreflex system is one of the primary mechanisms regulating moment-by-moment BP, acting through coordinated cardiac (vagally mediated) and sympathetic vasomotor (sympathetically mediated) arms to minimize fluctuations in BP. The cardiac arm of the arterial baroreflex system (cBRS) buffers against excessive BP fluctuations by modulating heart rate in response to rapid changes in BP, whereas the sympathetic arm modulates peripheral vascular resistance. A failure to suppress HR in the setting of elevated BP, or an increase in HR in the setting of low BP, could lead to wider fluctuations in BP, both short-term and long-term. Moreover, blunted cBRS is associated with worse cardiovascular prognosis (37). Previous work has demonstrated that cBRS quantified using different techniques is blunted in PTSD. In a study assessing cBRS using the spontaneous method, PTSD had significantly reduced cBRS compared with controls with a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.95; 38). In addition, prior work from our group showed impairment in both sympathetic baroreflex sensitivity (sBRS) and cBRS assessed with pharmacologic manipulation of blood pressure (i.e., modified Oxford technique) in PTSD versus controls with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.95–1.22; 18). Contrary to our hypothesis, our current results do not show a relationship between BPV and cBRS within the PTSD group. Differences in measurement approach may contribute to heterogeneity across studies, as pharmacologic methods produce larger BP perturbations, whereas spontaneous methods rely on naturally occurring fluctuations. In the present study, we also applied more stringent sequence inclusion criteria (R2 ≥ 0.85 vs. R2 ≥ 0.5), which may improve specificity but reduce the number of qualifying sequences and increase estimation variance, thereby limiting power to detect group differences. In addition, previous studies were primarily composed of male participants (18, 39). Sex differences in baroreflex function may also contribute to variability in cBRS findings across studies, and the inclusion of a more sex-diverse sample in the present study could partially explain why our PTSD-related effects on cBRS were less robust than previously reported. In this context, the moderate effect sizes and trend toward lower cBRS in the PTSD group suggest that our study may have been underpowered to detect group differences in cBRS. Of note, prior work has shown that individuals with PTSD have heightened BP and SNS reactivity during sympathoexcitatory stressors such as handgrip exercise and mental stress (18, 39–41) and that this augmented sympathoexcitation is associated with increased 24-h ABPM-derived short-term BPV (33). Whether heightened SNS responses are linked to very short-term beat-to-beat BPV in PTSD remains to be tested.

Our finding that BPV was not associated with cardiovagal BRS aligns with prior evidence. In a cohort of young healthy adults, Young et al. (31) reported that cardiac BRS, using the sequence method, was not related to BPV indices, whereas reduced sympathetic BRS was associated with greater BPV. Taken together, these findings suggest that null BPV and cardiac BRS associations do not preclude baroreflex involvement; rather, they may indicate that other pathways (vascular or sympathetic baroreflex) mechanisms may be central to buffering short-term BP fluctuations. Beat-to-beat BPV may also be influenced by the degree of sympathetic transduction, i.e., the vasoconstrictive effect of each sympathetic burst, resulting in changes in BP. Greater sympathetic transduction produces larger vasoconstrictor responses that can broaden the distribution of SBP and MBP even without elevated SNS activity. In a previous study of 238 young healthy males and females, sympathetic transduction was significantly associated with beat-to-beat measures of DBP-SD and DBP-ARV, accounting for 9%–22% of the variance (42). Our laboratory’s prior work has shown that vascular α1-adrenergic receptor sensitivity, the major driver of vasoconstriction in response to SNS activation, was significantly higher in PTSD compared with age-matched controls (43). Therefore, although not measured in this study, it is biologically plausible that greater vasoconstrictive responses, particularly during stress, may contribute to greater beat-to-beat BPV indices in individuals with PTSD.

Importantly, our SBP-SD and SBP-CV results remained significant when controlling for prazosin, a commonly prescribed α1-adrenergic antagonist used for the treatment of nightmares in PTSD (44). A recent study in healthy adults showed BPV is significantly reduced by prazosin (45). Although the current study is not powered to detect differences between those treated and not treated with prazosin, it would be important to evaluate whether prazosin treatment may have beneficial effects on BPV in PTSD, since this drug is often prescribed in this patient population. Beyond adrenergic mechanisms, other neurohumoral and vascular alterations characteristic of PTSD may also contribute to elevated beat-to-beat BPV. For example, PTSD is characterized by increased renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system activation (46), elevated inflammation, impaired nitric oxide bioavailability (47, 48), and vascular stiffness (49, 50) that could also modulate beat-to-beat BPV by reducing vascular buffering capacity and amplifying pressure oscillations due to impaired vasodilatory capacity (49, 50). Future studies are needed to determine which pathway(s) are primary drivers of increased BPV in PTSD.

Finally, although autonomic imbalance has been reported to be elevated with greater PTSD symptoms compared with those with lesser symptoms (39), we did not detect a relationship between increased very short-term BPV and severity of PTSD, anxiety, or depression symptoms in the current study. Prior research in civilians who screened positive for PTSD found that momentary ecological assessments of anxiety were significantly associated with higher systolic blood pressure across a 24-h ABPM period, compared with those without a probable PTSD diagnosis (51). Similarly, a secondary analysis of young, healthy adults suggested that trait anxiety was correlated with both resting blood pressure and muscle sympathetic nerve activity (52). It is important to note, however, that we did not assess symptom severity on the same study day as the BPV visit. Although these measurements were obtained, on average, within 18 days of each other, there may have been fluctuations in symptom severity and BPV that limited our ability to detect a relationship due to the timing between symptom reporting and physiological measurement. Beat-to-beat BPV may be more strongly influenced by acute psychological symptoms. Consistent with our null findings for depression symptoms, a recent study by Darling et al. (53) found no differences in BPV indices or sBRS in individuals with major depressive disorder compared with healthy controls. Future studies that incorporate real-time or same-day symptom assessments may help clarify how psychological symptoms and emotional processes impact BPV indices acutely.

Limitations

As noted earlier, psychological symptoms were not measured on the same day as the visit when BPV was measured. Group differences in medication use, including SSRIs and prazosin, may have influenced results. Although the long-term cardiovascular effects of SSRIs remain unclear, their use could potentially alter autonomic function. The majority of our sample were veterans, and all visits took place at the Atlanta Veterans Affairs Medical Center. For some individuals, this setting may have induced stress depending on the nature of their index trauma, potentially influencing physiological testing responses. Most participants in the present study were Black, which limits the generalizability of findings to other racial or ethnic groups. However, this is also an important strength given the disproportionate burden of hypertension and autonomic dysregulation among Black adults (54, 55). An important limitation is that the relatively low number of detected sequences for cBRSup and cBRSdown in our resting recordings may reduce the reliability of these estimates. Menstrual cycle phase and hormonal status were unmeasured, potentially contributing to variation in BPV due to hormonal influences on vascular and autonomic function (56–58). The majority of the visits occurred in the morning; however, due to scheduling conflicts, some study visits occurred during the afternoon (PTSD: n = 13, CON: n = 8). Other unmeasured factors, such as aerobic fitness and sleep quality, may have also impacted BPV in PTSD (59, 60).

Perspectives and Significance

Due to the high burden of hypertension and CVD in PTSD, characterizing early physiological changes associated with cardiovascular risk may improve understanding of risk in individuals with PTSD. We found that very short beat-to-beat BPV indices are elevated in young, otherwise healthy individuals with PTSD. Importantly, increased very short-term BPV was independent of mean BP levels. These results demonstrate that very short-term BPV is feasibly attained in the outpatient clinical setting and may reflect altered cardiovascular regulation before the onset of hypertension in PTSD. Longitudinal and epidemiological studies are needed to determine whether BPV is a specific marker of future cardiovascular risk in individuals with PTSD.
