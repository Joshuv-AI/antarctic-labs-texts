Hierarchical microtopology and phase-specific delivery functionally restore ultralong nerve continuity across species
Authors: Shuxin Dai, Feiyi Li, Zheheng Bao, Zhuangzhuang Yang, Jin Wang, Lihua Liu, Mengyao Jia, Shuwei Wang, Zijian Zhang, Xinlong Wang, Yantao Zhao, Adam C. Midgley, Yumin Yang, Meng Fan, Deling Kong, Xianhao Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Repairing ultralong peripheral nerve defects remains a major clinical challenge, primarily due to the requirement for regenerative platforms to be capable of integrating spatial guidance with temporally resolved biochemical cues. To address this, we developed a clinically translatable, fully synthetic nerve conduit that aligns with native regenerative principles through hierarchical microtopological engineering and phase-specific molecular delivery. This design creates a proregenerative microenvironment by synchronizing structural cues with repair cascades. The neuroanatomically inspired core mimics the endoneurium and perineurium, providing extensive cell-scale contact guidance for axonal alignment and fasciculation. A dual-layered sheath ensures mechanical integrity, metabolic permeability, and selective exclusion of fibrotic tissue. Tailored to align with distinct phases of nerve repair, the conduit enables sequential release of spermidine and ascorbic acid. Spermidine resolves early inflammation, priming the niche for ascorbic acid–mediated debris clearance, axonal elongation, and remyelination. In both rat (2 centimeters) and beagle (5 centimeters) models of critical-sized sciatic nerve defects, the conduit supports structural and functional regeneration comparable to autografts while yielding superior outcomes in motor coordination and suppression of autotomy. This strategy offers a scalable and mechanistically informed solution for repairing ultralong nerve injuries with high translational promise.

INTRODUCTION

Functional restoration after peripheral nerve injury (PNI) remains a formidable clinical challenge, especially in long-gap lesions where current treatments yield inconsistent results. PNI commonly arises from trauma, surgical resection, or iatrogenic damage. These injuries can result in debilitating sensory and motor deficits, chronic neuropathic pain, and lifelong disability (1). Although end-to-end anastomosis is effective for short gaps (<5 mm), larger defects typically require grafting. For gaps exceeding 3 cm, autologous nerve grafts remain the only clinically viable option and are considered the gold standard (2–4). However, their utility is limited by donor site morbidity, size mismatches, and neuroma formation (5). These limitations underscore the urgent need for regenerative strategies capable of restoring functional continuity across extended peripheral nerve gaps.

Despite decades of development, nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) remain largely ineffective for ultralong defects (>3 cm), with outcomes rarely comparable to autografts (3, 6). To overcome this, researchers have explored various structural strategies inspired by the endoneurial and perineurial architecture of native nerves, including aligned micro-/nanofibers (7–9), microchannels (10–12), and microgrooves (13, 14), to provide directional cues for axonal regrowth. However, these approaches typically mimic only isolated features of the inner nerve sheaths. Aligned fibers suffer from irregular packing density and unstable guidance performance (7); microchannels lack cell-scale resolution and hinder nutrient/metabolite exchange (15, 16); and microgrooves provide limited contact area for sustained axonal alignment (15). These shortcomings highlight the need for a hierarchy-driven design that integrates fascicle-to-cell scale architecture, sustained guidance, and efficient molecular exchange by moving beyond current constructs.

In addition, the structural biomimicry of the epineurium, which serves as a key protective sheath enclosing nerve fascicle, has often been neglected in current designs. Clinical data on NGCs reveal frequent failure modes, including lumen collapse from muscular compression, suture dislodgement due to joint movement, and insufficient flexibility to accommodate deformation and torsional stress during joint-spanning repairs (15, 17, 18). An ideal sheath must combine mechanical robustness with adaptability, ensure metabolic permeability, and block fibrotic tissue invasion. However, achieving a unified design that meets these mechanical and biological demands remains an elusive goal.

Even with optimal structural design, successful nerve regeneration demands precise biochemical signaling tailored to each phase. Biomaterial-induced in situ regeneration unfolds through a sequence of cellular events (19). In the early phase, neutrophils recruit monocytes that differentiate into macrophages, whose polarization determines the resolution of inflammation and initiation of the repair cascade (20). During Wallerian degeneration, Schwann cells clear axonal and myelin debris and align into Büngner bands, providing essential guidance for axonal regrowth (21). In the subsequent phase, axonal elongation and remyelination restore the structural and functional integrity. Together, these dynamics reveal macrophage polarization and endogenous neural cell activity as pivotal targets for stage-specific modulation to enhance in situ nerve regeneration.

Although growth factor delivery has been extensively investigated for modulating the regenerative microenvironment, their clinical translation remains limited by poor stability, uncontrolled release, and imprecise dosing (19). To address these limitations, increasing attention has been directed toward endogenous metabolites and dietary nutrients, such as spermidine (SP) and ascorbic acid (AA), for their regenerative potential. SP promotes inflammation resolution through macrophage polarization and autophagy, with benefits demonstrated in cardiovascular (22, 23), neurodegenerative (24–26), and pulmonary diseases (27, 28). AA enhances tissue repair through antioxidant activity, matrix remodeling, and glial differentiation, with efficacy in wound healing (29, 30), cartilage repair (31, 32), and nerve regeneration (33–35). These small molecules provide a cost-effective and physiologically relevant alternative for phase-specific modulation (19). We hypothesize that sequential release of SP and AA can synergistically modulate immune and neural cell responses, thereby promoting functional regeneration. However, coordinating their delivery with the dynamic phases of nerve healing remains a critical bottleneck.

Here, we developed a regenerative engineering platform that orchestrates spatially precise structural guidance with temporally programmed small-molecule release, providing an integrated solution to the above challenges (Fig. 1). Leveraging a hybrid strategy of three-dimensional (3D) printing and wet spinning, we constructed a hierarchically organized core integrating aligned fibers and oriented microchannels (AFMC) from elastic biodegradable poly(l-lactide-caprolactone) (PLCL), enabling tissue-scale organization and cell-scale guidance. Encapsulating the core, a bilayered sheath consisting of a microporous inner layer together with a loosely woven outer layer provides structural integrity and physiological adaptability (Fig. 1B). For temporally resolved release, SP was adsorbed onto a polydopamine (PDA)–coated fiber surface, while AA was encapsulated in halloysite nanotubes (HNTs) embedded in the fiber matrix, forming a dual-release system (AFMC@SP@AA) (Fig. 1B). We systematically validated the structural and functional efficacy of AFMC@SP@AA in rat (2 cm) and beagle (5 cm) models of critical-sized sciatic nerve defects (Fig. 1, A and C). Overall, this platform provides a clinically scalable and mechanism-driven solution for repairing ultralong peripheral nerve defects.

RESULTS

Construction and characterization of hierarchically microstructured nerve conduit

The nerve sheath, composed of the endoneurium, perineurium, and epineurium, provides critical structural support in the peripheral nervous system and plays a pivotal role in maintaining the regenerative microenvironment (36). In this study, we developed a hierarchical microtopology nerve conduit to support nerve regeneration (Fig. 1). At its core lies a biomimetic unit designed to replicate the complex microarchitecture of the native endoneurium. This structure features AFMC that offer directional cues for axonal growth. Conventional tissue engineering techniques fall short in replicating such fine architectures. To address this challenge, we used a hybrid fabrication strategy combining 3D printing and wet spinning (fig. S1 and movie S1), enabling the construction of scaffolds with hierarchically organized architectures (Fig. 2A). Briefly, the AFMC membrane was generated through sacrificial templating of poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) fibers and then rolled into a tubular conduit, after which fiber removal produced the aligned microchannel architecture of the final construct (fig. S1 and movie S1). Selective removal of sacrificial 3D-printed fibers generated oriented microchannels (purple dashed lines), with diameters of 187.1 ± 32.75 μm in the rat conduit and 332.5 ± 28.66 μm in the beagle conduit, approximating native nerve fascicle dimensions and allowing tunable size and density to match different nerve types across species (Fig. 2, A and E, and figs. S1 and S2). Surrounding these channels, we incorporated highly aligned fibers (green fluorescence; 25.90 ± 2.82 μm in diameter) with consistent parallel orientation (Fig. 2, A and D), recapitulating the endoneurial fiber alignment and providing sustained directional guidance. Interfiber spacing formed microgrooves (white arrowheads; 1.96 ± 0.59 μm) closely matching native axon diameters (Fig. 2A and fig. S3), thereby promoting axonal alignment and minimizing misrouting. In addition, interfiber pores (blue dashed lines; 33.29 ± 18.06 μm) created a porous matrix that supported metabolic exchange and axonal fasciculation by maintaining a permissive growth microenvironment (Fig. 2, A and E). To enhance structural resilience under mechanical stress, we strategically integrated reticular fibers (red fluorescence) around the periphery of the endoneurial scaffold (Fig. 2A). These fibers formed a circumferential network that interlaced with the aligned core, reinforcing the construct against deformation. Compared to aligned-only scaffolds, this peripheral reticular architecture substantially improved shape retention under compressive loading (Fig. 2B), as quantified by a markedly higher area retention rate (Fig. 2C).

To further protect the regenerating nerve, we engineered a biomimetic dual-layer outer sheath that provides both mechanical reinforcement and biological insulation (Fig. 2F). In clinical scenarios, fibrotic scarring frequently compromises regeneration due to sheath failure, which permits infiltration of invasive myogenic or fibroblastic cells (37, 38). To mitigate this, we designed the inner sheath layer with densely packed microporous fibers (red fluorescence) that support solute exchange (fig. S4, A and B) while effectively blocking myogenic cell infiltration (Fig. 2, G to I), thereby enhancing barrier integrity and conduit durability. In contrast, the outer sheath layer comprises loosely woven fibers (green fluorescence; 25.44° ± 6.59°) fabricated via controlled wet spinning. This layer forms a deformable porous mesh that dissipates mechanical stress and enhances conduit flexibility (Fig. 2F and fig. S4C). The open structure facilitates nutrient exchange and metabolic waste removal (fig. S4, E and F). Notably, peripheral nerve injuries often occur near joints, where conduits are vulnerable to repeated bending and dynamic compression. This can result in lumen collapse, a phenomenon clinically referred to as the “kink” effect (17). Our flexible outer sheath design effectively resists such deformation, maintaining lumen integrity under dynamic physiological conditions (Fig. 2J and movie S2).

We next assessed the conduit’s mechanical robustness under surgical and physiological conditions. Compression tests (Fig. 2K) revealed excellent elasticity, with the conduit exhibiting strong stress recovery under 50% compressive strain. After 100 compression cycles, the conduit retained more than 80% of its initial stress response without visible deformation or collapse (Fig. 2L). Tensile testing showed an elongation at break of ~1000% (Fig. 2M), confirming superior tensile resilience. Furthermore, suture retention tests (Fig. 2N) demonstrated that the conduit could withstand forces exceeding 5 N, supporting secure surgical fixation and minimizing the risk of suture dislodgement due to in vivo muscular contractions.

AFMC structure enhances axonal alignment and Schwann cell migration

Defined topographical cues are known to enhance neural cell adhesion and axonal extension, thereby promoting directed migration across injury gaps (39). In our design, the cross-sectional view of the unfolded inner core reveals a composite design integrating AFMC, offering simultaneous directional guidance and spatial accommodation for neural cells (Fig. 3A). To evaluate the specific contribution of the composite structure (AFMC) to neural cell growth, we fabricated AF and MC membranes as single-variable control groups using wet spinning and 3D printing, respectively, and characterized their micro- and macroscale surface morphologies (Fig. 3B). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the groove width (126.20 ± 20.50 μm) and fiber diameter (26.08 ± 2.97 μm) of the AFMC membrane were comparable to those of the individual MC and AF membranes, validating the dimensional precision of our hybrid fabrication strategy (fig. S5). White light interferometry (WLI) (Fig. 3B) further revealed that the AFMC structure nearly doubled the specific surface area compared to control membranes (Fig. 3C), suggesting a larger cell-material interface that may enhance cell adhesion and promote scaffold cellularization.

To evaluate how topography affects axonal outgrowth, we seeded induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)–derived neuroepithelial organoids (Fig. 3D and fig. S6) onto different membrane substrates. After 7 days, immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3E) showed that axons on MC membranes aligned along the grooves, forming fasciculated bundles, whereas those extending beyond the grooves appeared disorganized and failed to bundle properly. On AF membranes, organoids exhibited bipolar axonal growth, with axons aligned parallel to the oriented fibers. However, the axons appeared thinner than those in the MC group, possibly due to insufficient axonal bundling or cell-cell interactions. (fig. S7). Notably, axons on AFMC membranes were tightly clustered within the microchannels and displayed more mature morphological features. Axons in the AFMC group exhibited significantly greater extension, reaching an average length of 1386.0 ± 255.9 μm and a maximum length of 1593.0 ± 335.2 μm (Fig. 3, F and G). Furthermore, the AFMC group showed significantly thicker nerve bundles, reflecting improved axonal alignment and fascicle formation (fig. S7).

Schwann cells, the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system, actively migrate to injury sites and form regeneration tracks that guide axonal elongation (40, 41). To assess the influence of surface topography on Schwann cell migration, we seeded 3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate (DiO)-labeled Schwann cells onto the membranes with a defined cell-free gap created using a custom insert (fig. S8). Live-cell imaging revealed that Schwann cells on MC and AFMC membranes exhibited significantly greater migration areas after 48 hours compared to the other groups (Fig. 3H). The maximum migration distance on AFMC membranes reached 1064.0 ± 116.0 μm, significantly exceeding the 821.0 ± 71.8 μm observed on MC membranes (Fig. 3I). We hypothesize that the microchannel structures facilitate clustered cell growth and accelerate collective migration, while the integrated AFMC architecture provides additional topographical cues that enhance directional migration. Furthermore, Schwann cells on both AFMC and AF membranes exhibited an elongated morphology (fig. S9), which is favorable for establishing continuous regenerative pathways across the injury gap (42).

AFMC nerve conduit promotes cellular infiltration and survival

A key challenge in repairing long-gap peripheral nerve defects is the lack of effective internal guidance structures within nerve conduits (15). Although lumen filling with scaffold materials can provide directional cues for axonal regeneration, excessive or disorganized filling may impede axonal ingrowth, highlighting the need to balance structural guidance with sufficient space for tissue infiltration. To evaluate the effect of the guided inner core on cellular infiltration and tissue ingrowth, we fabricated nerve conduits incorporating MC, AF, and AFMC structures. As shown in Fig. 4 (A and B), the AFMC conduits exhibited uniformly distributed microchannels at the hundred-micrometer scale and interconnected porous features at the 10-μm scale (Fig. 2E). The lumen porosity of AFMC conduits (58.06 ± 8.16%) was significantly higher than that of MC (32.70 ± 4.10%) and AF (42.50 ± 2.37%) conduits (Fig. 4C), indicating enhanced permeability for molecular diffusion (fig. S10).

To investigate how these intraluminal structures influenced cell infiltration, we used a dynamic seeding system in which nerve and Schwann cells were introduced into the conduit scaffolds under rotational flow (Fig. 4D and fig. S11). This approach facilitated medium perfusion through the luminal pores, promoting efficient cell entry and adhesion to the scaffold surface. After 3 days of culture, the conduits were sectioned, and the central regions were subjected to immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 4E, the number of adherent cells in the AFMC group was significantly higher than in the other groups.

To further assess infiltration under physiological conditions, we subcutaneously implanted the conduits in rats and conducted histological analysis (Fig. 4F). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining at 7 days postimplantation revealed that cells migrated along the aligned fibers and microchannel walls into the conduit lumen, displaying a characteristic bead-like clustering (Fig. 4G). Notably, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition was evident on the fiber surfaces, and AFMC conduits exhibited markedly greater intraluminal cell infiltration than the MC and AF groups (Fig. 4H). By day 14, ECM accumulation further increased in the AFMC group, and the appearance of red blood cells suggested the initiation of neovascularization (Fig. 4G), indicating the establishment of a rudimentary nutrient and oxygen transport system to support cell viability and regeneration within the conduit.

Caspase-3, a key marker of apoptosis, was used to evaluate cell viability (43). A lower proportion of Caspase-3–positive cells was observed in the AFMC scaffolds, indicating reduced apoptosis and improved cell survival. This suggests that the composite structure provides a biocompatible microenvironment favorable for nerve regeneration (Fig. 4, G and I). Collectively, both in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrated that AFMC-filled lumens enhance cell infiltration and retention while maintaining diffusivity, thereby establishing a regenerative niche conducive to neural repair.

Sequential delivery of SP and AA guides inflammatory resolution and neural remodeling in vitro

Beyond structural optimization, the temporal coordination of biochemical cues is essential for guiding each phase of nerve regeneration, especially in long-gap injuries (2, 44). Nerve repair progresses through distinct stages, including an acute inflammatory phase and subsequent matrix remodeling (45, 46). During the acute phase, the rapid release of anti-inflammatory agents suppresses excessive inflammation and limits tissue damage. In the remodeling phase, sustained delivery of regenerative signals supports tissue repair and axonal regrowth. In this study, SP and AA were selected as bioactive molecules to modulate immune responses and promote myelin remodeling (Fig. 5A). To achieve controlled sequential release, we encapsulated AA in HNTs and incorporated into the spinning solution, while SP was adsorbed onto PDA-coated fibers formed by posttreatment with dopamine solution (Fig. 5B and fig. S12). Fluorescence labeling revealed distinct tetramethyl rhodamine isothiocyanate (TRITC) and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) signals corresponding to HNT-loaded AA and PDA-bound SP, respectively (Fig. 5C). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) confirmed the encapsulation of AA within HNTs by detecting characteristic elemental signatures of the nanotube carriers (Fig. 5D).

Drug release kinetics (Fig. 5E) revealed a distinct temporal profile for SP and AA. SP exhibited a rapid-release profile, with more than 80% (80.98 ± 1.95%) released within 3 days and plateauing by day 8. In contrast, AA exhibited a sustained release pattern, continuing beyond the SP plateau and stabilizing around day 30. This sequential release strategy enables early immunomodulation by SP, followed by prolonged delivery of AA to support axonal extension and myelin remodeling during the matrix regeneration phase.

To validate the immunomodulatory function of SP, we used a lipopolysaccharide-induced macrophage polarization model. Macrophages are central regulators of postinjury inflammation, and their phenotypic polarization profoundly affects nerve regeneration. While classically activated M1 macrophages amplify neuroinflammation by releasing proinflammatory mediators, alternatively activated M2 macrophages facilitate inflammation resolution and promote tissue repair (7, 47). Upon SP treatment, immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked reduction in M1 macrophage proportion and a concomitant increase in M2 macrophages (Fig. 5F). Consistently, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis showed significant down-regulation of M1-associated genes and up-regulation of M2 markers (Fig. 5G), confirming that SP effectively reprograms macrophages toward a proregenerative phenotype.

To further elucidate the role of AA in promoting myelin remodeling, we evaluated its effect on Schwann cell function during the early regenerative phase. At this stage, repair-type Schwann cells (repair SCs) are crucial for clearing myelin debris and resolving inflammation, thereby facilitating the restoration of immune homeostasis (40, 48). Following myelin debris exposure, Schwann cells actively internalized fragmented myelin, as indicated by immunofluorescence staining for myelin protein zero (MPZ) (Fig. 5H). The proportion of MPZ-positive Schwann cells was significantly higher in the AA-treated group (48.54 ± 12.41%) than in the control group (28.65 ± 8.81%; P < 0.01) (Fig. 5I). In parallel, qPCR analysis demonstrated that AA treatment up-regulated repair SC–associated genes and key regulators of myelin biosynthesis (Fig. 5J), suggesting that AA enhances both the debris-clearing efficiency and remyelination capacity of Schwann cells.

Beyond modulating Schwann cell behavior, AA also promoted axonal development. In neural epithelial organoid cultures, AA treatment significantly enhanced axonal sprouting (Fig. 5K), as evidenced by increased branching in Sholl analysis (Fig. 5L). After 7 days of induction, the mean axon length in the AA-treated group reached 329.30 ± 118.70 μm, significantly longer than that of the control group (174.90 ± 76.37 μm; P < 0.01) (Fig. 5M). The maximum axon length also increased markedly in the AA group (437.20 ± 148.10 μm) compared to controls (271.10 ± 106.40 μm; P < 0.05) (fig. S13). Furthermore, a greater number of Schwann cells adhered to the extending axons (Fig. 5N), and upon continued induction, these cells expressed myelin basic protein (MBP) (fig. S14), indicating their participation in axonal myelination.

AFMC@SP@AA promotes inflammation resolution and Schwann cell–driven Wallerian degeneration at early regenerative stages

To construct a conduit capable of modulating early immune responses and promoting axonal regeneration, we first optimized the concentrations of SP and AA for dual incorporation (figs. S15 and S16). At the optimal ratio, SP and AA were embedded into the AFMC membrane, generating AFMC@SP@AA. In vitro assays confirmed that this formulation promoted macrophage polarization toward a proregenerative M2 phenotype (fig. S17) and enhanced axonal extension from neuroepithelial organoids (fig. S18). The AFMC@SP@AA membrane was then coiled and inserted into a dual-layered sheath to form the complete conduit, which was implanted into a 2-cm rat sciatic nerve defect model for in vivo evaluation (Fig. 6A). At 14 days postimplantation, immunofluorescence analysis of longitudinal sections revealed early regenerative responses (Fig. 6B). Four key locations along the regenerating nerve were examined: the proximal suture site (position 1), proximal entry zone of the nerve bridge (position 2), distal exit zone (position 3), and distal suture site (position 4). Axons in the AFMC@SP@AA group extended significantly farther than those in the Hollow, AFMC, and AFMC@SP groups (Fig. 6C), while no significant difference was observed compared with the AFMC@AA group. Quantification of Schwann cells at positions 2 and 3 showed markedly higher infiltration in all structurally guided groups versus Hollow (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, scaffolds incorporating SP alone or in combination with AA significantly reduced M1 macrophage polarization and promoted M2 differentiation compared to the AFMC group (Fig. 6, E to G, and fig. S19). Notably, SP demonstrated stronger immunomodulatory efficacy than AA, and dual incorporation of SP and AA resulted in the highest M2/M1 ratio.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying AFMC@SP@AA-mediated nerve regeneration, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on regenerated tissues harvested from the conduits 14 days postimplantation. Differential gene expression analysis using DESeq2 (49) identified 106 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the AFMC@SP@AA group compared to the AFMC group, including 78 up-regulated and 28 down-regulated transcripts (fig. S20 and table S1). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the up-regulated DEGs revealed significant activation of the adenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling pathway and several lipid metabolism–related pathways (Fig. 6H). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment further indicated increased expression of genes involved in Schwann cell dedifferentiation (40), phagocytosis (48), and lipid droplet metabolism (50) (Fig. 6, I to J), suggesting that AFMC@SP@AA promotes a repair-associated Schwann cell response during early nerve regeneration. Consistently, histological analysis of the distal degenerative region (position 4) showed reduced residual myelin, increased lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 6, K to M), and higher densities of activating transcription factor 3 (ATF-3+)/S100β+ and nerve growth factor receptor (NGFR)+/S100β+ Schwann cells in the AFMC@SP@AA group than in the AFMC group (fig. S21, A to D), supporting enhanced Schwann cell reprogramming and accelerated Wallerian degeneration in vivo.

Ex vivo sciatic nerve explants cultured on AFMC@SP@AA films showed more refined myelin fragmentation and increased degraded myelin products compared with the AFMC group (Fig. 6, N to P, and fig. S22). In parallel, cultured Schwann cells exposed to AFMC@SP@AA exhibited enhanced myelin debris phagocytosis, increased lipid droplet accumulation, and up-regulated reprogramming-related markers at both the immunofluorescence and protein levels (figs. S23 and S24). Together, these results support that AFMC@SP@AA promotes Schwann cell–mediated myelin clearance and a repair-associated Schwann cell phenotype.

AFMC@SP@AA enables autograft-comparable and behaviorally superior nerve regeneration at 3 months postimplantation

Three months postimplantation, the regenerative efficacy of the AFMC@SP@AA conduit was evaluated in a 2-cm rat sciatic nerve defect model (Fig. 7A). As shown in Fig. 7B, all retrieved conduits were encapsulated by fascia rich in capillaries without evidence of scar formation (fig. S25). The explanted conduits remained grossly intact without obvious fracture or collapse, and the regenerated nerve tissue showed preserved internal architecture in both longitudinal and cross-sectional views (fig. S26). Immunofluorescence staining of the bridging regions (positions 2 and 3) demonstrated that regenerated nerves within structured conduits (AFMC, AFMC@SP, AFMC@AA, and AFMC@SP@AA) displayed morphological characteristics comparable to those of the Autograft group. In the AFMC@SP@AA group, Schwann cells were elongated and aligned along regenerated axons, forming well-organized nerve fascicles (Fig. 7B). By contrast, the Hollow group exhibited disorganized axonal regrowth, accompanied by infiltration of nonneuronal cells and irregular axon distribution. Axons in this group failed to effectively span the bridging zone and were observed only near the proximal interface. Vascular density did not differ significantly between the AFMC@SP@AA group and the other groups (Hollow, P = 0.18; AFMC, P = 0.70; Autograft, P = 0.67) (Fig. 7, C and D, and fig. S25). MBP immunofluorescence staining showed greater myelination in the AFMC@SP@AA group than in the Hollow and AFMC groups, with no significant difference from the Autograft group (Fig. 7E and fig. S25). Consistently, axonal density across the proximal, bridge, and distal regions of the regenerated nerve was significantly higher in the AFMC@SP@AA group than in the Hollow group and comparable to that in the Autograft group (fig. S27). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the distal regenerated nerves further confirmed an increased proportion of myelinated axons in the AFMC@SP@AA and Autograft groups (Fig. 7F). To quantify myelin maturation, we calculated the g-ratio, defined as the ratio of the inner to outer diameter of myelinated fibers. A lower g-ratio indicates more complete myelination (9). The g-ratio followed the trend: Hollow (0.73 ± 0.11) > AFMC (0.63 ± 0.15) > AFMC@SP@AA (0.51 ± 0.08) ≈ Autograft (0.50 ± 0.09), with no significant difference between AFMC@SP@AA and Autograft groups (P = 0.98) (Fig. 7G). Similarly, the thickness of regenerated myelin in the AFMC@SP@AA group was comparable to that in the Autograft group (P = 0.99) (Fig. 7H). For comparison with normal nerve tissue, the g-ratio, MBP-positive cell density, and vessel density of native rat sciatic nerve are shown in fig. S28 as physiological benchmarks. Despite the favorable regenerative performance of the AFMC@SP@AA group, these parameters remained distinct from native nerve tissue.

Motor function recovery was assessed using the CatWalk system (Fig. 7A). As rats walked across an illuminated glass platform, a high-speed color camera positioned beneath the walkway captured the footprints in real time. Representative images from consecutive gait cycles are shown in Fig. 7Ia. Footprints from the nonoperated side (NS), highlighted in pink, display consistent patterns across all groups, characterized by normal toe extension. In contrast, footprints from the operated side (OS), marked in green, revealed varying degrees of motor dysfunction. Long-term denervation resulted in muscle atrophy of the hind paw, leading to excessive toe curling and reduced ground contact, often limited to the heel or a portion of the sole. Successful nerve regeneration restored control over the toe extensor and flexor muscles, allowing visible toe extension during locomotion (51). Notably, distinct extended toe imprints were observed only in the AFMC@SP@AA and Autograft groups. In contrast, the Hollow, AFMC, AFMC@SP, and AFMC@AA groups showed incomplete functional recovery, with toe prints exhibiting overlapping or curled patterns (Fig. 7Ia and fig. S29).

Figure 7Ib displays a 3D stress map generated from plantar pressure data collected by the CatWalk system. In this map, the xy plane represents the footprint area, while the z axis indicates peak pressure intensity. Both the AFMC@SP@AA and Autograft groups exhibited symmetrical pressure distribution between the left and right hindlimbs, with higher peak pressures observed at the heel and toe regions. Quantitative analysis showed that the stance phase duration of the injured limb was significantly longer in the AFMC@SP@AA group than in the Hollow group (P < 0.0001) and the AFMC group (P < 0.05), while remaining comparable to that in the Autograft group (P = 0.36) (Fig. 7L). In addition, the sciatic function index (SFI) was markedly improved in the AFMC@SP@AA group compared to the Hollow group (P < 0.001), with values closely matching those of the Autograft group (P > 0.99) (Fig. 7M).

Chronic denervation associated with long-gap nerve repair often leads to autotomy behaviors in rats, such as toe biting, due to sensory deprivation and neuropathic pain (52). Statistical analysis of autotomy scores revealed that the AFMC@SP@AA group significantly reduced both the incidence and severity of autotomy, even outperforming the Autograft group (Fig. 7, J and K). Electrophysiological evaluations further confirmed functional restoration (Fig. 7N and fig. S29). Compared to the Hollow (P < 0.001) and AFMC groups (P < 0.05), the AFMC@SP@AA group demonstrated significantly shorter latency periods (Fig. 7O), with values comparable to those observed in the Autograft group (P = 0.36). In addition, compound muscle action potentials (CMAPs) (Fig. 7P) in the AFMC@SP@AA group were significantly higher than those in the Hollow (P < 0.005) and AFMC (P < 0.05) groups and showed no statistical difference from the Autograft group (P = 0.74).

Muscle atrophy, a common outcome of prolonged denervation in long-gap nerve injuries (53), was evident in all groups after 3 months (fig. S30). As expected, the Autograft group exhibited the least atrophy, consistent with its role as the clinical gold standard. Notably, the AFMC@SP@AA group demonstrated significantly better preservation of muscle mass compared to the Hollow group. Histological staining further revealed more organized and densely packed muscle fibers in the AFMC@SP@AA group, indicating effective reinnervation and structural restoration comparable to that of the Autograft group (fig. S30).

AFMC@SP@AA restores nerve function in 5-cm beagle defects with autograft-comparable outcomes

To assess the translational potential of AFMC@SP@AA, we extended our investigation beyond the rodent model. In large mammals, peripheral nerve defects exceeding 3 to 4 cm are considered critical-sized injuries (54). To further evaluate the conduit’s regenerative capacity under clinically relevant conditions, we established a challenging 5-cm sciatic nerve defect model in beagle dogs. The AFMC@SP@AA conduit was used as a bridging scaffold, with autografts serving as the control (Fig. 8A). As shown in Fig. 8B, the implanted conduits exhibited a uniform brownish appearance and exceeded 5 cm in length. Notably, they retained morphological integrity without signs of kinking even after mechanical bending. By 6 months postimplantation, the scaffold had substantially degraded and was replaced by regenerated nerve tissue that appeared robust, vascularized, and well integrated. The regenerated nerve displayed seamless continuity with the host nerve at both proximal and distal junctions, closely mimicking native nerve morphology.

To assess functional recovery, we used a high-precision motion capture system to track and analyze gait patterns and joint movements following nerve repair (Fig. 8G). At 6 months postsurgery, the maximum running speed in the AFMC@SP@AA group reached 7.00 ± 1.00 km/hour, with no significant difference from the Autograft group (6.33 ± 1.52 km/hour; P = 0.56) (Fig. 8H). 3D joint motion reconstruction (Fig. 8G) visualized the complete gait trajectory throughout a full cycle, which consists of a stance phase (when the foot is in contact with the ground) and a swing phase (when the foot is lifted). Joint flexion and extension amplitudes reflect locomotor recovery, with greater angular variation indicating improved joint mobility (55). In the AFMC@SP@AA group, distinct transitions between flexion and extension were observed in the injured limb (green), suggesting restored motor control (Fig. 8G). In contrast, the Autograft group presented a more variable outcome, with one animal each exhibiting no autotomy (grade 0), mild autotomy (grade 1), and moderate autotomy (grade 2), respectively.

DISCUSSION

Although more than 10 NGC products have been approved by FDA (4), their indications remain limited to sensory nerve defects under 3 cm or prevention of neuroma formation (3, 5). For long-gap mixed or motor nerve injuries, such as those involving the sciatic or brachial plexus nerves, autologous nerve grafting remains the only widely recognized option (56). This clinical limitation arises from the dual complexity of peripheral nerve repair. First, peripheral nerves have a highly organized hierarchical structure, including aligned endoneurium, supportive perineurium, and protective epineurium. Existing conduits have difficulty simultaneously replicating this structural organization and maintaining mechanical stability in three dimensions (4, 15, 18). Second, the postinjury microenvironment undergoes distinct temporal phases, transitioning from inflammation and debris clearance to axonal extension and remyelination, which demands precise temporal regulation from the implant conduits. However, most available NGCs remain passive in biological function and lack the capacity for stage-specific intervention (2, 57, 58). To address these challenges, we developed the AFMC@SP@AA conduit, which integrates hierarchically organized topographies with spatiotemporally controlled small-molecule delivery. Structurally, a hybrid wet spinning and 3D printing approach generated an interconnected network of aligned fibers and microchannels, supported by a bilayered epineurium-mimetic sheath, thereby enhancing both axonal guidance and lumen stability. Functionally, dopamine-mediated surface modification and nanotube-based encapsulation enabled sequential release of SP and AA, providing phase-specific regulation of macrophage polarization and neural cell behavior. In both rat (2 cm) and beagle (5 cm) models, this system significantly improved axonal regeneration and functional recovery.

Multiscale topographical architectures are widely applied in tissue engineering and have shown clear benefits in neural regeneration (39, 59). Cellular responses depend strongly on scale: Micrometer-scale features regulate polarity, adhesion, and migration, whereas larger structures (hundreds of micrometers to millimeters) support fascicle alignment and tissue integration (12). Therefore, achieving structural synergy that transitions from cell-level guidance to tissue-level integration is a critical step toward functional nerve repair. However, most biomimetic designs remain restricted to a single level (15), such as micro-/nanofibers for cell alignment (7–9) or microchannels for bundle orientation (10–12). Attempts to combine features, such as aligned fibers with grooves (39, 60) or photolithography-based micro-/nanopatterns (61, 62), have largely yielded planar constructs lacking 3D spatial fidelity, with evaluations often limited to cellular outcomes. To overcome these limitations, we used a one-step integration strategy to fabricate a 3D conduit with preserved architectural fidelity. The AFMC core combined oriented microchannels for bundle-scale guidance and aligned microfibers for cell-scale alignment (Fig. 2A). Control conduits with fibers only (AF) or channels only (MC) confirmed that AFMC uniquely enhanced Schwann cell alignment and migration (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S9) and promoted axonal extension and fasciculation within a nerve organoid model (Fig. 3, E to G, and fig. S6). Notably, AFMC exhibited self-organized groove-like patterns and interfibrillar microchannels generated by sequential microfiber deposition (Fig. 2, A and E), which facilitated directional migration and molecular exchange. Permeability assays confirmed improved transport of both small and large molecules, which may explain the reduced apoptosis observed in subcutaneous models (Fig. 4, G and I). Together, these results demonstrate the strong biocompatibility and integration potential of the AFMC conduit.

Structural stability is critical for the long-term guidance of nerve conduits. Clinical practice has shown that conduits frequently undergo deformation or displacement in dynamic environments such as muscle compression and joint traction (17), and insufficient suture retention strength is a major cause of postoperative dislocation (18, 63). However, many current designs neglect compressive resistance, bending tolerance, and suture strength, limiting their use in high-stress regions requiring prolonged stability (15). Conduits made from common polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), polyglycolic acid, and polycaprolactone are biocompatible but remain prone to irreversible deformation or luminal collapse under mechanical loading (61). This issue remains underexplored in the literature. In contrast, PLCL exhibits superior flexibility, elasticity, and tensile properties, offering better mechanical adaptability (13). It has been widely applied in various flexible implants [e.g., nerve conduits (13, 64, 65), artificial blood vessels (66, 67), and injectable microspheres (68)] and has been clinically adopted in nerve repair products such as NEUROLAC and NEUROCAP, demonstrating strong translational potential. We therefore selected PLCL as the base material to enhance stability and surgical operability. Nonetheless, flexible materials alone cannot fully prevent structural failure resulting from prolonged deformation. In particular, highly oriented fibers and microchannel structures remain vulnerable to luminal closure during repeated loading (69, 70), which severely compromises axonal penetration. To address this, we reinforced the aligned core with a woven perineurium-mimetic layer to resist cyclic deformation (Fig. 2, B and C). In addition, a dual-layer epineurium-mimetic outer sheath was constructed, composed of an outer porous mesh layer and an inner dense support layer. The former helps dissipate external compressive stress and prevents radial collapse, while the latter enhances luminal strength and improves suture retention (Fig. 2, F and K to N). Collectively, this composite structure maintains morphological stability after undergoing stretching, compression, bending, and torsion (movie S2), markedly improving intraoperative handling while preserving long-term structural stability and sufficient mechanical support during degradation (fig. S36). These enhancements provide a solid foundation for reliable axonal regeneration.

Nerve regeneration is a dynamic process requiring coordinated activity of immune and neural cells (19). A single topographical cue may provide initial guidance but cannot meet the evolving demands of the repair microenvironment. Thus, temporally precise regulation of macrophage polarization and neural cell function is critical for effective regeneration. Prior attempts to deliver immunomodulatory factors such as interleukin-4 (IL-4) (47) and IL-10 (71), or neurotrophic factors such as NGF (9) and glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) (2), have been hindered by inherent instability, high production costs, and potential off-target effects of protein-based therapeutics (19). To overcome these barriers, we selected two small-molecule compounds (i.e., SP and AA) as temporally programmed modulators. Both agents have been approved by the FDA as clinical dietary supplements, with defined pharmacokinetic characteristics, low immunogenicity, and favorable barrier permeability (34, 72). Through multitarget mechanisms, they contribute to immune modulation and neural regeneration, thus demonstrating strong translational potential. On the basis of this rationale, we developed a composite delivery system aligned with the temporal rhythm of nerve repair. SP was rapidly released via PDA-mediated surface immobilization (Fig. 5, B, C, and E), while AA was encapsulated in HNTs within the PLCL matrix for sustained release over 30 days (Fig. 5, B, D, and E). In a rat model of 2-cm sciatic nerve defect, this system significantly enhanced endogenous regeneration. Compared with blank or single-molecule groups, the dual delivery conduit enhanced axonal bridging, myelination, functional recovery, and autotomy reduction (Fig. 7). These findings validate the feasibility of a temporally controlled small-molecule delivery strategy in promoting nerve regeneration.

We propose that the composite small-molecule delivery system promotes nerve regeneration through two sequential mechanisms: stage-specific immunomodulation and sustained support for remyelination. In the first phase, ~80% of SP and 30% of AA are released within 3 days postsurgery, jointly shifting the microenvironment toward a proregenerative state, as evidenced by enhanced M2 macrophage polarization and up-regulated anti-inflammatory gene expression (Fig. 5G and 6, E to G). Mechanistically, SP promotes autophagy and modulates protein acetylation to drive macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby efficiently alleviating early inflammation and reshaping the immune microenvironment (72). AA provides auxiliary support by reducing proinflammatory signaling and assisting Schwann cell–mediated debris clearance (33), further reinforcing the proregenerative niche (Fig. 5J). However, early immune modulation alone is insufficient for sustaining axonal elongation and myelin reconstruction, which require continuous regulatory signaling. In the second phase, sustained AA release over 30 days provides prolonged stimulation for nerve maturation. This involves enhanced collagen deposition, accelerated basal lamina reconstruction, and epigenetic modulation through DNA demethylation (35, 73, 74), activating genes essential for myelin biosynthesis. In neural organoid models, AA significantly promoted axonal elongation and branching (Fig. 5, K to N) and increased the expression of myelination-associated transcription factors (Fig. 5J), confirming its role in remyelination and neural maturation. Thus, this system establishes a dynamic regenerative microenvironment by temporally coordinating SP-initiated immune resolution with prolonged AA-driven neural maturation, offering mechanistic insight for the development of advanced NGCs.

Following encouraging outcomes in small animal models, we further evaluated the regenerative potential of the conduit under more stringent conditions. Prior studies show that when gaps exceed 3 cm, particularly in large animals, the efficacy of conventional treatments declines sharply (18). To assess translational potential, we validated AFMC@SP@AA in a 5-cm beagle sciatic nerve defect model. At 6 months postimplantation, the conduit supported robust axonal regeneration and remyelination (Fig. 8, C to F), with electrophysiological performance comparable to autografts (Fig. 8, J to L). Treated animals regained coordinated gait (Fig. 8, G to I, and figs. S32 and S33) and showed reduced autotomy (fig. S35). Motor recovery is a critical criterion for determining the clinical efficacy of nerve conduits. According to the British Medical Research Council grading system, antigravity movement (≥M3) defines the threshold for meaningful recovery (56). A large retrospective analysis (1980 to 2020) reported that even with autografts, fewer than 40% of patients with defects > 3 cm achieved M3 or better and for mixed nerve injuries > 4 cm, the rate fell below 13% within 6 months (75). Notably, all conduit-treated beagles reached independent running ability (M3) within 6 months, and one achieved jumping ability (movie S3), indicating near-complete recovery (M5) across hip, knee, and ankle joints. This provides strong evidence of substantial motor function recovery with a synthetic conduit in a 5-cm mixed nerve defect model in canines, surpassing prior benchmarks reported for autografts.

This study systematically validated the regenerative efficacy and cross-species applicability of our nerve conduit. Compared with representative previously reported studies summarized in table S5 (2, 10, 17, 38, 57, 60, 76–88), our conduit showed promising structural, functional, and translational potential for nerve repair. Despite the promising results, further long-term evaluations are warranted in models that more closely mimic human physiological conditions. Key parameters such as biocompatibility and pharmacokinetics under pathological conditions will be critical in determining the clinical feasibility of this approach. Building upon the design principles established here, future strategies may involve patient-derived iPSC-based neural organoids to create a “neural relay” for amplified regenerative signaling. In addition, incorporating microenvironment-responsive modifications could enable smart delivery, further enhancing therapeutic precision and efficacy. These strategies could bring this platform closer to personalized, disease-targeted clinical translation. In summary, our conduit achieved autograft-comparable regeneration across species and gap lengths without exogenous cells or proteins. It establishes a broadly applicable design framework for nerve conduit development and offers a practical, translatable strategy for peripheral nerve repair.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Poly(l-lactide-co-ε-caprolactone) (PLCL; 50:50 molar ratio, intrinsic viscosity: 2.6 to 2.8 dl/g) was purchased from Daigang Biomaterials Co. Ltd. (Jinan, China). Hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), l-ascorbic acid-2-phosphate trisodium salt, and xylene were obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Ethanol and acetone were supplied by Tianjin Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Tianjin, China). SP trihydrochloride was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and dopamine hydrochloride was obtained from Innochem (Beijing, China). HNTs were provided by Runwo Materials Technology Co. Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Rhodamine and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (10 to 12 weeks old, 360 to 460 g) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. Six adult male beagle dogs (9.5 to 10.5 kg, 8 to 12 months) were obtained from the Marshall Bioresources Beijing (China). Animal experimental groupings are provided in table S2. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experiments Ethical Committee of the First Central Hospital Affiliated to Nankai University (approval no. 2023-SYDWLL-000159). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering through the use of appropriate anesthesia and postoperative care.

Fabrication of hierarchical microtopology nerve conduit (AFMC conduit)

A 10% (w/v) spinning solution was prepared by dissolving PLCL in HFIP. Continuous fibers were extruded through a 22-gauge needle into anhydrous ethanol and collected on a rotating cylindrical collector to form a multilayered fiber membrane (core). The deposition sequence created a bilayer structure with cross-woven fibers (layer 1) and aligned fibers (layer 2).

PVA fibers were 3D printed onto layer 2, with the deposition direction parallel to the PLCL fibers. Additional layers of aligned (layer 3) and cross-woven fibers (layer 4) encapsulated the PVA template. After immersion in deionized water to remove the PVA, a multichannel fiber membrane was obtained (core).

For the outer sheath, PLCL fibers were collected on a tungsten rod via a wet spinning system. A 1:1 mixture of dichloromethane and anhydrous ethanol was sprayed onto the fibers to form a dense inner layer (13.30 ± 2.38% porosity), which was cured and dried. Additional fiber deposition formed a porous outer layer (43.37 ± 5.77% porosity). After removal from the rod, the double-layer woven tubular structure was formed. Last, the composite fiber membrane (core) was rolled and inserted into the lumen, completing the AFMC conduit assembly.

Loading of SP and AA onto the AFMC scaffold (AFMC@SP@AA)

l-Ascorbic acid 2-phosphate sodium (sodium ascorbate) was dissolved in ultrapure deionized water at a concentration of 50 mg/ml at room temperature. HNTs were added to the ascorbate solution at a weight ratio of 1:2 (HNT:AA), followed by 1 hour of ultrasonication to ensure homogeneous dispersion and prevent aggregation. To facilitate the infiltration of ascorbate into the lumen of the HNTs, cyclic vacuum treatment (degassing and repressurization) was performed using a vacuum pump. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged, and the resulting precipitate was freeze dried for 24 hours to obtain ascorbate-loaded HNTs (HNT@AA).

The HNT@AA powder was then incorporated into the PLCL spinning solution, and multichannel fiber membranes were fabricated using the previously described wet spinning and layer-stacking procedure. The rolled fiber membrane was inserted into the preformed sheath to construct the ascorbate-loaded conduit (AFMC@AA).

To introduce SP, the AFMC@AA scaffold was immersed in a dopamine hydrochloride solution (2 mg/ml in 10 mM tris-HCl buffer, pH 8.5) at 37°C for 12 hours to promote PDA coating formation. After rinsing three times with deionized water, the scaffold was incubated in an SP solution (1 mg/ml in 10 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.5) at 37°C for 24 hours. Following another round of triple rinsing and freeze drying, the final dual-functionalized scaffold (AFMC@SP@AA) was obtained, featuring surface-adsorbed SP and lumen-encapsulated ascorbate.

Characterization of the scaffold

The morphology of the dried samples was examined using an optical microscope (S8AP0, Leica, Germany). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Phenom Pro, Phenom-World BV, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) was used to analyze microstructures, while EDS and elemental mapping (JSM-7800F, Japan) were used for compositional analysis. Atomic force microscopy (Bruker, USA) was applied for force spectroscopy, and surface topography was assessed using WLI (NanoSystem, NV-2700, Korea). Rhodamine and FITC were used to simulate the incorporation of AA and SP, respectively, and the labeled fibers were imaged via confocal laser scanning microscopy (Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

To evaluate mechanical performance, the NGCs were tested using a universal mechanical testing system (HY-30080, China) at room temperature. Before testing, all samples were presoaked in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37°C for 12 hours to simulate physiological hydration conditions. For compression testing, 15-mm conduit sections were placed between two parallel plates and compressed at a rate of 1 mm/min until 50% strain. Resilience was assessed under cyclic compression (strain: 50%, speed: 5 mm/min), with each sample tested 100 times. For tensile testing, the ends of the conduits were secured in clamps and pulled at a rate of 10 mm/min. Real-time stress and strain data were recorded to evaluate mechanical behavior. Suture retention strength was tested by suturing one end of the conduit with a 3-0 suture, fixing the other end in a machine collet, and applying traction at 0.5 mm/min (n = 3 per group).

In vitro release of SP and AA

Scaffolds loaded with SP and ascorbate were cut into 1 cm–by–1 cm squares and immersed in 1 ml of PBS (pH 7.2) within Eppendorf tubes. The samples were incubated at 37°C on an orbital shaker, and the release medium was collected and replaced at predetermined time points. The concentration of released SP was quantified using an SP enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Fusheng, China), while ascorbate levels were determined by ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at a wavelength of 245 nm, using a standard calibration curve.

Growth and development of neuroepithelial organoids on membrane scaffolds

Undifferentiated human iPSCs were seeded onto Matrigel-coated six-well plates (BD Biosciences) and cultured in PGM1 medium (CELLAPY Biological Technology), with daily medium changes. When the cell confluence reached 60 to 70%, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 0.5 mM EDTA at 37°C for 1 min. After gentle rinsing, the cells were resuspended in PGM1 medium containing 10 μM Y27632 (MCE) and seeded at a 1:4 ratio into low-attachment six-well plates.

After 24 hours, embryoid bodies formed and were transferred to a neural induction medium (NIM) composed of DMEM/F-12 (Invitrogen), 1% (v/v) N2 supplement (Gibco), 1% (v/v) B27 supplement without vitamin A (Invitrogen), 1× GlutaMAX (Invitrogen), and 1× minimum essential medium nonessential amino acids (Invitrogen). Differentiation was initiated on day 0 by supplementing NIM with 3 μM SB431542 (Tocris Bioscience), 3 μM LDN193189 (Sigma-Aldrich), and 3 μM CHIR99021 (MCE), with medium changes every 2 days.

On day 8, the induced neuroepithelial organoids were transferred onto sterilized membrane scaffolds and cultured in NIM for an additional 7 days. Organoids were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and subjected to immunofluorescence staining to assess axonal outgrowth, which was quantified using the ImageJ software.

Collective migration of Schwann cells on membrane scaffolds

A custom-designed two-well culture insert (fig. S8) was used to generate a defined cell-free gap for migration assays. Sterilized membrane scaffolds with different topographies were placed into 12-well plates, and the inserts were positioned on top of the membranes. The wells were rinsed three times with PBS and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) before adding complete DMEM medium.

RSC96 Schwann cells were cultured in 10-cm dishes until reaching full confluence. The cells were labeled with Dil (Uelandy, China) at a final concentration of 1 μM for 20 min at 37°C, followed by washing with fresh culture medium. Labeled cells were then seeded onto the scaffolds. After 12 hours of incubation to allow cell adhesion, the inserts were carefully removed to establish a 2-mm-wide cell-free gap.

The collective migration of Schwann cells into the gap was monitored at 24-hour intervals using total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy (Thunder, Leica, Germany). The migration distances were quantified using the ImageJ software.

Evaluation of nerve conduit infiltration by neural cells

To assess cellular infiltration into the nerve conduits, a mixed suspension of PC12 and RSC96 cells (1:1 ratio, total density of 1 × 106 cells/ml) was prepared. Sterilized nerve conduits (2 mm in diameter, 5 mm in length) were immersed in the cell suspension and dynamically seeded using a miniature rotating scaffold seeder for 2 hours. The conduits were then transferred to fresh culture plates and maintained under static conditions for an additional 3 days. After incubation, the conduits were fixed in 4% PFA, cryosectioned, and subjected to immunofluorescence staining to evaluate cell infiltration within the lumen.

For in vivo assessment, subcutaneous implantation was performed in 8- to 10-week-old male SD rats. A 1.5-cm dorsal incision was made, and subcutaneous pockets were created by blunt dissection. Sterilized conduits (2 mm in diameter, 10 mm in length) were inserted into the pockets, and the incisions were closed with 3-0 sutures. To minimize pain and distress, all animals received perioperative analgesia. Specifically, meloxicam [1 to 2 mg/kg, subcutaneously (sc)] was administered ~30 min before surgery and once daily for 2 to 3 days postoperatively, depending on clinical assessment. Each rat received two implants (n = 6 total; 2 per animal). At 7 and 14 days postimplantation, tissues surrounding the implants were harvested and fixed in 4% PFA. H&E staining and immunofluorescence analysis were performed to assess cell infiltration. Imaging was performed using a total internal reflection fluorescence microscope (Thunder, Leica, Germany), and ImageJ was used for quantitative analysis.

Rodent peripheral nerve surgery and transplantation in a 2-cm defect model

Peripheral nerve repair was conducted in male SD rats (360 to 460 g, 10 to 12 weeks old). Animals were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, and a 2-cm sciatic nerve defect was created under a surgical microscope by excising a nerve segment from the left hind limb. For the rat model, conduits with an inner diameter of 2 mm and a length of 2 cm were used to bridge the sciatic nerve defect. Retraction of the nerve stumps was allowed to ensure a true 2-cm gap. In the experimental groups, nerve conduits were sutured to the proximal and distal nerve ends using 9-0 nylon sutures. For the Autograft group, the excised nerve segment was rotated 180° and reimplanted. Muscle and skin layers were closed with 6-0 and 3-0 sutures, respectively. Perioperative analgesia was provided as previously described for nerve repair using subcutaneous meloxicam (1 to 2 mg/kg, pre- and postoperative). Animals were housed under standard laboratory conditions and euthanized at predetermined points for histological and functional evaluation.

RNA sequencing

At 2 weeks postimplantation, regenerated nerve tissues from the Hollow, AFMC, and AFMC@SP@AA groups were harvested for transcriptomic analysis. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Library construction and sequencing were performed on the Illumina platform by Metware Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

DEGs were identified using DESeq2 with significance thresholds set at |log2foldchange| ≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using KOBAS, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Canine peripheral nerve surgery for a 5-cm segmental sciatic nerve repair model

A large-animal model of sciatic nerve defect was established using six adult beagle dogs (9.5 to 10.5 kg, 8 to 12 months old). All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Experiments Ethical Committee of the First Central Hospital Affiliated to Nankai University and conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Tianjin Committee on the Use and Care of Laboratory Animals. The dogs were randomly divided into two groups: AFMC@SP@AA conduit group (n = 3) and Autograft control group (n = 3). General anesthesia was induced and maintained via endotracheal intubation using 1 to 4% isoflurane. The left sciatic nerve was exposed through a skin incision and blunt muscle dissection. A 5-cm segment of the sciatic nerve was excised to create a critical-sized defect.

For nerve repair, the AFMC@SP@AA conduit group received tubular implants sutured to the proximal and distal nerve stumps using 6-0 nylon. Conduits with an inner diameter of 4 mm and a length of 5 cm were used for the beagle sciatic nerve repair model. In the Autograft group, the excised nerve segment was reversed and reimplanted using the same suturing method. Muscle and skin layers were closed using 3-0 sutures. All animals received perioperative analgesia to minimize pain and distress in accordance with institutional and international guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. Specifically, meloxicam (0.2 to 0.4 mg/kg, sc, once daily) was administered systemically, and bupivacaine (1.0 to 2.0 mg/kg) was locally infiltrated at the surgical incision site before wound closure. Animals were monitored at least once daily throughout the recovery period for signs of pain, infection, or other complications. At 6 months postsurgery, all dogs were humanely euthanized under deep anesthesia with intravenous pentobarbital sodium (100 to 150 mg/kg), in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020) and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (eight edition).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SEM, unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, USA). For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied, depending on the experimental design. A nested t test was used for analyses involving hierarchical data structures, such as multiple measurements per biological replicate. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Statistical significance was represented as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. For the in vivo studies, animal numbers were determined on the basis of the experimental design and prior experience with similar nerve regeneration studies, and the exact sample size for each experiment is provided in the corresponding figure legends and table S2. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during histological scoring and image quantification.
