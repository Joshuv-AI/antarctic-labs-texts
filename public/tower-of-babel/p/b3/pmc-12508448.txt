A hybrid approach for enhancing pseudo-labeling in medical images through pseudo-label refinement
Authors: Behnam Rahmati, Shahram Shirani, Zahra Keshavarz-Motamed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Segmentation of medical images is critical for the evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment of various medical conditions. While deep learning-based approaches are the dominant methodology, they rely heavily on abundant labeled data and face significant challenges when data is limited. Semi-supervised learning methods mitigate this issue but there are still some challenges associated with them. Additionally, these approaches can be improved specifically for medical images considering their unique properties (e.g., smooth boundaries). In this work, we adapt and enhance the well-established pseudo-labeling approach specifically for medical image segmentation. Our exploration consists of modifying the network’s loss function, pruning the pseudo-labels, and refining pseudo-labels by integrating traditional image processing methods with semi-supervised learning. This integration enables traditional segmentation techniques to complement deep semi-supervised methods, particularly in capturing fine edges where deep models often struggle. It also incorporates the smoothness of the edges in the segmentation and achieves a balance between deep learning and traditional methods through tunable parameters. Moreover, to address the problem of noisy or unreliable pseudo-labels, we utilize uncertainty-based pixel-level and image-level pruning of the pseudo-labels using a specific loss function, thereby improving the accuracy and robustness of the segmentation. We evaluated our approach on three different datasets from two imaging modalities (CT and MRI) and demonstrated its superior performance, highlighting its accuracy and robustness in the presence of limited labeled data. With only 15% of the labeled data, on the Sunnybrook Cardiac dataset, our approaches increased endocardium segmentation accuracy from 82.1% to 87.5%, and epicardium segmentation from 82.5% to 86.7%. On the COVID-19 CT lung and infection segmentation dataset, our approach improved left lung segmentation accuracy from 72.5% to 79.3%, and right lung segmentation from 75.8% to 81.6% when using only 15% of labeled data. On the Automated Cardiac Diagnostic Challenge dataset, with just 10% of labeled data, our approach increased endocardium segmentation from 91% to 93.7%, myocardium from 69.8% to 74.5%, and right ventricle from 76.7% to 82.1%. Our codes will be published in https://github.com/behnam-rahmati.

Introduction

Image Segmentation is a fundamental and challenging computer vision task with various applications in clinical and medical fields. In medical cases, segmentation is crucial for analyzing the performance of different organs and vital systems, such as the cardiac and respiratory systems. For instance, segmenting the left ventricle, right ventricle, and myocardium is essential for assessing cardiac function, detecting abnormalities, and guiding interventions such as surgeries or implantations. Precise delineation of these structures allows for the evaluation of heart wall motion, quantification of ejection fraction, and detection of conditions like hypertrophy or cardiomyopathies. Similarly, segmenting the left and right lungs is vital for identifying pathologies such as tumors, infections, COVID-19, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Accurate lung segmentation aids in monitoring disease progression, planning radiation therapy, and ensuring the correct delivery of treatments. Segmentation methods based on the Vision Transformers (ViTs)1 such as SAM2, and Detr3, or based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), such as U-net4, fully convolutional neural networks5 , DenseNet6, ResNet7, and DeepLab8, represent the leading approaches for this task. While these networks have shown great promise for image segmentation, achieving optimal performance often requires large-scale pixel-level annotations, which might be unavailable or hard to obtain especially for medical cases. Annotation of medical images is often time-consuming, prone to human errors, and requires the presence of an expert all contributing to making the annotations highly expensive. However, unlike the labeled annotations, unlabeled acquisitions are often available and combining them with the labeled data may offer a solution to the lack of labeled data challenge9.

Semi-supervised learning (Semi-SL) approaches leverage the unlabeled data in addition to the labeled ones aiming to alleviate the lack of labeled data problem. They are beneficial in cases where few annotations are provided in addition to a large set of unlabeled data. Several Semi-SL approaches have been proposed including pseudo-labeling10, consistency-regularization11–13, co-training14, generative models27, and graph-based models28. However, they might fail in some cases leading to unrobust training of the networks. They are also not specifically designed for medical images and using them can involve a number of challenges29 which should be resolved before application on medical images.

Traditional image processing approaches, although rarely used in the deep learning-dominated era, have promising advantages that make them suitable candidates for refining Semi-SL approaches. They offer reliable performance and robustness regardless of the availability or quality of labeled data, and can address challenges faced by deep learning approaches when incorrect predictions arise due to the scarcity of labeled data.

Related works

Medical image segmentation

Medical image segmentation is essential for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and disease monitoring, and thus remains a vital computer vision task. However, challenges such as variability in imaging conditions, inter- and intra-observer annotation inconsistencies, and the high cost of expert labeling complicate the development of reliable models. These challenges have spurred a surge of recent studies focusing on the segmentation task within the medical imaging domain.

For example, DA-TransUNet17 proposed a transformer-based model with dual attention mechanisms to enhance segmentation performance. FKD-Med18 introduced a federated learning framework utilizing knowledge distillation to improve segmentation efficiency while preserving data privacy. Another significant contribution is the Mutual Inclusion Mechanism for Precise Boundary Segmentation (MIPC-Net)19, which focuses on accurate boundary detection in medical images by incorporating mutual inclusion of position and channel attention.

More recently, Adaptive Bidirectional Displacement (ABD)43 introduces confidence-guided bidirectional patch perturbations to enhance consistency regularization for semi-supervised segmentation. In parallel, Cross Prompting Consistency with SAM (CPC-SAM)44 integrates the Segment Anything Model as a prompting regularizer to improve training efficiency and label propagation in low-annotation regimes. These recent methods demonstrate how emerging paradigms such as prompt-based learning and displacement-driven consistency are shaping the next generation of medical image segmentation algorithms. Since lack of labeled data is a major challenge in medical images, several recent works have focused on semi-supervised learning methods such as pseudo-labeling in medical domains.

Pseudo-labeling

Pseudo-labeling is a widely used category of semi-supervised learning (Semi-SL) techniques10,30–35, where labels are assigned to unlabeled data, treating these labels as ground truth. The model evaluates its predictions on the unlabeled data, and the label corresponding to the highest predicted probability is selected as ground-truth to re-train the model, effectively minimizing entropy9.

Pseudo-labeling algorithm

The algorithm can be performed iteratively or in only one iteration. The one-hot hard thresholding assigns each sample to the class with the highest probability.

Various techniques have been developed to improve the quality of pseudo-labels in pseudo-labeling approaches. MixMatch15 enhances pseudo-labels by averaging predictions from multiple augmentations. Methods like FixMatch16, ReMixMatch31, and UDA20 use confidence thresholds to create pseudo-labels for weakly augmented samples, which are then used as annotations for strongly augmented ones. To further refine pseudo-labeling, some approaches dynamically adjust the thresholds21,22. Symbolic knowledge, such as logic rules, has been employed to identify and correct potential prediction errors23. FreeMatch26 introduces a self-adaptive mechanism that adjusts the pseudo-labeling confidence threshold according to the current training state, thereby minimizing the impact of noisy pseudo-labels. Additionally, numerous recent works have utilized pseudo-labeling, making significant contributions to the field of medical image segmentation24,25,36–38.

Deformable models

Deformable models39–41 are classical methods used in image segmentation. These models represent curves or surfaces within an image domain that can adaptively change shape under the influence of internal and external forces. Internal forces help maintain the smoothness of the model during deformation, while external forces, derived from the image data, guide the model toward object boundaries or other relevant features.

By ensuring that boundaries remain smooth and incorporating prior knowledge about an object’s shape, deformable models demonstrate resilience against image noise and gaps, enabling the integration of boundary elements into a consistent mathematical framework. Deformable models have been widely and effectively used in medical image segmentation42,47–51. For instance, studies such as42,47 applied deformable models as a post-processing method to enhance the predictions of deep neural networks.51 introduced a fully unsupervised automatic segmentation approach for the left ventricle using deformable models. Additionally,49 combined deformable models with deep learning to improve the network’s segmentation accuracy, while48 integrated deformable model theory directly into the loss function of a deep neural network.

Although deformable models are widely used for medical image segmentation, they present several challenges. For example, while they may improve pseudo-labels in certain regions, they can also degrade results in others, as demonstrated in Fig. 2. A comprehensive analysis of these issues is presented in section 5.4.

Total variation

Total variation in image processing is widely used to minimize differences between neighboring pixels, ensuring a smooth and continuous output. This technique plays a significant role in regularizing optimization tasks, particularly in medical image segmentation. By leveraging total variation, noise and artifacts can be effectively reduced, while preserving critical details like edges. As a result, it has become a common method in various medical image segmentation applications due to its ability to maintain the integrity of essential image features.52,53.

Model uncertainty

Uncertainty estimation techniques are employed to evaluate the confidence or stability of the network’s predictions. Common methods include Bayesian deep learning54–56, ensemble models57,58, and dropout59,60. Despite these options, many semi-supervised learning approaches favor pixel-wise self-entropy due to its straightforward nature.

Methods

Overview

In this work, we propose an approach for enhancing pseudo-labeling in semi-supervised learning (semi-SL) for medical image segmentation. This enhancement is applied in three steps: 1) reliable pseudo-label selection. 2) a hybrid semi-SL approach combining pseudo-labeling and deformable models, 3) incorporating a total variation regularization term. Reliable pseudo-label selection

One of the biggest challenges associated with pseudo-labelling is noisy/ incorrect pseudo-labels. In such cases, the initial network is not well trained and predictions on the unlabeled data may result in wrong pseudo-labels. Using these pseudo-labels as ground-truth will lead to unrobust networks. To address this challenge and to prevent overfitting to incorrect labels, they should be detected and discarded or refined before re-training the network. The confidence of the network’s predictions can be utilized to estimate the reliability of each pseudo-label.

There are various methods for confidence and uncertainty estimation of predictions. Self-entropy is used in this work, due to its simplicity. Pixel-level filtering

An alternative approach is to focus on utilizing reliable pixels within each image, rather than relying on entire reliable images for re-training the network. Using this approach, after the unreliable pixels (of each image) are detected, the network is trained only on the reliable ones using a masked cross-entropy loss.

Hybrid Semi-SL approach combining pseudo-labeling and deformable models

However, the network’s predictions might fail to capture the edges in some cases which is a challenge in pseudo-labeling. We use a deformable model’s approach39 to refine the incorrect pseudo-labels. We also utilize a shape prior term to strike a balance between the network’s predictions and the deformable model’s output. Total variation regularization term

Although deformable models will assist the network in the cases that it has missed the boundaries, post-processing the pseudo-labels with the deformable models refines only the training data. It does not guarantee the re-trained network’s predictions.

To ensure that the predictions of the re-trained network have smooth and less noisy boundaries, we modified the re-training network’s loss function to penalize the noisy predictions. This loss function modification was achieved by incorporating a total variation regularization term.

This term is used to discourage sharp variations.13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathcalligra{\scriptstyle L}\hspace{000.1cm} = \mathcalligra{\scriptstyle L}\hspace{000.1cm}_{original} + \lambda \mathcalligra{\scriptstyle L}\hspace{000.1cm}_{TV} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcalligra{\scriptstyle L}\hspace{000.1cm}$$\end{document} is the overall loss function, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcalligra{\scriptstyle L}\hspace{000.1cm}_{original}$$\end{document} is the original loss function of the network, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcalligra{\scriptstyle L}\hspace{000.1cm}_{TV}$$\end{document} is the total variation loss term. Enhanced Pseudo-labeling for Medical Image Segmentation

In our proposed approach, the self-entropy threshold for pseudo-label pruning was determined through a grid search across a range of values, evaluated on the provided datasets to optimize the trade-off between retaining reliable pseudo-labels and filtering noisy ones. This ensured high-confidence labels were prioritized for re-training. Lower thresholds risked including unreliable predictions, while higher thresholds reduced the training set excessively, both impacting segmentation quality.

For image-level pruning, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcalligra{\scriptstyle R}\hspace{000.1cm}(X^{u})$$\end{document} criterion (Equation 3) was tuned to exclude high-uncertainty images, enhancing model robustness. Pixel-level pruning employed a masked cross-entropy loss to focus on reliable pixels.

Post-pruning, deformable models (Section 3.3) refined pseudo-labels by adjusting contours to better capture anatomical boundaries, guided by shape priors to prevent over-deformation. The deformable model parameters, including shape prior, length, and region weights, were tuned via grid search on a subset of images to balance boundary smoothness and fidelity to initial predictions. For more details about the parameter selection and tuning, see the discussion subsection.

Experiments

Setup

Datasets

We evaluated our method on three publicly available datasets of MRI, and CT images including Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Challenge (ACDC)61, Sunnybrook Cardiac Data (SCD)62, and COVID-19 CT Lung and Infection Segmentation Dataset (CCLISD)63.

ACDC dataset Includes 150 volumetric MR image sequences from different patients, which comprise a total of 1981 Cine MRI scans. The dataset contains ground truth data for the right ventricle, as well as the endocardial and epicardial walls of the left ventricle. For our experiments, we pooled all 2D slices from the train and online sets, shuffled them, and randomly selected 400 2D images for training. For the test set, we shuffled all slices from the official test set and randomly selected our test images.

SCD Dataset Includes cine MRI of 45 subjects with different cardiac conditions. The dataset is available in three parts each part including 15 subjects: training, validation, and online. For each subject, there are roughly 200-300 DICOM images available where the annotations are only available for the end-diastole and end-systole phases. In our experiments, we shuffled each split separately and randomly sampled training and test images from the training and test subjects, respectively

CCLISD Dataset This dataset contains labeled COVID-19 CT scans from 20 patients and each scan includes 200-300 2D images. Left lung, right lung, and COVID-19 infections ground-truths are provided. In our experiments, we pooled all 2D slices (across all subjects), shuffled them, and randomly selected slices for training and testing.

Performance metrics

We calculated the Dice index and Jaccard index as the evaluation metrics. Dice index calculates the overlap by comparing the intersection to the sum of set elements, while the Jaccard index measures the intersection over the union of the sets. Having two different regions A and B, Dice and Jaccard indexes are defined below.15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & Dice(A,B) = \frac{2 \cdot |A \cap B|}{|A| + |B|} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & Jaccard (A,B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Networks and details

We implemented the Dense-net6, DeepLabv3+8, 2D U-net4, and the FCN architecture of64 for comparing the fully supervised segmentation results. For our semi-supervised approach, we used the FCN architecture of64, and 2D U-net due to their relatively low number of parameters which is suitable for the low data regime.

We randomly selected 400, 400, and 300 2D images from the provided images of SCD, ACDC, and CCLISD respectively as the training images and 50 images as the test images. From the training part, in our Semi-SL approach, we randomly selected different portions of labeled data and the rest of our selected training set was used as unlabeled data. We reported these portions in Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10. For the Sunnybrook dataset, we used 5%, 10%, 15%, and 25% of labeled data for endocardium segmentation, and 7%, 10%, and 25% for epicardium segmentation. For the CCLISD dataset, we used 7%, 10%, 15%, and 20% for the left lung, and 7%, 15%, and 20% for the right lung. For the ACDC dataset, we used 7%, 10%, and 20% of labeled data for all three anatomical structures: endocardium, myocardium, and right ventricle. The 7% split was included to probe the model’s performance under low-supervision scenarios and to analyze sensitivity to small variations in label availability.

We center cropped the images into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$256\times 256$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100\times 100$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$150\times 150$$\end{document} from CCLISD, SCD, and ACDC respectively. When choosing random images, to avoid the potential impact of selection bias in our results, we selected the train set randomly 3 times and averaged the results.

We compared our method with supervised results, PL10, and also investigated the effect of each of the four stages of our framework: 1- Refining the pseudo-labels with deformable models (DM). 2- Combining deformable model’s refinement and the total variation regularization term in the loss function (DM + TV). 3- The previous terms plus pixel-level pruning of the pseudo-labels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{prune}$$\end{document}). 4- The previous terms plus the image-level pruning of the pseudo-labels (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{prune}$$\end{document}). We also compared our method with a segmentation adapted version of FixMatch. It is worth noting that FixMatch is originally designed for semi-supervised learning object classification task. In order to adapt it for segmentation, we had to make some modifications. We used augmentations such as vertical and horizontal flip, affine transformation, gaussian blur, gaussian noise, and elastic transformation. More details about implementation of the FiXmatch will be released in our Githup page.

Discussions and conclusion

Deformable models vs total variation

As discussed earlier, deformable models refine the boundaries in the cases where the networks have incorrect predictions. However, this modification applies only to the pseudo-labels and does not guarantee the smoothness of the final predictions. To guarantee the smoothness of the boundaries of the segmentation contours in the medical images we modified the loss function and incorporated a total variation (TV) loss. We compared the effect of the Deformable models (DM) and (DM+TV) on average in Table 11. Figure 10 also demonstrates the effect of these terms. Pixel-level vs image-level discarding

We proposed two different methods for discarding unreliable pseudo-labels: pixel-level filtering and image-level filtering. For pixel-level filtering, we employed a masked cross-entropy loss, as detailed in Section 3.2.2. In the case of image-level filtering, we introduced a criterion for evaluating pseudo-labels at the image level, as described in Section 3.2.1.

While image-level discarding of the unreliable pseudo-labels is not well-studied, our experimental results show that it has more effect than pixel-level discarding (Tables 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10).

Parameter optimization

We optimize each group of parameters independently, as they do not influence each other’s performance. For examples, the optimization of deformable model parameters is decoupled from the training and testing phases, since their effectiveness can be directly evaluated by inspecting the resulting contours. To tune these parameters, we randomly selected a subset of images and random subset of pre-trained model checkpoints. We then applied deformable models to refine the initial predictions from those checkpoints (supervised only checkpoints). This involved conducting a grid search over a range of values for each parameter.

Similarly, for the training and pseudo-labeling parameters, we performed independent grid searches. Given that the training parameters are commonly shared across most semi-supervised learning methods, we initialized them based on values reported in the literature and further tuned them by sweeping across linear and/or logarithmic scales.

While our current approach involves per-dataset tuning of hyperparameters, several strategies could mitigate this need in future work. One direction is to treat these parameters as learnable and optimize them jointly with model weights using bilevel optimization or gradient-based techniques (e.g., GradNorm). Alternatively, adaptive loss weighting schemes that scale regularization terms based on uncertainty or prediction confidence could dynamically adjust these parameters during training. Meta-learning across multiple datasets is another promising approach to identify hyperparameter configurations that generalize well, reducing or eliminating the need for manual tuning.

Challenges and limitations of deformable models

Deformable models have the capacity to improve segmentation results under ideal conditions and when supported by appropriate priors. Nevertheless, there are situations where these models underperform, resulting in flawed masks. In such cases, relying solely on deformable models to refine the initial masks produced by deep learning can actually degrade the outcome. Limitations

While this approach addresses many issues, it has two notable limitations. Firstly, it requires careful tuning of various parameters, such as the shape prior term and the total variation term, as well as the initial contour. For instance, setting a very high shape prior term can overly restrict the evolution of deformable contours, even when they could substantially improve upon the initial pseudo-labels generated by neural networks. This restriction can hinder contour evolution in both beneficial and detrimental scenarios. We employed a grid search to optimize these parameters and enhance performance. Secondly, deformable models incur high computational costs, resulting in a training process that is slower compared to pseudo-labeling. The third limitation of our work is the lack of 3D context or awareness. The networks are trained on 2D images and, during inference, generate segmentations for individual 2D slices, which are stacked afterward. However, future work can incorporate 3D connectivity across slices, 3D shape, and inter-slice variations by training a dedicated 3D-aware network. Finally, the last limitation of our work is using relatively small train and test datasets.

Computational cost

Although deformable models are computationally expensive, in our semi-supervised learning framework, they are used only during the generation of training data (pseudo-labels) and not during inference. This means that, although training may require more computational resources, the inference phase remains extremely fast and lightweight. Given this, our model can be trained with reasonable computational resources, while inference can be performed on very limited hardware. For instance, in scenarios such as real-time ultrasound analysis in ambulances, where computing resources are minimal, our method can run efficiently on edge devices—unlike other models that require powerful GPUs. Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a semi-supervised learning framework for tackling the lack of labeled data problem in medical image segmentation. Our framework introduces a hybrid approach combining the pseudo-labeling approach with the traditional image processing methods, introduces a novel re-training loss function using total variation and masked cross-entropy terms, and prunes unreliable pseudo-labels through uncertainty-based, pixel-level, and image-level pruning criteria. The framework was assessed on three datasets from CT and MRI modalities, showcasing its performance through comprehensive experimental results.
