Assessing the nutritional equivalency of DL-methionine and L-methionine in broiler chickens: a meta-analytical study
Authors: Roya Asasi, Hamed Ahmadi, Mohammad Amir Karimi Torshizi, Rasoul Vaez Torshizi, Farid Shariatmadari
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe purpose of this study was to perform a meta-analysis comparing the biological efficiency of DL-methionine and L-methionine (DL-Met and L-Met) in broiler nutrition. This analysis was based on a dataset comprising experiment results published in peer-reviewed papers since 2007. All experiments investigated the response of broilers (aged 0–21 d) to different dietary concentrations of DL- and L-Met, achieved by supplementing crystalline Met. A graphical analysis was conducted on the database using ellipses coverage and nonparametric density techniques. Two methods of linear and nonlinear exponential meta-regression analysis were used to determine relative bioavailability based on average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) responses to dietary Met sources. The analyses of ADG and FCR obtained from both linear and exponential models showed a relative difference between the tested methionine sources. The results showed that both ADG and FCR were improved in L-Met compared to DL-Met supplemented diets. Linear regression analysis revealed that DL-Met was 94.97% (for ADG) and 95.63% (for FCR) as efficacious as L-Met (P < 0.01). In contrast, the analysis of the fitted nonlinear exponential model showed that the biological efficiencies of DL-Met were 91.33% and 76.57% of the values for L-Met for ADG (P = 0.01) and FCR (P = 0.09), respectively. Based on the meta-analytical results, an equivalence of relative biological efficiency of DL-Met in comparison with L-Met for young broilers could not be confirmed.Key words: bioefficacy, broiler, meta-analysis, methionine

INTRODUCTION

Efficient utilization of available crystalline amino acids (AAs) is crucial for achieving optimal growth, minimizing costs, and reducing the environmental impact of commercial broiler production. Methionine (Met) is considered the first limiting amino acid in corn-soybean meal-based diets for poultry (Dilger and Baker, 2007), and its functional roles are critical for the development and overall health status of broiler chickens (Luo and Levine, 2009; Wang et al., 2019).

In commercial poultry production, all the crystalline AAs used as supplements are in their natural utilizable form, that is, L-isomer, except for Met. Methionine may be utilized in its synthetic racemic mixture of D-Met and L-Met isomers, commonly referred to as DL-Met. However, the birds need to convert the D-Met form into L-Met to make it available for protein synthesis and other essential metabolic functions. It is unclear if this conversion process is 100% efficient and if all the supplemented D-isomer can be converted into L-isomer for further utilization (Shen et al., 2015).

The small intestine can absorb both D- and L-Met through diffusion or multiple carrier-mediated transport systems. However, biological utilization of D-Met is dependent on its conversion to L-Met, as observed in previous studies (Gilbert et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). The liver and kidney of poultry have specific enzymatic pathways developed to produce L-Met from isomers and analogs of Met, suggesting that birds can utilize these compounds (Thwaites and Anderson, 2007; Fang et al., 2010).

The relative biological efficiency of DL-Met and L-Met is a topic of controversy. While some reports suggest that D- and L-Met have equivalent efficacy (Dilger and Baker, 2007; Dilger et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2017), others indicate that D-Met is inferior when compared for biological efficacy (Ribeiro et al., 2005; Shen et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2019). Consequently, the consensus on the equivalency of Met sources remains undecided.

Meta-analysis is a reliable method to merge the results of various studies into a single statistical framework that provides general valid inferences (Van Houwelingen et al., 2002; Ahmadi and Rodehutscord, 2012). Both simple and complex structured meta-analyses, which include treatment effects and explanatory variables across several experiments, can be performed using statistical linear and nonlinear mixed models (Sauer et al., 2008), as well as more advanced models (Siegert et al., 2015). Several experiments and analyses have been published for comparing Met sources in broilers (Jansman et al., 2003; Kratzer and Littell, 2006; Vazquez-Anon et al., 2006; Sauer et al., 2008; Zelenka et al., 2013; Shen et al., 2015; Agostini et al., 2016; Esteve-Garcia and Khan, 2018; Wang et al., 2019). It would be beneficial to evaluate these published studies together in order to examine the hypothesis of the nutritional equivalency of DL- and L-Met in broiler chickens for practical feeding recommendations.

The aim of this study is to perform a meta-analysis on the published data, using linear and exponential regression models, to investigate the efficiency of DL- and L-Met in broilers based on average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) as response variables.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was based on data obtained from the literature and did not involve the use of animals; thus, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approval was not obtained.

To ensure the relevance and quality of our data, we applied specific criteria for study inclusion. Our approach was to reduce the potential effects of publication bias by carefully including studies directly related to the comparison between DL- and L-Met in broiler chickens. Additionally, we addressed the common challenge of statistical heterogeneity in meta-analyses, stemming from variations in experimental design, by employing strategies aimed at its mitigation. This involved the deliberate selection of studies that shared comparable methodologies and concepts, resulting in a more cohesive conceptual framework among the studies included. These measures collectively may increase the reliability of our analysis and mitigated the potential impact of diverse experimental conditions on the validity of our conclusions.

Data Visualization and Analysis

Prior to conducting the statistical parametric analysis, a visualization process was conducted on the database using ellipses coverage and nonparametric density techniques developed in JMP Pro 14 (JMP, Version 14, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 1989–2023) as previously described in (Hyndman, 1996; Quinn and Keough, 2002).

Ellipses coverage is a visualization technique used to select a certain level of coverage for the data points. In this study, the technique was used to create a plot of the ADG and FCR with 90% confidence ellipses drawn for broiler chickens fed diets supplemented with either DL- or L-Met. Nonparametric density estimation is a way to estimate the probability density function of a random variable without making assumptions about its distribution. In the context of the current study, nonparametric density was used to visualize the density of the data points in the ADG and FCR plot. The density estimation was done using JMP Pro 14 software and displayed as 2 quantile density contours in different colors. The first contour included 50% of the smoothed density, while the second contour included 90% of the smoothed density. This allowed the researchers to better understand the distribution of data points and make more informed statistical decisions.

In order to compare the 2 sources of Met and determine the relative bioavailability (RBA) of DL-Met to L-Met, 2 different regression models were applied: multilinear regression equations and nonlinear exponential regression. The absolute level of supplemental DL-Met or L-Met was used for the x-axis in the regression analysis. The data for each response were analyzed using the MIXED and NLMIXED procedures of SAS 9.4 (SAS, Version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

The models fit was examined using R2, RMSE, and BIC while the difference between calculated those values are less than 5% suggests equal fit between models (Ahmadi, 2017). Based on this criterion, it appears that both the linear and exponential models had similar fit for both ADG and FCR.

In relation to ADG, the linear model exhibited a divergence from 100, with a RBA of DL-Met to L-Met calculated at 94.97 (P < 0.01). Conversely, the exponential model demonstrated a RBA of DL-Met to L-Met amounting to 91.33 (P = 0.01. DISCUSSION

From a visual standpoint, the initial step in any analysis involves graphically examining the data. This approach not only allows for the assessment of assumptions in planned analyses but also facilitates gaining familiarity with the dataset. Interpreting ellipse and nonparametric density plots of ADG and FCR against DL-Met or L-Met entails comprehending the relationship between these variables and the information visually presented by the plot. The more elongated shape of the ellipse in the DL-Met graph, coupled with a lower degree of correlation, indicates higher variability in ADG as DL-Met levels change. This suggests that different levels of DL-Met supplementation may result in a wider range of ADG responses. Since the ellipse orientation is the same for both graphs, it implies that L-Met has a similar influence on ADG and FCR as DL-Met. However, the less elongated shape of the ellipse in the L-Met graph, accompanied by a relatively higher degree of correlation (0.41 compared to 0.39), indicates relatively lower variability in ADG compared to DL-Met. This suggests that variations in L-Met levels may lead to a narrower range of ADG responses compared to DL-Met. Moreover, the calculated center point values represent the average ADG and FCR observed when DL-Met or L-Met is supplemented at specific levels. With a slightly higher center point value for ADG and a lower center point value for FCR, L-Met supplementation suggests a potential for slightly improved average ADG and FCR compared to DL-Met.

Numerous studies employing various approaches have been conducted to assess and compare the effectiveness of DL-Met and L-Met as dietary supplements (Jansman et al., 2003; Hoehler, 2006; Dilger and Baker, 2007; Sauer et al., 2008; Shen et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019). However, the findings of these studies have been inconclusive, likely due to variations in bird factors, experimental designs, statistical and data analysis methods, as well as the sensitivity of bioassays utilized. To obtain an unbiased and quantitative estimation of the relative efficacy of these nutrients, it is crucial to employ appropriate data analytical frameworks. Traditionally, linear and exponential regression models have been employed to compare the relative biological value of different methionine sources, with each source having its own specific response curve. By comparing the predictions of these models, a more nuanced and tailored evaluation of the efficiency of DL-Met and L-Met can be achieved (Vazquez-Anon et al., 2006; Esteve-Garcia and Khan, 2018). Additionally, this meta-analysis focused on carefully selecting studies directly pertinent to the DL- and L-Met comparison in broiler chickens to minimize the inclusion of less relevant ones, thereby reducing potential publication bias impact. Traditional methods for assessing publication bias, such as funnel plot analysis or Egger's test, were not utilized. Publication bias typically arises from a tendency to publish studies with positive or significant outcomes while excluding those with negative or nonsignificant results. In our case, the selection process aimed at studies directly addressing DL-Met and L-Met comparability in broiler chickens, with the belief that this selective approach helps mitigate the influence of publication bias on the findings.

In the present study, we utilized both linear and exponential regression models to evaluate the biological efficiency of these 2 methionine sources, aligning with previous studies that employed similar concept (Jansman et al., 2003; Hoehler, 2006; Sauer et al., 2008; Shen et al., 2015). Our meta-analysis results indicated that supplemental L-Met exhibited an advantage over DL-Met in promoting the performance of broilers at an early age (1–21 d).

Consistent with our findings, Wang et al. (2019) conducted a 21-day study on broiler chickens and estimated RBA of L-Met compared to DL-Met to be 141.5% for ADG and 189.1% for feed efficiency. In a separate study by Shen et al. (2015), which focused on growth performance, redox status, and gut development in young broiler chickens, L-Met supplementation exhibited superior effects compared to DL-Met. Specifically, L-Met supplementation was associated with improved redox status and enhanced gut development in chicks, along with better growth response compared to those fed diets with DL-Met. These advantages may be attributed to the higher efficiency of L-Met, relative to DL-Met, in protein synthesis in specific tissues such as skin and muscle in chickens (Saunderson, 1987; Fang et al., 2010). From a physiological perspective, animal cells can only utilize L-isomers of amino acids, while D-isomers must undergo conversion to the corresponding L-isomer before being incorporated into protein synthesis. Consequently, L-Met, rather than D-Met, demonstrates greater efficiency in promoting body weight gain in broilers through direct incorporation into protein biosynthesis. This inference is supported by various studies (Shen et al., 2015; Esteve-Garcia and Khan, 2018; Wang et al., 2019). However, some reports in broilers suggest that DL-Met and L-Met exert similar effects on growth performance and carcass characteristics (Hoehler, 2006; Dilger and Baker, 2007; Dilger et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2017). Consequently, it is crucial to exercise caution when interpreting data and research outcomes related to the effects of L-Met and DL-Met. In our study, we considered sex and age as random factors. Analyzing them separately would have split our data into smaller groups, making the meta-analysis less reliable. To overcome this, we adopted a broader approach by treating sex and age as potential sources of variation across the entire dataset. Treating them as random factors helped us account for differences related to methionine sources without compromising the reliability of our analysis. This approach, along with its reasoning, has been used and explained in previous research. While it would be ideal to analyze age and sex separately with a large dataset, many poultry nutrition studies lack the extensive data needed for this kind of analysis.

CONCLUSIONS

The meta-analytical study on the growth performance dataset revealed that supplementation with either DL-Met or L-Met had positive effects on overall weight gain and feed efficiency of young broilers (0–21 d of age). However, the findings also suggest that there may be a difference in the relative biological efficiency of DL-Met and L-Met for broilers. This is supported by the significant (P < 0.05) or nearly significant (P < 0.10) differences from 100 (i.e., similar efficacy) in the RBA of DL-Met to L-Met shown by both the linear and exponential models for ADG and FCR. Therefore, the hypothesis of the equivalency of relative biological efficiency of DL-Met and L-Met for young broilers could not be confirmed based on the results of this study.
