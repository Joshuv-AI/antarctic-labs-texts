Phosphorus-32 microspheres: A dual-modality transarterial radioembolization approach for hepatocellular carcinoma therapy and Anti-PD1 immunotherapy potentiation
Authors: Shipeng Dai, Xunzheng Su, Zhuozheng Li, Hongyu Wang, Li Liu, Yuchen Xie, Yue Chai, Yueran Chen, Zhaoyang Zhao, Bo Luo, Jie Kong, Yanshu He, Hengsong Cao, Maiqi Xin, Guoqiang Shao, Yadong Shi, Fei Xiong, Weiwei Tang, Jinhua Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransarterial radioembolization (TARE) is a key therapy for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) management and downstaging. While 90Y microspheres (glass/resin) are widely used, their clinical application is limited by complexity, short half-life, and high costs. Thus, novel radionuclide microspheres are crucial. This study developed phosphorus-32-loaded microspheres (32P-MS). In vitro, 32P-MS dose-dependently suppressed HCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while inducing apoptosis. In vivo, 32P-MS TARE achieved tumor vascular embolization, reducing tumor vol/wt (confirmed by Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography (PET-CT), Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining, TUNEL/Ki67 assays without systemic toxicity. RNA sequencing and mass cytometry analyses revealed 32P-MS upregulated FABP1+PD-L1+ myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC), linked to immunosuppression. Mechanistic investigations, including molecular docking, co-localization, and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, demonstrated that 32P-MS activated the FABP1/PPARG/PD-L1 axis in MDSC. Genetic ablation of FABP1 or pharmacological inhibition with Orlistat reversed PD-L1 expression and augmented anti-tumor efficacy. Combining 32P-MS with anti-PD1 therapy synergistically suppressed tumor growth, reduced MDSC infiltration, and reinvigorated CD8+ T cell activity, significantly improving treatment sensitivity. 32P-MS is a promising HCC therapeutic with dual anti-tumor and immune-modulatory functions, providing a compelling rationale for integrating radioembolization with immune checkpoint blockade to counteract immunosuppressive resistance in HCC.Keywords: Phosphorus-32-loaded microspheres, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Myeloid-derived suppressor cell, Transarterial radioembolization, PD1

Introduction

Primary liver cancers, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), are a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. While surgical resection combined with adjuvant chemotherapy remains the primary curative approach for localized disease, many patients present with inoperable tumors at diagnosis [2]. In such cases, locoregional therapies, including thermal ablation, chemoembolization, and radiation therapy, are essential treatment options [3]. Among these, transarterial radioembolization (TARE) has emerged as a versatile and effective strategy for tumor control and downstaging in hepatic malignancies [4]. TARE involves the intra-arterial delivery of radioactive microspheres directly to hepatic tumors, significantly prolonging time to progression in HCC patients with limited therapeutic alternatives [5].

Currently, TARE primarily utilizes glass or resin microspheres (20–60 μm in diameter) impregnated with radioactive yttrium-90 (90Y). These microspheres are delivered into the hepatic arterial system near the target lesion, where they become entrapped in the tumor's arterial vasculature, accumulating and undergoing radioactive decay to deliver localized tumoricidal radiation doses [6,7]. 90Y emits pure beta particles with an average half-life of 64.2 h and decay energy of 2.28 MeV, enabling the delivery of high radiation doses to tumors that are difficult to achieve with other modalities [7]. However, the clinical application of 90Y microspheres is not without challenges. The complex administration process carries a risk of microsphere diversion to non-target organs, such as the lungs and gastrointestinal tract, potentially leading to severe complications like intestinal perforation and radiation pneumonitis [[8], [9], [10]]. To mitigate these risks, the TARE procedure requires preliminary gastroduodenal artery embolization, comprehensive imaging evaluation of liver-lung shunting, and subsequent re-interventional embolization of tumor-supplying arteries [[8], [9], [10]]. Additionally, 90Y's relatively short half-life and substantial cost highlight the need for alternative radionuclide microspheres with improved therapeutic profiles for HCC treatment.

Phosphorus-32 (32P), a β-emitting radionuclide, offers unique advantages in targeted radiation therapy, particularly for localized tumors. Studies by RA Pattillo et al. [11] and Wen Gao et al. [12] have demonstrated the efficacy of 32P-chromic phosphate in treating disseminated intraperitoneal ovarian cancer and inhibiting postoperative occult lymphatic metastasis in lung cancer, respectively. Notably, 32P's lower β-particle energy (average 0.695 MeV) compared to 90Y results in reduced tissue penetration depth (average 1.2 mm), minimizing collateral damage to adjacent healthy tissues—a critical advantage for tumors located near sensitive structures such as bile ducts or gastrointestinal organs [13]. The extended half-life of 32P (14.3 days) provides logistical flexibility, allowing ample time for dose calibration, quality control, and precise administration. Additionally, its shorter radiation range enhances safety by reducing the risk of radiation-induced complications (e.g., hepatitis or gastrointestinal ulcers) and alleviating post-treatment radiation exposure concerns for caregivers [14]. Liu Lu et al. [15] demonstrated that 32P-glass microspheres administered at doses of 1830–3660 Gy in a human liver cancer xenograft model exhibited potent anticancer effects without damaging surrounding normal tissues, with significant tumor suppression observed within three days of intratumoral injection. However, glass microspheres have limitations, including their high specific gravity, which can lead to tube clogging and uneven distribution. Furthermore, glass microspheres require pre-loading with radionuclides before distribution, resulting in elevated production, transportation, and cost challenges. To address these limitations, we developed novel phosphorus-32-loaded microspheres (32P-MS) in this study. These microspheres were synthesized by emulsifying polypropyleneimine hydrochloride, partially neutralized with sodium hydroxide, in liquid paraffin, followed by crosslinking with epichlorohydrin. In preclinical studies using rat and mouse HCC models, TARE with 32P-MS effectively achieved tumor vascular embolization, significantly reducing tumor volume and weight, as confirmed by PET-CT imaging, HE staining, and TUNEL/Ki67 assays, all without inducing systemic toxicity. Mechanistic investigations revealed that 32P-MS activated the FABP1/PPARG/PD-L1 axis in myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC), as validated through molecular docking, co-localization, and co-immunoprecipitation assays. Importantly, the combination of 32P-MS with anti-PD1 therapy synergistically suppressed tumor growth, reduced MDSC infiltration, and reinvigorated CD8+ T cell activity, significantly enhancing treatment sensitivity compared to monotherapy approaches. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of 32P-MS as a dual-function agent with both antitumor and immune-modulatory properties, offering a promising strategy for advanced HCC treatment. While 90Y microspheres have established a significant role in TARE for HCC, 32P microspheres present unique advantages, including reduced tissue penetration, extended half-life, and enhanced safety profile. These characteristics position 32P as a promising alternative or complementary option in the evolving landscape of HCC treatment.

Results

Synthesis of 32P-loaded radioembolization microspheres

Cross-linked microspheres were obtained by emulsifying polyacrylimine hydrochloride partially neutralized with sodium hydroxide in liquid paraffin and then cross-linking it with epichlorohydrin. Dried microspheres were sieved into different particle sizes for embolization in rats (Fig. 1A–B) and the particle size of the microspheres was calculated (Fig. 1C–D). Optical micrographs and SEM images have validated the spherical morphology and uniform particle size distribution of microspheres. After swollen, the diameter of the microspheres becomes 1.5 times the initial value. The compression retention test of microspheres showed that at strains of 50 % and 80 %, the maximum forces were 682.5 gf and 1178.9 gf, respectively, and they were able to sustain without yielding for 10 s (Fig. 1E–F), indicating that the microspheres meet the mechanical strength requirements for TARE therapy.

Owing to the negative charge of phosphate ion, it can be effectively encapsulated by the positively charged microspheres. Microspheres exhibit rapid adsorption kinetics (Fig. 1G), achieving loading efficiencies of 63.1 ± 2.2 %, 81.5 ± 0.8 %,68.2 ± 5.3 %, 76.7 ± 2.9 %, and 86.2 ± 1.1 % at 5, 10, 30, 60, and 120 min, respectively, and a cumulative loading of 90.4 ± 3.2 % within 12 h. The phosphate loading has exceeded the required loading for the experiment in 1 h. Optical and SEM images of the microspheres after loading with phosphate ion show no significant change in the morphology of the microspheres (Fig. 1I–J). EDS analysis revealed that in addition to the chlorine and oxygen elements present in the microspheres, a characteristic peak of phosphorus was also observed (Fig. 1H), and the phosphorus element was uniformly distributed within the microspheres (Fig. 1K), confirming the successful encapsulation of phosphate ions in the microspheres. Given that the tumor microenvironment is weakly acidic, the release rate was determined in normal saline at pH 6.5. The results, as shown in Figure, indicate that the release rates of phosphate were 10.6 % ± 1.1 % at 1 h, 16.5 % ± 1.5 % at 2 h, 21.7 % ± 1.8 % at 4 h, 26.3 % ± 2.1 % at 8 h, 45.2 % ± 1.6 % at 12 h, and 34.5 % ± 2.8 % at 24 h. Ultimately, a cumulative release of 71.5 % ± 1.9 % phosphate ions was achieved within one week (Fig. S1A). Under neutral conditions, the phosphate release rates were 15.1 % ± 3.0 % at 1 h, 19.7 % ± 2.8 % at 3 h, 26.2 % ± 2.4 % at 6h, 30.1 % ± 2.2 % at 12 h, 34.1 % ± 1.8 % at 24 h, and 39.7 % ± 1.5 % at 48 h. Ultimately, a cumulative release of 52.0 % ± 1.1 % phosphate was achieved within one week (Fig. S1B). This indicates the capability for sustained local radiotherapy over an extended period.

32P-MS inhibits the proliferation, invasion and migration of HCC in vitro

To investigate the effect of 32P-MS on HCC cells, we treated HCCLM3 and Hep-3B cells with different doses (0.1 mCi, 3.7 MBq; 0.2 mCi, 7.4 MBq; 0.4 mCi, 14.8 MBq) of 32P-MS radiation. Colony formation and EdU assays showed that 32P-MS inhibited HCC cell proliferation, and the higher the dose, the more inhibited the cell proliferation (Fig. 2A–D). Flow cytometry revealed that 32P-MS facilitated cancer cell apoptosis (Fig. 2E–F), which increased with the increase of dose. Compared with the control group in the transwell assay, 32P-MS inhibited relative migration and invasion rate, the higher the dose, the inhibition of malignant phenotype of cells was more obvious (Fig. 2G–H).These results indicate that 32P-MS has good anti-HCC function in vitro, and the higher the dose, the better the anti-cancer effect.

TARE with 32P-MS shows good embolization performance and significantly inhibits tumor growth in SD rat HCC model

To verify the embolization and anticancer effects of 32P-MS, a drug-induced in-situ SD rat model of HCC was used for further validation. As shown in Fig. 3A, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) results showed that TARE with 32P-MS significantly embolized vessels compared to pre-treatment. Emission Computed Tomography (ECT) showed that the nuclides were mainly concentrated in the liver (Fig. 3B). Both transverse and longitudinal images of PET-CT showed that the tumors of the same rat after 7 days of treatment were significantly smaller than those before treatment (Fig. 3C). Tumor samples were taken for HE staining, and the results indicated that the tumor heterotypic nuclei in the 32P-MS-treated group were significantly lower than those in the control group and MS group (Fig. 3D). TUNEL staining indicated that the necrosis rate in the 32P-MS-treatment group was higher than that in the control group and MS group (Fig. 3E–F). Ki67 staining indicated that the tumor cell proliferation rate in the 32P-MS-treatment group was lower than that in the control group and MS group (Fig. 3G–H). In order to evaluate the effects of TARE with 32P-MS on other important organs, we used HE staining to confirm that compared with the control group, no more severe damage to the heart, lung, kidney,and spleen (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, 32P-MS treatment did not significantly promote liver fibrosis in rats (Fig. S2A). The results of the above drug-induced in-situ model of HCC in rats showed that the TARE with 32P-MS had good vascular embolization function, anticancer effect,and biosafety.

32P-MS inhibits tumor growth in C57BL/6 mouse HCC models and shows a good biosafety

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of 32P-MS against HCC, Hepa1-6 cells were implanted into C57BL/6 mice via subcutaneous injection. The mice were subsequently randomized into control, MS group and 32P-MS group to systematically assess the antitumor efficacy. According to the results, compared with control group, the volume and weight of the tumor decreased significantly after 32P-MS injection (Fig. 4A–C). TUNEL staining indicated that the tumor cell necrosis rate of the 32P-MS-treatment group was significantly higher than that of the control group, MS group and 32P group (Fig. 4D–E). Ki67 staining indicated that the tumor proliferation ability of the 32P-MS-treatment group was lower than that of the control group, MS group and 32P group (Fig. 4F–G). In addition, biosafety experiments suggested that the injection of the 32P-MS had no significant effects on the heart, spleen, lung and kidney of mice (Fig. 4H). 32P-MS did not induce liver fibrosis in mice(Fig. 4I, Fig. S2B). These results suggest that 32P-MS has excellent anticancer effects and good safety profile in mouse HCC subcutaneous tumor models.

32P-MS therapy results in an increased number of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC in HCC

To reveal the complex mechanism by which 32P-MS inhibits HCC cell progression, we embarked on an exploration of tumors from 32P-MS and control group using RNA sequencing. Our aim was to discern differentially expressed genes and pathways influenced by 32P-MS. The volcano map showed differentially expressed genes after 32P-MS was treated, 142 genes were up-regulated and 38 genes were down-regulated (Fig. 5A). The radar map shows the 30 up/down regulated genes with the lowest p-value (Fig. 5B). Based on the KEGG analysis results, it was observed that genes exhibiting differential down-regulation were significantly enriched in several key such as tumor and protein transport,while the up-regulated genes in pathways were mainly concentrated in chemokine secretion and neutrophil enrichment (Fig. 5C–D). To explore the changes of tumor microenvironment caused by 32P-MS, we detected subcutaneous tumor samples from mice injected with 32P-MS and control by mass cytometry. We recycled single, viable, and intact CD45+ immune cells from selected cells in the respective tissues. CD45+ immune cell aggregation and subgroup annotation were observed in all samples. In total, 41 distinct cell clusters were identified, each defined by specific markers corresponding to the respective cell type (Fig. 5E–G). The results showed that compared with the control group, the relative proportion of endothelial cells, epithelial cells, eosinophils, macrophages, NK cells in the 32P-MS group decreased, while the relative proportion of fibroblasts, MDSC increased (Fig. 5H–I). Both RNA sequencing and mass cytometry results showed that 32P-MS administration significantly inhibited cancer cells, but had activation effects on myeloid cells, which aroused our interest to further study its molecular mechanism. Furthermore, we performed mass cytometry on tumor tissues from mice treated with 32P-unloaded microspheres. The results demonstrated that the administration of empty MS exerted only a marginal effect on the tumor immune microenvironment, with no significant changes observed in the enrichment of MDSC (Fig. S3A–S3H). These findings strongly suggest that the modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment is primarily driven by 32P-MS rather than the microsphere carrier itself.

MDSC is myeloid suppressor cells, which can exert immunosuppressive function through various ways and mechanisms, and thus play an indirect role in inhibiting immune response [16]. Firstly, we analyzed scRNA-seq data (GSE149614) under the isolation of 10 patients with HCC tumor tissues. The UMAP plot demonstrated that 10 common immune cell clusters were named according to marker identification and merger (Fig. 6A–C). For example, the B cell cluster mainly expressed CD79A, IGLC3, IGHG2 and the DC cluster mainly expressed CD1C, CD1E, IDO1 (Fig. 6C). As displayed in Fig. 6D, histograms indicate the specific expression of each cell cluster in different samples. For example, the expression of T/NK cells in tumor group was lower than that in normal group. The expression of SMC, fibroblast, MDSC and macrophage was higher than that in normal group (Fig. 6D).These results suggest that MDSC may play an important role in HCC progression. We further carried out a detailed analysis of myeloid cells and found that myeloid cells were mainly divided into 6 cell populations, which were DC-CD1C, DC-CLEC9A, MDSC-ITGAM, Mono-S100A8, TAM-GPNMB and TAM-LYVE1 (Fig. 6E–F). We found that FABP1 and PD-L1 genes were highly enriched in the MDSC subgroup (Fig. 6G). To our surprise, the results of the mouse tumor mass cytometry showed that FABP1 and PD-L1 proteins were also highly expressed in the MDSC subgroup of the 32P-MS group (Fig. 6H–I), which coincided with the two results. We further demonstrated by immunofluorescence staining that the expression of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC in tumor tissues of mice in the 32P-MS group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 6J), which was consistent with the sequencing results. These results indicate that 32P-MS causes the increase of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC while fighting cancer, which may be a potential factor leading to HCC recurrence. Targeting FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC immune cell population after TARE with 32P-MS treatment can further enhance the anti-tumor effect in HCC.

FABP1 promotes the expression of PD-L1 by regulating PPARG, thus maintaining the function of MDSC in HCC

However, what is the molecular mechanism of the increase of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC? Why does 32P-MS treatment lead to simultaneous increase of FABP1 and PD-L1 in MDSC? Previous literature found that FABP1 can promote the expression of PPARG in TAM, leading to the increase of PD-L1 [17]. We further verified in MDSC cells that the expressions of FABP1, PPAGR, and PD-L1 were all increased after the addition of 32P-MS, and these proteins were significantly inhibited after the addition of orlistat, an inhibitor of FABP1 (Fig. 7A, Fig. S4A–S4B). Molecular docking indicates the specific sites of interaction between FABP1 and PPAGR proteins (Fig. 7B). Co-localization suggests that FABP1 and PPAGR were co-localized in the cytoplasm of MDSC, but PPAGR is also abundant in the nucleus (Fig. 7C). Co-IP experiments demonstrated that the FABP1 and PPAGR indeed interact in MDSC (Fig. 7D). Further experiment verified that after adding FABP1 inhibitor Orlistat to MDSC stimulated by 32P-MS, the expressions of FABP1, PPARG and PD-L1 were reduced compared with those in the 32P-MS group alone (Fig. 7E–G, Fig. S4C). In vivo experiments in FABP1−/− mice further demonstrated that the tumors significantly decreased after the combination of 32P-MS compared with the control group (Fig. 7H–J). Ki67 staining indicated a decreased proliferation ability, and the expression of MDSC in the combination group was significantly reduced compared with the non-combination group (Fig. 7K–L). The above results indicated that 32P-MS caused the increase of FABP1 in MDSC, and further regulated the expression of PPARG to promote the increase of PD-L1. Inhibition of FABP1, combined with 32P-MS, can effectively inhibit HCC progression.

32P-MS significantly enhances the sensitivity of anti-PD1 in HCC treatment

At present, PD1 monoclonal antibody is the most widely used immunosuppressant for HCC cancer treatment in clinical practice, but its effective rate is only 30 %. It is urgent to enhance the sensitivity of PD1 monoclonal antibody in HCC therapy. Since 32P-MS can lead to the increase of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC, we adopted the strategy of combining microspheres with anti-PD1 to further evaluate the expansion of the use of 32P-MS. The results showed that the tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in the combination group compared with the treatment alone (32P-MS, anti-PD1) (Fig. 8A–B). Ki67 staining showed lower tumor proliferation in the combined group than in the single group (Fig. 8C). In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed that the number of MDSC cells in the combined treatment group was significantly lower than that in the single 32P-MS treatment group (Fig. 8E–F), the number of CD8+ T cells was higher than that in the single treatment group (Fig. 8D–F), and the number of CD8+ exhaustion T cells was significantly lower than that in the single treatment group (Fig. 8D–F). These results suggest that 32P-MS can significantly enhance anti-PD1 sensitivity in HCC treatment, and the combination of 32P-MS can effectively inhibit the progression of HCC and reshape the immunosuppressor microenvironment.

Discussion

TARE has emerged as a viable and promising therapeutic modality for HCC. However, the advent of immunotherapy has ushered in a new era in HCC treatment. Notably, cancer immunotherapies, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), have revolutionized the clinical management of HCC in recent years. The combination of bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) and atezolizumab (anti-PD1) has demonstrated superior efficacy compared to the traditional first-line therapy, sorafenib [18]. Min Kyung Park et al. [19] reported a remarkable treatment response in a patient with advanced HCC and portal vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT) following concurrent administration of TARE and atezolizumab/bevacizumab. Despite these promising results, large-scale clinical trials investigating the combination of TARE and immunotherapy for HCC are still ongoing. In this study, we developed a novel 32P-MS for HCC treatment. The in vitro properties of 32P-MS, including microsphere morphology, 32P loading efficiency, and release kinetics, were systematically evaluated. In a drug-induced in situ rat model of HCC, 32P-MS-TARE demonstrated effective embolization function and specific enrichment of 32P in the liver region. Importantly, 32P-MS exhibited no adverse effects on other organs, such as the heart, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, highlighting its excellent biosafety profile. Both rat and mouse tumor models consistently demonstrated significant anticancer efficacy with 32P-MS. Our findings suggest that 32P-MS represents a cost-effective alternative therapy for HCC patients, complementing existing 90Y-based TARE strategies. This novel approach not only addresses the limitations of current therapies but also offers a promising avenue for enhancing treatment outcomes in HCC. The microspheres are formulated as blank carriers, designed to be loaded with radionuclides on-demand during clinical applications. This approach not only enhances operational flexibility but also significantly reduces healthcare costs while improving clinical convenience. By integrating the unique properties of 32P with advanced microsphere technology, this study provides a foundation for further exploration of combined therapeutic strategies in HCC management.

The biggest feature of this study lies not only in elucidating the anticancer mechanisms of 32P-MS but also in conducting an in-depth investigation into its specific effects on the tumor microenvironment (TME). Multiple immunosuppressive cell populations within the TME have been identified as key drivers of tumor growth and metastasis. Notably, regulatory T cell (Treg), MDSC, and tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) are strongly associated with poor prognosis in HCC [20]. In this study, we demonstrated that 32P-MS administration significantly increased the population of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC in HCC, as evidenced by RNA sequencing and mass cytometry analyses. Mechanistically, FABP1 promotes PD-L1 expression by regulating PPARG, thereby sustaining the immunosuppressive function of MDSC in HCC. MDSC plays a pivotal role in immune suppression and tumor immune evasion through both direct and indirect mechanisms. They not only directly inhibit the activation and function of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as IL-10, TGF-β, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) but also amplify immunosuppression by promoting Treg expansion [21]. Furthermore, MDSC enhances tumor immune evasion and invasiveness by upregulating PD-L1 expression and promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [22]. Our findings reveal that 32P-MS induces FABP1 upregulation in MDSC, which in turn modulates PPARG to drive PD-L1 expression. Interestingly, this is not the first study to report that radiation therapy induces MDSC expansion. Liangliang Wang et al. [23] demonstrated that ionizing radiation triggers MDSC proliferation and YTHDF2 expression in both murine models and humans. Pharmacological inhibition of YTHDF2 was shown to counteract MDSC-mediated immunosuppression and enhance the efficacy of combined radiation therapy and anti-PD-L1 treatment. Similarly, Yuzhu Hou et al. [24] reported that radiotherapy promotes metastasis by inducing immune suppression through PD-L1 upregulation and MDSC infiltration in distal sites. Blockade of the PD-L1/CXCL10 axis or MDSC infiltration during irradiation was found to improve abscopal tumor control and reduce metastasis. These studies collectively highlight that radiation therapy not only exerts direct anticancer effects but also reshapes the TME, particularly by promoting MDSC expansion. Our findings align with these observations, further underscoring the dual role of radiation therapy in both tumor suppression and TME modulation. By elucidating the mechanisms through which 32P-MS influences MDSC function and PD-L1 expression, this study provides critical insights into the complex interplay between radiation therapy and the immune landscape of HCC, paving the way for novel therapeutic strategies.

However, every coin has two sides. Although FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC induced by 32P-MS is immunosuppressive cells, it brings a new opportunity for anti-PD1 immunotherapy. In recent years, immunocombined targeted therapy has become the first-line systematic therapy for advanced HCC. Nevertheless, its objective response rate remains approximately 30 %, with primary immune resistance exceeding 60 % [25]. Identifying the responsive population to HCC immunotherapy and elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying immune resistance have become critical yet challenging areas of research.Previous studies have identified several resistance mechanisms associated with anti-PD1 immunotherapy, which can be categorized as follows: 1) Target-loss resistance: In this scenario, tumor cells fail to express PD-L1, or infiltrating lymphocytes lack PD1 expression, suggesting the involvement of alternative immune escape pathways; 2) Primary drug resistance: These patients exhibit PD-L1 expression and T cell infiltration within the TME but do not respond to PD1/PD-L1 antibody therapy, often due to the presence of an immunosuppressive TME. 3) Secondary drug resistance: Such patients initially demonstrate a positive response to PD1/PD-L1 antibody therapy but subsequently experience disease progression or relapse, driven by enhanced immune escape mechanisms or potential genetic mutations [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. In the present study, we demonstrated that 32P-MS significantly enhances the sensitivity of anti-PD1 therapy in the treatment of HCC. The combination of 32P-MS and anti-PD1 effectively inhibits HCC progression. Our 32P-MS strategy is primarily designed for HCC patients who exhibit intrinsic resistance to anti-PD1 therapy. Following 32P-MS administration, the TME undergoes significant remodeling, particularly through the expansion of FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC. This transformation enables patients who initially lacked PD1/PD-L1 targets to become eligible for immunotherapy, thereby overcoming primary resistance and expanding the therapeutic potential of anti-PD1-based treatments. These findings highlight the dual role of 32P-MS in modulating the TME and its capacity to convert non-responsive patients into candidates for effective immunotherapy. By addressing the limitations of current immunotherapies, this approach offers a promising strategy to improve clinical outcomes in advanced HCC.

While our research has delved deeply into the mechanisms of 32P-MS in HCC treatment and proposed it as a potential alternative to 90Y microspheres, the long-term survival outcomes for patients and the comparative efficacy of 32P-MS versus 90Y microspheres remain to be validated through large-scale, high-quality clinical trials. We hope that our findings will pave the way for future advancements, offering patients more effective and cost-efficient therapeutic options.

Conclusion

In summary, We have developed 32P-MS as an innovative TARE product for the treatment of HCC (Fig. 9). 32P-MS exhibited potent anti-tumor efficacy, including dose-dependent suppression of HCC proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, as well as significant tumor vascular embolization and growth inhibition in vivo across rodent models. Mechanistically, 32P-MS uniquely remodeled the immunosuppressive TME by upregulating FABP1+PD-L1+ MDSC through activation of the FABP1/PPARG/PD-L1 axis. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of this axis reversed PD-L1 expression and enhanced therapeutic outcomes, highlighting its pivotal role in immune evasion. Notably, the combination of 32P-MS with anti-PD1 therapy synergistically amplified anti-tumor responses by reducing MDSC infiltration, reinvigorating CD8+ T cell activity, and overcoming immunosuppressive resistance. These findings establish 32P-MS not only as a cost-effective and practical alternative to 90Y microspheres but also as a dual-functional agent capable of integrating radioembolization with immune checkpoint blockade. This combinatorial approach holds substantial translational promise to address the limitations of current HCC therapies and improve clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Preparation of 32P-MS

Polypropyleneimine hydrochloride was partially neutralized with sodium hydroxide and subsequently emulsified in liquid paraffin. The resulting emulsion was then crosslinked with epichlorohydrin to form crosslinked microspheres. The product was dried and sieved to obtain particles of the desired size for subsequent experiments. The microspheres were then introduced into a Na2H32PO4 solution (0.02 mCi/mg) and mixed on a vortex mixer for 1 h. After sedimentation, the supernatant was decanted, and the microspheres were resuspended in a mixture of physiological saline and iohexol (5.7:4.3, v:v).

Characterization and drug delivery capacity of MS

MS Imaging: (1) SEM Imaging: The samples were prepared by freeze-drying to preserve their structural integrity and subsequently characterized using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) to analyze their surface morphology and microstructure. (2) Optical Microscopy Imaging: Optical microscopy was utilized to examine the morphological characteristics of dried microspheres, swollen microspheres, and the phosphate ion loading system, providing insights into their structural changes and functional behavior.

Size Distribution: The size distribution of the microspheres was determined using an optical microscope equipped with an ocular micrometer scale. A minimum of 200 microspheres were individually measured, and their diameters were recorded based on the graduations of the scale. This method was applied to assess the size distribution of dry microspheres (DM) as well as their swollen state.

Mechanical Properties: Microspheres with a swollen particle size of 500 μm were subjected to compression tests, where they were compressed to 50 % and 80 % of their original size, respectively, and held for 10 s. The samples were then allowed to slowly recover to their initial state, demonstrating their mechanical resilience.

Loading MS with Phosphate Ion: To evaluate the phosphate ion loading capacity, 1.0 mg of microspheres was added to 2.5 mL of a 0.01 M phosphate solution. The loading efficiency was determined by measuring the phosphate concentration in the supernatant at various time intervals using a phosphate assay kit.

EDS Testing: A freeze-dried sample was evenly coated onto conductive adhesive and placed in the SEM sample chamber for energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis. Surface analysis was conducted to determine the distribution of elements within the sample area. The EDS testing was performed at an acceleration voltage of 3 kV with a resolution of 2 nm, ensuring precise elemental mapping and characterization.

Phosphate release testing: Microspheres loaded with phosphate ions were added to 50 mL of physiological saline and continuously stirred at 100 rpm in a water bath at 37 °C. Supernatant samples (1 mL) were collected at 0, 5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 480, 720, 1440, 2880, 4320, 5760, 7200, 8640, and 10080 min, respectively. The absorbance wavelength of the samples was measured using a phosphate assay kit (Beijing Bioss Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), with the volume of the solution being replenished to the original level after each sampling.

Cell cultures

Mouse HCC cells (Hepa1-6) and human HCC cells (Hep-3B and HCCLM3) were obtained from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection. These cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, USA) under standard conditions of 37 °C and 5 % CO2 in a humidified incubator. The culture medium was supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) to support cell growth and maintain sterility.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from healthy donor blood samples using Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation. The isolated PBMC were resuspended in MDSC induction medium, which consisted of RPMI 1640 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (Gibco, USA), 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA), 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (MedChemExpress, USA), and 20 ng/mL interleukin-6 (IL-6) (MedChemExpress, USA). The cell concentration was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL, and 1 mL of the cell suspension was seeded into each well of a 24-well plate. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator for 7 days, with the medium replenished every 2 days to ensure optimal growth conditions. After the induction period, the cells were harvested using enzyme-free dissociation buffer and centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of PBS for cell counting. MDSC was then isolated using a CD33+ magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) kit, following the manufacturer's protocol. The purity of the sorted MDSC was validated by flow cytometry. The isolated cells were either cryopreserved for future use or immediately utilized for downstream functional assays. This research protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, adhering strictly to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. Prior to their inclusion in the study, all participants provided written informed consent, demonstrating their voluntary agreement to participate in the research activities.

Cell proliferation assay

Clone Formation Assay: HCC cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well and cultured for 10 days to allow colony formation. After the incubation period, the cells were fixed with methanol and stained with Giemsa solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to visualize the colonies. The colonies were then imaged and quantified using standard methods to evaluate clonogenic potential.

EdU Proliferation Assay: Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell-Light 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) DNA Cell Proliferation Kit (RiboBio, China). Briefly, 2 × 104HCC cells were plated in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then incubated with a 50 mmol/L EdU solution for 2 h to allow the incorporation of EdU into proliferating cells. Following incubation, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. In accordance with the manufacturer's protocol, the cells were treated with Apollo Dye Solution to label EdU-positive cells and counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. The stained cells were imaged and quantified using an Olympus FSX100 microscope (Olympus, Japan). The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated to determine the proliferation rate of the HCC cell lines.

Cell apoptosis assay

HCC cells were analyzed for apoptosis using an Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, the cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI according to the manufacturer's instructions to distinguish between viable, early apoptotic, and late apoptotic or necrotic cells. The stained cells were then subjected to flow cytometry analysis using a FACScan instrument (BD Biosciences, USA). Data acquisition and analysis were performed using FlowJo V10 software (Tree Star, USA) to quantify the percentage of apoptotic cells across different cell lines. This assay provided a comprehensive evaluation of apoptosis induction in the HCC cell populations.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

To evaluate the migratory and invasive capabilities of HCC cells, Transwell migration and invasion assays were performed. For the migration assay, HCC cells were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts (Corning, USA) at a density of 5 × 10^4 cells in 200 μL of serum-free DMEM (Gibco, USA). The lower chambers were filled with 600 μL of DMEM supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator for 24 h, the cells that migrated through the membrane were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) and stained with 0.1 % crystal violet (Beyotime, China). For the invasion assay, the upper chambers were pre-coated with 50 μL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM and incubated at 37 °C for 4–6 h to form a gel. HCC cells were then seeded into the coated upper chambers, and the lower chambers were filled with 10 % FBS-supplemented DMEM. After incubation for 24 h, the invaded cells were fixed, stained, and quantified. In both assays, the stained cells were imaged under a light microscope, and cell counts were performed in three randomly selected fields per chamber to ensure statistical reliability.

Experimental model of diethylnitrosamine-induced HCC in rats and TARE surgery

The animal experiments were conducted under the approval of the Animal Management Committee of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2405041), ensuring full compliance with institutional ethical standards for animal care and research. To establish an orthotopic liver cancer model, two-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were administered a single intraperitoneal injection of diethylnitrosamine (DEN; 25 mg/kg) to initiate tumorigenesis. This was followed by twice-weekly intraperitoneal injections of carbon tetrachloride (CCL4; 5 mg/kg) over a 24-week period to promote carcinogenic progression. Liver lesions were dynamically monitored using color ultrasound, with tumor size, edge morphology, and signal characteristics assessed throughout the study.

Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and positioned supine on the operating table. The tail was disinfected, and a site with prominent caudal artery pulsation (typically 1–2 cm from the caudal root) was selected for local infiltration anesthesia using lidocaine. A puncture needle was inserted into the artery at a 30°angle, and a guide wire was advanced following blood return. A catheter sheath was then inserted along the guide wire. Contrast agent was injected through the catheter, and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) imaging confirmed the catheter tip's position in the target blood vessel. After catheter withdrawal, the puncture site was compressed for 5–10 min to ensure hemostasis. Post-procedural monitoring included observations of rat activity, feeding behavior, and tail blood circulation.

Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) was utilized to evaluate tumor status before and after TARE therapy. Additionally, emission computed tomography (ECT) was employed to assess the hepatic enrichment of 32P-MS post-surgery. These imaging modalities provided comprehensive insights into tumor progression and therapeutic efficacy.

In vivo tumor mice model and treatment

The Animal Management Committee of Nanjing Medical University approved the animal experiments (IACUC-2405041), and all experimental techniques and animal care comply with the institutional ethical standards for animal-related research. C57BL/6 wild-type mice were bought from the Nanjing Medical University Model Animal Research Centre. Before tumor implantation, all animals were examined to make sure they were healthy and free of illness.

For the first mice model part,the hepa1-6 cells were injected into mice (n = 5 in each group). We divided tumor model mice transplanted with carcinoma into 2 groups, each containing 5 mice: the NC group and 32P-MS group. When the tumors grew into 80–150 mm3 in volume, 32P-MS (0.4 mCi, 14.8 MBq, once) was intratumoral injected into the mice in different groups.

For the second mice model part,we divided tumor model mice transplanted with carcinoma into 3 groups, each containing 5 mice: the NC group, the FABP1−/− group, and the FABP1−/−+32P-MS group. These groups received treatments that corresponded to their respective groups.

For the third mice model part, when the tumors grew into 80–150 mm3 in volume, 32P-MS (0.4 mCi, 14.8 MBq, once) was intratumoral injected into the mice in different groups. On the eighth day and then twice a week after that, mice were intraperitoneally I.P. injected with 6.6 mg/kg of anti-PD1 (Bioxcell, USA). The mice were euthanized on the 28th day after surgery and the liver tumor tissues were removed. Tumor dimensions were assessed at 7-day intervals. The calculation of tumor volume employed the formula: volume (mm3) = width2 × length/2.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to a series of preparatory steps for immunohistochemical staining. Initially, the sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated to remove wax. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the sections in a 3 % hydrogen peroxide solution (Beyotime, China) for 10 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to the target antigens. After thorough washing, the sections were treated with a biotinylated secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the sections were exposed to a streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) complex (Beyotime, China) to facilitate signal amplification. The stained sections were visualized under a light microscope, and the results were analyzed to evaluate protein expression levels.

For fluorescent immunohistochemistry, tissue samples were fixed at room temperature using a 4 % paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. To enhance antibody penetration, the samples were permeabilized with a 0.05 % Triton X-100 solution for 5 min. Non-specific binding sites were blocked by incubating the samples overnight in a 2 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) PBS solution. The samples were then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following primary antibody incubation, the samples were treated with either Alexa Fluor-conjugated or HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h. To visualize nuclei, the samples were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, China). After mounting, fluorescent images were captured using a Zeiss laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) to analyze the spatial distribution and intensity of target proteins.

RNA sequencing and pathway analysis

Total RNA was isolated from 5 × 106 HCC cells treated with control and 32P-MS. Library construction and sequencing were performed by OE Biotech(Shanghai, China). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation and enrichment were conducted using the DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). We considered pathways with Q value ≤ 0.05 as significantly enriched.

Mass cytometry

We obtained tissue samples from the control and 32P-MS tumor-bearing groups. We then processed mouse tumor tissue using the Miltenyi Mouse Tumor Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), in which Percoll removes debris and divides red blood cells. More experimental details refer to our previous research work [29].

Single-cell RNA data analysis

We sourced our data from the GEO database GSE149614 under the isolation of 10 patients with HCC tumors and normal tissues. We selected single cells for further study based on the following criteria: UMI counts between 3000 and 40,000; and mitochondrial percentages less than 10 % of the overall UMI count. It was transformed from the standardised gene expression scale (in UMI) to LOG2 (UMI+1).

Western blotting and co-immunoprecipitation

Protein extraction was performed using RIPA lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The extracted proteins were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were probed with primary antibodies specific to the following targets: FABP1 (1:1000, Abcam, USA), PD-L1 (1:1000, Proteintech, China), PPARG (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), and GAPDH (1:2000, Proteintech, China) as a loading control. After incubation with the primary antibodies, the membranes were treated with a peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, China). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) assay, and images were captured for quantitative analysis.

For the Co-IP assay, cells were lysed using pre-chilled lysis buffer (Absin, China) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (Absin, China) to inhibit protease activity. The cell lysate was incubated on ice for 5 min and then centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. Monoclonal antibodies against the target protein (5 μg) were added to 500 μL of the cell lysate and incubated overnight at 4 °C to allow antigen-antibody complex formation. Protein A and Protein G (5 μL each, Absin, China) were then added to the mixture and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with gentle mixing. The resulting immunocomplexes were pelleted by centrifugation and washed three times with wash buffer (Absin, China). The pellet was resuspended in 40 μL of SDS sample buffer (Absin, China), vortexed briefly, and centrifuged to remove debris. The supernatant was analyzed by western blotting to confirm the presence of the target protein and its interacting partners.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using the GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad software Inc., USA), and two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. A t-test or One-Way ANOVA was used to analyze and compare the data. The following notation was used to represent different significance levels: ns for p ≥ 0.05, ∗ for p < 0.05, ∗∗ for p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ for p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ for p < 0.0001.
