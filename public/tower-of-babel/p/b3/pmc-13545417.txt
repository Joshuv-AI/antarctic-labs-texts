Neuromodulation in epilepsy: From cellular mechanisms to network-guided therapy
Authors: Catherine J. Chu, Xue Han, R. Mark Richardson
Published: 2026

Electrical stimulation has been used to treat seizures in patients with epilepsy for decades, yet the clinical ability to deliver stimulation still exceeds our understanding of what stimulation does to neurons, circuits, and disease trajectories. Over 100,000patients with drug-refractory epilepsy have been treated with vagal nerve stimulation [1] and many thousands with intracranial neuromodulation [2], including children. Despite the adoption of these tools by patients and medical professionals, many uncertainties remain, including why a particular stimulation protocol helps one network but not another, which signal should trigger treatment, how stimulation effects evolve from milliseconds to years, and how therapeutic benefit should be balanced against the long-term consequences of an implanted or repeatedly applied intervention.

The field of neuromodulation in epilepsy now includes vagus nerve stimulation, transcranial magnetic stimulation, focused ultrasound, and open-loop and responsive intracranial stimulation. Together, these approaches frame epilepsy as a disorder of distributed, state-dependent networks that may be measured and modified. This special issue of Neurotherapeutics follows that translational continuum from cellular mechanisms, to enabling technology, to advanced clinical application. Across the articles, a common message emerges: the future of neuromodulation will depend less on identifying a universally effective tool, target or stimulation approach than on learning how to match an intervention to the biology, dynamic brain state and developmental stage, and goals of an individual patient.

This special issue begins at the cellular scale. Ravasio and colleagues [3] review cellular responses to electrical stimulation provided by optical voltage and calcium imaging studies that overcome stimulation artifacts that obscure the neural response in electrophysiological studies. Optical approaches reveal that stimulation does not produce a uniform shift toward excitation or inhibition. Responses vary with pulse structure, frequency, cell class, anatomical layer, distance, and disease state, and may differ during stimulation and in the seconds or minutes that follow. These methods offer an important bridge between applied waveforms and cellular mechanisms, while the authors appropriately emphasize the limitations of preclinical preparations and the need to establish which findings generalize to the human epileptic brain.

Cowan and colleagues [4] extend this mechanistic perspective to human single-neuron recordings. Clinical stimulation mapping and device programming rely largely on field potentials, which reflect aggregate synaptic and population activity but do not necessarily reveal the firing output of local neurons. Available microelectrode studies show heterogeneous excitation and suppression after stimulation, influenced by cell type, distance, and the pathological status of the sampled tissue. This variability may help distinguish local recruitment from inhibition and seizure-generating tissue from propagation pathways. However, neuronal sampling is sparse, extracellular cell classification remains probabilistic, and additional microelectrodes are usually implanted for research rather than direct clinical benefit. Single-neuron responses may, therefore, complement rather than replace mesoscopic measurements, and their clinical value will require standardized paradigms, reproducible analyses, and careful ethical oversight.

Fisher and colleagues [5] review describes pulsed focused ultrasound as a second route to cellular and network modulation. Candidate mechanisms span mechanosensitive ion channels, preferential recruitment of inhibitory interneurons, astrocytic signaling, restoration of excitation-inhibition balance, disruption of pathological coupling and hypersynchrony, and longer-term plastic or neuroprotective effects. Preclinical findings and early human studies justify continued clinical development, while also underscoring the need for standardized exposure reporting, rigorous sham conditions that address auditory and hemodynamic confounds, direct confirmation of target engagement, and long-term imaging and neuropsychological follow-up.

Mechanistic insight can be translated only through technology capable of measuring and controlling the relevant biology. Zhu and colleagues [6] provide a device-level comparison of contemporary implantable closed-loop and sensing-enabled neurostimulation systems. Their most important conclusion is that clinically meaningful differences arise less from the nominal range of stimulation parameters than from the architecture of sensing: which contacts can record, whether recording can continue during stimulation, which frequencies are accessible, how stimulation artifact is managed, whether raw waveforms or only derived features are retained, and how much autonomous control the implanted system can exercise. The responsive neurostimulation system remains the most mature epilepsy-specific example, while several adaptive deep brain stimulation platforms bring capabilities developed largely in movement disorders. The next generation of devices for use in epilepsy will need to balance power, storage, bandwidth, artifact suppression, battery longevity, and clinical usability while undergoing epilepsy-specific validation.

Lundstrom and Rotenberg [7] consider the future of transcranial magnetic stimulation and other noninvasive approaches. They describe potential roles for TMS-EMG and TMS-EEG measures of cortical excitability, stimulation of accessible nodes connected to deeper or multifocal networks, EEG-gated repetitive TMS, and pharmacological enhancement of stimulation-induced plasticity. These possibilities are attractive because TMS can be repeated and modified with relatively low procedural risk. Nonetheless, TMS responses vary with anatomy, vigilance, medication, development, and technical artifact; reliability across centers remains uncertain; and superficial stimulation may not adequately control distributed or deep networks. Prospective studies must determine whether TMS-derived measures are sufficiently stable to guide therapy and whether personalized, state-dependent protocols can produce durable seizure reduction.

Bubrick and colleagues [8] provide a complementary clinical overview of focused ultrasound in epilepsy. They distinguish high-intensity ultrasound for thermal ablation from low-intensity transcranial ultrasound for reversible modulation and consider additional applications, including blood-brain barrier opening, targeted drug delivery, and ultrasound-mediated uncaging. Focused ultrasound may, therefore, become not one therapy but a platform for noninvasive access to deep targets and spatially restricted delivery of treatment. Early human studies demonstrate feasibility and physiological effects, but outcomes remain preliminary and variable. Accurate in situ dosimetry, skull-dependent beam distortion, verification of the intracranial focus, limited access to superficial neocortical targets, and treatment logistics remain substantial barriers.

This special issue next turns to the mechanistic uncertainties and technological constraints that must be addressed in the clinical application of neuromodulation for epilepsy. Mithani and colleagues [9] examine deep brain stimulation in pediatric drug-resistant epilepsy through the developing connectome. Children are not simply smaller adults: structural connectivity, inhibitory signaling, cognition, behavior, and psychosocial function continue to mature as seizures and stimulation interact with development. Heightened plasticity may permit beneficial remodeling of pathological networks, but chronic modulation of highly connected hubs may also perturb normal trajectories. Given limited and heterogeneous pediatric evidence, the authors call for syndrome- and network-informed targeting, pediatric-specific biomarkers, multicenter studies, and long-term outcomes that include cognition, quality of life, school participation, behavior, and family burden. They also emphasize assent, hardware revisions, access to specialized follow-up, and transition to adult care.

McCarthy and Burdette [10] focus on responsive neurostimulation programming, a process that is sometimes treated as secondary to implantation but in practice constitutes the therapy. They describe an iterative progression from detection of the seizure body to earlier ictal, pre-ictal, and potentially pro-ictal biomarkers, with stimulation intended to provide both acute seizure disruption and slower network remodeling. Their concept of a patient-specific therapeutic peak is particularly important: stimulation must be sufficient to influence the network, but excessive charge density or poorly matched frequency may reduce efficacy, provoke symptoms, or worsen seizures. Biomarker selection and parameter optimization remain largely empirical, and practices vary across centers. Responsive neurostimulation should, therefore, be understood as a longitudinal experiment in which detections, stimulation settings, and outcomes are repeatedly reassessed rather than fixed at implantation.

Merricks and colleagues [11] provide the circuit framework for thalamic neuromodulation. Their review cautions against treating the thalamus as a homogeneous target. The anterior, centromedian, mediodorsal, and pulvinar nuclei participate in different corticothalamic and limbic networks, and their recruitment varies with seizure type, cortical onset, and propagation pattern. Timing may be as important as location: early thalamic recruitment can support rapid spread, whereas increasing thalamocortical synchronization near seizure termination raises the possibility of engaging endogenous termination mechanisms. A thalamic field-potential change may also represent synaptic input without proving that local neurons generate the pathological output. Stereo-EEG, tractography, and evoked-potential mapping may improve target selection, but candidate targets must ultimately be tested against patient-specific physiology and clinical response.

Tanoue and colleague [12]s place safety and long-term management at the center of treatment selection [12]. They organize adverse events associated with vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation, and responsive neurostimulation into procedure-related, stimulation-related, device-related, and long-term management domains. This framework exposes burdens obscured by comparisons based only on median seizure reduction. The modalities differ in intracranial risk, stimulation-related symptoms, neuropsychiatric and cognitive effects, hardware failure, imaging restrictions, battery replacement, programming demands, and the infrastructure required for follow-up. Adverse events are not separate from efficacy: they influence candidacy, counseling, adherence, reversibility, access, and whether benefit can be sustained over decades.

Piper and Tisdall [13] close the translational arc by reviewing the trials that established surgical neuromodulation and the studies needed next. Landmark trials of vagus nerve stimulation, thalamic deep brain stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, and epicranial stimulation have expanded options for focal and generalized epilepsy. Their history also cautions against simple cross-modality rankings because studies differ in populations, targets, blinded periods, follow-up, seizure ascertainment, and programming. Seizure diaries and responder rates remain useful but incompletely capture unrecognized seizures, severity, cognition, quality of life, caregiver burden, and developmental change. Future trials must address personalized targeting, adaptive stimulation, pediatric populations, comparative effectiveness, economics, and long-term outcomes; adaptive or biomarker-enriched designs may be particularly valuable in a heterogeneous disorder.

Taken together, these articles define neuromodulation as a multiscale interaction among waveform, cell type, anatomy, network state, development, and time. The next advances will depend on linking these levels. Optical imaging can identify candidate cellular mechanisms; human unit recordings can test whether field responses reflect local recruitment; chronic sensing can reveal state-dependent biomarkers; structural and functional mapping can guide network targets; and better trials can determine whether these measurements improve outcomes that matter to patients. This reciprocal path from mechanism to clinic and back will require standardized reporting of stimulation dose and target engagement, transparent publication of negative results, interpretable biomarkers, equitable long-term care, and outcomes that extend beyond seizure counts. This is an especially exciting moment for the field, as rapid advances in noninvasive and less invasive neurosurgical technologies are creating new opportunities to precisely modulate pathological brain networks, with direct relevance and growing therapeutic promise for patients with epilepsy.
