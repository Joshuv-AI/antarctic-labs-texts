Burden of Illness Associated With Respiratory Syncytial Virus, Influenza, and Coronavirus Disease 2019 in Infants and Young Children in Ontario, Canada, 2018–2023: A Population-Based Canadian Immunization Research Network Study
Authors: Sarah C J Jorgensen, Alejandro Hernandez, Sarah A Buchan, Tiffany Fitzpatrick, Astrid Guttmann, Shaun K Morris, Jeffrey C Kwong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) transitions to endemicity and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza reestablish their seasonal circulation patterns, understanding their comparative burden on infants and children can guide health system responses and funding priorities.MethodsThis was a population-based cohort study of children aged <5 years in Ontario, Canada, from September 2018 to August 2023 using linked health administrative databases.ResultsSeasonal cohorts comprised 731 838 to 763 660 children. RSV- and influenza-related hospital admission rates immediately decreased in March 2020. In 2021–2022, RSV-related admissions rebounded but remained 8%–11% lower than prepandemic seasons, whereas 2022–2023 RSV-related admissions increased 105%–113% versus prepandemic seasons and peak admissions occurred in November versus December. Influenza did not rebound until 2022–2023, when admissions were 28%–37% higher than prepandemic seasons. Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)–related admissions remained low until 2021–2022 and were lower than RSV-related admissions across all age groups during prepandemic and pandemic seasons. By contrast, 2021–2022 COVID-19-related admissions exceeded prepandemic influenza-related admissions by 30%–40% but decreased by 45% in 2022–2023 and were lower than prepandemic influenza-related admissions, except among infants aged <12 months who remained at highest risk. There was no distinct seasonal pattern for COVID-19–related admissions during the study.ConclusionsRSV remains a major cause of childhood hospitalization. Although COVID-19 appears to be receding, its disease burden as it transitions to endemicity remains to be established. The unprecedented peaks in RSV-related hospital admissions during 2022–2023, together with the return of influenza and ongoing SARS-CoV-2 transmission, underscore the need to strengthen systems for real-time surveillance and to proactively prepare for critical healthcare scenarios.Keywords: children, COVID-19, infants, influenza, respiratory syncytial virus

Respiratory viruses are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in infants and young children worldwide [1, 2]. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the most common cause of acute lower respiratory tract infection and hospitalization in this age group, leading to an estimated 30 million cases and 3.6 million hospital admissions in children aged <5 years globally in 2019 [1]. Infants entering their first RSV season are among those at highest risk for severe illness [1]. Preterm birth, chronic lung disease, and congenital heart disease further increase the risk of severe illness, yet RSV is also a leading cause of hospital admission among healthy term-born infants, with approximately 80% of infants hospitalized with RSV having no preexisting medical conditions [3]. Likewise, infants and young children bear a disproportionate burden of influenza-associated morbidity, with an estimated 10 million cases and 870 000 hospital admissions in children aged <5 years worldwide in 2018 [2].

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic response disrupted the typical seasonal circulation of endemic respiratory viruses in Canada [4–10] and many regions worldwide [10–22]. Public health interventions put in place to limit the spread of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) were associated with sharp declines in non–SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses, whereas a resurgence of infections, characterized by interregional variability in seasonality and intensity, has been reported after transmission mitigation strategies were relaxed [4–22]. The epidemiology of COVID-19 in infants and young children has also evolved alongside increasing levels of population immunity, the development and deployment of vaccines and therapeutics, and the emergence of new variants [23–26].

As SARS-CoV-2 transitions to an endemic virus and RSV and influenza reestablish their seasonal circulation patterns, understanding their comparative burden on infants and young children can guide health system responses and funding priorities. However, to date, there have been limited comparative population-level data, particularly when all 3 viruses were co-circulating [8, 11]. In this study, we present annual RSV, influenza, and COVID-19–related hospital and intensive care unit (ICU) admission rates among children aged <5 years in Ontario, Canada, over 5 respiratory seasons from 2018–2019 to 2022–2023. We also report characteristics of hospitalized infants and young children and evaluate in-hospital and postdischarge outcomes. Although our primary interest was the comparative burden of illness when all 3 viruses were co-circulating (ie, 2021–2022 and 2022–2023), we included prepandemic seasons to illustrate temporal changes in burden of illness.

METHODS

Study Population and Setting

Ontario is Canada's most populous province with approximately 15.6 million residents (2023), including more than 700 000 children aged <5 years, and a single-payer, publicly funded healthcare system [27]. Approximate start dates of COVID-19 pandemic waves in Ontario and a timeline of associated public health restrictions are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

We created 5 cohorts corresponding to 5 respiratory seasons, defined as 1 September to 31 August of the following year, from 2018–2019 to 2022–2023. We included all infants and children aged <5 years (hereafter referred to as children for brevity) who were born in Ontario and were eligible for the Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) during a given respiratory season. We excluded those whose birth record could not be linked to a maternal delivery record and those with data quality problems (eg, invalid birth or death date). Children entered the cohort on 1 September of the respiratory season or, for newborns, on the date of discharge from their birth admission. Children were followed until the end of the respiratory season (ie, 31 August of the following year); their fifth birthday; loss of OHIP eligibility (eg, due to emigration from Ontario); hospital admission due to RSV, influenza, or COVID-19; or death, whichever occurred first. Children who were censored due to RSV, influenza, or COVID-19–related hospitalization could reenter the cohort for that specific infection 30 days after hospital discharge, and children remained at risk for the other respiratory viruses during the 30-day postdischarge period. Children could be included in multiple respiratory season cohorts if they met all other inclusion criteria.

Data Sources

We used multiple health administrative databases, which were linked with unique coded identifiers and analyzed at ICES, an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze healthcare and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement. We report non-zero values with fewer than 6 observations as <6 in accordance with Ontario's privacy laws to prevent reidentification. Details on databases are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Outcomes

We identified RSV, influenza, and COVID-19–related hospital admissions from the Canadian Institute for Health Information's Discharge Abstract Database (CIHI-DAD) using validated algorithms based on virus-specific International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision, Canada (ICD-10-CA) codes (Supplementary Table 3) [28, 29]. Algorithms have previously demonstrated moderate sensitivity and high specificity for virologically confirmed RSV-related (76% and 95%, respectively) and influenza-related (75% and 99%, respectively) hospital admissions among children aged <5 years in Ontario [28]. The sensitivity and specificity of ICD-10-CA code U07.1 (virologically confirmed COVID-19) for inpatients with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test in Ontario during 2020 were 98% and 100%, respectively [29]. We restricted COVID-19–related outcomes to those with the ICD-10-CA code in the most responsible diagnosis field to minimize inclusion of children whose hospitalization was not primarily due to COVID-19. We also included ICD-10-CA codes U07.2 (virus not identified, 0.8% of COVID-19–related admissions) to capture children whose COVID-19 diagnosis was supported by a home rapid antigen test, which were increasingly available in Ontario after March 2021, and U07.3 (multisystem inflammatory syndrome associated with COVID-19, 2.5% of COVID-19–related admissions), which was implemented in Canada in January 2021 [30]. We identified neonatal and pediatric ICU admissions and ascertained the use of respiratory support using special care unit variables and Canadian Classification of Interventions (CCI) codes in CIHI-DAD and the OHIP database. We identified hospital readmissions and emergency department visits within 30 days of discharge from CIHI-DAD and the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System, respectively. We obtained data on death within 30 days of hospital admission from CIHI-DAD and the Registered Persons Database.

Covariates

We extracted sociodemographic and clinical characteristics from multiple linked databases. We used the Registered Persons Database to ascertain age, sex, and postal code. We linked each child's postal code to the 2021 Canadian Census to determine rurality and area-level marginalization across 4 dimensions: (1) material resources; (2) households and dwellings; (3) age and labor force; and (4) racialized and newcomer populations [31]. We identified the presence of comorbidities using ICD-10-CA and CCI codes in CIHI-DAD and the Same Day Surgery Database. We determined gestational age at birth, birthweight, delivery method, multiple births, and number of siblings at birth (derived from maternal parity) using the MOMBABY database, which contains deterministically linked maternal and newborn hospital delivery records from CIHI-DAD. For children with >2 primary care visits during the study, we calculated their continuity of care index, which reflects the distribution of primary care visits between different physicians, with values ranging from 0 (minimal continuity) to 1 (maximal continuity) [32]. We determined maternal immigration status using the Ontario portion of the Immigration, Refugee, and Citizenship Canada Permanent Resident Database. We ascertained maternal influenza vaccination status during the 2 previous respiratory seasons as a proxy for maternal health behavior, using the OHIP and Ontario Drug Benefit Claims databases. We derived information on maternal COVID-19 vaccination from COVaxON, a centralized vaccine registry that contains complete information on all COVID-19 vaccinations in Ontario. Health Canada approved COVID-19 vaccines for children aged 6 months to 5 years on 14 July 2022 [33]. However, uptake has been low in Canada and remains <10% in Ontario [34]. Vaccination in this age group is currently recommended for those with underlying medical conditions that place them at higher risk for severe COVID-19 [35].

Statistical Analysis

We summarized cohort characteristics using counts (percentages), means (± standard deviation [SD]), or medians (interquartile range [IQR]). We calculated hospital and ICU admission rates for each respiratory season and virus by dividing the total number of admissions by person-time of follow-up and we stratified by age group (0–2, 3–5, 6–11, 12–23, and 24–59 months). We calculated the percentage of hospitalized children who experienced in-hospital or postdischarge outcomes by virus and respiratory season. We conducted all analyses using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

RESULTS

Description of Cohorts

Seasonal cohorts comprised 731 838 to 763 660 children (Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Table 4). Baseline characteristics were generally stable over the 5 respiratory seasons, except for maternal influenza vaccination, which varied from 16% (2018–2019) to 31% (2021–2022), fewer live births in 2022–2023 (92 728) compared with previous seasons (114 754–116 090), and fewer children born to mothers who recently (≤2 years) immigrated to Canada in 2021–2022 (7154) and 2022–2023 (415) compared with previous seasons (14 805–18 387).

RSV-Related Outcomes

During the prepandemic period (ie, 2018–2019 and 2019–2020), there were 2125 (3.34 per 1000 person-years [PY]) and 2196 (3.47 per 1000 PY) RSV-related hospital admissions, respectively (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). There were distinct seasonal patterns, with peak admissions in December, and >97% of admissions occurred between October and April (Figure 1). Infants aged 0–2 months comprised 31% of RSV-related admissions during the 2 seasons and had admission rates 23–30 times higher than children aged 24–59 months (Supplementary Table 6). Additionally, those born prematurely and those with older siblings, pediatric complex chronic conditions, major congenital anomalies, and/or other comorbidities were overrepresented among children with RSV-related admissions compared with the overall cohort, although 75%–77% of admissions occurred among term-born children with no preexisting medical conditions (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Monthly counts of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), influenza, and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)–related hospital admission in children aged <5 years, Ontario, Canada, 2018–2019 to 2022–2023.

There was an immediate reduction in RSV-related hospital admissions following the declaration of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020, with <6 admissions in April 2020, compared with 58 in April 2019. In total, only 17 RSV-related admissions occurred during the 2020–2021 respiratory season. The rate of RSV-related admissions rebounded in 2021–2022 but remained 8%–11% lower than prepandemic seasons (1944 admissions, 3.09 per 1000 PY). The age distribution and temporal pattern of admissions were broadly comparable to prepandemic seasons (Figures 1 and 2). By contrast, in 2022–2023, the admission rate increased 105%–113% (4438 admissions, 7.12 per 1000 PY) compared with prepandemic seasons, and peak admissions occurred in November rather than December. Although infants aged 0–2 months continued to have the highest hospital admission rate in 2022–2023 (42.11 vs 3.19 per 1000 PY for children aged 24–59 months), the median age of hospitalized children increased from 6 (IQR, 2–22) months in 2021–2022 to 11 (IQR, 3–25) months in 2022–2023 (Table 1 and Figure 2). Other sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of children hospitalized due to RSV in 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 were comparable to those of children hospitalized during the prepandemic period (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 5). In-hospital and postdischarge outcomes in 2021–2022 and 2022–2023 were also comparable to prepandemic seasons: The mean ± SD length of admission was 4 ± 6 days, 12%–13% of hospitalizations resulted in ICU admission, and 1%–2% of hospital admissions required invasive mechanical ventilation (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 6). Children admitted to the ICU were younger; were more likely to have been born preterm, with low birthweight; and had a higher burden of pediatric complex chronic conditions, major congenital anomalies, and other comorbidities compared with hospitalized children not admitted to the ICU during their stay (Table 3). Death within 30 days of admission was rare (0 or <6 deaths each season).

Influenza-Related Outcomes

There were 941 (1.48 per 1000 PY) and 1008 (1.59 per 1000 PY) influenza-related hospital admissions in the 2 prepandemic seasons, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). Admissions peaked in January of prepandemic seasons, and >98% of admissions occurred between November and May during 2018–2019 (Figure 1). Like RSV, admissions due to influenza quickly dropped after March 2020 but, unlike RSV, they did not rebound until 2022–2023; there were <6 and 92 influenza-related admissions in 2020–2021 and 2021–2022, respectively, compared with 1266 admissions in 2022–2023 (2.03 per 1000 PY, 28%–37% higher than prepandemic seasons) (Figure 1 and Table 2). The age distribution and clinical characteristics of children hospitalized due to influenza were similar across all seasons when influenza was circulating (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 5). Although children aged 0–2 months had the highest rates of hospital admission, influenza-related admissions were more evenly distributed across age groups compared with RSV and COVID-19 (Figure 2). In-hospital and postdischarge outcomes were comparable across seasons, although there were changes by age group; for example, ICU admission rates were 188%–227% higher among infants aged 0–2 months in 2022–2023 than prepandemic seasons (Table 2 and Supplementary Table 6). Children admitted to the ICU were younger than hospitalized children who were not admitted to ICU, although they were older (median, 15 [IQR, 3–31] months) than children admitted to the ICU with RSV (median, 3 [IQR, 1–13] months) or COVID-19 (median, 7 [IQR, 2–20] months) (Table 3). Preterm birth, low birthweight, cesarean delivery, multiple birth, and comorbidities were higher among children with influenza admitted to the ICU compared with children hospitalized with influenza who were not admitted to the ICU. Death within 30 days of an influenza-related hospitalization was uncommon (0 or <6 deaths each season).

COVID-19–Related Outcomes

There were 16 COVID-19–related hospital admissions in children during 2019–2020, with the first admission reported in February 2020. Admissions remained low during 2020–2021 (190 admissions; 0.30 per 1000 PY) but increased following the emergence and spread of the Omicron variant in Ontario in December 2021 (1304 admissions during 2021–2022; 2.07 per 1000 PY) (Figure 1 and Table 2). Admission rates decreased 45% in 2022–2023 (704 admissions; 1.13 per 1000 PY) compared with 2021–2022 (Table 2). Admission rates were highest among infants aged 0–2 months and decreased with increasing age (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 6).

COVID-19–related admission rates were lower than RSV-related admission rates across all age groups during both prepandemic and pandemic seasons (excluding 2020–2021). By contrast, COVID-19–related admissions in 2021–2022 exceeded prepandemic influenza-related admission rates (30%–40% higher), but in 2022–2023 COVID-19–related admissions were 24%–29% lower than prepandemic influenza-related admission rates, except among younger age groups, who remained at highest risk (Figure 2 and Table 2). Repeat COVID-19–related admissions during the same season occurred in 15 and 18 children in 2021–2022 and 2022–2023, respectively. Unlike RSV and influenza, there was no distinct seasonal pattern for COVID-19–related admissions (Figure 1).

Children born prematurely and those with pediatric complex chronic conditions, major congenital anomalies, and/or other comorbidities were overrepresented among children with COVID-19–related admissions, although 75%–76% of admissions were for term-born children with no preexisting medical conditions (Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). A larger proportion of children hospitalized due to COVID-19 had no older siblings (42%–45%, similar to the overall cohort) compared with children hospitalized due to RSV (24%–28%) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 5). In-hospital and postdischarge outcomes are shown in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 6; the mean length of admission was 3 ± 4 days, 4%–7% of hospitalizations resulted in ICU admission, and 1%–3% required invasive mechanical ventilation. ICU admission rates were 9 (2021–2022) and 36 (2022–2023) times higher among infants aged 0–2 months compared with children aged 24–59 months. Fifty-four percent of children admitted to the ICU with COVID-19 were born at term and had no medical comorbidities, which was the lowest proportion among the 3 groups (RSV-related ICU admissions, 68%; influenza-related ICU admissions, 63%). Death was uncommon (0 or <6 deaths each season).

DISCUSSION

We leveraged a large population-based repository of linked clinical and sociodemographic administrative data to quantify the comparative burden of illness due to RSV, influenza, and COVID-19 among children aged <5 years during 5 respiratory seasons from 2018–2019 to 2022–2023 in Ontario, Canada. We found that public health measures used to stem the transmission of SARS-CoV-2 affected the transmission of RSV, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 differently and we detected ongoing changes in disease burden by pandemic phase. RSV was responsible for the highest illness burden across all seasons, except 2020–2021 when there was a near-complete absence of RSV (and influenza)–related hospital admissions. We observed a resurgence of RSV-related admissions in 2021–2022 and a more than doubling in admission rates in 2022–2023 compared with prepandemic seasons, with a shift to older age groups, although young infants continued to be disproportionately impacted by RSV. By contrast, influenza-related admissions did not rebound until 2022–2023. Admission rates due to COVID-19 among children remained low until the emergence and spread of Omicron in Ontario in late 2021. During the 2021–2022 respiratory season, COVID-19–related admission rates were higher than those for influenza in prepandemic seasons, especially among infants aged 0–2 months. By contrast, in 2022–2023, COVID-19 admission rates decreased and were lower than those for influenza in prepandemic seasons, except among infants aged 0–2 months who continued to have hospital admission rates that were 2–3 times higher than prepandemic influenza admission rates.

Although multiple studies have assessed changes in seasonality, incidence, and morbidity associated with RSV [4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 12–20, 22] and/or influenza [5–7, 10, 12, 20, 21] during the pandemic, few have estimated the population burden of illness due to SARS-CoV-2 relative to RSV and influenza among children during prepandemic and pandemic seasons [8, 11]. An active hospital-based surveillance network of 4 regional hospitals in Quebec, Canada, evaluated the viral etiology of acute respiratory infection hospital admissions during prepandemic (2012–2013 to 2019–2020) and pandemic (2020–2021 to 2022–2023) seasons [8]. Consistent with our findings, RSV was the predominant viral etiology among children (aged <17 years) in prepandemic seasons (48% of laboratory-confirmed respiratory infection admissions) as well as during 2021–2022 (34%) and 2022–2023 (39%) [8]. Unlike our study, detection of influenza remained below prepandemic seasons in 2022–2023 (24% vs 7%) [8]. SARS-CoV-2 was detected in 9% and 5% of pediatric laboratory-confirmed respiratory infection admissions in 2021–2022 and 2022–2023, respectively, in the Quebec study [8]. A study using data from 2 US hospital-based surveillance networks found that, compared with influenza-associated hospital admission rates during 3 prepandemic seasons (2017–2018 to 2019–2020), the COVID-19 admission rate during 2020–2021 was lower among children aged <5 years (66.8 for COVID-19 vs 70.9–91.5 for influenza per 100 000 children) [21]. By contrast, a population-based study from British Columbia, Canada, found that COVID-19 hospitalization rates during the initial Omicron wave (ie, the first 10 weeks of 2022) in children aged <5 years were higher than historical cohorts of patients with influenza, except for the 2009–2010 pandemic H1N1 influenza season [36]. Analysis of surveillance data from 2 public hospitals in Soweto, South Africa, from January 2015 to December 2022 found that the incidence of COVID-19 across all pandemic seasons was lower than the prepandemic incidence of RSV among children aged <24 months but was similar among children aged 24–59 months in 2022 [11]. Compared with influenza, the incidence of COVID-19 in 2022 was higher than prepandemic influenza seasons, especially among infants aged <3 months [11].

Our study has certain limitations. First, validated algorithms had very high specificity against laboratory-confirmed RSV-related [28], influenza-related [28], and COVID-19–related [29] hospital admissions, but only moderate sensitivity for RSV and influenza, and province-wide laboratory data were not available to confirm viral etiology in this study; hospital admission rates may therefore be underestimated. Second, although Ontario has a universal healthcare system, disruptions in healthcare access and delivery associated with the pandemic, together with fear of infection in healthcare settings, might have led some parents to delay or forgo medical care for their child, particularly for milder presentations [37, 38]. Moreover, during periods of limited hospital and ICU bed supply, thresholds to admit children might have increased, leading to underestimates of the true burden of illness. Third, laboratories and hospitals that were overburdened with testing for SARS-CoV-2 might have changed their testing and reporting practices for non–SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses [5]. RSV- and influenza-related admission rates might therefore be underestimated during periods of increased SARS-CoV-2 circulation. On the other hand, with the implementation of revised laboratory protocols following the onset of the pandemic and the increased use of multiplex polymerase chain reaction testing for respiratory pathogens among children [22, 39, 40], increases in RSV- and influenza-related hospital admission rates could be partially attributable to increases in testing. However, we did not observe declines in measures of disease severity among admitted cases as might be expected with increased detection of milder disease presentations, suggesting that our data likely reflect true changes in hospitalization rates rather than changes in testing practices.

Despite these limitations, this study fills an important knowledge gap by contextualizing the burden of COVID-19 in relation to RSV and influenza among children. Our study confirms that RSV continues to be a major cause of childhood hospitalization and although the COVID-19 pandemic appears to be receding, its disease burden as it transitions to endemicity remains to be established; however, like RSV, it currently remains a major source of morbidity among young infants and children with comorbidities. This study also highlights the uncertainty about the enduring consequences of pandemic response measures on morbidity associated with RSV and influenza in children. It is reasonable to expect that their temporal dynamics and epidemiology will continue to adjust differently, as each is uniquely influenced by factors that drive transmission [7, 41]. The unprecedented peaks in RSV-related hospital admissions during the winter of 2022–2023, together with the return of influenza and ongoing SARS-CoV-2 transmission, despite high levels of population immunity [23], underscore the need to strengthen systems for real-time surveillance and to proactively prepare for critical healthcare scenarios. Moreover, ongoing changes in the timing and epidemiology of RSV, influenza, and COVID-19 will need to be considered in the delivery of new and existing vaccines and therapeutics, and continued monitoring will be required to ensure these interventions remain effective in a changing viral landscape.
