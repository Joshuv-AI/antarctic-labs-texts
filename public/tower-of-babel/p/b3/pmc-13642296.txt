Cancer history and second cancer risk in polycythemia vera and essential thrombocythemia
Authors: Giuseppe G Loscocco, Fnu Aperna, Moazah Iftikhar, Priyansh Faldu, Masooma S Rana, Amritpal Singh, Tiziano Barbui, Paola Guglielmelli, Alessandro M Vannucchi, Animesh Pardanani, Naseema Gangat, Ayalew Tefferi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), including polycythemia vera (PV) and essential thrombocythemia (ET), have an increased risk of second cancers (SC), although determinants of risk remain incompletely defined. We retrospectively analyzed 1968 consecutive patients with PV or ET (median follow-up 11.2 years) to identify predictors of SC. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated using competing-risk methodology, and predictors were assessed using Fine-Gray regression. During follow-up, SC occurred in 404 patients (20%), with non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) representing the most frequent subtype. In multivariable models including all cancer types, prior cancer (sHR 2.02, p < 0.001), older age (per 10 years: sHR 1.20, p < 0.001), male sex (sHR 1.39, p = 0.002), and hyperlipidemia (sHR 1.41, p = 0.003) independently predicted SC. After excluding NMSC, prior cancer remained associated with SC risk (sHR 1.48, p = 0.048). Notably, prior NMSC strongly predicted subsequent NMSC (sHR 6.48, p < 0.001), followed by prior non-NMSC cancer (sHR 2.11, p < 0.001), age (sHR 1.29, p < 0.001), and male sex (sHR 1.43, p = 0.018). TET2 mutations showed a borderline association with NMSC risk (sHR 1.73, p = 0.055), while hydroxyurea was not associated with SC, with a non-significant trend toward increased NMSC risk (sHR 1.82, p = 0.110). These findings support risk-adapted cancer surveillance in PV and ET, with particular emphasis on regular dermatologic monitoring. Subject terms: Risk factors, Epidemiology

Introduction

Myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) constitute a group of disorders defined by clonal proliferation of stem cell-derived myeloid lineages, encompassing polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF). Both PV and ET are generally indolent conditions with extended clinical courses, distinguished by clonal expansion of hematopoietic stem cells and increased production of largely mature myeloid cells [1, 2]. These disorders are driven by somatic mutations affecting the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, including JAK2, CALR, and MPL mutations [3]. PV and ET are typically associated with an increased risk of thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications, as well as the potential for long-term disease evolution to myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukemia, leading to higher mortality than in the general population [4, 5]. In this regard, recent Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry data including 10,725 PV and 8768 ET patients reported similar five-year relative survival of 88.3% and 88.7%, and median overall survival of 11.9 and 12.1 years in PV and ET, respectively [6].

PV and ET treatment focuses on minimizing the risk of thromboembolic events and managing symptoms, whereas no treatment has been shown to prevent disease progression. Clinical management includes phlebotomies for PV with a hematocrit target <45%, and the use of antiplatelet agents such as acetylsalicylic acid, as well as cytoreductive agents such as hydroxyurea, interferon-α, anagrelide, busulfan, and ruxolitinib. These drugs are used for both PV and ET, tailored according to the specific disease and patient risk category [4, 5, 7, 8].

In the past decade, some studies reported a higher risk of developing a second cancer (SC) in MPN compared with the general population, including both lymphoproliferative neoplasms and non-hematologic malignancies [9–16]. Particularly, an increased incidence (1.5–3.0-fold) of skin, lung, kidney, and thyroid tumors was observed in patients with MPN older than 60 years. In contrast, the risk of other solid tumors, such as colon, breast, and prostate cancer did not significantly differ from that observed in the general population [16]. These investigations generally encompassed patients with broad non-homogeneous MPN entities and did not account for crucial factors such as prior clinical and oncological history, associated comorbidities, mutational profiles, or other relevant clinical and laboratory characteristics when evaluating risk associations and predictions.

The pathogenic mechanisms underlying the higher risk of SC in MPN remain unclear. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including shared genetic predispositions, an intrinsic tendency toward malignancy, the impact of cytotoxic anti-neoplastic therapies, and potential associations with chronic inflammation or immune system dysfunction [15, 17–22].

Findings across studies are heterogeneous, reflecting differences in patient populations, treatment exposures, and follow-up duration. Moreover, relatively few studies have specifically focused on patients with PV and ET, two MPN subtypes characterized by prolonged survival and therefore a potentially higher cumulative risk of developing additional malignancies. Beyond incidence, the clinical implications of SC in patients with PV and ET remain insufficiently defined. Moreover, it is unclear which clinical, molecular, or treatment-related factors are associated with an increased risk of SC development in this population. Understanding these determinants may guide long-term monitoring and treatment decisions. The current study seeks to fill knowledge gaps by retrospectively analyzing cancers occurring before/at or after MPN diagnosis in a cohort of 1968 PV and ET patients followed up at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota, USA.

Methods

Study population, clinical and laboratory data

Institutional review board approval was obtained from the Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, USA, to conduct minimum-risk research protocols allowing retrospective collection and analysis of patient data in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained according to institutional requirements.

The study population included patients with polycythemia vera (PV) and essential thrombocythemia (ET) evaluated between 1970 and 2024 and diagnosed and/or reviewed according to the 2022 International Consensus Classification (ICC) [1] and the 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) [2] classification. When available, diagnostic bone marrow biopsies were reviewed to avoid inadvertent inclusion of prefibrotic primary myelofibrosis, particularly in cases with anemia or leukoerythroblastosis.

All patients underwent mutational analysis for driver mutations (JAK2 V617F, CALR exon 9, and MPL W515). A targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel including 29 myeloid-relevant genes was available in 620 patients. Cytogenetic analyses were performed and reported according to the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN) criteria.

Definition of cancers and other clinical variables

Documented primary cancers were annotated into 17 categories, excluding pre-cancerous conditions, MPN progression, and tumor relapse. Second cancer (SC) was defined as any malignancy diagnosed ≥3 months after MPN diagnosis, whereas cancers diagnosed before or within three months of MPN diagnosis were classified as cancers occurring before or at the time of MPN diagnosis. Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases (AID) were diagnosed according to internationally recognized criteria specific for each disease, including both clinical manifestations and serologic markers. All thrombotic events were objectively confirmed by imaging studies, laboratory biomarkers, and/or electrocardiographic findings. Major arterial thrombosis included myocardial infarction, angina, cerebrovascular accident, transient ischemic attack, and peripheral, aortic, or mesenteric arterial thrombosis. Major venous thrombosis included deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, portal, splenic, mesenteric, or hepatic vein thrombosis, cerebral sinus thrombosis, and central retinal vein thrombosis. Similarly to SC, AID and thrombotic events were classified as occurring before/at or after MPN diagnosis, with events occurring after diagnosis requiring an interval of ≥3 months from MPN diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Clinical and laboratory parameters were collected at diagnosis or first referral, which occurred within one year of diagnosis and before cytoreductive therapy initiation. Continuous variables were summarized as median (range) and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons used the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and χ² test for categorical variables. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For outcomes with death as a competing event, cumulative incidence functions (CIF) were estimated using a competing-risk framework. This approach was applied to myelofibrosis progression, leukemic transformation, thrombotic events, and second cancers. Group differences were assessed using Gray’s tests, and sub-distribution hazard ratios (sHRs) were estimated using Fine-Gray regression models. These analyses evaluated the association between prior cancer history and clinical outcomes. Sensitivity analyses redefined prior cancer after excluding NMSC and after excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies. P-values for multiple pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm method.

Because detailed treatment exposure data were not consistently available, cytoreductive therapies were not modeled as time-dependent covariates. Exploratory analyses evaluated treatment exposure by restricting the cohort to patients treated with a single cytoreductive agent at diagnosis compared with untreated patients. Using this restrictive exposure definition, hydroxyurea was the only treatment group with sufficient sample size for formal analysis (n = 1033), and was compared with untreated patients (n = 178); other agents were too infrequently represented for reliable analysis. Multivariable Fine-Gray models were adjusted for baseline covariates associated with SC risk, including age, sex, MPN subtype, prior cancer history, and cardiovascular risk factors. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), JMP Pro 17.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), and R version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

The study population included 1968 consecutive patients with PV (n = 1001) and ET (n = 967) with a median follow-up of 11.2 (range 0.1–53) years. Baseline characteristics and events during the clinical course of PV and ET patients are reported in Table 1. The median age was 60 (18–96) years with ET younger than PV patients (58 vs. 62 years, p < 0.001); 56% were female, more represented in ET (64% vs. 49%, p < 0.001). The presence of at least one cardiovascular risk factor was reported in 1407 patients (73%), and the most common was hypertension (52%), followed by hyperlipidemia (47%), smoking (16%), and type 2 diabetes (12%). A history of autoimmune/inflammatory disease (AID) was reported in 6% of patients. Major thromboses at or prior to diagnosis were documented for 473 patients (24%), including arterial (n = 270, 14%) and venous (n = 244, 12%) events. All PV cases were JAK2 mutated, whereas a driver mutation was found in 873 (90%) ET patients: JAK2 in 599 (63%), followed by CALR type 1/1-like (n = 148, 15%), CALR type 2/2-like (n = 97, 10%), and MPL (n = 29, 3%); 8% were triple-negative (TN). Cytogenetic information was available in 1,418 cases (72%), and an abnormal karyotype was reported in 139 (10%) patients. Non-driver mutation information was available in 620 patients, and the most frequent variants were reported in TET2 (13%), ASXL1 (7%), DNMT3A (5%), SRSF2 (3%), SF3B1 (3%) and TP53 (2%).

During follow-up, major thrombosis after diagnosis was documented in 390 (20%) patients, including 230 (12%) arterial and 201 (10%) venous events. Progression to myelofibrosis and leukemic transformation was reported in 326 (17%) and 77 (4%) patients, respectively. A history of another cancer was reported in 598 (30%) patients and included cancers before/at (n = 291, 15%) or after (n = 404, 20%) the diagnosis of MPN.

Baseline clinical and laboratory characteristics of patients with and without SC are summarized in Table 2. Patients who developed SC were significantly older at MPN diagnosis compared with those who did not (64 vs. 59 years, p < 0.001). SC were more frequent among patients with PV (58% vs. 49%, p = 0.002) and among male patients (53% vs 41%, p < 0.001). Patients who developed SC also showed higher leukocyte counts (median 10.0 vs. 9.3 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.027) and absolute neutrophil counts (median 7.1 vs. 6.4 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.012), while platelet counts were lower (median 583 vs 620 × 10⁹/L, p = 0.012). Among cardiovascular risk factors, hypertension (63% vs. 49%, p < 0.001) and hyperlipidemia (59% vs. 44%, p < 0.001) were more frequent in patients with SC. In addition, these patients more frequently experienced arterial thrombosis before MPN diagnosis (19% vs. 12%, p = 0.002) as well as venous (19% vs. 8%, p < 0.001) and arterial thrombosis after diagnosis (17% vs. 10%, p < 0.001). A history of previous cancer before/at MPN diagnosis was significantly more common among patients who subsequently developed SC (24% vs. 12%, p < 0.001). Moreover, JAK2 mutations were more frequent in patients with SC (86% vs. 80%, p = 0.008), as well as abnormal karyotype (13% vs. 9%, p = 0.042). Among NGS-annotated patients, TET2 (20% vs. 11%, p = 0.013), SRSF2 (6% vs. 2%, p = 0.022), and SF3B1 mutations (6% vs. 2%, p = 0.007) were enriched in patients with SC.

Occurrence and spectrum of cancers

The temporal distribution of cancers before/at and after PV and ET diagnosis and the spectrum of cancer types are illustrated in Fig. 1and Fig. 2, respectively. Cancers were observed both before and after MPN diagnosis, with a substantial proportion identified during follow-up. The most frequent cancer type was non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) reported in 28% of patients with a cancer diagnosed before/at MPN, and in 47% of those with a SC diagnosis. The other SC types included lymphoid neoplasms (10%), melanoma (10%), prostate cancer (9%), and trachea/lung/bronchial cancer (8%), as the most frequent, with a broadly similar spectrum observed in patients with PV and ET. The detailed distribution of cancer types with frequencies calculated on the entire study cohort are shown in Suppl. Table 1. Among hematologic malignancies, lymphoid neoplasms (mainly chronic lymphocytic leukemia, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and multiple myeloma) were more frequent than myeloid neoplasms (including chronic myeloid leukemia and systemic mastocytosis) both before/at and after MPN diagnosis; however, no clear subtype-specific pattern was observed.

Impact of previous cancer history on survival and disease progression

Overall survival differed significantly according to prior cancer status and disease subtype. Kaplan-Meier and pairwise Cox regression analyses showed worse survival among patients with prior cancer in both PV (HR 2.30, p < 0.001) and ET (HR 2.16, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). These findings were consistent after excluding NMSC and both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, in a multivariable model adjusting for baseline clinical characteristics, including MPN subtype and JAK2 mutational status, prior cancer history was not independently associated with overall survival (p = 0.8). In contrast, older age (> 60 years) was strongly associated with inferior survival (HR 6.0, p < 0.001), as were male sex (HR 1.3, p = 0.020), hypertension (HR 1.6, p = 0.002), higher absolute neutrophil count (ANC, HR 1.1, p < 0.001), higher absolute monocyte count (AMC, HR 2.0, p < 0.001), and a history of arterial thrombosis (HR 1.4, p < 0.001). These findings were consistent after excluding NMSC and both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (Suppl. Table 2).

Impact of prior cancer history on venous and arterial thrombosis

Considering patients without prior cancer, those with PV had a significantly higher risk of venous thrombosis compared with ET patients (sHR 1.52, p = 0.007, Holm-adjusted p = 0.042). When stratified according to prior cancer status, differences in venous thrombosis were observed within the PV cohort across all the sensitivity analyses (Suppl. Table 3). In this regard, among PV patients, those with prior cancer had a borderline significant higher risk for venous thrombosis after the exclusion of NMSC (sHR 1.61, p = 0.043, Holm-adjusted p = 0.128) and both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (sHR 1.75, p = 0.022, Holm-adjusted p = 0.067). In contrast, within the ET cohort, prior cancer status was not consistently associated with differences in venous thrombosis risk across all the models. A comparable pattern was documented for arterial thrombosis within the PV cohort (Suppl. Table 3). In the analysis including all prior cancers, PV patients showed a higher arterial thrombotic risk compared with PV patients without prior cancer (sHR 1.50, p = 0.062, Holm-adjusted p = 0.253). This trend persisted after exclusion of NMSC (sHR 1.68, p = 0.040, Holm-adjusted p = 0.198) and was also observed in the model excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (sHR 1.83, p = 0.022, Holm-adjusted p = 0.108). In the ET group, prior cancer did not consistently affect arterial thrombosis risk in any model. Cumulative incidence curves for venous and arterial thrombotic events according to MPN subtype and prior cancer history are presented in Suppl. Figs. 4, 5, respectively.

In multivariable Fine-Gray competing-risk models adjusting for clinical covariates, prior cancer was not independently associated with venous thrombosis across any prior cancer definition, including all cancers (sHR 0.94, p = 0.79), excluding NMSC (sHR 1.26, p = 0.32), and excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (sHR 1.41, p = 0.14). Prior venous thrombosis emerged as the only consistent predictor of venous thrombosis across all three models (sHR 1.88, p < 0.001; sHR 1.86, p < 0.001; and sHR 1.86, p < 0.001, respectively). Similarly, prior cancer was not independently associated with arterial thrombosis in any multivariable model, including analyses considering all cancers (sHR 0.93, p = 0.72), excluding NMSC (sHR 1.06, p = 0.77), and excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (sHR 1.11, p = 0.63). In contrast, arterial thrombosis was significantly associated with older age, prior arterial thrombosis, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia in all sensitivity analyses (Suppl. Tables 4, 5).

Cumulative incidence and determinants of second cancers

The cumulative incidence of SC differed significantly across groups defined by MPN subtype and prior cancer history (Fig. 4). In analyses including all cancers, PV with prior cancer showed a significantly higher cumulative incidence compared with PV without prior cancer (sHR 2.24, p < 0.001, Holm-adjusted p < 0.001). Among patients without prior cancer, PV showed a higher cumulative incidence compared with ET (sHR 1.33, p = 0.017, Holm-adjusted p = 0.034). A similarly increased risk was observed when comparing PV with prior cancer to ET without prior cancer (sHR 2.96, p < 0.001, Holm-adjusted p < 0.001). These patterns were consistent across sensitivity analyses excluding NMSC (p = 0.002) and excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (p = 0.01), although some pairwise comparisons did not retain statistical significance after Holm-adjustment (Suppl. Fig. 6 and Suppl. Table 6).

In univariable Fine-Gray analyses, several clinical variables were significantly associated with increased SC risk. Hyperlipidemia was strongly associated with SC risk across all models (all cancers: sHR 1.90, p < 0.001; excluding NMSC: sHR 1.82, p < 0.001; excluding NMSC and hematologic malignancies: sHR 1.73, p < 0.001). Increasing age (per 10 years) was consistently associated with higher risk (sHR 1.31, p < 0.001; sHR 1.19, p < 0.001; sHR 1.21, p < 0.001, respectively across models). Similarly, hypertension, arterial thrombosis before/at diagnosis, and male sex were associated with increased risk across models (Suppl. Table 7).

Prior cancer was consistently associated with an increased risk of SC, including all cancers (sHR 2.82, p < 0.001), excluding NMSC (sHR 1.87, p < 0.001), and excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (sHR 1.89, p = 0.003). Of note, AID history was associated with increased risk of SC both including all cancers (sHR 1.57, p = 0.015) and after the exclusion of NMSC (sHR 1.70, p = 0.022). Among molecular variables, TET2 mutations were significantly associated with SC risk in the analysis including all cancers (sHR 1.66, p = 0.022), whereas TP53 mutations were significantly associated in the analyses excluding NMSC (sHR 3.03, p = 0.033) and excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (sHR 3.54, p = 0.016). Similarly, SRSF2 and SF3B1 mutations were significantly associated in the analysis including all cancers (SRSF2: sHR 3.06, p = 0.013; SF3B1: sHR 2.59, p = 0.030) and in the analysis excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies (SRSF2: sHR 3.52, p = 0.025; SF3B1: sHR 3.24, p = 0.029) (Suppl. Table 7).

In multivariable Fine-Gray models adjusted for clinical covariates, age, male sex, and hyperlipidemia emerged as the most consistent predictors of SC risk across analyses (Fig. 5). Particularly, in the model including all cancers, prior cancer (sHR 2.02; p < 0.001), older age (per 10 years, sHR 1.20, p < 0.001), male sex (sHR 1.39, p = 0.002), and hyperlipidemia (sHR 1.41, p = 0.003) were independently associated with increased SC risk. After exclusion of NMSC, prior cancer (sHR 1.47; p = 0.048), older age (sHR 1.10, p = 0.018), male sex (sHR 1.37, p = 0.021), and hyperlipidemia (sHR 1.50, p = 0.008) remained independently associated. In the most restrictive model excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies, older age (sHR 1.13, p = 0.010) and male sex (sHR 1.36, p = 0.038) retained independent significance. An exploratory multivariable model including molecular variables confirmed hyperlipidemia and male sex as independent predictors of SC risk, while TET2, SRSF2, and TP53 mutations lost significance after adjusting for clinical factors. SF3B1 mutations retained significance in the all-cancers model (sHR 2.52, p = 0.035; Suppl. Table 8).

The cumulative incidence of NMSC differed significantly according to prior cancer status. Patients with prior NMSC showed the highest cumulative incidence of subsequent NMSC, followed by those with prior other cancer (non-NMSC), whereas those without prior cancer had the lowest risk (Gray p < 0.001, Fig. 6A). Pairwise comparisons confirmed significantly higher risk among individuals with prior NMSC compared with those with prior other non-NMSC (sHR 3.37, p < 0.001) and those without prior cancer (sHR 10.86, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses according to MPN subtype demonstrated a consistent pattern across PV and ET, with the highest cumulative incidence observed among individuals with prior NMSC in both subtypes (Suppl. Discussion

In this large single-center cohort of 1,968 patients with PV and ET, we provide a comprehensive evaluation of cancer occurrence before and after MPN diagnosis, with particular attention to determinants of secondary cancer (SC) and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC). Our findings confirm that cancer represents a frequent long-term complication in MPN and identify prior cancer history, especially prior NMSC, as a dominant predictor of subsequent malignancy risk. Consistent with prior population-based studies, SC occurred in approximately one-fifth of patients during follow-up, underscoring the substantial oncologic burden associated with MPN [10, 13, 22].

The cumulative incidence of SC differed according to MPN subtype and prior cancer history, with PV patients and those with prior malignancies showing higher cumulative risks in unadjusted analyses. However, in multivariable analyses including all cancer types, prior cancer history emerged as an independent predictor of subsequent cancer risk, together with older age, male sex, and hyperlipidemia. Notably, the association with prior cancer persisted after excluding NMSC from the outcome definition, although in the most restrictive model, excluding both NMSC and hematologic malignancies only age and male sex retained independent significance. These findings indicate that prior cancer history represents a clinically meaningful marker of future cancer susceptibility, particularly for cancer categories characterized by strong host- and surveillance-related components [13, 15, 22, 23].

The prominent role of NMSC represents one of the most clinically relevant observations of this study [13, 22]. NMSC was the most frequent malignancy both before and after MPN diagnosis, and prior NMSC emerged as the strongest predictor of subsequent NMSC development, with a magnitude of effect exceeding that of all other clinical variables. This finding suggests the existence of an underlying susceptibility phenotype in selected patients, potentially reflecting cumulative ultraviolet exposure, impaired immune surveillance, or underlying genomic instability. Importantly, prior non-NMSC malignancies were also associated with increased NMSC risk, supporting the hypothesis that cancer susceptibility in MPN patients may not be organ-specific but rather reflects broader host-related predisposition mechanisms.

Age and male sex were consistently associated with increased risk of both SC and NMSC, in agreement with established epidemiologic patterns in the general population. Notably, hyperlipidemia emerged as a consistent independent predictor of SC risk across multiple models. While cardiovascular risk factors are common in MPN, the biological link between metabolic dysregulation and carcinogenesis is increasingly recognized, particularly in the context of chronic inflammation and oxidative stress [17, 20, 24]. Whether hyperlipidemia represents a direct contributor to carcinogenesis or a surrogate marker of systemic inflammatory and metabolic burden remains uncertain [25–28]. Statin exposure and detailed lipid profiles were not systematically available, precluding assessment of whether specific lipid abnormalities or lipid-lowering therapy modified SC risk. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as supporting statin use for cancer prevention, but rather as highlighting dyslipidemia and metabolic risk as clinically informative markers of cancer susceptibility. These findings support the concept that cancer risk in MPN may be influenced not only by hematologic disease biology but also by systemic inflammatory and metabolic factors.

Our molecular analyses provide additional insights into potential biological contributors to cancer susceptibility. In univariable analyses, mutations involving genes associated with clonal hematopoiesis and genomic instability, including TP53, SF3B1, and SRSF2, were associated with increased risk of SC. However, these associations were attenuated in multivariable models, suggesting that molecular signals may partly reflect underlying clinical risk profiles rather than independent determinants. Exploratory analyses suggested a borderline association between TET2 mutations and increased NMSC risk. Given the established role of TET2 in immune regulation and inflammatory signaling [29]. This observation is biologically plausible and aligns with emerging evidence linking clonal hematopoiesis to increased cancer susceptibility and impaired immune surveillance. Nonetheless, the limited availability of molecular data restricts definitive conclusions and highlights the need for larger molecularly annotated cohorts.

An additional area of clinical relevance relates to the potential role of cytoreductive therapy. In exploratory analyses restricted to patients with clearly defined baseline treatment exposure, hydroxyurea use was not independently associated with increased risk of SC [30]. However, a non-significant trend toward increased NMSC risk was observed [31]. Other agents, including ruxolitinib, were too infrequently represented under this predefined single-agent exposure framework to permit reliable analysis of their association with NMSC risk. These findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the absence of detailed treatment timing and cumulative dose information, and they should not be interpreted as excluding a potential therapy-NMSC relationship. Instead, intrinsic patient-related factors including age, prior cancer history, and systemic comorbidities, appear to play a more substantial role in shaping cancer susceptibility [17, 20, 24].

Taken together, these findings support a model in which cancer risk in MPN patients reflects the interaction between disease-related biological factors, host susceptibility, and environmental exposures. Chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and clonal hematopoiesis may collectively create a permissive environment for genomic instability and tumorigenesis. In this context, the observed association between prior NMSC and subsequent cancer risk may represent a clinically identifiable marker of heightened susceptibility rather than an isolated dermatologic phenomenon.

From a clinical standpoint, our findings have direct implications for long-term management of patients with PV and ET. The markedly elevated risk of NMSC among patients with prior skin cancer strongly supports the implementation of structured dermatologic surveillance in this subgroup [32–34]. While these findings do not mandate broad changes to current treatment algorithms, prior NMSC may reasonably inform individualized treatment discussions, particularly when therapies with potential cutaneous toxicity are being considered. Similarly, the association between metabolic risk factors and cancer development highlights the importance of aggressive management of cardiovascular comorbidities as part of comprehensive MPN care. These observations reinforce the concept that long-term outcomes in MPN patients extend beyond thrombosis and transformation and require a multidisciplinary preventive approach.

Strengths include the large cohort size, long follow-up, competing-risk methodology accounting for death, and systematic sensitivity analyses across multiple cancer definitions.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective study design introduces the possibility of residual confounding and incomplete data capture. Treatment exposure was defined at baseline, and detailed longitudinal treatment data were not consistently available, limiting the ability to evaluate dose-dependent effects. Molecular data were available only in a subset of patients, reducing statistical power for mutation-specific analyses. Additionally, environmental exposures such as ultraviolet radiation and differential intensity of dermatologic and oncologic surveillance, which are known determinants of NMSC detection, were not available and may represent unmeasured confounders. Patients with prior malignancies, particularly prior NMSC, may have undergone more frequent evaluations, potentially increasing the detection of subsequent cancers.

In conclusion, the current study demonstrates that SC represents a frequent and clinically relevant complication in patients with PV and ET, with NMSC emerging as the dominant malignancy subtype. Prior cancer history, particularly prior NMSC, together with older age, male sex, and hyperlipidemia, identifies patients at increased risk of subsequent malignancy. These findings support the implementation of risk-adapted surveillance strategies and highlight the importance of integrating oncologic and metabolic risk assessment into the long-term management of patients with MPN. Future prospective studies with comprehensive molecular profiling and longitudinal treatment data are needed to refine risk stratification and clarify mechanisms of cancer susceptibility.
