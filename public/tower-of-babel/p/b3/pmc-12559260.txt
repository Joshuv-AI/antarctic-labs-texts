Phishing detection on webpages in European non-English languages based on machine learning
Authors: Dan Komosny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMachine learning-based phishing detection is crucial for preventing zero-day attacks. State-of-the-art phishing detection performs well on English webpages. However, it is not adequately accurate for webpages in minor languages. This work significantly improves phishing detection in European countries where minor languages are predominantly spoken. The proposed language-based phishing detection outperforms state-of-the-art methods for webpages in minor languages, achieving 99% accuracy on local webpages in the following countries: the Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Croatia, Hungary, Lithuania, Latvia, Poland, Romania, Serbia, Slovakia, and Slovenia. The main improvement lies in reducing the false positive rate, where local benign webpages are incorrectly identified as phishing. The proposed method reduces the false positive rate by up to a factor of 10 for webpages in minor languages. The results are statistically robust across different webpage sets, scaled for real-world use. The Shapiro-Wilk test confirms a normal distribution of the results in each tested country, with p-values consistently above 0.05. Paired T-tests yield p-values consistently below 0.05, indicating statistically significant improvements in each country. The low variability in the results further demonstrates the robustness of the proposed method. To foster reproducibility, the source code, datasets, and raw results are made publicly available, including approximately two million local webpages from 16 countries. Reference data from English-speaking and major-language-speaking countries are also included. Introduction

Phishing is one of the most pervasive and damaging cyber threats, targeting individuals, organizations, and critical infrastructure for financial gain or unauthorized access. While generic phishing attacks target a wide range of victims, tailored phishing campaigns are increasingly sophisticated, focusing on specific individuals, groups, or organizations. These tailored attacks often evade traditional detection mechanisms, such as phishing blacklists, due to their single-use nature or delayed inclusion in such lists 1,2. Machine learning-based phishing detection can identify zero-day phishing attacks before they are added to blacklists. However, despite recent advances 3,4, a critical gap remains. While state-of-the-art phishing detection models perform well on English and other widely spoken languages, their performance is inadequate for webpages in minor languages.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing a language-based phishing detection model that improves accuracy for webpages in minor languages. The proposed model is based on a comprehensive analysis of URL terms from local webpages in countries where minor languages are predominantly used. The model significantly reduces false positives, benign webpages incorrectly identified as phishing, while maintaining high detection accuracy. The model is rigorously evaluated across 12 European countries: Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Croatia, Hungary, Lithuania, Latvia, Poland, Romania, Serbia, Slovenia, and Slovakia, and compared against state-of-the-art phishing detection. The results show a significant reduction in false positives, achieving an overall accuracy of 99 % across the countries.

The results are also statistically validated using real-world-sized test sets. The Shapiro-Wilk test confirms the normal distribution of the results, and paired T-tests demonstrate statistically significant improvements (p-values < 0.05) in each country. The model’s robustness is further demonstrated by its consistent performance across different test datasets. To promote reproducibility and further research, the source code, datasets, and raw results are made publicly available. The datasets include approximately two million local webpages from 16 countries, along with reference data from English-speaking and major-language-speaking countries, such as the United Kingdom, Germany, France, and Spain.

The proposed language-based phishing detection improves cybersecurity in countries with minor languages, which have been neglected in research. This work advances a more inclusive approach to phishing detection, ensuring that users, regardless of their language, are adequately protected. This section provides an overview of previous research, with a focus on machine learning-based approaches. It also reviews state-of-the-art methods and their performance across different datasets.

The search process included research databases and repositories such as IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ACM Digital Library, and Google Scholar. Training data

The choice of training data for phishing detection models is critical for effective detection, with URLs being the most preferred source (50 %), followed by network information (19 %) and webpage content (15 %)5. URLs offer significant advantages over other training data sources, including reduced training and detection times, as they eliminate the need to crawl webpages before analysis. This makes them ideal for resource-constrained environments, such as mobile devices, where computational efficiency is important 6. URLs are also simple to extract and process, enabling scalable and lightweight detection systems. Techniques such as natural language processing and transformer-based models have been successfully applied to URL-based phishing detection, achieving high accuracy with minimal computational overhead.

Network information, including IP addresses, domain registration periods, DNS records, and website traffic volume, also provides valuable insights into website legitimacy. Phishing domains often exhibit distinct characteristics, such as shorter registration periods, which can serve as strong indicators of malicious intent 7. However, obtaining and processing this data typically involves querying external databases or services, which can introduce latency in real-time detection. Similarly, analysis of webpage content, including textual elements, can improve detection accuracy 8. For example, analyzing dominant colors and CSS properties has proven effective in distinguishing phishing pages from legitimate ones 9,10. However, these methods require complete rendering and parsing of webpages, making them computationally intensive and less suitable for real-time applications or resource-constrained devices. The choice of training data involves a trade-off between accuracy and computational efficiency. URLs remain the most practical option for lightweight and real-time phishing detection, especially in resource-constrained environments. Network and webpage content-based approaches, while effective, are more suitable for scenarios where latency is less critical.

Zero-day attacks

The detection of zero-day attacks, especially phishing, remains a critical and evolving challenge in cybersecurity. The ease with which phishing webpages can be created worsens this issue. Individuals with even average technical skills can deploy phishing sites using available free tools 11,12. As a result, a vast number of new phishing webpages are continuously generated, necessitating advanced detection mechanisms. Recent advancements in machine learning have shown promise in addressing these persistent threats. Work 13 proposed an innovative framework that converts portable executable files into colored images, extracts deep features using fine-tuned deep learning models, and employs one-class support vector machines for detection. This approach eliminates the need for manual feature extraction, improving both efficiency and scalability. The framework achieved an accuracy of 99.30 % on the Malimg dataset, especially in detecting zero-day and polymorphic malware. However, phishing detection presents unique challenges that remain unresolved. Phishing attacks often leverage social engineering tactics, which can bypass learning-based systems 14. Moreover, the rapid evolution of phishing techniques, including the use of generative artificial intelligence, introduces new threats that demand adaptive and real-time detection mechanisms. These gaps highlight the need for innovation in phishing detection to keep pace with sophisticated attack strategies.

Adversarial attacks

Adversarial attacks in phishing detection pose a significant challenge, especially for pre-trained machine learning models that rely on static feature representations 3,15. These models are often trained on datasets dominated by major languages such as English, leaving them vulnerable to manipulations involving terms from minor or less-common languages. Attackers exploit this limitation by incorporating words or characters from scripts such as Cyrillic, Arabic, or Chinese into URLs, creating deceptive patterns that appear legitimate. For example, a URL such as http://example.com/prihlaseni (using the Czech word for login) might evade detection because the model does not handle such linguistic diversity. Techniques such as TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency), which rely on term rarity and frequency, may fail to accurately assess the significance of these terms 16. Without exposure to diverse linguistic patterns, these models struggle to generalize across languages, making them vulnerable to manipulation. This vulnerability highlights the need for more inclusive and adaptive approaches to phishing detection in multilingual contexts.

Work 17 evaluated several machine learning models, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Decision Tree, K-Nearest Neighbors, Support Vector Classifier, Linear Support Vector Classifier, and Multinomial Naive Bayes (NB). The authors conducted experiments with varying proportions of training and test data, ranging from 10 to 90 %. Phishing detection accuracy generally increased with a higher proportion of training data for most models. Precision did not show a notable difference with an increased percentage of training data, exhibiting a decreasing trend for some models, such as the Support Vector Classifier and MultinomialNB. Recall improved across all models under investigation.

Work 18 revealed significant differences between the phishing detection results obtained from datasets B and C, webpages from top-visited domains and webpages with login forms, respectively. Each dataset contained 30 k benign and phishing webpages in equal proportions. The analyzed models included Logistic Regression, MultinomialNB, Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, AdaBoost, XGBoost, and K-Nearest Neighbors. Initially, the models were evaluated using handcrafted URL features. Subsequently, automatic URL features were extracted using Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) at the character N-gram level. The findings demonstrated that the best accuracy achieved was 97 % for both datasets. This suggests that automatic feature selection using TF-IDF is an effective strategy when paired with prevalent models. This state-of-the-art approach and the reported results are further supported by 6,19.

Observed keypoints

It is widely acknowledged that machine learning-based phishing detection achieves high accuracy on generic URL datasets 19,20. However, the effectiveness of the results is significantly influenced by the training datasets used, particularly the types of benign webpages included. It is also crucial to use up-to-date datasets of benign and phishing webpages. Work 18 showed that phishing detection models trained on outdated data experience reduced performance when the data are one year old. A model trained on data that were four years old failed to achieve 90 % accuracy, even though it had previously performed well on current data. Language-based phishing detection

This section introduces and evaluates a novel approach to phishing detection tailored for non-English webpages, particularly those in European countries where minor languages are spoken. The first subsection introduces the challenges of phishing detection in countries with minor languages and proposes a solution leveraging custom datasets. The second subsection presents the results of the proposed method, demonstrating significant reductions in false positive rates across multiple countries. The third subsection provides rigorous statistical validation of the model’s performance, ensuring its reliability and robustness in real-world scenarios.

Method for non-English speaking countries

Phishing detection is effective for English webpages, but its performance diminishes for local webpages in countries where minor languages are predominantly spoken. A significant number of benign websites in these countries are misclassified as phishing due to the misinterpretation of unique URL terms. The following analysis illustrates this issue. Figure 1 shows frequent terms found in webpage URLs from an English-speaking country (United Kingdom) and three major language-speaking countries (Germany, France, and Spain). In contrast, Fig. 2 highlights frequent terms in the URLs of webpages from four European countries where minor languages are spoken (Czech Republic, Estonia, Croatia, and Romania). A visual comparison reveals notable differences between English, major language-speaking countries, and countries with minor languages. The differences in URL terms are particularly evident among the countries in the second figure. Figures A.1 and A.2 in Appendix A provide additional details on URL terms from webpages in eight European countries with minor languages, highlighting differences between these countries.

Each model was applied to different URL datasets sourced from related work and public repositories. The best phishing detection results in these countries were achieved using training data compiled from 21. This is because the data comes from a raw collection of crawled webpages. Thus, there is no bias toward global webpages, frequently accessed webpages, or webpages with specific content, unlike datasets used in related works. Statistical validation

To rigorously assess the robustness of the language-based phishing detection model with a tailored training dataset, statistical validation was conducted to evaluate its performance in real-world scenarios. A key challenge in evaluating phishing detection systems is the rarity of phishing attacks compared to benign webpages. This imbalance necessitates evaluating a large number of benign webpages over time to accurately estimate the false positive rate. Studies suggest that users visit approximately 50 webpages per day, or 1,500 per month 22. While this is an empirical value, it provides a reference for the required scale of testing. The test size was set to 3,000 benign local webpages, with 10 test sets used for validation to assess variability. This test set is crucial for evaluating the model’s sensitivity to specific data subsets. If performance metrics vary significantly across these test sets, it suggests that the model may not generalize well to real-world scenarios.

The Shapiro-Wilk test was first applied to assess the normality of the differences in false positive rates between the proposed language-based phishing detection with a tailored training dataset and a state-of-the-art baseline. The null hypothesis that the data follows a normal distribution was tested. A p-value greater than 0.05 failed to reject the null hypothesis, suggesting data normality. A paired t-test was then conducted to determine whether the differences in false positive rate between the two models are statistically significant. The paired t-test assumes normality in the differences between paired observations, which was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The null hypothesis for the paired t-test states that the mean difference in false positive rates between the two models is zero, indicating no significant difference in performance. The test was applied to the differences in false positive rates across the 10 test sets for each country. A p-value less than 0.05 for the paired t-test rejected the null hypothesis, suggesting that the observed differences in performance are statistically significant.

Discussion

This section discusses the limitations of the work and its broader implications. While the model demonstrates advancements in accuracy and false positive reduction across multiple minor languages, it is important to address potential threats and outline future research directions.

Adversarial attacks

Several threats to the language-based phishing detection model exist. First, the model is based on Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency for feature extraction, which has potential vulnerabilities. TF-IDF is susceptible to adversarial attacks, where attackers may manipulate URL terms to evade detection. For example, attackers may use terms from other minor languages or obfuscate malicious intent by embedding benign-looking terms. This limitation is particularly concerning in multilingual contexts, where the model may struggle to generalize across diverse linguistic patterns23,24. Second, the static nature of pre-trained models means they cannot adapt to new adversarial patterns, making them vulnerable to evolving attack strategies.

Time complexity

The time complexity of the MultinomialNB model, with features extracted using Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency, is primarily influenced by the dimensionality of the feature space and the number of training samples 25. The TF-IDF transformation process has a time complexity of , where is the number of documents and is the number of unique terms in the corpus. The MultinomialNB algorithm operates with a time complexity of during training, where is the number of classes (binary phishing or not phishing) and is the number of features. This linear scalability with respect to the number of features and samples makes MultinomialNB particularly suitable for large-scale phishing detection tasks, where computational efficiency is critical. However, the high dimensionality of the TF-IDF feature space can pose challenges, particularly in memory usage.

Known limitations

This work primarily focuses on European countries where a minor language is predominantly spoken. As a result, the proposed model may be limited by the dataset’s scope. Although the model was tested on 12 European countries with minor languages, its performance in non-European contexts or languages with different scripts, such as Cyrillic, Arabic, or Chinese, remains unverified. Additionally, the dataset’s reliance on Common Crawl data, while comprehensive, may not fully capture the linguistic diversity. Furthermore, the model’s effectiveness in detecting adversarial phishing attacks, which often use localized content to deceive specific targets, requires further investigation.

Future directions

Future work will focus on incorporating multilingual datasets and adversarial training techniques to enhance the model’s robustness and adaptability. It would be valuable to test whether state-of-the-art phishing detection methods perform well on webpages in the Cyrillic script, used in Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Mongolian, and Kazakh languages. Other minor scripts worth investigating include Greek, Georgian, Armenian, and Hebrew. This future research requires large datasets of local webpages from the respective countries. Such webpages can be compiled using language detection methods. It is also possible to combine machine learning with deep learning pattern recognition to improve phishing detection accuracy 26. The hybrid approach can use machine learning for real-time detection and deep learning, such as BERT and LSTMs, for post-efficiency validation.

Conclusion

The language-based phishing detection significantly reduces false positives, local benign webpages that are incorrectly identified as phishing, by focusing on the linguistic features of minor languages spoken in 12 European countries. The results demonstrate that the model achieves an accuracy of 99 % in most countries, with a notable reduction in false positive rates by up to a factor of 10. For instance, in Estonia, the false positive rate was reduced from 0.063 to 0.006, while in Latvia and Slovenia, the false positive rate decreased from 0.039 to 0.004 and 0.025 to 0.003, respectively. These improvements reflect the model’s ability to accurately identify benign webpages in minor languages, which are often misclassified by the state-of-the-art solutions.

The robustness of the model was validated using real-world test sets. Statistical analysis confirmed the significance of the improvements. The Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed the normal distribution of the results, with p-values consistently greater than 0.05 in each country. Paired T-tests demonstrated that the improvements in false positive rates were statistically significant, with p-values below 0.05 in each country. For example, in Estonia, the language-based model achieved false positive rates ranging between 0.004 and 0.009 across validation folds, while in Latvia, the rates remained consistently low, with a narrow range from 0.001 to 0.003. In Lithuania and Slovenia, the model maintained narrow ranges of 0.003 to 0.005 and 0.002 to 0.005, respectively. This consistency across different subsets of test data demonstrates the model’s applicability in real-world scenarios.

To promote reproducibility and further research, the source code, datasets, and raw results used in this work were made publicly available. The custom-compiled datasets comprise approximately two million local webpages from 16 countries, including reference data from English-speaking and major-language-speaking countries such as the United Kingdom, Germany, France, and Spain. The phishing dataset was sourced from a publicly available repository, while the benign datasets were compiled from Common Crawl data, ensuring diversity. The provided source code, raw results, including classification reports, confusion matrices, and improved detection samples, allow for validation of the findings.

Countries with minor languages have historically been neglected in phishing detection research. By addressing this challenge, this work contributes to more inclusive cybersecurity, ensuring that all users, regardless of their language, are adequately protected. Future research directions include extending the model to non-European languages with different scripts (Cyrillic, Arabic, or Chinese), investigating adversarial attacks, and exploring hybrid approaches that combine the model with deep learning techniques.
