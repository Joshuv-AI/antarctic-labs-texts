Prenatal sex discordance revealing SRY-positive 46,XX testicular disorder of sex development
Authors: Sebastian Tobia-Gonzalez, Natalie Barganski, Melissa Miller, Alicia Renck, Leon Smith-Harrison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract46,XX testicular disorder of sex development (DSD) is a rare condition usually diagnosed during adolescence or adulthood due to infertility or hypogonadism. Early diagnosis in phenotypically normal infants is uncommon. We report a male infant identified following discordant prenatal sex assessment, with noninvasive prenatal testing suggesting a female fetus and prenatal ultrasonography demonstrating male genitalia. Postnatal chromosomal analysis revealed 46,X,add(X) (p22.3) with SRY positivity. Physical examination showed normal male external genitalia with bilaterally descended testes and no genital anomalies. After two years of multidisciplinary follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable without gonadal abnormalities.Keywords: 46,XX testicular disorder of sex development; De la Chapelle syndrome; SRY-Positive XX male; Prenatal sex discordance

Introduction

46,XX testicular disorder of sex development (DSD), also known as De la Chapelle syndrome, is a rare condition characterized by the presence of a male phenotype in individuals with a 46,XX karyotype.1 The estimated incidence is approximately 1 in 20,000–25,000 male births.1,2 In most cases, the condition results from translocation of the sex-determining region Y (SRY) gene from the Y chromosome onto the X chromosome during paternal meiosis, leading to testicular differentiation despite the absence of a normal Y chromosome.2,3

The phenotypic spectrum of 46,XX testicular DSD is variable and depends on the amount of translocated Y chromosomal material and the presence of genes involved in testicular development and masculinization.1 Most affected individuals are diagnosed during adolescence or adulthood during evaluation for infertility, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, delayed puberty, gynecomastia, or small testes.4,5 Pediatric presentation is less common and is frequently associated with genital anomalies such as hypospadias, cryptorchidism, micropenis, or ambiguous genitalia.1,4

Early diagnosis of 46,XX testicular DSD during infancy is uncommon, particularly in patients with completely normal male external genitalia. Advances in prenatal genetic screening have increased recognition of discordance between chromosomal sex and fetal phenotypic sex, prompting earlier postnatal genetic evaluation in selected patients. Such early diagnosis is clinically relevant because affected individuals may later develop gonadal dysfunction, infertility, delayed puberty, or endocrine abnormalities.5,6

We present the case of an SRY-positive 46,XX phenotypically normal male infant identified following discordant prenatal sex assessment, with normal male external genitalia, bilaterally descended testes, and longitudinal multidisciplinary follow-up.

Case presentation

A male infant was referred for genetic evaluation at 2 months of age following discordant prenatal sex assessment. Noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) was consistent with a female fetus, while prenatal ultrasonography demonstrated male external genitalia. The patient was born at term following an uncomplicated pregnancy and delivery. There was no family history of disorders of sex development, infertility, or known chromosomal abnormalities.

Initial postnatal physical examination demonstrated normal male external genitalia with bilaterally descended testes. No hypospadias, micropenis, chordee, or ambiguous genitalia were identified. Growth parameters and neonatal adaptation were otherwise unremarkable.

Chromosomal analysis demonstrated a karyotype of 46,X,add(X) (p22.3), consistent with translocation of Y chromosome material onto one of the X chromosomes. Subsequent molecular genetic evaluation confirmed the presence of the SRY gene, supporting the diagnosis of SRY-positive 46,XX testicular disorder of sex development.

The patient underwent multidisciplinary follow-up with genetics, pediatric endocrinology, nephrology, and pediatric urology. Renal ultrasonography initially demonstrated mild bilateral pelviectasis, which resolved on subsequent imaging. Follow-up renal ultrasound at 2 years of age demonstrated normal kidneys and bladder.

At follow-up evaluation at 2 years and 6 months of age, the patient remained phenotypically male with no evidence of genital abnormalities. Both testes remained descended bilaterally. No testicular masses or signs of gonadal dysfunction were identified. The patient wore corrective lenses for astigmatism and was receiving speech and occupational therapy for mild developmental delay. Growth was appropriate, and no endocrine abnormalities had been identified at that time.

Genetic counseling was provided to the family regarding the underlying chromosomal abnormality, future fertility implications, and the potential risk of gonadal dysfunction. The family was informed that infertility due to azoospermia is commonly associated with this condition because genes required for spermatogenesis are typically absent. Long-term follow-up with endocrinology and pediatric urology was recommended to monitor pubertal development, gonadal function, and testicular health.

Discussion

46,XX testicular disorder of sex development (DSD), also known as De la Chapelle syndrome, is a rare condition with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 20,000–25,000 male births.1,2 Approximately 80–90% of affected individuals are SRY-positive due to translocation of the sex-determining region Y (SRY) gene from the Y chromosome onto the X chromosome during paternal meiosis.2,3 Presence of the SRY gene initiates testicular differentiation despite the absence of a normal Y chromosome.3

The clinical phenotype of 46,XX testicular DSD is variable and largely depends on the amount of translocated Y chromosomal material and the presence or absence of additional genes involved in testicular development and masculinization.1 Most reported patients present later in life with infertility, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, gynecomastia, delayed puberty, or small testes.4,5 Pediatric cases are uncommon and are frequently associated with genital anomalies such as hypospadias, cryptorchidism, micropenis, or ambiguous genitalia.1,4.

Early diagnosis of 46,XX testicular DSD during infancy remains uncommon. Most reported patients are diagnosed during adolescence or adulthood during evaluation for infertility, delayed puberty, gynecomastia, or hypogonadism.4,5 In contrast, pediatric diagnosis is frequently associated with genital ambiguity or other congenital genital anomalies prompting genetic investigation.1,4 The present case is unusual because diagnosis occurred during early infancy despite a normal male phenotype and absence of hypospadias or cryptorchidism. In this patient, prenatal discordance between noninvasive prenatal testing and fetal ultrasonographic sex assessment was the key factor leading to early chromosomal evaluation and diagnosis. This highlights the evolving role of prenatal genetic screening in facilitating recognition of rare disorders of sex development before the onset of endocrine or reproductive manifestations.

The present case is further notable because the patient demonstrated a completely male phenotype without hypospadias, cryptorchidism, or ambiguous genitalia. Bilaterally descended testes and normal external genitalia are less commonly emphasized in pediatric reports of 46,XX testicular DSD.1,4 The absence of significant genital anomalies likely reflects adequate expression of testis-determining genes contained within the translocated Y chromosomal material, including SRY.3

Early diagnosis in infancy allowed multidisciplinary follow-up involving genetics, endocrinology, nephrology, and pediatric urology. Long-term surveillance in these patients is important because gonadal dysfunction, delayed puberty, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, and infertility may become apparent later in life.5,6 Most affected individuals are infertile due to azoospermia resulting from absence of azoospermia factor (AZF) regions normally located on the Y chromosome and required for spermatogenesis.5,6.

Although the true risk of gonadal neoplasia in SRY-positive 46,XX testicular DSD remains uncertain, it appears lower than in other forms of gonadal dysgenesis associated with undescended gonads or ambiguous genitalia. Nevertheless, regular clinical follow-up and patient education regarding testicular surveillance are generally recommended.4, 5, 6, 7 In the present case, no gonadal abnormalities were identified during follow-up.

This case highlights the importance of considering disorders of sex development in the setting of prenatal sex discordance, even in patients with normal male genitalia. Early recognition allows appropriate counseling, coordinated multidisciplinary care, and long-term surveillance regarding endocrine function, pubertal development, fertility potential, and gonadal health.

Conclusion

SRY-positive 46,XX testicular disorder of sex development is a rare condition with a broad phenotypic spectrum. This case demonstrates that affected patients may present during infancy with completely normal male external genitalia and without classic findings such as hypospadias or cryptorchidism. Prenatal discordance between genetic screening and fetal phenotypic sex may provide an important early clue leading to diagnosis.

Early recognition of this condition allows appropriate multidisciplinary follow-up, parental counseling, and long-term surveillance regarding pubertal development, endocrine function, fertility potential, and gonadal health. This report contributes to the limited pediatric literature describing early diagnosis and longitudinal follow-up of phenotypically normal children with SRY-positive 46,XX testicular disorder of sex development.
