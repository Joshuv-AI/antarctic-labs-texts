Homovanillic acid improves anxiety by regulating F4/80+ microglia/macrophage in adult mice with neonatal cardiac injury
Authors: Zhenfei Wu, Zujuan Huang, Fengpei Ding, Hongbo Ma, Chuntao Deng, Changchun Zeng, Rong Chang, Wenbin Kuang, Yanli Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe heart-brain axis is a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality following ischemic injury. Pathological and psychological changes in the heart can lead to anxiety, cognitive impairment, and brain injury. Cardiac injury releases a substantial number of metabolites into the peripheral circulation, which exacerbates brain damage and dysfunction. Homovanillic acid (HVA), a metabolite of dopamine (DA), plays a crucial role in behavioral symptoms. However, there are limited studies examining the effects of cardiac metabolites released from an injured heart on behavioral symptoms in human juveniles, particularly in those who have suffered from severe cardiovascular diseases since childhood in human models. To study the potential role of injured cardiac metabolites on human juveniles’ behaviors, we use neonatal mouse heart cryoinjury to verify the mechanisms of heart-brain interactions in our study. Here, downregulated cardiac heart HVA is enriched in neonatal mouse injured heart at day 7 after injury, and meanwhile HVA treatment changes the physiology of the body such as heart weight/body weight, brain weight/body weight and thymus weight/body weight in mouse juveniles with chronic mouse cardiac injury. Additionally, HVA administration mediates anxiety-like behavior in the same group. Microglia/macrophages contribute to brain development and functions, potentially influencing on human behaviors, which is associates with MCP-1 expression. Mechanistically, we identifies the number of F4/80+ microglia/macrophage in adult mice with neonatal cardiac injury increases in HVA treatment, while there is no difference in mRNA and protein expression levels of MCP-1 at day 42 after injury. Thus, HVA may be a potential target in clinical behavioral symptoms with cardiovascular diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43510-y.Keywords: Homovanillic acid, F4/80+ microglia/macrophage, Neonatal cardiac injury, Behavioral symptomsSubject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Medical research, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases, including congenital heart disease, atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction (MI), cardiac hypertrophy, and heart failure, are a leading cause of death. Accumulating studies indicate that cardiac metabolism undergoes significant changes during cardiac remodeling after injury1–3. In failing hearts, there is a high consumption of fatty acids, ketones, and lactate, which play a protective role, while glucose intake is notably low4. Additionally, various metabolites influence brain function and mental health, including homovanillic acid5, S-Adenosyl-L-homocysteine6, ornithine7, galactose8, isovaleric acid9 and decanoyl-L-carnitine10. Further data shows that HVA could cross blood–brain barrier (BBB) to regulate central nervous system, which improves depressive-like behaviors in mouse models5. Although the evidence supporting the brain–heart axis is well documented, the metabolic interactions between the brain and heart following cardiac injury still remain unclear.

Mounting studies demonstrate that pathological and physiological changes in the heart contribute to depression5, cognitive impairment11,12, and brain injury13. For instance, optically evoked tachycardia induces anxiety-like behavior under risky conditions, suggesting that interactions between the brain and the heart participate in the states of emotional control14. Brain damage is considered one of common complications following Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) treatment, promoting cognitive impairment in children after congenital heart disease (CHD) surgery11,12. A recent study also suggests that early adolescents with complex CHD exhibit higher levels of stress markers and poorer executive function after open-heart surgery compared with control group15. Additionally, brain injury is a result of cardiopulmonary resuscitation13. Regarding the mechanisms of the cardiac-brain axis in the context of brain injury, energy metabolism disorders16, oxidative/nitrosative stress17, and inflammatory responses18 play a vital role in brain damage.

Cardiac injury releases a significant number of metabolites in peripheral and circulation contents, which exacerbates brain damage and function19. In contexts of cardiac arrest, the cessation of oxygen delivery and cardiac output leads to increased consumption of the high-energy substrate adenosine triphosphate (ATP), disrupting in Na + /K + ion exchange20. Primary injury increases the level of intracellular Ca + + , resulting in the production of glutamate. Because of metabolic changes of body after injury, there is an enhanced influx of intracellular Ca + + and cytoplasmic accumulation. Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction contributes to cellular energy failure, the generation of reactive oxygen species and the activation of pro-apoptotic proteins in Ca + + dependent manner, which accelerates neuronal damage19.Heart failure further contributes to intracellular Ca + + leakage by regulating ryanodine receptor type 2, resulting in cognitive dysfunction21. Also, S-adenosyl-L-homocysteine (SAH) suppresses autophagy22. Galactonic acid has been shown to induce brain damage and cognitive decline23,24. The expression of isovaleric acid increases in the early stages of stroke, and decanoylcarnitine is positively associated with the rate of recurrent stroke in patients9,10. These findings demonstrate that the changes in metabolites contribute to brain damage and behavioral symptoms. However, there is limited information identifying functions of metabolites released from the injured heart and their relationship to behavioral symptoms, especially in those with severe cardiovascular diseases from childhood.

Inflammation has been a central role of tissue injury and repair by regulating pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines25,26. High-fat/sugar diets contribute to cardiac injury and brain damage27,28 and meanwhile interactions between immune cells and metabolism are well investigated in tissue injury and repair29. A recent study illustrates that the number of monocytes increases in the brain following myocardial infarction (MI), which consequently enhances sleep in both human and murine models, benefiting healing and suppressing inflammation30. For instance, fatty acids promote macrophage activation via TLR4 activation31. In a MI mouse model, macrophages enhance glucose oxidation, while glycolysis decreases in CCR2 knockout mice32. Interestingly, these metabolites serve as vital regulators of brain function following injury. The levels of lactate are upregulated in the hippocampus of genetic AD model mice, and further data indicates that microglia promotes microglia dysfunction by enhancing the glycolysis/H4K12la/PKM2 pathway in this model33. Rosiglitazone upregulates fatty acid levels and “XOPHOS/FAO,” while alleviating lipid droplet accumulation and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in microglia. Also, there is an increase of pro-remyelination factors in microglia in rosiglitazone treatment34.

Homovanillic acid (HVA), a metabolite of dopamine (DA), plays a significant role in both brain and heart function. The level of HVA is elevated in the serum of patients with heart failure, as determined by the LC–MS/MS method35. A previous study indicates that mental stress contributes to left ventricular dysfunction, with further metabolic data revealing that HVA expression is 10% higher in this group compared to those with a normal left ventricular response36. Additionally, the plasma level of HVA is increased in older patients with cardiac diseases prior to cardiac surgery, which is associated with the occurrence of delirium37. Gut bacteria derived homovanillic acid inhibits LC3 degradation and SQSTM1/p62 proteins, which alleviate depression by regulating synaptic integrity5. HVA expression is associated with higher the condition-independent state association (CSA) score which is a score for the association of a given metabolite with the atherogenic states38. Although HVA is recognized as a biomarker in ischemic stroke, there are few studies exploring the relationship between HVA and cardiovascular diseases in neonates and children39. Furthermore, no significant difference in HVA expression has been observed between the sham group and MI group in an adult rat model40. Therefore, understanding the metabolites associated with heart injury in children and their impact on brain function offers novel insights into their behavior during adolescence.

Microglia/macrophages are regards as a dominant regulator of central nervous system diseases41, participating in neurodegeneration42, neuroinflammation43, neuropathic pain44, depression45, and anxiety46. In the context of brain inflammation and neurodegeneration, formyl peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) is significantly increased in both microglia/macrophages of central nervous system in a multiple sclerosis model, promoting the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and TNF-α from activated microglia and macrophages42. Targeting FPR1 reduces autoimmune reactions and suppresses microglia-orchestrated axonal degeneration42.With regard to mental diseases, modulation of microglia polarization towards the M2 phenotype alleviates depressive and anxiety-like behaviors by IFN-β treatment in an Alzheimer’s disease rat model46. However, there is little evidence whether HVA induces microglia/macrophage changes in the brain.

To explore whether metabolites released from neonatal injured hearts pose a threat to juvenile behavior at later developmental stages, we used neonatal mouse heart cryoinjury, LC–MS analysis, and the open-field test to investigate the mechanisms underlying heart–brain interactions. In the present study, our metabolic data indicated that the level of HVA in the injured neonatal heart was significant reduced in P8 CI group at day 7 post-cryoinjury compared with sham group. Although the ratios of heart weight/ body weight (HW/BW) and brain weight/body weight (Brain W/BW) significantly increased in the P8 CI PBS group compared with sham group, there were no differences in cardiac fibrosis, HW/BW, spleen weight /body weight (SW/BW) and Brain W/BW in P8 CI HVA group compared with P8 CI PBS group following cardiac injury. However, thymus weight /body weight (TW/BW) was remarkably lower in the P8 CI HVA group. Additionally, fluorescence intensity of LC3B of the injured heart in HVA treatment was no different with PBS treatment in the P8 CI group at day 42 after heart injury. More interestingly, in the open field testing, travelled total distance of HVA treated P8 CI group was greater than that of sham or PBS treated P8 CI group at 6 weeks post-injury. Pro- and anti- inflammatory markers of HVA treated P8 CI group reduced in hippocampus zone tissue at day 42 after CI. Finally, our data suggested the number of F4/80 + microglia/macrophages increased in the HVA-treated P8 CI group compared with PBS-treated P8 CI group. These data showed HVA treatment could influence behavioral outcomes in adolescents with neonatal cardiac injury, which may associate with regulating F4/80 + microglia/macrophages in the brain.

Materials and methods

Participants

With the patient’s consent, we investigated Pittsburgh sleep quality index (PSQI) from hospitalized patients with MI diseases at first week and showed the basic demographic and clinical features of study patients in supplemental Table 1. This investigation was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shenzhen Longhua District Central Hospital in accordance with World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki (WMA) (Ethical Application Ref: AF/SC-08/01.1/2023–020-01). All participants provided informed consent before the study began.

The investigation was conducted in accordance with national guidelines and the principles of Declaration of Helsinki, informed consent was obtained from all participants and all written informed consent of the subjects have been included in the Ethics approval.

Neonatal mouse heart cryoinjury (CI)

In this animal study, neonatal ICR mice (P8) weighing 5.3 ± 0.5 g and adult ICR mice (Age: 6w, Male and female, Weight: 28 ± 2 g) were sourced from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Foshan, Guangdong, China). CI was performed as described in previous study47. Briefly, P8 neonatal mice were subjected to hypothermic anesthesia for 4 min. An incision (< 1 cm) was made along the sternum and vertical of the chest muscles under a stereo microscope. After exposing the left ventricle (LV), blunt port copper wire (Area: 1 mm2) was frozen in liquid nitrogen and then placed on the LV (maintained time: ~ 5 s), an area of white frostbite appeared in the LV. After injury, the chest and skin were sewn up with sutures (5–0 and 7–0). Neonatal mice in the sham group were treated as described above, except that 1xPBS buffer was used instead of liquid nitrogen. After surgery, the neonatal mice were kept under a heat lamp and returned to their mother’s cage after recovery. The mice were then held in small standard laboratory cages and maintained under controlled temperature (25 ± 2 °C) and humidity (55 ± 10%). A 12-h light–dark cycle was maintained throughout the study period. All mice were provided with food and water. Timely reporting of illness and abnormal behavior was monitoring by trained personnel. All P8 neonatal mice were sacrificed at day 7 and day 42 following heart injury after anesthetizing with cold-ice (neonates) or 2% isoflurane (adults) in 95% oxygen and analyzed in a blinded manner.

Animal ethical statements

All animals were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Guangdong Medical University in accordance with the principles of animal welfare in the Institutional Animal Care (Ethical Application Ref: GDY2402297). All procedures in mice were performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Cedars-Sinai Medical Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Accordance statement

The authors confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the full ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org)48.

Homovanillic acid treatment

Neonatal ICR mice (Age: Day 8, Male and female, Weight: 5.3 ± 0.5 g) were intraperitoneal (I.P.) injected with HVA (H1252-250MG, Sigma-Aldrich) at 50 mg/kg per mouse at 0, 1, and 2 days after surgery. Mice were sacrificed at different time points after surgery.

Metabolite detection

Heart samples were thawed on ice-bath to diminish degradation. About 10 mg of each sample was weighted and transferred to a new 1.5 mL tube. Then 10μL of water was added and the sample was homogenated with zirconium oxide beads for 3 min and 120μL of Methanol containing internal standard was added to extract the metabolites. The sample was homogenated for another 3 min and then centrifuged at 18,000 g for 20 min. The 20μL of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate and following procedures were performed on an Eppendorf epMotion Workstation (Eppendorf Inc., Humburg, Germany). 20μL of freshly prepared derivative reagents was added to each well. The plate was sealed and the derivatization was carried out at 30 °C for 60 min. After derivatization, 330μL of ice-cold 50% methanol solution was added to dilute the sample. Then the plate was stored at − 20 °C for 20 min and followed by 4000 g centrifugation at 4 °C for 30 min. 135μL of supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate with 10μL internal standards in each well. Serial dilutions of derivatized stock standards were added to the left wells. Finally the plate was sealed for LC–MS analysis. Metabolic analysis on injured heart tissues of mice were conducted using the Q300 Metabolite Assay Kit49 (Human Metabolomics Institute, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China).

Open field test

After CI surgery, neonatal ICR mice (Age: Day 8, Male and female, Weight: 5.3 ± 0.5 g) were intraperitoneal (I.P.) injected with HVA (H1252-250MG, Sigma-Aldrich) at 50 mg/kg per mouse at 0, 1, and 2 days. Neonatal ICR mice with HVA treatment or PBS treatment and sham group were performed open field test at 42 days after heart injury (n = 3–5 per group). The open field apparatus for mice measures 50 cm × 50 cm × 40 cm. One hour before the experiment, the mice should be placed near the experimental area. Put the mice in the center or corner of the box and let them freely explore for 10 min. The movement trajectory of the mice within 10 min is recorded by SMART 3.0 software and further analyzed by video analysis software. The total distance moved, the time spent in the center position, and the total distance moved within 10 min are recorded. Before and after the experiment, the experimental apparatus should be thoroughly wiped with 75% alcohol to prevent odors from affecting the experimental results (Shenzhen Lingfu Top Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Shenzhen, China).

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

Mouse hearts and brains were perfused and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. For heart Masson staining, fixed hearts were embedded in OCT and then frozen sections were stained with Masson’s trichrome stain kit (Meilun, Dalian, China) and immunofluorescence in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The fixed brains were dehydratedin a Dehydrator (HP300, Dakewe Biotech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and embedded in an Embedding machine (JY-BMB, Hubei, Jinyuan Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Hubei, China) and then stained with H&E staining kit, and immunohistochemistry in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Shenzhen Lingfu Top Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Shenzhen, China). The primary antibody of macrophage staining: F4/80 (CST, Cat#30,325) and LC3B (ABclonal, #A11282).The fluorescence intensity of LC3B expression was analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (NIH, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

For cDNA synthesis, total RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA with Transcript First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (Takara Bio Inc, Dalian, China) according to the instructions. For qPCR, SYBR Premix Ex Taq kit (TakaraBio Inc, Dalian, Liaoning, China) was used. Real-time PCR was performed with ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. All the primers of RT-qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The hippocampus tissue were homogenized by using ice-cold glass grinding rod and lysed for 30 min in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA). After that, taking supernates and store samples at -80℃. The protein level of MCP-1 was measured by MCP-1 ELISA kit (RK00381, ABclonal, Woburn, MA 01801), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The results were presented as mean values + standard error of mean (mean ± S.E.M), and data were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (NIH, USA). The obtained data were found to be normally distributed when analyzed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The 2 tailed Student’s t test was used to calculate statistical differences between 2 groups, while One-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s method for multiple comparisons were performed in more than 2 groups. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 are statistically significant.

Results

Different metabolic changes in non-regenerating mice after cardiac injury

Recent data demonstrate cardiac metabolism is a hallmark of cardiovascular diseases and regeneration3,50,51. Cardiac injury regulates sphingolipid metabolism dynamics in neonatal mouse model, which induces heart regeneration52. Also, early adolescents with complex CHD have higher stress markers and poorer executive function following open-heart surgery compared with control group, which is associated with cognitive impairment in children11,12,15. Thus, we performed liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS) method to study metabolic changes of injured heart in P8 CI neonatal model. A total of 167 metabolites were classified into eight classes according to LC–MS data (Fig. 1A–D). Notably, 9 metabolites (Homovanillic acid, Xylulose, Ribulose, Norcholic acid [NorCA], Cystine, S-Adenosyl-L-homocysteine [SAH], Sebacic acid, Valeric acid and Tartaric acid) of P8 injured heart were a predominate decrease at day 7 after CI compared with sham group, while 7 metabolites (Propionic acid, Ornithine, Galactonic acid, Isovaleric acid, Threonine, Decanoylcarnitine, and Adipoylcarnitine) of that group were a significant increase (Fig. 1D). Many pathways in selected pathway-associated metabolites sets (SMPDB) were differentially enriched in P8 CI group compared with sham group and enriched compounds have been shown in each metabolite-associated pathway at day 7 after CI (Fig. 1E). More interestingly, SAH mostly participated in different metabolic pathways and HVA is only enriched in tyrosine metabolism (Fig. 1F).The regulatory mechanisms between tyrosine metabolism and heart-cerebrovascular diseases have been well investigated53,54 and meanwhile HVA was considered as a key regulator of depressive-like behaviors5. Furthermore, LC–MS data suggested that HVA was downregulated in the injured heart at day 7 compared with sham group (Fig. 1G).Our further data indicated that HVA was directly associated with SAH and oxoglutaric acid by using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 platform , which were downregulated accompanying with the reduction of HVA in LC–MS data (Fig. 1H). Notably, other metabolites, including threonine, isovaleric acid, ornithine, propionic acid, and galactonic acid, which were indirectly related to HVA, remarkably increased in P8 CI group at day 7 after injury compared with sham group. Thus, these data suggest that neonatal heart injury alters cardiac HVA content, providing an insight into cardiac repair or development following injury.

Homovanillic acid reduces cardiac hypertrophy and thymus weight after cardiac injury

To further investigate the potential role of HVA on cardiac function after injury, we performed CI injury in HVA-treated P8 group and PBS-treated P8 group, respectively. Our data showed no difference in the percentage of cardiac fibrosis between the HVA-treated P8 group and the PBS-treated P8 group at day 42 post-CI (Fig. 2A–C). Neonatal CI induced cardiac hypertrophy compared with sham group, and meanwhile the result of the brain weight/ body weight (brain/BW) was similar to the outcome of heart weight/ body weight (HW/BW), which was an increase in PBS treated P8 group following heart injury (Fig. 2D, E). However, there was no significant difference of HW/BW, brain/BW and spleen weight/body weight (SW/BW) in HVA-treated P8 CI group compared with sham or PBS-treated P8 CI group (Fig. 2D, E, G). Notably, we found that TW/BW of HVA group significantly reduced compared with PBS-treated P8 CI group (Fig. 2F). On the other hand, HVA treatment inhibited autophagy by reducing LC3 II expression in brain5. Thus, we performed immunofluorescence method to identify LC3B expression in both HVA and PBS treated P8 heart at 42 days after CI. The data demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity of LC3B expression in HVA treatment was no different with PBS treatment in P8 CI group at day 42 after heart injury (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, to study the role of HVA on inflammation after cardiac injury, we performed RT-qPCR to detect the pro- and anti-inflammatory markers in the injured heart at day 42, our data illustrated that mRNA expression levels of MCP-1, IL-6 and TNF-α were no remarkable difference between PBS-treated and HVA-treated P8 CI group. Additionally, HVA treatment did not change the levels of IL-10, although there was a decrease in the mRNA level of TGF-β (Fig. 2I). Thus, these data illustrated that HVA treatment could inhibit cardiac hypertrophy but no influence on inflammation following cardiac injury.

Homovanillic acid improves anxiety in adult mice with neonatal cardiac injury

As a previous study indicated, HVA treatment improves depression in adult mouse models with chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) and corticosterone (CORT)-induced depression5. In addition, myocardial injury (MI) has been shown to enhance sleep, thereby inhibiting cardiac damage and inflammatory responses30. Consequently, we first examined patients with MI to identify whether MI affects sleep quality in a clinical setting. Our assessment using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) revealed that patients (n = 15) with MI exhibited better sleep quality, as indicated by a lower PSQI score compared with normal (n = 14) (Fig. 3A). Thus, we investigated whether HVA treatment would improve the behaviors of adolescents with chronic cardiovascular diseases from childhood. The outcomes of open field test (OFT) suggested that the mean speed in center areas would reduce in both PBS and HVA P8 CI mice at day 42 after cardiac injury, although no significant difference was observed compared with sham group (Fig. 3B–D). Notably, the total distance traveled by HVA P8 CI mice significantly increased compared with sham group, and distance in center zones (also as a percentage) of HVA treatment was markedly greater than that of the sham or PBS P8 CI mice, and the time spent in the center zone (also as a percentage) for the HVA treatment was longer than that of the other two groups (Fig. 3E–G). Although the PBS-treated group did not exhibit marked anxiety-like behavior in the OFT compared with the sham group, HVA treatment enhanced total distance, distance in the center zone, and time spent in the center zone following cardiac injury. These indicators suggested that HVA treatment could reduce the likelihood of developing anxiety-like behavior in cardiac injury models.

The changes of inflammatory markers and F4/80 positive cells in adult brain with chronic cardiac injury after HVA treatment

Inflammation is a hallmark of tissue repair and regeneration25, and meanwhile behavioral symptoms such as sleep, could alleviate cardiac damage and inflammatory responses30. Thus, we performed RT-qPCR to testify the dynamic changes in inflammatory markers in HVA- and PBS-treated brain group at day 42 after neonatal heart injury. Our data indicated that pro-inflammatory markers of brain tissue in the HVA P8 CI group, such as MCP-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, were reduced compared with PBS P8 CI group at day 42 post-injury, although no significant changes were observed between the two groups (Fig. 4A, B). We found no differences in brain in the protein levels of MCP-1 among the sham, PBS, and HVA groups (Fig. 4C). Additionally, mounting evidence indicated that the percentage of macrophages reduced in MI group at day 7 but increased at day 3 after injury compared with sham group30. As a result, we identified whether HVA treatment influenced F4/80 + microglia/macrophage cells as well. Our immunohistochemistry (IHC) data confirmed that the number of F4/80 + microglia/macrophage cells showed no significant difference in the brain borders of mice among the sham, PBS, and HVA groups. Of note, HVA treatment mostly increased the number of F4/80 + positive cells in hippocampus zones at day 42 following neonatal cardiac injury compared with PBS treated P8 CI groups and had no significant difference with sham group (Fig. 4D–G). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that HVA treatment may modulate the number of F4/80 + microglia/macrophage cells, which in turn improved the behavioral symptoms of the animals.

Discussion

Homovanillic acid (HVA) plays a central role in behavioral symptoms, which improves depression in animal depressive models5. Additionally, HVA serves as a biomarker for Parkinson’s disease (PD) and schizophrenia55,56. A substantial body of references considered that metabolic regulation is crucial for tissue repair and regeneration. For instance, sphingosine kinase 2 (SphK2) is essential for sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) biosynthesis in sphingolipid metabolism, leading to cardiomyocyte proliferation and heart repair following myocardial injury52. One-carbon metabolism regulates neuronal regeneration through O-GlcNAc signaling in Caenorhabditis elegans57. However, there is limited research on the potential role of cardiac metabolites in the behaviors of juveniles with chronic cardiovascular diseases originating in childhood. Our present study demonstrated that cardiac injury resulted in a decrease of HVA in the injured heart, which was associated with depressive-like behaviors5. Furthermore, HVA was significantly enriched in tyrosine metabolism. A previous investigation indicated that L-tyrosine was related to oxoglutaric acid (also known as α-ketoglutarate) within metabolic pathways58. α-Ketoglutarate contributed to skin repair and regeneration, promoting wound healing59. Additionally, α-ketoglutarate alleviated inflammatory responses and enhanced M2 macrophage polarization as well as the proliferation of regulatory T cells60. Although S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) is not directly associated with HVA, previous studies have shown that SAH, a METTL3 inhibitor61, reduced levels of TNF-α and TAB3, preventing renal injury and inflammation62. Notably, our metabolic data illustrated that there was a decrease of α-ketoglutarate and SAH in P8 CI group following injury. Although LC–MS analyzed significant metabolic alterations between sham-operated and neonatal heart-injured mice, sex-specific differences in the cardiac injury did not show in our neonatal heart injury model. Thus, further studies should be investigated sex-specific metabolites between male and female neonatal mice after heart injury.

Our present study points to HVA as a key mediator of behavior that has no influence cardiac repair following injury. Upregulated HVA levels have been observed in patients with cardiac diseases37. Although injury resulted in a reduction of HVA, HVA supplementation did not improve cardiac fibrosis in adult mice with neonatal cardiac injury. Importantly, HVA treatment decreased the ratio of heart and brain weight to body weight at day 42 following P8 cardiac injury. A previous data demonstrated that HVA recovered Synapsin1 (SYN1) levels in depressive-like behavior mouse models5, and the palmitoylation and phosphorylation of SYN1 levels was associated with Ca2+ signaling pathways63. Ca2⁺/ CaMKII signaling plays a vital role in the progress of cardiac hypertrophy64. These studies suggested that HVA may regulate cardiac hypertrophy through SYN1/Ca2+/CaMKII signaling pathway. Additionally, there was no significant difference in pro- and anti-inflammatory markers in the injured heart areas between the HVA-treated and PBS-treated P8 cardiac injury groups. However, the ratio of TW/BW significantly reduced in HVA group, suggesting a potential alteration in the inflammatory response. Notably, HVA treatment resulted in a substantial improvement in behavioral symptoms, alleviating anxiety in adult mice with chronic cardiac injury, which was consistent with a previous study5.

Interestingly, HVA prevents LC3 degradation and SQSTM1/p62 proteins, which alleviate depression through regulation of synaptic integrity5, and meanwhile recruitment of monocytes increased in the brain following cardiac injury to enhance sleep, inhibiting cardiac damage and inflammatory responses30.However, in our neonatal cardiac injury model, HVA did not alter LC3B expression at day 42 following injury in border zones of injured heart tissues. A previous demonstrated that cardiac injury induced high-mobility group protein box 1 (HMGB1), resulting in an increasing expression of TLR4 and NF-κB to inhibit autophagic activity65. Thus, in terms of cardiac injury, activated inflammatory responses may be a dominate mediator in autophagic activity following heart injury, compared to HVA -regulated autophagy pathway. Although there was no significant difference of inflammatory markers in heart tissues among three groups, HVA treatment may regulate pro-inflammatory markers of brain. The results indicated that HVA may be a dominant mediator in brain rather than heart tissue, which was consistent with previous studies5.

Mounting studies demonstrated that HVA did not directly regulate dopamine metabolism, although the HVA/ dopamine ratio was widely used to assess the functional status of dopaminergic systems5,66–68. HVA considered as a product of dopamine metabolic pathway, and HVA levels increased, suggesting that central dopamine metabolism is recover to a more active state. By contrast, a potential role of HVA regulating dopamine expression or metabolism is little investigated. Additionally, our LC–MS data did not show whether HVA level is a positive or negative correction with dopamine levels. Thus, we identified the effect of HVA treatment on inflammatory responses in brain after heart injury due to our LC–MS data that downregulated HVA was detected in the injured heart area. After cardiac injury, there was no significant difference in pro-inflammatory markers in injured heart tissues between the HVA-treated group and the PBS-treated group, suggesting that HVA may not directly inhibit inflammation or may have effects on the injured heart following acute cardiac injury. Therefore, different cardiac injury models and time-points should be further investigated in HVA treatment. Moreover, our data indicated that the number of F4/80 + microglia/macrophages increased in the adult brain following neonatal cardiac injury after HVA treatment, although the protein level of MCP-1 in the brain was no difference in injured groups at day 42 post-injury. Taken together, these findings suggested that while HVA may not be associated with cardiac inflammation after injury, HVA treatment could improve anxiety in animals by regulating F4/80 + microglia/macrophages.
