EpCAM-targeted ZIF-8 nanocarriers for enhanced doxorubicin delivery in cancer therapy: a promising approach for tumor-specific drug release
Authors: Mojtaba Tarin, Yazdan Hasani-Nourian, Hosein Khoshsafar, Hasan Bagheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the development of a targeted drug delivery system utilizing zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8)-based nanocarriers functionalized with Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM) aptamers to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of Doxorubicin (DOX) against EpCAM-positive cancer cells. The inherent properties of ZIF-8, including its high surface area, structural stability, and pH-responsive degradation, make it an ideal vehicle for controlled drug release. The encapsulation of Doxorubicin within ZIF-8 nanocarriers, combined with surface modification using aptamers, is designed to direct DOX delivery specifically to tumor cells, thereby reducing off-target cytotoxicity. Our findings demonstrate that EpCAM-targeted, PEGylated ZIF-8 nanocarriers significantly improve the therapeutic efficacy of DOX while minimizing toxicity in non-cancerous cells. Furthermore, in vivo studies confirmed superior tumor suppression accompanied by reduced systemic toxicity. This research underscores the potential of EpCAM-modified ZIF-8 nanocarriers as a highly promising and innovative strategy for targeted cancer therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42521-z.

Introduction

Despite significant progress in oncology, cancer remains a primary global health challenge, representing one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide1–4. Current therapeutic strategies, while having evolved, are still fraught with substantial limitations5–8. Chemotherapy, a cornerstone of cancer treatment, often fails to achieve desired clinical outcomes due to its non-specific biodistribution, leading to severe systemic side effects that compromise therapeutic benefits5,9–13. Doxorubicin (DOX), a potent and widely used chemotherapeutic agent, exemplifies this challenge; its powerful anti-tumor activity is frequently overshadowed by a lack of selectivity, resulting in dose-limiting adverse effects such as cardiotoxicity and myelosuppression14–17. Consequently, there is an urgent and unmet need for advanced drug delivery systems that can enhance therapeutic specificity and efficacy.

In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative force, offering innovative solutions to overcome the setbacks associated with conventional cancer treatments18–21. Nanocarrier-based drug delivery systems provide distinct advantages, including improved solubility of hydrophobic drugs, extended systemic circulation, and the potential for targeted delivery to tumor sites, thereby increasing cellular internalization and therapeutic payload concentration where it is most needed22–24. Among the diverse classes of nanocarriers, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have attracted considerable attention owing to their exceptional properties, such as vast surface areas, tunable structures, and high drug encapsulation capacities25–29. These attributes make MOFs an exemplary platform for designing next-generation drug delivery systems.

A particularly promising subclass of MOFs for biomedical applications is the zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8). In addition to sharing the favorable structural characteristics of MOFs, ZIF-8 nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit remarkable chemical stability, excellent biocompatibility, and intrinsic pH-responsiveness, which is highly advantageous for cancer therapy25,26. This pH responsiveness arises from the acid-labile Zn²⁺-2-methylimidazole coordination bonds within the ZIF-8 framework; under mildly acidic conditions (e.g., tumor microenvironment and endo/lysosomal compartments) these bonds are cleaved more readily, accelerating framework degradation and DOX release. The acidic microenvironment characteristic of solid tumors triggers the degradation of the ZIF-8 framework, facilitating the on-demand release of encapsulated drugs directly at the tumor site25,26,30–35. This targeted release mechanism is critical for minimizing off-target effects and maximizing the therapeutic index of potent drugs like DOX. While ZIF-8 has been extensively studied, its application for the targeted delivery of DOX, particularly when combined with active targeting moieties, remains an area ripe for innovation and optimization32,36–44. Recent explorations have demonstrated the feasibility of multifunctional ZIF-8 nanoplatforms for co-delivering chemo-radiotherapy agents or for gene therapy, showcasing their versatility and potential for complex therapeutic applications45,46. Furthermore, the development of biomimetic systems, such as camouflaging ZIF-8 with plant-derived exosomes, points toward novel strategies to enhance safety and immune evasion47.

The primary innovation of this work is the integration of an active targeting strategy to overcome the limitations of passive delivery. We have functionalized the ZIF-8 nanocarrier surface with aptamers targeting the Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM). EpCAM is a well-validated tumor-associated antigen, overexpressed on the surface of numerous carcinomas and cancer stem cells (CSCs), making it an ideal target for cancer-specific therapies48–51. Recent studies have successfully employed EpCAM-targeting for delivering various therapeutic payloads, including CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids, siRNA, and chemotherapeutics, highlighting its efficacy in enhancing tumor regression and overcoming drug resistance52–54. Moreover, the development of dual-targeted systems and theragnostic platforms underscores the importance of precision in modern cancer therapy55,56. This study addresses the existing gap by systematically designing, fabricating, and evaluating an EpCAM aptamer-functionalized ZIF-8 nanocarrier for DOX delivery. Our novel nanocarrier is engineered to remain stable under physiological conditions while selectively targeting EpCAM-positive cancer cells, thereby ensuring a safe and effective release of DOX directly within the tumor microenvironment. We hypothesize that this targeted approach will significantly improve therapeutic outcomes and reduce the debilitating side effects associated with conventional DOX administration. Materials and methods

Materials

All chemical reagents and materials utilized in this investigation were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise specified. Key chemicals included zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O), 2-methylimidazole, Doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX), and a heterobifunctional polyethylene glycol (SH-PEG-COOH). Solvents such as ethanol (C₂H₅OH) and buffers like phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were also used. For covalent conjugation procedures, analytical-grade reagents 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich.

Preparation of ZIF-8 nanocarriers

ZIF-8 nanocarriers were synthesized through a gentle, solvothermal-based phase transformation method. In a typical procedure, 0.558 g of Zn(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O was dissolved in 15 mL of methanol to create Solution (A) Concurrently, 0.616 g of 2-methylimidazole was dissolved in 15 mL of methanol to form Solution (B) Solution A was subsequently added dropwise into Solution B under ultrasonication for 15 min at ambient temperature. The resulting white precipitate was collected via centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 10 min, washed twice with methanol, and re-dispersed in 15 mL of methanol with ultrasonication to generate Solution (C) Following this, an additional 0.558 g of zinc nitrate hexahydrate dissolved in 15 mL of methanol was added to Solution C. This final mixture was transferred into 50 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclaves and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 120 °C for 2 h. After cooling, the product was thoroughly washed with methanol via repeated centrifugation and finally dried in a vacuum oven at 30 °C.

Functionalization with EpCAM aptamers

To achieve targeted delivery, the ZIF-8 nanocarriers were functionalized with EpCAM aptamers. First, polyethylene glycol (PEG) linkers were attached to the ZIF-8 surface. A bifunctional PEG derivative (SH-PEG-COOH) was utilized, enabling the subsequent covalent attachment of the aptamer. The synthesized EpCAM aptamers were conjugated to the PEGylated ZIF-8 surface using EDC/NHS coupling chemistry. Unreacted reagents and byproducts were thoroughly removed by dialyzing the suspension against phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 24 h.

Drug loading and encapsulation efficiency

DOX was loaded into the ZIF-8 framework by dispersing the synthesized nanoparticles in an aqueous DOX solution adjusted to pH 5.5. This suspension was stirred for 12 h to facilitate the diffusion of the drug into the porous structure of the nanocarriers.

Characterization of nanocarriers

The structural and physical properties of the synthesized nanocarriers were comprehensively characterized. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to analyze the crystalline structure and phase purity of ZIF-8 before and after modification. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed to assess the thermal stability of the nanocarriers. Surface area and porosity were determined using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. Finally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to visualize the particle size, shape, and morphology.

In vitro release study

The pH-triggered release profile of DOX from the ZIF-8 nanocarriers was evaluated under conditions simulating both physiological pH (7.4) and the acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 5.5). Drug-loaded ZIF-8 particles were suspended in 2 mL of either PBS (pH 7.4) or citrate buffer (pH 5.5). The suspension was placed in a dialysis bag (molecular weight cutoff: 1000 Da) and immersed in a vessel containing 20 mL of the corresponding buffer, which was incubated at 37 °C with continuous agitation at 100 rpm. At predetermined time points (0, 2, 4, 8, 24, 48, and 72 h), 2 mL aliquots were collected from the external buffer and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh buffer to maintain sink conditions. The concentration of the released DOX in the aliquots was quantified using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 480 nm, with concentrations calculated from a standard calibration curve. This data was used to plot the cumulative release percentage over time.

Cellular assays

The in vitro cytotoxicity of the nanocarrier formulations was assessed using a standard MTT assay on both an EpCAM-positive human lung carcinoma cell line (A549) and an EpCAM-negative CHO cell line (CHO). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 8,000 cells per well and cultured overnight in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 24 h of attachment, the cells were exposed to various concentrations of free DOX, PEGylated DOX/ZIF-8, and aptamer-functionalized APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8, with final DOX concentrations ranging from 1.56 to 100 µg/mL. The plates were then incubated for 24, 48, and 72 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. At the end of each incubation period, the culture medium was replaced with 100 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL), and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 150 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was measured at 540 nm with a microplate reader. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values were subsequently calculated for each treatment group.

The A549 (human lung carcinoma, EpCAM-positive) and CHO (CHO, EpCAM-negative) cell lines were obtained from the National Cell Bank of Iran (Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran). Cell line authentication was confirmed via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. All procedures involving cell lines complied with institutional biosafety and ethical regulations.

To further investigate the mechanism of cell death, apoptosis was quantified using Annexin V/PI staining. After treatment, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 1 µL of propidium iodide (PI) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The samples were analyzed on a BD Accuri C6 flow cytometer, and the data were analyzed using standard flow cytometry analysis software to determine the percentage of viable, early apoptotic, and late apoptotic/necrotic cells.

In vivo studies

Anesthesia and euthanasia

For all procedures, mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally. Depth of anesthesia was verified by loss of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes.

At humane endpoints or study termination, euthanasia was performed by intraperitoneal overdose of the same ketamine/xylazine mixture (approximately five times the anesthetic dose). Death was confirmed by apnea, absence of heartbeat, and loss of reflexes before tissue collection.

On day 15, all animals were humanely euthanized following institutional ethical standards for humane sacrifice.

Individual tumor measurements (length and width), tumor weights, and animal weights were recorded for each mouse at all time points. A representative dataset is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data from at least three independent experiments are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To determine statistical significance between multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was used. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0).

Results

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

The crystallinity, phase composition, and structural integrity of ZIF-8 and its modified forms were evaluated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, as depicted in Fig. 1A. For pristine ZIF-8 (purple line), distinct diffraction peaks appeared at 2θ values of approximately 7.3°, 10.4°, and 12.7°, which correspond to the (011), (002), and (112) crystallographic planes, respectively. The sharpness and narrowness of these peaks confirm the high degree of crystallinity and phase purity characteristic of the ZIF-8 structure.

Upon loading with DOX, the resulting DOX/ZIF-8 sample (green line) exhibited diffraction peaks at nearly identical 2θ positions, indicating that the fundamental crystalline structure of the ZIF-8 framework was largely preserved. A slight broadening and minor variation in peak intensity were noted, suggesting subtle interactions between the encapsulated DOX molecules and the ZIF-8 framework that did not significantly alter the overall lattice parameters. Similarly, the PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 sample (red line) retained the primary diffraction peaks, although with some changes in intensity. This confirms that the additional surface functionalization with PEG did not disrupt the core crystalline structure. The preservation of the ZIF-8 framework after both drug loading and PEGylation is crucial, as it ensures the structural stability required for a reliable drug delivery vehicle. These results successfully demonstrate that ZIF-8 can be functionalized with both DOX and PEG without substantial loss of its crystalline integrity, highlighting its suitability as a robust nanocarrier platform for targeted therapeutic applications.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) interpretation

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was conducted to compare the thermal stability and decomposition profiles of ZIF-8, DOX/ZIF-8, and PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 (Fig. 1B). For pristine ZIF-8 (purple line), an initial minor weight loss below 200 °C was attributed to the evaporation of adsorbed water and residual solvent molecules from the pores. A major weight loss event commenced at approximately 400 °C, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of the 2-methylimidazolate organic linkers. This confirms the high thermal stability of the ZIF-8 framework.

The TGA curve for DOX/ZIF-8 (green line) displayed a similar initial desorption step, but the onset of major decomposition occurred at a slightly lower temperature, just below 400 °C. This subtle reduction in thermal stability suggests that the incorporation of DOX into the framework introduces interactions that may slightly weaken the structural integrity under thermal stress. The profile for PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 (red line) revealed a more pronounced initial weight loss due to the hydrophilic nature of PEG, which retains more water. Furthermore, the main decomposition of this functionalized sample began at a significantly earlier temperature of around 350 °C. This is primarily due to the lower thermal stability of the PEG polymer itself, combined with additional interactions from both PEG and DOX that collectively destabilize the framework. In summary, the TGA results confirm the successful functionalization of ZIF-8 with DOX and PEG, as indicated by the distinct changes in the thermal decomposition profiles. Although the modifications slightly decreased the overall thermal stability, the framework remains sufficiently stable for biomedical applications where such extreme temperatures are not encountered.

BET analysis interpretation

The surface area and porosity of the nanocarriers were assessed using BET nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis (Fig. 1C). The isotherm for pristine ZIF-8 (purple squares) showed a characteristic Type I curve, with a sharp increase in nitrogen adsorption at low relative pressures (P/P₀) before reaching a plateau at approximately 300 cm³/g STP. This pattern is indicative of a highly microporous material with a large surface area, a key property for efficient drug loading.

In contrast, the isotherm for ZIF-8/DOX (green circles) displayed a significantly altered profile. The quantity of adsorbed nitrogen increased more gradually across the pressure range and did not reach a distinct plateau, suggesting a substantial reduction in both surface area and porosity. This change confirms the successful loading of DOX molecules within the pores of the ZIF-8 framework, which physically occupy space and block nitrogen adsorption sites. While the ZIF-8 framework’s ability to adsorb gas is diminished, this reduction is a direct indicator of successful drug encapsulation, a critical step for its function as a drug delivery system.

Transmission electron microscopy analysis

The morphology and size of the synthesized PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 nanoparticles were visualized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), as shown in Fig. 1D. The images revealed that the nanoparticles were predominantly polyhedral, consistent with the characteristic crystalline structure of ZIF-8, with some appearing spherical. The particle size distribution was relatively uniform, with a calculated average particle size of 336.75 nm and a standard deviation of 93.16 nm. This uniformity is highly desirable for a drug delivery system, as it promotes consistency in drug loading capacity and release kinetics, ultimately contributing to more predictable and effective therapeutic outcomes.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis further confirmed the colloidal properties of the nanoparticles in aqueous suspension. The hydrodynamic diameters of ZIF-8, PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 were 335 ± 40 nm, 355 ± 45 nm and 372 ± 50 nm, respectively, with corresponding polydispersity index (PDI) values of 0.28, 0.32 and 0.36, indicating moderately polydisperse but colloidally stable suspensions. The zeta potentials were + 20 ± 3 mV for ZIF-8, + 2 ± 4 mV for PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and − 12 ± 5 mV for APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 (Table 2), consistent with successful PEGylation and subsequent introduction of negatively charged aptamer strands on the nanoparticle surface.

Free DOX demonstrated potent cytotoxicity against both cell lines, with slightly lower IC₅₀ values for A549 cells (4.58–7.65 µM) compared to CHO cells (5.41–10.96 µM). This suggests a limited intrinsic selectivity of DOX for cancer cells.

Crucially, the aptamer-functionalized APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation exhibited remarkable targeting selectivity. The IC₅₀ values in A549 cells were significantly lower (5.73–16.71 µM) compared to those in CHO cells (54.65–89.23 µM). This stark difference (p ≤ 0.05), with minimal toxicity toward the EpCAM-negative CHO cells, strongly indicates that the EpCAM aptamer effectively mediated targeted uptake and enhanced cytotoxicity in the A549 cancer cells.

Conversely, the non-targeted PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 system showed less specific cytotoxicity, with comparable IC₅₀ values in both A549 cells (16.12–34.07 µM) and CHO cells (17.10–39.52 µM). While encapsulation in ZIF-8 provided some modulation of toxicity, the lack of a targeting ligand resulted in generalized cytotoxic effects. The ZIF-8 nanocarrier alone demonstrated no observable toxicity in either cell line, confirming its excellent biocompatibility as a delivery vehicle.

These results collectively highlight the success of the targeting strategy. The APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation significantly improved the therapeutic window by selectively targeting and eliminating cancer cells while sparing non-cancerous cells. This enhanced specificity, attributable to the EpCAM aptamer, represents a substantial improvement over both free DOX and the non-targeted nanocarrier, promising a better safety profile for clinical applications.

Flow cytometry analysis

Annexin V/PI staining followed by flow cytometry was performed to quantify apoptosis and necrosis induced by the different treatments in A549 cells (Fig. 3). Dot plots were generated using consistent compensation, identical logarithmic axis scaling, and gates defined from unstained/single-stained controls. In the untreated control group, 90.8% of cells were viable (Q4), with low baseline levels of early apoptosis (5.13% in Q3) and late apoptosis/necrosis (3.47% in Q2).

Treatment with PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 produced a pronounced shift toward early apoptosis, with 90.5% of cells in Q3 and only 3.81% remaining viable (Q4).

The EpCAM-targeted APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 group showed a marked increase in late apoptotic/necrotic cells (34.5% in Q2) together with 34.2% in early apoptosis (Q3), while 31.0% remained viable (Q4), consistent with enhanced, aptamer-mediated delivery of DOX into EpCAM-positive cells.

Free DOX induced apoptosis as well, with 52.2% of cells in early apoptosis (Q3) and 16.6% in late apoptosis/necrosis (Q2), leaving 30.6% viable (Q4).

These flow cytometry results are consistent with the MTT assay findings. The superior induction of late apoptosis/necrosis by APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 correlates with its lower IC₅₀ value and confirms its enhanced efficacy. The data collectively validate that the EpCAM aptamer functionalization significantly potentiates the anti-tumor effect of DOX by ensuring its targeted delivery and potent induction of apoptosis in cancer cells.

In vivo study: body weight and tumor volume analysis

The in vivo therapeutic efficacy and systemic toxicity were evaluated over 15 days by monitoring tumor volume and body weight in xenograft-bearing mice (Fig. 4).

Mice treated with free DOX and non-targeted PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 experienced an initial decline in body weight, a common indicator of systemic toxicity. In contrast, the body weight of mice in the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 group remained stable throughout the study, comparable to the untreated control group. This stability strongly suggests that the targeted formulation effectively minimizes the systemic toxicity associated with DOX, likely by reducing off-target drug distribution and improving the overall safety profile.

Regarding therapeutic efficacy, the control group exhibited rapid, progressive tumor growth. While the PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and free DOX treatments moderately inhibited tumor growth compared to the control, the most profound therapeutic effect was observed in the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 group. This targeted formulation led to a marked and sustained suppression of tumor growth, with tumor volumes remaining minimal throughout the 15-day period. At the end of the study, the tumor volume in the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 group was significantly smaller than in all other groups (p ≤ 0.05).

These combined in vivo results demonstrate the superior performance of the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 nanocarrier. It not only achieves the most effective tumor suppression but also exhibits a significantly improved safety profile, as evidenced by the stable body weights. This enhanced therapeutic index is a direct result of the EpCAM aptamer-mediated targeting, which maximizes DOX accumulation at the tumor site while minimizing systemic exposure, making it a highly.

promising candidate for targeted cancer therapy.

Histopathology and toxicity study

Histopathological analysis of H&E-stained tissues was performed to assess therapeutic efficacy in tumors and potential toxicity in major organs after 15 days of treatment (Fig. 5). Tumor sections from all treated groups displayed areas of necrosis, but the effect was most pronounced in the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 group. This group exhibited extensive necrotic regions and a significant reduction in viable tumor cell density, providing clear evidence of enhanced anti-tumor activity due to effective, targeted drug delivery.

Examination of vital organs revealed a notable reduction in toxicity for the nanoparticle formulations. While heart tissues appeared normal across all groups, the liver and kidneys of mice treated with free DOX showed clear signs of inflammatory cell infiltration, indicative of hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity. In stark contrast, these inflammatory responses were minimal to absent in both the PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 groups. Similarly, lung sections from the free DOX group showed inflammation, which was not observed in the nanoparticle-treated groups. Spleen tissues remained largely unaffected across all treatments.

Collectively, the histological data confirm that the drug-loaded nanoparticles, especially the targeted APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation, provide superior tumor cell killing while significantly mitigating the off-target organ toxicities commonly associated with free DOX. The targeted system proves to be a safer and more effective therapeutic strategy.

To provide a semi-quantitative assessment of treatment-induced tumor damage, necrosis in tumor sections was scored on a 0–3 scale (0 = no necrosis, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate, 3 = extensive) by two independent observers in a blinded manner. The mean necrosis scores for the control, free DOX, PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 groups were 0.7 ± 0.4, 1.9 ± 0.5, 2.1 ± 0.4 and 2.8 ± 0.3, respectively (Supplementary Table S2). These data corroborate the qualitative histological observations and confirm that the EpCAM-targeted formulation induces the most extensive tumor destruction among all treatments.

Biodistribution of DOX in xenograft tumor-bearing mice

The biodistribution of DOX was assessed by measuring its fluorescence in harvested organs at 12- and 24-hours post-injection for the targeted (APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8), non-targeted (PEG-DOX/ZIF-8), and free DOX formulations (Fig. 6).

The targeted APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation demonstrated significantly enhanced tumor accumulation compared to the other groups. At both 12 and 24 h, the highest DOX fluorescence intensity was observed in the tumor tissue, with markedly lower accumulation in the liver, spleen, and lungs. This confirms the efficacy of the EpCAM aptamer in directing the nanocarriers to the tumor site while minimizing uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES).

In contrast, the non-targeted PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation showed the highest accumulation in the liver at both time points, with moderate accumulation in the tumor. The free DOX group exhibited widespread distribution across the liver, kidneys, and tumor, with significant levels also detected in the spleen and lungs, indicating a lack of specificity and high potential for systemic toxicity.

These biodistribution results provide compelling evidence for the superior tumor-targeting capability of the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation. This targeted delivery mechanism is key to enhancing the therapeutic index, concentrating the cytotoxic drug at the site of action while reducing exposure to healthy tissues.

Quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in excised tissues provided further insight into the biodistribution profile of the formulations. In tumors, the MFI values were 100 ± 20, 150 ± 25 and 250 ± 30 arbitrary units for free DOX, PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8, respectively, confirming that the EpCAM-targeted formulation achieves the highest tumor-associated DOX signal. In contrast, liver MFI values were 180 ± 25, 260 ± 30 and 200 ± 25 arbitrary units for the same groups, indicating strong hepatic uptake of PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 due to RES clearance and a relative shift of drug distribution from liver to tumor in the case of APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8. Consistent with these trends, MFI values in heart, kidney, spleen and lung (Supplementary Table S3) showed no abnormal accumulation of APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 in non-target organs compared with PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and free DOX.

Cellular uptake of CHO and A549 cells

The efficiency of cellular uptake was evaluated in EpCAM-negative CHO and EpCAM-positive A549 cells using fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry (Fig. 7).

In CHO cells (Fig. 7A), the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation showed only minimal red fluorescence, indicating very low non-specific uptake. In contrast, free DOX resulted in strong fluorescence, demonstrating efficient passive diffusion. These qualitative observations were confirmed by flow cytometry, where free DOX induced the largest shift in fluorescence intensity.

A strikingly different pattern was observed in A549 cells (Fig. 7B). The APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 formulation produced intense red fluorescence, indicating highly efficient cellular uptake and intracellular DOX release. This uptake was significantly greater than that of the non-targeted PEG-DOX/ZIF-8, confirming that the EpCAM aptamer actively facilitated internalization. Flow cytometry data corroborated these findings, showing that the mean fluorescence intensity for the APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 group was comparable to free DOX and significantly higher than the non-targeted nanoparticle group (p ≤ 0.05).

These results conclusively demonstrate the successful aptamer-mediated targeting of the ZIF-8 nanocarriers. The selective and enhanced uptake in EpCAM-positive A549 cells is directly responsible for the superior cytotoxicity and therapeutic efficacy observed in this study, while the minimal uptake in EpCAM-negative CHO cells explains the reduced off-target toxicity.

Discussion

This study successfully addressed key limitations of conventional doxorubicin therapy by integrating a porous ZIF-8 framework, PEGylation, and EpCAM aptamer functionalization into a single nanocarrier platform. The resulting APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 system was designed to improve tumor selectivity, enhance intracellular delivery to EpCAM-positive cells, and mitigate systemic toxicity through controlled, pH-responsive drug release.

The core innovation of this research lies in the combination of structural and functional features that collectively enhance selective targeting. DLS and zeta potential measurements confirmed successful stepwise surface modification, with the hydrodynamic diameter increasing from 335 ± 40 nm (ZIF-8) to 355 ± 45 nm (PEG-DOX/ZIF-8) and 372 ± 50 nm (APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8), and the zeta potential shifting from + 20 ± 3 mV to + 2 ± 4 mV and − 12 ± 5 mV, respectively. These changes are consistent with PEG shielding and the introduction of negatively charged aptamer strands and support the improved colloidal stability required for in vivo delivery.

Our in vitro and in vivo findings jointly highlight the functional consequences of these design features. Although free DOX remains the most potent formulation in terms of intrinsic in vitro cytotoxicity, the targeted formulation achieved significantly greater tumor suppression in vivo, as reflected by reduced tumor volumes and the highest necrosis score (2.8 ± 0.3) among all treatment groups compared with free DOX (1.9 ± 0.5) and PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 (2.1 ± 0.4). Moreover, quantitative biodistribution analysis showed that APT-PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 produced the strongest tumor-associated DOX signal (250 ± 30 a.u.), while partially reducing liver accumulation relative to PEG-DOX/ZIF-8 and avoiding abnormal accumulation in heart, kidney, spleen and lung. These results indicate that EpCAM-mediated active targeting acts synergistically with the EPR effect to bias DOX delivery toward the tumor and improve the overall therapeutic index.

It is also pertinent to consider the kinetic behavior of the system. The relatively moderate cumulative DOX release observed under static acidic conditions in vitro can be understood as a controlled-release profile that minimizes burst release while still enabling effective cytotoxicity once the carrier accumulates in tumor tissue and is internalized by cancer cells. Within the dynamic intracellular environment, where ZIF-8 is exposed to fluctuating pH, enzymes and trafficking pathways, the effective release rate is expected to be higher than that measured in dialysis experiments, which helps reconcile the observed in vitro and in vivo efficacy. Together, these data support the concept that optimizing biodistribution and release kinetics can be more important for therapeutic outcome than maximizing apparent in vitro potency alone.

Study limitations

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the investigation was conducted using a single cancer cell line and a corresponding xenograft model. The heterogeneity of human tumors, including variable EpCAM expression levels, could influence therapeutic efficacy in a clinical setting. Future studies should therefore validate these findings in diverse cancer models, including patient-derived xenografts (PDX). Second, while the 15-day in vivo study provided strong evidence of short-term efficacy and safety, a comprehensive evaluation of long-term toxicity and the potential for immunogenicity would be necessary before clinical translation. Third, the large-scale, reproducible synthesis of monodisperse and consistently functionalized nanoparticles remains a challenge in nanomedicine that needs to be addressed for clinical manufacturing. Finally, although our in vitro release study demonstrated clear pH-responsiveness, a more detailed kinetic analysis using models such as the Korsmeyer-Peppas model could provide deeper insights into the drug release mechanism.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the EpCAM-targeted ZIF-8 nanocarrier system developed in this study represents a significant advancement in the field of tumor-specific drug delivery. By strategically combining the pH-responsive properties of ZIF-8 nanoparticles with the high targeting specificity of EpCAM aptamers, we have created a sophisticated delivery vehicle that successfully enhances the therapeutic efficacy of Doxorubicin. Our comprehensive in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrate that this targeted approach not only achieves superior tumor suppression but also drastically reduces the systemic toxicity associated with conventional chemotherapy. This formulation ensures effective drug accumulation and release within tumor cells, minimizing off-target effects and presenting a safer and more efficient alternative for cancer treatment. These promising results highlight the immense potential of this nanocarrier system to revolutionize targeted chemotherapy, paving the way for future clinical applications in oncology.
