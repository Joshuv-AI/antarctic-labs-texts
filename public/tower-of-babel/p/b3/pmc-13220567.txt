The selection and strategy of stents in endoscopic drainage of unresectable perihilar cholangiocarcinoma: a meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoyi Guo, Jinqi Xing, Haifeng Luo, Di Zhang, Feng Gao, Zihao Dai, Jianfeng Huo, Zhuo Yang, Jiangning Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimEndoscopic biliary drainage serves as the primary palliative intervention for malignant hilar biliary obstruction (MHBO), especially unresectable perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA), with metal stents (MS) and plastic stents (PS) constituting the main therapeutic modalities. Despite widespread clinical adoption, persistent equipoise surrounds their comparative therapeutic efficacy, with current guidelines lacking consensus on optimal deployment strategies. Therefore, this study included the recent studies of endoscopic stent therapy for unresectable pCCA to explore further the drainage effect and postoperative complications of the two stents, and to provide clinical guidance for the selection of stent drainage. For unresectable pCCA, biliary drainage is the basis for subsequent systemic treatment and also one of the essential therapeutic methods for prolonging survival time. Endoscopic drainage has the advantages of less pain and better compliance with physiology compared with percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD). However, due to the difficulty of endoscopic treatment operation and the possibility of inducing biliary tract infection, especially for Bismuth-Corlette type III and above pCCA, endoscopic treatment is more challenging. Currently, there are still many controversial issues regarding the selection of stents and drainage strategies in endoscopic biliary drainage. This meta-analysis systematically evaluates contemporary evidence to establish evidence-based protocols for stent selection in MHBO, especially pCCA.MethodsA comprehensive search of multiple databases (Pubmed, MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane) and grey literature was conducted, covering articles from the inception of the databases until September 2024, without restrictions on publication year or language, provided they included at least an English abstract. Studies comparing MS and PS techniques through endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) were included. Outcomes analyzed included technical and clinical success, complications, stent patency, and re-intervention rates. Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan software, STATA 17.0, and R software on the data of interest extracted from the selected studies.ResultsA total of 10 cohort studies and two randomized controlled trials (RCT) were included, assessing 1 405 patients (714 patients in the MS group and 691 patients in the PS group). MS demonstrated comparable technical success rates [risk ratio (RR) = 1.00; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.97–1.04, I2 = 0%, p = 0.82] and clinical success rates [RR = 0.99; 95%CI 0.93–1.06, I2 = 1%, p = 0.75] to PS. MS exhibited superior stent patency [hazard ratio (HR) = 2.11; 95% CI 1.31–3.39, I2 = 0%, p = 0.002] with 68% reduction in stent occlusion [odds ratio (OR) = 2.89; 95% CI 2.08–4.03, I2 = 0%, p < 0.00001] to PS. Complication profiles significantly favored MS to PS: 63% lower cholangitis incidence [OR = 3.61; 95% CI 2.46–5.30, I2 = 32%, p < 0.00001]; 87% reduction in stent migration [OR = 7.87; 95% CI 2.56–24.17, I2 = 0%, p < 0.0003]; 68% fewer reintervention [OR = 3.17, 95% CI 2.37–4.23, I2 = 22%, p < 0.00001]. No significant differences emerged in pancreatitis [OR = 0.96; 95%CI 0.57–1.61, I2 = 1%, p = 0.87], hemorrhage [OR = 1.29, 95%CI 0.41–4.04, I2 = 0%, p = 0.66], or survival [HR = 1.01; 95%CI 0.91–1.12, I2 = 0%, p = 0.894].ConclusionThis meta-analysis establishes MS as the preferential modality for endoscopic MHBO management, demonstrating 2.1-fold longer patency duration and 63–87% risk reduction in major complications compared to PS. These findings strongly support protocolized MS deployment in unresectable pCCA, particularly for reducing cholangitis burden and reintervention frequency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03663-z.

Background

Malignant hilar biliary obstruction (MHBO) can arise from diverse etiologies, including perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA, around 60–80% of cases), gallbladder cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, or hilar metastatic lymph nodes [1, 2]. Each cause of obstruction is associated with different prognoses and treatment responses. Perihilar cholangiocarcinoma, also referred to as a Klatskin tumor, is the primary reason for MHBO, which manifests through obstructive jaundice, pruritus, and cholangitis, necessitating urgent biliary decompression [3]. The Bismuth-Corlette classification system stratifies biliary strictures anatomically, with type III-IV lesions presenting particular therapeutic challenges due to their advanced stage at diagnosis (median survival: 7–16 months) and technical complexity during endoscopic intervention [4]. Endoscopic stent placement has revolutionized palliative management since its inception in the 1980s for malignant biliary obstruction in pancreatic and biliary cancer cases [5]. According to the results of existing studies, in patients with malignant hilar biliary obstruction, biliary stent placement can not only alleviate symptoms of obstruction but also extend the survival time [6]. There are two common types of stents in clinical practice: metal stents (MS) and plastic stents (PS). Variations of designs and placement methods derived from these two stents, such as covered versus uncovered stents, unilateral versus single, stent-in-stent (SIS) versus side-by-side (SBS) [7]. Comparative analyses reveal that survival rates and prognoses vary across different disease types, stent types, implantation methods, and stent placement techniques.

Metal stents generally offer better resistance to occlusion and are suitable for long-term use, providing better expansion capacity and patency in larger bile ducts. However, they can be more complex to replace and difficult to remove once complications arise. Conversely, plastic stents offer easier exchangeability and cost-effectivenes. Nevertheless, plastic stents are more prone to occlusion, necessitating periodic replacements and resulting in shorter patency durations. Regarding safety, various studies indicated that metal stents may present a higher risk of migration during long-term use, although the overall complication rates remain within acceptable ranges. Plastic stents, being at a greater risk for occlusion, benefit from their ease of replacement, which can result in better short-term safety. Recent NCCN guidelines also recommend metal stents for predicted survival > 4 months but emphasized plastic stents for neoadjuvant scenarios requiring frequent stent revisions [6]. The therapeutic dichotomy persists due to some unresolved controversies as patency-survival paradox that despite longer stent durability with metal stents, survival benefits remain statistically equivocal [8, 9].

In summary, both types of stents have their specific indications and limitations in the treatment of MHBO. The choice of stent should be based on the patient’s specific condition, prognosis, and clinical circumstances. Due to the inconsistent conclusions across studies, it is essential to continue conducting systematic reviews and meta-analyses to clarify the specific applications of different stents in the treatment of MHBO, ultimately providing clearer guidance for clinical decision-making. Therefore, in order to collect the best available data from the existing literature, we have designed a systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the feasibility, safety, and efficacy of plastic and metal stents for palliative drainage in MHBO.

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted. This search strategy was applied to databases from their inception until September 2024: PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, China Science and Technology Journal Database, Wanfang database, and grey literature. Non-English studies were included if they provided English abstracts with sufficient methodological details.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently performed data extraction and quality assessment. The main data extracted from the original literature included: the first author, publication year, country/language, sample size, baseline characteristics of the patients, interventions, the type of study design, and key results. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool [10] and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [11] were used to assess the quality of included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and retrospective cohort studies, respectively. In case of disagreement between the two reviewers during the data extraction or quality assessment, a third reviewer was participated in the discussion to reach a consensus. Since this meta-analysis is based on published studies, no ethical approval or informed consent was required.

Definitions

Technical success was defined as the passage of the stent across the stricture along with the flow of contrast medium and/or bile through the stent. Clinical success was defined as either (1) a decrease in the total bilirubin level to ≤ 30% of the pretreatment value within 2 weeks or to ≤ 50% within 4 weeks or (2) no increase in the total bilirubin level over 1 mg/dL within 2 or 4 weeks if the pretreatment value was low (≤ 1 mg/dL) [12]. Overall survival time was defined as the duration between the time of diagnosis and that of death or last follow-up date. Stent patency was defined as the period between stent insertion and stent occlusion or patient death. Occussion was considered if patients had recurrent jaundice with cholestasis or cholangitis (fever, increase in serum bilirubin, leucocytosis) and dilatation of intrahepatic bile duct, revealed by imaging. Re-intervention rates. The definitions of procedure-related complications were modified from those of Cotton et al. [13]. and Freeman et al. [14]. Cholangitis was a serious procedure-related complication and defined as an elevation in total bilirubin with fever (≥ 38℃) without other infectious etiology. A bleeding event was defined when endoscopic hemostasis was required or blood transfusion was needed after a procedure. Post-endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography pancreatitis was defined as new or worsened abdominal pain with an elevation in serum amylase and lipase at least 3-fold the upper limit of normal for 24 h after a procedure. Perforation was defined as frank perforation during the procedure, free air in an abdominal X-ray after the procedure, or a perforation found in an abdominal image (computed tomography, etc.) performed for abdominal pain evaluation. Stent migration was defined as shifting of the stents into the duodenum or lodging in the distal bile duct below the stricture. Reintervention was performed when total bilirubin increased more than twice of the upper limit of normal or when symptoms of acute cholangitis such as fever and right upper abdominal pain occurred. Acute cholecystitis and liver abscess were confirmed by imaging studies [15].

Results synthesis and data analysis

For continuous variables, we used means or medians, accompanied by standard deviations and the total number of patients. Categorical variables were expressed as the absolute number of events and the total number of patients. The Mantel-Haenszel test was used to calculate both the conventional and absolute risk differences for each group. The mean and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each continuous outcome.

We defined the plastic stent (PS) group as the reference category for all statistical comparisons. Consequently, the reported odds ratio (OR) for outcomes quantifies the event risk in the PS group compared to the metal stent (MS) group. Thus, an OR > 1 indicates a higher risk associated with PS. The hazard ratio (HR) denotes the relative risk for the PS group versus the MS group. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the results were presented using forest plots.

The association between OS and stent patency time was derived as a weighted average of study-specific estimates of the hazard ratio (HR), using inverse variance weights [16]. The logarithm of the HR (logHR) was assumed to have a normal distribution. If CIs were reported, Standard Error (SE) for the logHRs were calculated. The logHRs and the corresponding SEs were used as data points for the meta-analysis. In our studies not quoting the HRs or CIs, these were calculated from the presented data using two of the following parameters: the HR point estimate, the log-rank statistic or its P value, the O-E statistic (the difference between the number of observed and the number of expected events) or its variance. If these data were unavailable, the total number of events, the number of patients at risk in each group, and the log-rank statistic or its P value were used to derive an approximate estimate of the HR. If, however, the only available data were in the form of graphical representations of the survival distributions, survival rates were extracted at specified times in order to reconstruct the HR estimate and its variance, under the assumption that the rate of patients censored was constant during the study follow-up [17]. With this last method, survival curves were read independently by all authors, after magnification, to reduce imprecision. When censoring data were presented, censored subjects were subtracted from the denominators by counting tick marks on survival curves [18].

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and examine whether treatment effects varied across different patient populations, we performed pre-specified subgroup analyses based on treatment intent. Studies were categorized into three subgroups: 1. Preoperative group: Preoperative drainage of jaundice; 2. Palliative group: palliative purposes for unresectable MHBO patients; 3. Mixed/Unclear group: Studies with mixed populations or unclear treatment intent. Subgroup analyses were conducted by including a categorical subgroup variable in the meta-analysis model. Between-subgroup differences were tested using the Q-test for subgroup differences, which evaluates whether effect estimates differ significantly across subgroups. Random-effects models were used within each subgroup to account for residual heterogeneity.

To assess the potential influence of study design, we performed sensitivity analyses stratified by study design quality. All included studies were classified into three categories based on the types of included studies: 1. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs): Studies using randomization for treatment allocation; 2. High-quality observational studies: NOS (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale) score ≥ 7; 3. Low-quality observational studies: NOS score < 7. This classification was based on the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies. Two independent reviewers assessed study quality, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

Separate meta-analyses were conducted for each design category using the same statistical methods as the primary analysis. Additionally, we performed meta-regression analysis with study design as a moderator variable to formally test whether study design significantly influenced treatment effects. The coefficient from meta-regression represents the difference in log effect size between design categories.

We conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our findings and identify potentially influential studies. This approach involves sequentially excluding each study from the meta-analysis and recalculating the pooled effect estimate based on the remaining studies.

The procedure was as follows: 1. For an outcome with k studies, perform k separate meta-analyses, each excluding one different study; 2. Record the pooled effect estimate and 95% CI for each analysis; 3. Compare these estimates with the overall effect estimate from the complete analysis; 4. Considere a study influential if its exclusion resulted in: a) A change in statistical significance (from significant to non-significant or vice versa); b) A change in the direction of effect; c) A substantial change in effect magnitude (> 20% relative change).

Heterogeneity was assessed using the Higgins method (I²). A fixed-effects model was applied when heterogeneity was found to be less than 50%. In cases with high inter-study heterogeneity (I² > 50%), a funnel plot was utilized for sensitivity analysis to identify publication bias (outliers). If the level of heterogeneity remained high even after excluding outliers, we retained them and applied a random-effects model to represent the results (true heterogeneity). Conversely, if the level of heterogeneity was low after excluding outliers, we used a fixed-effects model.

Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan software (Review Manager Software Version 5.4.1—Cochrane Collaboration © 2020), STATA 17.0, and R software (version 4.3.2) with the meta (version 6.5.0) and metafor (version 4.4.0) packages on the data extracted from the selected studies.

Results

Through systematic searches across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the China Science and Technology Journal Database, and the Wanfang Database, 1 035 potentially relevant articles were initially identified. Following duplicate removal and sequential screening of titles/abstracts, 87 articles underwent full-text evaluation. Ultimately, 12 studies met the inclusion criteria, comprising nine retrospective cohort studies, one prospective study, and two randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The study selection process is detailed in the flowchart (Fig. 1). The enrolled studies collectively analyzed 1 405 patients (MS: n = 714; PS: n = 691) undergoing endoscopic drainage, with comparative cohorts receiving either metal stents or plastic stents. Technical and clinical success

The pooled analysis demonstrated high technical success rates across studies (n = 8 447 patients), reflecting procedural expertise and providing robust foundations for subsequent complication analyses. Initial heterogeneity assessment revealed substantial variability (I² = 87%, Q-test p < 0.00001). Through L’Abbé and Galbraith radial plot analyses, two outlier studies were identified and excluded. Post-exclusion analysis showed comparable technical success between metal stents (MS) and plastic stents (PS) [risk ratio (RR) = 1.00, 95%CI 0.97–1.04, I² = 0%, p = 0.82] (Fig. 3).

Similarly, clinical success evaluation (n = 8 480 patients) exhibited marked heterogeneity (I² = 76%, Q-test p = 0.0002). Following exclusion of two heterogeneous studies, no significant inter-group difference emerged [RR = 0.99, 95%CI 0.93–1.06, I² = 1%, p = 0.75] (Fig. 4).

Overall survival time

The fundamental purpose of stent placement is to relieve obstruction, improve patients’ quality of life, and prolong their survival. Analysis of seven studies (n = 1 034) revealed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 60%, Q-test p = 0.019). Post-sensitivity adjustment, pooled data demonstrated comparable survival outcomes between MS and PS [Hazard Ratio (HR) = 1.01, 95%CI 0.91–1.12, I² = 0%, p = 0.894] (Fig. 5).

Stents patency time

Another main clinical manifestation for evaluating the effect of stent placement is the patency time of stents. Four studies (n = 306) exhibited significant heterogeneity (I² = 80.5%, Q-test p = 0.001). Exclusion of one outlier study revealed superior patency with MS [HR = 2.11, 95%CI 1.31–3.39, I² = 0%, p = 0.002] (Fig. 6).

Post-operational complications

In addition to the stent patency time, stent-related complications are also critical factors for evaluating different stents. More complications may lead to additional clinical interventions, higher costs, and a reduced quality of life for patients. Pooled analysis of five studies (n = 713) showed PS associated with more overall complications [Odds Ratio (OR) = 3.72, 95%CI 2.64–5.24, I² = 37%, p < 0.00001] following heterogeneity adjustment (I² = 65%, Q-test p = 0.02) (Fig. 7).

Post-ERCP Pancreatitis (PEP)

Pancreatitis is one of the most serious complications after stent implantation, with an extremely high mortality rate. This may be attributed to the selection of the guide wire entering the pancreatic duct during the procedure or the injection of a contrast agent into the pancreatic duct. In our analysis of postoperative pancreatitis, eight studies (n = 1 170) demonstrated homogeneous outcomes (I² = 1%), with comparable PEP rates between groups [OR = 0.96, 95%CI 0.57–1.61, p = 0.87] (Fig. 8).

Postoperative cholangitis

Cholangitis is another dangerous complication after stent implantation, and its incidence is usually higher than that of pancreatitis. Meanwhile, it is also one of the main causes of infection for patients with hilar cholangiocarcinoma. Analysis of eight studies (n = 1 147) revealed PS associated with more cholangitis, which means that MS significantly reduced cholangitis risk [OR = 3.61, 95%CI 2.46–5.30, I² = 32%, p < 0.00001] with acceptable heterogeneity (Fig. 9).

Stent occlusion

Stent occlusion is the most common factor contributing to stent dysfunction and impacting the duration of stent patency. This occlusion often results from tumor growth or debris accumulation. Five homogeneous studies (n = 863, I² = 0%) confirmed PS inferiority in occlusion prevention [OR = 2.89, 95%CI 2.08–4.03, p < 0.00001] (Fig. 10).

Stent migration

Stent migration is the second major significant factor affecting the stent patency time. After resolving significant heterogeneity (I² = 76%, Q-test p = 0.002) through study exclusion, PS showed markedly higher incidence of stent migration [OR = 7.87, 95%CI 2.56–24.17, I² = 0%, p < 0.0003] (Fig. 11).

Hemorrhage

Hemorrhage is a common complication in any surgical procedure. In the case of stent implantation, bleeding is typically minor. The common treatment involves the use of hemostatic clips or the application of hemostatic drugs after the surgery. Massive bleeding that endangers life is relatively rare. Analysis of four studies (n = 830) revealed no inter-group difference in hemorrhage [OR = 1.29, 95%CI 0.41–4.04, I² = 0%, p = 0.66] (Fig. 13).

Re-intervention rates were the number that the patients participating in the study required clinical intervention caused by any complications. In our analysis, PS significantly associated with higher reintervention requirements across six studies (n = 938) [OR = 3.17, 95%CI 2.37–4.23, I² = 22%, p < 0.00001] (Fig. 15).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the main conclusions, we performed subgroup analysis among different qualities studies: RCTs, high quality studies(NOS ≥ 7), and low quality studies (NOS< 7) (Fig. 17) between the two groups in terms of clinical outcomes (technically success, clinical success, postoperative pancreatitis, postoperative cholangitis, stent occlusion, liver abscess, hemorrhage, pos-ERCP Cholecystitis, reinterventions rate, overall survival time, and stent patency time). Patients in the studies involved in outcomes including all complications and stent migration were completely high quality studies. Besides, we designed leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for included studies (Fig. 18). Both subgroup analysis among different qualities studies and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis indicated that there were no significant heterogeneity among included studies. Besides, especially in subgroup analyses that included only RCTs, the direction of effect estimates was consistent with the overall results.

Discussion

The predominant mortality determinant in biliary malignancies stems not from metastatic progression but from uncontrolled biliary sepsis, particularly prevalent in perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA) management. While distal biliary obstructions may be effectively managed through single-stent deployment or percutaneous approaches, the complex anatomical landscape of hilar lesions necessitates meticulous long-term drainage planning in both the number and strategy for the placement of drainage stents. Critical considerations encompass Bismuth-Corlette classification, tumor burden, ductal caliber, and hepatic functional reserve. Furthermore, reliable assessment of classification can be challenging before and during surgery, even with preoperative and intraoperative cholangiography. Direct visualization through choledochoscopic examination is often necessary for proper treatment. Improper drainage can lead not only to inadequate jaundice reduction but also to the risk of triggering uncontrollable biliary infections due to contrast agent injection and stent reflux. Current consensus guidelines from the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) and Asia-Pacific working groups emphasize maintaining ≥ 50% functional liver drainage volume to optimize clinical outcomes and minimize cholangitis risk [25, 26].

Plastic stents are replaceable and more prone to occlusion, requiring regular replacement. In contrast, metal stents may provoke a more significant inflammatory response and tumor overgrowth, making replacement more challenging. Therefore, PS retain utility in resectable cases due to exchangeability, while MS are preferred for palliative management of advanced disease [6, 9].

Our meta-analysis synthesizes evidence from twelve studies (n = 1 405) on the effects of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) implantation of metal and plastic stents for MHB, which surpass conventional single-center trial capacities, to elucidate stent selection paradigms in unresectable pCCA especially. In our meta-analysis, we defined the plastic stent (PS) group as the reference category for all statistical comparisons. Consequently, the reported odds ratio (OR) for outcomes quantifies the event risk in the PS group compared to the metal stent (MS) group. Thus, an OR > 1 indicates a higher risk associated with PS. The hazard ratio (HR) denotes the relative risk for the PS group versus the MS group. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the results were presented using forest plots. The findings establish plastic stents (PS) associated with a higher risk, which means that metal stents (MS) as superior to PS in multiple domains: extended patency duration (HR = 2.11, 95%CI 1.31–3.39), reduced complication burden (OR = 3.72, 95%CI 2.64–5.24) and lower reintervention frequency (OR = 3.17, 95%CI 2.37–4.23). Notably, MS demonstrated particular efficacy in mitigating cholangitis (OR = 3.61), stent occlusion (OR = 2.89), and migration (OR = 7.87). However, technical/clinical success parity (RR ≈ 1.00) and equivalent overall survival (HR = 1.01) between stent types underscore the multifactorial nature of pCCA prognosis. These observations align with recent cost-efficacy analyses highlighting MS’ economic viability despite higher initial costs [27].

The Bismuth-Corlette classification emerged as a critical determinant of stent performance in our review. Although not explicitly analyzed by subgroup, studies involving Type III/ IV strictures [8, 9] reported higher reintervention rates with PS, likely due to their narrower lumens and susceptibility to tumor overgrowth in complex hilar anatomy. Conversely, MS demonstrated superior patency across all stages, but their advantage was most pronounced in advanced strictures (HR = 2.11), aligning with the ESGE guideline’s emphasis on MS for high-grade obstructions.

The included studies predominantly addressed unresectable hilar biliary obstruction [8, 9, 12, 19, 20, 27, 28], with two focusing on preoperative drainage for pCCA [22, 29]. Compared with patients with more advanced stages who were inoperable, preoperative drainage generally exhibited a longer survival period and fewer complications. Although there are differences in the treatment goals between preoperative pCCA and unresectable MHBO, they are highly similar in the pathophysiological mechanism of biliary obstruction, stent placement technique and risk of postoperative complications, so the combined analysis is clinically reasonable. We performed subgroup analyses for different treatment purposes, and although subgroup analyses did not show statistical differences, there are still differences in clinical management and prognosis between the preoperative and palliative care populations, and further stratified analyses are warranted in future studies. David et al. found that preoperative MS deployment correlated with prolonged survival [29], this likely reflects selection bias toward earlier-stage tumors. While the incidence of postoperative complications, such as pancreatitis and cholangitis, were higher than other studies. Meanwhile, Florian et al.‘s META analysis which included 25 studies indicated that patients who received preoperative drainage had a higher overall complication rate [30]. This might be attributable to the longer survival time associated with these interventions, which regarded that elevated complication rates in preoperative stenting warrant careful risk-benefit evaluation.

Heterogeneity in outcomes may reflect evolving endoscopic techniques. Earlier studies [21] reported lower clinical success rates (85–90%) compared to recent trials [28](95–98%), underscoring the impact of improved ERCP skills and stent technology. This temporal trend may partially explain the lack of survival benefit with MS, while they reduce cholangitis and reinterventions, survival remains tied to tumor biology and systemic therapy access. Notably, centers with high procedural volumes [20] achieved comparable technical success between MS and PS, suggesting that operator experience mitigates some risks associated with either stent type. Paik et al. also indicated that failed interventions significantly compromise survival [31]. As most of modern high-volume centers reported, the success rate for stent implantation, both technical and clinical, have improved over 90% significantly in recent years [8, 9, 12, 19–24, 27–29].

Additionally, it is often recommended to use bilateral or multiple stents to achieve more thorough drainage to enhance liver drainage volume [6]. Our studies also included various stent types. For example, Choi et al. included bilateral stents, such as stent in stent (SIS) and stent by stent (SBS) [8]; Kim et al. included bilateral metal stents (BMS) and multiple plastic stents (MPS) [24]. Despite theoretical advantages of multi-stent drainage, these stent configurations analysis revealed no definitive superiority of specific bilateral deployment techniques. While this reflects clinical variability and enhances the external validity of our findings, it may also introduce confounding. For instance, outcomes could be influenced by the drainage strategy itself rather than solely by stent type. It is noteworthy that such technical variability is a recognized factor affecting outcomes even within the same stent category, as evidenced by reports of differing adverse event rates among different plastic stent designs [32]. Our sensitivity analyses suggested that the core comparative findings between MS and PS were robust. Moreover, SIS and SBS are technically challenging procedures that necessitates operator expertise and individualized strategy formulation, with technical success rates ranging from 73% to 100% [33]. Besides, stents above the papilla, referred to as “inside stents (iS)”, have been evaluated using both metal and plastic materials [34]. Among these, inside plastic stents (iPSs) is favored for patients with unresectable malignant hilar obstruction, as they facilitate reintervention and extent stent patency by preventing reflux of bacteria-containing duodenal juice [35]. However, a common challenge during endoscopic placement of multiple iPSs is the proximal migration of the initially placed stent during subsequent insertions. Several techniques to prevent migration have been reported [32, 36]. Yuichi et al. also proposed a slip-knot thread method for performing the anchor-wire technique which proved effective in avoiding proximal displacement of the preceding iPS during the endoscopic placement of multiple iPSs [37].

There was some heterogeneity in stent placement strategies among the studies included in this review, including different techniques such as unilateral and bilateral drainage, side-by-side and stent-in-stent placement. Because of the limited number of relevant studies, we were not able to perform subgroup analyses according to placement. However, this technical diversity reflects the real situation in clinical practice to a certain extent, enhancing the external applicability of the conclusions of this study. More important, despite the differences in placement methods, the main findings - that metal stents were significantly superior to plastic stents in patency, complication risks, and reintervention rates - were consistent across sensitivity and subgroup analyses, which suggests that stent material itself, rather than placement technique, may be the dominant factor affecting efficacy. Future studies should further standardize placement strategies in order to assess their independent effects more precisely.

In addition to the stent itself, various prognostic factors will influence the evaluation of stent drainage effectiveness. These factors include the types of tumor pathology, the use of adjuvant therapy such as chemotherapy, patients’ basic physical conditions and medical history, economic conditions, and treatment compliance. Emerging adjuvant therapies, including photodynamic therapy (PDT) and radiofrequency ablation (RFA) show promise in enhancing stent patency and survival when combined with biliary drainage [26]. Therefore, a large number of clinical studies are necessary to constantly summarize and specify better drainage strategies aimed at improving the survival time and quality of life of inoperative patients.

The results of subgroup and sensitivity analyses support the robustness of the conclusions of this meta-analysis, showing that the advantages of MS are consistent across study designs, populations, and treatment purposes. However, these analyses are exploratory in nature and there may still be unmeasured confounding factors such as stent placement technique, differences in center experience. More prospective and standardized multicenter RCTs should be carried out for further verification.

We acknowledge that our present study has several limitations. The first one is the lack of prospective protocol registration which means that we did not register their systematic review protocol in PROSPERO or any other publicly accessible registry. Secondly, predominance of observational designs over RCTs. Although observational studies can be subject to selection bias, this study was controlled for by NOS scoring, sensitivity analysis, and exclusion of heterogeneous studies, and the RCT subgroup results were consistent with the overall results, supporting the robustness of the conclusions. Besides, heterogeneity necessitating exclusions, especially the heterogeneity in the endoscopic techniques for stent placement, encompassing unilateral versus bilateral drainage and specific bilateral deployment methods (e.g., SBS versus SIS). Although our sensitivity analyses suggested comparative findings between MS and PS, this methodological heterogeneity underscores the need for future randomized trials that directly compare not only stent materials but also standardized placement protocols in well-defined patient subgroups. Additionally, variability in outcome reporting metrics so that it is difficult to manage the original data from studies. It can make better revision improvements in our retrospective study.

Conclusion

Our meta-analysis establishes metal stents (MS) as the preferential therapeutic modality for endoscopic management of unresectable perihilar cholangiocarcinoma (pCCA). MS demonstrated substantial clinical advantages over plastic stents (PS), particularly in stent patency time (2.1-fold longer patency duration), total complications (63–87% risk reduction), postoperative cholangitis, stent occlusion, stent migration and reintervention rates. More prospective multicenter RCTs evaluating hybrid approaches such as MS with intraluminal brachytherapy and next-generation stent technologies are urgently needed. Standardization of drainage adequacy metrics and complication reporting frameworks will enhance future evidence synthesis.
