Efficacy of individual increment on house dust mite sublingual immunotherapy in low-response patients with allergic rhinitis
Authors: Jie Qi, Beina Liu, Chong Shuai, Zhihuai Dong, Mang Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the clinical improvement of the incremental dosage regimen in allergic rhinitis (AR) patients with low response to sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT). This retrospective study included 65 AR patients with low response to dust mite SLIT after 6-month treatment. Patients were divided into regular-dose (RD) group (n = 23) and high-dose (HD) group (n = 42). The RD group maintained the previous standard dose, while the HD group received the higher tolerated dose and further categorized into 2 subgroups based on increased doses. Total nasal symptoms score (TNSS), total medication score (TMS), combined symptom and medication score (CSMS), and visual analog scale were compared at baseline, 6 months, and 1 year. Safety was assessed by reported adverse events (AEs).There were no significant differences between RD and HD groups at baseline and 6 months. However, patients in the HD group showed significantly lower TNSS, TMS, CSMS, and visual analog scale at 1 year compared to the RD group (all P <.01). Continued improvements in TNSS, TMS, and CSMS were only found in the HD group from 6-month to 1-year treatment (all P <.01). Moreover, there was no statistical difference between HD subgroups at any follow-up points. In addition, a higher proportion of patients in the HD group discontinued medication. No difference was observed in AEs between RD and HD groups. Dose increment after 6-month SLIT treatment could significantly enhance efficacy in low-response AR patients over a 1-year course without raising the risk of AEs.

1. Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is a prevalent inflammatory respiratory disease, affecting 10% to 40% of the global population.[1] In China, the prevalence of AR among adults has been reported to range from 9.8% to 23%, with a significant increase over the past decade.[2] House dust mite (HDM) is the predominant allergen that triggers AR, with quite a high sensitization rate in southern China.[3] As a public health concern, AR has brought great burden on people’s quality of life and socio-economic development, and effective medical interventions are expected to mitigate these adverse effects.

Treatments for AR include symptomatic medications and allergen-specific immunotherapy (AIT), the latter being the only disease-modifying modality that targets the patient’s immune response to allergens.[4,5] Sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT), a safe and convenient AIT route, features agile dosing as a liquid preparation, aligning with precision medicine.[6,7] In China, SLIT especially HDM-SLIT has shown notable short-term and long-term clinical benefits to AR patients.[8,9] Previous studies on SLIT have demonstrated a clear relationship between the maintenance dose of allergen and clinical efficiency,[10–12] indicating that the efficacy increases with the dose.[13]

Despite the excellent efficacy of SLIT, not all patients could achieve the expected and satisfactory outcomes. The effectiveness of SLIT varies from person to person, ranging from 50% to 80%.[8,14] In general, delivering a dose that is below the level needed for efficacy is not beneficial to patients. Because SLIT allows to adapt treatment by fine-turning the administered dose, it could be a promising strategy to offer optimal clinical improvement for patients. A recent study confirmed that the individual efficacy improved significantly through increasing the dose in low-response patients after 6-month HDM-SLIT.[15] Beyond alleviating rhinitis symptoms, increasing the dose also markedly reduced consumption of anti-allergic medications. However, clinical evidence based on dose adjustment for SLIT remains limited. This retrospective study particularly focused on AR patients with low response to HDM-SLIT, aiming to evaluate the efficacy improvement and safety of increasing the dose of HDM-SLIT in these patients over 1-year treatment, thereby strengthening the clinical evidence base for individual dose adjustment and providing actionable guidance for optimizing treatment regimens in low-response patients in real world.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design

The retrospective study was conducted at the Department of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital. Data from AR patients who started SLIT in the hospital from 2018 to 2023 were collected and analyzed. Patients were interviewed after 6 months of SLIT treatment, and those with low response to SLIT were collected and further divided into the regular-dose (RD) group and high-dose (HD) group. Low response was defined as that the combined symptom and medication score (CSMS) reduction rate varies between 20% and 50% when compared with the baseline. In the RD group, patients still received the regular dose during subsequent treatment, while patients in the HD group received the higher maintenance dose. According to the difference in the final maintenance dose, patients in the HD group were classified into HD3 and HD4 subgroups, and the final maintenance dose for patients in each subgroup was the individual maximum tolerated dose. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (acceptance number: 2024-2543-01) and in accordance with Ethical Guidelines, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and relevant regulatory requirements.

2.2. Treatment regimen

Patients involved in the study received treatment with standardized Dermatophagoides farinae drops (Chanllergen; Zhejiang Wolwo-Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China) labeled from 1 to 5 with increasing allergen protein concentrations of 1, 10, 100, 333, and 1000 μg/mL, respectively. The drugs were administrated once a day at the regular time, and the drops should be held under the tongue for 1 to 3 minutes until swallowed.

All patients were treated according to the drug dosage schedule as shown in Table 1. Specifically, from the first to the third week of treatment, patients received specifications of No. 1 to 3 with escalating doses of 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, and 0.50 mL per day. Then, patients received the specification of No.4 with a dose of 0.15 mL per day for 2 weeks. From the sixth week, patients received No.5 with a maintenance dose of 0.10 mL per day until dose adjustment. After 6-month treatment and evaluation, patients in the RD group were administered the dose of 0.10 mL drops of No.5 per day, while patients in the HD group received the incremental regimen, with 0.15 and 0.20 mL drops of No.5 per day in the HD3 and HD4 subgroups, respectively. Patients were allowed to use oral/ nasal antihistamines and intranasal corticosteroids in case of aggravated symptoms and adverse events (AEs) during the whole treatment.

2.3. Study patients

Patients included in the study met the following criteria: patients were diagnosed with AR according to the ARIA guideline;[1] sensitization to Dermatophagoides farinae confirmed by positive skin prick test or positive serum-specific immunoglobulin E (IgE) detection (positive level ≥++); patients with a reduction rate of 20%–50% in CSMS (low response) after 6 months of treatment; have received SLIT for at least 1 years, and had complete and available information of follow-up visits; patients who possessed the comprehensive understanding of the objective and procedure of the therapy and signed informed consent before prescription. The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with moderate to severe asthma or uncontrolled asthma (FEV1 <70% of predicted value), autoimmune diseases, concomitant treatment with β-antagonists or angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitors. In addition, patients with <1 year of treatment follow-up visits or incomplete individual information at any time point during treatment were excluded from the study. We included to the analysis only complete variables.

2.4. Efficacy evaluation

Patients who received SLIT were asked to record all symptoms and medicine consumption at the first visit before treatment (baseline); meanwhile, all patients accepted follow-up visits in the hospital or online every 3 months. At each time point, the physician asked for and recorded the information on rhinitis symptoms and medicine consumption of each patient for the recent week, then calculated the daily average score of symptoms and medication. The efficacy of this study was assessed with total nasal symptoms score (TNSS), total medication score (TMS), CSMS, and visual analog scale (VAS) at 3-time points (baseline, 6 months, and 1 year after SLIT).

TNSS was defined as the sum of 4 nasal symptoms (nasal obstruction, nasal itching, sneezing, and rhinorrhea) scores, ranging from 0 to 12. The score of each symptom was assessed on a 4-point scale (0 = no symptoms, 1 = mild symptoms, 2 = moderate symptoms, and 3 = severe symptoms).[16]

TMS was assessed based on the daily symptomatic medication usage, which follows the principle: 0 = no medication usage, 1 = only antihistamines, 2 = nasal spray corticosteroids with/without antihistamines, 3 = oral corticosteroids with/without antihistamines, or nasal spray corticosteroids.[17] The maximum of TMS was 3.

CSMS was defined as the sum of the average score of 4 nasal symptoms (nasal obstruction, nasal itching, sneezing, and rhinorrhea) and the total score of symptomatic medication, ranging from 0 to 6. The calculation formula is CSMS = TNSS/4 + TMS.[16]

VAS was a subjective measure to evaluate the severity of a patient’s symptoms scored using a 10 cm-scale ruler.[18] The 2 ends of the ruler were 0 and 10, corresponding to no symptoms and the most severe symptoms, respectively. Patients marked the severity of symptoms on the ruler according to their real feelings, and the number corresponding to the marked place was the VAS score.

2.5. Safety assessment

AEs experienced by patients were recorded through every follow-up visit during the treatment. All AEs were addressed under the recommendation of the physicians. The safety was assessed based on the occurrence rate and severity of AEs associated with SLIT drops.

2.6. Statistical Analysis

Data statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 20.0 software and data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 Software. All statistical difference was analyzed by nonparametric test. Values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. χ2 test was used to analyze the difference of categorical variables between groups. Intergroup comparison of efficacy was performed by the Manm-Whitney U test, and the Friedman M test was used for intragroup comparison at different time points. P < .05 was considered a significant difference.

3.2. Comparison of efficacy between RD and HD group

No statistically significant difference in CSMS, TNSS, TMS, and VAS was observed at 6 months of SLIT treatment between the RD and HD groups (Fig. 2). However, following the increment of maintenance dose in the RD group after 6-month treatment, significant differences in these clinical outcomes were observed between the 2 groups after 1-year treatment (all P <.01), with a lower level of CSMS, TNSS, TMS, and VAS in the HD group (CSMS: 1.11 ± 1.02; TNSS: 3.38 ± 2.42; TMS: 0.26 ± 0.59; VAS: 3.10 ± 2.02) than the RD group (CSMS: 2.77 ± 0.69; TNSS: 5.35 ± 1.34; TMS: 1.43 ± 0.66; VAS: 5.35 ± 1.07). In both groups, CSMS, TNSS, TMS, and VAS exhibited significant decreases at 6-month treatment compared to baseline (all P <.01). In the RD group, significant improvement was observed in VAS after 1-year treatment compared to 6-month (P <.01), whereas no significant improvements were observed in other scores. However, CSMS, TNSS, TMS, and VAS in the HD group all obtained significant continuous improvements after 1 year of treatment from 6 months (all P <.001).

3.3. Symptomatic medication usage

Patients in the RD group and HD group all had taken symptomatic medications before the treatment. After 6-month treatment, the percentages of patients who did not receive symptomatic medications were 8.7% (2/23) and 28.6% (12/42) in the RD group and HD group, respectively, and no significant difference was observed between the 2 groups (Fig. 3; P = .062). The percentage significantly increased to 81.0% (34/42) in the HD group after 1-year treatment (P <.001), while it was still 8.7% (2/23) in the RD group. There was a significant difference in the percentage of medication discontinuation between the 2 groups after 1 year of treatment (P <.001), with a higher discontinuation in the HD group than in the RD group.

3.4. Comparison of efficacy between subgroups in the HD group

To evaluate the efficacy of different maintenance doses in the HD group, further comparison was performed between the HD3 and HD4 subgroups. In both subgroups, CSMS, TNSS, TMS, and VAS were significantly lower at any visits within 1-year treatment than those of baseline (Fig. 4; all P <.05), and these scores showed a significant decrease from 6 months to 1 year (P <.01). Importantly, there was no significant difference in CSMS, TNSS, TMS, and VAS between the HD3 subgroup and HD4 subgroup at baseline and follow-ups during treatment.

3.5. Safety

Out of the 65 patients who underwent SLIT treatment for AR, a total of 7 (13.8%) patients experienced 9 cases of AEs (Table 3). For the RD group, there were 3 cases of AEs occurred in 3 patients. For the HD group, there were 4 patients reported 6 cases of AEs, including 3 (50%) occurred in the escalation phase and 3 (50%) occurred during the initiation of individualized dose adjustment in the maintenance phase. No serious AEs or epinephrine were reported during treatment. Most of the AEs were local adverse reactions which manifested as oral numbness, tongue swelling, local skin eczema, and rhinitis aggravation. All of the AEs were mild and moderate, and the majority of them could be resolved within a week without specific intervention. Others could be also alleviated through the administration of antihistamines. There were no significant differences in the incidence and severity of AEs between the RD group and HD group (P = .661).

4. Discussion

This study analyzed the dose effect and safety of SLIT in low-response patients with AR, and the key results are as follows: no significant differences in CSMS, TNSS, TMS, or VAS scores were seen between the RD and HD groups at 6-month SLIT. However, after 6-month dose increment, the HD group showed significant improvements in these outcomes at 1 year, outperforming the RD group. In addition, safety was consistently preserved even with the increased dose.

SLIT is an effective approach for respiratory allergic diseases, especially AR. Since the 1980s, a large number of clinical trials and meta-analyses of SLIT have been conducted. These studies have demonstrated that SLIT exerts a positive impact on alleviating rhinitis symptoms. Beyond symptom relief, SLIT also helps reduce the use of medications and improve quality of life.[19–21] It is a fact that different patients exhibit varying different response thresholds and tolerance to immunotherapy with the same allergen.[22] Because SLIT is an etiological treatment approach to follow precision medicine, it enables to tailor medical needs to align with the profile of individual patients, thereby ensuring optimal clinical outcomes.[23] In a retrospective study involving French allergists, about 95% of physicians adjusted the dose of SLIT in about 20% of patients during the maintenance phase.[6] In addition, several researchers have explored the relationship between the maintenance dose of SLIT and clinical efficacy.[12,13,24]

Some clinical practices at home and abroad have confirmed that the standard registered dose administration of SLIT may not effectively control some patients’ symptoms, but increasing the dosage can result in enhanced clinical improvement.[25] Didier et al reported that a higher dose of SLIT effectively alleviated the symptoms of rhinoconjunctivitis in patients, whereas a low dose proved to be ineffective.[26] According to clinical practice experience in China, most patients achieve early efficacy of SLIT within 6 months, and dosage adjustment at this point can facilitate rapid clinical improvement for low-response patients. Gao et al observed a significant improvement in CSMS by increasing the dosage administered to low-response patients at 6-month SLIT treatment, and there was no significant difference in CSMS between the low-response and high-response groups after 2 years.[15] Our study also individualized dose increment in low-response patients with AR after 6 months of SLIT. At 6 months, there was no significant difference in efficacy between the RD and HD groups, while a significant difference was observed following 1 year of treatment. It is noteworthy that TNSS, TMS, CSMS, and VAS in the HD group showed significant improvement after 1 year of treatment compared to 6 months. In addition, we addressed the limitations mentioned in the previous study[15] by setting up a low-response group without dose adjustments for control analysis, thus better demonstrating the benefit of individualized increments for improved efficacy in low-response patients.

The healthcare expenditure incurred by allergic diseases, including AR, places a heavy burden on patients and society, and AIT can provide long-term benefits by reducing the costs of hospitalization and symptomatic medication.[27,28] Previous studies have shown that the proportion of patients not taking antihistamines and/or nasal steroids increased with the duration of SLIT treatment.[29] In our study, the same results were observed in the HD group. The proportion of patients discontinuing medication in the HD group continued to increase throughout treatment, reaching 81.0% after 1 year of treatment, which was a nearly 2-fold increase from 6 months, whereas the proportion of patients discontinuing medication in the RD group after 1 year of treatment did not change significantly from that at 6 months and was only 8.7%. These results suggested that the proportion of medical discontinuation in low-response patients can be effectively increased by increasing the dose, thus reducing medication costs.

Previous trials have shown that SLIT has a dose-response relationship, with efficacy increasing with increasing dose; however, our results showed no significant difference in efficacy improvement between the different subgroups in the incremental group (HD3: 50% increment; HD4: 100% increment), which is consistent with the results reported by Wang et al.[30] This may be due to the fact that each individual received the maximum tolerated dose within the study design, which is considered to be the most effective.[6,9] Therefore, patients in each subgroup achieved the desired improvement in efficacy regardless of the final maintenance dose after the incremental dose. In summary, adjusting the dose of SLIT administered at 6 months based on the patient’s level of response to SLIT therapy resulted in better therapeutic outcomes, and after 1 year of treatment, more than 80% of patients no longer required symptomatic medications.

The safety of SLIT has been investigated and confirmed in a large number of clinical trials, and for studies involving dose adjustments, the safety profile after increasing the dose is also a key concern. Some studies have shown that a dose-dependent increase in efficacy was not associated with an increase in AEs,[15,31] whereas in other studies it could be seen that higher doses led to the occurrence of higher frequency and more severe AEs.[12] In our study, AEs experienced by patients in both groups were mild or moderate, mainly characterized by common tongue itching or swelling, with no serious AEs occurring during treatment, and there was no significant difference in the incidence and severity of AEs between the 2 groups. The dose increment did not increase the incidence and severity of AEs in patients, which was in line with the previous reports. This may be because the final dose in the HD group was the individual’s tolerated dose, maintaining a balance between effectiveness and safety.

This study has 4 limitations: first, the sample size is limited; second, the retrospective design may have risks of selection and information bias; third, the study excluded patients with incomplete follow-up information to ensure data quality, but this may reduce the representativeness of the study population; fourth, the single-center setting limits result generalizability to other regions. Consequently, our findings may not apply to all AR populations. Future research should use multicenter, prospective designs with larger sample sizes, and incorporate confounder adjustment to provide more robust evidence for personalized SLIT regimens in AR patients.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this retrospective study suggested that HD SLIT was associated with improved outcomes in low-response AR patients. During 1 year of SLIT treatment, increasing the dose at 6 months could significantly improve clinical symptoms and reduce the reliance on anti-allergic medications. This study provides valuable guidance for clinical practice regarding individualized dose adjustment in SLIT.
