Biomedical Applications of Nanozymes: An Enzymology Perspective
Authors: Vasily G Panferov, Xiaohan Zhang, Ka‐Ying Wong, Jung Heon Lee, Juewen Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanozymes are catalytic nanomaterials that transform enzyme substrates into their corresponding products, offering enhanced stability and a cost‐effective alternative to traditional enzymes. As nanomaterials, they possess unique physicochemical properties and catalytic mechanisms distinct from those of enzymes. Such differences have profound, yet often neglected, implications in biomedical applications. In the context of enzymology, this review compares nanozymes and enzymes, with a focus on redox reactions. This review begins with the classification of nanozymes based on the types of reactions they catalyze, with the ability to exhibit multiple catalytic activities being a prevalent characteristic. The use of the Michaelis‐Menten model for both enzymes and nanozymes is discussed in detail, and the Michaelis constant, maximum reaction rate, and turnover number values are compared. The performance of nanozymes in crowded environments and under extreme conditions is also compared to that of enzymes. We discuss the kinetic factors influencing nanozyme performance, the impact of active site shielding, and the activity under non‐physiological conditions. We then compiled recent trends in the biomedical applications of nanozymes, focusing on both the production and scavenging of reactive oxygen species. This review links fundamental enzymology to nanozyme catalysis, providing a key reference for the rational use of nanozymes.Keywords: Biosensors, Catalysis, Drug delivery, Kinetics, Nanozymes

Introduction

Nanozymes are nanomaterials that catalyze the conversion of enzyme substrates to products under physiologically relevant conditions, and some common substrates include glucose, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB).[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 ] While the concept of nanozymes is often traced to early reports of peroxidase‐like activity of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, and a Zn2+ complex immobilized on gold nanoparticles,[ 5 , 6 ] studies from decades ago had already demonstrated the catalytic activities of nanomaterials toward typical enzyme substrates.[ 7 , 8 , 9 ] With the emergence of the concept of nanozyme, this field has become one of the fastest‐growing research areas in nanoscience.[ 1 , 10 , 11 ] In 2022, nanozymes were recognized by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry as one of the Top Ten Emerging Technologies.[ 12 ]

Natural enzymes typically function within narrow physiological ranges of pH, temperature, and substrate concentration, rapidly losing activity outside these conditions. In contrast, nanozymes operate across a wider range, retaining catalytic activity from −20 °C to over 100 °C and tolerating substrate concentrations up to 10 M H2O2 nearly three orders of magnitude higher than typical peroxidases.[ 13 , 14 , 15 ] A unique advantage of nanozymes is their nanoscale dimensions. Nanozymes can be easily conjugated with enzymes, antibodies, or aptamers, expanding their functionality.[ 16 ] Individual nanozymes can be functionalized with hundreds of biomolecules, providing multivalent binding.[ 17 ] Therefore, nanozymes serve not only as enzyme substitutes in traditional areas like bioanalytical sensing,[ 18 ] environmental remediation,[ 19 ] and nanomedicine,[ 20 ] but also in roles beyond those of natural enzymes such as drug delivery via the enhanced permeability and retention effect[ 21 ] and catalysis under non‐physiological conditions.[ 22 ]

Most nanomaterials exhibit some degree of peroxidase‐like activity, and many also demonstrate other catalytic functions, such as hydrolase‐, catalase‐, and oxidase‐like behaviors.[ 23 ] Furthermore, a single nanomaterial can possess multiple types of enzymatic activity, which can be further modulated by the size, shape, porosity, and surface modification of the nanozyme. Most nanozymes operate via mechanisms fundamentally different from those of their enzymatic counterparts.[ 24 ] Peroxidase‐like activity in nanozymes may proceed via two distinct mechanisms: the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or direct electron transfer without ROS formation.[ 25 ] Peng and colleagues showed that iron oxide nanozymes mimic peroxidase activity through the generation of diffusive ROS,[ 26 ] whereas horseradish peroxidase (HRP) catalyzes reactions by confining highly oxidative species close to its active center. Hydroxyl radicals (•OH) are the most commonly detected ROS species produced by prooxidant nanozymes, although the formation of other species such as hydroperoxyl (HOO•), superoxide (O2 •−) has also been reported.[ 27 , 28 , 29 ] Due to their high reactivity, hydroxyl radicals can non‐specifically react with a wide range of organic molecules, raising concerns about the substrate specificity and potential toxicity of such catalysts for biomedical applications. Therefore, considering the mechanistic and kinetic differences between enzymes and nanozymes is crucial for their appropriate use and is the central focus of the present article.

In this article, biomedical applications refer to both therapeutic applications and biosensing. Although many studies compared nanozymes and enzymes in terms of activity,[ 1 ] discussion of fundamental enzymology aspects and their implications to biomedical applications is lacking. This review aims to address this critical gap by comparing nanozymes and enzymes in terms of activity, kinetic behavior, and mechanistic insights. By compiling key properties of nanozymes, such as its composition, therapeutic mechanism and surface modification, from over 100 recent publications, we highlight how these distinctions carry significant implications for biomedical applications.

Classification of Nanozymes

Natural enzymes are traditionally categorized into seven classes: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases, and translocases. Based on this classification, more than 95% of reported nanozymes mimic oxidoreductase activity.[ 23 ] Thus, nanozymes remain limited in the diversity of reaction types compared to natural enzymes. Within the oxidoreductase category, the most common nanozyme activities include peroxidase (POD)‐, oxidase (OXD)‐, catalase (CAT)‐, and superoxide dismutase (SOD)‐like activities (Figure 1a). Considering nanozymes as a class of enzyme‐mimicking agents, the predominant focus on oxidoreductase activities is not common. In contrast, artificial enzymes have been developed to catalyze a broader range of reactions, including hydrolysis (for both small molecules[ 30 ] and high‐molecular weight substrates[ 31 ]), amination of keto acids,[ 32 ] and various other enzyme‐like transformations.[ 33 ] For many artificial enzymes, significant progress has been made in understanding their reaction mechanisms and in the rational design of functional groups. In contrast, nanozymes have not yet reached this level of reaction diversity or mechanistic understanding. For example, while hydrolase‐mimicking nanozymes are of practical interest (e.g., for carbohydrate hydrolysis in biovalorization processes), their further development requires fundamental studies on catalysis at the interface between rigid nanoparticles and high‐molecular‐weight molecules, including investigations into catalytic specificity, the effect of substrate crystallinity, and various other influencing factors.

The predominance of oxidoreductase‐like nanozymes can be attributed to the inherent accessibility for many multivalent metal‐based nanomaterials for redox reactions. Using POD nanozymes as an example, many types of materials exhibit such activity (Figure 1b). In addition, oxidoreductase activities address important practical needs. For example, nanozymes with peroxidase‐like activity are frequently proposed as functional alternatives to HRP in bioassays and as pro‐ or antioxidant agents in therapeutic applications.[ 27 ] Furthermore, experimental procedures for evaluating POD‐like activity are simple using commercial chromogenic substrates such as TMB, 2,2′‐azinobis [3‐ethylbenzothiazoline‐6‐sulfonic acid]‐diammonium salt (ABTS), and o‐phenylenediamine (OPD). For the most common POD, OXD, CAT, and SOD‐like activities, the substrates are similar (Figure 1c). Below, we compare nanozymes with enzymes in the context of enzymology, and relate this discussion to their biomedical applications.

Nanozyme and Enzyme Catalysis in a Kinetic View

A meaningful, “apple‐to‐apple” comparison of enzymes and nanozymes must account for a fundamental difference in their catalysis: enzymes function as homogeneous catalysts in solution, whereas nanozymes typically act as heterogeneous catalysts at solid–liquid interfaces. Consequently, distinct kinetic models have traditionally been used to describe these systems. Enzyme catalysis is described by the Michaelis‐Menten (MM) model, which is based on several assumptions regarding reagent concentrations and rate‐limiting stages. Because nanozymes are often developed as functional enzyme substitutes, MM kinetics has been commonly applied to characterize them, although nanozyme catalysis is fundamentally heterogeneous and often follows different reaction mechanisms.

MM kinetics, the most prevailing model for enzyme catalysis, was originally formulated based on Victor Henri's early work on the hydrolysis of sucrose by invertase. The MM model describes a reaction mechanism in which an enzyme (E) binds reversibly to a substrate (S) to form an enzyme–substrate complex (ES), which then undergoes a catalytic transformation to form an enzyme–product complex (EP), ultimately releasing the product (P) and regenerating the free enzyme. The model (Equation 1) quantifies the relationship between substrate concentration [S] and reaction rate v, and introduces two key parameters—the Michaelis constant (KM ) and the maximum reaction rate (Vmax ). KM represents the substrate concentration at which the reaction rate reaches half of Vmax , often interpreted as an indicator of substrate affinity.

While this model has been widely applied to characterize both enzymes and nanozymes, its foundational assumptions, such as uniform catalyst distribution, rapid equilibrium and steady‐state formation of ES, and homogeneous conditions, may not fully hold for nanozymes. In heterogeneous nanozyme catalysis, the reaction rate is influenced by substrate adsorption, surface diffusion, and desorption processes. Nevertheless, most industrial applications today involve immobilized enzymes that function via heterogeneous catalysis. In such cases, apparent kinetic parameters from the MM model are still useful even if they differ from the intrinsic parameters of soluble enzymes.[ 35 ] Naidja et al. used the MM model to describe the non‐enzymatic oxidation of catechol by δ‐MnO2 before the term “nanozyme” was coined.[ 36 ] Although the catalytic rate was three to four orders of magnitude lower than that of natural tyrosinase, the data fit well with the MM kinetics. In today's terms, δ‐MnO2 would be considered an OXD‐mimicking nanozyme. A functional comparison of enzymes and nanozymes in the framework of MM kinetics was introduced by Gao et al., who compared Fe3O4 and HRP.[ 5 ] Since then, the MM model has been widely adopted in nanozyme research and it remains a pragmatic and informative approach for optimizing nanozyme performance.

The use of MM kinetics for nanozymes has faced justified criticism, particularly when applied without a clear understanding of the underlying catalytic mechanism or quantification of active sites.[ 37 ] A good fit of experimental data to the MM equation does not imply that the reaction mechanism follows homogeneous catalysis or enzyme‐like mechanisms.[ 38 ] Heterogeneous nanozymes may also exhibit bell‐shaped pH and temperature optima, similar to enzymes, and can still be fitted with MM kinetics.[ 39 , 40 ] Nanozymes function as heterogeneous catalysts, and therefore, kinetic models such as the Langmuir–Hinshelwood and Langmuir–Rideal mechanisms are more appropriate than the traditional MM model used for enzymes.[ 41 , 42 ] Both models account for the adsorption of at least one substrate molecule to the surface of a catalyst.[ 43 ] The Langmuir–Hinshelwood model assumes that in bimolecular reactions, both substrates adsorb onto adjacent active sites on the catalyst surface, react to form a product, and then desorb. The rate‐limiting step can be the adsorption of a reactant, the surface reaction, or the desorption of the product. Consequently, the reaction order with respect to each substrate can vary. Although initially developed for gas‐phase reactions, this model can be adapted to solution‐phase systems under certain assumptions.[ 44 ] Gu et al. experimentally demonstrated that the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model accurately describes the POD activity of a Pt nanozyme.[ 45 ] Sun et al. applied the same model to describe catalytic activities in Au nanorods, Pt nanoparticles, and Pt‐coated Au nanorods.[ 46 ] Önal described the glucose oxidase‐like activity of Au nanoparticles using a Langmuir–Rideal mechanism, in which glucose adsorbs to the nanoparticles followed by oxidized by O2 to form gluconic acid and H2O2.[ 39 ] For nanozymes, the rate‐limiting step may not be product formation, but could instead be substrate adsorption[ 47 ] or product desorption.[ 48 , 49 ]

KM in Nanozyme and Enzyme Catalysis

KM values are frequently reported for nanozymes and compared not only across different nanozymes but also against enzymes. The most extensively studied system is POD‐mimicking nanozymes, which involve two substrates—H2O2 and an electron donor (e.g., TMB, ABTS, or DAB).[ 50 ] For POD activity, KM for both substrates must be estimated. A bibliographic analysis reveals considerable dispersion of reported KM values among published nanozymes, with no clear correlation to the type of material used (Figure 2a, Table S1). This suggests that KM is not solely a property of the nanoparticle material but also depends on factors like size, shape, and surface functionalization.[ 51 , 52 ]

Many articles compare KM values of nanozymes to those of natural enzymes and interpret lower KM values as evidence of superior catalytic efficiency. In enzymology, low KM values are important for in vivo catalysis, where substrates are typically in the sub‐mM to µM range rate, and KM should be comparable with the typical substrate concentrations to ensure efficient catalysis. However, for nanozymes, such evolutionary logic is not applicable. KM for nanozymes does not directly reflect binding affinity unless the surface is engineered to recognize the substrate.[ 53 , 54 ] Nonetheless, the apparent KM value remains critical for optimizing nanozyme catalysis in various applications.

In some articles, authors claim the superiority of nanozymes based solely on a lower KM value. However, KM alone does not reflect overall catalytic activity. In most analytical applications, where nanozymes are used as functional replacements for enzymes, substrate concentrations are not physiologically constrained and can be optimized for nanozyme catalysis. Thus, nanozymes with high KM values can still be used for catalytic signal amplification and facilitate highly sensitive detection, but they require significantly higher H2O2 concentrations compared to HRP.[ 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 60 ] We demonstrated that HRP and Au@Pt nanozymes show significantly different KM values for H2O2 (0.38 and 540 mM, respectively, Figure 2b).[ 61 ] While HRP is inhibited by H2O2 concentrations higher than 1 mM, the Au@Pt nanozyme can tolerate up to 4 M H2O2. Using a low H2O2 concentration for nanozymes (although perfectly suitable for HRP) led to up to 30‐fold worse detection limit in some cases.[ 61 , 62 ]

In therapeutic applications, POD nanozymes generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, making them promising pro‐oxidant agents for killing tumor cells and pathogens. Physiologically relevant concentrations of H2O2 in cancer cells are indicated in Figure 2a with a blue arrow.[ 63 , 64 , 65 ] Most nanozymes have higher KM values (1–100 mM) and therefore catalyze reactions under kinetically limited conditions in biological systems (e.g., 0.1%—1% of Vmax ).

To overcome KM ‐related kinetic limitations due to substrate concentration, additional H2O2 may be introduced. Wang et al. used Co–V mixed metal nanozymes with POD and OXD activities for killing bacterial cells and confirmed a direct relationship between H2O2 concentration and antibacterial activity.[ 68 ] Without extra H2O2, relatively weak OXD activity led to low ROS generation and poor antibacterial performance. Adding 50 µM H2O2 achieved high antibacterial activity. Such supplementation with exogenous H2O2 is often reported as necessary to improve therapeutic outcomes.[ 29 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 ] For some biomedical applications, nanozymes with relatively high KM values can still be effectively used, such as in wound disinfection or biofilm treatment.[ 69 , 73 , 74 , 75 ] Gao et al. used 1% H2O2 (≈300 mM) together with the POD Fe3O4 nanozymes to degrade Streptococcus mutans biofilm for dental applications.[ 76 ]

Some applications used self‐generating H2O2. For example, AuNPs catalyzed glucose oxidation forming gluconic acid and H2O2 (Figure 2c, I).[ 66 ] Gluconic acid reduced the pH of the medium, enhancing POD activity that also used the generated H2O2 (Figure 2c, II). From a kinetic perspective, insufficient H2O2 generation may limit assay sensitivity because the second reaction would be kinetically constrained. The authors reported a KM for POD activity of ≈120 mM, indicating the need for a prolonged first reaction. Indeed, detectable TMB concentrations were observed only after 3 h of catalytic reaction (Figure 2c, III). The total reaction time was 7 h, producing TMB with an absorbance of ≈0.6, a result typical for a 1 min HRP reaction at the nanogram scale.[ 77 , 78 ] Using more active nanozymes or enzymes would still require at least 20 min of incubation.[ 79 , 80 , 81 ] Li et al. utilized a similar principle of in situ H2O2 generation by glucose oxidase and subsequent ROS formation by a POD‐mimicking Fe ionic covalent organic framework.[ 67 ] The authors confirmed antibacterial activity of the nanozyme both embedded in a hydrogel (Figure 2d, I) and in solution (Figure 2d, II). Without glucose (Figure 2d, II, bar 3), the nanozyme showed no antibacterial activity, and antibacterial activity was significantly improved when glucose was added (Figure 2d, II, bar 6).

For intracellular or intratissue applications, nanozymes with KM values comparable to those of enzymes or even lower are desirable,[ 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 ] although this is often challenging to achieve. Using MM kinetics and typical intracellular H2O2 concentrations (50–100 µM), we estimate that the in vivo reaction rate for nanozymes with KM ≥ 100 mM would be only 0.01%–0.05% of the in vitro reaction rate. The observed therapeutic effects likely arise not solely from the kinetically limited pro‐oxidant activity, but also from combined activities such as glutathione oxidase‐mimicking, antioxidant functions (CAT, SOD), and the organism's own stress responses.[ 87 , 88 ]

Vmax , kcat and Specific Activity in Nanozyme and Enzyme Catalysis

Since Vmax depends on the concentration of the nanozyme, this parameter is determined does not reflect the intrinsic activity. Even a low‐activity nanozyme, if measured at sufficiently high concentrations, can exhibit a higher Vmax than HRP. Thus, comparing Vmax values across different studies does not often provide meaningful information, although Vmax can be useful for optimizing parameters such as size, shape, morphology within a single study. To address this limitation, kcat = Vmax /[E] is typically reported.

The turnover number (kcat ) determines the rate of the reaction at saturating substrate concentration, recalculated per catalytic unit ([E]). Using kcat ensures more accurate comparison of catalytic activities between enzymes and nanozymes. However, quantifying kcat is challenging for nanozymes. While Vmax can be experimentally measured, the determination of [E] is often debatable.[ 89 , 90 ] In general, there are four approaches for estimating [E] (Figure 3a).

The first approach uses the concentration of nanoparticles (Figure 3a, I), with each nanoparticle considered a single catalytic unit. The concentration of particles can be calculated or experimentally measured.[ 84 ] However, even small nanoparticles may contain thousands of surface atoms that could potentially serve as active centers, leading to significant overestimation of true catalytic turnover. The second approach uses the total concentration of atoms (Figure 3a, II), but the inclusion of atoms inside particles (do not involve in catalysis) can lead to underestimation of catalytic turnover.[ 77 ] Activities calculated using this method are accurate only for single‐atom nanozymes and certain nanozymes with special geometries, such as 2D materials and nanorods. The third approach uses the total number of surface‐exposed atoms (Figure 3a, III), which is more reasonable since only surface atoms participate in catalysis. The fourth approach calculates activity based only on the number of atoms directly involved in catalysis (Figure 3a, IV). However, no technique can unambiguously quantify the number of active atoms on nanozyme surfaces (chemisorption is probably the most relevant technique for this purpose).[ 91 ]

Using our data for Pt nanoparticles, we observed dramatic differences in kcat values calculated by different approaches (Figure 3b). For Vmax = 1 µM s−1 and a fixed Pt concentration (25 µM), kcat varied by up to five orders of magnitude. This matches calculations by Zandieh et al, who also reported such discrepancies.[ 90 ] When each nanoparticle was considered a catalytic unit (Figure 3a, I), kcat values were comparable to those of HRP. However, recalculation based on surface atoms showed kcat values that were three to four orders of magnitude lower. Fan et al. reported kcat values for a POD‐mimicking Ru nanozyme using approaches I and III (Figure 3a), and the results differed by two to three orders of magnitude.[ 92 ] Wei et al. compared three approaches (Figure 3a, I–III) for kcat calculation for a Pt nanozyme.[ 69 ] As the Pt particles were small (3–5 nm) with a high fraction of surface‐exposed atoms, kcat values calculated using approaches II and III were similar. However, the kcat value calculated using the first approach was more than two orders of magnitude higher. The authors advocated for the third approach as the most accurate for comparison of heterogeneous catalysts. Garcia‐Peiro et al. compared three approaches for kcat calculation (Figure 3a, I–III) for glucose oxidase‐mimicking activity of AuPt and Pt nanoparticles.[ 93 ] The kcat value calculated using the first approach reached 105–106 s−1, whereas the third approach provided more realistic values of approximately 10 s−1.

Calculating the kcat value of nanozymes remains a trade‐off between available methodologies and underlying assumptions. Notably, many publications did not clearly describe the method used, making comparison challenging. Clear reporting of kcat calculation methods and the inclusion of raw data will ensure reproducibility and enable accurate comparison of activities.

The specific activity of an enzyme is a derivative parameter from MM kinetics. One enzyme activity unit (U) is defined as the amount of enzyme that produces 1 µM of product in 1 min under optimal conditions. Similarly, the specific activity of nanozyme is calculated per milligram of nanoparticles (U mg−1).[ 94 , 95 ] Measurements of specific activity require conducting catalysis under optimal conditions; therefore, KM should be determined beforehand. Compared to KM and kcat , specific activity is reported relatively rarely for nanozymes (Figure 3c, Table S2). As is well known for enzymes, specific activity values can vary significantly depending on the substrate. Since specific activity is calculated using the total mass of the nanoparticles, it includes materials that does not participate in catalysis.

Literature analysis confirms that most specific activity values for nanozymes (Figure 3c, black dots) are lower than those of enzymes (shown by the region above the green dotted line). We separately analyzed data for single‐atom nanozymes and platinum group metal nanozymes. While single‐atom nanozymes have high efficiency in utilizing catalytic metals, they also include the mass of the catalyst support, leading to only a modest overall increase in average specific activity (16.7 U mg−1) compared to platinum group metal nanozymes (2.9 U mg−1).[ 96 ]

Specificity of Nanozyme Catalysis

In enzyme catalysis, various types of specificity exist, and the most relevant ones for oxidoreductases include reaction specificity and substrate specificity.[ 97 ] Reaction specificity refers to the ability of a catalyst to catalyze a specific type of reaction (e.g., POD activity without OXD), while substrate specificity defines the ability to catalyze a reaction using a particular substrate over others. When comparing the catalytic specificity of enzymes and nanozymes, both reaction and substrate specificities of nanozymes are typically lower. This is usually explained by the long evolutionary history of enzymes, which has led to highly optimized active sites for specific substrate binding (high substrate specificity) and transformation into specific products (high reaction specificity). Engineering nanozymes with high specificity has important implications for applications. High specificity minimizes off‐target reactions, reducing potential side effects and enhancing therapeutic selectivity. In bioanalytical assays, high specificity ensures accurate detection of the target analyte, avoiding false positives from structurally similar molecules.

For a quantitative evaluation of reaction and substrate specificities, kcat /KM can be compared.[ 98 ] Enzymes that function effectively at low substrate concentrations (low KM ) are considered efficient. Although this approach has limitations, particularly when comparing different enzymes with the same substrate,[ 99 ] it has been widely adopted in the literature. The difficulties associated with calculating kcat for nanozymes (Section 3.2) make the use of kcat /KM less popular for nanozymes. Nevertheless, this ratio remains a valuable descriptor when assessing specificity within the same study, especially when different substrates are compared using the same nanozyme.

Reaction Specificity

Despite the perception of excellent specificity for enzymes, they can still catalyze additional (often unexpected) reactions. For example, o‐succinylbenzoate synthase (EC 4.2.1.113), a carbon‐oxygen lyase, catalyzes not only its canonical dehydration reaction but also racemization of N‐acyl amino acids (EC 5.1.1) with a kcat /KM ratio three orders of magnitude lower.[ 100 ] As shown by Njuma et al.[ 101 ] an enzyme from Mycobacterium tuberculosis demonstrated both catalase (kcat ≈ 8 × 103 s−1, KM ≈ 3 mM) and peroxidase activity (kcat ≈ 10 s−1, KM ≈ 0.27 mM), with kcat /KM values differing by two orders of magnitude. Promiscuous catalase activity for HRP has also been reported.[ 102 , 103 , 104 ]

Nanozymes exhibit lower reaction specificity and often combine multiple catalytic activities (Figure 4a). Many also show simultaneous pro‐ and antioxidant activities, which complicates a clear understanding of their therapeutic mechanisms.[ 105 ] Unlike enzyme promiscuity, which is usually much lower than canonical activity, the multiactivity of nanozymes is often pronounced and functionally significant. Multiple articles have discussed the multiactivity of nanozymes and methods for their regulation.[ 10 , 97 , 106 , 107 , 108 ] A significant proportion of published studies focus exclusively on POD activity, often without assessing closely related functions such as OXD or CAT activity. In fact, many nanozymes categorized as POD‐mimicking may also possess OXD and CAT activities. We found that many metallic and metal oxide nanozymes exhibit simultaneous POD, OXD, and CAT activities. The relative OXD activity varies considerably, from less than 1% of POD activity (e.g., Au@Pt, Au@Pd) to 30%–50% for NiO.[ 34 ] For Mn2O3 nanozyme, the OXD activity even exceeds the POD activity, explaining previous observations of decreased catalytic activity for Mn oxide nanozymes in the presence of H2O2.[ 109 ] A detailed analysis of the simultaneous POD, OXD, CAT, and SOD activities of Pt nanozymes was reported by Cursi et al.[ 110 ] These findings underscore the need for more comprehensive characterization of nanozyme catalytic profiles, rather than assuming single‐function behavior based on initial screening results.

Implication of Reaction Specificity on Biomedical Applications

Unregulated enzyme promiscuity can lead to pathological conditions due to the generation of harmful reaction products.[ 111 ] Mechanisms to minimize harmful promiscuous activity have been evolved. From a kinetic perspective, promiscuous reactions often require substrate concentrations much higher or lower than those typically encountered in vivo, favoring canonical (desired) reactions. Thus, most enzymes have evolved to prioritize their primary activity. Similarly, biomedical applications of nanozymes with low reaction specificity may also lead to potentially harmful reactions, although sometimes they can be engineered to improve therapeutic outcomes. Han et al. used CeVO4 nanozyme with pH‐dependent SOD and POD activities for simultaneous cancer cell killing and repair of ionizing radiation‐damaged neuron cells.[ 112 ] In neuronal cells (with a near‐neutral pH), the nanozyme acted around mitochondria, exhibiting SOD antioxidant activity. In glioblastoma cells, the nanozyme accumulated in acidic lysosomes, activating its POD activity, leading to ROS generation and tumor cell damage. Fan et al. reported an N‐doped carbon nanozyme with simultaneous POD, OXD, CAT and SOD activities.[ 88 ] The POD and OXD activities generate ROS and induce cell death, while the CAT and SOD activities in opposite scavenge ROS and protect cells from toxic H2O2. The authors showed that the nanozyme accumulated in acidic lysosomal environments, favoring POD and OXD activities to damage cancer cells. The effect of multiactivity for therapeutic applications is more difficult to study due to the complexity of the biological environment, but it can be reasonably assumed that multiactivity may produce more pronounced problems given the chemical complexity of the in vivo systems.

For analytical applications, three main scenarios emerge regarding the impact of low reaction specificity (multiactivity) of nanozymes: In most cases, multiactivity has no significant impact. For example, in microplate‐based enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)‐like assays, lateral flow tests, or colorimetric sensors, nanozymes replace HRP as signal generators. In such assays, the presence of additional activities usually does not affect the analysis because the end‐point signal (signal from chromogenic substrates like DAB or TMB) remains the same regardless of the reaction mechanism. In the second scenario, multiactivity can negatively affect the results. CAT activity of Au@Pt nanozymes led to excessive O2 formation, interfering with optical readouts.[ 34 ] In assays relying on POD activity, the coexistence of OXD activity can lead to false positives. This occurs in direct H2O2 assays,[ 113 ] or cascade assays where H2O2 generation is proportional to the concentration of analytes like glucose,[ 114 ] choline,[ 115 ] xanthine,[ 116 ] or cholesterol.[ 117 ] In these cases, nanozymes with OXD activity may oxidize TMB directly, bypassing the intended H2O2‐dependent reaction. In the third scenario, some assays rely directly on multiactivity, notably cascade assays. In cascade assays, the product of the first reaction serves as the substrate for the second reaction.[ 118 ] The most common example is OXD activity of the first nanozyme generating H2O2, which is then consumed by a POD nanozyme to generate a detectable signal.[ 80 ]

Substrate Specificity and its Impact on Biomedical and Analytical Applications

Enzymes in general have high substrate specificity (i.e., one enzyme–one substrate), although many oxidoreductases and hydrolases have broader substrate specificity, which can be beneficial from an evolutionary perspective. For example, cytochrome P450 enzymes (oxidases) and glutathione S‐transferases catalyze detoxification reactions involving a wide range of xenobiotics. Notable examples include the bioactivation of a broad range of non‐toxic or low‐toxicity compounds by cytochrome P450 enzymes.[ 119 ] HRP also has very broad substrate specificity, oxidizing various natural polyphenols and synthetic substrates.[ 120 ] Another example is laccase, which naturally oxidizes various polyphenols.[ 121 ] Thus, enzymes have evolved substrate specificity sufficient for their biological functions.

The Fenton mechanism is commonly utilized by POD‐ and OXD‐like nanozymes,[ 25 ] and ROS are expected to react with a broad spectrum of substrates, resulting in broad substrate specificity. He et al. reported that Pt‐chondroitin sulfate nanozyme showed OXD‐like catalytic activity for TMB, N,N‐diethyl‐p‐phenylenediamine, 4‐aminoantipyrine and N‐ethyl‐N‐(3‐sulfopropyl)‐3‐methylaniline sodium salt, ABTS, and OPD.[ 122 ] Komkova et al. reported multiple substrates for the POD‐like activity of Prussian blue nanoparticles.[ 123 ] Carboxymethylcellulose‐modified Pt nanozyme with laccase‐like activity also oxidized various phenolic compounds.[ 124 ] Similarly, Mn–guanosine monophosphate nanozymes can oxidize diverse phenols and degrade various dyes,[ 125 ] and Ir nanoparticles oxidized DAB, ABTS, OPD, and TMB.[ 126 ]

Only a few studies have reported nanozymes with high substrate specificity. Li et al. reported exceptional substrate specificity in a laccase‐mimicking SA CuZn‐N/C nanozyme.[ 127 ] The authors speculated that the distance between metal ions facilitated specific binding and oxidation of epinephrine, whereas other commonly used substrates (dopamine, 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, noradrenaline) were not oxidized. They leveraged this high substrate specificity for continuous monitoring of epinephrine in the brain. To enhance substrate specificity in nanozymes, strategies include optimization of the active center to mimic natural enzyme active sites[ 128 , 129 , 130 ] and molecular imprinting of the substrate (Figure 4b).[ 53 , 131 , 132 ]

For biomedical applications, substrate specificity of nanozymes should be considered from a kinetic perspective. While multiple substrates might be present, the reaction rate will be determined by the K M for each substrate and its concentration. For analytical applications, the broad substrate specificity of nanozymes needs to be carefully considered during substrate optimization. Taking colorimetric assays as an example, the choice of substrate depends on the nanozyme's kcat for the substrate, the extinction coefficient (ε, M−1 cm−1) of the product, and the background noise.[ 50 ] Optimization of the substrate composition[ 133 ] and selection of substrates based on the assay format (e.g., insoluble products for paper‐based tests[ 134 ]) are essential.

Specific Features of Nanozyme Catalysis in Biological Systems

The real‐world biological environment, such as intracellular conditions or bodily fluids, is far more complex than the idealized conditions of a test‐tube assay. Factors such as macromolecular crowding and biomolecular corona formation can dramatically influence nanozyme performance. Almost all published studies measure the kinetic parameters of nanozymes in pure buffers, largely ignoring the matrix effects on nanozyme catalysis. This contrasts with enzymes, for which matrix effects have been extensively studied. Many reports document significant changes in both KM and Vmax under real‐world conditions.[ 135 , 136 ] These considerations are crucial for translating nanozyme catalytic performance from in vitro tests to practical biomedical applications.

Macromolecular Crowding

Macromolecular crowding refers to the high concentration of macromolecules, especially proteins, in biological fluids, which impacts molecular interactions, diffusion, and catalytic activity. For example, typical concentrations of soluble proteins in serum range from 60 to 80 mg mL−1s, and Escherichia. coli cytoplasm contains over 100 mg mL−1 of proteins.[ 137 , 138 ] For enzymes, macromolecular crowding can have opposing effects. On one hand, crowding significantly slows diffusion, potentially decreasing catalytic activity. On the other hand, crowding can shift equilibrium toward association, which may increase activity for some enzymes, particularly those that require subunit association for activity. For instance, Zimmerman et al. demonstrated increased activity of two DNA ligases under crowding conditions induced by polymers and proteins.[ 139 ] Conversely, Aumiller et al. reported decreased HRP activity in crowded conditions.[ 140 ] Notably, the impact of crowding varied depending on the substrate (TMB vs. OPD), highlighting the complexity of crowding effects, which can differentially influence KM and Vmax (both increasing, both decreasing, or one increasing while the other decreasing).[ 141 ]

When applied to nanozymes, another layer of complexity emerges. In addition to the crowding effects seen with enzymes (such as excluded volume and slowed diffusion), the active sites on the nanozyme surface can be shielded by matrix components. This effect is unique to nanozymes and is observed even at very low concentrations of matrix components. Based on our estimations, even a 10 000‐fold diluted human serum contains enough proteins to cover the nanozyme surface and reduce its activity.[ 141 ] This shielding of active sites underscores the need for surface functionalization strategies to reduce biomolecular corona formation.[ 142 ] For example, coating the nanozyme surface with bovine serum albumin significantly reduces the impact of surface shielding (Figure 5a). The lowest inhibition was observed for HRP (Figure 5a), where the activity was only slowed due to diffusion kinetics. Cong et al. conducted a detailed analysis of protein corona formation upon incubation of Au@Pt rod‐shaped nanozymes in plasma.[ 143 ] Binding of proteins resulted in the decrease of POD, OXD and CAT activities (Figure 5b). Fibrous proteins formed mesh‐like structures on the nanoparticle surfaces, causing greater inhibition of all types of catalytic activity compared to globular proteins.

Practically, when the macromolecular crowding and surface shielding effects are considered, the commonly used protocols for kinetic measurements in pure buffer likely overestimate the catalytic activity of nanozymes under real‐world conditions. Therefore, researchers are urged to evaluate nanozyme catalysis under more realistic conditions (e.g., by creating macromolecular crowding or using conjugated nanozymes), to obtain accurate activity estimates. Finally, as reported above, many nanozymes exhibit simultaneous multiple catalytic activities. Theoretically, crowding conditions may favor the promiscuous activity of nanozymes due to high local concentrations of unintended substrates (e.g., glucose, glutathione, ascorbate, nucleic acids, etc.).

Intracellular and Microenvironment Factors

For biomedical applications, the chemical and physical conditions of the microenvironment where nanozymes are intended to operate also need to be considered. Below, we briefly discuss the main factors that affect the kinetic behavior of nanozymes.

First, the pH of the tissue microenvironment may differ from the optimal conditions for nanozyme activity. Typically, POD nanozymes are most active in acidic environments (pH 3–5), while CAT and SOD nanozymes have higher pH optima.[ 145 ] Within cells, the pH of various organelles also differs significantly. For instance, lysosomes have a pH around 4.5, whereas the pH inside mitochondria is approximately 8.0. Tumor cells tend to have more acidic intracellular environments. Wang et al. utilized nanozymes with POD, SOD, and CAT activities.[ 146 ] While SOD and CAT were employed in normal tissues to protect against ROS, POD activity was leveraged to generate ROS in the acidic environment of cancer cells. Chen et al. demonstrated that Fe2O3 and Fe3O4 nanozymes in the neutral cytosolic environment exhibited CAT‐like activity without producing hydroxyl radicals, whereas in the acidic pH of lysosomes, hydroxyl radicals were formed during POD‐like activity.[ 147 ] Zhang et al. reported a pH‐activatable OXD‐like activity in PtCo@graphene nanozymes for the selective treatment of Helicobacter pylori.[ 144 ] The nanozyme generated ROS in the acidic gastric environment but showed suppressed activity at the higher pH of the intestinal environments (Figure 5c). The authors confirmed selective bactericidal activity for H. pylori with no harmful impact on symbiotic bacteria.

For analytical applications, the mismatch between the pH optimum of POD nanozymes and the sample matrix is often reported in cascade biosensors (e.g., glucose detection). The first step in these biosensors involves oxidation of glucose by an enzyme with a neutral pH optimum. As the reaction proceeds, the pH drops due to accumulation of gluconic acid. Such cascade assays require prolonged incubation (typically hours) to generate sufficient H2O2 and lower the pH for POD nanozyme activation. Consequently, POD nanozymes with low KM and high activity at neutral pH are needed for such applications.[ 148 , 149 , 150 ]

Second, the concentrations of O2 and H2O2 vary significantly across compartments, often kinetically limiting nanozyme activity as discussed in Section 3.1. New developments must account for the fact that low intracellular concentrations of substrates, along with the presence of antioxidants, can significantly reduce the efficiency of pro‐oxidant nanozyme activity. For instance, glutathione and other antioxidants can quench the ROS generated by nanozymes, thereby diminishing catalytic efficiency.[ 151 ]

Third, nanozyme catalysis in real applications occurs in the presence of natural matrix components, which may interfere with catalysis. The interfering effects of endogenous enzymes on catalytic signal amplification have been investigated.[ 13 , 152 , 153 ] Kinetically, these enzymes compete with nanozymes for substrates and increase background signals. Several studies have also noted the interfering effects of antioxidants on nanozyme‐based assays. He et al. reported that ascorbic acid interfered with the colorimetric assay of acetaminophen by reducing the OXD‐like activity of Pt nanoparticles.[ 122 ] To address this, the authors leveraged the ascorbate‐oxidase‐like activity of Pt nanoparticles, enabling interference‐free detection of acetaminophen in tablets containing ascorbic acid.

Nanozyme Catalysis Under Extreme Conditions

Enzymes are naturally adapted to demonstrate their highest activity under physiological conditions (temperature, pH, substrate concentration). For example, the protease pepsin has maximum activity at physiological pH 2 to operate in the acidic gastric environment. Exposure to non‐physiological conditions generally leads to structural changes in the active site and a reduction in enzyme activity. Enzymes from thermophiles and psychrophiles have adapted to function at high and low temperatures, respectively, far from the optimal temperature range for mesophilic enzymes.[ 154 , 155 ]

Similar to enzymes, nanozymes have an optimum range of pH, temperature, and substrate concentrations. In general, nanozymes operate within similar temperature and pH ranges as enzymes but exhibit better stability under non‐physiological conditions. Yuan et al. reported that Cu2O nanozymes exhibit stable POD activity across a pH range of 2–14.[ 26 ] The higher stability of nanozymes arises from the lack of a complex 3D active site formed by weak interactions, making them less susceptible to external perturbations.

The high stability of nanozymes has been leveraged for various applications. For example, the degradation of microplastics is an important environmental challenge.[ 156 ] Although microplastics are relatively small, they typically measure several hundred micrometers, and their polymer chains are tightly packed, limiting enzyme access. Among different microplastics, polyethylene terephthalate (PET) has been the most studied for enzyme degradation. Despite efforts to engineer PETase enzymes that function at high temperatures, most can only operate up to 50 °C.[ 157 ] Our group used magnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles as POD nanozyme to extract and degrade microplastics.[ 22 ] At pH 1 and temperatures close to the melting point of the microplastics (130°C–260 °C), complete degradation can be achieved. Catalysis in such extreme conditions is unachieved by any enzyme.

Another practical example of nanozyme thermostability is the removal of endogenous peroxidase background activity in food samples. While such enzymes can be washed away in ELISA protocols, they are difficult to remove in lateral flow assays and other wash‐free biosensors. The presence of endogenous peroxidases generates a high background signal during nanozyme‐based signal amplification, risking false results. Our lab developed a battery‐driven assay using Joule heating to denature endogenous peroxidases in food samples.[ 13 ] After 3–5 min of heating at 80 °C, all natural peroxidases were denatured, while Au@Pt nanozymes (among others) maintained their POD activity. As a result, catalytic signal amplification could be successfully performed even in samples with high endogenous peroxidase content in wash‐free assays.

Beyond high‐temperature stability, nanozymes can also operate at low temperatures. Qin et al. reported a single‐atom iron nanozyme with phosphorus coordination that exhibits lipid oxidase‐like activity at −20 °C and 4 °C.[ 158 ] This nanozyme catalyzed lipid peroxidation, leading to the disruption of enveloped viruses, a technology applicable to antiviral packaging and personal protective equipment. Liu et al. recently engineered a perovskite hydroxide with oxidase‐like activity that retained over 90% of its activity at 0 °C, using this property to develop biosensors suitable for cold conditions.[ 159 ] Cursi et al. demonstrated that a Pt nanozyme showed maximum CAT activity at 70 °C and maintained activity even near the freezing point.[ 110 ]

The ability of nanozymes to maintain catalytic activity under extreme conditions sets them apart from natural enzymes. This unique stability opens opportunities for their application in environmental remediation, biomedical analysis, and industrial catalysis in conditions where natural enzymes would be inactivated.

Implications for Biomedical Applications

After comparing nanozymes and enzymes in the context of enzymology, we then review biomedical applications of nanozymes. Numerous reviews are available on nanozyme‐based disease treatment and we do not intend to repeat those contents. Instead, we have surveyed a total of 120 papers (Tables S3 and S4) related to using redox nanozymes for therapeutic applications published over the last 5 years to achieve a statistical understanding of the field.

ROS Producing and Scavenging Nanozymes for Therapy

The majority of redox nanozymes have been used for either producing or scavenging ROS in therapeutic contexts. Figure 6a shows a histogram covering 16 types of disease models, ranging from cancer to Alzheimer's disease. As shown in Figure 6b, 44% of the surveyed nanozymes relied on their intrinsic catalytic activity for therapy, while the remaining 56% incorporated additional strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy. The blue bars represent nanozymes for ROS production, whereas the orange bars indicate ROS‐scavenging nanozymes. It is easy to see that treatment of each disease is associated with only one type of nanozyme function (i.e., no mixed blue and orange bars, Figure 6a).

Only five diseases used ROS‐producing nanozymes, all of which are related to cancer or infection. The remaining 11 diseases relied on ROS‐scavenging nanozymes, targeting conditions such as inflammation, ROS‐caused neurological diseases, ulcers, and diabetic bone damages. For example, Liu et al. reported an integrated cascade nanozyme for treating two inflammatory bowel diseases (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, Figure 6c).[ 160 ] This nanozyme contained a Mn‐porphyrin metal‐organic framework (MOF), which converts oxygen radicals to H2O2. This MOF hosted Pt nanoparticles as a secondary nanozyme with catalase‐mimicking activity, catalyzing the disproportionation of H2O2 into water and oxygen. Upon oral administration, 0.1 mg kg−1 of the nanozyme was delivered to a murine model. After a 3‐day treatment, the group treated with the cascade nanozyme showed increased body weight and colon length comparable to those of the healthy control group.

Feng et al. reported a V2C MXene nanozyme for treating Parkinson's disease.[ 161 ] This MXene nanozyme can mimic up to six enzyme activities as showing in Figure 6d, including SOD, CAT, POD, glutathione peroxidase (GPx), thiol peroxidase (TPx), and haloperoxidase (HPO). It exhibits high biocompatibility and provides robust in vitro cytoprotection against oxidative stress, increasing the viability of L929 cells from 10% to 75%, while reducing ROS levels to nearly zero at a concentration of 200 µg mL−1. However, the multifunctional nature of this nanozyme makes it difficult to identify the primary contributor to the therapeutic effect.

Nanozymes that produce ROS are mostly used in cancer treatment and antibacterial applications. Figure 6e shows a self‐assembled single‐atom nanozyme used for treating hypoxia of solid tumors via photodynamic therapy, as reported by Wang et al.[ 162 ] It consists of single‐atom ruthenium catalytic sites anchored in a Mn3[Co(CN)6]2 MOF and encapsulated the chemotherapeutic agent chlorin e6 (Ce6). This nanozyme acts in a catalase‐like manner to generate oxygen. It exhibited high catalytic durability for rapid O2 generation from endogenous H2O2 to overcome tumor hypoxia, attributed to the six unsaturated Ru─C6 coordination sites. The MOF also enabled targeted delivery of Ce6 to the tumor site, resulting in partial tumor growth inhibition to 0.19 g after 14 days of treatment.

Stimuli‐Enhanced Nanozymes

Given the nanomaterial nature of nanozymes, they can also interact with chemical and biological cues, as well as with radiation. Many studies have taken advantages of these properties. Approximately 28% of the nanozymes were assisted with NIR‐II light (wavelength 900—1880 nm), owing to its superior tissue penetration ability. In most cases, NIR light was used to induce photothermal effects (Figure 6b). For example, Chang et al. reported a single‐atom Pd nanozyme for photothermal cancer therapy (Figure 7a).[ 163 ] The Pd atoms were deposited on ZIF‐8, exhibiting POD‐ and glutathione oxidase‐like activities, as well as efficient photothermal conversion under NIR II irradiation. Tumor growth in the Pd single‐atom nanozyme group was significantly inhibited by 13‐fold compared to the PBS control group and by one‐fold compared to the group treated with 1064 nm light alone (Figure 7b). These combined functionalities promoted ferroptosis, as evidenced by the upregulation of lipid peroxides and ROS. In most cases, such photothermal effects enhance the overall therapeutic efficacy of nanozymes. Moreover, a few papers reported the use of other light sources. For example, Wang et al. applied a 671 nm laser with a single‐atom nanozyme for cancer treatment, achieving nearly 90% cancer cell death when the laser and nanozyme were used in combination.[ 162 ]

In addition to light, some nanozymes were designed to be activated by acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) to enhance the precision of therapy. For example, Wang et al. reported a nanozyme Ag2S@Fe2C‐DSPE‐PEG‐iRGD for tumor vascular normalization and tumor destruction via TME unlocking.[ 164 ] The unique characteristics of the TME, including elevated acidity and excessive H2O2 levels, enable highly active nanozymes to undergo Fenton and Fenton‐like reactions for ROS generation, offering a promising strategy for chemodynamic therapy. As shown in Figure 7c, DSPE‐PEG fully degraded in PBS at pH 5.4 after 48 h. Subsequently, AgS2 facilitated photothermal therapy, while Fe2+ and carbon dots contributed to ROS generation. Furthermore, other external stimuli such as ultrasound,[ 165 , 166 ] magnetic fields,[ 167 ] and Xrays[ 168 ] have also been reported to enhance or activate the therapeutic effects of nanozymes.

For example, inflammatory bowel disease is a widely used model for oral delivery, since nanozymes can naturally reach the bowel following ingestion. Many other target sites, such as implanted tumors, teeth and wounds, can also be accessed relatively easily.

In contrast, IV and intraperitoneal injections rely on blood circulation for delivery, and the majority of nanozymes (up to 99%) may not reach the intended disease site.[ 169 ] The IV injection method is most commonly used in cancer treatment research. We also noticed that 84% of studies characterized nanozyme activities in buffer and then directly applied the nanozymes for therapeutic purposes, whereas only 16% also evaluated nanozyme activity in biological fluids such as serum (Figure 8b). As discussed above, nanozyme activity can be significantly affected by the high concentration of serum proteins and the presence of other interfering molecules.

Type of Nanozymes for Therapy

We then summarized the types of nanomaterials used, as shown in Figure 8c. Due to the diversity of materials, it is difficult to describe each individually. Therefore, we listed the main active metal components for metal‐containing nanozymes, while also including a few metal‐free nanozymes.

Iron is the most frequently found element in therapeutic nanozymes appearing in 32 studies, 15 for ROS production and 17 for ROS scavenging. Among these, iron oxide and Prussian blue are dominating iron‐containing nanozymes. Both have been reported for ROS generation and scavenging. Typically, iron oxide is used for ROS production, while Prussian blue is mostly used to reduce ROS levels. For example, Ma et al. reported a Prussian blue nanozyme as a pyroptosis inhibitor to alleviate neurodegeneration.[ 170 ] Its ROS scavenging performance was determined by electron spin resonance (Figure 9a), showing effective elimination of hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions, as well as catalytic decomposition of H2O2 to produce oxygen. Both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that the Prussian blue nanozyme attenuated activation of microglial NOD‐like receptor family pyrin domain‐containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes and caspase‐1 by scavenging ROS. This, in turn, reduced gasdermin D cleavage and pro‐inflammatory cytokine production, ultimately inhibiting microglial pyroptosis.

Similarly, iron oxide has been reported for both ROS generation and scavenging. Xing et al. used Prussian blue for breast cancer therapy via ROS generation through a Fenton reaction.[ 171 ] Hollow mesoporous Prussian blue (HMPB) nanocubes containing Fe2+/Fe3+ redox pairs were employed to construct iron‐loaded liposomes (HMPB@Lip). These nanozymes catalyzed lipid peroxidation to induce ferroptosis. As shown in Figure 9b, the ROS levels in the HMPB@Lip and HMPB@Lip + groups were 2.6 and 3.5 times higher than the control group, respectively, indicating their potent ROS‐generating capability in tumor cells.

Regarding iron oxide, Zhou et al. found that Fe3O4 nanoparticles reduced ROS levels in a genetic model of diabetes (Figure 9c).[ 172 ] Treatment with Fe3O4 nanoparticles decreased exogenous H2O2‐induced ROS levels, confirming their ROS scavenging function at the whole‐cell level. This was attributed to the localization of Fe3O4 nanoparticles in the cytosol, where they alleviate oxidative stress through catalase‐like activity. This process activates the energy sensor adenosine 5‐monophosphate‐activated protein kinase (AMPK), thereby promoting glucose metabolism and insulin response. In another study, Chen et al. reported heparin‐conjugated Fe@Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Fe@Fe3O4@heparin) as peroxidase‐mimicking nanozymes designed to generate ROS for anti‐tumor therapy (Figure 9d).[ 173 ] The therapeutic effect was enhanced by a synergistic mechanism involving an ultrasound‐stimulated Fenton reaction and elevated intratumoral H2O2 levels induced by β‐lapachone (La). After treatment of mice implanted with 4T1 tumors, this nanozyme caused a 56% apoptosis rate in tumors with minimal effect on body weight.

Among other commonly used metals, copper was primarily employed for ROS production, whereas manganese‐, platinum‐, and cerium‐based nanozymes were mostly utilized for ROS scavenging. Other metals were used much less, with Au‐, Ni‐, Mo‐, Ti‐, and Ag‐based nanozymes being primarily applied for ROS scavenging.

Aside from metal‐containing nanozymes, many non‐metal nanozymes have also been developed in recent years. For example, carbon dots has been reported in seven studies, of which two were used to eliminate ROS and five were used to generate ROS.[ 174 , 175 , 176 ] The surface of carbon dots is often rich in functional groups such as hydroxyl (─OH), carboxyl (─COOH), and amino (─NH2) groups. These groups have reducing activities and can neutralize ROS (such as •OH and H2O2) through electron transfer or hydrogen‐donation mechanisms.[ 177 ] On the other hand, under light irradiation (especially ultraviolet or visible light), carbon dots absorb photons to generate electron‐hole pairs. These carriers can react with water and oxygen to produce ROS.

Surface Modification of Nanozymes for Therapy

Surface modification of nanozymes has been made to improve dispersion in water, reduce toxicity, increase circulation time, and achieve targeting. We have summarized 26 types of modifications (Figure 8d). Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is the most commonly used, and it was used equally in nanozymes for the prodution and scavenge of ROS. PEGylation is primarily employed to improve the blood circulation time. For example, Zeng et al. reported a biocompatible drug‐free ceria nanozyme (CeNP‐PEG) with regenerable scavenging activities against multiple ROS (Figure 10a).[ 178 ] They coated PEG on the nanozyme to improve biocompatibility, where cellular uptake was obvious as early as 15 min after the addition of 1.0 µg mL−1 CeNP‐PEG. Aside from PEG, other polymers such as polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) and hyaluronic acid have also been reported for similar purposes. Interestingly, MOFs have become common materials for interfacing with nanozymes, and at least 19 studies have been published on this topic in the past 5 years. Unlike inert polymers, MOFs can often increase the catalytic activity of nanozymes and achieve additional nanozyme loading. Yu et al. reported a Cu‐based MOF for efficient osteoarthritis treatment.[ 179 ] As shown in Figure 10b, CuNx sites were anchored on a porous Cu MOF by combining Cu atoms with interconnected N (4,4′‐bipyridine) via self‐assembly. This construct exhibits multiple activities (i.e., SOD‐like, CAT‐like, and •OH scavenging activities) while showing negligible pro‐oxidant activities (POD‐ and OXD‐like). Compared to CuNC and CuO, the •OH‐scavenging activity of the Cu MOF was 3.3 times higher than that of CuO and, unlike CuNC, did not produce highly toxic ROS under acidic conditions.

Moreover, some biomaterials have also been used to modify nanozymes, such as erythrocyte membrane, siRNA and peptides. These biological species improved circulation, introduced additional functions, and enhanced penetration across difficult barriers such as the blood–brain barrier (BBB). For example, Chen et al. developed a nanozyme [MIL‐101‐NH2(Fe/Cu)] with ROS scavenging activity, designed to encapsulate the neuroprotective agent rapamycin and functionalized with BBB‐targeting ligands (transferrin) on its surface (Figure 10c).[ 180 ] This design enabled enhanced drug retention and controlled release within ischemic brain lesions, facilitating synergistic therapy for ischemic stroke. Utilizing a receptor‐mediated transcytosis pathway, the transferrin‐coated nanoparticles efficiently crossed the BBB and selectively accumulated at the site of cerebral ischemic injury in mice.

In addition, nucleic acids have emerged as promising surface modifiers for nanozymes due to their specific target recognition, conformational polymorphism, and high programmability. For example, Zhang et al. synthesized a DNA‐gated and self‐protected bioorthogonal catalyst by modifying a Pd nanozyme with highly programmable DNA molecules for cancer therapy (Figure 10d).[ 181 ] The monolayer DNA coating, consisting of C‐rich single‐stranded DNA (cDNA) and MUC‐1‐targeting aptamer (mDNA), facilitated selective cancer cell targeting while functioning as a gatekeeper to regulate substrate access to the Pd° catalytic sites. This design enabled controlled and selective prodrug activation within cancer cells, thereby enhancing therapeutic precision and minimizing off‐target effects.

Pharmacokinetics of Nanozymes

The clinical translation of nanozymes requires a thorough understanding of their pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles. Among the 120 studies evaluated in Tables S3 and S4, only 16 explicitly investigated PK and the data were mostly presented in their supplementary materials. The biodistribution of nanozymes is highly dependent on their size, morphology, surface charge, and surface functionalization. Several studies have assessed systemic circulation and organ distribution. Ultra‐small nanozymes (<10 nm), such as carbon dots, exhibit rapid systemic clearance, with half‐lives ranging from a few minutes to approximately 2 h.[ 182 , 183 , 184 ] In contrast, medium‐sized nanozymes (20–100 nm) demonstrate improved retention, with serum half‐lives between 4 and 9 h.[ 185 ] Surface modifications significantly impact nanozyme circulation time. In one case, PEGylation of nanozymes increased their serum half‐life from 1.2 to 8.6 h. Similarly, macrophage‐cloaked nanozymes extended circulation time from 48 to 138 min.[ 186 ]

Unlike endogenous enzymes, nanozymes are not subject to metabolic transformation and do not engage in intrinsic biochemical pathways. However, their elimination profiles remain poorly characterized. Only a few studies have quantified nanozyme content in urine 13 h after injection, suggesting renal clearance as a route for ultra‐small particles.[ 184 ] Larger nanozymes, which cannot pass through the glomerular filtration barrier, are more likely to be sequestered by hepatic cells and eliminated via the hepatobiliary system into feces. Additionally, alternative clearance pathways, such as lymphatic trafficking and degradation in lymph nodes, have been proposed but remain largely unexplored.

Summary and Outlook

In this article, we assess nanozyme catalysis through the lenses of kinetics, multi‐enzyme activities, stability, and matrix effects, exploring these dimensions by comparing them with natural enzymes within the framework of enzymology. Although using enzymology terminology to describe nanozymes may not be conceptually precise, it still provides practical value. We emphasize the importance of paying close attention to fundamental aspects of reaction kinetics to make meaningful comparisons. We articulated the importance of KM , which can be a limiting factor in therapeutic applications but can be readily solved for bioanalytical applications. We also caution against calculating kcat and directly comparing nanozyme activities in buffers and biological matrices. Vmax , which is often used for comparison, cannot justify claims of higher activity, especially when nanozymes are compared across different studies. Even after careful analysis of the key kinetic parameters of various nanozymes, no specific material clearly stands out for biomedical applications. This contrasts with natural enzymes, such as HRP, which remains widely recognized and used despite ongoing efforts to find alternatives.

Despite structural and mechanistic differences, nanozymes can function as effective enzyme replacements and can even operate under conditions that inactivate natural enzymes. While published research often highlights the advantages of nanozymes, careful comparison of their catalytic activities with that of natural enzymes carries critical implications for biomedical applications. With impressive progresses made, numerous research directions remain open within the field of nanozymes, and some of our thoughts are listed below.

A stronger connection of nanozyme activity and therapeutic effects. After two decades of development, it has become clear that a stronger connection between nanozyme activity and observed therapeutic effects need to be established. This comes in a few aspects: 1) comparison of activities in buffer versus nanozyme activity with limited substrate concentration in cells or in blood (the KM consideration). 2) The effect of the intracellular environment and the presence of crowded proteins and competing substrates needs to be fully studied. When administered intravenously, nanozymes first interact with blood, and in some cases, they are internalized by cells, encountering the intracellular milieu. For specialized applications, they may also contact other bodily fluids, such as tears.[ 21 ] Most studies have not taken these factors into consideration when evaluating nanozyme activities. Comprehensive PK studies are therefore critical not only for elucidating the processes of absorption, biodistribution, metabolism, and excretion, but also for understanding how physiological interactions modulate nanozyme efficacy. 3) Side reactions due to other types of activities need to be understood. Most nanozyme studies focus on a single catalytic activity in buffer with a specific substrate, yet in vivo, their multiactivities may lead to unintended reactions with diverse substrates. For instance, CeO2 nanoparticles display a broad range of catalytic functions, raising the question: which specific activity or combination of activities are responsible for their observed biological effects? We emphasize the need to evaluate nanozyme activity in blood serum alongside buffer conditions and to test a broader range of substrates, particularly redox‐active ones prevalent in biological samples, such as ascorbate, lactate, glucose, and uric acid. At a minimum, ex vivo assessments of catalytic activity in serum should be conducted. 4) Pharmacodynamic (PD) evaluations are essential to systematically characterize the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of nanozymes. Currently, most studies assessed nanozyme activity at a single time point, often using one or two doses in a limited number of in vitro or in vivo models. This approach may underestimate the complexity of nanozyme action in biological systems. To establish a robust correlation between nanozyme activity and therapeutic outcomes, dose‐dependent and time‐dependent studies are necessary. Key PD parameters include the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), half‐maximal effective dose (ED50), and maximum efficacy (Emax). Additionally, mechanistic studies involving multiple disease models are required to elucidate systemic pathways and potential off‐target effects. A stronger connection of nanozyme activity and therapeutic effects. After two decades of development, it has become clear that a stronger connection between nanozyme activity and observed therapeutic effects need to be established. This comes in a few aspects: 1) comparison of activities in buffer versus nanozyme activity with limited substrate concentration in cells or in blood (the KM consideration). 2) The effect of the intracellular environment and the presence of crowded proteins and competing substrates needs to be fully studied. When administered intravenously, nanozymes first interact with blood, and in some cases, they are internalized by cells, encountering the intracellular milieu. For specialized applications, they may also contact other bodily fluids, such as tears.[ 21 ] Most studies have not taken these factors into consideration when evaluating nanozyme activities. Comprehensive PK studies are therefore critical not only for elucidating the processes of absorption, biodistribution, metabolism, and excretion, but also for understanding how physiological interactions modulate nanozyme efficacy. 3) Side reactions due to other types of activities need to be understood. Most nanozyme studies focus on a single catalytic activity in buffer with a specific substrate, yet in vivo, their multiactivities may lead to unintended reactions with diverse substrates. For instance, CeO2 nanoparticles display a broad range of catalytic functions, raising the question: which specific activity or combination of activities are responsible for their observed biological effects? We emphasize the need to evaluate nanozyme activity in blood serum alongside buffer conditions and to test a broader range of substrates, particularly redox‐active ones prevalent in biological samples, such as ascorbate, lactate, glucose, and uric acid. At a minimum, ex vivo assessments of catalytic activity in serum should be conducted. 4) Pharmacodynamic (PD) evaluations are essential to systematically characterize the therapeutic efficacy and safety profile of nanozymes. Currently, most studies assessed nanozyme activity at a single time point, often using one or two doses in a limited number of in vitro or in vivo models. This approach may underestimate the complexity of nanozyme action in biological systems. To establish a robust correlation between nanozyme activity and therapeutic outcomes, dose‐dependent and time‐dependent studies are necessary. Key PD parameters include the half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), half‐maximal effective dose (ED50), and maximum efficacy (Emax). Additionally, mechanistic studies involving multiple disease models are required to elucidate systemic pathways and potential off‐target effects. This comprehensive analysis can more convincingly link nanozyme catalytic activities to the proposed therapeutic effect and expand the nanomedicine potential of nanozymes.

Although therapeutic efficacy is often demonstrated in published studies, we argue that rigorous control experiments are essential to confirm that these effects stem from the proposed catalytic activity. Given the complexity of nanozyme catalysis in biological systems, and the fact that many nanozymes exhibit both pro‐ and antioxidant activities, it is often impossible to pinpoint the exact therapeutic mechanism. One way to improve reliability is to design robust control experiments. For new materials, comparisons with classical nanozymes are essential (e.g., Fe3O4 nanoparticles as a control for new peroxidase nanozymes). Similarly, for new surface engineering approaches, closely related materials can serve as controls. We stress that it is critical to have a broadly accessible system as a benchmark. Such control systems would also allow other laboratories to have a common reference point to compare their work.

We briefly addressed nanozyme toxicity. While most therapeutic nanozyme studies report low toxicity in cellular and animal models, these assessments typically rely on standard assays, such as cell viability, oxidative stress, and histological analysis, covering only short‐term effects without exploring the long‐term fate of these nanomaterials. Overall, well‐organized toxicity data are missing for most studies. Given that many therapeutic nanozymes generate ROS and contain heavy metals, a deeper understanding of the reactions underlying their therapeutic benefits, inflammatory responses, and potential toxicity is critical. Modulating multi‐enzyme activities. Since many nanozymes for multiple activities, a challenge is to rationally enhance the desired activity (e.g., SOD) while suppressing others (e.g., POD, OXD). Such functional tailoring is important for various areas of application. Furthermore, engineering nanozymes with absolute substrate specificity remains challenging. Combining both reaction and substrate specificity would greatly improve the functional mimicry of natural enzymes by nanozymes.

On the fundamental side, a consensus on the best approach to accurately determine kinetic parameters has yet to be established. Notably, within the same study, where experimental conditions are consistent, differences in nanozyme performance rarely exceed two orders of magnitude, suggesting that reported larger variability in different studies is largely due to inconsistent protocols. Some claimed high catalytic activities raised concerns about the accuracy of the calculations. Developing standardized measurement protocols and rigorous data reporting will help address these issues, which will in turn benefit biomedical application studies. Most of these recommendations are made to better link enzymology to practical applications. We believe that the success of nanozymes will be built on a solid basis of their chemical and biochemical understanding.
