Nanotechnology for water disinfection and biofilm control: mechanisms, applications, and future outlook
Authors: Mohamed Azab El-Liethy, Bahaa A Hemdan, Gamila E El-Taweel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanotechnology has emerged as an advanced, sustainable approach to controlling the spread of waterborne pathogens in aquatic environments, addressing a critical global health challenge that causes millions of deaths each year. Increasing pressures from rapid population growth, industrial expansion, and climate change have heightened the need for innovative, efficient water treatment technologies. Although conventional disinfection methods, such as chlorination and ozonation, remain widely used, their use is often associated with the formation of harmful disinfection by-products (DBPs). These limitations have stimulated interest in nanomaterials as alternative antimicrobial agents. Nanoparticles exhibit strong antimicrobial activity, broad-spectrum effectiveness against viruses, bacteria, and protozoa, and a lower potential for by-product formation than traditional disinfectants. This review comprehensively evaluates nanoparticle classifications, synthesis strategies, and their functional advantages in water and wastewater treatment systems. It further explores the mechanisms underlying nanoparticle-mediated pathogen inactivation and biofilm disruption, while discussing current technological advancements and practical challenges. The limitations of conventional treatment approaches are also addressed in the context of emerging global water stressors. Overall, this review provides an integrated, up—to-date perspective on nanomaterial-based water disinfection and biofilm control, emphasizing the relationship between nanomaterial physicochemical properties and their antimicrobial performance.Keywords: Nanotechnology, Nanoparticles (NPs), Waterborne pathogens, Biofilm control, Water disinfection, Disinfection byproducts (DBPs)

Introduction

Water is one of the most vital components for all living forms. It is necessary for cell composition and renewal, and is vital to the survival of life on Earth [1]. Water has been linked to the spread of diseases. Polluted water, untreated wastewater, and insufficiently treated wastewater may contain large amounts of pathogenic microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, and protozoa [2]. Moreover, pathogenic microorganisms may also be transmitted into groundwater, surface water and recreational water (e.g., swimming pools, springs, hot tubs, spas and fountains) [3]. The most common waterborne pathogens that enter the human body through ingestion of contaminated water are Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella spp., pathogenic E. coli, Shigella spp., and Campylobacter spp., which are considered major causative waterborne bacteria. In addition, enteroviruses, noroviruses, and hepatoviruses can cause gastrointestinal illnesses. Furthermore, the protozoan parasites including Cryptosporidium, Parvum, and Giardia are also significant causes of gastrointestinal infections [4]. Reducing the number of viable microorganisms to acceptable levels and eliminating or inactivating all microbial pathogens capable of causing human diseases are the primary goals of drinking water and wastewater treatment. Despite the significant progress made in sanitation and water treatment initiatives to improve public health, point-source outbreaks and sporadic cases of waterborne illnesses still occur [5]. Industrialization, global population growth, and climate change directly affect the long-term development of the world’s water resources. A 70% increase in agricultural output is projected to be required by 2050 to meet the demands of global population growth, which is expected to rise from 7.8 billion in 2020 to 9.7 billion in 2050 and 10.9 billion by 2100. Since 70% of the planet’s freshwater withdrawals are currently used for agricultural irrigation, the demand for freshwater, particularly for food production, is substantial. As a result, the safe reuse of treated wastewater for irrigation is essential to mitigate water deficit [6]. The demand for clean water is increasing globally. Therefore, water disinfection plays an essential role in reducing serious illnesses associated with waterborne diseases. Drinking water treatment plants (DWTPs) use water disinfection to eliminate pathogenic microorganisms and make sure the drinking water distribution systems (DWDSs) meet the standards for safe drinking water consumption. In addition to this, water disinfectants are also needed to protect DWDSs form biofilm formation [7, 8].

The most commonly used chemical disinfectants are chlorine and chlorine derivatives such as chlorine dioxide (ClO2) and hypochlorite salts (ClO−). The benefits of chlorine and its derivatives include cost-effectiveness, ease of handling, high effectiveness against various microbial pathogens, taste improvement and long persistence in DWDSs for biofilm control. Nevertheless, chlorine may suffer from some limitations such as producing DBPs (e.g. trihalomethanes (THMs), haloacetic acids (HAAs) and aldehydes) which frequently occur after disinfection processes using chlorine [5]. Since DBPs are deemed "harmful to human health," water companies are required to monitor and take extra precautions when they are detected in raw water sources [9–11]. In addition, the strong oxidant ozone (O3) is widely recognized as an effective, safe, and broad-spectrum disinfectant. However, it is rarely used for disinfection in large water treatment plants due to its high cost and short-lived disinfectant effect [12]. Application of UV technology for water disinfection has high efficacy against chlorine-resistant protozoa and does not produce DBPs [13]. Unfortunately, UV disinfection does not provide any residual in treated water, so it cannot prevent biological recontamination in the distribution network [14].

Nanotechnology is being positioned as one of the century's most important technologies. Nanotechnology has many applications in various domains, including industries, biotechnology, storage technologies, optical and photonic applications, and health care. It is currently widely employed in environmental cleanup strategies for environmental protection through wastewater treatment and pollution control [15]. Nanotechnology may support effective methods for disinfecting, using, and reusing water, as well as the creation of next-generation water supply systems. Because of their superior qualities and efficiency, nanomaterials (NMs) are especially well-suited for the treatment of water and wastewater [16].

Contaminated water can be purified using nanotechnology through filtration or separation methods. In order to remove water from both organic and inorganic pollutants, nanomaterials are widely used as separation media. By using nanotechnology, water treatment costs can be drastically decreased on a large scale, along with the cleanup time, and pollutant levels in ecosystems can be almost completely eliminated [15].

This current state of knowledge provides a comprehensive overview of the main categories of nanomaterials (NMs), as well as the synthesis and characterization of nanoparticles (NPs). It also explains how to employ NPs to disinfect water from bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. In addition, several limitations, such as toxicity, operational requirements, and reusability, were identified for their practical application in large-scale water and wastewater treatment. Ongoing research and continuous production are expected to drive the development of affordable, effective, and environmentally friendly nanomaterials for widespread applications in the near future. Unlike previous review articles that broadly address nanotechnology in environmental remediation, this review specifically focuses on nanomaterial-mediated microbial inactivation in water systems, with particular emphasis on biofilm control. This work uniquely integrates (i) antimicrobial mechanisms of nanomaterials, (ii) biofilm formation and resistance pathways, and (iii) the relationship between nanomaterial physicochemical properties and disinfection efficiency. By linking these aspects, this review provides a more focused and mechanistic understanding that is not comprehensively addressed in previous studies.

Waterborne pathogens

Conditions known as "waterborne diseases" are those caused by pathogenic microorganisms or their toxins and spread through water. Illnesses can be contracted through bathing and cleaning, as well as through consuming contaminated food or water. A significant portion of the world's population lacks access to clean, safe water that is necessary for basic needs like drinking. Around 1.1 billion people worldwide do not have access to clean water sources, and 2.3 billion do not have proper sanitation [17]. This is typically attributed to the fact that 785 million people require basic drinking water management, with 144 million of those people depending on surface water [18]. Approximately 1.3 million children die each year as a result of drinking water contaminated with waterborne pathogens [19].

Waterborne pathogens cause high mortality rates in developing nations, particularly in Africa and Southeast Asia, where diarrheal diseases account for 1.46 million deaths [20]. Protozoan infections that spread through water are the second factor contributing to deaths in children under five. Protozoa cause 1.7 billion diarrheal cases globally, resulting in 842,000 deaths annually [21]. Conversely, 60,000 cases of cryptosporidiosis are reported annually in the UK due to the widespread transmission of Cryptosporidium spp. through tap water [22]. Each year, waterborne pathogens cause 12 to 19.5 million waterborne illnesses in the United States [23].

In the USA, there were about 33,600,000 cases of illness, of which 7,150,000 cases were linked to diseases spread by water, resulting in 6,630 fatalities. On the other hand, 1,330,000 cases of severe norovirus infection were reported, followed 4,670,000 cases of severe otitis diseases. Moreover, 322,000 cases of cryptosporidiosis and 415,000 cases of giardiasis resulted in severe illnesses, respectively. Additionally, it is estimated that 2,330,000 domestically acquired waterborne enteric illnesses and 96,000 domestically acquired respiratory illnesses occurred in the USA. The two illnesses most frequently linked to waterborne transmission deaths in the United States are Legionnaires' disease and Pseudomonas pneumonia. According to a report, 131 cases of faecal-oral route diseases and 5,530 cases of respiratory diseases have been linked to waterborne pathogens, representing 83% of all acquired waterborne deaths [24].

When water sources are contaminated with human and animal waste, a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms can enter the water supply. The WHO drinking water quality guidelines list the principal waterborne pathogenic microorganisms that can be transmitted through drinking water. Conventional water disinfections

Chlorine

Chlorine is a common oxidant used to disinfect drinking water and limit microbial growth in WDSs. It interacts with various cellular compounds and influences metabolic and physiological processes [32].

When chlorine is added to water, two chemical species, collectively known as free chlorine, are formed. These two species exhibit very different behaviors: hypochlorite ion (OCl−, negatively charged) and hypochlorous acid (HOCl, electrically neutral). In addition to being more reactive than the hypochlorite ion, hypochlorous acid possesses greater oxidizing and disinfecting properties. The reactions involved in the formation and decay of chlorine occur in two phases. Equation (2) describes the reaction of hypochlorous acid, a weak acid, which produces protons and the hypochlorite ion, (OCl-).

Because chlorine compounds carry an electrical charge that differs from that of negatively charged microorganisms, electrically neutral hypochlorous acid can oxidize the cell wall at lower pH. This increases membrane permeability, leading to the release of essential cellular components or the inactivation of enzymes, ultimately causing cell death [33]. It can also cause nucleic acid damage, inhibit ATP production, fragment proteins, and damage bacterial membranes by altering their permeability [34].

According to Jiang et al. [35], conventional disinfectants such as chlorine (Cl), chlorine dioxide (ClO2), chloramine, sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl), and O3 interact with microbes, natural organic matter (NOM), and bromide/iodide to form distinct classes of DBPs that are extremely toxic. The major DBPs compounds are trichloroacetic acid (TCAA) and dichloroacetic acid (DCAA) followed by THMs. Occurrence of NOM in raw water during chlorination disinfection step leads to the production of THMs and haloacetic acids (HAAs) and this is correlated with seasonal variations and water quality. Additionally, algal organic matter (AOM) generates carbonaceous disinfection byproducts (C-DBPs) and nitrogenous disinfection byproducts (N-DBPs) depending on the concentration of AOM, disinfectant dose, pH, and contact time [36]. Hence, the coagulation/flocculation step in the water treatment process is an important step for achieving high removal efficiencies of NOM and AOM, while adjusting the coagulant dose is important step for removing the suspended solids [37].

Ozone (O3)

Ozone is considered a potent water disinfectant. As a strong oxidant, ozone accepts electrons from other substances. Its oxidative power makes it an effective disinfectant, capable of reducing contaminants in water [38]. Upon introduction into water, ozone immediately oxidizes and eliminates microbial pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, and parasites. As a result, the harmful pollutants are removed by weakening, rupturing, and inactivating microbial cells. Ozone-based water treatment systems, such as activated carbon filtration, can also remove turbidity, unpleasant tastes, and odors from water [39, 40].

One of the main disadvantages of ozone as a water disinfectant is its ability to form DBPs. The concentration of brominated and iodinated THMs and HAAs increases when ozonation is followed by the chlorination process during DWT [41]. According to Watson et al. [42], bromides that are converted to brominated DBPs in surface waters can pose hazards to aquatic life and living organisms, particularly the endocrine system. Bromide and iodide halogenated ions affect the formation of Cl-DBPs, Br-DBPs, or I-DBPs in the presence of chlorine, chloramine, or ozone, in addition to THMs, HAAs, and N-nitrosodimethylamine [43].

After disinfection, carboxylic acids can still form, especially if ozone and chlorine are used [44]. The amount of these substances varies depending on raw water quality and DWT procedures. Jurado-Sánchez et al. [45] found that two DWTPs with distinct process outlines contained 35 aliphatic and aromatic acids. While the second plant used ozonation and chlorination, the first plant used chlorination/chloramination. The second plant's carboxylic acid content was six times higher, with particularly high levels in autumn and winter. Kimura et al. [46] have identified N-nitrosodimethylamine (NDMA) and hydroxylamine as DBPs that arise from ozone-induced water disinfection, particularly under conditions of pH, temperature and ozone dosages.

Ultraviolet (UV)

Pathogens in water can be effectively inactivated using UV disinfection technology. In recent years, UV irradiation has gained popularity as a water treatment technique due to its advantages of effective disinfection and relatively low formation of DBPs [47]. Compared to conventional chemical disinfection methods (such as chlorination or ozonation), UV radiation offers many benefits, including no chemical addition, no formation of hazardous DBPs, and the prevention of the development of bacteria resistant to disinfectants [48].

Worldwide, there are already over 7000 municipal UV disinfection installations and tiny residential UV disinfection systems available, demonstrating the growing popularity of UV disinfection as an alternative to chemical additives for treating surface water [47]. In contrast to UVC radiation, which is produced at 254 nm by UV mercury lamps commonly used, less research has been conducted on the inactivation process of UVA radiation (wavelengths greater than 320 nm). Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are important intermediates produced by the photooxidation of oxygen and are largely responsible for the indirect inactivation effect of UVA, which also includes oxidative damage to DNA and other cellular components [49, 50]. According to Li et al. [51], microbial inactivation is induced by UVA-LED radiation at 365 nm, which generates hydroxyl radicals (·OH) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). However, by scavenging these reactive intermediates, catalase and mannitol can effectively protect microorganisms from UVA radiation. Reactive intermediates, especially OH radicals and H2O2, slow the growth of microbial pathogens and cause oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and cell membranes. While UVA indirect damage takes longer to occur than UVC direct damage, it is thought to be permanent, in contrast to UVC damage, which may be repaired by DNA repair mechanisms [47, 52].

Thus, UVA radiation's ability to inhibit self-repair processes is a benefit for microbe inactivation. In addition, compared to UVC radiation, UVA radiation is more soluble and an penetrate mixtures more deeply, resulting in improved disinfection of turbid water and wastewater [48, 49]. On the other hand, depending on chlorine dosage, contact time, and water quality, the generation of trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids (HAAs) during disinfection remains low and consistent when chlorine is used in combination with UV light. Furthermore, during UV/chlorine disinfection, nitrogenous disinfection byproducts (N-DBPs) or chloroform (CHCl3) may develop[53, 54]. Definition of nanotechnology

Norio Taniguch first used the word "nanotechnology" in 1974. Because of their high surface area-to-volume ratio, nanoparticles (NPs), which are particles with sizes between one and one hundred nanometers (nm), have special properties. Strength, dissolving qualities, mobility, and responsiveness are all improved by this trait [55, 56].

Nanomaterials (NMs) are defined as "materials with any external dimension in the nanoscale or having an internal structure or surface structure in the nanoscale" by the European Union (EU) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) [57]. Moreover, the EU and ISO define the nanoscale as the size range between about 1 nm and 100 nm, and NPs are defined as "nano-objects with all three external dimensions in the nanoscale" according to their definitions. When NMs are compared to bulk versions of the identical materials, their higher surface area-to-volume ratio from the decrease in particle size makes them more reactive [57].

Over the past fifty years, nanotechnology has emerged as the most popular and well-researched field of study because of its distinct physiochemical behavior [58]. One the one hand, the enabling nanotechnology is positioned as one of the century's most difficult critical technologies. Research framework programs aimed at advancing nanotechnology and commercializing new materials and processes have cost billions of dollars. For instance, the National Nanotechnology Initiative (Arlington, VA, USA) received more than 1.5 billion US dollars in federal funding in 2015. The initiative supports nanotechnology-related initiatives in 20 departments and independent agencies. Moreover, the European Union's Horizon 2020 research and innovation framework programme provides US$110 billion over seven years to support research and innovation projects. In 2014, US$92 million was allocated for water innovations and US$686 million for nanotechnology. There are calls for "low-energy solutions for drinking water production" that incorporate the use of innovative nano-engineered materials and procedures for water applications in an effort to bring together water and nanotechnology [59].

Globally, studies are being conducted to understand the benefits and drawbacks of nanotechnology and its uses across a variety of fields, including materials science, biomedical research, and space exploration. Numerous disciplines, including applied physics, material science, chemistry, biology, biomedical engineering, surface science, electrical engineering, and robotics, are involved in nanotechnology [60]. Nanotechnology is being used in medicine to develop biosensors, tissue engineering, nanoscale diagnostic tools, and drug delivery via nanoparticles [60]. Nanomaterials can potentially be used to control bacterial infections. Nanomaterials are increasingly used as alternatives to antibiotics to target bacteria. It now seems to be present in almost every facet of our daily products. It is essential for sensors, the creation of new materials for energy storage systems, improving overall food quality, sports, cosmetics, tissue engineering, diagnosis and disease monitoring, genetic changes linked to cancer, operating machinery, regenerative medicine, vaccine development, water disinfection, etc.[58, 61, 62]. To avoid producing toxic DBPs using chemical disinfectants, some alternative disinfection techniques are needed. In place of chemical disinfection techniques, nanoparticles are considered a good alternative. Not only do they not produce disinfection byproducts in drinking water, but they are also comparatively inert. According to Ali and Ahmad [15], some bionanocomposites are important components of inexpensive water decontamination treatments.

Classification of nanomaterials

Since the physicochemical characteristics of nanomaterials essentially determine their antimicrobial performance in water disinfection, a thorough grasp of their synthesis methods, structural classification, and characterization techniques is necessary for the logical design and optimization of nano-enabled water disinfectants. NPs are classified based on their dimensions, materials, and sources. It is important to note that NMs are also classified into three major groups based on their mode of action: nanoadsorbents, nanocatalysts, and nanomembranes [63].

Classification of nanomaterials based on their dimensions

Four dimensions are used to classify NMs according to their structural properties. Examples of systems with restricted electron mobility in all three dimensions (X, Y, and Z) include fullerenes, quantum dots, and clusters in zero dimensions (0D). In one dimension (1D), this refers to free-electron motion restricted to the X axis. Carbon nanotubes, nanowires, and nanorods are a few prominent examples. Free-electron mobility in the two-dimensional (2D) domain occurs in the X–Y plane and is observed in materials such as graphene sheets, carbon-coated nanoplates, and multilayer nanoparticles. Lastly, free electron mobility is constrained in all three directions in three dimensions (3D), when structures such as polycrystals, dendrimers, and liposomes are present (Fig. 1) [64–67]. Classification of nanomaterials based on their sources

NMs can also be divided into four kinds depending on their sources (Fig. 2b) [66], as follows:

i. Natural nanomaterials (NMs): These are materials at the nanoscale that naturally arise in the environment as a result of biological species or human activity. Natural activities that include photochemical reactions, volcanic eruptions, forest fires, erosion, and the production of shed skin and hair by living creatures such as plants and animals, are among the processes that create them [69–71].

ii. Incidental non-hazardous materials (NMs): These are produced as byproducts or side products of human activities and anthropogenic processes. They can originate from a variety of sources, including road vehicles, trains, ships, airplanes, and even natural events like forest fires [71–73].

iii. Engineering nanomaterials (ENMs): Using physical, chemical, or hybrid techniques like milling or self-assembly, ENMs are purposefully created with certain features. They may influence a range of consumer goods, including textiles, food contact materials, cosmetics, and improvements in the detection and treatment of diseases. They may also be discharged into the environment via industrial or environmental uses. According to El-Kalliny et al. [66], ENMs are also connected to computer science applications, energy storage, and waste cleanup. iv. Biological NMs: NPs and nanostructures are naturally produced by living things, including viruses, bacteria, algae, and plants, as well as complexly organized mammals, birds, and humans. Because of their tiny size, certain living creatures, such as bacteria (30–700 μm) and viruses (10–400 nm), are referred to as nano-organisms. These include viruses, fungi, yeast, algae, and nanobacteria [72, 74, 75].

Preparation and synthesis of nanoparticles

NPs, which are tiny entities ranging in size from 1 to 100 nm, exhibit unique physical and chemical properties that give them tremendous value across numerous disciplines, including materials science, electronics, medicine, and environmental science. The synthesis of NPs involves a diverse range of techniques, including physical, chemical, and biological methods. Subsequently, we shall undertake a comprehensive examination of these methodologies (Fig. 3).

Different approaches for the preparation and synthesis of NPs depend on chemical, physical, and biological methods

Chemical methods

Chemical methods involve chemical reactions that lead to the formation of NPs. These methods offer precise control over the size, shape, and composition of the NPs. The sol–gel process is widely regarded as the preferred chemical method for fabricating nanomaterials, including NPs, films, nanorods, nanowires, and composites, due to its manifold intrinsic benefits. The main advantages of sol–gel chemical processes include the ease with which compositions can be controlled, as well as the ability to regulate density, homogeneity, purity, and particle growth [76]. Another chemical method is the pulsed laser method, which entails using a motor-driven solid round disc for rotation, which is a critical component following to the introduction of reducing agents [77]. By subjecting the rotating disc to laser beams, heated areas are generated on its surface [78]. Furthermore, the spray pyrolysis (SP) procedure commences with the generation of aerosol particles, which are subsequently evaporated to increase their concentration. Ultimately, the thermolysis process results in the formation of dense particles. Notably, MgO and ZrO2 nanoparticles can be produced using SP. One notable benefit of employing spray pyrolysis is its ability to generate NPs in a single phase, thereby streamlining the overall synthesis process [76]. On the other hand, the precipitation method offers several benefits, such as straightforwardness, rapid production, precise control over particle size and composition, control over particle surface state, and overall homogeneity. However, organic solvents that may harm both humans and the environment should not be used in this procedure [76].

Physical methods

Physical methods involve transforming bulk materials into NPs through physical processes. Some common physical methods for nanoparticle synthesis include ball milling and vapor phase preparation. The process used to produce nanopowders involves applying high-energy collisions induced by spheres; this method is also known as mechanical alloying or ball milling. Significantly, this methodology distinguishes itself by facilitating the accurate and uniform distribution of oxide particles within nickel-based composites, an accomplishment that is arduous to achieve using conventional powder metallurgy approaches [79]. However, physical vapor deposition (PVD) is a type of vacuum deposition technique that is specifically utilized in the fabrication of thin nanoparticle coatings [76]. The PVD is dependent on the interactions between gases capable of forming oxide, nitride, or hydride particles and the vaporized source materials. In the realm of thin-film techniques, direct current sputtering and radiofrequency sputtering are prevalent methods for generating NPs [80].

Biological methods

For the microbial production of NPs, green or microbial synthesis using microorganisms, including bacteria, actinomycetes, yeast, fungi, and algae, is preferred due to several advantageous characteristics. The aforementioned elements comprise the expeditious rate of microorganism proliferation, the simplicity of their cultivation, and their capacity to flourish in conditions of ambient temperature and pressure [81]. Moreover, it is within the nature of microorganisms to produce NPs composed of inorganic compounds; this ability is enabled by their capacity to withstand hostile metal environments via intracellular and extracellular reduction mechanisms [82]. For the synthesis of NPs, biological methods employ living organisms or their components. These methodologies provide sustainable and environmentally favorable strategies for the synthesis of nanoparticles [83].

Characterization of nanoparticles

It is crucial to carry out morphological and chemical characterization of the prepared NPs to ascertain their size, shape, structure, magnetic charge properties etc. [84]. There are currently many techniques available for characterizing NPs, including transmission electron microscopy (TEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), zeta potential, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF), atomic force microscopy (AFM), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), dynamic light scattering (DLS), nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR), and many more [85]. Mechanism of action of nanoparticles as water disinfectants

A wide range of unique nanomaterials, including CNTs, nano-Ag, nano-TiO2, nano-ZnO, and so forth, demonstrate potent antibacterial activities via the generation of ROS, the release of toxic metal ions, and the disruption of cell membrane integrity upon direct interaction. Without releasing harmful DBPs, these nanomaterials provide a secure and effective alternative to conventional disinfectants [91].

Due to its long history of antimicrobial properties, silver (Ag) and its compounds are used in water treatment and disinfection applications. It is well known that Ag-NPs exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. The Ag-NPs' chemical affinity for phosphorus and sulfur, which is essential to their biological activity, underlies their antimicrobial activity. Large amounts of free Ag+ are released by Ag-NPs, and these ions can attach to the thiol groups in proteins and enzymes to render them inactive. Additionally, Ag+ damages DNA and RNA and inhibits their replication. When exposed to ultraviolet light, Ag-NPs exhibit photocatalytic activity, making them effective antimicrobial agents [15]. Zerovalent Ag-NPs have demonstrated antimicrobial and antiviral properties, making them a promising material for use in water treatment facilities. At the moment, many water treatment technologies purify water using membranes embedded with Ag NPs. There are already some products on the market that use silver nanoparticles, such as the Aqua Pure and Marathon systems. Additionally, ceramic microfilters for pathogen disinfection use AgNPs [15].

Furthermore, TiO2 NPs are used extensively in disinfection applications today due to their strong antimicrobial activity. In the presence of UV light or sunshine, titanium dioxide nanoparticles exhibit excellent photocatalytic activity and are readily available, inexpensive, and of low toxicity [92]. The generation of hydroxyl free radicals and peroxides in the cell upon exposure to UV light is responsible for the antibacterial activity. By doping with additional metals, the antimicrobial activity of titanium dioxide can be increased. According to Ali and Ahmad [15]. TiO2NPs can also be applied as a coating, suspension, or membrane filter on the surface of a UV reactor. On the other hand, ZnO NPs exhibit potent antibacterial activity against a wide range of bacteria. ZnO NPs are still being investigated for their potential to remove organic contaminants from drinking water, much as titania. Although the antibacterial properties of ZnO NPs are not fully understood, it is thought that the particles' photocatalytic activity results from the oxidation of cell components by H2O2. According to another theory, these nanoparticles penetrate cells, disrupt the integrity of the cell membrane, and prevent bacterial growth. Zinc oxide nanoparticles have been utilized by some scientists [15].

The bactericidal activity of the material can be enhanced by varying nanoparticle size, as smaller particles have a greater impact on bacterial membrane surfaces. The thin peptidoglycan cell walls of Gram-negative cells, sometimes referred to as cell envelopes (7–8 nm), are made up of at least two layers of lipopolysaccharides. Gram-positive cells, on the other hand, usually have thick cell walls (20–80 nm) formed of a stiff layer of peptidoglycans made of sugars and amino acids [93]. A number of theories have been proposed to explain the antibacterial properties of polymeric and bio-based NPs. According to one theory, polymeric NPs bind to DNA after penetrating the cell membrane, inhibiting the synthesis of ribonucleic acid (RNA). Peptides and chitosan are employed as antimicrobial agents in membrane applications or for coating the exterior of water tanks [15]. Three different mechanisms exist for damaging bacteria: oxidative stress, intracellular penetration and damage, and cell wall and membrane disintegration. These NP sizes allow them to readily penetrate the peptidoglycan layer and generate ROS such as OH radicals and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (Fig. 5), which heighten cellular toxicity and oxidative stress, preventing cells from respiring and increases their susceptibility to harm [54, 68]

Action mechanisms of ROS through OH−, H2O2, O−22, O−2 production for microbial deactivation including cell wall rupture, nucleic acid denaturation, damage to mitochondria, nucleus and/or Golgi apparatus

The interaction between the negatively charged cell membrane and the positively charged metal/metal oxide ion disrupts the cell structure and causes cell leakage. It has also been demonstrated that nanoparticles are capable of causing asymmetric fissures in the cell wall, which allow the particles enter the periplasmic space and eventually the cell. Depending on how badly the membrane is broken, metal or metal oxide nanoparticles can enter the cell by interacting with proteins, enzymes, and DNA [94]. Cell death is thus the consequence of nanoparticles rupturing the cell wall and membrane, which negatively affects both DNA and proteins (Fig. 6).

Action mechanisms of metal/metal oxide NPs against microbial cells through the generation of ROS

Fullerene (C60) is used as a water disinfectant against pathogenic microorganisms. Fullerenes can produce ROS, leading to the rupture of bacterial and viral DNA. Membrane biofouling, a common issue in membrane filters, can be solved by fabricating a fullerene-based membrane. Certain fullerene derivatives, such as amino fullerene, which can generate singlet oxygen when exposed to visible light below 550 nm, may find application in photocatalytic water treatment. Viral inactivation and pharmaceutical degradation have both been studied using amino fullerene exposed to visible light [15].

However, there remains uncertainty regarding the precise toxicity mechanisms, necessitating further research to elucidate the toxicity pathways before nanoparticles (NPs) are applied in practical applications [95]. Applications of nanoparticles for water disinfections

Due to their distinct qualities over other bulk metal equivalents, metal NPs have garnered a lot of attention in the past 20 years. Large surface-area-to-volume ratios, large surface energies, and tunable shapes are some of the properties of metal NPs. Controlling the size, dispersity, and shape of nanoparticles is essential because these attributes dictate how they behave in the intended application [97]. Recently, efforts have been focused on developing metal NPs-based water treatment technologies, and products have entered the market. The most common metal/metal oxide NPs is Ag NPs, TiO2 NPs and ZnO NPs, and also CNTs and CNFs [98, 99].

To produce safe, clean water for drinking, medical, and medicinal purposes, water filtration is an essential process. In this procedure, potentially contaminated or polluted water sources' particulate matter, including suspended particles, bacteria, and other dangerous biological and chemical contaminants, is removed or reduced [100]. Membrane technology has seen a notable surge in interest in recent years, with nanofiltration (NF) membranes emerging as a major breakthrough. Because of their capacity to filter at the molecular level, NF membranes are engineered with a molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) for uncharged particles in the nanometer range [101]. NF membranes provide a relatively novel and effective method for purifying drinking water and wastewater, and their use has grown in popularity [102]. Positioned between ultrafiltration (UF) and reverse osmosis (RO), nanofiltration is a pressure-driven membrane technique. NF membranes have certain features, such as pore diameters of 0.5–2.0 nm and working pressures of 5–20 bar. As shown in Fig. 7, they include improved rejection capabilities compared to both RO and UF techniques, low rejection of monovalent ions, high rejection of divalent or higher-valent ions, high flux, low energy consumption, and high rejection capabilities [103].

Constructed nano-membrane technology for water purification

It is a relatively new development in membrane technology and comes in both aqueous and non-aqueous forms. Because of its unique filtration mechanism and the availability of a wide range of membranes, NF is one of the most important and widely used techniques in wastewater treatment. NF membranes are suitable for filtering out almost all organic and inorganic contaminants, including a significant number of hazardous microbes from wastewater [104, 105].

NF membranes are inexpensive, simple to make, and incredibly flexible. The two most widely used types of NF membranes are ceramic and polymeric membranes. Due to their high fouling rate and poor chemical resistance, polymeric membranes have a limited lifespan. Conversely, ceramic membranes have greater mechanical, chemical, and thermal stability. Numerous nanofilter membranes, including nanoscale zeolites and nanofibers, have been used to purify water [98]. NanoCeram, a product of Argonide Corporation in Sanford, USA, is already patented. This sol–gel process yields large amounts of this high-surface-area NanoCeram. Such a membrane uses electrostatic forces to ensnare bacteria, viruses, and proteins. The seawater treatment membrane is marketed by NanoH2O, Los Angeles, CA, USA, and is in the early stages of commercialization [15]. Some studies about applications of NPs against microorganisms

Drinking water tainted with bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, and rickettsia is a major contributor to many illnesses in underdeveloped countries, endangering human health and accounting for millions of deaths worldwide. Disinfection procedures are used to lower the amount of microorganisms in water to an acceptable level in an attempt to solve this important problem. Wastewater treatment often uses conventional disinfection techniques such as hypochlorite, reverse osmosis, chlorine dioxide, chloramines, ozone, free chlorine, and peracetic acid. Nevertheless, there are drawbacks to these techniques, such as high energy consumption, the need for expensive equipment, and the production of many disinfection byproducts (DBPs), which give rise further concerns [106]. Large surface areas and specialized reactivity characteristics are two of the special qualities of nanomaterials that make them more effective than traditional techniques for inactivating viruses in water [107]. Nanomaterials can inactivate pathogens through various mechanisms, including photochemical reactions and surface-based electrostatic interactions. These processes demonstrate the adaptability of nanomaterials in water treatment by causing cell wall breakdown, the production of ROS, and the targeted administration of disinfectants [108]. Because of their remarkable surface properties and reactivity, NPs are a viable option for wastewater disinfection, as they facilitate the suppression of microorganisms in water. Research has investigated the use of nanomaterials, including carbon nanotubes (CNTs), polymeric NPs, zinc oxide (ZnO), copper oxide (CuO), silver (Ag), and titanium dioxide (TiO2) as potential agents for the disinfection of wastewater [109–111].

Application of NPs for biofilm

Mechanism of biofilm formation and resristace

The dynamic, multi-step process of biofilm formation starts with the initial, reversible attachment of planktonic bacterial cells to biotic or abiotic surfaces. Flagella, pili, fimbriae, and the production of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) then mediate irreversible adhesion, which firmly anchors cells to the substrate and initiates community development [146]. As the biofilm matures, microorganisms multiply and release a highly hydrated EPS matrix made up mainly of proteins, lipids, polysaccharides, and extracellular DNA. This creates a three-dimensional structure with water channels that help distribute nutrients and remove waste, improving structural stability and group survival, as shown in Fig. 8 [147]. Quorum sensing, a cell-to-cell communication mechanism that allows bacterial populations to coordinate gene expression linked to adhesion, virulence factor production, matrix synthesis, and biofilm dispersal in response to population density, tightly regulates the transition from attachment to maturation [148]. Moreover, due in large part to limited antimicrobial penetration through the EPS matrix, modified microenvironmental conditions (such as oxygen and nutrient gradients), and the presence of metabolically dormant persister cells that are inherently resistant to antimicrobial stress, biofilm-associated microorganisms show noticeably higher resistance to antimicrobial agents than their planktonic cells [149]. Because of the presence of metabolically dormant persister cells, the protective EPS matrix that restricts disinfectant penetration, and the activation of stress-response and DNA repair mechanisms that allow microbial survival and regrowth following treatment, biofilms show enhanced resistance to conventional disinfectants like chlorine, UV irradiation, and ozone [150]. Biofilm communities undergo phenotypic and genetic changes that confer enhanced antimicrobial tolerance and persistence in harsh environmental conditions. These changes include the activation of stress-response pathways, the upregulation of efflux pumps, and horizontal gene transfer events (Fig. 8) [151].

Biofilm formation and resistance mechanisms against conventional water disinfectants

Biofilm control using nanomaterials

Microbial biofilms consist of cells affixed to a surface and contain a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances. Depending on the duration and location, the biofilm may contain one or more species of bacteria. Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and yeast, including Candida albicans, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus species, Klebsiella pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus, may be involved. According to microbial biofilms can form on medical advice, the inner surfaces of water pipes and tanks, and among other surfaces [152]. Moreover, the development of biofilm is a significant obstacle in this regard, since it intensifies the difficulty of managing these microbes [153]. Biofilm management can occur using conventional chemical methods such as chlorine, H2O2, and O3, but it has some limitations. Microbial biofilms can be effectively controlled and prevented by integrating NPs [152].

Hemdan et al. [7] studied the effect of Ag NPs as anti-biofilm against E. coli that formed on the surface of different pipe materials. These pipe materials were already used in drinking water distribution systems. The results evidenced that, Ag NPs are considered the most powerful antibiofilm agent against E. coli bacterium. Ag NPs were evaluated as antibiofilm using a microtiter plate assay against two MDR K. pneumoniae strains, MF953599 and MF953600, in a different study. At a concentration of 100 μg/mL of Ag NPs, the percentage biofilm inhibition was assessed to be 64% for K. pneumoniae strain MF953600 and 86% for MF953599. The toxicity results demonstrated the safety and nontoxicity of Ag NPs at concentrations 15 and 120 μg/mL. Ag NPs are safe antibacterial and antibiofilm compounds against MDR K. pneumoniae, according to this study's findings [154].

In 2018, Singh and colleagues investigated the production of Ag NPs for preventing biofilm formation. Rhodiola rosea rhizome extracts are used in a straightforward and environmentally friendly production process that doesn't require extra reducing, stabilizing, or capping age. When tested against P. aeruginosa and E. coli, Ag NPs showed minimum inhibitory concentrations of 50 and 100 mg/mL, respectively. 100 and 200 mg/mL were the corresponding minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs). According to these findings, utilizing the rhizome extracts of the medicinal plant R. rosea offers a practical method for producing green NPs with antibiofilm properties [155]. Paramagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SIONPs) were used as an antibiofilm agent to prevent the growth of P. aeruginosa on a microtiter plate, as assessed by 0.1% w/v crystal violet (CV) staining. The results exhibited that, SIONPs with 30 μg/ml concentration were able to reduce biofilm in 11 P. aeruginosa isolates [156].

The antibiofilm properties of hybrid Chitosan/CaO-Based Nanocomposites doped with plant extracts from Melia azedarach and Azadirachta indica have been investigated. Additionally, MBC were created using bacterial strains and agar plating. The synthesized nanocomposite exhibited antibacterial activity against L. monocytogenes, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and E. coli. After cultivating biofilm with sub-MICs of the synthesized nanocomposite, the crystal violet assay was used to quantify the results. As biofilm inhibitors, the hybrid Chitosan/CaO-Based Nanocomposites that were loaded with the ethanol fractions from the leaves of A. indica and M. azedarach performed exceptionally well. This suggests that they have strong potential for use in various applications [157].

Advantages and limitations of NPs as water disinfectants

Advantages of NPs as water disinfections

Among the dangerous byproducts of the strong oxidising agents (chlorine, chloramines, and ozone) currently used to disinfect water are aldehydes, haloacetic acids, and trihalomethanes. Some of these are extremely toxic and can lead to cancer in humans. However, detrimental DBPs were not produced by NMs [5]. When it comes to treating water and wastewater, nanotechnology has many benefits. It offers more effective techniques for detecting and treating water pollution, with nanomaterial-based methods being particularly successful [158]. When it comes to removing heavy metals, dyes, pesticides, and small molecules from contaminated water, nanoparticles are highly effective due to their unique properties, which set them apart from bulk materials [159]. In addition to being ecologically beneficial, nanotechnology offers a sustainable means of treating heavy metals and other pollutants [160]. Nanomaterials can remove organic matter, fungi, bacteria, and viruses from wastewater due to their broad contact area, which enables strong adsorption and reactivity [161]. Furthermore, nanotechnology offers the potential to produce less expensive nanoparticles from inexpensive, sustainable raw materials, thereby lowering operating costs in wastewater treatment facilities [162]. However, in addition to the potential and challenges of employing nanoparticles for heavy metal removal, it is necessary to consider the technological and economic viability of nanomaterials.

Green nanotechnology is becoming more and more popular as a viable method of disinfecting water [163]. This novel method calls for the ecologically benign synthesis of nanomaterials using biological extracts from plants, algae, bacteria, and fungi. Using cellular extracts to treat water and wastewater is an economical, ecologically friendly, sustainable, and energy-efficient approach [164] conducted a thorough study that focused on the production and uses of biogenic nanomaterials in wastewater treatment. Numerous studies have examined the biogenic generation of nanoparticles. The biogenic generation of palladium nanoparticles, for example, was reported by Hennebel [165], demonstrating the promise of eco-friendly approaches in nanomaterial synthesis. Furthermore, a review by Ali et al. [166] concentrated on the utilization of bacteria to create biogenic nanoparticles and their uses in the treatment of wastewater and water. Together, these findings highlight the increased interest in and promise for environmentally friendly, sustainable water disinfection solutions offered by green nanotechnology.

Carbon dots (CDs) have recently emerged as a promising class of non-toxic organic nanoparticles for water disinfection and biofilm control. CDs are ultrasmall (< 10 nm) carbon-based nanomaterials enriched with oxygen- and nitrogen-containing surface functional groups that provide high aqueous dispersibility, chemical stability, and excellent biocompatibility. Unlike many metal-based nanoparticles, CDs exhibit low cytotoxicity and minimal risk of secondary environmental contamination, making them attractive for sustainable water treatment [167]. Their antimicrobial activity is mediated through multiple mechanisms, including photo-induced generation of ROS under UV or visible light, electrostatic interaction with negatively charged microbial membranes leading to membrane disruption and leakage of intracellular contents, and intracellular oxidative damage to proteins and nucleic acids after cellular penetration [168]. In addition, CDs interfere with biofilm development by inhibiting microbial adhesion and oxidatively degrading EPS, thereby enhancing microbial susceptibility to disinfection. Owing to their environmental safety, visible-light activity, and tunable surface functionality through heteroatom doping or surface modification, carbon dots represent a promising next-generation alternative to conventional metal and metal-oxide nanomaterials for antimicrobial water treatment, although further research is required to optimize large-scale production and long-term stability in real water systems [169].

Limitations of NPs as water disinfectants

Nanotechnology-based strategies represent a transformative approach for advancing water purification technologies by enhancing antimicrobial efficiency and operational cost-effectiveness. Nevertheless, the transition from laboratory-scale investigations to real-world implementation requires comprehensive large-scale validation, standardized evaluation protocols, and long-term performance assessments. Economic feasibility remains a decisive factor, as scalable and low-cost fabrication methods are essential for widespread adoption. Furthermore, addressing potential nanotoxicity, environmental impact, and long-term material stability is crucial to ensure safe, sustainable, and regulatory-compliant application of nano-enabled water disinfection systems [170].

Conclusions and future prospective

In summary, this review paper discusses the revolutionary significance of nanotechnology in redefining water treatment systems and tackling the urgent global health challenge posed by waterborne illnesses. The unique characteristics of nanomaterials, particularly nanoparticles, have been shown to be powerful tools for disinfecting water, overcoming the drawbacks of conventional techniques. The assessment emphasizes the importance of cutting-edge water technologies made possible by nanotechnology. These methods reduce the production of hazardous disinfection byproducts by using the special antibacterial properties of nanomaterials, especially nanoparticles. This results in increased disinfection efficiency and a more ecologically friendly method. Reusing treated wastewater, made possible by nanotechnology, is becoming essential to sustainable water management. The capacity of nanotechnology to ensure wastewater treatment quality opens new applications in areas such as industrial operations and irrigation, supporting a water consumption system that is resource-efficient and circular. The assessment looks forward, discussing the sector's difficulties and opportunities. For nanotechnology to be used responsibly and widely in water treatment, it is necessary to conduct ongoing research on the toxicity of nanomaterials, optimize operational considerations, develop novel nanomaterials, and establish strong monitoring and regulatory frameworks. The potential of nanotechnology lies in its ability to deliver safe, effective, and sustainable water disinfection technologies, thereby providing a revolutionary solution to the world's water problems. With advances in research and the strengthening of multidisciplinary cooperation, nanotechnology has the potential to completely transform the water treatment industry and ensure the availability of clean water for communities around the globe.

Future perspectives

More investigation is needed to determine the potential toxicity and environmental effects of nanoparticles used in water treatment. Detailed safety standards for the use of nanotechnology in large-scale water treatment operations will require long-term research to evaluate impacts on ecosystems and human health. An examination of the past may focus on the operational features and scalability of water treatment systems that use nanotechnology as they move from labs to real-world applications. There is a need to continue focusing on the development of nanoparticles for water treatment. In the future, retrospectives may examine the development of novel, environmentally benign antimicrobial nanomaterials with tailored functions for specific infections. Retrospectives may assess the socioeconomic ramifications, acceptability, and efficacy of nanotechnology-driven water management solutions, providing insight into their applicability and usefulness across a range of communities.
