Peptide-Catalyzed Asymmetric Amination of Sulfenamides Enabled by DFT-Guided Catalyst Optimization
Authors: Nicolò Tampellini, Eun Seo Choi, Scott J. Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The enantioselective synthesis of chiral S­(IV) and S­(VI) compounds has received heightened interest from the medicinal and synthetic chemistry communities due to the improved potency and selectivity of S-chiral small molecules and agrochemicals relative to classical achiral analogs. Yet, the advancement of stereocontrolled methods to access many of these compounds is still in its infancy, particularly for highly nitrogenated species. In this work, we present a catalytic enantioselective amination of sulfenamides mediated by a peptide-based guanidine catalyst, affording desymmetrized sulfinamidines in good to excellent enantioselectivities. Highlights of the methods are the commercial availability and bench stability of the aminating reagent; the low catalyst loading; and the convenient, open-to-air room temperature setup. Catalyst optimization from a mildly selective hit was guided by DFT-based atomistic modeling, which unveiled the insufficiencies of initial and intermediate catalyst designs. The results of this work highlight an example of computationally guided catalyst optimization in generating novel asymmetric solutions to unsolved problems in asymmetric synthesis, particularly in optimizing highly tunable catalyst designs.

Introduction

Recently, stereogenic-at-sulfur compounds have received significant interest from medicinal and synthetic chemistry standpoints due to multiple advantageous traits. On top of their configurational stability, these functional groups can act as bioisosteres of traditional sulfonamides as well as carbon stereocenters, notably when mimicking tetrahedral transition states. − The medicinal and agrochemical potential of these underexplored and diverse functional groups is starting to become more widely appreciated as their accessibility is starting to be facilitated by methodological developments. , For example, IFM Therapeutics recently disclosed a new generation antagonist of the NLRP3 inflammasome featuring a chiral sulfonimidamide (Figure a). In particular, the chiral sulfonimidamide-containing analogs of (R) configuration proved consistently more potent than the previous generation nonchiral sulfonyl ureas.

Yet, the advancement of stereocontrolled methods to access many of these classes of compounds is in its infancy, even more so for highly nitrogenated, chiral sulfur­(IV) and sulfur­(VI) functional groups (Figure b). While a number of stereospecific processes have been reported to increase the oxidation state of sulfur­(IV)-containing functional groups (oxidation, nitrene transfer, − oxidative fluorination/substitution , ), access to enantioenriched sulfur­(IV) species (sulfinamides and sulfinamidines) is often required to obtain enantioenriched sulfur­(VI) species (sulfonimidamides, sulfondiimidamides). As an elegant asymmetric example, the Lopchuk group developed a modular, stereospecific multistep synthesis of sulfonimidamides starting from commercially available, enantiopure Ellman’s tert-butane sulfinamide. Alternatively, some S­(VI) desymmetrization approaches have been reported by our group and others. −

In contrast, catalytic, asymmetric access to nitrogenated sulfur­(IV) compounds usually requires breaking symmetry at the earlier sulfur­(II) stage (sulfenamides), either by asymmetric oxidation or amination (Figure b). While there is robust precedent for asymmetric oxidation − of these species, asymmetric amination of sulfenamides has been much less explored. While a report from the Saito group established a stoichiometric approach based on a novel camphor-derived oxaziridine (Figure c), a catalytic, asymmetric amination of sulfenamides remains highly desirable. To the best of our knowledge, only one catalytic methodology has been reported to date, and relies on a copper-catalyzed SH2 reaction from an aminyl radical. While highly stereoselective, this methodology is limited to secondary amines.

Inspired by these reports, we envisioned a catalytic, asymmetric amination of sulfenamides via the action of a chiral base (or cation) steering the reactivity of a sulfenamidate anion toward a readily available, electrophilic aminating reagent (Figure d). We discovered that a commercial O-acylhydroxylamine serves as the ideal candidate, and that peptidyl guanidines , can be optimized to be excellent catalysts for the transformation. Catalyst optimization toward high selectivity was guided by computational atomistic modeling of the reaction, which revealed how the modulation of key moieties on the catalyst contributed to achieving high enantioselectivity across a broad range of substrates.

Results and Discussion

Our investigation began by surveying the reactivity of different aminating reagents toward acyl sulfenamides. Notably, we found commercially available O-(4-nitrobenzoyl)­hydroxylamine 3 to be a convenient, bench stable, and highly effective reagent for the desired amino group transfer. In the presence of an inorganic base (K2CO3) and catalytic amounts of organic bases, a single amino group was cleanly transferred to sulfenamides, yielding sulfinamidines (i.e., 2a, Figure a) with no detectable trace of doubly aminated sulfondiimidamides (i.e., 2aa). We speculate that base-catalyzed activation of the substrate is related to an alpha-effect type enhancement of nucleophilicity. , Quantum mechanical analysis of 1a and its anion show how deprotonation causes a dramatic increase of the HOMO orbital energy, which features its largest coefficient on sulfur (see Section 6 of the Supporting Information for computational details). With this first proof of concept of a catalytic paradigm, we turned our attention to enantioselective catalysis.

While several classes of chiral bases were competent catalysts for the reaction, most failed to induce any significant degree of enantioinduction (catalysts 9, 10, , 11, Figure b). In contrast, a promising avenue for optimization was offered by tetrameric guanidine-containing peptides, , one of which afforded the best selectivity in the initial screen. Tetrameric peptide catalyst 4 afforded sulfinamidines 2a in 65:35 er in CH2Cl2 (70:30 er in CDCl3, under otherwise identical conditions). Of note, of the numerous peptide-based catalysts of this type that we screened (as part of a large library of over 60), no readily extractable homologies emerged upon which to design a catalyst optimization campaign (See Figure S4 in the Supporting Information for a sample of the screened catalysts). Instead, we posited that atomistic computational modeling of the reaction with DFT calculations might better inform catalyst optimization. Cognizant of the many challenges in the computational design of enantioselective catalysts, in particular with conformationally flexible systems, − we were nonetheless encouraged by the increasing sophistication of DFT-based modeling of asymmetric catalysis. − To apply this strategy, we started by modeling the reaction mechanism with a simplified catalyst (pentamethylguanidine, see the Supporting Information for computational methodologies and details).

We were able to validate a working hypothesis of a base-accelerated reaction where the sulfenamide anion [1a-H]− attacks the nitrogen atom of 3. The substitution at nitrogen occurs via a trigonal bipyramidal transition structure, in line with the known requirement for substitution at neutral nitrogen investigated by the seminal work of Beak. , This computational analysis also excluded a nucleophilic catalysis mechanism, which appeared much higher in energy (See Section 6 of the Supporting Information). Next, we modeled the enantiodetermining amination transition state in a conformationally exhaustive way using hit catalyst 4 (Figure a). We were not surprised to observe a plethora of energetically accessible activation modes, with eight topologically distinct ones within 2 kcal/mol (G298.15 K) from the most stable. This observation prompted us to devise a strategy to bias the interaction landscape toward a single interaction mode. Inspecting the three-dimensional structures, we observed that steric tuning of the methyl group of the acetamido arm on initial catalyst 4 could prove pivotal in achieving this outcome, as sterically demanding groups only appeared geometrically compatible with one activation mode leading to the (S) enantiomer of 1b (Figure a, left, teal), while causing a steric incompatibility with other modes (Figure a, right, red). Excitingly, catalyst analogs incorporating the sterically more demanding diphenylacetamido (Figure b, 5, pink) and dicyclohexylacetamido (Figure b, 6, pink) moieties quickly delivered more selective catalysts, with up to 79:21 er for product 2a (Figure b). Still, additional increases in selectivity were desired. On the positive side, modeling of the reaction with catalyst 6 did in fact display a single energetically accessible mode, as we had hoped. Thus, with the goal of further separating the energy of favored and disfavored pathways, we attempted to model different reaction manifolds that could account for the imperfect selectivity of catalyst 6. Transition states with different catalyst and product molecularity were assessed, but no better pathway leading to the minor enantiomer was found (best pro-R transition state features single molecules of catalyst 6, sulfenamide 1a, and aminating reagent 3, at +4.7 kcal/mol relative to the lowest energy pro-S, see Table S3). We thus speculated that the minor enantiomer could still be generated through other background pathways, perhaps not involving the catalyst. These could become increasingly relevant if product inhibition reduced the active concentration of the catalyst, via strong complexation of either product 2a or the carboxylate resulting from aminating reagent 3. Both these species can form stable complexes with guanidinium ions and might be involved in parasitic processes (see Table S2 for computed thermodynamic equilibrium energetics). This hypothesis found some experimental validation, in that decreased conversion and selectivity were observed conducting the reaction in the presence of one equivalent of racemic product (See Figure S7 in the Supporting Information). Further substantiation of this hypothesis was provided by the beneficial effects of sequestering the potassium p-nitrobenzoate with the addition of water to the reaction mixture (see further section on reaction scale-up).

We also found computational support for a product inhibition hypothesis as the catalyst turnover step appeared endergonic: displacing the products of the reaction from the catalyst to exchange with the starting material in order to restart the catalytic cycle was associated with a positive free energy (Figure and Table S4). This insight generated two further optimization strategies: 1) tuning the catalyst to favor product release and starting material binding (by reducing ΔG°turnover), and 2) accelerating the catalyzed reaction (by reducing ΔG‡). These approaches are conceptually analogous to reducing the so-called “energetic span” of the catalytic cycle. Both modifications could help improve the efficiency of the enantioselective catalytic cycle over spurious stereorandom processes. We envisioned that inverting the stereochemistry at the i+3 position of the catalyst (from L to D) could achieve the goal of lowering the reaction activation energy by providing a larger, less hindered pocket to accommodate aminating reagent 3 to react with the bound substrate (Figure b). Computationally, all i+3 analogs modeled retained the transition state topology of catalyst 6 (LPhe). However, while catalyst 6 and 7 (LPhe and DPhe) showed similar activation and turnover energies (ΔG‡ and ΔG°turnover), catalyst 8 (DPhg) featured a significant decrease of both quantities, which was attributed to an unexpected additional hydrogen bond interaction with aminating reagent 3 in the favored transition state. The DPhg residue of 8, creating more space on the top face of the catalyst compared to DPhe-containing 7, alters the trajectory of incoming aminating reagent 3, which is now able to also interact with the i residue carbonyl oxygen (TMGA, tetramethylguanidylalanine, see Figure for energetic data and Figure S11 for a three-dimensional view of the transition states and the hydrogen bonds).

Indeed, appreciable improvements in selectivity were achieved, qualitatively in line with the energetic data extracted from the modeling (product 2a: from 79:21 er with 6 to 82:18 er with 8; product 2b: from 78:22 er with 6 to 84:16 er with 8; Figure and Table S3). At this stage, with improved catalyst 8 in hand, we returned to the issue of turnover, which was likely continuing to be responsible for stereochemical leakage.

In parallel, we conducted a small screen of substituted benzamides, which also proved to illuminate critical interactions. We noticed a significant effect on the ring substitution pattern on selectivity, with meta-substituted benzamides performing consistently better than the relative para isomers, with a new best selectivity for meta-iodo product 2e (92:8 er with catalyst 8; Figure ). Intriguingly, the positive influence of the meta substitution on selectivity was directly reflected computationally in a lower ΔG°turnover (Figure and Table S3).

The apparent turnover-favoring effect of meta substituents prompted us to seek a rationale for the observed trend. From the computed structural models, it appeared that the preferred conformation of the catalyst-substrate complexes of meta-substituted substrates 1c–e was consistent with the meta substituent facing the interior part of the binding pocket with the catalyst (see Figure S12 for a three-dimensional view). We speculate that this might be due to a good surface complementarity and substituent polarizability of the moieties leading to a large dispersion energy contribution in dictating the preferred conformation, overriding steric effects. , This hypothesis was qualitatively validated by noncovalent interaction plotting, which confirmed the presence of a large attractive interaction surface. Interestingly, we also obtained a good correlation between the turnover free energy (ΔG°turnover, as defined in Figure ) and the observed selectivity (as ΔΔG‡ obs), with a Pearson correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.88, validating the intimate relationship between the turnover energy and selectivity across different substrates and catalysts (see Table S4 for energetic data and Figure S13 for the associated plot).

With optimized catalyst 8 in hand, we fine-tuned the reaction conditions to achieve robust and convenient access to enantioenriched material on larger scales (0.05–0.1 mmol, initial optimization conducted on 0.01 mmol scale). During exploration experiments on these larger scales, we noticed at first a less consistent conversion and selectivity profile among certain substrates, in some cases with consistently reduced selectivity relative to the optimization scale. We reasoned that the heterogeneity of the reaction mixture (due to K2CO3, KHCO3 and potassium p-nitrobenzoate), could be the reason behind scalability issues. Remarkably, the addition of water to the reaction (1:1 v/v ratio with the solvent) was found to be an operationally simple solution to address these issues, leading to a homogeneous, biphasic system with results comparable to water-free conditions, as well as excellent reproducibility and scalability. The beneficial effect of water in larger scale reactions is thus interpreted not as a mechanistic divergence from the water-free conditions, but as a solution to favor bulk-phase reactivity over, possibly, solid–liquid interface processes. These biphasic conditions also allowed further fine-tuning of reaction conditions, most notably by switching the solvent to toluene (0.025 M w.r.t. sulfenamides 1) and decreasing the catalyst loading to 2 mol % of 8 (optimization carried out for product 2j, 97:3 er, 78% isolated yield under final conditions. See Table S1 for a complete optimization table).

With the optimized conditions in hand, we assessed the reaction scope for different sulfenamide starting materials. The meta-substitution effect we observed during catalyst optimization was still present under fully optimized conditions, with all meta-substituted substrates (1c–i) showing better selectivity than meta-H 1a (Figure ). For example, in the S-aryl series, selectivities ranged from 91:9 er (2g) to 96:4 er (2i). Surveying substitution effects on sulfur, we were pleased to observe that primary alkyl (2j, 2n, 2o; each 97:3 er), secondary alkyl (2k, 97:3 er; 2p, 96:4 er) and even tertiary alkyl products (2l, > 99:1 er; 2m, 98:2 er) were all obtained with high enantioselectivity. Tert-butyl containing product 2l showed the best selectivity at >99:1 er. Pyridyl substrate 2q, on the other hand, exhibited reduced selectivity relative to benzene analog 2e (88:12 er versus 95:5 er, respectively), perhaps due to the emergence of other noncovalent interactions. Still, pyridyl rings appear to be tolerated. In a more complex, drug-inspired example, Celecoxib-derived sulfenamide 1s was converted into sulfinamidine 2s in 92:8 er (60% isolated yield), showcasing a multistep strategy , to convert primary sulfonamides into chiral, enantioenriched sulfur­(IV) functional building blocks with high nitrogen content. Notably, this reaction could be scaled up to 1 mmol, still employing 2 mol% of catalyst and maintaining identical conditions, to give 2s with identical er (92:8 er) and with an isolated yield of 49% (239 mg) after a single chromatographic purification.

Of great reassurance, we were also able to obtain a crystal of enantioenriched product 2i. Heavy atom X-ray crystallography confirmed the absolute stereochemistry of the S­(IV) atom in the products as (S), as predicted by the computational modeling.

Following literature precedents on synthetic elaboration of sulfinamidines, ,,,,, the products obtained with this methodology are expected to be convenient entry points into numerous enantioenriched sulfur­(VI) functional groups like sulfonimidamides and sulfondiimides, with cleavage of the substituted benzoyl protecting group at the sulfur­(VI) stage. ,

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have developed an asymmetric, catalytic amination of sulfenamides to sulfinamidines, providing efficient catalytic access to enantioenriched sulfur­(IV) species with high nitrogen content. The reaction is mediated by a peptide-based guanidine catalyst in a biphasic reaction medium. The optimization of the catalyst from a mildly selective hit was both examined and guided by atomistic DFT modeling. This quantified the energetics of different processes and hinted at specific issues that limited the selectivity of the system, like shallow activation mode landscapes and positive turnover free energy. The synergy of atomistic modeling and experimental exploration culminated in the highly active and selective catalyst 8, able to afford sulfinamidine products 2a–s in up to >99:1 er at room temperature, mostly in good to excellent yields. We envision that the optimization strategy of this work and the new peptidyl catalyst 8 will both inspire the development of new asymmetric methodologies as well as constitute a convenient entry point into the chemical space of enantioenriched, highly nitrogenated sulfur species.
