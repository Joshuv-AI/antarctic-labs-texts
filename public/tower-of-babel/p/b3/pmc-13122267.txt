Endoscopic Classification of Severe Spontaneous Epistaxis: A Case‐Series of 445 Patients
Authors: Wen Zhang, Guangjin Chen, Jérôme R. Lechien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo identify the anatomical location and characterize the histopathological findings of severe spontaneous epistaxis through endoscopic evaluation and tissue biopsy.MethodsA retrospective analysis of prospectively recruited 445 consecutive patients with severe spontaneous epistaxis was conducted at a tertiary medical center between January 2022 and December 2024. All patients underwent systematic endoscopic assessment to localize the bleeding site. Based on endoscopic morphological characteristics, cases were classified as papillary epithelioid elevation‐related epistaxis (PEE) or mucosal erosive elevation‐related epistaxis (MEE). Lesional tissue was excised when feasible for histopathological analysis. Clinical characteristics, hemorrhagic patterns, therapeutic responses, and clinical outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsAmong 445 patients, 358 (80.4%) presented with PEE, while 87 (19.6%) demonstrated MEE. Histopathological examination revealed lobular capillary hemangioma in all 177 biopsied PEE lesions, representing 39.8% of the total cohort. PEE exhibited higher proportions of arterial/pulsatile bleeding (52.2% vs. 24.1%, p < 0.001) and severe blood loss (26.5% vs. 11.5%, p = 0.009) compared to MEE. Anatomical distribution patterns differed significantly (p < 0.001): PEE demonstrated widespread involvement throughout the nasal cavity, including olfactory cleft (20.4%) and inferior meatus (11.7%), whereas MEE predominantly localized to the anterior nasal septum (80.5%). Surgical intervention achieved superior cure rates compared to conservative management in both PEE (95.0% vs. 13.8%, p < 0.001) and MEE (89.7% vs. 58.3%, p < 0.001) groups.ConclusionEndoscopic morphological classification into PEE and MEE phenotypes is associated with distinct bleeding patterns and treatment responses. Lobular capillary hemangioma was the most frequently identified histopathological finding among biopsied PEE lesions; however, given incomplete pathological sampling, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive evidence of causality.Evidence Level4.

Introduction

Epistaxis is one of the most common emergencies in otolaryngology, with a lifetime prevalence ranging from 60% to 80% in the general population [1]. It is estimated that up to 10% of cases require medical intervention, with 1%–2% associated with a significant survival risk [2, 3, 4]. Severe spontaneous epistaxis, which is defined as persistent or recurrent bleeding despite nasal pressure, is a challenging condition associated with a significant cost burden for healthcare resources. The anterior nasal septum, particularly the Kiesselbach's plexus area, has long been recognized as the most frequent site for epistaxis, accounting for about 90% of cases [5, 6]. Historically, this anatomical predilection was attributed to the rich vascular anastomoses between external and internal carotid branches in this region. However, the exact pathophysiological mechanisms triggering spontaneous bleeding from these sites remain incompletely understood, with the following traditional contributing factors: mucosal dryness, trauma, hypertension, anticoagulant therapy, and local inflammation [7, 8]. To date, the histopathological characteristics of bleeding sites in severe spontaneous epistaxis have received minimal attention in the literature, with most studies focused on anatomical localization and treatment outcomes. Interestingly, a few pathological reports have described the presence of vascular malformations, hemangioma‐like lesions, or granulation tissue at bleeding sites [9, 10], but systematic histopathological studies in large cohorts are still lacking [1, 11].

The aim of this study was to characterize the morphological and histological patterns of the nasal bleeding sites in severe spontaneous epistaxis and to compare the treatment outcomes according to lesion types.

Methods

Settings and Patients

This study is a retrospective analysis of consecutive prospectively recruited patients with severe spontaneous epistaxis requiring emergency intervention, conducted at the Affiliated Taizhou People's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Taizhou, Jiangsu, China) between January 2022 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Taizhou Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval no. LSKY 2025–074‐01). Informed consent was waived given the retrospective nature of the analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional guidelines. The study was initiated after repeated observations of a substantial proportion of nasal bleeding sites exhibiting discrete papillary elevations with characteristic morphological features, rather than simple mucosal erosions or exposed vessels.

Inclusion criteria consisted of (1) presence of severe spontaneous epistaxis requiring specialist otolaryngologic assessment and intervention (ranging from hospital‐grade conservative packing to surgical arrest) at initial presentation; (2) complete medical records including detailed endoscopic findings; and (3) a minimum follow‐up period of 6 months. The following exclusion criteria were considered: (1) traumatic epistaxis (including nasal surgery, foreign bodies, or obvious external trauma); (2) epistaxis secondary to sinonasal tumors; (3) Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia (Osler–Weber–Rendu syndrome) patients; (4) identified systemic bleeding disorders (e.g., hemophilia, von Willebrand disease, severe thrombocytopenia (< 20,000/μL)); and (5) active cocaine or other intranasal drug abuse.

During the study period, our center managed approximately 3348 cases of epistaxis. The 445 patients included in this study represent a specific subset (approximately 13.3%) presenting with severe, intractable bleeding requiring hospitalization or advanced intervention, distinct from the routine mild epistaxis cases managed in the outpatient setting.

Systematic Endoscopic Assessment

Patients underwent a systematic endoscopic assessment (SEA) using 0° and 30° rigid endoscopes (Karl Storz, Germany), performed under local anesthesia (4% lidocaine plus 1:1000 epinephrine) or general anesthesia, depending on the clinical emergency and patient tolerance. The following steps were carried out: (1) removal of clots and secretions to visualize the nasal cavity, (2) sequential inspection of all anatomical nasal regions, including the nasal septum (anterior, middle, posterior), lateral nasal wall, turbinates (inferior, middle, superior), nasal floor, and olfactory cleft, (3) bleeding site identification through the description of the active bleeding site or the report of clot, papillary elevation, or mucosal erosion; (4) morphological documentation of lesion appearance, size, color, surface characteristics, and bleeding pattern, and (5) photographic capture of lesions through high‐resolution endoscopic images.

Based on the endoscopic morphology, bleeding sites were classified as: papillary epithelioid elevation‐related epistaxis (PEE) or mucosal erosive elevation‐related epistaxis (MEE). The diagnostic criteria for PEE were defined as follows: any visible mound‐like elevation protruding from the nasal mucosa at the bleeding site, regardless of size (typically 0.1–1.5 cm). These lesions appeared round or hemispherical, with a bright red or pale red surface. A defining feature was active bleeding—often arterial or pulsatile—located at the apex or surrounding the base of the elevation, triggered by minimal contact. Clinicians carefully differentiated PEE from submucosal vascular dilations, which typically presented as elongated, tubular vascular prominences rather than discrete, raised tissue masses (see Figure 1 for differentiation). In borderline cases where mucosal erosion and slight elevation coexisted, classification was based on the predominant morphological feature at the active bleeding point, acknowledging an inherent degree of subjectivity in endoscopic assessment.

Clinical Assessment and Outcomes

Bleeding volume was estimated using a standardized tissue saturation method consisting of the use of standard tissue paper (20 cm × 20 cm) applied to the bleeding site. A fully saturated tissue was estimated to contain 10–15 mL of blood. In some cases, medical gauze pads (5 cm × 5 cm) were used, with each fully saturated pad containing 12 mL of blood. For active bleeding, direct clinical aspiration and measurement of blood loss were used when possible with the aspiration device, paper, or gauze pads. Mild (blood loss: < 75 mL), moderate (blood loss: 75–200 mL), and severe bleeding (blood loss > 200 mL) findings were collected.

The following outcomes were additionally collected: bleeding duration and frequency; hemodynamic compromising findings (tachycardia, hypotension, orthostatic symptoms); hemoglobin drop > 20 g/L from baseline; transfusion requirement; patient‐reported symptoms (dizziness, weakness, syncope); and bleeding flow. The bleeding flow was considered as slow (persistent slow trickle, non‐pulsatile, forming droplets), moderate (steady continuous flow, without obvious pulsation, forming a continuous stream), or rapid (arterial/pulsatile bleeding, with rapid pooling of blood). When possible, all assessments were independently evaluated by two otorhinolaryngologists. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion to reach consensus.

Histopathological Examination and Outcomes

Histopathological sampling was selective and limited to technically accessible PEE lesions. MEE lesions were not routinely biopsied, and therefore alternative vascular or benign proliferative lesions cannot be excluded.

For PEE patients, excisional biopsy was performed for technically accessible lesions without a significant risk to patient health. Lesions were sent to the pathology department fixed in 10% formalin and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. All pathological lesions were analyzed by a board‐certified head and neck pathologist.

Treatment Protocols and Outcomes

Regarding the patient and bleeding features, the therapeutic strategies consisted of conservative approaches (i.e., topical vasoconstrictors, anterior nasal packing, posterior packing with Foley catheter or posterior packs, supportive care (intravenous fluids, transfusion), treatment of hypertension and coagulation) or surgical procedures (e.g., electrocoagulation, excisional biopsy). In all cases, the decision to proceed with surgical intervention was based on the failure of conservative measures, endoscopic identification of a discrete bleeding lesion amenable to targeted therapy, or patient/physician preference after discussion of risks and benefits.

Patients were followed for 6 months. Treatment failure was defined as any recurrence of epistaxis requiring medical intervention during the follow‐up period. Patients with treatment failure underwent repeated intervention, and subsequent outcomes were tracked. Patients were followed at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post‐discharge. Note that phone follow‐up was carried out for patients unable to attend in‐person visits.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the normality of distribution assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage. Continuous variables between PEE and MEE groups were compared using independent t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, and categorical variables were compared using chi‐squared tests. Contingency table analysis was performed for differences in bleeding site distribution between epistaxis types and between PEE patients with and without pathology. Due to low expected frequencies (< 5) in some cells, the Monte Carlo chi‐squared test (with fixed row and column margins and 100,000 random samples) was performed in addition to the conventional Pearson chi‐squared test to obtain robust significance estimates. All tests were two‐sided. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Monte Carlo chi‐squared test was implemented in Python (using scipy and custom randomization algorithms), with a two‐sided significance level of α = 0.05.

To control for the potential confounding effects of antiplatelet/anticoagulant use and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), a multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of “severe bleeding.” To address the issue of multiple testing, a Bonferroni correction was applied to the alpha level for post hoc pairwise comparisons to reduce the risk of Type I errors.

Missing Data Handling

Data were analyzed on a complete‐case basis. Patients with incomplete follow‐up (< 6 months) or those who underwent surgical intervention without a complete preoperative endoscopic map (n = 12) were excluded from the final analysis to ensure data integrity.

Results

Patients and Settings

A total of 445 patients met the inclusion criteria, including 241 males (54.2%) and 204 females (45.8%; Table 1). The mean age of patients was 57.9 ± 16.8 years (range: 11–94 years). Age distribution peaked in the 5th and 6th decades, with 126 patients (28.3%) aged 50–59 years and 99 patients (22.2%) aged 60–69 years. The epistaxis was localized in the left nasal cavity in 236 patients (53.0%), while 209 patients (47.0%) had right‐sided bleeding. Common comorbidities included hypertension (193 patients, 43.4%), diabetes mellitus (63 patients, 14.2%), and coronary artery disease (45 patients, 10.1%). Regarding medication use, 67 patients (15.1%) were on antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs. Most patients presented with moderate bleeding (279 patients, 62.7%), while 61 patients (13.7%) had mild bleeding and 105 patients (23.6%) reported severe bleeding. Bleeding flow was considered as slow in 87 patients (19.6%), moderate in 150 patients (33.7%), and severe in 208 patients (46.7%). In the male group, the following biological outcomes were found: mean RBC of 4.5 ± 0.5 × 1012/L; mean hemoglobin of 140.9 ± 15.9 g/L; and mean hematocrit of 42.3% ± 4.4%. In the female group, the mean RBC was 4.2 ± 0.5 × 1012/L, the mean hemoglobin was 125.4 ± 16.8 g/L, and the mean hematocrit was 38.5% ± 4.4%.

Endoscopic Classification

Overall Patterns

Inter‐observer reliability analysis was performed to validate the endoscopic findings. The agreement between two independent observers for the PEE versus MEE classification was substantial, with a kappa value of 0.795 (SE = 0.047, z = 16.802, p < 0.01). Similarly, the classification of nasal septal deviation demonstrated strong agreement (kappa = 0.852, SE = 0.020, z = 41.936, p < 0.01). It should be noted that the grading of bleeding volume and flow rate was derived from the initial emergency assessments by attending physicians and was not subject to inter‐observer reliability testing.

Based on systematic endoscopic assessment, two distinct morphological patterns were identified: PEE in 358 patients (80.4%) and MEE in 87 patients (19.6%). In the PEE group, the lesions appeared as discrete papillary or polypoid elevations (0.1–1.5 cm in diameter) with a smooth, pale red to deep red surface, and exhibited characteristic arterial pulsatile bleeding upon minimal contact, with minimal surrounding inflammatory changes (Figure 1). Laterality distribution showed 192 left‐sided (53.6%) and 166 right‐sided (46.4%) cases. MEE commonly presented as mucosal erosions or ulcerations with surrounding inflammation, exposed submucosal vessels, and often significant inflammatory exudate. The bleeding pattern was predominantly continuous rather than pulsatile. Laterality distribution was relatively balanced, with 44 left‐sided (50.6%) and 43 right‐sided (49.4%) cases (Figure 2).

PEE and MEE Comparison

The comparison of clinical findings between PEE and MEE is reported in Table 2. PEE and MEE groups were comparable for age distribution, biological sex ratio, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, comorbidity, biological parameters (RBC, hemoglobin, hematocrit), and bleeding laterality distribution. Antiplatelet/anticoagulant use was more common in MEE compared to PEE patients (33.3% vs. 10.6%, p < 0.001). Clinically, the PEE group reported a significantly higher proportion of severe bleeding compared to the MEE group (26.5% vs. 11.5%; p = 0.009). A similar observation was found for the proportion of severe/pulsatile bleeding, which was significantly higher in PEE compared to MEE patients (52.2% vs. 24.1%; p = 0.001). The distribution of bleeding sites showed significant differences between PEE and MEE groups (χ 2 = 32.872, p < 0.001). Due to low frequencies (< 5) in some categories of the bleeding site distribution table, categories with sparse frequencies (e.g., middle meatus, nasal floor, turbinate surfaces) were merged to form a 2 × 5 contingency table for robustness; the result remained significant (χ 2 = 25.938, p < 0.0001). Thus, the distribution of bleeding sites differed significantly between the two epistaxis types, and the Monte Carlo simulation confirmed the robustness of this result, consistent with the conventional chi‐squared test. While both groups primarily involved the anterior nasal septum, this concentration was more pronounced in MEE (70 patients, 80.5%) than in PEE (187 patients, 52.2%). PEE exhibited a markedly wider anatomical distribution throughout the nasal cavity. Besides the anterior nasal septum, significant proportions were observed in the olfactory cleft (73 patients, 20.4%), inferior meatus (42 patients, 11.7%), middle/posterior nasal septum (25 patients, 7.0%), medial/inferior surfaces of the turbinates (12 patients, 3.4%), and nasal floor (11 patients, 3.1%). Other sites of the lateral nasal wall were rare in PEE (5 patients, 1.4%), and middle meatus involvement was least common (3 patients, 0.8%). In contrast, MEE showed a highly concentrated distribution pattern. Following the primary anterior nasal septum location (80.5%), only the olfactory cleft (9 patients, 10.3%) and other sites of the lateral nasal wall (4 patients, 4.6%) showed notable involvement. Other sites including the inferior meatus (1 patient, 1.1%), medial/inferior turbinate surfaces (2 patients, 2.3%), and middle/posterior nasal septum (1 patient, 1.1%) were rarely affected. No MEE cases were observed in the nasal floor or middle meatus.

Although anticoagulant/antiplatelet use was more frequent in the MEE group, the multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that the PEE morphology remained an independent predictor of severe bleeding (odds ratio: 3.49, 95% CI: [1.98,6.14], p < 0.05) after adjusting for medication use, age, and hypertension. This suggests that the bleeding severity in PEE is likely driven by intrinsic vascular pathology rather than medication‐induced coagulopathy alone.

Histopathological Findings

Of the 358 PEE patients, 177 (49.4%) underwent accurate histopathological examination. The remaining 181 patients (50.6%) did not undergo biopsy due to patient refusal (n = 125, 69.1%), clinical judgment deeming biopsy unnecessary given the characteristic endoscopic appearance (n = 45, 24.9%), or technical failure to obtain adequate specimens in tight anatomical spaces (n = 11, 6.1%). The 177 biopsied specimens revealed consistent histopathological features diagnostic of lobular capillary hemangioma (LCH; Figure 1). There were no malignancies or other arteriovenous malformations. In the PEE group, 154/177 patients (87.0%) had a lesion with a diameter ≤ 0.4 cm, while 18 (10.2%) and 5 (2.8%) had lesions of 0.4–1.0 cm and > 1.0 cm, respectively. No histopathological examination was performed for MEE patients because the therapeutic strategy primarily consisted of conservative management or simple cauterization without tissue excision.

Treatment Modalities and Outcomes

Treatment strategies depended on the morphological pattern (Table 3 and Figure 2). In the PEE group, conservative management was used in 116 patients (32.4%), whereas 242 patients (67.6%) underwent surgical intervention (primarily electrocoagulation or excisional biopsy). In contrast, a higher proportion of MEE patients received conservative management (48 patients, 55.2%), with 39 patients (44.8%) undergoing surgical cauterization without tissue excision. In the PEE group, conservative management resulted in a low 6‐month success rate (n = 16/116; 13.8%), with most patients requiring re‐intervention. In contrast, surgical intervention in PEE patients achieved a high success rate (n = 230/242; 95%). In the MEE group, conservative management achieved a success rate of 58.3% (n = 28/48), with other patients requiring surgical intervention. The inter‐group comparison revealed that the conservative treatment‐related success rate was significantly higher in MEE compared to PEE (p < 0.001).

Note that PEE patients with and without pathological confirmation of the diagnosis did not differ in terms of demographic features, bleeding volume and flow, anatomical distribution, RBC, and hemoglobin (Appendix 1). Discussion

This study is the largest systematic investigation of histopathological patterns in severe spontaneous epistaxis through detailed endoscopic assessment. Our findings suggest two distinct morphological types—PEE and MEE—with substantially different pathophysiological mechanisms, anatomical distributions, and therapeutic responses. The most striking observation is the high prevalence of LCH in PEE patients, identified in all 177 biopsied cases (39.8% of the total cohort). It is worth noting that LCH is the currently accepted histopathological term for lesions historically referred to as “pyogenic granulomas” of the mucous membranes [11]. Therefore, the absence of “granulomas” in our pathology reports reflects a terminology update rather than a clinical anomaly, clarifying the potential query regarding the differential diagnosis.

While previous studies have noted LCH as a cause of epistaxis, these were largely limited to case reports or small series. This study represents a large‐scale systematic histopathological investigation using endoscopy. We acknowledge that only 49.4% of PEE patients underwent biopsy, introducing potential selection bias. However, the detection of LCH in all 177 biopsied cases (39.8% of the total cohort) remains a significant finding. Even accounting for the patients who were not biopsied, this high absolute number suggests that LCH is a critical and historically under‐recognized pathological mechanism in severe spontaneous epistaxis, rather than a rare occurrence. Nevertheless, given the incomplete sampling, these findings should be viewed as evidence warranting further investigation rather than definitive proof of causality for all PEE cases. The clinical significance of this finding is substantial: PEE exhibited markedly different bleeding characteristics compared to MEE, including higher proportions of arterial/pulsatile bleeding (52.2% vs. 24.1%) and severe blood loss (26.5% vs. 11.5%). Moreover, the anatomical distribution patterns differed significantly, with PEE demonstrating widespread involvement throughout the nasal cavity—particularly in the olfactory cleft (20.4%), inferior meatus (11.7%), and lateral nasal wall (7.0%)—while MEE predominantly localized to the anterior nasal septum (80.5%). These morphological distinctions directly have treatment implications. Surgical intervention achieved superior success rates in both groups compared to conservative management, with the difference being particularly highlighted in PEE patients. However, treatment recommendations should not be overly prescriptive. Treatment decisions should therefore be individualized, integrating lesion morphology, bleeding severity, patient comorbidities, and patient preference rather than relying on morphological classification alone. Conservative management remains a valid first‐line approach for patients with smaller lesions, those at high surgical risk, or based on patient preference. The decision to proceed to surgery implies a selection bias, as surgery was often reserved for cases where conservative measures failed or lesions were distinctively accessible. Furthermore, our follow‐up period was limited to 6 months; long‐term recurrence rates for both modalities require further investigation in longitudinal studies. Importantly, our data demonstrate that PEE patients with and without pathological confirmation exhibited similar demographic features, bleeding characteristics, anatomical distribution, and therapeutic outcomes, supporting the clinical utility of endoscopic morphological classification alone for guiding treatment decisions. This finding has important practical implications: in emergency settings where immediate histopathological confirmation is not feasible, the characteristic endoscopic appearance of papillary elevations with arterial bleeding can reliably guide therapeutic strategy toward surgical excision or electrocoagulation rather than conservative management.

The high prevalence of LCH in our cohort contrasts with traditional epistaxis literature, which primarily focused on anatomical localization and risk factors without systematic histopathological examination [12]. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, the small size of most lesions (87.0%, ≤ 0.4 cm) makes them easily overlooked during conventional anterior rhinoscopy, particularly in the presence of active bleeding. Second, routine clinical practice rarely includes detailed endoscopic evaluation or histopathological confirmation of bleeding sites, leading to potential underdiagnosis [1, 11]. Third, LCH can be mistaken for inflammatory polyps or granulation tissue without tissue examination. Our systematic endoscopic assessment protocol addressed these limitations, enabling precise lesion characterization and targeted biopsy where feasible. The pathophysiology of LCH remains incompletely understood, though upregulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), local trauma, and abnormal vascular proliferative responses have been implicated [13, 14]. The higher prevalence of antiplatelet/anticoagulant use in MEE patients suggests that medication‐induced coagulopathy may contribute to mucosal erosion and bleeding in this subgroup, while PEE development appears driven primarily by intrinsic vascular proliferation rather than medication factors.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data may introduce information bias, although this was mitigated through standardized endoscopic assessment protocols and detailed documentation. Second, histopathological confirmation was not obtained in all PEE patients due to patient refusal, clinical judgment, or technical difficulties in some tight anatomical spaces. However, the remarkable similarity in clinical features and treatment responses between biopsied and non‐biopsied PEE cases provides indirect but consistent support for LCH as a frequent underlying pathology among PEE lesions. Third, certain clinical assessments—particularly bleeding volume estimation and flow characterization—involve subjective evaluation despite standardized protocols, potentially introducing observer variability. Fourth, the emergency nature of severe epistaxis management may have precluded systematic endoscopic assessment in some cases or required immediate intervention before complete assessment. Finally, the single‐center tertiary care setting may limit generalizability, as patient demographics, referral patterns, and clinical practices may differ across healthcare systems and world regions.

It is important to contextualize these findings within the broader patient volume. These 445 patients represent a subset of severe cases requiring admission or complex intervention, derived from a larger pool of epistaxis patients presenting to our emergency department. Therefore, the high prevalence of PEE/LCH reported here applies specifically to the “severe/refractory” epistaxis population and should not be extrapolated to the general population with mild nosebleeds.

In addition, the high proportion of PEE observed in this cohort likely reflects a selection phenomenon inherent to severe or refractory epistaxis. Raised or nodular vascular lesions are more prone to fail conservative measures and therefore more likely to prompt specialist referral, systematic endoscopic evaluation, and surgical management. Consequently, such lesions may be overrepresented in hospitalized or refractory epistaxis populations compared to routine outpatient cases.

We observed a notably higher prevalence of LCH in our cohort compared to some Western literature. Beyond potential selection bias, this raises the question of regional or demographic variability. Our study population is based in Eastern China; whether environmental factors, regional dietary habits, or specific genetic predispositions contribute to a higher incidence of vascular proliferative lesions in this demographic remains unclear. Future multi‐center, international collaborative studies would be valuable to distinguish global pathophysiological patterns from regional specificities.

Conclusion

LCH represents the most frequently identified pathology among biopsied PEE lesions; however, due to incomplete pathological sampling, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive evidence of causality. The correlation between macroscopic appearance and histopathological findings supports the clinical utility of systematic endoscopic assessment for directing treatment decisions, even without immediate histopathological confirmation. Prospective multicenter studies with systematic pathological sampling are warranted to validate these observations and assess their generalizability.
