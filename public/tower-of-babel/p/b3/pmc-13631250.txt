Beneficial effects of Chlorella species supplementation in the general population: a GRADE-assessed systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis
Authors: Helia Mardani, Mohammad Amin Karimi, Mohammad Sharifi, Dorsa Ghazvineh, Zahra Yari, Ali Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the potential benefits of Chlorella supplementation in the general population, with a focus on its impact across a broad spectrum of health-related biomarkers and clinical parameters. A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, CENTRAL, Web of Science, Scopus, and EMBASE databases was conducted up to February 20, 2025. A total of 24 studies were included. Chlorella supplementation led to significant reductions in body fat percentage (Weighted Mean Difference [WMD] = −0.73%, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: −1.18 to −0.29), body mass index (BMI) (WMD = −0.30 kg/m2, 95% CI: −0.48 to −0.13), weight (WMD = −1.28 kg, 95% CI: −2.00 to −0.56), waist-to-hip ratio (WMD = −0.01%, 95% CI: −0.02 to −0.01), homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (WMD = −0.22 units, 95% CI: −0.35 to −0.09), insulin (WMD = −0.86 μU/mL, 95% CI: −1.45 to −0.27), high sensitivity C-reactive protein (WMD = −0.56 μg/ml, 95% CI: −1.02 to −0.11), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (WMD = −6.20 mg/dl, 95% CI: −8.33 to −4.08), total cholesterol (WMD = −8.03 mg/dl, 95% CI: −10.60 to −5.46), triglycerides (WMD = −2.91 mg/dl, 95%CI: −5.70 to −0.12), malondialdehyde (WMD = −1.84 nmol/ml; 95% CI: −3.24 to −0.45), and leptin levels (WMD = −0.71 ng/ml; 95% CI: −1.27 to −0.16). Conversely, catalase (WMD = 22.06 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: 3.19 to 40.93) increased significantly. While the evidence for BMI was rated as low, the certainty of evidence for most other outcomes was very low according to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE). Our findings underscore the potential of Chlorella supplementation to support metabolic health in general population; however, given the overall very low certainty of evidence based on the GRADE assessment, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Introduction

Health issues have been the most challenging part of each society. The more people gain health literacy, the more they seek safer and affordable ways of maintaining their well-eing [1]. On the one hand, those who are suffering from diseases have a strong preference to take drugs with fewer side effects, which notably contain more natural components. On the other hand, those working out regularly or experiencing a disease-free lifestyle tend to increase their performance [2] and stay healthy for a longer time [3]. Consequently, it has gained interest among people who want to consume supplements concurrently, which are rich in micro- and macro-nutrients to improve their quality of life [4]. Among these interventions, Chlorella supplementation has recently drawn attention for its plus points.

Chlorella is a unicellular green algae that has been applied in nutraceuticals, the biotechnology industry, and biopharmaceuticals [5]. Additionally, it has numerous benefits regarding its bioactive compounds, namely proteins (either essential or non-essential amino acids) [6], vitamins (B vitamins), carotenoids (β-carotene and lutein) [7], and minerals. Due to the presence of chlorophyll and dietary fiber, Chlorella contributes to anti-inflammatory [8], antioxidant, and detoxification [9]. Also, dietary fibers can control glycemic fluctuations, help with weight loss [10, 11], improve lipid profile, and liver function [12]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that Chlorella supplementation has possible benefits of this plant in adipokine modulation [13], heart rate (HR) reduction, and blood pressure (BP) regulation [14], however, these findings need further affirmation.

This study aims to evaluate the potential benefits of Chlorella supplementation in the general population, focusing on its vast impact on different conditions. While many studies have concentrated on specific clinical populations [12, 15, 16], our research investigates the broader function of Chlorella as a food supplement for the general population. Most existing documents have focused on Chlorella properties in diseases or animal studies, leaving a gap in understanding its role in the non-clinical population. Besides, this study was conducted to fill the gaps by employing a precise dose-response meta-analysis and systematic review methodology. We also applied the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework to evaluate trials and the associations between causal factors such as Chlorella treatment duration, dosage, and several health indices. The latest statistical methods, including dose-response analyses and meta-regression, were also used to assess the influence of Chlorella across different populations and health conditions. Last but not least, our findings will provide valuable perceptions for healthcare providers and public health suggestions, emphasizing Chlorella’s potential role in different health areas.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis are in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17]. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251062488 to guarantee methodological rigor and transparency.

Search strategy, study selection, and data extraction

A systematic search was carried out through Web of Science, EMBASE, PubMed, Scopus, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), studies published up to February 20, 2025. The search strategy integrated terms such as ‘Chlorellas,’ ‘Chlorella,’ ‘microalgae,’ ‘C. pyrenoidosa,’ ‘C. vulgaris,’ and ‘clinical trial,’ considered for indexing systems, and employed MeSH and EMTREE terms based on each database’s indexing system (Supplementary Table 1).

We also recognized additional studies through a manual search in the reference lists of relevant reviews and publications. We included trials that evaluated the effects of Chlorella supplementation on outcomes such as BP, glycemic control, lipid profile, leptin levels, liver function tests, anthropometric measures, oxidative stress markers, and HR in adults. Eligible studies were those conducted on the general population, defined as adults without underlying chronic diseases, regardless of BMI classification. This included individuals with a healthy weight, as well as those classified as overweight or obese. Letters, reviews, animal studies, in vitro research, and observational studies were excluded. No restrictions were applied regarding language, publication status, or intervention duration during study selection.

According to predefined eligibility criteria, abstracts and titles were independently screened by two reviewers (A.J. and H.M.). Relevant full-text articles were then assessed for inclusion. Notably, any possible discrepancies were addressed via discussion or by the correspondence author (Z.Y.). By employing EndNote X7, duplicate studies were eliminated, and by applying a standardized form, data were extracted.

Author, publication year, geographical location, population characteristics, intervention details, Chlorella dosage, and treatment duration were included as extracted data. Besides, glycemic markers, lipid criteria levels, leptin levels, anthropometric indices, BP, liver enzymes, oxidative stress parameters, and HR were included as the initial outcomes.

Methodological quality assessment and evidence strength evaluation

The Cochrane risk of bias 2 tool was employed to evaluate the methodological quality of included studies. Autonomously, two reviewers (M.Sh. and D.Gh.) evaluated risk of bias by focusing on completeness of outcome data, allocation concealment, randomization, selective reporting, and blinding. In addition, discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (M.A.K.).

The GRADE framework was applied to assess the certainty of the evidence, which evaluates each outcome based on consistency, study limitations, precision, directness, and risk of publication bias. Furthermore, the grading and addressing any disagreements were carried out by two reviewers (H.M. and A.J.) in consultation with a third reviewer (M.S.).

Statistical analysis

Stata version 15.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas) was used for all statistical analyses. The initial objective was to assess the impact of Chlorella supplementation on anthropometric indices, BP, inflammatory biomarkers, glycemic control, liver function tests, oxidative stress parameters, lipid profiles, leptin levels, and HR. According to the included studies, sample sizes, pre- and post-intervention methods, means, and standard deviations (SDs) were extracted.

Weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were expressed to show the influences of Chlorella supplementation. Variability across studies was assessed using a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method). Next, the I² statistic, with percentages of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively, was applied to evaluate heterogeneity.

Subgroup analyses were categorized based on country (Iran, Japan, Korea, other countries), publication year (≤2020, >2020), sex (both, female, male), intervention age (<40 years, ≥40 years), baseline body mass index (BMI) (healthy weight [≤25 kg/m²], overweight/obese [>25 kg/m²]), intervention duration (≤8 weeks, >8 weeks), intervention type (tablet, capsule, other forms or not mentioned), intervention approach (Chlorella, Chlorella with exercise), Chlorella species (Chlorella vulgaris, Chlorella pyrenoidosa, not mentioned), control approach (placebo or usual care, exercise), dosage (≤1500 mg/day, >1500 mg/day), and sample size (≤25, >25 participants). Meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine the influence of Chlorella dosage and intervention duration on the outcomes [18]. Additionally, a non-linear dose–response model was applied to evaluate the association between different supplementation levels and observed health effects, with the aim of supporting evidence-based recommendations for hypothesized optimal dose and duration [19]. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s tests [20].

Results

Study selection

A systematic search was conducted across PubMed (n = 846), Web of Science (n = 3864), Scopus (n = 2360), Embase (n = 9155), Cochrane (n = 724), resulting in a total of 16,949 studies. Thereafter, 7989 studies were screened for titles and abstracts following duplicates removal (n = 7589) and excluding irrelevant studies (n = 746) and animal research (n = 625). From these, 7745 records were excluded due to either title mismatches (n = 4741) or non-relevant abstracts (n = 3004). Full-text retrieval was pursued for 244 articles, though 189 were not retrieved, resulting in 55 studies for eligibility assessment. During full-text evaluation, 34 studies were excluded based on various criteria including unsuitable study design (n = 12), irrelevant outcomes (n = 10), non-human subjects (n = 2), insufficient data (n = 1), intervention mismatch (n = 2), population mismatch (n = 4), or duration limitations (n = 3) (Supplementary Table 2). In total, 24 studies were included in the analysis, with 21 identified through screening and 3 through manual searches. The studies were published between 1975 and 2024, involving 815 subjects (intervention: n = 415; control: n = 400). Studies were conducted in Japan [14, 21–26], Iran [13, 27–38], Korea [39, 40], Taiwan [8], and Germany [41], with designs ranging from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and double-blind placebo-controlled studies to crossover and controlled trials. Sample sizes varied widely, from 8 to 52 participants. Most trials focused on both sexes [8, 14, 23–25, 40, 41], while others focused exclusively on females [13, 22, 26, 29, 31, 32, 34, 37–39] or males [21, 27, 28, 30, 33, 35, 36]. Chlorella supplementation doses spanned 135–15,000 mg/day, with durations lasting 1–12 weeks. Control groups received placebo (e.g., lactose, starch, sucrose, and colorant) or usual care, with twelve studies combining Chlorella with exercise regimens (e.g., high-intensity interval training [HIIT], and interval resistance training [IRT]).

The sample sizes for the intervention and control groups across various outcomes were as follows: BF%: n = 248 (intervention: 124, control: 124); BFM: n = 74 (intervention: 37, control: 37); BMI: n = 511 (intervention: 259, control: 252); WC: n = 162 (intervention: 81, control: 81); weight: n = 564 (intervention: 282, control: 282); HC: n = 90 (intervention: 46, control: 44); WC: n = 90 (intervention: 46, control: 44); weight: n = 631 (intervention: 320, control: 311); WHR: n = 170 (intervention: 86, control: 84); DBP: n = 175 (intervention: 93, control: 82); SBP: n = 175 (intervention: 93, control: 82); glucose: n = 266 (intervention: 136, control: 130); HOMA-IR: n = 206 (intervention: 104, control: 102); insulin: n = 154 (intervention: 130, control: 124); hs-CRP: n = 84 (intervention: 42, control: 42); HDL-C: n = 341 (intervention: 175, control: 166); LDL-C: n = 341 (intervention: 175, control: 166); TC: n = 317 (intervention: 162, control: 155); TG: n = 341 (intervention: 175, control: 166); ALT: n = 92 (intervention: 46, control: 46); AST: n = 92 (intervention: 46, control: 46), CAT: n = 136 (intervention: 69, control: 67); GPx: n = 136 (intervention: 69, control: 67); MDA: n = 228 (intervention: 114, control: 114); SOD : n = 124 (intervention: 62, control: 62); TAC: n = 124 (intervention: 62, control: 62); leptin: n = 76 (intervention: 38, control: 38).

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on anthropometric measures

Chlorella supplementation demonstrated statistically significant reductions in BF% (WMD = −0.73%; 95% CI: −1.18 to −0.29; p = 0.001; I² = 28.9%; p = 0.179) (Fig. 2), BMI (WMD = −0.30 kg/m2; 95% CI: −0.48 to −0.13; p = 0.001; I² = 21.3%; p = 0.186), weight (WMD = −1.28 kg; 95% CI: −2.00 to −0.56; p < 0.001; I² = 20.6%; p = 0.173), and WHR (WMD = −0.01%; 95% CI: −0.02 to −0.01; p < 0.001; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.859). Non-significant effects were observed for BFM (WMD = −0.57 kg; 95% CI: −1.30 to 0.17; p = 0.129; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.696), HC (WMD = −0.81 cm; 95% CI: −2.54 to 0.93; p = 0.363; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.898), and WC (WMD = −1.70 cm; 95% CI: −3.93 to 0.54; p = 0.137; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.988).

Publication bias assessments using the Egger’s test yielded non-significant results for BF% (p = 0.686), BMI (p = 0.926), HC (p = 0.204), WC (p = 0.566), and WHR (p = 0.247). In contrast, the result for weight showed potential evidence of bias (p = 0.006). Sensitivity analysis identified no studies whose exclusion altered the statistical significance of the pooled effects for any outcomes.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on blood pressure

Our study showed no statistically significant effects on DBP (WMD = −0.33 mmHg; 95% CI: −2.81 to 2.15; p = 0.795; I² = 38.7%; p = 0.163) (Fig. 3) or SBP (WMD = −0.42 mmHg; 95% CI: −2.76 to 1.93; p = 0.728; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.483).

The Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias for DBP (p = 0.899) or SBP (p = 0.902). Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the results, as omitting any individual study did not affect the overall significance of the findings.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on glycemic profile

Based on the result of meta-analysis, there were significant reductions in HOMA-IR (WMD = −0.22 units; 95% CI: −0.35 to −0.09; p = 0.001; I² = 75.7%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 4) and insulin levels (WMD = −0.86 μU/mL; 95% CI: −1.45 to −0.27; p = 0.004; I² = 83.8%; p < 0.001). Non-significant effects were observed for FBG (WMD = −1.62 mg/dl; 95% CI: −3.45 to 0.21; p = 0.083; I² = 25.5%; p = 0.193).

Publication bias assessments based on Egger’s test indicated potential bias for HOMA-IR (p < 0.001). However, no publication bias was detected for FBG (p = 0.427) or insulin (p = 0.095) based on this test. Sensitivity analysis identified that excluding the study by Sanayei et al. [33] altered the significance of the effect on FBG (WMD: −2.10 mg/dl; 95% CI: −3.85 to −0.34). No influential studies were detected for HOMA-IR or insulin based on sensitivity analysis.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on hs-CRP

hs-CRP levels were significantly reduced by Chlorella supplementation (WMD = −0.56 μg/ml; 95% CI: −1.02 to −0.11; p = 0.015; I² = 90.9%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

No significant publication bias was observed for this parameter based on Egger’s test (p = 0.298). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the overall effect on hs-CRP remained stable and was not influenced by the exclusion of any single study.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on lipid profile

Chlorella supplementation significantly reduced LDL-C (WMD = −6.20 mg/dl; 95% CI: −8.33 to −4.08; p < 0.001; I² = 16.7%; p = 0.275) (Fig. 6), TC (WMD = −8.03 mg/dl; 95% CI: −10.60 to −5.46; p < 0.001; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.500), and TG (WMD = −2.91 mg/dl; 95% CI: −5.70 to −0.12; p = 0.041; I² = 25.8%; p = 0.183). Although not statistically significant trends were observed for HDL-C (WMD = 1.82 mg/dl; 95% CI: −0.04 to 3.68; p = 0.055; I² = 33.8%; p = 0.112).

Based on Egger’s test no significant publication bias was detected for HDL-C (p = 0.079), LDL-C (p = 0.115), TC (p = 0.228), or TG (p = 0.432). Sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding the studies by Sandgruber et al. [41] (WMD: 2.52 mg/dl; 95% CI: 1.08 to 3.95), Otsuki et al. [14] (WMD: 1.98 mg/dl; 95% CI: 0.09 to 3.87), and Kwak et al. [40] (WMD: 2.19 mg/dl; 95% CI: 0.30 to 4.07) resulted in a significant effect on HDL-C. Additionally, removing five studies —Karbalamahdi et al. [30] (WMD: -2.78 mg/dl; 95% CI: −5.67 to 0.12), Sandgruber et al. [41] (WMD: −2.50 mg/dl; 95% CI: −5.11 to 0.12), Sanayei et al. [33] (WMD: −2.95 mg/dl; 95% CI: −5.93 to 0.03), Delfan et al. [37] (WMD: −3.25 mg/dl; 95% CI: −6.69 to 0.18), Delfan et al. [13] (WMD: −2.19 mg/dl; 95% CI: −5.23 to 0.85)—resulted in a significant reduction in TG levels. The pooled estimates for LDL-C and TC remained unaffected regardless of individual study exclusion.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on liver function tests

According to the pooled results, no significant effects were observed for ALT (WMD = −2.77 U/L; 95% CI: −5.80 to 0.26; p = 0.073; I² = 77.1%; p = 0.013) (Fig. 7), and AST (WMD = −1.73 U/L; 95% CI: −3.97 to 0.51; p = 0.129; I² = 62.5%; p = 0.069).

Egger’s test showed no evidence of publication bias for either ALT (p = 0.554) or AST (p = 0.700). Sensitivity analysis indicated that the exclusion of the studies by Sandgruber et al. [41] (WMD: −4.56 U/L; 95% CI: −6.70 to −2.43) and Chiu et al. [8] (WMD: −1.07 U/L; 95% CI: −2.08 to −0.07) could significantly influence the overall result for ALT. Moreover, the removal of the study by Chiu et al. [8] (WMD: −0.86 U/L; 95% CI: −1.49 to −0.22) would alter the significance of the overall effect on AST.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on oxidative stress parameters

Our analysis showed that Chlorella supplementation significantly elevated CAT (WMD = 22.06 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: 3.19 to 40.93; p = 0.022; I² = 99.4%; p < 0.001) (Fig. 8), while MDA levels were significantly reduced (WMD = −1.84 nmol/ml; 95% CI: −3.24 to −0.45; p = 0.009; I² = 99.8%; p < 0.001). No significant effects were observed for GPx (WMD = −6.33 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: −19.24 to 6.59; p = 0.337; I² = 99.3%; p < 0.001), SOD activity (WMD = 12.39 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: −2.00 to 26.78; p = 0.091; I² = 78.6%; p < 0.001), or TAC (WMD = −0.09 mmol/L; 95% CI: −1.35 to 1.17; p = 0.886; I² = 99.7%; p < 0.001).

Egger’s test indicated no evidence of publication bias for CAT (p = 0.550), GPx (p = 0.462), MDA (p = 0.501), or TAC (p = 0.822), whereas evidence of publication bias was observed for SOD (p = 0.004). Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that exclusion of the studies by Tofighi et al. [34] (WMD: 23.91 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: −8.87 to 56.69) or Chiu et al. [8] (WMD: 20.97 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: −0.87 to 42.81) influenced the overall effect for CAT. Similarly, the pooled estimate for TAC was affected by the exclusion of the study by Tofighi et al. [34] (WMD: 0.38 mmol/L; 95% CI: 0.20 to 0.56). For SOD, omitting the study by Tofighi et al. [34] (WMD: 18.02 U/g.Hb; 95% CI: 7.47, 28.58) similarly impacted the overall effect.

Effects of Chlorella supplementation on leptin

Chlorella supplementation significantly reduced leptin levels (WMD = −0.71 ng/ml; 95% CI: −1.27 to −0.16; p = 0.012; I² = 0.0%; p = 0.538) (Fig. 9).

No evidence of publication bias was detected for leptin (p = 0.789) based on Egger’s test. Sensitivity analysis showed that exclusion of the study by Delfan et al. [13] (WMD: −0.43 ng/ml; 95% CI: −1.22 to 0.35) could alter the significance of the effect on leptin.

Subgroup analysis

The country subgroup analysis revealed significant improvements in BF%, BMI, weight, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C, TG, and MDA exclusively in the Iranian studies. For LDL-C and TC, significant decreases were observed in studies conducted in Iran and other countries. FBG levels, however, were not affected in any subgroup.

Subgroup analysis based on publication year demonstrated that studies published after 2020 reported significant reductions in BF%, weight, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, and TG. In contrast, studies published in or before 2020 showed significant improvements in BMI and FBG. No significant effects on MDA were observed in either subgroup.

Based on subgroup analysis by sex both male and female subgroups exhibited significant decreases in BF%, BMI, and MDA. However, significant improvements in weight, FBG, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C, and TG were observed only in male participants. Reductions in LDL-C and TC were significant across all sex categories.

According to the subgroup analysis by intervention age, participants aged <40 years experienced significant reductions in BF%, BMI, weight, HOMA-IR, insulin, TC, TG, and MDA. LDL-C levels were significantly decreased in both age groups. No significant effects on FBG or HDL-C were detected in either subgroup.

Subgroup analysis stratified by baseline BMI revealed significant improvements in BF%, BMI, weight, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, and TG in participants classified as overweight or obese (>25 kg/m²). MDA remained the sole outcome with significant effects across both subgroups. No significant changes in FBG were observed in either subgroup.

Moreover, there were significant reductions in LDL-C, TC, and MDA across both ≤8 weeks and >8 weeks subgroups, based on subgroup analysis by intervention duration. For outcomes such as BF% and BMI, significant decreases were observed only in the ≤8 weeks subgroup, while reductions in weight, FBG, HOMA-IR, insulin, TG, and increased HDL-C were exclusive to the >8-week study.

Subgroup analysis based on intervention type revealed significant reductions in BF% for both tablet and capsule subgroups. However, reductions in BMI, weight, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C, TC, and TG were observed only in studies using capsule formulations. Improvements in FBG were exclusive to the tablet subgroup. LDL-C and MDA levels decreased significantly in both capsule and “other forms or not mentioned” subgroups.

Regarding intervention approach, significant reductions in BF%, BMI, HOMA-IR, and insulin were observed in the Chlorella with Exercise subgroup. In contrast, the Chlorella-only subgroup demonstrated significant decreases in TC, and MDA. Both subgroups demonstrated significant improvements in weight, LDL-C levels, while no significant changes were found in FBG, HDL-C, or TG in either group.

In terms of Chlorella species, significant reductions in BF% and MDA were observed in both Chlorella vulgaris and “not mentioned” subgroups. Notably, LDL-C and TC improvements were observed in both Chlorella vulgaris and Chlorella pyrenoidosa subgroups. BMI, weight, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C, and TG showed significant improvements only with Chlorella vulgaris. FBG levels remained unaffected across all subgroups.

Subgroup analysis by control approach showed significant reductions in TC, and MDA for the placebo or usual care subgroup, whereas the exercise subgroup demonstrated significant decreases in BF%, BMI, weight, HOMA-IR, and insulin levels. Weight and LDL-C improved significantly in both subgroups. No significant changes in FBG, HDL-C, or TG were observed in either subgroup.

In addition, subgroup analysis by Chlorella dosage demonstrated significant reductions in BF%, insulin, LDL-C, TC, and MDA across both ≤1500 mg/day and > 1500 mg/day subgroups. Reductions in BMI and increases in HDL-C were observed exclusively in the ≤1500 mg/day subgroup, while HOMA-IR and TG improved significantly only in the >1500 mg/day subgroup. Weight and FBG remained non-significant in all subgroups.

Subgroup analysis by sample size revealed significant improvements in BF%, BMI, weight, FBG, HOMA-IR, insulin, HDL-C and TG in studies with ≤25 participants. Because this review assessed multiple primary outcomes and numerous exploratory subgroup domains, the possibility of false-positive findings due to multiple comparisons cannot be excluded. Bonferroni correction applied to the primary outcomes showed that the most central findings remained statistically robust, whereas subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory (Supplementary Table 3).

Meta-regression and nonlinear dose–response analysis

Meta-regression highlighted a significant inverse relationship between Chlorella dosage and HDL-C levels (p < 0.001), indicating that higher doses may adversely affect HDL-C levels. In contrast, longer intervention durations were associated with increases in HDL-C (p = 0.015) and reductions in weight (p = 0.018) (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5).

Non-linear dose-response models revealed that higher doses were significantly associated with reductions in weight (p = 0.040; approximate dose ≈ 1800 mg/day), FBG (p = 0.006; approximate dose ≈ 1800 mg/day), and HOMA-IR (p = 0.030; approximate dose ≈ 1200 mg/day). Conversely, increasing doses were also linked to elevations in LDL-C (p = 0.001; approximate dose ≈ 1800 mg/day), BF% (p = 0.013; approximate dose ≈ 1200 mg/day), and TG (p = 0.016; approximate dose ≈ 8000 mg/day). For HDL-C, non-linear analysis also identified a significant association with intervention duration (p = 0.006), with an estimated duration of 12 weeks explored for its potential benefits (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3).

Discussions

Summary of Findings

This is the first GRADE-assessed systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis evaluating the effects of Chlorella supplementation on cardiometabolic outcomes in the general population. However, the interpretation of these findings should be tempered by substantial limitations in the quality of the included evidence. Based on ROB2, almost all of the 24 trials were judged to be of poor quality (particularly in allocation concealment and blinding), with only two studies rated as fair quality. Consistent with these methodological weaknesses, the GRADE evaluation classified the certainty of evidence for most outcomes as very low. Consequently, any recommendations derived from these analyses, including optimal intake levels or specific intervention strategies, must be made with extreme caution and are considered highly preliminary. These limitations substantially constrain the confidence with which any observed associations can be interpreted.

Our analysis included 24 clinical trials involving 815 subjects and demonstrated that Chlorella may beneficially influence body composition, glycemic control, lipid metabolism, inflammation, oxidative stress, and leptin levels. The most consistent improvements were observed in BMI, WHR, BF%, LDL-C, TC, HOMA-IR, and antioxidant enzyme activity. In terms of anthropometric measures, Chlorella supplementation resulted in reductions in WHR, BMI, BF%, and weight. These effects were more pronounced in individuals under the age of 40, those with overweight or obesity, and in those receiving capsule-based Chlorella vulgaris at doses ≤1500 mg/day for durations of eight weeks or less. The reductions were also evident in trials that combined Chlorella with exercise regimens such as high-intensity interval or resistance training. Non-significant changes were observed for BFM, WC, and HC.

In relation to BP, Chlorella supplementation showed no significant effects on either SBP or DBP across the included studies. These findings were consistent across all subgroup analyses, including those stratified by age, sex, intervention duration, dosage, or BMI. Regarding the glycemic profile, supplementation reduced HOMA-IR and insulin levels, particularly among younger individuals with higher BMI and in studies lasting more than 8 weeks. FBG remained unchanged across most subgroup analyses, indicating that the primary benefits may lie in improved insulin dynamics rather than fasting glucose regulation.

Regarding inflammatory markers, a significant reduction in hs-CRP was observed, with some variation across studies. These reductions were more evident in participants receiving Chlorella without concurrent exercise. However, sensitivity to individual study exclusions suggests some variability in the robustness of this outcome. Therefore, due to the observed heterogeneity, the interpretation of these findings should be done with caution. In terms of lipid profile, Chlorella supplementation consistently lowered LDL-C, TG, and TC. Modest and non-significant trends were found for HDL-C, with improvements limited to certain subgroups such as overweight individuals, males, and those receiving higher doses for longer durations. Both LDL-C and TC reductions were also observed in the ≤ 8 weeks and > 8 weeks subgroups.

Regarding liver function parameters, no significant changes were detected in ALT or AST following Chlorella supplementation. Subgroup analyses by age, sex, and dosage did not yield consistent patterns for either enzyme. For oxidative stress markers, Chlorella supplementation significantly increased CAT activity while reducing MDA levels. These effects were more notable in younger adults and individuals with a BMI > 25 kg/m². No meaningful effects were observed for GPx, SOD, or TAC, and results varied depending on the specific species and formulation of Chlorella. Recognizing this variability, it is crucial to acknowledge the considerable heterogeneity present across studies investigating these oxidative stress outcomes. This pronounced inconsistency in findings underscores the need for cautious interpretation of the pooled estimates. The high heterogeneity observed suggests that a single, overarching conclusion may oversimplify the complex effects on oxidative stress. Therefore, while the aggregated data provides an indication, individual study findings and their specific contexts warrant careful consideration to fully understand the nuanced impact of Chlorella on oxidative stress. Finally, supplementation with Chlorella was associated with a significant reduction in leptin concentrations, particularly in overweight participants and those receiving capsule-based Chlorella vulgaris. However, it is crucial to note that the certainty of evidence for most of these outcomes is very low, and for BMI specifically, it is low, as indicated by the GRADE assessment. These findings should be interpreted with significant caution due to the substantial risk of bias identified in the majority of the included studies.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings are consistent with previous animal and human studies demonstrating the cardiometabolic benefits of Chlorella supplementation [42–44]. Discrepancies emerged when comparing our results with prior research, potentially reflecting variations in study populations, dosages, duration of interventions, or health conditions of participants. Kazeminejad et al., [45] for instance, reported no significant impact of Chlorella supplementation on anthropometric indices, including BMI, WC, and BF%, contrasting with our observed significant reductions in these metrics [45]. Similarly, Fallah et al. [43] documented substantial improvements in lipid parameters such as TC and LDL-C, as well as SBP, DBP, and FBG; however, they found no significant changes in TG, HDL-C, or BMI [43]. Our analysis partially aligns with these results, notably regarding lipid improvements in LDL-C and TC, and suggests borderline beneficial trends for HDL-C, but diverges concerning BP and glycemic control.

In contrast, Hosseini et al. [10], in an 8-week RCT involving type 2 diabetic patients, observed no significant effects on anthropometric measures, lipid profiles, or glycemic control following supplementation with 1500 mg/day Chlorella. The lack of significant outcomes in their study may be attributable to the shorter duration or lower dosage compared to our research parameters [10]. Additionally, Ebrahimi-Mameghani et al. [46] noted significant improvements in FBG and lipid profiles among non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) patients, aligning partially with our lipid-related findings but differing in glycemic outcomes [46].

Furthermore, Yarmohammadi et al. [15] reported a significant reduction in AST following Chlorella supplementation, while ALT and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels remained unchanged, closely matching our findings regarding hepatic enzyme profiles [15]. Regarding oxidative stress, our results are largely consistent with those of Sikiru et al. [47], who demonstrated increased antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, GSH) and reduced MDA in Chlorella-supplemented rabbit models [47]. Similarly, Mueller et al. [48] reported enhanced antioxidant defenses and microbiota modulation in fish subjects given Chlorella [48]. Additionally, Horii et al. [49] illustrated synergistic benefits of combining Chlorella supplementation with aerobic exercise on insulin signaling and glycemic control in diabetic rats, potentially explaining differences observed between single-intervention studies and those employing combined therapeutic approaches [49]. Our findings of decreased leptin levels, along with increased adiponectin following Chlorella supplementation, support emerging evidence from previous studies like Itakura et al. [50], which reported Chlorella’s ability to suppress resistin gene expression in borderline diabetics. These results highlight the potential of microalgae in modulating adipokine profiles and improving metabolic health [50]. However, direct comparative studies specifically investigating adipokine responses to Chlorella supplementation in humans are still limited, warranting further investigation to confirm and expand upon these promising outcomes.

Mechanisms of action

Chlorella has the potential to influence body composition due to its high concentration of dietary fibers, both soluble and insoluble, which enhance feelings of fullness, potentially leading to decreased overall calorie consumption [51–53]. Moreover, it contains non-starch polysaccharides, dietary fiber components resistant to digestion, which may help prevent fat accumulation and support weight regulation [54, 55]. Additionally, certain types of algae, including Chlorella, provide considerable amounts of high-quality protein, potentially aiding in the preservation of lean muscle mass and influencing hormones involved in appetite control, such as cholecystokinin [56, 57]. Moreover, the carotenoids present in Chlorella may inhibit dietary fat absorption and its subsequent transportation through competitive interactions in the gastrointestinal tract. This mechanism potentially lowers overall caloric intake, which may contribute weight loss [43].

This supplement demonstrates potential cardiovascular benefits through several distinct yet interconnected mechanisms. Its high arginine content enhances nitric oxide (NO) synthesis, which promotes vasodilation and may subsequently contribute to improved arterial compliance, as indicated by reductions in brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV) observed in clinical studies. Additionally, Chlorella contains significant levels of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which can lower BP by suppressing sympathetic nervous activity and inhibiting angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) [58, 59]. Furthermore, Chlorella’s mineral composition, notably magnesium and potassium, supports vascular relaxation, contributes to sodium excretion, and reduces arterial stiffness, collectively assisting in BP regulation [14, 24, 60]. Higher concentrations of inflammatory markers and oxidative stress are associated with increased arterial stiffness and elevated homocysteine levels, both established pathways contributing to hypertension. Chlorella, rich in omega-3 fatty acids and carotenoids, exhibits anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that may help in mitigating these harmful processes. Through its ability to decrease oxidative stress, inflammation, and homocysteine levels, Chlorella supplementation may support endothelial integrity and promotes cardiovascular health [61].

Emerging evidence suggests that Chlorella supplementation positively impacts glycemic regulation, as evidenced by reductions in FBG concentrations, circulating insulin levels, and glucose area under the curve (AUC) during oral glucose tolerance testing (OGTT), coupled with improved quantitative insulin sensitivity check index (QUICKI) values. These beneficial metabolic effects have been associated with the activation of the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K) signaling pathway, increased phosphorylation of protein kinase B (Akt), and enhanced translocation of glucose transporter type (GLUT) 4 within skeletal muscle. Such molecular changes exhibit a strong positive correlation with improvements in glycemic parameters [49]. Additionally, Chlorella supplementation may decrease circulating non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), further enhancing glucose uptake and suppressing hepatic glucose production through alterations in gene expression associated with insulin signaling pathways [62, 63].

Chlorella demonstrates antioxidant properties through both the direct neutralization of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the enhancement of endogenous antioxidant defense mechanisms. This microalga is abundant in bioactive antioxidants such as vitamins C and E, carotenoids including lutein, polyphenolic compounds, and omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, which collectively contribute to the scavenging of free radicals and inhibition of lipid peroxidation [64–66]. Furthermore, Chlorella supplementation may elevate the activity of critical antioxidant enzymes (CAT, GPx, and glutathione [GSH]) thus potentially fortifying cellular resilience against oxidative damage [39, 67, 68]. Experimental animal studies have consistently reported reductions in markers of oxidative stress, such as MDA and 4-hydroxyalkenal (HAE), while clinical trials involving populations such as smokers and athletes have shown improvements in TAC along with decreased indicators of oxidative harm [67, 69].

The anti-inflammatory actions of Chlorella primarily involve modulation of the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which critically governs the transcription of inflammatory mediators [70]. Specifically, Chlorella suppresses the expression of inducible NO synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), thereby potentially diminishing the synthesis of inflammatory compounds, including NO, prostaglandins (PGE2 and PGF2α), and cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [70–73]. Chlorella-11, a distinct bioactive peptide isolated from Chlorella, has been identified to specifically inhibit NF-κB activation and the subsequent induction of iNOS, reducing inflammation in macrophages stimulated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [74, 75]. In vivo animal model studies further confirm that Chlorella supplementation markedly suppresses inflammatory cytokine production and reduces tissue edema [74]. Additionally, Chlorella may influence transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathways, providing further protective effects against oxidative stress and fibrosis, particularly within hepatic tissues [68].

Chlorella exerts beneficial effects on hepatic function and lipid metabolism through several interconnected biological pathways. The significant carotenoid content of Chlorella interferes with intestinal micelle formation, thereby reducing lipid absorption and subsequently limiting lipid accumulation within the liver [76]. Additionally, its high dietary fiber content binds to bile acids within the gastrointestinal tract, potentially facilitating their excretion and may prompt hepatic cholesterol utilization for bile acid synthesis, which may contribute to lowering cholesterol concentrations in hepatic tissues [77]. Chlorella’s composition includes notable levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), especially omega-3 fatty acids, which activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs). Activation of these receptors may enhance fatty acid oxidation within hepatic cells while concurrently inhibiting lipogenic processes [77–79]. Furthermore, Chlorella contains niacin, which potentially suppresses hepatic triglyceride synthesis by downregulating diacylglycerol acyltransferase-2 (DGAT2), thereby reducing very-low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (VLDL-C) secretion and possibly elevating HDL-C through increased expression of apolipoprotein A-I (apo A-1) [77–81].

Moreover, Chlorella provides antioxidant compounds such as chlorophyll and vitamin E, which modulate hepatic gene expression by downregulating lipogenic genes, including sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c), acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), and fatty acid synthase (FAS), while simultaneously upregulating genes associated with fatty acid oxidation, notably carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) and acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1) [24, 82–85]. It may also enhance hepatic insulin sensitivity by lowering circulating NEFAs and reducing oxidative stress, thus reinforcing insulin signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT [62, 85, 86]. Finally, Chlorella supplementation favorably alters gut microbiota composition, promoting the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), particularly butyrate, which contributes to improved lipid metabolism and diminished hepatic inflammation through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and modulation of the gut-liver axis [87–89].

Chlorella may promote hepatic health through a variety of molecular and cellular mechanisms. Its hepatoprotective properties primarily stem from robust antioxidant capabilities, enabling it to effectively scavenge free radicals and reduce oxidative stress within hepatocytes [15]. This antioxidant action enhances the activities of intrinsic enzymatic antioxidants, including CAT, SOD, and GPx, alongside augmenting levels of non-enzymatic antioxidants such as reduced GSH [90, 91]. By mitigating oxidative stress, Chlorella may help stabilize hepatocyte membrane integrity, consequently potentially minimizing the release of hepatic enzymes like ALT and AST into circulation [15, 92].

Safety and tolerability considerations

Our systematic review and meta-analysis highlight the positive effects of Chlorella species supplementation on various surrogate health markers in the general population. However, it is essential to underscore the limitations associated with the current body of evidence, particularly concerning safety and long-term efficacy [93]. A notable methodological shortcoming across the included studies is the insufficient documentation of adverse events, with few trials implementing comprehensive safety monitoring protocols. While existing literature suggests that Chlorella supplementation is generally well-tolerated, with mild gastrointestinal disturbances such as nausea and bloating being the most frequently reported side effects, significant safety concerns remain [74, 75]. The potential for heavy metal contamination and harmful cyanotoxin accumulation raises important health considerations, particularly when Chlorella is sourced from suboptimal environments [94, 95]. Prolonged consumption of contaminated products could lead to severe health issues, including hepatotoxicity. Therefore, it is crucial for consumers to select Chlorella supplements from reputable manufacturers that adhere to rigorous quality control measures and undergo third-party testing [96]. Given the predominance of short-term studies and the limited safety data available, any clinical recommendations regarding Chlorella supplementation should be approached with caution, weighing the observed benefits against potential risks and individual patient factors.

Clinical implications of findings

The present GRADE-assessed systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that Chlorella supplementation may exert multifaceted benefits on cardiometabolic health in the general population. Statistically significant improvements were observed in several anthropometric parameters, markers of insulin sensitivity, selected lipid indices, and inflammatory biomarkers. However, the absolute magnitude of these effects was generally modest, and their clinical relevance should therefore not be overstated. Chlorella supplementation may contribute to favorable changes in body composition and metabolic parameters, but the current evidence is insufficient to conclude that these changes translate into clinically meaningful reductions in cardiometabolic risk. The observed reductions in HOMA-IR, circulating insulin, LDL-C, and hs-CRP may indicate a potentially supportive metabolic effect, while the trends toward HDL-C improvement and changes in oxidative stress biomarkers suggest possible broader biological activity. Taken together, these findings support the hypothesis that Chlorella may exert multidimensional effects on glucose metabolism, lipid regulation, inflammation, and redox balance; nevertheless, given the modest effect sizes, substantial heterogeneity, and low to very low certainty of evidence, these results should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as suggestive rather than definitive.

The hormonal effects, particularly reductions in leptin levels, provide additional insight into Chlorella’s role in adipokine regulation and appetite control. By potentially improving leptin sensitivity, Chlorella may help address the hormonal disturbances that sustain metabolic dysfunction, further explaining its impact on body composition and metabolic markers. These effects, combined with its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, position Chlorella as a promising adjunct for individuals with early metabolic alterations such as insulin resistance, mild dyslipidemia, or low-grade inflammation. Importantly, the intervention was not associated with adverse changes in liver enzymes or blood pressure, indicating a favorable safety profile suitable for long-term preventive strategies.

From a clinical perspective, dosing and duration appear to play a critical role, with potential optimal effects observed at moderate daily intakes (1500–3000 mg) sustained over at least 8–12 weeks. The nuanced dose-response patterns emphasize the importance of individualized treatment goals. Capsule formulations and integration with lifestyle interventions, particularly exercise, seem most effective. Overall, Chlorella supplementation can be recommended as part of a precision nutrition approach aimed at young to middle-aged adults with emerging metabolic risk, complementing lifestyle modification and supporting long-term cardiometabolic health and healthy aging.

Considering the current limitations of the evidence, the uncertainty surrounding several findings, the substantial geographic concentration of the included studies, particularly in Iran and Japan, and the marked heterogeneity in intervention characteristics, including the very wide range of Chlorella dosages, formulations, and treatment durations, recommendations for Chlorella supplementation should be approached with considerable caution. This heterogeneity complicates direct comparison across studies and limits the ability to derive precise, universally applicable clinical recommendations, particularly with respect to optimal dosing strategies. Therefore, any practical application of these findings should involve careful individualized assessment, recognition of the possibility of limited external validity, and close consideration of the confidence levels and methodological constraints underlying the reported outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

A central strength of this GRADE-assessed review lies in its adherence to rigorous methodological standards. The protocol was designed in alignment with PRISMA guidelines, employing a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases, and manual reference screening, minimizing omission of relevant studies. Advanced statistical methods, including meta-regression and non-linear dose–response analyses, enabled detailed exploration of Chlorella’s effects across populations, interventions, and outcomes. Integration of the GRADE framework strengthens interpretability, offering transparent insights into evidence quality and consistency. Inclusion of trials from diverse regions increases generalizability.

In contrast to previous meta-analyses that have primarily focused on isolated outcomes or select biomarkers, the present study takes a broader, integrative approach. By evaluating a wide spectrum of cardiometabolic parameters (including anthropometric indices, insulin sensitivity, lipid markers, inflammation, and oxidative stress), this study provides a more holistic understanding of Chlorella’s potential role in preventive health. Additionally, the larger pooled sample size contributes to increased analytical power and robustness, allowing for more confident interpretation of subgroup-specific effects related to dosage, duration, and participant characteristics.

However, certain limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. The methodological quality of the included trials was variable, with many studies exhibiting concerns related to inadequate blinding and unclear allocation procedures. Such limitations introduce potential biases that may influence pooled estimates and compromise the internal validity of our conclusions. To mitigate the impact of these methodological heterogeneities, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses stratified by sample size (≥25 vs <25 participants) and publication year (≥2020 vs <2020), which provided insights into whether study quality improvements over time or adequate sample sizes influenced the robustness of our findings.

Another important limitation is the marked geographic concentration of the included trials, most of which were conducted in Iran and Japan. This distribution may partly reflect the long-standing use and commercialization of Chlorella as a functional food in East Asia, particularly Japan, as well as more recent active research interest and trial feasibility in Iran. Nevertheless, such clustering raises concerns about external validity, because regional differences in dietary habits, supplement formulations, lifestyle patterns, co-interventions, gut microbiome composition, and genetic background may influence response to supplementation. Therefore, the observed benefits, especially those driven primarily by Iranian studies, should be interpreted cautiously and not assumed to be universally generalizable.

Considerable heterogeneity was also present across trials, likely stemming from differences in Chlorella species, administration forms (capsules, tablets, or extracts), intervention durations, and baseline characteristics of participants. This heterogeneity complicates the synthesis of findings and necessitates cautious interpretation when extrapolating results to broader populations. To address this critical limitation, we performed extensive subgroup analyses examining intervention type (tablets, capsules, and other forms), Chlorella species (C. vulgaris, C. pyrenoidosa, and unspecified), dosage categories (≥1500 mg/day vs <1500 mg/day), and intervention duration (≥8 weeks vs <8 weeks). These analyses provided valuable insights into which formulations, species, dosages, and treatment durations appeared to yield the most consistent beneficial effects, thereby enhancing the clinical applicability of our findings.

Moreover, the geographic concentration of studies, particularly in Iran and Japan, may limit global applicability, especially in regions with differing dietary habits, microbiome compositions, or genetic predispositions. To explore this geographic bias, we conducted subgroup analyses by country of origin (Iran, Japan, Korea, and other countries), which suggested important regional variations in treatment responses and helped identify populations where Chlorella supplementation may be most beneficial.

The demographic composition of study populations also varied considerably, potentially affecting the generalizability of results across different population subgroups. We addressed this limitation through comprehensive demographic stratification, including analyses by baseline BMI categories (healthy weight ≤25 kg/m² vs overweight/obese >25 kg/m²), age groups (<40 years vs ≥40 years), and sex distribution (both sexes, female-only, and male-only studies). These subgroup analyses help elucidate differential treatment effects across demographic categories, providing crucial information for personalized supplementation recommendations.

The intervention approaches also differed across studies, with some trials combining Chlorella supplementation with exercise interventions while others used Chlorella alone. To account for this methodological diversity, we performed subgroup analyses comparing Chlorella-only interventions versus Chlorella combined with exercise, as well as different control approaches (placebo/usual care vs exercise controls). These analyses helped isolate the independent effects of Chlorella supplementation and identify potential synergistic benefits when combined with lifestyle interventions.

The short duration of most interventions may further restrict the ability to evaluate long-term safety and efficacy, an important consideration for translating findings into sustained clinical practice. While our duration-based subgroup analyses (≥8 weeks vs <8 weeks) provided some insights into temporal treatment effects, the overall paucity of long-term studies remains a significant limitation. Future research should prioritize well-designed randomized controlled trials with extended follow-up, greater geographic diversity, standardized intervention protocols, and comprehensive demographic representation to validate and expand upon the present findings. The extensive subgroup analyses conducted in this review suggest potential parameters for designing future investigations by identifying key moderating factors and suggested intervention durations and doses.

Future research directions

The findings of this study highlight several avenues for advancing understanding of Chlorella’s therapeutic potential. Building on subgroup analyses identifying key moderating factors such as potential optimal dosage (≥1500 mg/day), intervention duration (≥8 weeks), and population-specific responses, future RCTs should prioritize standardized protocols incorporating these parameters. Longer-term studies exceeding the typical 8–12 weeks are also needed to assess sustainability and cumulative effects on cardiometabolic health.

To enhance the cumulative value, interpretability, and external validity of future research, we strongly recommend that subsequent RCTs adopt more homogeneous methodological approaches and recruit participants from more geographically and ethnoculturally diverse populations. Specifically, future studies should strive for greater standardization across key parameters, including supplement form and formulation, intervention duration, and precise dosage regimens. Such uniformity would facilitate more robust comparisons between studies, allow clearer dose-response assessments, improve generalizability across populations with different dietary, lifestyle, and genetic backgrounds, and ultimately accelerate the translation of findings into clinical practice.

Another priority is geographic diversification, as most existing studies are concentrated in Iran and Japan. Regional variations in response suggest that genetic polymorphisms, dietary habits, gut microbiome composition, and environmental factors may modulate efficacy. Expanding trials to populations in Africa, South America, and other underrepresented regions would strengthen global applicability.

The potential synergy between Chlorella supplementation and exercise warrants mechanistic research to explore how its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties may enhance exercise-induced adaptations in mitochondrial biogenesis, insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolism. Such work could provide insights that inform precision nutrition protocols optimizing timing, dosage, and formulation in relation to physical activity.

Considerable heterogeneity exists across intervention types (tablets, capsules, extracts) and species (C. vulgaris vs. C. pyrenoidosa), with limited reporting on bioactive compound concentrations, extraction methods, or quality control. Future trials should employ standardized extracts with quantified levels of chlorophyll, carotenoids, and peptides to clarify dose-response relationships and mechanisms.

Differential effects observed across demographic subgroups also suggest value in personalized approaches. Age- and sex-specific responses, BMI-stratified interventions, and pharmacogenomic studies could help tailor recommendations and identify genetic variants influencing metabolism and efficacy.

Beyond cardiometabolic outcomes, emerging evidence points to potential roles in cognitive function, immune modulation, and gut microbiome regulation. Well-designed trials with validated measures are needed to confirm these broader benefits.

Finally, translational research bridging mechanistic studies and clinical applications remains essential. Biomarker-guided trials and standardized methodologies could accelerate integration of findings into clinical practice, advancing Chlorella from a promising nutraceutical to a validated intervention for cardiometabolic health.

Conclusion

This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that Chlorella supplementation may exert beneficial effects on various health-related outcomes in the general population, including anthropometric indices, glycemic and lipid profile, inflammation, oxidative stress parameters, and leptin levels. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger effects among younger participants, those with higher BMI, and those with longer intervention durations. Despite the overall very low certainty of evidence for most parameters, the GRADE assessment indicates that these findings should be interpreted with caution, and any clinical or practical decision-making based on the current evidence should be approached conservatively. Further high-quality, large-scale studies are warranted to strengthen these findings.
