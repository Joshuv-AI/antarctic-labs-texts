Expanding the boundaries of EUS: EUS-guided fiducial placement and fine-needle biopsy in oligometastatic disease of the adrenal gland
Authors: Ricardo Marrero Torres, Hari Gopakumar, Dushyant Singh Dahiya, Aqsa Khan, Uday Patel, Sahib Singh, Talia Malik, Marzena Muller, Mariajose Rojas de Leon, Neil R Sharma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadiation therapy, including Intensity Modulated Radiation Therapy (IMRT) and Stereotactic Body Radiation Therapy (SBRT), is a crucial treatment for various malignancies, though side effects often arise from radiation exposure to healthy tissues. Fiducial markers are used in SBRT to optimize tumor targeting and minimize damage to surrounding tissues. Traditionally, fiducials are placed surgically or percutaneously, but EUS offers a safer, less invasive, and potentially cost-effective alternative. While fiducial placement within the pancreas has been well-studied, there are limited data on EUS-guided fiducial marker placement in non-GI organs, particularly the adrenal glands.MethodsThis retrospective case series evaluated EUS-guided fiducial marker placement in the left adrenal gland in 6 patients from 2020 to 2023. Patients were selected following multidisciplinary tumor board discussions, and all procedures were performed using a 22-gauge Menghini-tip needle under EUS guidance. Data were collected on patient demographics, tumor characteristics, technical success, and clinical outcomes following fiducial placement.ResultsThe procedure was technically successful in all patients, with no reported adverse events. Four fiducials were placed in each patient, aiding SBRT planning. Five patients proceeded with SBRT, and 80% achieved a complete local response on radiological follow-up. No significant fiducial migration was noted. The findings suggest that EUS-guided fiducial placement for adrenal tumors is safe and effective.ConclusionThis is the first case series demonstrating the safety and technical success of EUS-guided fiducial placement in the adrenal gland. This technique offers an alternative to more invasive approaches, facilitating precision radiotherapy for adrenal metastases, and highlights the expanding role of interventional endoscopists in oncology care. Further prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and expand their clinical application.

Radiation therapy is an effective treatment modality and has been used for several types of malignancies since the early 18th century. As with any type of treatment, it has side effects, most of which are the result of excess radiation on healthy tissue. Radiation therapy can be delivered in 2 major categories: intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT). Conventional radiation therapy, IMRT, delivers radiation with lower intensity over larger areas, usually in a span of 6 to 9 weeks. SBRT, on the other hand, delivers high-intensity radiation over targeted areas in a span of 3 to 5 treatments.1,2

To maximize tumor control while minimizing toxicity and collateral damage to normal tissue in the target organs, fiducial markers were designed to act as guidance for image-guided radiotherapy. In the beginning, these radiopaque markers were placed surgically or percutaneously using US or CT guidance. Both approaches have a higher morbidity if compared to an endosonographic approach. Surgical placement is a more invasive route and percutaneous approach could lead to higher risk of blood vessel and organ injury. In addition, the EUS-guided approach may offer benefits in terms of cost compared with the surgical and CT-guided approach.1,2 In addition, rapid onsite specimen evaluation, commonly used with EUS, allows for confirmation of carcinoma before fiducial placement with EUS.

EUS has changed this paradigm. EUS allows for precise placement of fiducial markers for SBRT. Newer, preloaded needles allow for greater ease of placement of cylindrical markers. Moreover, now we can confirm the diagnosis, perform locoregional staging and place fiducials in a single session of EUS.

Placement of fiducial markers within the pancreas has been well described in the literature.3, 4, 5 EUS fiducial placement can be done under US guidance only, which was the approach used in the current study. Currently, there are also preloaded fiducials that are commercially available, making the procedure even more rapid. EUS-FNA of the adrenal gland (not fine-needle biopsy [FNB], however) has also been described.6 There are few data on placement of fiducial markers in other organs outside the GI tract via EUS guidance.

We present the first case series in the English literature of patients with EUS-guided placement of fiducials to the adrenal gland.

Methods

This was a retrospective case series. After obtaining institutional review board (IRB) approval, data were collected, through review of electronic medical records, on patients who underwent EUS-guided left adrenal fiducial placement between 2020 and 2023 at our institution (Parkview Regional Medical Center, Fort Wayne, Ind, USA). Subjects were identified by retrieval of their information from an electronic medical record database of patients in which EUS was performed. To narrow our search, we used the CPT code 43253 coupled with the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, parent code C79. One hundred percent of the patients had only one lesion in the adrenal gland and no metastasis to other organs. These patients were referred for fiducial placement after multidisciplinary tumor board discussion of previous cross-sectional images.

All procedures were performed by an advanced endoscopy trained operator using a 22-gauge fork-tip needle for initial biopsy, and, through the interchangeable sheath-based system, a 22-gauge Menghini-tip needle with preloaded fiducial markers was used to place 4 fiducials under endosonographic guidance only. Four fiducials were used to help facilitate contouring (drawing onto CT renderings for radiation mapping in 3 dimensions). The fiducials were placed in the periphery of the tumor with slight change in angle of the needle penetration in the second pass for mapping it in 3 dimensions. Fiducials were placed at least 1 cm apart to facilitate detection when contouring was done for SBRT planning. All patients underwent FNB with rapid on-site evaluation performed in the same session. FNB was defined as use of a fork-tip biopsy needle that was passed into the target. The tissue was expressed to slide for touch preparation, with remaining tissue going directly into a formalin jar for histology (no cell block was required). The slide was prepared and evaluated in the endoscopy room by an experienced cytopathologist. Two to 4 passes per patient were performed.

All patients had onsite diagnosis of carcinoma in the adrenal gland leading to subsequent same-session fiducial marker placement. No peri-interventional antibiotics were provided in these patients. All patients had solid lesions. In a large retrospective study, analogous to ours, involving patients with pancreaticobiliary malignancies, no significant infectious adverse events were observed, although the data on antibiotic administration were sparse.7 Propofol sedation without intubation was administered by an anesthesiologist for the procedure.

We did not obtain consent from patients because this study was IRB approved as an except review based on minimal risk and de-identified data use.

Results

Our series included 6 patients. Two were female and 4 were male, with a mean age of 59 years (range, 51-71 years). Lung cancer was the primary malignancy in two-thirds of the patients. The mean size of the adrenal tumor was 21.8 ± 8.7 mm in the long axis according to endosonography. A 22-gauge Menghini-tip needle with preloaded fiducial markers was used to place 4 fiducials at the periphery of the adrenal tumor in all patients. No periprocedural or immediate postprocedural adverse events were reported.

All patients had oligometastatic disease to the adrenal gland. The procedure was technically successful in all cases (100%). In 83% of the cases, the fiducials were placed in the same session as left adrenal metastasis FNB. Five of the 6 patients went on to receive and complete radiation palliative therapy via SBRT. One patient opted not to have SBRT due to changes in clinical status but was able to have contour mapping and was deemed a technically successful placement.

The patients received a median of 5 fractions of SBRT (3-10 fractions) with a maximum mean dose of 51 Gy (30-59 Gy). Patient no. 1 has the BRCA1 (breast cancer gene 1) mutation, alongside her metastatic breast cancer (Table 1) for which she required follow-up EUS for pancreatic cancer screening; during this evaluation, a significant decrease was noted in the size of the left adrenal nodule (Figs. 1 and 2). Patient no. 2 had stable disease defined by neither a ≥30% decrease nor a ≥20% increase in size. Eighty percent of the patients who received SBRT achieved complete response based on surveillance radiologic studies. There was no “clinically significant” fiducial migration noted by the radiation oncologists participating in patient care radiation planning during the time frame of this study. “Clinically significant migration” was defined as migration that did not allow for delivery of stereotactic radiation due to lack of ability to note the fiducials for contouring during radiation planning.

Discussion

Malignant tumors of the adrenal glands, as in the liver, most commonly are metastases arising from a non-adrenal primary site. In a population-based study of adrenal masses, >85% of malignant lesions were considered secondary adrenal tumors, with the lung as the most frequent primary organ.7 Likewise, in the current series, two-thirds of the patients’ primary malignancy was lung, either small or non–small cell carcinoma. All the patients had a stage IV diagnosis because of their metastatic nature, but all met the criteria for oligometastatic disease defined as no more than 5 metastatic sites.8

A recent meta-analysis, comprising 1155 patients in whom fiducials were placed under EUS guidance, reported only a 3% migration rate and 4% adverse events.3 The adverse events were mild and dependent on the target organ for fiducial placement (eg, acute and increased pancreatic and liver enzyme levels in the pancreas and liver, respectively). The same article reported 98% technical success. The authors suggest that the most common factors leading to this 2% technical failure were intervening vessels, the use of a 19-gauge needle system, and the trans-duodenal approach. It is important to note that none of these fiducials was placed in the adrenal gland. Our series provides novel aspects in this regard.

The main finding from the current case series was that fiducials placed under EUS to the left adrenal gland was a safe technique, with none of the patients reporting postprocedural adverse events. All the patients who were planned to undergo fiducial placement endosonographically were completed successfully, with subsequent SBRT contouring and mapping by a radiation oncologist confirming adequate fiducial marker placement (100% technical success).

The study patients were selected by a multidisciplinary tumor board for SBRT as it was deemed this would be a more cost-effective and less morbid option than surgery for these patients. Because our interventional endoscopy group has close collaboration with oncology for clinical work and research, patients were referred for EUS-guided biopsy and placement. This emphasizes the evolving role from “Interventional GI” to “Interventional Endoscopist,” extending our expertise beyond the traditional GI tract organs. SBRT is used in select cases of locally advanced cancer in delicate organs to reduce collateral damage to healthy tissue (eg, lung, pancreas) and, in some cases, for oligometastatic disease to reduce patient morbidity and cost. In some of these cases, the metastasis was small and the positron emission tomography scan or MRI was unclear; EUS therefore had the advantage of tissue diagnosis, additional core for molecular analysis, and same-session fiducial placement.

Given the known safety of fiducials being placed under EUS guidance, compared with a surgical or percutaneous approach, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network recommends it to be placed by using an endosonographic approach specifically in pancreatic adenocarcinoma.9 There are no significant data in the realm of EUS-guided fiducials for adrenal glands. There is a risk with interventional radiology when a biopsy is conducted percutaneously in the adrenal gland. This risk has been reported to be around 2.8%.10 Placement of fiducials increases that risk, to a limited degree.

Surgical resection of the adrenal gland or adrenalectomy continues to be the criterion standard for patients with isolated adrenal metastases. Despite this alternative, some patients are not good surgical candidates based on comorbidities or even patient preferences. SBRT is an excellent option with good outcomes. Limited data on the head-to-head efficacy of SBRT versus surgery for oligometastatic disease are available.

A systematic review including >1000 patients with adrenal metastasis who subsequently underwent SBRT reported 83% local control at 1 year.11 Similarly, in our series, 4 of the 5 patients showed complete response by imaging, for 80% local control. We hypothesized that the lack of response in patient no. 2 is because the primary malignancy was renal cell carcinoma, which has been classified as less radiosensitive or radioresistant.12 Patient no. 5 was not able to receive SBRT to the left adrenal gland as he later developed disease progression with more symptomatic metastases that were given priority for palliation.

It is imperative for interventional endoscopists trained and willing to place fiducials endosonographically to understand diagnosis, staging, and management of advanced malignancies. Moreover, it is highly recommended to have a multidisciplinary meeting that includes the medical oncologist, radiation oncologist, surgical oncologists, pathologists, and other healthcare workers; the goal is to review these cases with interventional endoscopy and gastroenterology participation to advance the field and incorporate new diagnostic and treatment strategies, as well as optimize patient outcomes.

The benefit of fiducial placement stems from its nature to work as a marker for adequate tumor visualization during radiotherapy and, at the same time, allow proper respiratory motion management, reducing the target of volume radiation with the goal of avoiding neighboring organs at risk. In the adrenal glands, the organs at risk include the spinal cord, kidneys, liver, duodenum, small and large bowels, and stomach.

Another important aspect is the likely cost-effectiveness, as it is possible to perform a biopsy and place the fiducials in the adrenal gland during the same session. In addition, per a cost-effectiveness study in prostate cancer that compared SBRT with IMRT, it was shown that the former modality provides more significant healthcare cost savings.2

Among the strengths of the current study is that this is the first report, to our knowledge, of the use of EUS for fiducial core biopsy (FNB) and adrenal fiducial implantation in a case series. We show in this pilot series that fiducials can be placed in distant metastasis such as the left adrenal gland safely and effectively by using EUS guidance. These positive observations can generate hypotheses that should be supported by more structured studies (eg, prospective trials), which, if proven to be effective and safe, could change clinical management in the future.

The limitations of this study, inherent to its methodology of being a descriptive study, are the inability to detect causality and being unable to be generalized in a community setting. Another limitation is the small number of subjects who underwent fiducial placement. Further studies should be done in a prospective and controlled scenario to confirm our observations.

In conclusion, the role of interventional endoscopists is evolving beyond “gastroenterology.” We believe the field can play a key role as part of multidisciplinary care in patients with complex diagnoses such as advanced malignancies. As interventional endosonographers, we can provide patients with innumerable diagnostic, staging, and therapeutic options. This is the first series showing that a core biopsy can be conducted and fiducials can be placed safely into the adrenal gland under EUS guidance in this group of patients so that they can benefit from synergistic but focused radiotherapy.
