Biodegradable coating based on chitosan and sea buckthorn for fish fillet preservation
Authors: Ayşe Kara, Gonca Alak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effectiveness of a chitosan-based edible coating enriched with sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides L.) oil (HrEO) in extending the shelf life and preserving the quality of refrigerated fish fillets. In line with the increasing interest in natural preservation strategies, HrEO—characterized by its high antioxidant capacity—was incorporated into a chitosan (Ch) matrix to develop a bioactive coating formulation. The coated fillets were stored at + 4 °C, and their quality was assessed over 12 days through microbiological (total aerobic mesophilic bacteria, lactic acid bacteria, psychrotrophic bacteria, yeast–mold, Enterobacteriaceae), chemical (TVB‑N, TBA, pH), and sensory analyses. The HrEO + Ch coating exhibited a pronounced inhibitory effect on microbial growth, particularly by reducing total aerobic mesophilic bacteria counts. Compared to the control and Ch-only treatments, the HrEO + Ch application extended the shelf life by approximately 8% and 4%, corresponding to an estimated 3‑day improvement. Additionally, the incorporation of HrEO positively influenced the colour attributes and sensory acceptance of the fillets, enhancing their overall visual and organoleptic quality. These findings highlight the potential of HrEO-enriched chitosan coatings as an effective natural preservation strategy for maintaining the freshness and consumer appeal of fish fillets during chilled storage.Keywords: Fillets, Sea buckthorn oil, Chitosan, Bio-based coatings, Fillet quality, RefrigerationSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Zoology

Introduction

The continuous change in consumer demands and sales trends leads to innovative approaches in food packaging technologies. Edible film and coating technology has emerged with the increasing demands for quality food consumption, the search for new technologies for food preservation, plastic debris from synthetic polymers (such as polyethylene (PE)) and the idea of producing new technologies from sustainable resources. Materials present as thin layers between food components that can be consumed are called “edible films.” For these films to be accepted by consumers, they must preserve the original properties of the unprocessed food as closely as possible1–4.

Nowadays, consumers prefer minimally processed, natural products. With the demand of consumers for less processed foods, new packaging technologies called “active and smart packaging” have been developed. In active packaging, it is aimed to improve the functions of active ingredients by adding them to the packaging material and to extend the shelf life by keeping the quality of the food constant5.

One application of active packaging is the use of edible films containing antimicrobial and antioxidant substances. In this system, these substances can migrate from the coating matrix to the food, allowing them to remain in the coating for a longer period and enhancing their effectiveness against microorganisms. The migration rate may vary depending on the polymer structure of the coating material. A practical example of this approach is the incorporation of naturally derived essential oils with antimicrobial and antioxidant properties into edible films and coatings1–3,6.

As natural preservatives, essential oils (EOs) are effective only at specific critical concentrations, and the dosage must be carefully optimized to achieve the desired antimicrobial and antioxidant effects7. However, their use is limited at high concentrations, as it may cause changes in taste and color8.

Recently, the use of antioxidant-rich plants as dietary supplements has been increasing in the literature9. Since it is difficult to obtain the necessary antioxidants and beneficial compounds in sufficient amounts on a daily basis, dietary supplements provide a practical means to facilitate their daily intake10. Among medicinal and aromatic plants, sea buckthorn (Hippophae rhamnoides L.) stands out as a shrub-like species characterized by its thorny, orange-colored fruits. It naturally grows along riverbanks, roadsides, and sandy soils. The fruits, which are the primary part utilized, are rich in bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, flavones, carotenoids, and various vitamins. Sea buckthorn has been reported to enhance immune defense mechanisms and exhibit protective effects against influenza, common cold, pneumonia, cardiovascular diseases, cancer, tumors, ulcers, and other infections, largely due to its high vitamin C content and antioxidant properties11,12. Yet, other antioxidants such as tocopherol, tocotrienol, carotenoids and flavonoids are present in small amounts9,13.

The aim of this modelling-based study is to investigate, for the first time, the potential use of Hippophae rhamnoides L. essential oil (HrEO)—a medicinal and aromatic plant component known for its enzyme activity, lipid peroxidation mechanism, and notable antimicrobial and antioxidant properties—in bio-based coatings as a natural food preservative. It is hypothesized that incorporating HrEO into bio-based coatings will enhance the antimicrobial and antioxidant properties of the coating material, thereby improving the safety and shelf life of aquatic food products.

Material and method

Trial materials and trial design

The research was carried out in the laboratories of Atatürk University and Recep Tayyip Erdoğan University. In the study, 180 rainbow trouts (O. mykiss) fillets with an average weight of 28015 g obtained from Atatürk University Faculty of Fisheries, Inland Fish Research and Production Centre are used (Erzurum, Türkiye). The EO (Hippophae rhamnoides L., HrEO) is obtained cold pressed from a commercial company (Polente, İstanbul, Türkiye). Chitosan obtained from a commercial company (Low viscous chitosan, Sigma-Aldrich, Mw ≈ 50–190 kDa, Israel, ). After dissolved in acetic acid as a 1.5%, w/v solution overnight at room temperature4. Completed the dissolution process, chitosan solutions were enriched with EO via adding to this solution at %2 HrEO14. For obtaining a homogeneous mixture, the solutions are stirred again in a magnetic stirrer for 15 min and left to rest (30 min) at room temperature.

Application of coating solutions to fillets

By applying the dipping method to the prepared coating solutions, the fillets were kept in the solution (not less than 30 s) under sterile conditions and all fillets’ surfaces were covered3,4 (Table 1). The coated fillets were stored under refrigerator conditions (4 ± 1οC) for 12 days. Microbiological, chemical and sensorial analyzes were performed to determine fillet quality and shelf life at certain intervals (0., 3., 6., 9. and 12 days) during this storage period.

Cold storage analyses

Microbial activity analysis

Microbial analyses of the fillet samples were performed for total aerobic mesophilic bacteria, lactic acid bacteria, psychrotrophic bacteria, yeast and mold, and Enterobacteriaceae. For the analyses to be performed under sterile conditions, 25 g of sample was taken and homogenised in Stomacher by adding 250 mL of saline water. Appropriate dilutions were prepared from this homogenate and analyses were performed for each bacterial species by considering specific media and incubation conditions (Table 2)15,16. After incubation, colonies were counted manually. The results were expressed as log colony-forming units per gram (log CFU/g) of sample.

The following parameters, which give results in a short time and are widely used in the monitoring of chemical changes occurring in fish muscle during deterioration, were taken into consideration.

The resulting solution was treated with NaOH and placed in the distillation apparatus. The resulting distillate was titrated until achieving pink colour and TVB-N value was calculated as mg/100 g16,18.

The resulting distillate was titrated until achieving pink colour and TVB-N value was calculated as mg/100 g16,18.

Determination of TBA (thiobarbituric acid) value: The thiobarbituric acid (TBA) analysis was carried out to determine the degree of lipid oxidation of samples according to the method described by19. For TBA analysis, 10 g of croquette samples were mixed with 50 mL distilled water and homogenized using homogenizer for 2 min. The homogenized samples, 47.5 mL distilled water, and 2.5 mL of 4 M HCI were added in Kjeldahl tubes. The distillation was continued until 200 mL of distillate was collected and 5 mL of distillate and 5 mL of TBA reagent were placed in a capped glass tube and boiled in a water bath for 35 min. The absorbance of the solutions in the tubes cooled at room temperature was measured with a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV 160) at a wavelength of 532 nm. The TBA value was calculated by multiplying the absorbance value by a factor of 7.8 and results were expressed as mg malondialdehyde/kg of samples.

Sensory analyses

The analyses were carried out by ten panelists. The panelists evaluated the raw fillets of the treatment groups in terms of colour, appearance and odour. A hedonic scale with a score range of 1–3 (very poor - unacceptable), 4–5 (intermediate), 6–7 (good), 8–9 (very good) was used for the panellists’ evaluations20. Scores of 4 or above were considered acceptable, indicating that the samples met the minimum sensory quality standards for consumption.

Statistical analyses

A two-way ANOVA was conducted using the “general” linear model (GLM) procedure (SPSS, 20.0). Out put on the quality parameters were analyzed using the SPSS statistical package. The GLM procedure included the main effects of the HrEO, storage time, and 2-way interactions between these factors. Each analysis was performed in three replicates (n = 3), and the mean values were used for statistical evaluation. Duncan’s multiple comparison test was applied in order to find the statistical differences between significant means (P < 0.05) (SPSS 20.0).

Results

Studies on medicinal plants and the active substances obtained from them and the interest in these plants have increased considerably in recent years. Lately, many researches have been carried out on the effect of Hippophae rhamnoides L. fruit on human health in the medical field. But no studies investigating the potential food preservative effects of the oil of Hippophae rhamnoides L. have been found.

Microbiological assessment

In the present study, this vegetable oil is used as a food preservative in bio-based coatings for the first time based on its known antioxidant / antimicrobial potential; and considering the findings obtained; it is determined that the single and integrated use of HrEO with chitosan is significantly (p < 0.05) effective on microbiological parameters on shelf life (Fig. 1).

The influence of storage time and HrEO on microbiological status of fillets (log cfu/g).

The results of total aerobic mesophilic bacteria, psychrotrophic bacteria, lactic acid bacteria, Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas bacteria determined during cold storage (4 ± 1οC) in rainbow trout fillets treated with HrEO enriched coating solutions are given in Fig. 1. As seen in Fig. 1, the number of target bacteria increased in all groups as the storage period progressed. In terms of total aerobic mesophilic bacteria number, the highest increase in the treatment groups is observed in the control group. This is followed by Ch > HrEO + Ch > HrEO, respectively. On the 9th day of storage, the number of bacteria in the control group reached 6–7 cfu/g, which is accepted as the limit value according to research [21, 22 ]. A similar tendency is determined in the number of psychrotrophic bacteria, and an increase is recorded in parallel with the increase in storage time.

While the number of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) is 102 cfu/g in all groups at the beginning of storage (day 0), the highest number of LAB is detected at 107- 106 cfu/g on the last day of storage as Control > HrEO > Ch > HrEO + Ch, respectively; and the number of lactic acid bacteria increased as the storage period progressed. When the numbers of Enterobacteriaceae and Pseudomonas are analysed, the lowest levels in both groups are detected in Ch and HrEO + Ch groups.

Chemical assessment

Regarding the chemical parameters, pH, TVB-N and TBARS index values of the groups in which HrEO is applied alone or in combination with Ch (HrEO + Ch) showed significant decreases at the end of storage compared to the group with chitosan alone (Ch) (Fig. 2). When the single use of HrEO is compared with the control group (day 12 is taken as reference), the changes in these parameters are determined decrease as pH: 0.6%, TVB-N: 45.4% and TBARS: 40%. While the control (29.33 mg/100 g) exceeded the TVB-N consumable limit value (25 mg/100 g)14 by 17.32% on the 12th day of storage among the application groups, the other groups remained below this value even on the last day of storage by 17% (Ch), 36% (HrEO) and 37.6% (HrEO + Ch), respectively.

Among the applications, the lowest scores in colour, odour, and appearance on the last day of storage are determined in the control group and the highest scores are determined in the HrEO group among the application groups. In the changes between both groups, HrEO is determined increase 38.6%, 38.6%, and 54.1% in colour, odour, and appearance, respectively, compared to the control (Table 3). HrEO + Ch application showed higher scores in colour (increase 83%) and odour (increase 6.4%) and a 10.42% decrease in general appearance compared to Ch. However, on the 12th day of storage, all organoleptic properties of the control group fillets remained at moderate (> 4.0) values, while HrEO and HrEO + Ch groups maintained moderate and good bands.

Discussion

EO are produced from parts of plants as defence mechanisms against bacteria. These naturally occurring antimicrobial/antioxidant agents are highly complex mixtures of often hundreds of individual aromatic EO compounds extracted from different plant materials (such as bark, stems, leaves, roots, fruits, and flowers)23. The hippophae, which has found use in a wide variety of fields, owes this to the fact that it is rich in vitamins (especially A, C and E), soluble fibres, carotenoids, phenolics, flavonoids, organic acids, fatty acids, alkaloids, phytosterols and organosulphur compounds24. In our research findings, it has been determined that HrEO and HrEO + Ch have inhibitory effect on bacteria. Similarly, antimicrobial activity of Hippophae rhamnoides L. has been demonstrated in some studies. It has been observed that phenolic groups in HrEO prevent the growth of gram-negative bacteria (GNB)25. The overall antimicrobial activity of essential oils depends on their components. The main components of most EO include 1,8-cineole, terpinolene, terpinen-4-ol, methyl eugenol, (E)-nerolidol, (E)-methyl iso-eugenol, and (E)-β-caryophyllene. Moreover, the composition of EO is highly variable according to different geographical, environmental, and ecological conditions. EO and their components, due to their low molecular weight and lipophilic properties can cross membranes of cell, modify bilayers of phospholipid, increase fluidity of membrane, and cause leakage of cytoplasmic and ions contents. GNB may be more resistant to essential oils than Gram-positive bacteria (GPB) due to their structure of cell wall26 The reason for the variable effect of HrEO on bacteria in our study may be attributed to the fact that GPB are more sensitive than negative bacteria and the differences between cell wall compositions. As is well known, GP bacteria contain an outer layer of peptidoglycan, which is an ineffective permeability barrier27.

It has been reported that the phenolic and oil contents of Hr are effective in weakening the pathogenic effects of Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans28 and similarly, 1,5-dimethyl citrate in fruits may have potential effects in the treatment of inflammatory diseases. In another study, the minimum inhibitory concentration for Bacillus cereus and E. coli is determined as 25 µg/mL and 50 µg/mL, The minimum growth inhibition concentrations for Bacillus subtilis, B. cereus, and E. coli are given as 12.5 µg/mL, 12.5 µg/mL, and 25 µg/mL, respectively (12 Wang et al., 2024). In another study, it has been reported that Hr showed inhibitory effect on B. cereus, B. subtilis, B. coagulans, Yersinia enterocolitica, Listeria monocytogenes bacteria at 200–350 ppm levels27.

pH has a momentous role in the activity of the essential oils (EO) used. Because the waterproofness of EOs increases at low pH and reason them to work more efficiently14. The pH increase observed for all treatment groups may be as a consequence of the accumulation of having the qualities of alkali compositions such as ammonia (NH3) produced by the action of microorganisms and endogens. Along with this, in our study, it can be understood from the lower pH value that HrEO oil inhibits endogenase protease activity more at high concentrations. The tendency of the essential oil to dissolve in the lipid phase of the food due to its hydrophobic property at low pH is known to increase antimicrobial activity3. This is consistent with our microbiological analysis results. The determined pH results showed a significant increase in the pH of all samples during the storage period (Fig. 2). This increase is probably due to the production of amino group degradation by-products such as NH3, ketones and aldehydes during bacterial growth and storage, which cause the breakdown of amino acids, and enzymatic degradation of cellular contents30. The pH changes are very slight in the HrEO application group, which is attributed to the preventive effect of the coating material against gas permeability in addition to the decrease in activity of enzyme and growth of microbial attributed to the antimicrobial/antioxidant properties of HrEO29.

TVB-N is an important indicator for assessing the freshness of fish fillets and is an alkaline nitrogenous substance resulting from the breakdown of nucleic acids and proteins as a result of microbial spoilage. The remarkable increase observed during storage, especially in the control group, can be attributed to spoilage-causing bacteria switching from glycogen-dependent bacteria to protein-degrading bacteria31. In addition, high TVB-N levels indicate that nucleic acids and proteins are degraded because of enzyme activity, particularly microbial activity producing nitrogenous essential compounds. This pioneers to changes in the appearance of unpalatable odours, texture and tastes32–34. In fact, TVB-N is an index for the freshness of fish. According to European Union regulations, this parameter’s levels exceeding 35 mg/100 g are considered inadmissible for aquatic products. For O.mykiss fillet, this level is typically about 25 mg/100g32. The study findings indicated that the TVB-N level on the first day is 5–7 mg/100 g, after which there is a significant increase in TVB-N levels throughout storage among the groups (Fig. 2). The main reason for the dramatic increase in frozen storage time, especially in the control group, is the degradation of protein in the fillets due to the activities of proteolytic bacteria32. The results show that TVB-N levels are within acceptable limits in all groups except the control group where TVB-N in fish fillet exceeded 25 mg/100 g. For the other application groups, this showed that the coating also prevented the degradation of amino acids. The coating processes significantly reduced the rate of increase in TVB-N. Despite the significant effect of the Ch coating in reducing the upward trend of TVB-N, the inclusion of HrEO intensified the synergistic effect of the coating. These effects are related to the antimicrobial effect of HrEO. The compounds contained in the essential oil penetrate the cytoplasmic membrane of microorganisms, causing an electrolyte imbalance and leading to their subsequent decomposition. Disruption of the cytoplasmic membrane leads to release of ATP and depletion of cellular energy stores, ultimately resulting in cell death30.

It is known that food components of antioxidants such as anthocyanins and phenolic substances found especially in fruits and vegetables have positive effects on health9. The crude fruit extract of HR is found to be rich in both lipophilic and hydrophilic antioxidant enzymes. TBA index is an indicator of the extent of lipid oxidation (LO). In the first step of LO, hydroperoxides are generated which are irresolute and break down into compositions such as aldehydes. These compounds can form a coloured complex with the TBA reagent30. In our study, TBA index increased with storage time, the increase in TBA index in the early stage of storage may indicate the formation of MDA from lipid oxidation, while in later stages it may have decreased on account of reactions with other components (e.g. fish components containing nucleic acids, nucleosides, amino acids, and proteins from phospholipids)31. However, since HrEO does not contain ascorbic acid oxidase, which is responsible for the breakdown of ascorbic acid, it contains abundant vitamin C; and the vitamin content is also positively correlated with the content of β-carotene, organic acids, and ascorbic acid35. In contrast to this picture, the known increase in lipid oxidation in fillets containing HrEO is probably related to the development of phenolic aldehydes due to the degradation of some phenolic compounds of phenolic-rich agents36. In the findings we obtained, the increase in TBA index levels during storage may be due to both moisture loss during storage and oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. The findings showed that the coating material caused a significant reduction in the rate of TBA index value increases. While the inclusion of HrEO in the Ch coating significantly reduced the rate of TBA index level change, the coating material containing 2% HrEO showed the highest TBA index levels after the control group. The coating acted as a barrier, preventing the interaction of air with the fish fillet surface, and reducing its permeability, leading to a decrease in the oxidation rate and inhibition of lipid oxidation30.

It has been determined that there is a deterioration in the application group fillets depending on the storage period and the fillets become organoleptically unacceptable with a gradual decrease in the sensory score. Whilst the highest rate of decrease in the scores is observed in the control group, the results corresponding to TBA index and TVB-N values detected that the fillets are subjected to deterioration and changes in deterioration occurred. Similar results to our research findings have been recorded by37 for the same fish species. On the other hand, the increase in thiobarbituric acid (TBA) is accompanied by a decrease in the quality of the fish fillet and changes in taste and colour. Likewise, pH increases have adverse effects on the colour, texture, taste, and shelf-life quality of fish during cold storage30.

Different some factors (the pigment content, physical structure of fish fillets, and free water amount etc.) significantly affect the colour properties38. Enzymatic activity during cold storage leads to changes in the structure of myofibrillar proteins (MPs) and protein oxidation, affects light scattering and causes changes in L* values4. Coating processes not only reduce the rate of these changes, but also act as a barrier against lipid oxidation and the formation of oxidation by-products. These oxidation products may serve as a source of carbonyl compounds that accelerate the Maillard reaction30. The main flavonoid species of HrEO are white anthocyanin, isorhamnetin, kaempferol, epicatechin, catechin, myricetin, and quercetin39. Carotenoids are one of the main components and key characteristics of HrEO. These compounds are widely used as natural colorants in food products35. Color measurements showed that the high a* values in the HrEO and HrEO + Ch groups indicate an intense red tone in the fillets. The observed differences in color are attributed to variations in carotenoid levels resulting from the use of HrEO40. Furthermore, the yellowing of fillets during refrigerated storage is likely related to their carotenoid content41. These findings highlight the dual role of HrEO as both a food additive and a natural colorant. It has been assumed that the type, concentration, and interaction mechanism of phenolic additives cause changes in the colour of the coating, so that the opacity, colour values or transparency may change. In this mechanism, it has been determined that phenolic extracts or phenolics absorb light and cause a decrease in the L* values of the coating materials, namely darkening42. The presence of anthocyanin pigments and antioxidants in the film can enhance the redness (> a*), yellowness (> b*), and darkness (< L*) of film samples as the concentration of herbal agent in the film increases43. The orange, red, yellow, and purple hues of plants and fruits are caused by anthocyanins and flavonoids, which are natural pigments, and phenolic additives in films are known to give films a reddish or yellowish colour42.

In studies aimed at improving the quality and sensory acceptability of fish fillets44, developed alginate-based edible coatings containing ginger and thyme essential oils (EO), reporting that fish treated with coatings containing thyme essential oil, in particular, showed higher sensory acceptability in terms of odor according to consumer evaluations. Similarly45, reported that coatings obtained from chitosan and chitosan nanoparticles combined with clove oil significantly improved the sensory quality scores of grey mullet (Mugil cephalus) fillets during 24 days of cold storage compared to the control group. These findings indicate that bio-based coatings containing essential oils and chitosan are effective in enhancing the shelf life and sensory quality of fish products, and they show a similar consistency with the effects observed in our study using coatings containing sea buckthorn oil and chitosan.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the biological activities and potential applications of sea buckthorn oil (HrEO) in the aquaculture processing sector. The results demonstrate that incorporating HrEO into chitosan (Ch)-based coatings effectively preserves the quality of Oncorhynchus mykiss fillets during frozen storage. Optimization of HrEO and Ch concentrations, or testing on different fish species, could further enhance its efficacy. Sensory, chemical, and microbiological analyses revealed that HrEO contributes significantly to extending seafood shelf life while maintaining fillet quality.

Furthermore, HrEO exhibits strong potential as a natural additive across broader food sectors, supporting the development of environmentally friendly, safe, and cost-effective products aligned with sustainable and green agricultural practices. The findings provide a robust foundation for further exploration of HrEO as a multifunctional natural preservative in functional food industries, facilitating rapid integration studies. Future research should also address nutritional implications, regulatory considerations, and sustainability to fully realize HrEO’s potential in global markets.
