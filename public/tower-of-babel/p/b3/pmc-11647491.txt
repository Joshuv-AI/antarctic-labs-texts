Practice patterns of cardiovascular magnetic resonance use in the diagnosis of pediatric myocarditis: A survey-based study
Authors: Hannah M Jacobs, Jonathan H Soslow, Matthew D Cornicelli, Shae A Merves, Ruchira Garg, Mehul D Patel, Arpit Agarwal, Nilanjana Misra, Michael P DiLorenzo, M Jay Campbell, Jeremy Steele, Jennifer Co-Vu, Joshua D Robinson, Simon Lee, Jason N Johnson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is used to diagnose myocarditis in adults and children based on the original Lake Louise criteria (LLC) and more recently the revised LLC. The major change included in the revised LLC was the incorporation of parametric mapping, which significantly increases the sensitivity and specificity of diagnosis. Subsequently, scientific statements have recommended the use of parametric mapping in the diagnosis of myocarditis in children. However, there are some challenges to parametric mapping that are unique to the pediatric population. Our goal is to characterize clinical CMR and parametric mapping practice patterns for the diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatric centers.MethodsThe Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance Evaluation in Return to Athletes for Myocarditis in Coronavirus Disease 2019 and Immunization Consortium (CERAMICi) created a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) survey to evaluate clinical practice patterns for diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatrics. This survey was distributed to the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance community.ResultsFifty-nine responses from 51 centers were received, with only one response from each center being utilized. Only 35% (18/51) of centers (37% (14/38) North America, 31% (4/13) international) reported using CMR routinely in all patients with a suspicion of myocarditis. Diagnostic uncertainty was noted as the most important reason for CMR, while cost was noted as the least important consideration. The majority of centers reported using the revised LLC (37/51, 72%) compared to original LLC (7/51, 14%) or a hybrid criteria (6/51, 12%). When looking at the use of parametric mapping, only 5/47 (11%) for T1 mapping and 11/49 (22%) for T2 mapping reported having scanner-specific pediatric normative data.ConclusionRoutine CMR imaging for diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatrics is infrequently performed at surveyed centers despite the focus on a group of non-invasive cardiac imagers. While the majority reported using parametric mapping, few centers reported having pediatric scanner-specific normative data. This highlights an important gap in the utilization of CMR that may aid in the diagnosis of myocardial disease.Keywords: Myocarditis, Lake Louise criteria, Parametric mapping, Pediatric

Background

Myocarditis is a rare inflammatory condition of the heart muscle that occurs in children anywhere between 0.8 and 2.13 cases per 100,000 [1], [2]. Clinical presentation can be highly variable ranging from almost subclinical to fulminant cardiovascular collapse requiring mechanical circulatory support [3], [4]. While endomyocardial biopsy (EMB) has historically been the reference standard for diagnosing myocarditis, cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) utilization has greatly increased over time [5], [6]. In 2009 the original Lake Louise criteria (LLC) consensus statement required at least two of the three following criteria for CMR diagnosis of myocarditis with a sensitivity of 76% and specificity of 96%: edema based on T2 weighted (T2w) imaging, hyperemia or capillary leak based on early gadolinium enhancement (EGE), and necrosis or fibrosis based on late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) [7].

As the use of parametric mapping became more prevalent, multiple studies demonstrated an increase in the sensitivity and specificity of CMR through the addition of T1, T2, and extracellular volume (ECV) mapping, even in children [8], [9]. Given these findings, the revised LLC were published in 2018, incorporating T1 and T2 mapping and simplifying the criteria to having an abnormality in a T2-based marker suggesting myocardial edema (T2w imaging or T2 mapping) and a T1-based marker suggesting non-ischemic myocardial injury (T1 mapping, ECV, or LGE) [10]. The recent 2021 American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement on the Diagnosis of Myocarditis in Children and the 2022 Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (SCMR) expert consensus statement for CMR of acquired pediatric heart disease also recommend using parametric mapping to diagnose and/or confirm acute myocarditis by CMR [11], [12]. Despite this, barriers exist to utilizing parametric mapping in pediatrics, such as the availability of mapping sequences, technical challenges with optimizing sequences in patients with fast heart rates, additional data required (i.e. hematocrit within 24 h) for ECV calculation, and the need for scanner- and sequence-specific normative data. Limited real-world knowledge is available on how pediatric centers are utilizing parametric mapping and the barriers to its adoption.

Our aim is to perform a preliminary assessment of the practice patterns among pediatric centers to determine what criteria are used for diagnosing myocarditis by CMR and to determine what barriers exist for centers that have not adopted the revised LLC.

Methods

Consortium description

The Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance Evaluation in Return to Athletes for Myocarditis in Coronavirus Disease 2019 and Immunization (CERAMICi) Consortium was created during the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic to respond to the gap in data surrounding myocarditis after SARS-CoV-2 infection. The consortium consists of 13 pediatric institutions with a goal to better understand post-viral myocarditis diagnosis and prognosis in pediatric and young adult patients, particularly athletes [13].

Survey details

The CERAMICi Consortium created a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) survey to evaluate clinical practice patterns for diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatric patients (see Supplementary Information for survey) [14]. This survey was widely distributed to the SCMR community in 2022 and responses were solicited via Pediatrics/Congenital Heart Disease section mailing lists, newsletters, and direct communication. If multiple responses from the same institution were received, the initial response was analyzed. Individuals were asked about their institution’s location, interpreters of CMR, volume of both pediatric and adult congenital patients, and volume of myocarditis scans. Additionally, we ascertained practice patterns for utilizing CMR in the diagnosis of myocarditis as well as follow-up studies, considerations before ordering a CMR, including cost, patient clinical status, and diagnostic uncertainty.

General information was obtained such as magnet vendor, model, and Tesla (T) strength as well as the type of contrast utilized. The types of CMR sequences used for myocarditis were asked, specifically the use of parametric mapping sequences, barriers to parametric mapping, and what normative data were utilized for each. A detailed question about defining myocarditis by CMR, utilizing either the original LLC, revised LLC, or a hybrid LLC (non-ischemic LGE only and T2-based abnormality), was used. Additional questions related to post-processing software, reporting elements, research sequences, and image analysis were also asked.

Statistical analysis

Responses were evaluated based on total number and percent. Responses were then divided by region (North America versus international) and annual CMR volume in pediatric and adult congenital patients (small <251, medium 251–500, large >500 CMR/y). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts ). The study was approved by the University of Tennessee Health Science Center and Le Bonheur Children’s Hospital institutional review boards.

Results

There were 59 responses from 51 centers (Table 1). Multiple responses from the same institution were excluded for a total of 51 unique results. Thirty-eight respondents were from pediatric centers in North America with 37 from the United States (21 different states), 1 from Canada, and 13 were from international centers with 9 respondents from Europe, 3 from Asia, and 1 from South America (Fig. 1). Survey results were broken down by center CMR volume as well (Fig. 2). There were a total of 19 (37%, 19/51) small centers, 18 (35%, 18/51) medium centers, and 14 (27%, 14/51) large centers who responded.

CMR utilization

Only 14 (37%, 14/38) North American centers and 4 (31%, 4/13) international centers routinely performed CMR in all patients with clinical suspicion of myocarditis. More centers stated they only performed a CMR in cases of diagnostic uncertainty (10/51, 20%), performed CMR some of the time (10/51, 20%), or rarely performed CMR (1/51, 2%) than in all patients. Twelve (24%, 12/51) centers stated they performed CMR in all patients with clinical suspicion of myocarditis who could complete CMR without anesthesia. There was a higher proportion of North American centers performing over 20 pediatric myocarditis scans per year compared to international centers (13/38, 34% vs 2/12, 17%, 1 participant did not respond). EMB was infrequently utilized in those surveyed, with 43/50 (86%, 1 participant did not respond) reporting that they rarely or never performed EMB when diagnosing myocarditis and only 7/50 (14%) reporting that they performed EMB in cases of diagnostic uncertainty.

When evaluating factors that impact clinical practice patterns, diagnostic uncertainty followed by clinical status were the largest factors in considering whether to obtain a CMR across all institutions. Cost was noted as the least important factor. When specifically inquiring how important cost of care is in obtaining a CMR, 21 (41%, 21/51) centers stated it was unimportant; this result did not differ by size or location: small (8/19, 42%), medium (6/18, 33%), large (7/14, 50%) or geography: North America (15/38, 40%) and international (6/13, 46%). When asking centers in what setting CMR is typically utilized to diagnose myocarditis, 8 (16%, 8/51) centers perform CMR while inpatient, 2 (4%, 2/51) centers perform CMR outpatient after discharge, and 39 (76%, 39/51) centers perform CMR inpatient or outpatient. There was no difference in the timing of CMR for diagnosis of myocarditis between the North American and international centers or small, medium, and large volume CMR centers.

CMR diagnosis of myocarditis

The majority of centers reported using the revised LLC (37/51, 72%) compared to original LLC (7/51, 14%) or a hybrid LLC (6/51, 12%). One (2%, 1/51) institution did not reply to this question. This did not significantly change when looking across the various center locations and CMR volumes (Fig. 3). During the routine CMR myocarditis protocol, all 51 (100%, 51/51) centers reported performing volumetrics and function, 45 (88%, 45/51) performed T2w imaging, 15 (29%, 15/51) centers performed EGE imaging, 49 (96%, 49/51) performed LGE imaging, and 12 (24%, 12/51) performed strain imaging. There was no difference in volumetrics and function, T2w imaging, EGE imaging, LGE imaging, and strain between the North American and international centers or small, medium, and large volume centers. Thirty-nine (76%, 39/51) centers report T2w findings, 12 (24%, 12/51) centers report EGE findings, 48 (94%, 48/51) report LGE findings, and 5 (10%, 5/51) report strain findings during a myocarditis evaluation. There was no difference in reporting T2w imaging, EGE imaging, LGE imaging, or strain imaging between the North American and international centers or small, medium, and large volume centers.

While most centers reported using parametric mapping as part of the revised LLC, there were very few centers with scanner-specific pediatric normative data as recommended by the SCMR [12], [15]. More specifically, 48 (94%, 48/51) institutions reported obtaining native T1 mapping images in their protocol but only 40 (78%, 40/51) were utilizing or reporting the data. Forty-three (84%, 43/51) institutions reported obtaining T2 mapping images, but only 39 (77%, 39/51) used it clinically in reporting. ECV fraction values were the least likely to be calculated, with only 35 (69%, 35/51) institutions reporting obtaining/calculating it in their protocol and only 31 (61%, 31/51) utilizing it in the clinical evaluation of myocarditis. The largest barrier noted for ECV values was obtaining patient hematocrit.

T1 mapping and normal values

The most common T1 mapping technique was Modified Look Locker Inversion recovery with 42/47 (89%) institutions utilizing this technique. For native T1 mapping, 23 centers (49%, 23/47, 4 centers did not respond) reported using native T1 mapping reference values. Fig. 4 shows the source for normative T1 mapping values broken down by site location. Only 15 centers (32%, 15/47) reported using institutional normative native T1 mapping values from a pediatric cohort for interpreting native T1 mapping. Other responses included utilizing published pediatric or adult native T1 mapping normal values from similar scanners or utilizing institutional normative native T1 mapping values from an adult cohort. Eleven centers (23%, 11/47) reported that they did not have T1 mapping normal values and 2 (4%, 2/47) marked other but did not otherwise comment. When asked why native T1 mapping is not being performed, two institutions stated it was not available and one institution stated that they are still validating institutional normal values.

T2 mapping and normal values

Reported T2 mapping sequences varied more compared to T1 mapping. The most common T2 mapping sequences included T2-prep balanced steady-state free precession (24/41, 59%), followed by gradient spin echo (9/41, 22%) and T2-prep gradient recalled echo (GRE) (6/41, 15%). For T2 mapping, 20/43 centers (47%) reported using normal T2 mapping reference ranges. Fig. 4 shows the source for T2 mapping normal values broken down by site location. Only 13 centers (30%, 13/43) reported using institutional scanner-specific normative T2 mapping values from a pediatric cohort for interpreting T2 mapping. In centers not utilizing T2 mapping, five institutions stated it was not available, two reported using T2 mapping for research only, and one institution stated that they were still validating normal values.

Extracellular volume fraction and normal values

ECV fraction calculation showed a similar breakdown (Fig. 4) with only seven (20%, 7/35) centers reporting using normal ECV reference ranges. Seven (20%, 7/35) centers reported that they did not have ECV normal values they reported and one (2%, 1/35) marked other but did not otherwise comment. Regarding why ECV mapping was not being performed, 4 centers stated that ECV was not available, 1 noted that ECV mapping was used for research only, 10 reported that hematocrit was not routinely obtained, and 1 institution reported that they were still validating normal values.

Discussion

Our study aims to examine the utilization patterns of CMR to diagnose myocarditis in pediatric centers, practice patterns in parametric mapping, and potential barriers to its use in pediatric patients. The results of our survey show that although expert consensus recommendations are to use CMR to diagnose myocarditis in pediatric patients, only 35% (18/51) of centers (37% (14/38) of North American, 31% (4/13) of international) reported using it routinely in all patients with a suspicion for myocarditis [12]. We also found that while most centers reported obtaining parametric mapping and utilizing revised LLC to diagnose myocarditis, a minority of centers reported utilizing scanner-specific pediatric normative data, as recommended by SCMR.

This is the largest survey evaluating current CMR practice patterns in the evaluation of myocarditis in pediatric patients. We received a wide range of responses across multiple pediatric CMR centers across the world with various center volumes. This included 38 centers in the USA and Canada representing a majority of the top 50 U.S. News & World Report Best Children’s Hospitals for Cardiology & Heart Surgery [16]. Our survey results reflect CMR being performed on four major vendor platforms (Siemens, Philips, General Electric, and Canon) with multiple gadolinium-based contrast agents and doses. Therefore, this is a good representation of global CMR use to assess myocarditis in children from various locations around the world with various experiences in CMR in children.

Despite receiving survey responses from a non-invasive imaging-focused group, CMR utilization to diagnose myocarditis was low. Diagnostic uncertainty was noted as the most important reason for CMR, suggesting that centers are forgoing CMR when a patient is felt to clinically fit the diagnosis of myocarditis. Pediatric echocardiograms with good image quality showing grossly abnormal left ventricular function in the setting of minimal dilation may contribute to this clinical diagnosis. However, the recent 2021 AHA Scientific Statement on Pediatric Myocarditis reports that there is no current evidence to support using clinical features alone to confirm the diagnosis of myocarditis [11]. EMB was not a potential factor as we found that the majority of responses reported using EMB rarely or never at most centers, again suggesting that clinical features were primarily being used as the basis for diagnosis. The use of EMB to diagnose myocarditis continues to be rarely utilized as it is invasive, has decreased sensitivity from sampling errors, and is associated with complications [5], [17]. With a reported CMR utilization rate of only 35% (18/51), the findings of our survey suggest that CMR is currently being underutilized in the diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatrics. When accounting for centers that perform CMR in patients who do not require anesthesia, 59% (30/51) of centers routinely utilize CMR in pediatric patients with clinical suspicion of myocarditis. This increase in CMR utilization when anesthesia is not required indicates that general anesthesia is a possible barrier in younger patients or high acuity patients in obtaining a CMR. Despite this, CMR utilization to diagnose myocarditis remains relatively low and is likely multifactorial, including clinical judgment, institutional practice patterns, and general anesthesia.

In addition, our survey results showed that cost was the least important consideration for ordering CMR. When centers perform CMR to evaluate myocarditis in children, the study is performed as an inpatient solely or an inpatient or outpatient a majority of the time. The timing of the CMR may explain part of the reason cost was the least important consideration as centers in the USA avoid insurance precertification when studies are done as inpatient. Despite the potential concern for higher health care costs in recommending greater utilization of CMR, evaluating pediatric myocarditis with CMR has been shown to result in health care savings for extended length of stay [6].

Additionally, we reviewed the technical aspects of CMR in the diagnosis of myocarditis, including the version of the LLC utilized. Of the centers that responded to our survey, the majority (72%, 37/51) reported using the revised LLC to define myocarditis. Centers rarely utilized the original LLC (14%, 7/51) consistent with a previous multicenter study performed before the adoption of the revised LLC [5]. The previous multicenter study of CMR myocarditis techniques reported a low use of the original LLC due to the rare use of EGE that was also observed in this study [5]. We report slightly higher use of T2w imaging compared to the previous multicenter study (88% vs 66%) [5]. From this survey, most centers reported routinely using (96%, 49/51) and reporting (94%, 48/51) LGE, but the use of LGE was not unanimous across centers, which is different than previously reported [5]. The rare centers that reported not routinely using LGE reported routine use of T1 and ECV mapping. The consistent use of the revised LLC across centers regardless of CMR volume or center location and the rare avoidance of LGE reporting indicates an increased use of T1 and T2 mapping to diagnose myocarditis in children by CMR [18].

When examining the use of parametric mapping, a smaller number of centers reported having scanner-normative data (32% (15/47) for native T1, 30% (13/43) for T2 mapping). This suggests that some pediatric centers may be using parametric mapping for diagnosis without scanner-specific pediatric normative data, using parametric mapping techniques against recommendations from the SCMR [12], [15]. In addition, several centers responded that they are using normative data consisting of either published pediatric, published adult, or adult scanner-specific normative values in their clinical interpretation of parametric mapping.

This highlights the challenges in clinical implementation of parametric mapping in the pediatric population. The ideal normative value dataset would consist of prospectively recruited healthy control pediatric patients on each magnet. However, performing CMR in the pediatric population comes with unique considerations and challenges compared to imaging in adults [19]. The use of general anesthesia, and even the injection of intravenous contrast, for the purposes of a control study is generally not allowed by internal review boards. The recruitment of pediatric patients for research studies has previously been described and presents an additional unique set of challenges [20]. Pediatric centers are left utilizing “controls of convenience” with structurally and functionally normal hearts, such as evaluations for chest wall deformity, frequent ventricular ectopy, etc. This can limit the number of patients who are available and may result in some patients with abnormal myocardium who are inadvertently included.

Ideally, improving generalizability between different magnets (either based on field strength, vendor, or sequence) could address these issues. This could be addressed by the ongoing pediatric multicenter normative data collection or by improvements in sequence design. Though the most recent parametric mapping guidelines suggest that ECV is more generalizable and that institutional data are not necessary, the lack of robust normative ECV data in pediatric populations is a further limitation that should be addressed [15].

Limitations

One of the major limitations of the study was the sample size and the survey-based nature of our data. While we were able to note trends in myocarditis practice and reporting, it was difficult to meet statistical significance given our smaller sample size when comparing location and center volume. Therefore, statistical comparisons are not reported in this article. However, the primary goal of this study was to determine how pediatric centers were diagnosing myocarditis and how they were using parametric mapping. Even with small sample sizes, the survey supports the idea that practice pattern variations are broad and not exclusive to one group. This survey was also targeted toward members of the pediatric and congenital heart disease CMR community, which does not capture all physicians interpreting pediatric CMR, or those ordering CMR. Nonetheless, given the CMR-specific population, this survey was sent to, the low reported utilization rate for CMR for diagnosis myocarditis may actually be an overestimate of real-life practice patterns. Several studies have shown the utility of CMR in predicting clinical outcomes after myocarditis recovery in children, but this survey did not address prognostic value of CMR directly [3], [21].

Conclusions

In conclusion, CMR is underutilized in the diagnosis of pediatric myocarditis, despite the lack of evidence supporting clinical features alone to confirm diagnosis. While the majority of centers that responded to our survey are following the consensus recommendations to utilize parametric mapping and revised LLC in the diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatric patients, our findings suggest that appropriate normative values are not being utilized. This highlights the challenges associated with using parametric mapping in pediatrics. Further guidance and consideration of modified recommendations are needed to promote the appropriate use of parametric mapping for the diagnosis of myocarditis in pediatrics. Repeating this survey in the future may help determine the trends in the utilization of CMR techniques to diagnose myocarditis.
