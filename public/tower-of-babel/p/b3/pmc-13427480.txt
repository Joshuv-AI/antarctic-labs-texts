Balancing essential amino acids in low-protein diets: Impacts on growth performance, amino acid transport, and cecal microbiota in yellow-feathered chickens
Authors: Xinyue Liang, Jinling Ye, Sai Zhang, Mahmoud M Azzam, Xiajing Lin, Manting Ma, Xinyang Dong, Shuangshuang Zhai, Zongyong Jiang, Shouqun Jiang, Dong Ruan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of partially removing essential amino acids (methionine, tryptophan, arginine, and isoleucine) from a low-protein diet on medium-speed yellow-feathered broilers aged 1–30 days. The evaluation focused on growth performance, physiological and biochemical indicators, amino acid transport, intestinal microbiota, and plasma free amino acid profiles. A total of 1,080 one-day-old yellow-feathered broiler hens were randomly assigned to six groups: a standard protein group (SD, CP 20.5%), a low-protein group (LP, CP 18%), and four amino acids groups that 20% reduction relative to the LP group in methionine (LP-Met), tryptophan (LP-Trp), arginine (LP-Arg), isoleucine (LP-Ile), respectively. Each group consisted of six replicates, with 30 chickens per replicate, the experiment lasted for 30 days. The results showed that the final body weight (BW) and average daily gain (ADG) of the LP group were significantly lower than those of the SD group (P < 0.05). The LP-Arg group exhibited the lowest FBW and ADG and the highest feed conversion ratio (P < 0.05), while the LP-Ile and LP-Met groups showed significantly higher protein deposition rates compared with the SD and LP groups (P < 0.05). The LP group exhibited elevated plasma level of triglycerides, blood urea nitrogen, and growth hormone, along with decreased levels of insulin-like growth factor-1 (P < 0.05). Reduction of Met or Trp decreased TG level compared with those observed in SD group, whereas reduction of Arg or Ile lowered BUN level (P < 0.05). The expression of the neutral amino acid transporter (B0AT) and excitatory amino acid transporter 3 (EAAT3) in the jejunum of the LP group was significantly upregulated (P < 0.05), with partial recovery observed after the reduction of Met, Trp, Arg, and Ile. Moreover, the LP group exhibited reduced α-diversity in the cecal microbiota and a decreased ratio of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (P < 0.05). The LP-Met group showed enrichment of beneficial bacteria such as Faecalibacterium, whereas the LP-Trp group was characterized by an increased the abundance of Firmicutes phylum (P < 0.05). The LP-Arg and LP-Ile groups exhibited a trend toward recovery of gut microbiota diversity. The LP group exhibited reduced levels of taurine and cysteine (P < 0.05), with differential responses observed among the amino acid reduction groups (e.g., elevated Thr in the LP-Met group and decreased Arg in the LP-Arg group). In summary, Arg reduction in LP diet had the most pronounced inhibitory effect on growth, whereas dietary Met and Ile reductions promoted greater protein deposition. These findings highlight the importance of precise amino acid balance to optimize production performance and health in yellow-feathered broilers fed low-protein diets.Keywords: Amino acid balance; Yellow-feathered chickens; Amino acid transporters; Protein metabolism, Intestinal microbiota

Introduction

In recent years, with the continuous rise in global feed ingredients costs and increasing environmental regulations, low-protein feeding strategies has gradually become a hotspot in the research and practice of animal nutrition. Low-protein feed aims to maintain animal growth performance and health status while reducing nitrogen emissions and improving feed resource utilization efficiency (Wang et al., 2024a). However, this approach often carries the risk of amino acid imbalance, particularly in feeding systems where unconventional protein sources partially replace soybean meal. Imbalances in amino acid composition may constrain growth potential and impair metabolic functions (Maynard et al., 2022). Currently, low-protein diets for broilers involve precise optimization of essential amino acids (EAAs) levels to reduce crude protein content without compromising production performance. In the process of organisms’ protein synthesis, the availability of EAAs is a key determinant of translational efficiency and protein quality. For broilers, this process depends on dietary supply, especially under low-protein diet strategies. Deficiency of critical amino acids can trigger metabolic sensing mechanisms, affecting growth performance and physiological homeostasis (Chrystal et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024). Therefore, it's crucial to reassess EAAs concentration in low- protein diets to determine the optimal amino acid profile.

Determining of the “ideal amino acid ratio”involves establishing the optimal proportions of EAAs in the diet relative to Lysine. This ratio varies depending on animal species, growth stage, diet composition, and production objectives (Wu et al., 2022). Previous studies on broiler amino acid requirements were primarily based on conventional protein levels or soybean meal-based diets, and the established amino acid patterns may not be fully applicable when directly applied to miscellaneous meal-type low protein diets. This discrepancy arises because the digestibility of amino acids in miscellaneous meal, the interaction patterns among amino acids, and changes in protein turnover metabolism under low protein conditions can all alter the efficiency of amino acid utilization and their limiting order. Additionally, modern broiler genetic improvements have accelerated growth rates, dynamically altering amino acid requirements and necessitating continuous nutritional updates.

The intestine, as the primary site for nutrient digestion and absorption and a critical immune barrier, plays a pivotal role in determining overall performance and disease resistance. Deficiencies or imbalances in dietary amino acids can directly impair the morphology of intestinal villi, reduce digestive enzyme activity, and disrupt tight junction integrity, thus compromising intestinal barrier function and increasing the risk of pathogen invasion (Chen et al., 2017). Additionally, amino acids regulate the differentiation, proliferation, and effector functions of immune cells (Yang et al., 2023). Furthermore, when establishing an amino acid balance model for low-protein diets, it is essential to incorporate physiological health indicators such as liver function, antioxidant capacity, and immune function into a comprehensive evaluation framework to achieve synergistic optimization of production performance and overall health. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of balancing EAAs in low-protein diets on growth performance, amino acid transport, and cecal microbiota in yellow-feathered broilers.

Materials and methods

Birds, experimental diets, and housing

The medium-growing yellow-feathered chickens (Youhuang strain) were obtained from Heyuan Lihua Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd (Heyuan, China). The experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Animal Science, Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Approval No. GAASISA-2024-019). A total of 1,080 one-day-old female yellow-feathered chickens (32.5 ± 0.5 g) were randomly allocated to six dietary groups, with 6 replicates of 30 chickens per replicate. The control group was a standard protein group (SD), with feed nutritional levels [CP: 20.5%; standardized ileal digestible Met, Trp, Arg, and Ile: 0.43%, 0.20%, 1.05%, and 0.68%, respectively]. The low-protein group (LP) had a crude protein level reduced by 2.50%, with amino acid contents consistent with the SD group. The low-protein low-methionine group (LP-Met), low-protein low-tryptophan group (LP-Trp), low-protein low-arginine group (LP-Arg), and low-protein low-isoleucine group (LP-Ile) were established by reducing the levels of Met, Trp, Arg, and Ile 20% reduction relative to the LP group, while maintaining the levels of other amino acids unchanged (Table 1, 2). All amino acid levels were expressed as standardized ileal digestible amino acids. The nutrient requirements for Yellow-feathered chickens recommended by the Ministry of Agriculture of China (NY/T 3645-2020). The experiment was lasted for 30 d under the standard rear and management.

Growth performance

All chickens were weighed on a pen basis at the start and end of the 30-day experiment, and feed intake was recorded on a per replicate basis. The average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR, g feed/g BW gain) were calculated for the experimental period. Mortality records were taken daily, and the mortality rate was calculated.

Sample collection

At 22:00 on the day before the end of the 30-day trial, feed and water were withdrawn. The next morning, two birds per replicate with body weights (n = 12) close to the mean were selected. A 5 mL blood sample was collected from the wing vein, centrifuged at 4,000 × g for 10 min to separate plasma, and stored at −80°C for determination of plasma biochemical parameters. The liver was dissected and sampled, with two liver tissue samples placed in 2 mL sterile microcentrifuge tubes, labeled, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. These samples were then transferred to an ultra-low-temperature freezer for storage prior to analysis of hepatic enzyme activities. Two jejunal samples were also collected, placed in 2 mL sterile microcentrifuge tubes, labeled, and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. These samples were transferred to an ultra-low-temperature freezer for storage for further analysis. Furtherly, cecal segments were collected, placed in sterile sampling bags, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. They were stored at −80°C for analysis of intestinal microbiota composition.

Analysis of plasma and liver biochemical parameters

The commercial assay kits for total plasma protein (TP), albumin (ALB), uric acid (UA), triglycerides (TG), cholesterol (TC), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and activities of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. The concentrations of plasma growth hormone (GH), insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), insulin-like growth factor binding protein 5 (IGFBP-5), and hepatic glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. All kits were purchased from Jiangsu Enzyme Immune Industry Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from jejunal and ileal tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis; only samples with an RNA integrity number ≥ 6.0 were used for subsequent analysis. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript RT Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Primers (Table S1) were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 based on chicken (Gallus gallus) GenBank sequences and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). PCR products were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis prior to real-time PCR analysis. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, annealing at X°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. Each 20 μL reaction contained 1 μL cDNA (200 ng), 1 μL of each primer (10 pmol), SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa), and nuclease-free water. All target genes were run in duplicate. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001) and normalized to the control.

Full-length 16S rRNA sequencing of gut microbiota

Cecal digesta microbiota was analyzed via full-length (V1–V9) 16S rRNA sequencing. Bacterial DNA was extracted with E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA) per manufacturer’s instructions; genomic DNA quality was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 27F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-GTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) using ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR thermocycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific); amplicons were sequenced on PacBio RS II platform (Majorbio Bio-Pharm, Shanghai, China). Raw reads were demultiplexed, quality-filtered (QIIME) to retain high-quality sequences, then clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTU) (97% similarity) via UPARSE v7.1. Taxonomy was assigned to representative sequences using RDP Classifier v2.2. Alpha diversity (observed species, Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson, Good’s Coverage) was assessed via Mothur v1.30.1. Beta diversity was evaluated using weighted/unweighted UniFrac distances between OTU. PLS-DA (R package mixOmics, https://www.mixOmics.org) revealed inter-treatment differences in gut microbiota composition.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as means, standard error of mean or P-values. Replicate (n = 6) was taken as the experimental unit. Except where noted otherwise, 2 sampled birds per replicate were used. One-way ANOVA was employed to statistically test differences among treatment groups after normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed. If significant differences were observed, the Tukey’s HSD test was used to further identify specific intergroup differences. The statistical significance level was set at P < 0.05, and all statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.0 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Growth performance

Table 3 presents the growth performance and body protein deposition data of chickens from day 1 to day 30. There were no significant differences in the initial body weights of broilers across groups (P > 0.05), indicating good uniformity of the starting experimental birds. Different low-protein amino acid balance models significantly affected BW, ADG, ADFI, FCR, and body protein deposition of medium-speed yellow-feathered broilers at 30 days of age. In terms of growth performance, compared with the SD group, the LP group showed significantly lower FBW and ADG at 30 d of age but did not exert a significant effect on FCR. Among the four amino acids reduced in this study, the LP-Arg group exhibited the greatest inhibition of growth performance, with significantly lower FBW and ADG at 30 d of age compared to the other treatment groups (P < 0.05). Additionally, ADFI in the LP-Arg group was the lowest (P < 0.05), while FCR was significantly higher (P < 0.05).

Expression of genes related to protein metabolism in the liver and jejunum

As shown in Table 6, Table 7, it presented the effects of the low-protein amino acid balance model on the mRNA expression levels of liver protein metabolism-related genes in 30-day-old yellow-feathered broilers. The results indicated that, under the experimental conditions, neither dietary protein level nor the amino acid balance model significantly affected the expression of the analyzed liver protein metabolism-related genes (P > 0.05). Specifically, the relative expression levels of key genes involved in protein synthesis regulation signaling pathways, such as IGF-1, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), 4E Binding Protein 1 (elF4E), and ribosomal protein kinase 1 (S6KB1), showed no significant differences among experimental groups. Additionally, the expression levels of proteins reflecting protein degradation pathways, including 20S proteasome (20S) and tissue proteinase B (CTSB), as well as the metabolic regulator Strogin-1, were also not significantly different across treatments (P > 0.05).

Gut microbiome

Fig. 1 evaluated the effects of different dietary treatments on the intestinal microbiota structure of broilers through α-diversity, β-diversity, and microbial community composition analysis. The results of α-diversity showed that the Shannon index in the LP-Trp group was significantly higher than that in the SD control group, while the Simpson index was significantly lower than both the SD group and LP group (P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in the Ace and Chao indices among groups (P > 0.05). PCoA analysis revealed that SD control group samples were distinctly separated from all treatment groups (R = 0.446, P = 0.001), indicating significant dietary treatment effects on overall microbial community structure. At the phylum level, the microbial community was dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroides. Compared to the SD group, LP-Met, LP-Trp, LP-Arg, and LP-Ile groups exhibited increased Firmicutes abundance and decreased Bacteroides abundance, with LP-Trp group showing the highest Firmicutes abundance (P < 0.05). At the genus level, SD group was characterized by dominant Phocaeicola genera, whereas LP-Met, LP-Trp, LP-Arg, and LP-Ile groups showed significant enrichment of beneficial bacterial genera such as Faecalibacterium (P < 0.05, Table S2). LEfSe analysis further demonstrated that SD group exhibited significant enrichment of Bacteroides-related taxa, LP-Met group was characterized by Akkermansia, LP-Arg group showed enrichment of Firmicutes-related microbial communities, and LP-Trp group exhibited a more balanced overall microbial community structure. The comprehensive results indicate that LP-Trp treatment demonstrates the most significant improvement in intestinal microbial community diversity and structure.

Discussion

The LP group significantly reduced final body weight at 30 days of age. However, reductions in specific amino acids within a low-protein diet did not further impair growth but instead improved nitrogen utilization efficiency, providing new insights into precision nutritional regulation in broilers. This suggests that growth performance under LP feeding is not solely determined by crude protein level but is strongly dependent on the balance and functional availability of limiting amino acids. Among the four amino acids reduced in this study, Met and Ile reductions promoted greater protein deposition, whereas Arg reduction resulted in the poorest growth performance and feed efficiency, consistent with (Verhelle et al., 2024). The improved protein deposition in the LP-Ile group may be attributed to the close metabolic relationship between Ile and leucine as branched-chain amino acid isomers. Partial Ile reduction does not significantly impair protein synthesis or disrupt BCAA homeostasis, consistent with (Zeitz et al., 2019). This result suggests possible compensatory anabolic responses to moderate Ile reduction, though the underlying mechanisms require further study. Therefore, Ile may not be a strictly limiting amino acid in this nutritional context, particularly when overall amino acid balance is preserved. Similarly, Met reduction enhanced protein deposition, in agreement with (Conde-Aguilera et al., 2025), who demonstrated that Met limitation induces tissue-specific metabolic adaptation. Such adaptation includes improved translational efficiency in muscle tissues and a more efficient balance between protein synthesis and degradation. It is well known that Arg is a functional amino acid with multifaceted biological roles beyond protein synthesis, serving as a precursor for polyamines, creatine, and nitric oxide, thereby regulating cell proliferation, vascular function, immune modulation, and antioxidant defense (Gupta et al., 2024). The strong growth depression observed under Arg reduction reflects its central role in nitrogen metabolism and systemic physiological regulation. However, protein deposition in the LP-Arg group was not significantly reduced, possibly because the applied Arg reduction level did not reach a physiological threshold sufficient to limit protein accretion. Whether this reflects a non-linear response would require testing multiple levels of Arg reduction. It has been estimated that a 1% reduction in dietary crude protein can decrease nitrogen emissions by 8–10% (Alfonso et al., 2022). Therefore, optimizing amino acid balance within reduced-protein formulations is not only nutritionally relevant but also environmentally essential for sustainable poultry production systems.

The LP diet obviously altered plasma metabolites and endocrine responses, whereas targeted amino acid reductions partially alleviated these effects, highlighting the importance of maintaining metabolic homeostasis through precise amino acid regulation. The LP group increased TG levels, reflecting impaired lipid metabolism and altered nutrient partitioning under protein deficiency, consistent with (Xiao et al., 2025). This metabolic shift likely reflects reduced hepatic oxidative capacity and altered energy utilization efficiency. Reductions in Met or Trp were associated with reduced TG levels, suggesting possible lipid metabolism reprogramming that warrants further mechanistic investigation. Methionine reduction has been reported to upregulate FGF21 signaling, which enhances lipid oxidation, suppresses hepatic lipogenesis, and promotes thermogenic energy expenditure in adipose tissues (Fang et al., 2021). Similarly, Trp reduction may reduce hepatic VLDL assembly and secretion, thereby decreasing circulating TG levels and limiting hepatic lipid export (Fouad et al., 2021). Together, these findings suggest that specific amino acid reductions can reprogram lipid metabolism under low-protein stress. The LP group also exhibited elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN), reflecting increased amino acid catabolism and reduced nitrogen utilization efficiency. A reduction in Arg or Ile leads to a decrease in BUN levels. This suggests that specific amino acid reductions may rebalance nitrogen metabolism by limiting excess amino acid degradation and improving metabolic partitioning. Protein deficiency increased growth hormone (GH) but decreased insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), consistent with (Lauterio et al., 1987), indicating disruption of the GH–IGF-1 axis. This endocrine pattern reflects reduced hepatic anabolic signaling and compensatory endocrine adaptation to nutrient deficiency. Most amino acid reductions partially restored GH and IGF-1 levels, particularly Met and Trp, consistent with (Wang et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2013). These results highlight that Met and Trp play central roles in regulating endocrine-metabolic integration and growth signaling pathways. Liver function indicators (AST, ALT, GDH) reflected metabolic adaptation to amino acid reduction (Lala et al., 2023). Reduced IGF-1 under LP conditions agrees with (Saxena et al., 2020), while partial recovery in amino acid-reduced groups may involve IGFBP-mediated modulation of IGF-1 bioavailability and signaling efficiency (Jousse et al., 1998). Reduced AST and GDH likely reflect adaptive reductions in amino acid catabolism and ammonia detoxification requirements (Luczkowska et al., 2024). The reduction in Trp and Ile decreased AST and ALT activities, suggesting reduced hepatic metabolic load. Met reduction was associated with changes in liver enzyme activities, the mechanisms of which require further study. In contrast, Arg enhances ALT activity, indicating its unique amino acid-specific effect on hepatic enzyme regulation (Missiaen et al., 2022). These findings indicate distinct amino acid-specific effects on hepatic metabolic stress and enzyme regulation. Overall, Ile reduction primarily influenced IGF-1 regulation, whereas Arg reduction exerted a stronger impact on hepatic nitrogen metabolism and cellular stress responses, highlighting amino acid-specific metabolic specialization.

At the signaling level, mTOR-related genes (IGF-1, mTOR, elF4E, S6KB1, 20S, CTSB, Atrogin-1) showed no transcriptional changes, indicating that mTOR signaling regulates cell groth, proliferation, and protein synthesis after phosphorylation (Wolfson et al., 2017; Saxton et al., 2017). Similar observations were reported by Ma et al. (2023). The liver maintained metabolic stability through prioritized allocation of nutrients to essential physiological functions. The absence of changes in protein degradation-related genes suggests that hepatic proteostasis is more stable than skeletal muscle under amino acid reduction (Mordier et al., 2000; Schneider et al., 2014). This stability may be supported by enhanced recycling of endogenous amino acids and increased utilization of non-essential amino acids such as glutamine (Selle et al., 2024). Intestinal amino acid transporters (B0AT, EAAT3) exhibit upregulated expression under LP diet, may suggesting a potential enhancement of absorption capacity (Corzo et al., 2011). This response was attenuated after specific amino acid reductions, indicating that individual amino acids regulate intestinal nutrient sensing and transporter expression. Trp and Ile reductions influenced EAAT3 expression differently, consistent with their roles as limiting amino acids (Ruan et al., 2023). Met and Arg showed weaker regulatory effects (Fagundes et al., 2020; Barekatain et al., 2021). B0AT expression further increased under amino acid reduction, this suggested an enhancement of the compensatory absorption mechanism (England et al., 2024).

Low-protein diets significantly reduced gut microbial diversity and altered composition (He et al., 2025). Firmicutes decreased, whereas Bacteroidetes and opportunistic pathogens increased (Chen et al., 2025; Emami et al., 2021). Reduction of butyrate-producing bacteria weakened intestinal barrier integrity and anti-inflammatory capacity (Wang et al., 2024b), while Desulfovibrio expansion may promote inflammation through hydrogen sulfide production (Qi et al., 2024). Met and Trp reductions altered microbial structure without restoring diversity, reflecting their dual role as metabolic substrates and signaling molecules (Zuo et al., 2022; Seo et al., 2023). The decrease in Arg and Ile levels partially reflects the recovery of the microbiota. Arg may regulate microbiota via nitric oxide-mediated immune modulation (Okda et al., 2025; Guerra et al., 2019), while Ile may act through mTOR-associated nutrient sensing pathways (Green et al., 2023). These results support the existence of a protein-amino acid-microbiota axis.

Plasma amino acid profiles were significantly altered. Met reduction increased Thr and Ser via one-carbon metabolic adaptation (Li et al., 2023; Pranzini et al., 2022). Trp reduction activated the GCN2-ATF4 pathway, increasing asparagine metabolism (Solvay et al., 2023; Krall et al., 2021). Arg reduction increased Pro via protein catabolism and metabolic rerouting through the arginine-ornithine axis (Uyanga et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). The reduction in Ile increased Lys, likely as a result of branched-chain amino acid antagonism and metabolic compensation (Oliveira et al., 2023). All LP groups exhibited reduced sulfur-containing amino acids (Tau and Cys), reflecting adaptive prioritization of antioxidant and detoxification pathways under metabolic stress (Nagaraj et al., 2025). This indicates hierarchical allocation of amino acids to essential physiological functions during nutritional restriction.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that an LP diet reduced 30-day BW in yellow-feathered chickens and disrupted plasma lipid and protein metabolism, hormonal profiles, and sulfur-containing amino acids, LP diet with dietary amino acid balance could partially reverse the negative effects. The LP diet also reduced cecal microbial α-diversity, altered the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio; Met and Trp balance could promote the colonization that considered as beneficial bacteria (e.g., Faecalibacterium).
