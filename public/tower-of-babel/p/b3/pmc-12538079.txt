Identifying social isolation profiles among young and middle-aged patients with cancer in Western China: A latent profile analysis
Authors: Lu Yang, Xia Liu, Huiqiong Xu, Guorong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo identify the various profiles of social isolation among 18–59-year-old patients with cancer in Western China and examine their demographic, clinical, and cultural predictors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 300 patients from a tertiary hospital who completed standardized assessments of social isolation (Social Avoidance Scale, UCLA Loneliness Scale) and family functioning. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) was used to identify the subgroups, and multinomial logistic regression was used to analyze predictors of the profiles.ResultsThree distinct latent profiles were identified: “avoidance-dominant” (52.3%), which was characterized by high levels of social avoidance (12.52 ​± ​1.38) and low loneliness (30.87 ​± ​6.89), “loneliness-dominant” (27.0%), which was characterized by high levels of loneliness (53.15 ​± ​6.24) and low social avoidance (2.07 ​± ​1.38), and “balanced” (20.7%), which was characterized by balanced scores on both the measures. Individuals with fatigue, employment status, personality traits, and family dynamics significantly predicted profile membership (P ​< ​0.05) compared with those with a balanced profile.ConclusionsSocial isolation was heterogeneous among young and middle-aged patients with cancer. Fatigue significantly predicted distinct patterns of social isolation. Furthermore, exploratory findings indicated a potential role of religious beliefs in the avoidance-dominant profile; however, replication with larger samples is required. Family dynamics may buffer the risk of isolation in patients prone to avoidance, whereas those dominated by loneliness may lack such safeguards. Health care providers can implement tailored interventions to mitigate social isolation based on these varying profiles.

Introduction

Cancer is an increasing global health challenge, with the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) projecting 35 million new cases by 2050.1 Notably, approximately 50% of newly diagnosed cases occur in patients aged under 60 years.2 Although advancements in treatment have improved five-year survival rates to over 40.5%,3,4 this progress has highlighted the importance of addressing psychological well-being among younger patients with cancer. Research has indicated that disease burden, treatment demands, and financial strain often resulted in significant psychological challenges, including social isolation, anxiety, and stigma.5,6 Among these, social isolation has emerged as a particularly prevalent and pernicious consequence for young and middle-aged patients with cancer, and can profoundly impact their quality of life and treatment outcomes.5,7

Social isolation encompasses both subjective experiences, such as feelings of loneliness and helplessness, and objective behaviors, such as social avoidance and reduced social interaction.8 Family dynamics, particularly the interplay between roles, communication patterns, and collective coping, may critically shape these dimensions within Chinese families.9,10 Confucian values that emphasize familial interdependence can buffer the risk of isolation through enhanced emotional cohesion. Patients experiencing social isolation frequently reported social anxiety, avoidance behaviors, and profound loneliness.9 Furthermore, research revealed that severe social isolation significantly impacted disease trajectories in patients with cancer,10,11 which potentially led to depression, decreased mobility, reduced self-care capacity, diminished treatment compliance, and delayed medical interventions.10 Social isolation was also associated with altered hormone levels, compromised immune function, accelerated cancer cell proliferation, and reduced survival rates.10,11

Although often conceptualized as a singular phenomenon, social isolation is a multidimensional construct that comprises both objective and subjective elements. This study operationalized social isolation through three distinct yet interconnected dimensions: social avoidance (behavioral), social anxiety (emotional-cognitive), and loneliness (subjective experiential). Social avoidance is characterized by reduced social engagement and withdrawal behavior. Social anxiety is characterized by fear and discomfort in social situations and involves emotional and cognitive barriers. Loneliness is one’s subjective perception of isolation, and reflects the disparity between an individual’s desired and actual social connections. These dimensions were selected based on established research that highlights their integral roles in the experiences of social isolation among patients with cancer.12,13

Young and middle-aged adults experience significant development across physical, emotional, cognitive, and social domains.14 A cancer diagnosis and subsequent treatment during this critical period can lead to profound alterations in an individual’s occupation and lifestyle, which can also potentially result in health and social functioning impairments. Research has indicated varying degrees of social isolation among patients with cancer;11, 12, 13 some studies reported a 33% prevalence rate in patients with advanced cancer and high post-diagnosis isolation levels among patients with ovarian cancer.15 Furthermore, qualitative research has highlighted substantial post-diagnosis social isolation among patients with ovarian cancer.16 A systematic review identified associations between social isolation and physical and mental health status, social support, and treatment modalities.17 Nevertheless, research on the patterns of social isolation among young and middle-aged patients with cancer is lacking, particularly regarding distinct profiles and associated risk factors.

Contemporary methodologies for assessing social isolation among patients with cancer suffer from a lack of standardization. Researchers have employed various instruments that focus on specific aspects of social isolation, such as the Social Provisions Scale,11 Lubben Social Network Scale-6,18 and Los Angeles Loneliness Scale.18 Nevertheless, singular instruments may not adequately encompass all the dimensions of social isolation. Some researcher have acknowledged its complex and multifaceted nature and embraced further holistic approaches by utilizing the Chinese versions of the Social Avoidance Scale, Social Anxiety Scale, and UCLA Loneliness Scale in conjunction.12

Although variable-centered approaches can measure the severity of social isolation, a person-centered methodology, such as Latent Profile Analysis (LPA), is superior for uncovering the heterogeneous patterns of how patients experience social isolation. He et al.’s foundational LPA study identified distinct isolation profiles, which demonstrated its value.12 However, their study was limited to patients with breast cancer, leaving a critical knowledge gap. Experience of social isolation is unlikely to be uniform across different oncological diagnoses; varying physical symptoms, treatment burdens, prognoses, and social stigmas can fundamentally shape a patient’s relationship with their social world. Moreover, its characteristics among young and middle-aged patients with cancer remain inadequately understood. Therefore, we employed LPA, a statistical methodology, to examine the relationships between exogenous continuous indicators while maintaining local independence.19,20 Our study built upon prior work and addressing its limitations. This study used a heterogeneous cancer sample (including liver, colorectal, and other cancers) to develop a comprehensive and generalizable model of social isolation. We aimed to assess whether the profiles identified in a single-cancer cohort could be replicated, or whether qualitatively different subtypes would emerge that better reflected the diverse coping strategies in a broader patient population.

This study was grounded in the social exclusion theory,21 which posited that social exclusion generated social isolation; furthermore, socially vulnerable populations experienced marginalization due to personal, familial, or social-environmental factors.21, 22, 23 Our theoretical framework selected the explanatory variables, which included personal factors (disease characteristics, treatment modalities, personality traits, symptom burden), familial factors (relationships, financial status), and socio-environmental factors (employment status, living conditions), as well as the outcome variable of social isolation. Consistent with previous research, we conceptualized social isolation as a multidimensional construct that included dimensions of avoidance, anxiety, and loneliness.18 Collectively, these dimensions offered a comprehensive framework to understand the complex nature of social isolation.

Based on the social exclusion theory, this study aimed to: (1) identify distinct social isolation profiles in a diverse sample of young and middle-aged patients with cancer, (2) investigate the demographic, cultural, and clinical risk factors associated with each profile, and (3) provide a robust evidence base for developing targeted interventions sensitive to patients' varied experiences.

Methods

Study design and participants

This descriptive cross-sectional study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. Participants were recruited from the oncology wards of a tertiary general hospital in Sichuan, China, between December 2023 and April 2024. Inclusion criteria were patients: (1) aged 18–59 years, (2) who had a pathologically confirmed cancer diagnosis with a disease duration of at least 3 months, (3) were aware of their cancer diagnosis, (4) possessed normal literacy levels, and (5) voluntarily provided written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included those with a history of mental disorders or current cognitive impairment.

Sample size

Dual-consideration approach was used to determine the required sample size. According to the general principles of medical statistics, the minimum sample size should be 10 times the number of independent variables (18 variables ​× ​10 ​= ​180). Furthermore, contemporary literature on LPA suggested that a sample of at least 300 participants was often necessary to ensure model stability and reliable parameter estimation.24 After comprehensive consideration and consideration for potential non-response rates, 320 questionnaires were distributed to ensure sufficient statistical power. The final analysis comprised 300 valid responses.

Data collection

Data were collected during participants' hospitalization period (Monday to Sunday afternoon). Clinical characteristics, which included cancer diagnosis, staging, surgery history, and chemotherapy history, were obtained from the patient’s medical records and verified by the treating physicians. Current symptoms, such as fatigue and pain, were assessed through patient self-report via yes/no questions during the survey. After informed consent was obtained, trained research nurses distributed the questionnaires that included both demographic information and social isolation scales. Participants independently completed these questionnaires when they felt comfortable. After completion, participants were provided a hand cream as compensation.

Specific criteria were established for questionnaire validity to ensure data quality. Questionnaires were considered invalid if they: (1) had > 20% missing responses, (2) exhibited obvious response patterns that suggested non-thoughtful completion, (3) had incomplete demographic information, or (4) withdrew consent.

Measurements

Demographic, cultural, and clinical variables

The demographic questionnaire included sociodemographic (sex, age, education level, economic status, and work status) and clinical characteristics (diagnosis, staging, surgery history, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy history).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 and Mplus version 7.6. Scores from the social isolation questionnaires were standardized using Z-scores prior to analysis. LPA was conducted using Mplus to identify distinct subgroups of patients based on three dimensions of social isolation: social avoidance, social anxiety, and loneliness. Models with 1–4 latent profiles were sequentially fitted and compared.

Optimal number of profiles was determined based on a combination of statistical indices and theoretical considerations.29 We assessed the model fit using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), alongside Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT).30 Classification certainty was evaluated via Entropy. The three-profile solution was selected as optimal as it demonstrated: (1) a substantially improved fit over the two-profile model and diminishing rate of improvement for the four-profile model, (2) high classification certainty (Entropy ​= ​0.915), (3) non-significant LMRT (P ​= ​0.878), which indicated the four-profile model was not a significant improvement, and (4) superior theoretical interpretability, clinical relevance, and a balanced distribution of participants across profiles (52.3%, 27.0%, and 20.7%).

Following the LPA, participants were assigned to the profile for which they had the highest posterior probability. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize sample characteristics. Social isolation scores across the three profiles were compared via a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Fisher’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) post-hoc tests. Associations between demographic/clinical characteristics and profile membership were examined via chi-square tests and multinomial logistic regression. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the multinomial logistic regression, the “Balanced type” (Profile 3) was used as the reference category to examine predictors of profile membership. Supplementary Table S1 presents the detailed coding for all demographic and clinical variables.

We excluded 20 questionnaires that did not meet our validity criteria due to incomplete responses (n ​= ​10), obvious repeat answers (n ​= ​2), failure to meet the inclusion criteria (n ​= ​5), or information errors (n ​= ​3). Participants' mean age was 46.81 years (SD ​= ​9.56). Most were male (60.7%) and married (75.0%); the most common cancer diagnoses were liver (33.3%) and colorectal cancer (32.0%). Influencing factors of latent profile membership

We examined the distribution of demographic and clinical characteristics across the three latent profiles. Chi-squared tests revealed significant differences between the profiles based on age, marital status, cancer stage, income, education level, working conditions, fatigue, personality, and family relations, among other factors (all P ​< ​0.05). Table 1 presents the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of each profile.

A multinomial logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify key predictors of profile membership, with the balanced type as the reference category. Our analysis identified several key predictors of profile membership (Table 4). Compared with the balanced type, the avoidance-dominant type was significantly associated with an extroverted personality (odds ratios [OR] ​= ​0.174, P ​= ​0.001), harmonious family relationships (OR ​= ​5.673, P ​= ​0.003), and no fatigue (OR ​= ​3.195, P ​= ​0.030). Furthermore, the loneliness-dominant type was more likely to be associated with having an introverted personality (OR ​= ​0.100, P ​< ​0.001) and being unemployed or taking sick leave (OR ​= ​0.125, P ​= ​0.001). Discussion

To our knowledge, this was the first study to use LPA to examine social isolation heterogeneity among a diverse sample of young and middle-aged patients with cancer in Western China. LPA identified three distinct social isolation profiles. First, the Avoidance–Dominant profile (52.3%) demonstrated that high social avoidance could occur without high anxiety or loneliness, which challenged linear views of social isolation. Second, the Loneliness–Dominant profile (27.0%) indicated that subjective isolation existed despite regular social contact, which suggested that isolation was not always due to physical withdrawal. Third, the Balanced profile (20.7%), with moderate scores across all dimensions, represented a unique subgroup with a distinct risk pattern. These findings, which were undetectable by traditional variable-centered methods, suggested the need for tailored interventions based on how isolation manifests in each subgroup.

Profile characteristics

The Avoidance–Dominant profile (Profile 1) was characterized by high social avoidance but low levels of loneliness and social anxiety. This seemingly paradoxical pattern could be explained by several mechanisms. Our multinomial regression analysis identified several predictors of Profile 1. First, association with the absence of fatigue suggested these patients maintained physical capability for social interaction; however, they chose to limit it, possibly as a proactive coping strategy rather than being forced by physical limitations. Second, extroverts provided inherent sociability resources that buffered subjective isolation despite reduced social contact, which reduced their sense of social isolation.31 Regarding harmonious family relationships, empirical research suggested that cohesive Chinese families provided strong emotional support and acted as a buffer against external stressors, which potentially enabled selective social withdrawal without profound loneliness.32 Strong family support may provide sufficient emotional security to allow selective social withdrawal without increasing loneliness.

In addition, we observed a statistical association between the avoidance-dominant profile and religious beliefs. However, this finding must be interpreted with extreme caution and is likely a statistical artifact. This was due to the extremely small number of participants who reported religious beliefs in the “loneliness-dominant” and “balanced” profiles (four and three participants, respectively), which severely undermined the statistical power and robustness of this specific analysis. Therefore, we removed any substantive interpretations to avoid overstating an unreliable association. Future research should investigate this issue with a larger and more religiously diverse sample.

The loneliness-dominant type (Profile 2) exhibited an intriguing pattern of low social avoidance but high loneliness and anxiety. The association with unemployment/sick leave status helped explain this pattern; these patients may maintain social interactions out of necessity for practical support during treatment and financial dependencies, despite significant emotional distress. Their continued social engagement might be driven by practical needs rather than personal preference, which could potentially explain the coexistence of high anxiety and low avoidance. Furthermore, prevalence of introverted personalities in this group suggested that while these patients maintained social contact, their interactions did not effectively meet their emotional needs, which lead to persistent feelings of loneliness despite social engagement. A survey of patients with colorectal cancer found that patients with a type D personality were negative and prone to negative and suppressed emotions, which affected their social interaction ability.33 This emphasizes the need for medical staff to pay attention a patient’s personality characteristics when providing psychological interventions.

Furthermore, our results revealed that the balanced type (Profile 3) exhibited intermediate scores in all dimensions. In Profile 3, family harmony facilitated a further balanced approach to social engagement. This suggested that family support served different functions in different contexts, which either enabled selective withdrawal or promoted balanced engagement. Interviews with patients with cancer in China revealed that reduced communication and estrangement among family members lead to social isolation.34

Cultural interpretation

Our data revealed that family relationships were strongly linked to social isolation. In traditional Chinese culture, young and middle-aged individuals are central family members who receive significant emotional support. Rooted in Confucian collectivism, families serve as key sources of collective coping and emotional support, especially during a crisis, such as cancer.35,36 Chinese families often function as interdependent units, and members adapt roles and communicate to manage challenges. This explained why harmonious relationships reduced isolation in patients with the avoidance-dominant personality; cohesive families may absorb external social pressures, which may allow patients to strategically withdraw without perceived abandonment.37,38 In contrast, fragmented families may exacerbate loneliness by failing to provide such buffers. However, chronically closed environments can leave patients disconnected from society and unable to access new information, which can lead to limited career development possibilities and cognitive decline.39 Patients should be encouraged to interact with the outside world, provided with a low-stress environment, helped with rebuilding confidence, and encouraged to expand their social circles. Simultaneously, nurses should develop family-centered intervention strategies40 for patients with avoidance-dominant personality and engage caregivers in role-negotiation workshops to legitimize selective social withdrawal. Furthermore, family-mediated employment counseling should be facilitated for loneliness-dominant groups to restore socioeconomic roles and reduce isolation.

Young and middle-aged individuals have important family and social responsibilities.41,42 However, cancer treatment can disrupt their work and life and change family roles. Patients' declining participation in social activities can lead them being gradually pushed to the margins of society through the interaction of physical/mental, family, social environment, and other factors; thus, patients may become vulnerable and isolated. Research suggested that higher the family cohesion, the more the individual will feel the power of family affection when experiencing traumatic events; this can effectively improve their sense of security and belonging and help them feel less lonely.43 Even when external social interactions were limited, patients achieved emotional satisfaction. Patients who lacked family attention were more likely to experience helplessness and negative emotions. We proposed that high avoidance may represent strategic adaptation rather than distress-driven isolation in supportive family environments. However, the specific mechanisms of “buffering” and “strategic withdrawal” were interpretations based on cultural theory and literature; our study did not directly measure these dynamics. Therefore, medical staff should pay attention to patients' family support networks, especially the relationship between patients and their main caregivers, strengthen family cohesion, and reduce social isolation. Future studies should explore the factors related to differences in social isolation levels and guide individualized clinical interventions.

Implications for nursing practice and research

These findings have important implications for managing social isolation among young and middle-aged patients with cancer in China. Patients without fatigue exhibited lower social isolation levels, which suggested the potential benefits of integrating fatigue management into treatment protocols. A higher risk of social isolation was observed among those with long-term unemployed. This was as cancer treatment is costly and time-consuming for middle-aged and young patients, who are generally important family laborers. Economic burden on patients who are unemployed or on sick-leave is heavy; furthermore, they lack corresponding social security, which suggests that these patients may suffer from serious negative emotions, with a high degree of social isolation. Medical staff should pay attention to patients' working status, understand their social roles, and strengthen social security systems for middle-aged and young patients with cancer.

Additionally, the influence of personality traits and family relationships on social isolation highlights the importance of family involvement in patient care and development of individualized support strategies. Health care providers should assess patients' individual, clinical, and family circumstances to deliver targeted interventions that effectively improve their quality of life.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data were collected from a single center, which may have limited the generalizability of our findings. Further multicenter studies are required to validate these results in a broader population. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causality. Longitudinal research should track the trajectory of social isolation profiles over time and understand how they change based on treatment and life events. Third, this study’s broad age range (18–59 years) encompassed several distinct developmental stages. This heterogeneity limited our ability to conduct a formal sensitivity analysis that compared the unique challenges of young adults versus middle-aged patients. Social isolation patterns may differ significantly between age groups; hence, future research should investigate these age-related differences with larger stratified samples. Fourth, although our sample size was adequate for primary LPA, it was insufficient for some granular multinomial regression analyses. This was particularly evident when we examined predictors with low prevalence, such as religious beliefs, where the small number of participants in certain subgroups undermined the statistical power of the findings. Larger samples are required to reliably assess the influence of such factors.

Conclusions

These results confirmed the heterogeneity of social isolation in young and middle-aged patients with cancer, which was divided into three subgroups: “Avoidance-dominant,” “Loneliness-dominant,” and “Balanced.” This study also sheds light on important insights into the relationship between these latent profiles and various factors, such as employment status, fatigue levels, personality traits, and family dynamics. Oncology nurses and clinicians should pay attention to these risk factors and proactively identify patients who may be experiencing maladaptive forms of social isolation. These findings enhance understanding of social isolation profiles and provide a solid foundation for the early identification of at-risk groups and development of tailored person-centered intervention programs.
