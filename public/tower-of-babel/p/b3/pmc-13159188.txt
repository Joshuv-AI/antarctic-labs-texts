Comparative outcomes of retreatment vs follow-up in DTC patients with intermediate response following initial radioactive iodine therapy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jiaxin Miao, Wensi Cheng, Lin Ding, Hongliang Fu, Yafu Yin, Shuo Huang, Weiwei Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal management for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) patients with an indeterminate response (IDR) to initial radioactive iodine(RAI) therapy remains a clinical dilemma. This study aimed to compare outcomes between active surveillance and RAI retreatment in IDR patients and to identify predictors of persistent non-excellent response (NER).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 136 DTC patients classified as IDR 6 months after initial RAI therapy. Patients were categorized into a Retreatment Group (n = 18), who received additional RAI treatment, and a Follow-up Group (n = 118), managed with active surveillance. Treatment response was assessed during follow-up.ResultsThe rate of excellent response (ER) was significantly higher in the Follow-up Group compared to the Retreatment Group (58.47% vs. 33.33%, P = 0.046). In the Follow-up Group, multivariate analysis identified older age (≥ 45.50 years; OR, 1.07; P < 0.001), lateral cervical lymph node metastasis (N1b; OR, 2.92; P = 0.016), and higher stimulated thyroglobulin (sTg) level at first follow-up (≥ 2.025 ng/mL; OR, 1.31; P < 0.001) as independent predictors of NER. A nomogram incorporating these three factors demonstrated good predictive accuracy, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.752, which was confirmed by internal validation (AUC = 0.752).ConclusionFor DTC patients with an IDR, active surveillance was associated with a higher rate of ER compared to immediate RAI retreatment. A nomogram based on age, N1b status, and sTg level can effectively stratify the risk of NER, potentially guiding more individualized management decisions for this patient population.Keywords: Differentiated thyroid cancer, Radioactive iodine therapy, Indeterminate response

Introduction

Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) is increasingly diagnosed worldwide [1, 2]. Postoperative management commonly involves radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy following total thyroidectomy, which serves as a fundamental approach to ablate residual thyroid tissue and reduce recurrence risk [3, 4]. According to the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines, treatment response is categorized into four distinct categories: excellent response (ER), biochemical incomplete response (BIR), structural incomplete response (SIR), and indeterminate response (IDR). While management strategies for patients exhibiting ER and SIR are relatively well-defined, those classified under IDR present significant clinical challenges [3, 5].

An IDR is characterized by nonspecific elevation of thyroglobulin (Tg) levels, with unstimulated Tg (uTg) 0.2–1.2 ng/ml or stimulated Tg (sTg) 1–10 ng/ml, and stable or declining anti-Tg antibody (TgAb) levels without definitive structural evidence of disease [3]. This ambiguous status presents a common clinical dilemma: whether to pursue additional RAI treatment—potentially leading to overtreatment and unnecessary radiation exposure — or to continue active surveillance, which may delay intervention in those with occult persistent disease [6].

Studies have reported that approximately 15–20% of IDR patients may eventually develop structural recurrence [7, 8]. Retreatment of RAI is sometimes advocated in the hope of achieving ER [9]; however, empirical evidence supporting its efficacy in IDR patients is scarce. Additionally, there are no reliable predictors to identify which IDR patients would progress or whether they would benefit from RAI retreatment. Current guidelines provide limited specific recommendations for this population, and empirical RAI retreatment remains common in many practices despite insufficient evidence supporting its benefit.

In this retrospective cohort study, we aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of IDR patients who either underwent retreatment with RAI therapy or active surveillance. In addition, we sought to identify predictive factors associated with treatment response, with the goal of developing a practical risk-stratification tool that may guide individualized clinical decision-making for this unique and heterogeneous patient population.

Patients and methods

Patients and RAI treatment

This retrospective cohort study analyzed consecutive patients who underwent initial RAI therapy for DTC at our institution between January 2018 and March 2024. All patients underwent total thyroidectomy with/without central neck or lateral neck dissection and had histopathological confirmation of DTC. All patients received RAI treatment according to the risk class based on the TNM staging system (American Joint Committee on Cancer/International Union against Cancer) and as recommended by the 2015 ATA guidelines. RAI activities in the retreatment group were administered based on risk stratification according to 2015 ATA guidelines [3]. Patients with distant metastases at presentation, incomplete follow-up data, or positive TgAb status interfering with Tg measurement were excluded from the analysis. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital) waived the requirement for informed consent. The approval number is XHEC-D-2025-154.

Treatment response assessment and follow-up

All patients were evaluated for treatment response at approximately 6 months after initial RAI therapy (first follow-up) and regularly thereafter. For the first assessment, patients underwent thyroid hormone withdrawal for 3–4 weeks prior to the evaluation, including sTg, TgAb, neck ultrasound and the diagnostic whole-body scan (DxWBS). During subsequent follow-up, patients were advised to return every 6–12 months for evaluation with uTg, TgAb measurement and neck ultrasound. Follow-up duration was calculated from the date of the initial RAI therapy to the date of the last follow-up at our hospital. Patient response status (ER vs. NER) was determined based on the most recent available follow-up data.

Treatment response was defined according to 2015 ATA guidelines. IDR: uTg 0.2–1.2 ng/mL or sTg 1–10 ng/mL with negative TgAb level, and/or nonspecific findings on neck ultrasound or faint uptake in the thyroid bed on DxWBS, without structural evidence of disease. ER: uTg < 0.2 ng/ml or sTg < 1 ng/ml with negative TgAb level, and no structural or functional evidence of disease. NER (non-excellent response): Conversely, patients who did not meet the criteria for ER were classified as having a NER.

Management of IDR patients

For patients with IDR, there are currently no established guidelines recommending either routine retreatment or continued observation. In our cohort, the management decision was made collaboratively by physicians and patients after thorough discussion of the uncertainty surrounding optimal management, the potential risks and benefits of additional RAI treatment, and the patient’s individual clinical context. Patients identified as having IDR at the first follow-up were subsequently categorized into two groups based on the subsequent management strategy: the retreatment group (those who received additional RAI treatment immediately after being classified as IDR at the first follow-up assessment approximately 6 months after initial RAI therapy) and the follow-up group (those managed with active surveillance).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 29.0.2.0 for Windows (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range based on their distribution, which categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To identify the most relevant clinical predictors, variable selection for predicting NER in the follow-up cohort was performed using LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation. The optimal penalty parameter (λ) was determined based on the minimum standard validation. Significant variables identified by LASSO were subsequently incorporated into univariate and multivariate logistic regression models to identify independent predictive factors for NER. Results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

A prognostic nomogram was developed using the R package “rms” to provide an individual estimate of the probability of NER, based on the predictors identified by the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The model’s predictive performance was evaluated through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with internal validation conducted via bootstrap resampling with 1000 repetitions. Calibration was evaluated using Hosmer–Lemeshow test and calibration curves. For all analyses, a significant level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 136 DTC patients who were identified as IDR approximately 6 months after the initial RAI therapy was included in this study. Among these, 18 (13.24%) opted for additional RAI treatment (Retreatment Group), while the remaining 118 patients (86.76%) were managed with active surveillance (Follow-up Group).

Baseline demographic, clinical and pathological characteristics of both subgroups are summarized in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, gender distribution, histological subtype, TNM stage, pre-RAI therapy sTg and TSH levels, or initial administered RAI activity (all P values > 0.05) (Table 1).

Treatment outcomes: follow-up vs. retreatment group

In the Retreatment group, 6 (33.33%) patients achieved ER, 11 (61.11%) maintained an IDR, and 1 (5.56%) experienced BIR (Table 1). In the Follow-up group, 69 (58.47%) attained ER, 44 (37.29%) persisted with IDR, and 5 (4.24%) exhibited disease progression, displaying a significantly higher rate of ER in the Follow-up Group compared to the Retreatment Group (58.47% vs. 33.33%, P = 0.046).

STg levels at first follow-up were comparable between groups (Retreatment Group: 4.40 ± 2.15 ng/mL vs. Follow-up Group: 3.55 ± 2.39 ng/mL, P = 0.157). The absolute change in Tg levels (ΔTg) from pre-RAI therapy to first follow-up was also similar between the two groups (Retreatment Group: 6.53 ± 6.24 ng/mL vs. Follow-up Group: 6.78 ± 13.57 ng/mL, P = 0.940). Follow-up duration was significantly longer in the Follow-up Group compared to the Retreatment Group (38.02 ± 21.20 months vs. 22.88 ± 10.48 months, P < 0.001).

Subgroup analysis: ER vs. NER in each group

Within the Retreatment Group (n = 18), no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics or treatment parameters were found between patients who achieved ER (n = 6) and those with NER (n = 12) (Table 2). A non-significant trend was observed where patients with NER had higher sTg levels at the first follow-up compared to those who achieved ER (mean 5.02 ± 2.22 vs. 3.16 ± 1.45 ng/mL, P = 0.083).

Predictors of treatment response in the follow-up group

LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation identified six clinically relevant risk factors for NER in the follow-up group: advanced age, N1b, RAI activity, Tg level, TSH levels at first follow-up, and follow-up duration (Fig. 1). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that age (OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.02–1.11, P = 0.003), N1b stage (OR = 2.92, 95% CI: 1.22–6.98, P = 0.016), and sTg level at first follow-up (OR = 1.31, 95% CI:1.09–1.56, P = 0.003) (Table 4). ROC analysis identified the optimal cutoff values: sTg level at the first follow-up ≥ 2.025 ng/ml (AUC = 0.700, sensitivity 88%, specificity 51%, P < 0.001) and age ≥ 45.5 years (AUC = 0.655, sensitivity 51%, specificity 71%, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2).

Nomogram construction and validation

A prognostic nomogram was constructed based on the three independent predictors (age, N1b, and sTg level at first follow-up) to estimate the probability of NER in IDR patients managed with active surveillance (Fig. 3). The model demonstrated an AUC of 0.752 (95% CI: 0.667–0.838) (Fig. 3). Internal validation with 1000 bootstrap repetitions yielded a consistent AUC of 0.752 (95% CI: 0.667–0.838). Calibration was satisfactory, with a mean absolute error of 0.063 between predicted and observed probabilities (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The optimal management strategy for patients with DTC exhibiting an IDR to initial radioiodine therapy remains a significant clinical debate, balancing the risks of overtreatment against potential disease progression. Current guidelines acknowledge this uncertainty [5], describing a 15–20% risk of structural disease recurrence in patients with IDR and in other cases the indeterminate findings are either stable or tend to resolve [3, 10]. Therefore, our study had two primary aims: to compare clinical outcomes between active surveillance and additional RAI treatment in IDR patients, and to identify predictors of treatment response to facilitate risk stratification.

In this retrospective cohort study, IDRs were recorded in approximately 14% of the whole population, a similar probability to previous studies [11, 12]. We demonstrated that an active surveillance approach was associated with a significantly higher rate of ER compared to retreatment strategy. Furthermore, we identified key prognostic factors—age, lymph node metastasis (N1b), and sTg levels at the first follow-up—that can stratify the risk of NER among patients under surveillance and incorporated these into a clinically practical nomogram.

Our primary finding—that patients managed with active surveillance achieved a significantly higher rate of ER compared to those who underwent additional RAI therapy in IDR patients—challenges the notion that empirical retreatment is beneficial in this clinical scenario [13, 14]. This observation can be plausibly explained by the indolent nature of most DTC and the known potential for spontaneous resolution of nonspecific biochemical or imaging findings over time [14–16]. A significant proportion of IDR patients may harbor dormant disease or non-specific remnants that gradually resolve without further intervention, as supported by longitudinal studies documenting high rates of spontaneous conversion to ER [16]. In this context, retreatment may constitute overtreatment, exposing patients to unnecessary radiation and potential side effects without conferring additional clinical benefit. Our data align with the evolving paradigm in the 2025 ATA guidelines, which caution against routine empirical high-dose RAI therapy for patients with elevated Tg levels but negative imaging findings [5]. Therefore, an initial strategy of active surveillance appears safer and more effective for the majority of IDR patients, reserving intervention for those with clear signs of progression.

The second key contribution of this study is the development of a risk stratification tool for patients under active surveillance. We confirmed that the first post-treatment assessment (approximately 6 months) provides critical prognostic information. Specifically, an sTg level ≥ 2.025 ng/mL at the first follow-up emerged as a highly sensitive marker for predicting NER, offering a more actionable threshold for clinical decision. This aligns with the concept of dynamic risk stratification, where the response to initial therapy supersedes initial pathological staging in predicting long-term outcomes [17, 18]. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have proposed similar sTg thresholds for risk stratification in IDR populations [11], with minor variants likely attributable to differences in cohort characteristics and follow-up duration.

Furthermore, we identified advanced age (≥ 45.5 years) and the presence of lateral cervical lymph node metastasis (N1b) as independent predictors of NER. The prognostic role of age is well-established in DTC [19, 20], and its significance in our IDR cohort reinforces its value even within this intermediate-risk category. Similarly, the strong association of N1b status with persistent/progressive disease underscores the persistent impact of initial tumor burden, corroborating previous reports that extensive nodal involvement portends a less favorable response [21, 22]. The derived nomogram integrating these three factors demonstrated good discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.752) and calibration, providing a visual tool for clinicians to estimate individual patient risk and tailor the intensity of subsequent monitoring accordingly.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the non-randomized, retrospective design introduces the potential for selection bias and confounding by indication, despite balanced baseline characteristics. Second, the significantly longer follow-up duration in the surveillance group, while reflecting real-world practice, may have biased outcomes in favor of observing spontaneous ER. Third, the single center design and the sample size, particularly of the retreatment group, limit statistical power for subgroup analysis and generalizability. Consequently, the findings and trends in this study should be validated with larger and more balanced data. Finally, the nomogram requires external validation in independent, larger prospective cohorts before clinical application can be recommended.

Conclusions

In this cohort of DTC patients with an IDR to initial RAI therapy, additional RAI therapy did not yield superior treatment responses compared to active surveillance and instead exposed patients to the unnecessary risks associated with additional radiation. This supports a paradigm shift away from empirical retreatment for most IDR patients. For those under active surveillance, a nomogram incorporating age, N1b status, and first follow-up sTg level can effectively stratify the risk of a non-excellent response, facilitating personalized monitoring intensity. These findings advocate for a conservative, risk-adapted approach to managing IDR, pending validation from larger prospective studies.
