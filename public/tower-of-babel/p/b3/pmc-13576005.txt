Biphasic Amiodarone-Induced Thyroid Dysfunction: De Novo Hypothyroidism Followed by Delayed Type 2 Thyrotoxicosis: A Case Report
Authors: Nawras M Ibrahim, Sravani Karuchola, Samir Ouais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmiodarone disrupts thyroid function in 15% to 20% of patients through iodine overload and direct cytotoxicity, with amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT) classified as type 1 (iodine-driven excess synthesis in abnormal glands) or type 2 (destructive thyroiditis in normal glands). Type 2 AIT often manifests late or after drug discontinuation due to amiodarone’s prolonged tissue persistence (terminal half-life 26–107 days, mean approximately 53 days), and biphasic patterns where hypothyroidism precedes thyrotoxicosis are rarely reported, particularly de novo in previously euthyroid patients. This case involved a 66-year-old man with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and normal baseline thyroid function (thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH] 2.5 mIU/L, free T4 1.1 ng/dL) treated with amiodarone 200 mg daily for 22 months (cumulative dose approximately 13,200 mg), who developed hypothyroidism (TSH 6.34 mIU/L), leading to amiodarone discontinuation and levothyroxine initiation. Nine months postdiscontinuation, subclinical hyperthyroidism emerged (TSH 0.066 mIU/L), prompting levothyroxine cessation, and 11 months postdiscontinuation, overt thyrotoxicosis presented with palpitations, 6.4 kg weight loss, and atrial flutter (TSH 0.008 mIU/L, free T4 3.24 ng/dL, free T3 6.8 pg/mL). Negative thyroid autoantibodies and ultrasound showing diffuse thyromegaly with reduced parenchymal vascularity on color flow Doppler supported a type 2 AIT diagnosis, as this imaging feature is most specific for distinguishing subtypes. Thionamide therapy proved ineffective, but prednisone (40–50 mg daily, tapered over 4 months) restored euthyroidism within 4 months. This case demonstrated sequential dual toxicity in a typical thyroid, with type 2 AIT emerging nearly 1 year postdiscontinuation, highlighting the need for extended postdiscontinuation monitoring in select cases pending prospective data.

Introduction

Amiodarone, a class III antiarrhythmic agent containing 37% iodine by weight, affects thyroid function in 15% to 20% of patients via excess iodine delivery (Wolff-Chaikoff effect) and direct follicular cytotoxicity. 1,2 Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT) is categorized as type 1 (iodine-induced synthesis in predisposed glands with nodular disease or latent Graves' disease) or Type 2 (destructive thyroiditis in normal glands), although mixed forms occur, and the 2016 American Thyroid Association (ATA) Guidelines acknowledge that “the classic division of AIT into two subtypes likely represents an oversimplification.” 3

Type 1 AIT typically presents within months to 1 year of therapy (median 3.5 months), but type 2 AIT usually develops several years after initiation (median 30 months). 3,4 Type 2 AIT is characterized by absent or minimal hypervascularity on color flow Doppler sonography (CFDS), which is the most specific imaging feature for distinguishing subtypes, negative thyroid autoimmunity, and glucocorticoid responsiveness. 3,5,6

Postdiscontinuation AIT represents a clinically important phenomenon. The 2016 ATA Guidelines note that 19% of AIT cases develop postdiscontinuation (mean 5.5 months), with 95% being type 2 AIT. 3 This delayed presentation reflects amiodarone’s prolonged pharmacokinetics, with a terminal half-life of 26 to 107 days (mean approximately 53 days), as extensive tissue stores are depleted. 1 Current guidelines recommend monitoring thyroid function “at 3- to 6-month intervals” during therapy, but they provide no specific recommendations for postdiscontinuation surveillance duration. 3

Biphasic thyroid dysfunction (hypothyroidism preceding thyrotoxicosis) is infrequently reported, especially de novo in euthyroid patients. The authors have reported a case of sequential dual thyroid dysfunction with type 2 AIT emerging 11 months postdiscontinuation in a patient with documented normal baseline thyroid function, highlighting diagnostic complexity and surveillance implications.

Case Patient Presentation

A 66-year-old man with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease (post-stenting), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and obstructive sleep apnea had normal baseline thyroid function (thyroid-stimulating hormone [TSH] 2.5 mIU/L [reference 0.42–5.47], free T4 1.1 ng/dL [reference 0.8–1.8]). He initiated amiodarone 200 mg daily for rhythm control, continuing for 22 months (cumulative dose approximately 13,200 mg).

After 22 months of therapy, routine monitoring revealed hypothyroidism (TSH 6.34 mIU/L, free T4 0.8 ng/dL), attributable to the Wolff-Chaikoff effect from iodine overload. 1 Amiodarone was discontinued, and levothyroxine 50 mcg daily was initiated. He underwent successful atrial fibrillation ablation concurrently.

Nine months postdiscontinuation, thyroid function testing showed subclinical hyperthyroidism (TSH 0.066 mIU/L). Levothyroxine was discontinued due to palpitations and recurrent atrial fibrillation; no antithyroid medication or other thyroid-specific therapy was given during the 9- to 11-month interval. The patient was monitored expectantly during this period.

Eleven months postdiscontinuation, symptoms dramatically worsened over 2 weeks: palpitations (heart rate 140–150 bpm), chest pain radiating to shoulders, exertional dyspnea, heat intolerance, and 6.4 kg unintentional weight loss. Physical examination revealed atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response and palpable diffuse thyromegaly without nodules, exophthalmos, lid lag, and pretibial myxedema.

Laboratory testing confirmed overt thyrotoxicosis: TSH 0.008 mIU/L, free T4 3.24 ng/dL (reference 0.8–1.8), free T3 6.8 pg/mL (reference 2.3–4.2). Thyroid peroxidase antibody was 24 IU/mL (reference < 34), thyroglobulin antibody was < 1 IU/mL (reference < 9), and thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin 111% (reference < 140%), excluding Graves' disease or autoimmune thyroiditis.

Thyroid ultrasound revealed diffuse thyromegaly (right lobe 6.1 × 2.6 × 2.5 cm; left lobe 5.4 × 2.4 × 2.3 cm) with mildly heterogeneous parenchyma. CFDS demonstrated reduced parenchymal vascularity without the diffuse marked hypervascularity (“thyroid inferno” pattern) characteristic of type 1 AIT, and CFDS is the most specific imaging feature for distinguishing AIT subtypes. 3,5,6 Subcentimeter colloid cysts and benign-appearing cervical lymph nodes were noted; no suspicious nodules were identified, as shown in the Figure.

Cardiovascular evaluation showed atrial flutter, preserved ejection fraction (55%–60%), elevated troponin (type 2 non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction), and elevated NT-proBNP, linking thyrotoxicosis to cardiovascular decompensation.

Clinical Reasoning/Determining an Approach

Amiodarone causes thyrotoxicosis through 2 distinct mechanisms. Type 1 AIT results from iodine overload triggering excess thyroid hormone synthesis (Jod-Basedow effect) in glands with preexisting nodular disease or latent Graves' disease. The massive iodine load from amiodarone (each 200 mg tablet delivers approximately 75 mg of organic iodine, roughly 500 times the average daily requirement) overwhelms the normal autoregulatory Wolff-Chaikoff escape mechanism, providing excess substrate for unregulated hormone production. Type 2 AIT results from direct cytotoxicity. Amiodarone and its active metabolite desethylamiodarone accumulate within thyroid follicular cells and induce apoptosis and inflammatory destruction of the follicular epithelium. This leads to disruption of follicular integrity and leakage of large quantities of preformed thyroid hormones (T4 and T3) from colloid stores into the circulation. This process is analogous to the hormone release seen in subacute thyroiditis, but it is triggered by drug toxicity rather than viral infection.

The “thyroid inferno” is a CFDS pattern first described by Ralls et al (1988) in Graves' disease, characterized by multiple small areas of diffusely increased intrathyroidal blood flow visible throughout the gland in both systole and diastole. This creates a vivid mosaic of red, blue, and white color signals that resemble a “firestorm” within the parenchyma. This pattern reflects markedly increased glandular vascularity driven by active thyroid hormone synthesis and is characteristic of type 1 AIT and Graves' disease. Its absence (as in this patient, who demonstrated reduced parenchymal vascularity) is the hallmark of type 2 AIT, where the thyroid gland is not hyperactive but rather is undergoing destructive release of preformed hormone stores.

Type 2 AIT diagnosis was established based on convergence of clinical and imaging criteria: normal baseline thyroid function excluding type 1 predisposition, negative autoantibodies, postdiscontinuation presentation (95% of postdiscontinuation AIT is type 2), and reduced vascularity on CFDS. 3,5,6

Treatment Pathway

Treatment began with propylthiouracil (PTU) 100 mg every 8 hours for 2 weeks. This was ineffective, reflecting preformed hormone release characteristic of destructive thyroiditis rather than new hormone synthesis. 5 Methimazole 10 mg twice daily followed with minimal response. Both were discontinued; prednisone was started at 40 mg daily, increased to 50 mg for persistent symptoms, then tapered over 4 months. 3,5 Rate control was achieved with metoprolol, anticoagulation was continued, and amiodarone was permanently avoided.

Symptoms resolved within 2 months of prednisone initiation, and biochemical euthyroidism was achieved by 4 months (TSH 0.760 mIU/L, free T4 1.00 ng/dL), as shown in Table 2. Cardiovascular status stabilized.

Discussion

This case illustrated amiodarone’s sequential dual toxicity mechanisms in a typical thyroid: early Wolff-Chaikoff hypothyroidism from iodine excess, followed by delayed cytotoxic type 2 AIT from direct follicular damage. 1,3 Documented baseline euthyroidism confirms sequential thyroid dysfunction rather than unmasked latent disease.

Thyrotoxicosis presenting 11 months postdiscontinuation aligns with amiodarone’s prolonged pharmacokinetics. The 2016 ATA Guidelines note that 19% of AIT cases develop postdiscontinuation (mean 5.5 months), with 95% being type 2 AIT. 3 Amiodarone exhibits biphasic elimination with an initial 50% reduction in plasma concentration 2.5 to 10 days after cessation, followed by a terminal half-life of 26 to 107 days (mean approximately 53 days) as extensive tissue stores deplete. 1 This prolonged elimination explains delayed presentations nearly a year after discontinuation.

A recent population-based cohort study demonstrated that amiodarone in hypothyroid atrial fibrillation patients confers 5-fold increased thyrotoxicosis risk (adjusted hazard ratio, 5.18; 95% confidence interval, 3.48–7.69), although whether amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism specifically confers vulnerability requires further investigation. 7 Although this association does not establish causation, and the study included patients with preexisting hypothyroidism (not specifically amiodarone-induced), this case provided clinical support for heightened vigilance in patients who develop amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism.

Color flow Doppler demonstrated reduced parenchymal vascularity without the “thyroid inferno” pattern characteristic of type 1 AIT; as previously noted, CFDS is the most specific imaging test for distinguishing AIT subtypes. 3,5,6 However, the 2016 ATA Guidelines acknowledge that CFDS has substantial limitations. Among patients classified as type 2 based on absent vascularity, only 58% responded to corticosteroids, and 33% of patients in one series could not be subtyped based on CFDS findings. 3 The Guidelines further note that “the classic division of AIT into two subtypes likely represents an oversimplification” and that “many patients cannot be readily classified into one of the two AIT subtypes.” 3

In this case, definitive thionamide failure and glucocorticoid responsiveness provided the strongest evidence for type 2 AIT classification, as treatment response serves as the ultimate diagnostic determiner when imaging findings are uncertain, as shown in Table 1. 3,5,8

Subclinical hyperthyroidism at 9 months progressing to overt thyrotoxicosis reveals gradual destructive evolution. This progression underscores the importance of frequent testing during high-risk intervals postdiscontinuation. Current guidelines recommend monitoring thyroid function every 3–6 months during therapy but do not specify postdiscontinuation duration. 3,9 Recent epidemiologic data support the need for extended surveillance. A 2025 Icelandic nationwide cohort study of 262 euthyroid patients initiating amiodarone found a 38.5% cumulative 5-year incidence of thyroid dysfunction (95% confidence interval, 30.4–45.7), with the highest yearly incidence of AIT (9.8%) occurring during the third treatment year. 2 This high cumulative incidence, combined with the 95% prevalence of type 2 AIT in postdiscontinuation cases, underscores the importance of cautious monitoring extending well beyond drug cessation. 3 This case raised the hypothesis that extended postdiscontinuation surveillance may be warranted in selected patients with prior amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism, although the optimal duration and frequency require prospective validation.

The rapid response to glucocorticoids (symptom resolution within 2 months, biochemical euthyroidism by 4 months) confirmed type 2 AIT and is consistent with the literature showing that type 2 AIT typically responds to prednisone 40 mg daily, with improvement occasionally seen as early as in 1 week. 3,5 The failure of both PTU and methimazole further supported the diagnosis, as thionamides are ineffective in destructive thyroiditis where preformed hormone is released rather than newly synthesized. 5,8 A matched retrospective cohort study demonstrated that glucocorticoids are superior to thionamides as first-line treatment for type 2 AIT, with 76.2% of patients achieving euthyroidism within 40 days on glucocorticoids vs only 14.3% on methimazole. 8

The development of atrial flutter with rapid ventricular response and type 2 non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction highlighted the serious cardiovascular consequences of AIT. Amiodarone-associated thyrotoxicosis is associated with more than doubling of adverse cardiac events and worse outcomes than in Graves’ or toxic nodular hyperthyroidism, especially in patients with left ventricular dysfunction. 6 This patient’s underlying coronary artery disease and atrial fibrillation made thyroid hormone excess particularly deleterious. Recent real-world data demonstrate that appropriate first-line medical therapy for AIT substantially reduces cardiovascular events (4.5% vs 33.2%) and hospitalizations (6.5% vs 24.9%) compared to inappropriate therapy. 10

Limitations included absence of radioiodine uptake measurement, which was not performed due to prior initiation of PTU, as antithyroid drugs can artificially suppress uptake and confound interpretation 11 (although it is expected to be low/suppressed in type 2 AIT regardless). Limitations also included lack of serial interleukin-6 measurements. Still, the 2016 ATA Guidelines note that interleukin-6 levels “overlap extensively between the two subtypes and are therefore also not useful” for definitive differentiation. 3

Conclusions

Amiodarone can induce sequential dual thyroid dysfunction in typical thyroids: hypothyroidism during therapy followed by type 2 AIT nearly a year postdiscontinuation. This case raised the hypothesis that patients with amiodarone-induced hypothyroidism may warrant extended postdiscontinuation surveillance, although prospective studies are needed to establish evidence-based protocols. Treatment response remains the ultimate diagnostic arbiter when distinguishing type 1 from type 2 AIT, with thionamide failure and glucocorticoid responsiveness confirming type 2 pathophysiology.

Patient Perspective

The patient noted marked quality-of-life improvement following resolution of thyrotoxicosis; palpitations ceased, energy returned, and weight stabilized. He appreciated detailed explanations of amiodarone’s biphasic thyroid effects and consented to publication to help educate clinicians about this uncommon presentation.
