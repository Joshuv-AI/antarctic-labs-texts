The Role of Child Maltreatment and Revictimization on Substance Use Trajectories Among Women Involved in the Criminal Justice System
Authors: Abenaa A Jones, Sienna Strong-Jones, Rachael E Bishop, Kristina Brant, Jacquelyn Campbell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explored the mechanisms underlying the relationship between trauma in childhood and adulthood and substance use initiation and recovery from opioid use disorder (OUD). We triangulated the perspectives of 20 women with current/previous OUD and 22 criminal legal and drug treatment professionals. Themes centered around the long-term effects of unaddressed childhood trauma, family- and partner-based trauma across the life course, sexual trauma, and traumatic events due to opioid use. Findings highlighted the unique trauma-related needs of women involved in the criminal legal system who also use drugs. Approaches to care that are gender-specific, person-centered, and trauma-informed would facilitate retention in drug treatment programs.Keywords: Child maltreatment, criminal justice system, criminal legal system, substance use, trauma, women

INTRODUCTION

A significant majority of women who are incarcerated (ranging from 50 to 80%) have experienced victimization, such as physical abuse, sexual abuse, and emotional abuse (Jones et al., 2021; Karlsson & Zielinski, 2020; Saxena & Messina, 2021), far surpassing the prevalence observed among non-incarcerated women (Radatz & Wright, 2017) and incarcerated men (Messina et al., 2007). The role of trauma in the development of substance use disorders (SUDs) highlights the need for effective interventions to improve the outcomes of survivors.

Child Maltreatment, Justice Involvement, and Substance Use

One significant trauma an individual can face is child maltreatment, defined as a caregiver’s act or failure to act that results in serious physical, emotional, and sexual harm or exploitation to children (Font & Maguire-Jack, 2020). Within the United States (U.S.), females experience child maltreatment at a slightly higher rate than males. According to the 2023 Child Maltreatment report, girls experience victimization at a rate of 7.9 per 1,000 in the population, and boys experience victimization at a rate of 6.9 per 1,000 in the population (U.S. Department of Health & Human Services, 2025). Child maltreatment has been associated with poor outcomes across the lifespan, such as involvement in the criminal legal system (Boppre & Boyer, 2021). Additionally, females who have experienced revictimization—sexual, emotional, and physical harm in adulthood (Fereidooni et al., 2024)—face an elevated risk of involvement in both the juvenile legal and criminal legal systems (Brav, 2021; Widom et al., 2018).

In addition to criminal legal involvement, women who experienced child maltreatment and revictimization in adulthood also have elevated odds of developing SUDs (Cicchetti & Handley, 2019; Guastaferro et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2021). Recent studies (Austin et al., 2018; Merrick et al., 2020; Santo et al., 2021) have found that child maltreatment is common among individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD), ranging from 16% to 57.4% and spanning various forms of maltreatment. Child sexual abuse (CSA), in particular, is frequently reported among individuals with OUD, with 41% of women with OUD disclosing a history of CSA compared to 16% of men (Santo et al., 2021).

Child Maltreatment Among Girls and Adulthood Revictimization Among Women

According to a recent meta-analysis, almost 50% of individuals who experienced CSA are susceptible to revictimization (Walker et al., 2019). Although the majority of current revictimization research has focused on sexual revictimization, a substantial proportion (from 24 to 90%) of individuals who experienced multiple types of childhood maltreatment may also encounter revictimization during their adult years (Walker & Wamser-Nanney, 2023).

The relationship between child maltreatment and sexual and physical revictimization is also stronger among women than men (Karsberg et al., 2019). Furthermore, women who are victimized in both childhood and adulthood are exposed to an elevated risk of adverse outcomes, surpassing those who experienced victimization only in childhood or adulthood (Classen et al., 2005; Kimerling et al., 2007), suggesting a chronic trauma effect.

Revictimization and Life Chaos Among Women and Substance Use Outcomes

Child maltreatment and adulthood victimization may increase vulnerability to conditions including depression, anxiety, emotional dysregulation, PTSD, and SUD (Löfberg et al., 2023; Fereidooni et al. 2024; Young & Widom, 2014). The use of substances as a coping mechanism can be understood through two pathways. In the direct pathway, substances are used to selfmedicate traumatic emotions; in the indirect pathway, substances are used as a means of distancing oneself from these emotions and their related mental and bodily states (Schimmenti et al., 2022). Yet, such substance misuse may hinder self-protective behaviors, leave individuals vulnerable due to intoxication, and heighten the probability of entering risky or perilous situations (Walker & Wamser-Nanney, 2023).

Previous studies have also indicated connections between child maltreatment and intimate partner violence (IPV) in women with OUD, as experiencing child maltreatment may normalize abusive behaviors (Engstrom et al., 2008). Consequently, many women involved in the criminal legal system facing drug possession charges or charges motivated by an underlying SUD (e.g., survival sex work) have faced both child maltreatment and adult victimization (Jones et al., 2018; Jones, Dyer, et al., 2019; Jones, Gerke, et al., 2019).

Current Study

As extant research and theories posit, child maltreatment has long-term adverse effects, and women are disproportionately impacted. While child maltreatment and subsequent revictimization are linked with substance use and criminal legal involvement, our understanding of the mechanisms underlying these correlations is limited (Schimmenti et al., 2022; Widom et al., 2018). Perspectives from criminal-legal involved women with both current and past SUDs may provide insights into the mutually reinforcing issues of substance use and child and adult trauma and adversity. The current study draws on these perspectives to explore the mechanisms between childhood trauma, adulthood trauma, and substance use initiation, treatment, and recovery. This study also incorporates the perspectives of criminal legal and substance use treatment professionals, triangulating perspectives to understand these issues from multiple stakeholders. This research contributes to our understanding of the link between child maltreatment, revictimization in adulthood, and substance use outcomes among criminal-legal involved women and may inform the development of effective interventions to support and sustain recovery.

METHODS

Study Overview

This study draws on in-depth interviews (n = 42) conducted in 2022 with women with current or previous OUD (n = 20), SUD treatment professionals (n = 12), and criminal legal professionals (n = 10) who have experience working with women who use opioids (Jones et al., 2024). The sample sizes of 10–12 participants are recommended for saturation, where enough data are generated to draw conclusions and new data do not add additional insights (Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). The larger study, approved by the Penn State University Institutional Review Board, aimed to assess factors associated with successful OUD treatment, particularly the use of medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), among criminal-legal involved women with OUD, as criminal-legal involved individuals currently have the lowest referrals to MOUD treatment (Krawczyk et al., 2017). Participants were asked about their backgrounds, their experience using drugs or working with women who use drugs, perceived facilitators and barriers to OUD treatment (particularly treatment involving MOUD), and gender-specific issues faced in OUD treatment. The issues of child maltreatment and reoccurring trauma arose frequently throughout the interviews.

Eligibility Criteria and Recruitment

The study utilized informational flyers sent to MOUD treatment programs and women’s prisons, direct calls to women’s prisons, chain referrals, and online advertisements to recruit participants. Eligibility criteria varied for the different groups of participants. For women who currently use or previously used opioids illicitly, eligibility criteria included being 18years or older, having a life-time history of taking MOUD, having a history of criminal legal involvement, and living in Pennsylvania.

SUD treatment professionals were eligible to participate if they practiced in Pennsylvania and either (1) prescribed methadone, naltrexone, and/or buprenorphine, (2) provided complementary behavioral modification counseling as part of an MOUD treatment program, or (3) provided other care for women being treated with MOUD. The final sample included SUD treatment directors (n = 2), a MOUD provider, nurses (n = 2), counselors (n = 3), case managers/recovery coaches (n = 3), and a research assistant who supervised case managers in a drug treatment program.

Criminal legal professionals were eligible if they worked in corrections, sentencing, treatment court, or law enforcement in Pennsylvania with criminal-legal involved women who use substances. The final sample included treatment court professionals (n = 4), law enforcement (n = 3), state prosecutors (n = 2), and a corrections employee.

Data Collection and Analysis

Trained study staff conducted semi-structured interviews with eligible participants that lasted around 1 hour. All participants provided informed consent and were told they could forego any questions they did not want to answer. Interviews began when verbal consent was given. All participants received a $50 gift card for their participation.

Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and de-identified for confidentiality. The interview used open-ended questions, and the data analysis process was conducted inductively so that participants’ responses guided coding. The research team used NVivo 14 software to conduct a thematic analysis. First, three researchers developed an initial set of primary codes through a detailed review of the transcripts. All participants were asked similar questions to gauge similarities and differences in perceptions between the women and professionals, and all interviews were analyzed using the same codes. Second, following the establishment of these primary themes, more detailed secondary themes were developed through an inductive approach by reviewing the transcripts. This process contributed to the categorization of core elements of the primary themes. The inductive coding process ensured that the study had achieved thematic saturation. One of three researchers on the study team, all who were not involved in the initial code generation, independently coded each group of transcripts (e.g. affected women, criminal legal professionals, SUD treatment professionals), which were all double-checked by the P.I. for consistency (Apsley et al., 2024; Skogseth et al., 2024; Strong-Jones et al., 2024). This paper draws on the primary code “The Intersection of Trauma and Opioid Use.” The research team thematically characterized the experiences shared by the women by analyzing the text within this code. Subsequently, the coded text from the professionals’ interviews was reviewed to find support for the women’s experiences, identify any discrepancies (absent), and explore systemic factors influencing the women’s experiences.

RESULTS

Findings Overview

The average age of women with past or current OUDs was 37, and 70% identified as non-Hispanic white. Most SUD treatment professionals identified as female (80%) and non-Hispanic white (70%), with an average age of 48. Their total years of experience ranged from 4 to 25 years with an average of 12.25 years. Six of the SUD treatment professionals disclosed that they were in recovery themselves, and of the four who elaborated further, the length of recovery time ranged from 6 to 20 years. Similar to the SUD treatment professionals, 70% of the criminal legal professionals identified as female, and 90% identified as non-Hispanic white, with a mean age of 44. The average of their total years of experience was 16 years, with answers ranging from 6 to 36 years.

Results from thematic analysis revealed four main trauma-related themes that impacted women’s experiences with OUD: child maltreatment, adulthood victimization, traumatic events due to opioid use, and the need for trauma-informed care. We want to provide a content warning as some of the participants’ quotations may be graphic yet impactful.

Child Maltreatment

Participants frequently identified child maltreatment as a contributor to OUD in adulthood. While child maltreatment occurs during early developmental periods (i.e., before age 18), its effects carried over into adulthood for most of our sample. Participants recounted the lasting role of repressed trauma from their maltreatment experiences on opioid use initiation and recovery later in life.

Child Maltreatment and Opioid Use Disorder

The childhood experiences recounted by participants with OUD included various forms of child maltreatment perpetrated by caretaker(s). These potentially traumatic experiences (PTEs), defined as adverse events that pose a risk to an individual’s physical and mental health (Sloover et al., 2024), can be categorized along two dimensions: direct/indirect and chronic/acute. Direct forms of maltreatment occurred when a PTE happened directly to the participant, whereas indirect forms of maltreatment occurred when participants witnessed a PTE. Maltreatment also differed in acuity, with some experiences being chronic and others consisting of a single, acutely formative event.

Most often, participants reported experiencing chronic child maltreatment. These participants described how their homes’ “wild and chaotic” circumstances contributed to ongoing trauma throughout years of a “bad upbringing.” The lingering effects could be either direct or indirect. Indirect effects often stemmed from child neglect, which was sometimes, but not always, due to parental substance use. When asked could have prevented her opioid misuse and justice involvement, one woman with a history of OUD (Participant 37) told us:

I would change my childhood. I would change my history, my background where I came from. I feel if I didn’t come from a bunch of drunk, fucked up bikers that I wouldn’t be the misfit that I am … It just surrounds my entire life from when I was a kid onward.

The participant highlights the role of chronic neglect from her caregivers, which she believed if addressed early when she was a child, would have made a difference.

Similarly, another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 33) shared that she was without parental guidance before she became an adult, and the stress and environments she was subject to fueled her drug use:

Dropping out at 15 and being pregnant at that time, I didn’t realize how hard it was to find work. It was just delivering newspapers and selling drugs and renting out rooms was all I could do till about 18.

This participant had to navigate parenthood as a child herself without proper guidance or protection. Other participants experienced different forms of chronic maltreatment with direct effects, including emotional and sexual abuse. For example, another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 34) described being kicked out of one abusive home and placed in another:

She [her mother] chose him [an abusive boyfriend] and kicked me out … I went to my uncle’s house … He would always have “family time”. Now he never really touched me down there … But he would grab my boobs and tell me to turn around and look at my ass. He got the doors off of the things [hinges] so I couldn’t close the door … I couldn’t dress without him being able to look. When I finally spoke up about it, and there was an investigation, I got in trouble … I just stopped talking about everything after that.

Participants also recounted experiencing acute PTEs centered around significant events, such as witnessing a gruesome accident (indirect) or being sexually assaulted (direct). One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 30) recounted the impact of witnessing her father get severely injured in a motor vehicle accident:

… he was coming down the street on his way home, and he was really drunk and he crashed the motorcycle into a car that was in front of me, and then he flopped in the air and down on the road and he just laid there … I just remember going out to the middle of the road. He was laying there, and I was like, “Daddy! Daddy!” Yelling for him to get up. He was just motionless. He did survive it, but he had brain damage as well as some physical damage … Life was never the same … I was so young, it was just hard for me to understand and accept and be okay.

This participant noted that her teacher shared with her mother concerns of the trauma she endured watching the accident. However, the participant’s mother did not seek out any counseling or related services for her, and the trauma she endured from the accident remained unresolved.

Although these PTEs occurred early in life, their effects were far-reaching, influencing the opioid use initiation and recovery trajectories of the women in our sample in adulthood. Participants across subgroups all spoke to the lasting effects of unprocessed child maltreatment on OUD in adulthood. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 32) described the mechanism by which acute and chronic maltreatment can lead to substance use initiation:

What I find to be consistent with early drug use and heavy drug use is those kids are growing up in broken homes where their parents are convicted criminals or drug addicts themselves, and they’re exposed to that drug use and that erratic behavior. If you grow up with two parents that are addicts, it puts on a lot of stress throughout your life as a young kid. That is traumatic stress because it’s repeated and nonstop.

Participants also spoke to the barriers that intrusive traumatic memories created in their recovery journeys. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 31) shared:

I was abused as a child, and that’s still affecting me every day … I’m sitting here on house arrest by myself with all these repressed memories from my childhood … that are just coming up in my head while I’m alone in my bedroom … I don’t feel safe. I don’t feel good. I’m scared.

This woman described how her past child maltreatment impacted her psychologically, creating an additional challenge to navigate on top of her opioid use issues and justice system involvement.

Several SUD treatment professionals and criminal legal professionals echoed these sentiments. One SUD treatment professional (Participant 5), for example, pointed to the necessity of processing child maltreatment to see successful MOUD treatment outcomes:

[I]f we don’t get to the root of the issue, we’re not going to help with any of it. Most of them have suffered. I’d say 98% of the people that I deal with have suffered some sort of adverse childhood trauma.

A criminal justice professional (Participant 11), who also had a history of opioid use similarly shared what she saw as a root cause of substance use issues among the women she worked with:

Sexual trauma, whether that be rape or molestation, when a client was younger that they’ve never really processed and they’ve kind of held it in … I think from a professional and a lived experience perspective, the direct result of trauma and not processing trauma is substance use.

As these perspectives demonstrate, the importance of successfully treating the effects of child maltreatment emerged as a key factor in promoting successful MOUD treatment.

Lifetime Exposure to Potentially Traumatic Events

Participants’ narratives also pointed to the impact of lifetime exposure to PTEs on their OUD trajectories. Whereas child maltreatment is rooted in a specific time frame, lifetime exposure to PTEs concerns the accumulation of events across the lifespan. The women in the study experienced PTEs across the lifespan within three domains: family-based trauma, partner-based trauma, and sexual trauma.

Family-Based Trauma

PTEs within the family included health challenges and deaths. Several participants recounted the traumatic loss of their children, parents, and/or grandparents and connected these losses to substance use initiation or return to use. For example, one woman with a history of OUD (Participant 23) described the profound trauma of losing her young daughter to shaken baby syndrome and detailed how this experience led her to use opioids to cope. Another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 26) detailed the daily struggle of maintaining recovery when coping with loss:

I was having a really bad day because, by then, I had already lost my dad. He had passed away, and I was just not myself. I was struggling every single day to just maintain being clean …. I just wish they [SUD treatment staff] would just look at our story and try to understand that it’s not easy for some people, and sometimes people do turn to use drugs or something because they just desperately need to forget.

Compounded traumatic family events exerted a lifetime toll on participants and often led to opioid use to cope with the resulting pain and sadness.

Partner-Based Trauma

The women in our study also described victimization stemming from romantic partners in adulthood. These experiences included domestic violence, infidelity, and abandonment. Participants shared the despair and hopelessness they faced when stuck in abusive relationships. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 26) shared, “I was physically abused by my ex and … I honestly thought I was going to die. I didn’t want to live anymore because I was beaten so bad.”

But it was not just current partners that inflicted these harms; past partners also often created cycles of victimization that the women could not escape. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 31) recounted the trauma she endured at the hands of her ex-partner and explained how it inhibited her recovery:

My ex strangled me in front of my son and almost killed me, and he got [pretrial diversion] for it. He’s already violated it twice … If he completes it up until July, he gets this wiped off his record. I couldn’t stay clean because he was breaking the PFA [protection from abuse order] and using me and messing with my head.

Similarly, another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 40) detailed how intimate partner violence impacted her substance use:

I met a guy who I thought was great. I ended up getting pregnant … and he became very abusive. Tried to get out of the relationship. When I did that, he beat me up really bad and he took the baby, and I started using really heavy after that, really heavy … I was in another very abusive relationship, but it didn’t really matter because I was just so bad on the pills that nothing really fazed me. That went on for years.

This woman had started using drugs at the age of 11 with her mother and other family members and had also engaged in sex work at a young age. She told us that her abusive relationships in adulthood intensified her use. Like family-based trauma, partner-based trauma similarly contributed to participants using drugs for the first time or returning to drug use while pursuing recovery.

Sexual Trauma

Finally, sexual abuse and assault contributed to women’s lifetime exposure to trauma. When describing the victimization that led their female clients to substance use, one SUD treatment professional (Participant 9) shared, “A vast majority of them have been in some way sexually assaulted, raped, beaten. I’ve heard tremendous horror stories.” A woman with a history of OUD (Participant 33) told us how her sexual trauma directly led to an increase in her substance use, as she tried smoking crack for the first time to induce a miscarriage:

After being raped and finding out that I was pregnant, I thought that I could smoke crack and that … that would just give me a miscarriage. I spent my first income tax check on getting $1,000 worth of crack and tried to smoke it all in one night.

Sexual trauma also shaped treatment and recovery outcomes through others’ reactions to disclosing that trauma. In addition to enduring instance(s) of sexual trauma and coping with those unprocessed and repressed memories, recounting the event(s) can be retraumatizing, especially when met with invalidation and blame. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 34) explained how painful it was to be blamed by her mother when she disclosed a past sexual assault:

When I first told her that I was raped, she didn’t believe me. She told me that I was going to go to Hell because God does not look at sex before marriage very good. That made me feel so impure and so disgusting that I didn’t want to do anything. I felt like I was already trash at that point.

Some women also described being retraumatized by the systems with which they interact. As one woman with a history of OUD (Participant 31) disclosed:

There’s a lot of victim shaming around here [in the criminal justice system]. It’s like—my son’s father was physically abusive. My ex was physically abusive. I got molested as a child, but basically, I feel like now people think it’s just me, like I’m the problem. That’s the feeling I get.

It was not only the traumas themselves that could motivate substance use; others’ reactions could amplify the negative impacts of traumas and further motivate them.

SUD treatment professionals described how sexual trauma could impact women’s willingness to seek help. One SUD treatment professional (Participant 8) shared that sexual trauma contributes to deeply engrained beliefs where women “have low self-esteem, so they feel like they’re not loved, so they feel like they got to settle for second best, or no best.” Another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 21) who now works in the recovery field shared:

A lot of women have gone through traumatic pregnancies, abortions, rapes, a lot of sexual abuse … In my experience doing assessments with women specifically, I have to ask them all those questions, and it’s sometimes answered so nonchalantly, like, “Yes, I was a prostitute. I sold my body for drugs. I was beaten, I was raped … It comes so much with the lifestyle that it’s just so nonchalant now, but nobody’s really dealing with the trauma and all the stuff that comes along with that.

As this professional expressed, some women experience numbness or detachment from their pain, which can make uncovering these traumas and addressing them even harder. She further argued that, despite how common these adverse sexual experiences are, treatment programs often are not well-equipped to handle them. Yet, when women are able to receive the necessary recovery services, they can recover their ability to feel their emotions. This was illustrated by one woman with a history of OUD (Participant 24):

I felt great after I got sober, felt great. I hadn’t felt like that for, I didn’t know what that felt like, like the feelings, it was just like– I had trained myself not to cry to show emotion from using, so it was nice to actually be able to cry so much.

As the woman described, she learned to suppress her emotions to cope with the realities of addiction, and recovering her emotions, even those related to sadness, was a positive experience.

Traumatic Events Due to Opioid Use

As described above, substance use itself—or the state’s responses to substance use—can precipitate trauma. Therefore, many women entered cycles of reoccurring trauma and substance use. One SUD treatment professional (Participant 9) acknowledged that, while traumas are certainly present before women begin using substances, they can occur even more frequently during active addiction:

[T]he majority of the trauma happens after they start using drugs … Especially in the time of their active addiction, they’re very accepting of it. Almost to the point where it becomes second nature where they think that they’re not worthy of anything else.

Elsewhere, we have illustrated the trauma women face relating to the loss of child custody (Apsley et al., 2024), criminal legal involvement (particularly having withdrawal symptoms while incarcerated) (Strong-Jones et al., 2024), and sexual exploitation stemming from substance use (Jones et al., 2025). Here, we further demonstrate that OUD itself can propagate traumatic experiences, even when not incarcerated.

Past Opioid Use and Withdrawal as Trauma

Women with lived experience reported events they experienced during recovery that reminded them of their active periods of substance use. These events could trigger negative feelings associated with the periods of their use. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 21) discussed a traumatizing ordeal of having to get blood drawn, as it reminded her of the experience of injecting drugs and the physical injuries she had sustained from that practice:

“I was an IV drug user, and I had to get blood work while I was in inpatient … I was unaware of that when I went into treatment. I think I maybe had nine days sober at the time. My arms and hands were still very scabbed just due to me being an IV user. The thought of having to have a needle in my arm or hand sounded terrible to me … The nurse drawing blood was new, wasn’t that great at it. I remember having quite the meltdown … I basically refused because I said it would be incredibly triggering to me.

The distress of this ordeal led to the participant threatening the nurse, setting her treatment experience off on a negative note.

While opioid use may temporarily relieve physical and psychological distress, dependence on opioids also often leads to an intense fear of experiencing withdrawal symptoms. Withdrawals themselves were often described by participants as traumatic, and fear of experiencing withdrawal could hinder willingness to seek MOUD treatment. The woman above (Participant 21) recalled:

At 33-years-old, I was broken, I destroyed my life, and I was terrified. I was terrified of being sick and how bad it was, and me being in an uncomfortable environment. It was really just the fear and anxiety of being sick and not being somewhere where I felt comfortable.

Similarly, when asked about barriers to seeking treatment, another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 27) stated, “The first thing was the sickness. An addict will do anything damn near to not be sick. Every time it gets worse and worse so yes.” This participant decided to try MOUDs to aid in her recovery. Unfortunately, she experienced precipitated withdrawal and a severe allergic reaction, and the experience of this sickness led her to return to use. She recalled, “It was like dopesick [but] a thousand times worse. It was terrible. So of course, I went out and used as soon as I left the hospital.”

While fears of withdrawal and the reality of withdrawal can prohibit individuals from seeking treatment, desperation to avoid withdrawal can also lead people into situations they deeply regret. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 26) recalled:

The worst [crime I ever committed] was when my ex-husband … forged his dad’s name, and we had took money out of his dad’s bank account to get high … so we didn’t get sick. To go to work and everything else, we needed to be functioning.

Another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 33) described stealing her ailing mother’s pain medications to avoid severe withdrawal symptoms:

Oh, God, it was terrible feeling sick and hot and cold and nauseous, no type of appetite, very irritable … It got to the point almost like a game to me where my mom was hiding the pills and I would find them. She wouldn’t want to send me to jail or anything, but, oh, my God, she yelled at me. She tried hitting me, beating me with her cane, throwing stuff at me … I really, really feel a lot worse about the situation with her passing away than at the time.

In these cases, the events that spiraled from attempting to avoid withdrawal were experienced as traumatic. These events plagued the women who experienced them as they attempted to work toward recovery.

Distrust in Systems to Protect and Serve

The vulnerability that women experience while using opioids and fearing withdrawal can also lead them to exploitation, including by the state. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 30) relayed:

One of the times I was arrested … the cops … convinced me to be this informant, and to help them bust a couple people … When you’re really, really sick and in withdrawals, and they say, “You’re either going to go right to jail right now tonight, or we’ll let you go,” in my mind, I was like I probably have 10 different drug dealers … probably about 8 of them has ripped me off before. I was like, how guilty would I feel giving them one of those dudes?

The participant told us that, in hindsight, she would have said no; it was against her morals. However, she agreed to the task because of her intense physical need to avoid withdrawal. After agreeing once, she was then asked for further help from the police, in exchange for protection:

“Every time you run up here and you buy some bags,” he goes, “I want you to come by my office here. I want you to just give me one of those bags … ” Now, he didn’t tell me why he wanted me to do that … It smelled like a setup to me.

The above situations broke the participant’s sense of trust in the state. She told us:

When you’re a little girl, and you think of doctors and cops and police and people in authority positions, that they’ve got your best interests, and they’re the protectors … It was just odd to me to feel he’s just setting me up to go right back out there and keep using. In fact, he wants me to … That did a mind fuck on me a little bit. I began, from there on, not trusting the system at all, or law enforcement at all.

While she was glad to not get criminal charges, she felt confused that law enforcement was encouraging her to use, rather than connecting her to help. This incident stuck with her and eroded her trust in the systems around her.

Another woman with a history of OUD (Participant 36) similarly discussed her distrust of the criminal legal and treatment systems that grew over the period of her opioid use. As a Black woman herself, this participant relayed her observations that these systems tend to not offer Black women, and Black people in general, the alternative treatments they offer to White people:

I do notice a lot of Black women don’t get offered drug court. A lot of Black people in general. It’s predominantly White people that are getting drug court and the treatment and all that.

This participant recounted how her best friend was offered the chance to participate in treatment court, while her own request was denied:

She went to long-term treatment. Then from there, she did a halfway house, and then, I believe, it was house arrest and random check-ins. The services were just widened … When I was incarcerated, I inquired about it and requested it, but I was denied it.

She was never given a reason why she was denied. Her own experience and her observations of others led her to distrust the criminal legal and treatment systems’ ability to help Black individuals with SUDs. She highlighted the racial trauma she endured in the criminal legal system and its connections to her ongoing substance use.

Addressing Trauma-Related Treatment Needs

All participant groups widely espoused the need to restructure treatment systems and provide trauma-informed care in order to facilitate women’s recovery. There was especially a call for comprehensive interventions.

Offering Comprehensive Therapies with MOUD Treatment

MOUD treatment can provide valuable care to women seeking recovery from OUD; however, our participants acknowledged the shortcomings of the current MOUD treatment model for many women. For example, one SUD treatment professional (Participant 9) shared that trauma-informed training was not provided in her formal education; instead, she took the initiative “to learn a lot of trauma-based care to help the women I assist.”

That same participant further shared that many women receiving MOUD treatment do not also receive counseling. MOUD outpatient programs often only offer case management services, and MOUD inpatient programs that offer counseling often lack trauma- and grief-related training for their counselors. In describing the current structure of many MOUD treatment programs, this SUD treatment professional (Participant 9) said:

[Y]ou go into a treatment center and you just sit. They might put you into some groups, they might put you into some meetings, but you’re not relating with anybody on any level … You’re not being held accountable and you’re not having an outlet for your trauma.

She felt that the counseling provided often was not sufficient for the complex needs of women who had experienced significant trauma over their lifetimes.

The women in our sample shared similar shortcomings of the current MOUD treatment model. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 31) expressed her frustration with the explicit guidelines in one treatment program to not talk about past traumas:

They [treatment staff] were like, “You can’t talk about rape. You can’t talk about violence.” They’re like, “You’re here for 30 days. If we open up that can of worms, it’s not safe for you.” Then what are we doing here? It’s basically just 30 days away from drugs in your hometown.

This same participant also shared her specific therapeutic needs and asserted that working with counselors who are not trauma-informed can be damaging:

When things get hard, I still want to use, and I don’t know how else to fix that except maybe extensive therapy … I have to build a whole new life. I have to fix the negative storylines I have about myself. I have to heal from abuse. I have to treat my mental health, and that stuff doesn’t really come with the drug treatment program necessarily … I’m going to start grilling my therapists before we start, because if you’re not really trauma informed, you can completely ruin me.

This woman felt that she was never offered the advanced therapies that could help her unpack past trauma. She had started to research trauma-informed care on her own so that she could specifically search out what might help her heal.

When traumas can be identified and processed in safe spaces, and professionals in both the criminal legal and substance use treatment systems are trained in trauma-informed practices, women seeking recovery will have access to person-centered, affirming care. One woman with a history of OUD (Participant 21) attested to the importance of such care through a positive experience she had had while in treatment court:

I was actually in a long-term relationship where that individual … died of an overdose. That happened two weeks prior to when I was supposed to graduate drug court. My probation officer reached out to me and said, the team would offer you to stay on the program an extra three months to make sure you’re on stable ground. I voluntarily stayed in drug court for another three months, just so I could help process, go through all that and make sure I was in a better mindset when I graduated.

After going through a traumatic loss in the program, her treatment court case management team provided her with additional services so she could process her loss.

DISCUSSION

Key Results

We explored the mechanisms connecting childhood and adulthood trauma to substance use initiation and OUD recovery among criminal-legal involved women, triangulating insight from women with lived experience and professionals working with women with OUD. Interview data revealed the effects of three different types of trauma on substance use and recovery outcomes.

First, study participants detailed the long-term effects of unaddressed childhood trauma, spanning two dimensions: direct/indirect and chronic/acute. Traumas varied concerning whether the participant was the recipient or witness to the trauma and whether the trauma was ongoing or a single defining event. The professionals in our sample echoed what women shared about the prevalence and magnitude of traumatic childhood experiences among women with OUD, emphasizing the profound impact of repressed and unprocessed trauma on addiction later in life. In a previous study, we described how intergenerational substance use can influence women’s own OUD trajectories by normalizing substance use (Jones et al., 2024). Here, we emphasize that intergenerational substance use does not simply normalize substance use but instead can induce trauma that may influence substance use initiation as a child ages.

Second, participants discussed lifetime exposure to trauma, including traumas experienced throughout adulthood. Being exposed to family- and partner-based trauma across the life course, as well as experiencing sexual abuse or assault, comprised the main subthemes within this category. These traumas often accumulate over time to impact recovery success.

Third, while these two themes point to past traumas as influences for substance use initiation and return to use during recovery, opioid use itself also perpetuated exposure to further traumatic events. Emergent subthemes centered around grief and loss,, the complexities of criminal-legal involvement, and interpersonal and systemic exploitation while using opioids or pursuing recovery from OUD.

The experiences disclosed by our participants reflect trends across empirical literature on OUD and trauma. Women consistently report experiencing CSA (Evans et al., 2020; Winstanley et al., 2020) and sexual violence in adulthood (e.g., IPV, sexual assault) (Martin et al., 2022; Pallatino et al., 2021; Williams et al., 2021) at higher rates than men, although it is worth noting that the true rate of sexual victimization among men is likely higher than reported due to shame, stigma, and a weakened sense of agency (Hlavka, 2017). Women with childhood trauma are at greater risk of being diagnosed with OUD (Tang et al., 2020) and co-occurring mental health disorders (Evans et al., 2020). Pregnant and parenting women are at an even higher risk for unsuccessful MOUD treatment when considering the intersection of childhood trauma and opioid-related stigma in adulthood (Gannon et al., 2021; Preis et al., 2020). Furthermore, women who experience IPV are more likely to have substance use recurrence and lower retention rates in MOUD treatment programs than women who do not face continued trauma in adulthood (Martin et al., 2022). Our participants emphasized gender-based differences in the impacts of trauma, identifying repressed sexual trauma as one of the main barriers to successful MOUD treatment for women.

All of our study participant groups expressed the resounding need for trauma-informed and gender-specific care across MOUD treatment programs and the criminal legal system. Many participants described effective MOUD treatment programs as containing multiple forms of counseling to facilitate healing from unprocessed trauma, promote effective coping with mental and emotional distress, and provide family support. Nonetheless, such care was rarely practiced in those spaces where our participants engaged in treatment, both inside and outside of the criminal legal system. Other scholars have also identified these unmet needs as vital components of MOUD treatment for women with a history of trauma (Huhn & Dunn, 2020). Exposure to trauma in childhood and adulthood has been shown to increase self-reported opioid cravings (Ibañez et al., 2022), as women often use substances to cope with the traumas they have endured (Pallatino et al., 2021).

Alongside the need for trauma-informed care, participants across all groups alluded to further difficulties addressing unprocessed trauma. Their responses revealed a tension between the potentially retraumatizing nature of recounting past events and the importance of processing trauma through discussing their past. One woman shared how invalidation during disclosure amplified the negative impact of her trauma. Another mentioned how some treatment programs have explicit guidelines against discussing past traumas, despite the women’s willingness to talk about it. Both instances suggest that the current MOUD treatment model is ill-equipped to address unprocessed trauma. This is especially pertinent when considering that MOUD outpatient programs may only provide case management services, and of the MOUD programs that provide counseling, counselors may not have received trauma-specific trainings. Our findings echo the call for developing and implementing evidence-based and trauma-informed guidelines, procedures, and trainings for SUD treatment professionals to best meet the multifaceted needs of the women they serve (Saraiya et al., 2020; Winiker et al., 2023).

The women and professionals in our study also highlighted women’s unique and interconnected treatment needs. Participants emphasized the importance of bolstering parenting/family supports (Jones et al., 2024; Apsley et al., 2024), offering trauma-centered therapeutic interventions (Jones et al., 2025), and providing treatment for co-occurring mental illnesses (Skogseth et al. 2024), all of which have been previously identified as opportunities to reduce stigma (Gannon et al., 2021) and improve care for women in MOUD treatment (e.g., Evans et al., 2020; Preis et al., 2020). Importantly, trauma-informed treatment approaches (e.g., mindfulness, emotion regulation, interoceptive awareness) have been proven effective in helping women achieve recovery by reducing cravings and improving depression symptoms (Price et al., 2019). Wider implementation of women-specific, trauma-informed treatment approaches would reduce barriers to care and enhance treatment retention and sustained recovery, especially for women needing a safe space to heal from trauma (Springer et al., 2020).

Limitations and Strengths

This study’s findings should be considered alongside its limitations. First, some traumatic experiences identified by participants were challenging to categorize into a single thematic code because many traumas were ongoing and cumulative. Second, given the sensitive nature of trauma disclosures, our telephone-based interview methodology afforded benefits and challenges, consistent with empirical literature comparing interviews occurring in person, over the telephone, and via videoconferencing platforms (e.g., Irani, 2019). Conducting interviews over the telephone may have provided some participants the anonymity they needed to disclose sensitive traumas; however, it may also have prevented the building of sufficient rapport, which other participants may need to disclose such events. Third, the interviews were conducted once and lasted approximately1 hour, potentially limiting the amount of information participants could provide at a single time point. Fourth, although the study examined experiences with opioids, participants may have prior experiences with multiple types of substances. To reconcile any elements of co-use, the interviewers asked questions specifically related to experiences with opioid use. Finally, the demographic variation within our sample could have been enhanced for under-represented groups in research, namely Black, Indigenous, and Hispanic/Latina participants. A more heterogeneous sample could better reflect the breadth and depth of traumas experienced by criminal-legal involved women with OUD and their role in MOUD treatment success.

Nonetheless, our study strengths center around understanding the mechanisms by which compounding trauma impacts substance use among women involved in the criminal legal system. Given the frequency with which participants identified the unique trauma-related needs of women, approaches to care that are gender-specific, person-centered, and trauma-informed would facilitate retention in MOUD treatment by allowing women to process traumas. Addressing this gap is vital for enhancing our comprehension of revictimization and developing more targeted intervention strategies for trauma and substance use.
