Ideal cardiovascular health (CVH) behaviour is associated with a lower presence of colorectal adenomas and serrated polyps
Authors: Yan Gong, Qian Qin, Ying Sui, Jiaoyan Li, Suying Ding, Yansong Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between the American Heart Association (AHA) Life's Essential 7 (LE7) score and the risk of precancerous colorectal lesions remains unclear. The aim of this study was to explore the associations between ideal cardiovascular health (CVH) behaviours and colorectal cancer (CRC) precursors.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study using health check-up data from the PLA General Hospital from 2013 to 2019. All the subjects underwent routine physical examination and colonoscopy. The seven CVH variables are divided into poor (0), moderate (1) and ideal (2) levels to obtain a CVH score ranging from 0 to 14 points. Multiple logistic regression models were used to assess the associations between the CVH score and CRC precursors.ResultsOf the 5627 participants included, 418 (7.43%), 2044 (36.32%) and 3165 (56.25%) were classified as having poor, moderate and ideal CVH scores, respectively. Adenomas and serrated polyps (SPs) were found in 682 (12.12%) and 416 (7.39%) participants, respectively. A 1-unit increase in the CVH score was associated with a 7%, 10.4% and 12.5% decrease in the risk of adenoma, advanced adenoma (AA) and SP, respectively. The risk of adenoma, AA and SP in the poor CVH subgroup was 1.41, 1.82 and 2.1 times greater, respectively, than that in the ideal CVH subgroup. Subgroup analysis revealed that the negative association was significantly greater for men and participants older than 45 years. A restrictive cubic spline indicated that when the CVH was less than 10 points, only the risk of SPs gradually increased.ConclusionsOur findings suggested that promoting adherence to optimal CVH may be beneficial for preventing CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24942-z.Keywords: Ideal cardiovascular health, CVH, Colorectal adenoma, Serrated polyps

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the fifth leading cause of cancer-related death [1]. Recently, a 2020 report from a secondary analysis of global cancer statistics revealed that the incidence rate of CRC has increased rapidly in China due to changes in lifestyle and the rapid aging of the population [2]. The majority of related studies have confirmed that most malignancies grow slowly and develop from adenomatous polyps or serrated polyps (SPs) [3]. The SPs included hyperplastic polyps (HPs), traditional serrated adenomas (TSAs), and sessile serrated adenomas/polyps (SSAs/Ps) [4, 5]. The causes of colon cancer are complex; in addition to family history and inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes, and hyperlipidaemia have also been shown to be colorectal risk factors. Furthermore, lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption and a high-fat diet, have been proven to play detrimental roles in preventing colorectal cancer [6–8]. Some of these factors have different correlations with SPs than with conventional adenomas [9]. However, previous studies have explored the associations between individual lifestyle factors and colon polyps, ignoring the fact that each specific healthy lifestyle factor can coexist with other behavioural factors, thereby generating a synergistic effect on human health.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major health concern worldwide [10, 11]. Many cohort studies have shown that healthy lifestyle behaviours are effective in preventing and even treating CVD. In 2010, the American Heart Association (AHA) proposed Life's Simple 7 (LS7) as an indicator of cardiovascular health (CVH) to further improve the health status of the general population [12]. Seven cardiovascular behaviour-related measures were identified, including four desirable behaviours (nonsmoking, physical activity, normal body mass index (BMI), and healthy diet) and three desirable health factors (normal blood glucose levels, blood pressure levels, and total serum cholesterol levels). However, there are certain differences in the distributions of ideal CVH measures among different countries and regions [13–15]. Owing to differences in dietary habits and units of measurement, the dietary recommendations presented by local dietary guidelines are not the same. Since CVD and CRC precursors share a number of risk factors, such as smoking, unhealthy diet, obesity, diabetes mellitus and hyperlipidaemia [16–20], some studies have investigated the effect of an overall healthy lifestyle on the risk of CRC precursors, including adenomas and SPs [21, 22]. However, the correlation between the incidence of precancerous CRC lesions and the synthetic effect of ideal cardiovascular factors remains uncertain.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the associations of desirable cardiovascular metrics with conventional colorectal adenomas and SPs in a large-scale health checkup cohort to provide guidance for the primary prevention of colon cancer. Additionally, this study provides some evidence for the development of follow-up guidance or policies for the prevention of colon cancer through different lifestyle combinations.

Methods

Study population

A total of 7101 participants who underwent health check-ups and colonoscopies at the Institute of Health Management of the Chinese People's Liberation Army General Hospital from February 2013 to December 2019 were included in the baseline study. The admission criterion was as follows: participants who underwent colonoscopy and whose data were complete. Initially, we excluded 1002 participants with missing colonoscopy information and 223 participants with incomplete diagnostic or pathological data. We also excluded 64 subjects with lower gastrointestinal tract symptoms (such as abdominal pain, diarrhoea, constipation, and black stool), 112 subjects with inflammatory bowel disease, and 5 subjects with unexpected weight loss of more than 4.5 kg in the past 6 months. Furthermore, we excluded subjects with a history of lower digestive tract surgery (n = 24); serious organ diseases affecting the heart, lung, liver, or kidney disease (n = 13); a history of pregnancy or any type of cancer (n = 20); abnormal blood coagulation (n = 10); and mental disorders (n = 1). A total of 5627 consecutive asymptomatic patients were ultimately included. The flow chart is shown in Figure S1. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese People's Liberation Army General Hospital (S2021-636–01). All the participants were informed that their clinical data could be used anonymously for scientific research, and they all signed an informed consent form.

Criteria for ideal CVH indicators

CVH was assessed in accordance with the LS7, which includes four behaviours (smoking status, physical activity, healthy eating score, and body mass index (BMI)) and three health factors (fasting blood glucose levels, blood pressure levels, and total serum cholesterol levels). Each indicator was classified as poor, moderate, or ideal and assigned 0, 1, or 2 points, respectively. The overall CVH score is the sum of the scores for seven indicators, ranging from 0 to 14. The total CVH score is divided into three levels: poor (0–6), moderate (7–9), and ideal (10–14) (Table 1) [23].

The criteria for the ideal cardiovascular health (CVH) score were established in the present study

The specific classification and assessment criteria were as follows. (1) Smoking was defined as consuming ≥ 10 cigarettes per day for at least 1 year, and smoking cessation was defined as the continuous cessation of smoking for more than one year. (2) Physical activity was divided into three levels: (a) adequate physical activity was defined as engaging in vigorous activity for at least 3 days per week (with a minimum total physical activity of 1500 metabolic equivalent (MET-) minutes per week) or 7 days a week (achieving a total physical activity of at least 3000 MET-minutes per week); (b) moderate physical activity was defined as engaging in at least 20 min of vigorous physical activity per day for a minimum of 3 days per week or at least 30 min of nonvigorous physical activity per week for a minimum of 5 days per week or at least 5 days of physical activity per week, achieving a minimum of 600 MET-minutes per week but not exceeding 3000 MET-min; and (c) inadequate physical activity was defined as any level of physical activity that did not meet the abovementioned criteria. (3) The criteria for determining dietary factors were as follows [24]: (a) standard daily intake of cereals (including coarse cereals): 250 g-400 g; nonstandard intake: < 250 g or > 400 g; (b) standard daily vegetable (≥ 300 g) and fruit (≥ 200 g) consumption; (c) standard daily intake of dairy products: 250 g-350 g; inadequate intake: < 250 g and excessive intake: > 350 g; (d) standard daily intake: 50 g-100 g; livestock and poultry meat: 50 g-75 g; eggs: 25 g-75 g; inadequate intake: < 125 g and excessive intake: > 225 g; and (d) standard daily edible oil consumption: < 30 g; additionally, salt intake did not exceed 6 g for compliance; otherwise, compliance was not met. Additionally, since daily sodium intake is not easy to assess accurately and Chinese people generally exceed the standard of salt intake, this study used the taste of individuals, compared with that of people around them, to judge very light and slightly light as consistent and the same, slightly salty and very salty as inconsistent. (4) A body composition analyser was used to measure body weight and height, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Overweight was defined as a BMI of 24 kg/m2 to 28 kg/m2, and obesity was defined as a BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 according to the 2016 “expert consensus on medico-nutritional treatment of overweight and obesity in China” [25]. The criteria for blood pressure, fasting blood glucose and total cholesterol are shown in Table 1.

Definitions of traditional adenomas and serrated polyps

All colonoscopies were performed by experienced gastroenterologists in accordance with standard clinical procedures at the gastroenterology centre of the Health Management Institute of the PLA General Hospital. The colorectal lesions found during the colonoscopy were carefully examined, and the polyp excision specimen was evaluated by further pathological diagnosis. Clinical information, including the morphological characteristics of the lesion, anatomical location (distance from the anus), size, number, and pathological findings, was collected and recorded. Adenomas are classified into advanced and nonadvanced adenomas, where advanced adenomas are defined as those ≥ 10 mm in diameter and/or with high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia and/or at least 20% villus composition, and nonadvanced adenomas include all other adenomas [26]. Proximal colon polyps included polyps located in the caecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, transverse colon or splenic flexure. Distal colon polyps included polyps located in the descending colon, sigmoid, or rectal colon.

Covariables

Covariant information, including sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex, education, and marital status, was also collected through questionnaires. The level of education was divided into primary school and below, secondary education (junior high school, senior high school, and technical secondary school), and higher education (junior college, university undergraduate, and postgraduate). The laboratory test indices included lipid levels, blood glucose levels and other routine parameters.

Statistical analysis

Variables are presented as standard deviations (continuous variables) or percentages (categorical variables) as appropriate. Descriptive statistics were performed according to the category of the CVH indicators, and where appropriate, chi-square tests, ANOVA or Kruskal‒Wallis tests were used to explore subgroup differences.

We applied a logistic regression model to explore the associations between adenoma, advanced adenoma, and serrated polyps and CVH scores. In the regression model, we treated the CVH score as a continuous variable and a categorical variable. We used three regression models: Model 1 was the unadjusted model; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was adjusted for all covariates. We also included all components of the CVH score to evaluate the associations between adenoma or SP and CVH components. Additionally, we used a restricted cubic spline curve to visualize the relationships between the adenomas, advanced adenomas, or SPs and the CVH indices. We also performed a stratified analysis to explore whether these associations changed according to age and sex. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All the statistical analyses were performed using R software.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Overall, 5627 participants were included in the final analysis. The mean age of the participants was 48.62 (SD, 7.23) years, and 1332 (23.67%) participants were female. Of these, 418 (7.43%), 2044 (36.32%) and 3165 (56.25%) participants were classified as having poor, moderate and ideal CVH scores, respectively (Table S1). The majority of participants (n = 3632) were free of these lesions, and 682 (12.12%) and 416 (7.39%) participants were diagnosed with adenomas and serrated polyps, respectively. Table 2 shows the baseline characteristics of the included participants, and Fig. 1A shows the distribution of CVH scores among the included participants. Among the different categories of CVH scores, almost all the characteristics were significantly different (p < 0.05). Men and those with lower education levels were more likely to have poorer CVH scores with higher triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and uric acid (UA) levels. Compared with those with high CVH, participants with low CVH and moderate CVH scores had a significantly increased prevalence of adenomas (15.48% vs. 13.77% vs. 9.40%); this phenomenon was also observed in advanced adenomas and serrated polyps, indicating that participants with poor CVH scores tended to have a higher prevalence of adenomas and serrated polyps. Similarly, patients with adenomas, advanced adenomas, and serrated polyps tended to have lower CVH scores (Fig. 1B-D).

Associations between colorectal adenomas and serrated polyps and CVH scores

To further explore the characteristics of adenomas, advanced adenomas, and serrated polyps, we evaluated the relationships between CVH scores and colorectal adenomas and serrated polyps via logistic regression analyses. We observed that CVH scores were inversely associated with a greater risk of adenomas, advanced adenomas, and serrated polyps (odds ratio (OR) = 0.904 for adenomas, 95% CI = 0.873–0.937; for advanced adenomas, OR = 0.896, 95% CI = 0.834–0.963; for serrated polyps, OR = 0.856, 95% CI = 0.819–0.894). This association remained significant after adjusting for age and sex in Model 2. After further adjusting for multiple confounding factors in Model 3, the results revealed that each 1-unit increase in the CVH score was associated with a 7%, 10.4% and 12.5% decrease in the risks of adenoma (OR = 0.930, 95% CI = 0.886–0.977), advanced adenoma (OR = 0.896, 95% CI = 0.811–0.990) and serrated polyps (OR = 0.875, 95% CI = 0.824–0.930), respectively (Table 3).

These results remained robust when the CVH score was treated as a categorical variable in Model 1, Model 2 and Model 3. Specifically, after adjusting for all covariates in Model 3, compared with those in the ideal CVH group, participants with low CVH had a significantly increased risk of incident adenomas (OR = 1.414, 95% CI = 1.012–1.975), advanced adenomas (OR = 1.822, 95% CI = 0.933–3.559), and serrated polyps (OR = 2.110, 95% CI = 1.427–3.120) (Table 3).

To clarify the dose‒response relationships between the CVH scores and adenomas, advanced adenomas, and serrated polyps, a restrictive cubic spline was further used to visualize the associations. In the curve, we identified a key point: a CVH score of 10. When the CVH score is > 10, the risk of adenoma and serrated polyps gradually decreases; however, when the CVH score is less than 10 points, only the risk of serrated polyps gradually increases. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the primary prevention of serrated adenomas (Fig. 2).

Association between separate CVH scores and the risk of adenoma, advanced adenoma and serrated polyp

In contrast to the findings for adenoma components, we observed that the detection rate of serrated polyps was greater in participants who reported unhealthy eating (OR = 3.043, 95% CI = 1.572–5.889), smoking (OR = 2.203, 95% CI = 1.773–2.737), overweight/obesity (OR = 1.705, 95% CI = 1.269–2.290), poor blood pressure (OR = 1.426, 95% CI = 1.101–1.847) or hyperglycaemia (OR = 1.513, 95% CI = 1.080–2.120) than in those with ideal CVH scores. There were no significant differences in the adenoma detection rate according to the other four factors. Notably, smoking was more strongly associated with serrated polyps than with adenomas (Table S2).

Associations between colorectal adenomas or serrated polyps at different sites and CVH incidence

We performed logistic regression analyses to assess the relationships between adenomas and serrated polyps at different sites and CVH scores. The results revealed that the CVH score was negatively correlated with adenomas in the left colon (OR = 0.928, 95% CI = 0.862–0.999), serrated polyps in the left colon (OR = 0.866, 95% CI = 0.780–0.961), and serrated polyps in the right colon (OR = 0.883, 95% CI = 0.820–0.950).

When the CVH score was treated as a categorical variable, participants with worse CVH scores had a significantly greater risk of serrated polyps in both the left colon and right colon. After adjusting for all the covariates, compared with those in the ideal CVH group, the CVH group with the worst scores had a twofold greater risk of having serrated polyps located in the left colon (OR, 2.168; 95% CI: 1.134–4.145) and the right colon (OR, 2.154; 95% CI: 1.339–3.465) (Table 5).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were used to assess the robustness of the associations between adenomas, advanced adenomas, and serrated polyps and CVH scores. According to the stratified analysis by sex, there was a negative association for males but not for females, where the ORs for adenomas, serrated polyps, and advanced adenomas in the poor CVH score group were 1.478 (1.050–2.082), 2.129 (1.426–3.180), and 1.994 (0.997–3.984), respectively. Additionally, only serrated polyps were negatively associated with female sex. According to the stratified analyses by age, participants with poor CVH scores were at greater risk of adenomas, serrated polyps, and advanced adenomas when they were older than 45 years, corresponding to OR values of 1.510 (1.030–2.213), 2.212 (1.373–3.564), and 1.906 (0.935–3.884), respectively (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This cross-sectional analysis of a representative check-up population revealed that CVH scores were negatively associated with the severity of adenomas and serrated polyps. More precisely, the risks of adenomas, advanced adenomas and serrated polyps were 1.41, 1.82, and 2.11 times greater, respectively, in the poor CVH score group than in the ideal CVH score group. In particular, there was a dose‒response relationship between CVH scores and serrated adenomas. Additionally, subgroup analysis revealed that results were robust.

To date, a limited number of studies have investigated the clustering effects of healthy lifestyle factors on the risk of CRC precancerous lesions. Two European cross-sectional studies reported a negative association between adherence to a healthy lifestyle and the risk of colorectal cancer at different stages driven by the traditional adenoma pathway [27, 28]. Another case‒control study from the United States reported the dangerous effects of an unhealthy lifestyle on overall adenomas [29]. Our findings verified the above conclusion that, compared with those in the ideal CVH group, the detection rates of adenomas and advanced adenomas were 1.41- and 1.82-fold greater in the CVH group with poor scores respectively. However, whether serrated pathological lesions are associated with unhealthy lifestyles is unclear. One study from Israel revealed that a healthy lifestyle pattern (eating a healthy diet, quitting smoking, participating in physical activity, and maintaining a normal weight) was associated with a 41% lower risk of overall serrated polyps. This result was confirmed in another study of an Asian population. However, the power of the above studies was very limited due to the small sample size of serrated polyp patients (n = 75) and the use of independent variables, including five lifestyle factors [30] – i.e., smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, diet and weight [21]. Surprisingly, when we included CVH scores along with lifestyle indices and biochemical indices, a dose‒response relationship between CVH scores and serrated adenoma was observed. In particular, when the CVH score is greater than 10, the incidence of serrated adenoma gradually decreases. When the CVH score is less than 10, the risk of serrated adenoma gradually increases. These findings confirmed that the risk of colonic polyps and serrated adenomas gradually decreases with the CVH score level gradually increases. Having more than five ideal CVH indicators was protective factors for the occurrence of colon polyps. This suggested that cardiovascular health and precancerous lesions of colon cancer share the common risk factors and increase the risk of colon cancer through common pathways, such as inflammatory responses [31].

In addition to the results of the CVH synergistic score analysis, the associations of the independent factor CVH with precancerous risk should also be noted

Conclusions

In our study, we found that smoking was strongly negatively associated with both adenomas and serrated polyps and that the association with serrated polyps was stronger than that with adenomas, which was in line with the findings of previous studies [7, 32]. In fact, the underlying mechanism between smoking and serrated polyps remains unclear. Smoking may promote abnormal DNA promoter methylation, leading to microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)- and CpG island methylator (CIMP)-positive tumours [33, 34]. Additionally, cigarettes contain a variety of carcinogens, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which directly permeate the colon mucosa or circulatory system, thereby increasing the risk of colon polyps. In addition to smoking, a high BMI is associated with an increased risk of adenomas and serrated polyps, as obesity accelerates inflammation and alters the abundance of specific microorganisms, thereby promoting the development of adenomas and serrated polyps [35, 36]. Our study also revealed hyperglycaemia as a potential risk factor, with possible mechanisms focused on insulin resistance [37, 38]. Therefore, ideal CVH measures, including quitting smoking, maintaining normal blood glucose levels and maintaining a normal BMI, play crucial roles in reducing the burden of colorectal cancer precursor lesions.

Subgroup analysis indicated that the negative association between adenomas and CVH scores was more pronounced in male patients. Furthermore, the risks of adenomas, serrated polyps, and advanced adenomas in participants older than 45 years with poor CVH scores increased by 1.51 times, 2.21 times and 1.90 times respectively. More attention should be given to low CVH scores among men older than 45 years for the prevention of precursor colon cancer lesions.

Our current study provided some new insights on the associations between CVH scores and the severity of adenomas and serrated polyps. Our study further highlights the link between the risk of full-stage lesions and CVH scores in two different pathways of colorectal carcinogenesis and supports previous research showing a negative association between CRC precancerous lesions and a healthy lifestyle. Additionally, we used the ideal CVH score group as a reference group to further attract the attention of individuals with poor lifestyle habits. Above all, our findings suggest that we should pay more attention to CVH scores for prevention of adenomas and serrated polyps.

Our results may have significant clinical significance. First, our research results may inform the public of the importance of healthy behaviors and normal blood pressure, blood sugar and blood lipid levels in the prevention of CRC precancerous lesions. Second, the CVH score quantified the comparison of the detection rates of colonic polyps and serrated adenomas among participants with different scores, and more intuitively demonstrated the impact of healthy behaviors on the occurrence of ADs and SPs from the dimensions of gender and age. Therefore, CVH can effectively convey to the public that maintaining ideal CVH is also very helpful for preventing colorectal cancer and its precancerous lesions.

We highlighted several advantages of our study. First, unlike previous studies that investigated the associations between lifestyle indicators and precancerous CRC status, our study included metrics of ideal cardiovascular health, including not only lifestyle indices but also biochemical indices, which increase the reliability of our findings. Second, this study was based on a large-scale health cohort to investigate the association between healthy lifestyles and the risk of colorectal polyps. Moreover, colorectal polyps were accurately diagnosed by colonoscopy and pathology, and all the clinicians involved were blinded to the lifestyle data of the participants.

However, several limitations should be noted when our results are interpreted. First, the reverse causal relationship cannot be ruled out due to the cross-sectional design of the study. Second, lifestyle factors such as food intake were collected from the questionnaires, but not based on objective markers of food. There may be some recall and misclassification biases among these variables. Third, lifestyle factors are evaluated only at baseline, and more frequent measurements may provide more useful information. However, on the whole, most adults have relatively stable lifestyles. Although the aim of our study was to investigate the cumulative effects of lifestyle factors in the form of CVH scores, CVH did not provide any information on which clustering of lifestyle factors or components had greater predictive value. This can be regarded as another limitation of our study. Fourth, data on the prevalence of synchronous adenomas and serrated polyps cannot be obtained because only experienced gastroenterologists collect the lesion tissues of the most advanced lesions according to a unified protocol for further pathological diagnosis, similar to previous cross-sectional studies based on colonoscopy, focusing on the two carcinogenic pathways of colon cancer. Finally, although many potential confounding factors were considered in multivariable analyses, the residual confounding factors could not be excluded.

In conclusion, in this large cross-sectional study based on the colonoscopy results of the physical examination population, we found that adhering to a healthy lifestyle and maintaining normal blood pressure, blood sugar and blood lipid levels were negatively correlated with the risks of ad and SPs, and its contribution to SPs was greater than that to ADs. Our research results may help further confirm the preventive role of good cardiovascular health indicators in the occurrence of colorectal cancer and emphasize the significance of primary prevention through lifestyle intervention.
