PEST++IES How Many Iterations and Realizations, Finding the Point of Diminishing Returns
Authors: Trent J Farnum, Andrew T Leaf, Michael N Fienen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPEST++IES (White 2018; White et al. 2020) is widely used in the groundwater modeling community for its ability to perform computationally efficient history matching and uncertainty analysis in a highly parameterized context. One primary advantage of using an iterative ensemble smoother is that the number of model runs required per iteration depends on the number of realizations in an ensemble, not the number of parameters in each realization. However, this raises the question: what is the optimal number of realizations and iterations to use for any one model before the point of diminishing returns? Using a modified version of the Freyberg model (Freyberg 1988; Hunt et al. 2020), different parameter and observation scenarios were evaluated for four iterations and ensembles of 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 realizations. To match observations, PEST++IES altered hydraulic conductivity (k), both globally across the model and locally at three different pilot point densities, as well as global recharge (via a single multiplier), global river conductance, and individual well flow rates. Risk‐based well capture zone results (Fienen et al. 2022a) and estimated hydraulic conductivity fields from each scenario were quantitatively and qualitatively compared against the “truth” model and its outputs. Across the cases examined, ensemble sizes of 100 to 250 realizations and two PEST++IES iterations were generally sufficient to achieve good results.

Introduction

In recent years, the iterative ensemble smoother (iES; Chen and Oliver 2012) implemented in PEST++ (White 2018; White et al. 2020) has become increasingly popular for groundwater model parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification, in part because of its computational efficiency—especially in problems with many adjustable parameters. Unlike traditional parameter estimation algorithms that require a forward model run for each evaluation of a parameter sensitivity, the iES computes approximate sensitivities from an ensemble of parameter realizations (Fienen et al. 2025), requiring only one model run for each realization (White 2018). A key question for practitioners with limited computing resources is the number of ensemble realizations needed for effective parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification.

Previous authors (Hunt et al. 2021; Kitlasten et al. 2022) have suggested that the number of ensemble realizations should reflect the dimensionality of the parameter estimation solution space. In practice, this guideline can be difficult to follow, as the dimensionality of the solution space is often poorly known and dependent on the content of the observation dataset and the model complexity. Established methods for estimating the solution space dimensionality (e.g., Doherty and Hunt 2009) require computation of a full Jacobian matrix, with a forward model run for each parameter, which may be impractical in an iES context with many parameters.

Among practitioners, ensemble sizes of ~50 to 500 are common, and often dependent on available computing resources; the higher end of this range is generally assumed to encompass the solution space for most problems (e.g., Hunt et al. 2021; Kitlasten et al. 2022; Fienen et al. 2022a, 2022b; Hugman et al. 2023). But while a solution‐space dimensionality guideline may be suitable for history matching (of model outputs to field observations), model predictions may also be sensitive to model inputs that are uninformed by the history matching (Moore and Doherty 2005; Doherty and Moore 2020). Model predictions may also be sensitive to low‐probability parameter combinations that are not adequately sampled with smaller ensemble sizes. In these cases, larger ensembles may be needed to achieve statistical stationarity (i.e., the point at which the results do not change with further sampling). An appropriate ensemble size can be determined by successively evaluating a forecast of interest at increasing numbers of realizations (Fienen et al. 2022a).

This study seeks to examine the effects of ensemble size on the dual goals of parameter estimation and predictive performance, across varying levels of observation data and model parametrization detail, in the context of source water capture zone (Barlow et al. 2018) predictions for water supply wells. Using a well‐known synthetic model (Hunt et al. 2020; White et al. 2020), we evaluate the performance of risk‐based capture zone estimates (Fienen et al. 2022a) and reproduction of the “true” hydraulic conductivity field, across four hypothetical observation datasets and three levels of hydraulic conductivity parameterization detail. Results are summarized at each iES iteration into single metrics, allowing for comparison across the different model configurations, ensemble sizes, and iES iterations. A reproducible workflow designed to run on a single computer is included in the Supporting Information.

Model Description

Modified versions of the Freyberg problem (Freyberg 1988; Hunt et al. 2020) were constructed using MODFLOW 6 (Langevin et al. 2017, 2024) and Flopy (Bakker et al. 2016, 2024). The steady‐state model consists of a single, unconfined layer representing a shallow, water‐table aquifer that is bounded by no‐flow boundaries on the bottom and west, north, and east sides. Boundary conditions include areal recharge, a river that runs north–south through the problem domain, six extraction wells, and a high‐conductance general‐head boundary (e.g., Langevin et al. 2017) along the southern edge (Figure 1).

Scenario construction.

A “truth” version of the model was created at a uniform discretization of 6.25 m. Well rates were modified at three wells to maximize drawdown while preventing model cells from drying out, minimizing drawdown at the well on the east side of the river, and creating an intermediate level of pumping at two of the wells. The river stage and general heads along the southern boundary were increased uniformly by 10 m to decrease the chance of wells going dry during simulations. To simulate natural variability, the recharge field was assigned spatially correlated noise using the fieldgen2d_sva function in pypestutils (https://github.com/pypest/pypestutils), with a correlation length of 375 m (~4% of the model domain length), a variance of 0.0001 (as a fraction of the mean) and no anisotropy. River cell conductances were also assigned uncorrelated noise with a coefficient of variation of 20%.

A second “scenario” model that attempts to simulate the “truth” model was created at a uniform discretization of 25 m. A coarser discretization was selected for this model to reflect the simplification inherent in “real‐world” modeling problems, while maintaining small enough cells to avoid weak sink issues (e.g., Abrams et al. 2013). The scenario model was parametrized as follows:

A single global multiplier for hydraulic conductivity (starting value of 1, bounds 0.1–10) was applied to a starting value of 10 m/d (compared to a mean for the truth model field of 6.9 m/d). Local heterogeneity was considered using a grid of pilot point multiplier parameters (starting values of 1, bounds 0.032 to 32) that were applied on top of the global multiplier to develop a composite set of hydraulic conductivity estimates for each model realization.

Observations for history matching included heads extracted from the truth model solution (described below), and the net flux to the river. All head observations were assigned weights of 1 and noise (in the observation ensemble realizations) of 0.25 m. Net flux to the river was assigned a weight inversely proportional to the observed flux times a coefficient of variation (CV) of 10%, and observation noise based on a CV of 5%.

Capture zones for each of the six extraction wells were developed in the truth and scenario models using the MODFLOW 6 Particle Tracking (PRT) Model (Langevin et al. 2024). Particles were started at the water table (with one particle per cell) in every active cell of the model domain, and tracked forward, with the default PRT Model behavior allowing particles to pass through weak sinks. The PRT model was artificially limited to a simulation timeframe of 9131.25 days (25 years) and a porosity value of 0.1. The computed capture zones included model cells associated with particles that were captured by a well within the timeframe limit.

Scenario Construction

To find the point where increasing the number of iterations and/or realizations no longer returns a substantial benefit, otherwise known as diminishing returns, a scenario matrix of different levels of observation data and hydraulic conductivity parameterization detail was created. Four realizations of head observations extracted from the “truth” model solution were considered. The “Random 25” and “Random 100” scenarios represent situations where head observations are well‐distributed across the model domain at different densities, while the “Clustered 25” and “Clustered 100” realizations consider the common field problem of head observations being unevenly distributed, using the same numbers of observations. (Figure 1).

Three densities of hydraulic conductivity pilot point locations were considered across the scenarios. Regular spacings of 10, 25, and 50 cells resulted in grids of 800, 128, and 32 pilot points. Parameterization of the model boundary conditions was held consistent across the scenarios.

The four observation and 3 pilot point configurations resulted in 12 different scenario groups.

For each scenario group, ensembles of 10, 25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 realizations of the model parameter set were generated using pyEMU (White et al. 2016). To promote spatial continuity between hydraulic conductivity pilot points, a prior covariance matrix based on the pilot point variogram with an alpha value of 3 pilot point spacings was used in the realization draws. PEST++IES was run for four iterations with each ensemble (Table 1).

Probabilistic Capture Zone Analysis

For each PEST++IES run, probabilistic capture zones (e.g., Fienen et al. 2022a) were generated at each iteration. In each model run, a binary array was constructed with ones in cells associated with particles that were captured by a well and zeros elsewhere. The arrays for each ensemble were then summed and divided by the ensemble size to develop a single array of capture probabilities. Capture zone limits were determined from the capture probability array by selecting cells within a designated risk threshold (e.g., Fienen et al. 2022a). For this study, thresholds of 10, 50, and 90% were examined. For brevity, only results from the 50% threshold (cells with >50% probability of capture) are discussed here; results from the 10 and 90% thresholds are available in the Supporting Information. Application of the capture probability threshold results in a single binary array of capture/no capture that can be compared to the truth model capture zone.

Evaluation of Capture Prediction Performance

Capture zone predictions generated by each ensemble from the risk threshold were compared to the truth model capture zone and assigned a score using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC; Matthews 1975).

MCC was chosen over the alternative receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) method (Junge and Dettori 2018) due to sensitivity, specificity, precision and negative predictive value being better incorporated within the calculation (Chicco and Jurman 2023). A score of 1 indicates perfect prediction, 0 is no better than random chance and −1 indicates complete disagreement with the “truth.”

Results and Discussion

Figure 2 shows examples of probabilistic capture estimates compared to the truth model capture, and the resulting MCC scores for one of the scenario groups, at the second PEST++IES iteration. Compared to higher or lower capture probability thresholds, the 50% threshold provides the best comparison to the single, deterministic capture zone from the truth model, as higher probability capture limits tend to shrink with increasing ensemble size, and lower probability limits tend to expand, both resulting in degraded MCC scores.

Probabilistic capture zones compared against the “truth” capture and >50% MCC scores. Red circles indicate the “truth” capture; dashed white line indicates >50% probability of capture from iES simulation.

Figure 3 compares MCC scores at the various ensemble sizes, at each iES iteration, across the matrix of scenario groups. Consistent with previous experience (e.g., Hunt et al. 2021; Fienen et al. 2022a, 2022b; Leaf et al. 2024), the greatest gains in model performance are realized within the first two iES iterations. Overall, an ensemble size of 100 or 250 is sufficient in the cases examined to achieve stationarity in the results. An ensemble size of 50 may be adequate in some cases. Improvement in the MCC scores with successive iES iterations indicates that the capture zone predictions are relatively well informed by the head observation data.

Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) scores comparing risk‐based (>50% probability) capture estimates to the truth model capture zone. Higher scores indicate a better match to the “truth” than lower scores.

Hydraulic Conductivity Comparison

In addition to the risk‐based capture zone estimates, we looked at the effects of ensemble size on reproduction of the “true” hydraulic conductivity field. At each iES iteration and ensemble size, the mismatch between the estimated hydraulic conductivity field for the base ensemble realization and the true hydraulic conductivity field was quantified as root mean squared error (RMSE; Figure 4) across the entire array. The estimated fields for the base realization generally show improvement with increasing ensemble size, up to about 250 realizations, with some scenario groups achieving an RMSE close to the minimum with as little as 50 or 100 realizations. In general, at smaller ensemble sizes, the best results are produced by the 32 pilot point case, consistent with previous recommendations that the ensemble size should reflect the dimensionality of the solution space (Hunt et al. 2021; Kitlasten et al. 2022).

Root mean squared error (RMSE) in the base ensemble realization hydraulic conductivity field, across all scenario groups, ensemble sizes, and iES realizations.

Figure 5 provides a qualitative comparison of the hydraulic conductivity fields for the “Clustered 100,” 800 pilot point scenario group. The 10 and 25 realization cases have substantially greater mismatch, presumably because of low‐quality empirical sensitivities (parameter derivatives) resulting from too few realizations (that are unable to adequately sample the parameter space), and small, diffuse parameter sensitivities that are spread across a large number of pilot points. While 50 realizations result in a favorable capture score (Figure 3); substantial mismatch in the estimated hydraulic field implies that other forecasts that are sensitive to the structure of the field may be biased. An ensemble size of 250 results in near‐optimal hydraulic conductivity estimates for this case, in that RMSE does not appreciably improve with additional realizations (see Figure 4).

Conclusions

The goal of this study was to investigate the ensemble size and number of iterations that might be needed to achieve good predictive performance and parameter estimation with PEST++IES. We evaluated this question using a well‐known synthetic model in the context of risk‐based wellhead capture zone predictions. In addition, reproduction of the “true” hydraulic conductivity field was evaluated as a proxy for prediction types that may be sensitive to coarse detail in the hydraulic conductivity field structure. Twelve cases of varying hydraulic conductivity pilot point density and observation dataset information content were examined. In general, we found ensemble sizes of 100 to 250 and two iES iterations to be sufficient across all cases, with larger ensembles and additional iES iterations producing similar results. Where computational resources are scarce or for preliminary runs, an ensemble size of 50 may be adequate.

While these results are consistent with a diverse set of problems documented in previous work, we note that the ideal ensemble size is ultimately problem‐specific. In this study, we attempted to address this by evaluating different levels of observation and parameter detail. Nevertheless, many field situations may deviate from these findings and therefore benefit from experimentation with different ensemble sizes. The capture zone predictions examined here were relatively well informed by the observation data, as indicated by improvement in predictive performance with successive PEST++IES iterations. When predictions are not well informed by the observation data, larger ensemble sizes may be needed to adequately sample the null space, and additional iES iterations may degrade predictive performance. In other cases, interest in low probability outcomes may motivate the use of larger ensembles. Fienen et al. (2022a) provide an example of empirically determining an appropriate ensemble size through successive evaluation of a forecast at increasing numbers of realizations.
