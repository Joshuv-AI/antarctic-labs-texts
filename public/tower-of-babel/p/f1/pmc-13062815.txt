A multi-omics prognostic model and functional validation of HPGD in clear cell renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Yuelin Du, Yanghuang Zheng, Xiaojun Zhang, Hongbo Wang, Helin Zhang, Panfeng Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundClear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the most common subtype of kidney cancer, exhibits high molecular heterogeneity and poor clinical prognosis. Current prognostic models often lack functional relevance and fail to reflect the immunometabolic complexity of ccRCC. This study aimed to identify robust, functionally relevant prognostic biomarkers through integrative multi-omics analysis and to develop a clinically applicable risk stratification model.MethodsTranscriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), in-house proteomic profiles, and Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)-derived modules were integrated to identify consistently dysregulated genes in ccRCC. A four-gene prognostic signature [15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (HPGD), PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24] was constructed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression. Model performance was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests after median risk-based stratification and by time-dependent concordance indices (C-index) at 1, 3, and 5 years in training and validation cohorts. Gene expression was validated at the mRNA level using TCGA-Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC) RNA sequencing data and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) in 10 paired clinical samples, and at the protein level using the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) and Human Protein Atlas (HPA). Functional relevance was explored using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and immune cell infiltration profiling. Based on multi-omics findings, HPGD was selected for functional validation and stably overexpressed in Caki-1 cells, followed by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), colony formation, wound-healing, and Transwell assays.ResultsAll four genes were consistently downregulated in ccRCC at both mRNA and protein levels. The prognostic model effectively stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups with significantly different overall survival (OS) (log-rank P<0.0001; C-index >0.79). GSEA revealed that HPGD, GLOD5, and SMIM24 were negatively enriched in oncogenic pathways, including the cell cycle and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), while being positively associated with fatty acid metabolism. In contrast, PSAT1 was positively enriched in oncogenic signaling, glycolysis, and cholesterol homeostasis. Immune profiling revealed distinct associations with regulatory T cells, T helper 2 cells, natural killer cells, and mast cells. In vitro experiments demonstrated that HPGD overexpression significantly suppressed proliferation, migration, and invasion of Caki-1 cells.ConclusionsThis study established a novel four-gene signature with robust prognostic significance and biological relevance in ccRCC. The signature is closely associated with lipid metabolism and immune microenvironment remodeling, while functional assays support a tumor-restraining role for HPGD. This integrative multi-omics-based model may improve risk stratification and contribute to personalized clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), the predominant histological subtype of renal cell carcinoma (RCC), accounts for approximately 70–80% of all RCC cases and remains a significant clinical challenge due to its rising incidence, high mortality, and frequent resistance to standard therapies (1-3). Although advances in surgical resection, targeted agents, and immune checkpoint inhibitors have improved patient outcomes, durable responses remain limited, largely due to the molecular heterogeneity and therapy resistance characteristic of ccRCC (4,5). The traditional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system, while routinely used in clinical practice, lacks the resolution to capture this heterogeneity and offers limited prognostic accuracy (6). These limitations highlight the need for more refined and biologically informed prognostic models to guide risk stratification and personalized treatment.

With the advent of high-throughput transcriptomic and proteomic technologies, large-scale datasets such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) have enabled comprehensive molecular characterization of ccRCC (7,8). Numerous gene signatures and prognostic biomarkers have been proposed; however, many models suffer from overfitting, lack of cross-cohort validation, and limited biological interpretability due to reliance on transcriptomic data alone (9-11). Integrative multi-omics approaches that combine transcriptomic, proteomic, and network-based analyses have emerged as more robust strategies for biomarker discovery. Analytical methods such as Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression are particularly effective in refining gene candidates with prognostic value (12-14).

While inactivation of the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) gene and subsequent dysregulation of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are recognized as key drivers of ccRCC pathogenesis (15-17), accumulating evidence highlights the pivotal roles of metabolic reprogramming and immune microenvironment remodeling in tumor progression and therapeutic response (18,19). However, these metabolic and immune-related features remain insufficiently incorporated into current prognostic models. Although several molecular signatures have been reported, few have undergone functional validation or demonstrated clinical utility, particularly in relation to the metabolic reprogramming and tumor immune microenvironment (20,21).

In this study, we addressed these gaps by conducting a multi-omics integrative analysis combining transcriptomic datasets (GEO, TCGA), in-house proteomic profiling, and WGCNA-derived gene modules. This strategy enabled us to identify a robust set of dysregulated genes and construct a four-gene prognostic model—comprising 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (HPGD), PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24—using LASSO-Cox regression. The prognostic significance of the four genes were validated in independent datasets, and their expression was further correlated with metabolic pathways, immune cell infiltration profiles, and clinicopathological characteristics.

Among these genes, HPGD was of particular interest due to its established role in prostaglandin degradation and previously reported tumor-suppressive activity in multiple malignancies (22). To validate its functional relevance in ccRCC, we conducted in vitro experiments in Caki-1 cells. Our findings showed that HPGD overexpression significantly suppressed cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, providing the first direct evidence of its tumor-inhibitory role in renal cancer. These results not only reinforce the clinical significance of HPGD as a prognostic biomarker but also suggest its potential as a therapeutic target in ccRCC. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-608/rc).

Methods

Data acquisition and differential gene expression analysis

Data from the ccRCC transcriptome (GSE46699) and associated clinical metadata were sourced from the GEO repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Following standard data preprocessing, we obtained 130 qualified samples (63 normal, 67 tumor) with 20,188 protein-coding genes and matched clinical annotations. A global expression distribution boxplot was generated for quality control (Figure S1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from the GEO dataset (GEO-DEGs) were identified with limma [|log2 fold change (FC)| >1, adjusted P<0.05].

RNA sequencing data from TCGA-Kidney Renal Clear Cell Carcinoma (KIRC) cohort were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). The TCGA-KIRC cohort included 541 patients with ccRCC, with matched transcriptomic and clinical data used for model construction and validation. Following quality control and preprocessing, 613 samples (72 normal, 541 tumor) with 60,533 transcripts quantified as transcripts per million (TPM) were retained. Genes expressed in ≥50% of samples were filtered, resulting in 32,934 protein-coding genes. Differential expression analysis between tumor and normal tissues was performed using the limma package, and TCGA-DEGs were identified using the same screening criteria (|log2FC| >1, adjusted P<0.05).

Proteomic profiles from five paired ccRCC tumours and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from our previously published study (23). Due to the limited availability of high-quality paired specimens suitable for proteomic profiling, this dataset was primarily used as a complementary multi-omics layer rather than for independent model construction. Differential protein expression analysis was performed using the limma package with the same screening thresholds as described above, yielding differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) from the proteomic dataset (Proteomic-DEPs). Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 and pheatmap packages, respectively.

WGCNA

The GSE46699 dataset from GEO underwent preprocessing involving filtration of low-variance genes (retaining top 50% variance genes) and outlier removal through sample clustering, ensuring the gene expression matrix conformed to normal distribution with eliminated batch effects. The optimal soft thresholding power (β =9) was determined to achieve scale-free topology (R2 >0.8). A topological overlap matrix was constructed using weighted correlation coefficients raised to the β power, quantifying gene co-expression relationships. Hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree cut algorithm initially delineated gene modules, subsequently merged using a threshold of 0.25 for highly similar modules. Module Eigengenes (MEs) representing global expression patterns were calculated. Key modules exhibiting the highest correlation coefficients and significant associations with cancer tissues were identified, defined as WGCNA-colour modules. A module membership (MM)-gene significance (GS) scatter plot was generated to visualize gene-module relationships.

Core genes identification and functional enrichment analysis

Core genes were identified as the intersection of DEGs obtained from the preceding analyses, including GEO-DEGs, TCGA-DEGs, and Proteomic-DEPs, as well as key module genes (WGCNA color modules) identified through WGCNA. These core genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses to elucidate their molecular functions and pathways.

Signature genes screening and risk score model construction

Following the identification of the core genes associated with ccRCC development, the core genes were analyzed with LASSO-Cox regression to constructa risk score model. Firstly, LASSO regression was conducted through the glmnet package. The best penalty coefficient (lambda.min) was chosen using 10-fold cross-validation, keeping genes with regression coefficients that are not zero. The genes identified through LASSO regression were then included in a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Signature genes were those with a p-value under 0.05 and a hazard ratio (HR) 95% confidence interval (CI) that did not cover 1. The gene expression matrix (standardized expression values of the signature Genes) was integrated with clinical survival data (survival time and status). The combined dataset was split randomly into a training set, comprising 70% for model creation, and a validation set, making up 30% for separate validation. The prognostic risk score for each individual was derived using the formula Risk Score = Σ (Coefi × Expri), where Coefi corresponds to the Cox regression coefficient and Expri indicates the standardized gene expression. Based on the median value of the risk score, patients were categorized into high- and low-risk groups. Group stratification was visualized using risk score distributions and Kaplan-Meier survival curves, with statistical significance confirmed by a log-rank P<0.01. The concordance index (C-index), with >0.6 considered clinically significant, and model accuracy were assessed for the training and validation sets at 1, 3, and 5 years. Finally, we combined the signature genes with clinical factors (age and pathologic stage I–IV) and used the rms package to construct 1-, 3-, and 5-year nomograms that estimate patient survival probabilities for clinical application. The predictive accuracy of the nomogram was validated using calibration curves.

Differential expression of signature genes

Transcriptomic (TPM) data and clinical profiles for KIRC were retrieved from TCGA database. Differential expression analysis of signature genes were performed using the stats (version 4.2.1) and car (version 3.1-0) packages, including both paired (tumor vs. matched normal) and unpaired (tumor vs. normal samples) analyses. Subsequently, protein-level expression differences were analyzed in the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) dataset through the UALCAN online proteomics platform (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu).

Diagnostic and prognostic value and correlation analysis of clinical factors

Transcriptomic data and clinical metrics from TCGA-KIRC cohort were integrated to evaluate the clinical relevance and diagnostic-prognostic utility of signature genes. Differential expression across clinically stratified subgroups was assessed using stats and car packages, with results visualized via ggplot2. Diagnostic capacity was quantified through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis (pROC package) complemented by ggplot2 visualizations. Prognostic significance was concurrently evaluated for three endpoints: overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS). Statistical modeling was performed using the survival package for Cox regression and proportional hazards testing, with results visualized through the survminer and ggplot2 packages.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

To elucidate regulatory pathways of signature Genes in ccRCC, level 3 HTSeq-Counts RNA sequencing data from the TCGA-KIRC cohort were divided into high and low expression groups according to the expression levels of the target gene. Gene expression variance analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v17), followed by pathway enrichment investigation through clusterProfiler (v18-19). Significance thresholds for functional annotation were defined as adjusted p-value <0.05, false discovery rate (FDR) <0.25, and absolute normalized enrichment score (|NES|) >1, ensuring robust identification of biologically relevant pathways.

Immune infiltration analysis

The gene set variation analysis (GSVA) package (v1.46.0) implemented the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm to quantify immune cell infiltration in TCGA-KIRC cohort (24), with reference to 24 immune cell-specific marker genes curated by Bindea et al. (25). Spearman correlation analysis was subsequently performed to examine associations between immune infiltration levels and dichotomized expression subgroups (high/low) of signature genes. Significant correlations (P<0.05) were visualized through lollipop plots generated using ggplot2 package.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database

Levels of signature Genes-encoded proteins in RCC and normal kidney tissues were analyzed and compared using IHC data from the. HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org). The IHC scoring criteria were applied as follows: (I) staining intensity: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), 3 (strong); (II) percentage of positive cells: 0 (none), 1 (<25%), 2 (25–75%), 3 (>75%); (III) total IHC score: calculated by multiplying the intensity and percentage scores. Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Tissue collection and cell culture

A total of ten pairs of ccRCC and adjacent non-tumorous kidney tissues were sourced from The Second Hospital of Lanzhou University. Immediately after surgical resection, specimens were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen overnight, followed by long-term storage at −80 ℃ to maintain RNA integrity for subsequent molecular analyses. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University (Approval No. 2023A-096), and all patients provided written informed consent before tissue samples were collected. Human renal cancer cell lines (Caki-1 and Caki-2), the human embryonic kidney cell line (293T) and normal renal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) were generously provided by the Urology Research Laboratory at The Second Hospital of Lanzhou University. Caki-1 and Caki-2 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium (BasalMedia), while HK-2 and 293T cells were grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAN-Biotech GmbH) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from 10 paired ccRCC tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme). RNA concentration was quantified spectrophotometrically and normalized to 250 ng/µL. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed using the Vazyme kit, and the resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was subjected to quantitative PCR on the BIO-RAD CFX96 platform with SYBR Green fluorescence. Expression levels were normalized to β-actin and calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences (Table S1) were obtained from PrimerBank, and all statistical analyses and visualizations were carried out in GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Cell transfection

The Caki-1 cell line was used for lentiviral transfection. The HPGD overexpression construct and its corresponding negative control vector were purchased from Miaoling Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting the overexpression vector and packaging plasmids into 293T cells using polyethylenimine (PEI) MW40000 (Yeasen Biotechnology) as the transfection reagent. After 72 hours of puromycin (2 µg/mL) selection, stably transfected Caki-1 cells were obtained. The efficiency of transfection was confirmed by RT-qPCR. The lentiviral sequence used for HPGD overexpression is listed in Table S2.

Cell proliferation [Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)] and colony formation assays

For the CCK-8 assay, transfected Caki-1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2×103 cells per well in 200 µL of complete medium. At designated time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), the medium was replaced with 100 µL of fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent (Sunview, China). After 30 minutes of incubation at 37 ℃ in the dark, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). For colony formation assays, Caki-1 cells transfected with either vector or HPGD overexpression constructs were plated in 6-well plates (500 cells/well) and cultured for 10–14 days, with medium refreshed every 3–4 days. Colonies were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes, stained with 0.1% crystal violet for another 30 minutes, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), air-dried, and photographed. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted using ImageJ software. Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Wound healing assay

Caki-1 cells transfected with vector or HPGD overexpression constructs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4×105 cells/well and cultured overnight in complete medium at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 until confluence reached 100%. A straight-line scratch was made using a sterile 200 µL pipette tip to create a uniform wound. Detached cells were removed by washing with PBS three times, followed by the addition of serum-free medium. Images were taken at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope. Wound closure was quantified by measuring the migration area using ImageJ software.

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using Transwell inserts with an 8-µm pore polycarbonate membrane (Corning, USA). For the migration assay, 2×104 cells suspended in 100 µL of serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower compartment was filled with 600 µL of medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. In the invasion assay, the upper chamber was pre-coated with 80 µL of Matrigel (Corning), and 3×104 cells in 100 µL of serum-free medium were added. The lower chamber setup remained identical. After incubation at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 (24 hours for migration, 48 hours for invasion), the membranes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Cells that remained on the upper surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface were imaged using an inverted microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.2.1) was used for bioinformatics analyses, while statistical assessments were carried out using GraphPad Prism 9.0. All experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from three independent replicates. Statistical significance thresholds were defined as follows: *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001; ****, P<0.0001; ns (not significant) indicates P≥0.05.

WGCNA identifies MEbrown as the module most strongly associated with tumor samples in ccRCC

Based on the GSE46699 dataset, a WGCNA analysis was constructed, with tumor status in ccRCC used as the trait variable. After sample clustering and module detection, a sample dendrogram and trait heatmap were generated, showing a clear separation between tumor and normal samples (Figure 2A). A soft-thresholding power of β =9 was selected based on a scale-free topology fit index (R2 =0.9), ensuring that the network conformed to scale-free distribution characteristics (Figure 2B). Based on this parameter, a weighted co-expression network was built, and a gene dendrogram with module color assignment was generated (Figure 2C). Correlations between modules and the tumor trait were calculated and visualized in a module-trait correlation heatmap (Figure 2D). Among the 32 modules identified, MEbrown, MEred, and MEtan showed strong correlations with the tumor phenotype, with MEbrown showing the highest correlation (Cor =0.87, P=2e−40). Therefore, the 1,833 genes in the MEbrown module were selected as the WGCNA-MEbrown gene set for subsequent analysis. A strong correlation between MM and GS was observed in MEbrown, as shown in the scatter plot (Cor =0.96, P<1e−200) (Figure 2E).

Integration of multi-omics data and enrichment analyses identifies core genes related to metabolism in ccRCC

Integration of DEGs from GEO and TCGA, DEPs from proteomic datasets, and genes in the MEbrown module identified by WGCNA yielded 31 core genes potentially involved in ccRCC pathogenesis (Figure 3A). Functional enrichment through GO and KEGG revealed that the 31 genes are significantly associated with metabolic activity and membrane transport mechanisms (Figure 3B,3C). Specifically, they were enriched in (Figure 3B): (I) catabolic pathways of small molecules, carboxylic acids, and fructose (small molecule catabolic process, organic acid catabolic process, fructose metabolic process); (II) transmembrane transport of carboxylic acids and organic anions (carboxylic acid transmembrane transporter activity, organic anion transmembrane transporter activity); and (III) regulation of amino acid metabolism (amino acid metabolic process). Cellular localization indicated enrichment in apical cellular domains (apical plasma membrane) and peroxisomal subcompartments (peroxisomal matrix). Molecular functions involved oxidoreductase activity targeting CH-OH donor groups and transmembrane acid/anion transport. KEGG pathway analysis confirmed their roles in carbon metabolism, fructose/mannose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, and amino acid biosynthesis (Figure 3C).

Construction and validation of a four-gene prognostic signature and nomogram for ccRCC

To refine prognostic gene candidates for ccRCC, a LASSO-Cox regression model was constructed based on the 31 previously identified core genes. As shown in Figure 4A-4C, the LASSO-Cox model identified HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24 as signature genes with statistical significance (P<0.05). By integrating gene expression profiles with survival outcomes (including time and status), the dataset was randomly partitioned into a training set and a validation set in a 7:3 proportion for model development. The risk score for each patient was calculated based on the expression of four prognostic genes. Then divide the patients into high-risk group (red) and low-risk group (blue), with the median risk score as the threshold. A risk factor correlation graph (Figure 4D) was generated, showing that patients in the low-risk group had longer survival times.

Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated significantly worse survival in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (P<0.0001) (Figure 4E). To evaluate the predictive performance and generalizability of the risk score model, the C-index for 1-, 3-, and 5- year survival was calculated in both training (Figure 4F) and validation (Figure S2) sets. The C-indices stayed at clinically robust levels in both the training set (>0.791) and the validation set (>0.743), demonstrating that the prognostic model has strong discriminative power, good generalizability, and sound clinical utility.

Finally, using the four signature genes combined with age and pathologic stage (I–IV), we generated a nomogram to predict survival at 1, 3 and 5 years (Figure 4G). Calibration curves in both the training and validation sets showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed survival (Figure 4H,4I). To further evaluate the potential clinical utility of the prognostic nomogram, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed based on the nomogram-derived risk score in the TCGA cohort. As shown in Figure S3, the nomogram provided a higher net benefit than the treat-all or treat-none strategies across a wide range of threshold probabilities, indicating favorable clinical applicability for risk stratification in patients with ccRCC.

Concordant downregulation of the four signature genes in TCGA and CPTAC cohorts

Prognostic risk modeling identified a four-gene signature (HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24) with strong predictive value for ccRCC outcomes. Validation in the TCGA-KIRC cohort revealed significant dysregulation of these signature genes, evidenced by: (I) significantly downregulated mRNA levels in tumor vs. normal tissues (P<0.001, paired/unpaired analyses; Figure 5A,5B); and (II) concordant downregulation at the protein level in the CPTAC dataset (P<0.0001; Figure 5C-5F), aligning with our proteomic sequencing results. Collectively, these multi-platform analyses establish consistent downregulation of HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5 and SMIM24 across transcriptomic and proteomic levels in ccRCC.

Four signature genes exhibit distinct diagnostic and prognostic potential in ccRCC

Differential expression analysis confirmed the downregulation of HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24 in ccRCC. To assess their diagnostic and prognostic potential, we performed ROC curve analysis (Figure 6A) and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Figure 6B-6E). ROC analysis demonstrated diagnostic value for all four signature genes [area under the curve (AUC) >0.690], with HPGD and GLOD5 showing particularly high accuracy (AUC >0.9). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that higher expression of HPGD (HR =0.52, P<0.001), GLOD5 (HR =0.55, P<0.001), and SMIM24 (HR =0.46, P<0.01) was associated with improved OS. Conversely, elevated PSAT1 expression predicted poorer prognosis (HR =2.1, P<0.001). Therefore, HPGD, GLOD5 and SMIM24 constitute protective prognostic biomarkers, while all four signature genes show promise as diagnostic markers with PSAT1 serving as a notable risk indicator.

Given the diagnostic and prognostic relevance of HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24 in ccRCC, we further explored their associations with clinical parameters using TCGA-KIRC data. GSEA identifies distinct oncogenic and tumor-suppressive roles of the four signature genes in ccRCC

The expression of HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24 was significantly reduced in ccRCC, with notable correlations to prognosis and clinical parameters. GSEA was subsequently applied to explore their functional implications. GSEA revealed that all four genes modulate critical oncogenic pathways—E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) (Figure 8A-8D). Notably, HPGD, GLOD5, and SMIM24 exhibited negative associations with these pathways, consistent with tumor-suppressive functions, while PSAT1 showed positive associations, showing tumor-promoting activity. Further metabolic pathway analysis identified distinct signatures: all three suppressor genes (HPGD, GLOD5, SMIM24) positively associated with fatty acid metabolism (Figure 8A-8D), whereas PSAT1 correlated positively with cholesterol homeostasis (Figure 8B); both HPGD and PSAT1 additionally participated in glycolysis regulation (Figure 8A,8B). Together, these genes display opposing regulatory effects on tumor biology: HPGD, GLOD5, and SMIM24 likely inhibit tumor progression through pathway suppression, while PSAT1 may drive ccRCC development via pathway activation.

Immune infiltration profiles associated with the four signature genes in ccRCC

To investigate the roles of HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24 in the ccRCC immune microenvironment, we analyzed their correlations with immune cell subtypes (Figure 9A-9C). HPGD exhibited positive correlations with mast cells (R =0.348) and effector T cells (Tcm, Tem, Tgd; R =0.103–0.209), but negative correlation with regulatory T cells (Tregs; R =−0.176, P<0.05), demonstrating an immune-activating role that may enhance antitumor responses (Figure 9A). Conversely, PSAT1 positively correlated with T helper 2 (Th2) cells (R =0.338) and Tregs (R =0.210), while showing negative association with natural killer (NK) cell infiltration (R =−0.261, P<0.001), indicating immunosuppressive potential (Figure 9B). GLOD5 demonstrated dual regulation: it promoted Th17 cell infiltration (R =0.119) yet inhibited T helper cells (R =−0.167; P<0.01), implying complex modulation of T cell differentiation (Figure 9C). SMIM24, as an immunometabolic regulator, activated neutrophils (R =0.304) and Th17 cells (R =0.200), while suppressing Treg (R =−0.293) and Th2 cells (R=−0.313), supporting a potential antitumor role (Figure 9D). Notably, HPGD, GLOD5 and SMIM24 were all associated with reduced Treg infiltration. Although GLOD5’s correlation with Tregs did not reach statistical significance, a consistent suppressive trend was observed. In contrast, PSAT1 uniquely promoted Treg accumulation, indicating that metabolic reprogramming may differentially reshape immunosuppressive microenvironments through Treg regulation. The mechanisms mediating these effects require further investigation.

HPGD expression is reduced in ccRCC cells, while its overexpression suppresses cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

Among the four prognostic signature genes identified, HPGD attracted particular interest due to its established function in prostaglandin metabolism and reported tumor-suppressive effects in various cancers. However, its role in ccRCC remained unclear. To explore its biological relevance, we first examined HPGD mRNA expression in renal cancer cell lines (Caki-1 and Caki-2) and HK-2 cells using RT-qPCR. Both cancer cell lines showed lower HPGD expression relative to HK-2, with a statistically significant reduction in Caki-1 (P<0.05) (Figure 12A). Based on this result, Caki-1 was selected for functional experiments. A stable HPGD-overexpressing Caki-1 cell line was established via lentiviral transduction, and successful overexpression was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Figure 12B).

To evaluate the biological effects of HPGD overexpression on ccRCC cells, we conducted a series of in vitro functional assays. CCK-8 assays revealed that HPGD overexpression significantly inhibited cell proliferation over time compared to vector controls (Figure 12C). Similarly, colony formation assays demonstrated a marked reduction in colony-forming ability upon HPGD overexpression (Figure 12D,12E). Wound healing assays showed impaired migratory ability upon HPGD overexpression (Figure 12F, Figure S4A). Furthermore, Transwell assays confirmed that both migration and invasion capacities were significantly reduced in the HPGD-overexpressing group (P<0.01) (Figure 12G, Figure S4B,S4C). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that HPGD acts as a tumor suppressor in ccRCC by inhibiting proliferation, migration, and invasion in renal cancer cells.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated four signature genes—HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24—with strong diagnostic and prognostic potential for ccRCC. Through integrative multi-omics analysis combining GEO, TCGA, CPTAC, and in-house proteomic datasets, and using a rigorous bioinformatics pipeline involving WGCNA and LASSO-Cox regression, we successfully established a robust risk score model. This prognostic model distinguished between high- and low-risk patients with significant survival differences and showed robust accuracy (C-index >0.79). These findings suggest the model’s potential utility in clinical prognosis prediction.

Importantly, ccRCC is increasingly recognized as a metabolism-driven malignancy rather than a disease driven solely by classical oncogene addiction. Accumulating evidence indicates that ccRCC exhibits extensive metabolic reprogramming, including altered partitioning of glycolytic flux toward anabolic pathways (26-28), impaired mitochondrial bioenergetics and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (26,29), as well as profound lipid metabolic remodeling involving fatty acid utilization and cholesterol homeostasis (30-32). These metabolic features are now regarded as central drivers of ccRCC progression, therapy resistance, and immune modulation, positioning metabolic heterogeneity as a key determinant of clinical outcome (33-37).

Notably, all four signature genes showed consistent downregulation in ccRCC tissues across transcriptomic, proteomic, RT-qPCR, and IHC data, enhancing the reliability of our findings. Among the four genes, HPGD is a well-characterized tumor suppressor involved in prostaglandin degradation and has been shown to inhibit tumor growth and metastasis in lung cancer, colorectal cancer and breast cancer (22,38-41). In ccRCC, decreased HPGD expression was associated with poor survival and impaired immune response, particularly via suppression of effector T cell infiltration and mast cell recruitment. Similarly, GLOD5 and SMIM24 also showed favorable prognostic implications and were negatively correlated with regulatory T cell (Treg) and Th2 cell infiltration, indicating their potential roles in anti-tumor immunity.

Conversely, PSAT1 plays a critical role in the serine biosynthesis pathway and has been reported to promote proliferation and chemoresistance in multiple cancers, including hepatocellular and colorectal cancer (42-45). Especially in ccRCC, PSAT1 is not only associated with the prognosis of patients, but its upregulation can also promote the proliferation, metastasis, and resistance of tumors (46,47). Our analysis also revealed that PSAT1 expression was markedly increased in ccRCC patients with advanced clinical stage and poor survival, consistent with its pro-tumorigenic function. These functional differences were further supported by GSEA, which showed that HPGD, GLOD5, and SMIM24 were negatively enriched in key oncogenic pathways (e.g., E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, and EMT), while PSAT1 was positively enriched in these pathways, especially those involved in glycolysis and cholesterol homeostasis. These findings underscore the dual nature of the signature: a suppressor group (HPGD, GLOD5, SMIM24) and a promoter gene (PSAT1), reflecting complex metabolic and immune regulatory mechanisms in ccRCC.

Compared with previous prognostic models that lacked functional validation (48,49), our study incorporated multi-level evidence, including transcriptomic, proteomic, and histological confirmation, enhancing its translational relevance. Moreover, the strong association of the four genes with immune infiltration profiles—especially the inverse correlations with Tregs and NK cells—suggests that this signature may also serve as a potential immune stratifier or even as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy responsiveness. Such immune-metabolic interactions have become increasingly important in the context of RCC, where immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown durable clinical benefits (50,51). Although the prognostic model was constructed and internally validated using the TCGA-KIRC cohort, its robustness and biological relevance were further supported by independent protein-level and experimental validation, including proteomic data from the CPTAC database, IHC evidence from the Human HPA, and RT-qPCR validation in an independent set of paired ccRCC and adjacent normal tissue samples.

Beyond metabolic reprogramming, ccRCC is also recognized as one of the most immune-infiltrated solid tumors (52-54). Previous studies have demonstrated that the ccRCC tumor microenvironment is characterized by abundant immune cell infiltration and complex inflammatory signatures, which critically influence tumor progression and responses to systemic therapies (55-60). Emerging evidence indicates that specific metabolic pathways can actively regulate angiogenesis, inflammatory signaling, and immune cell recruitment, highlighting a tight interplay between tumor metabolism and immune regulation (61,62).

In this context, our immune infiltration analyses suggest that the four-gene signature may reflect distinct immunometabolic states in ccRCC. HPGD, GLOD5, and SMIM24 were generally associated with reduced regulatory T cell (Treg) and Th2 cell infiltration and increased effector immune cell presence, consistent with a more immune-permissive microenvironment. In contrast, PSAT1 expression correlated with increased Treg and Th2 infiltration and reduced NK cell abundance, indicating an immunosuppressive phenotype potentially driven by tumor-promoting metabolic programs. These findings align with emerging concepts that metabolic reprogramming can shape immune landscapes and inflammatory processes in ccRCC, thereby influencing disease biology and therapeutic responsiveness.

To further explore the tumor-suppressive role of HPGD, we performed in vitro functional experiments using Caki-1 cells, which exhibit low baseline expression of this gene. Lentiviral-mediated overexpression of HPGD significantly inhibited cell proliferation, colony formation, migration, and invasion, supporting its capacity to restrain malignant phenotypes in ccRCC. These results corroborate our transcriptomic and clinical analyses, and align with previous reports of HPGD’s antitumor activity in other malignancies. To our knowledge, our study provides additional functional evidence supporting the inhibitory role of HPGD in renal cancer cells. Collectively, these findings reinforce HPGD as a biologically meaningful prognostic marker and suggest that restoring HPGD-associated pathways may represent a potential therapeutic avenue that warrants further investigation. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying HPGD-mediated tumor suppression in ccRCC remain to be fully elucidated. Given the enzymatic role of HPGD in prostaglandin metabolism, future studies incorporating direct quantification of prostaglandins, as well as investigations into potential epigenetic regulation of HPGD expression, may provide deeper mechanistic insights.

Nonetheless, this study has several limitations. First, the RT-qPCR sample size was relatively small, and larger, multi-center cohorts are needed to confirm the generalizability of our findings. In addition, validation of the prognostic model in larger, independent prospective cohorts would further strengthen its clinical applicability and generalizability, and should be addressed in future studies. Second, although immune correlations were identified in silico, functional assays, including immune cell co-culture and in vivo mouse models, are required to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which these genes influence the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, while functional assays supported the tumor-suppressive role of HPGD, the downstream metabolic and epigenetic mechanisms were not directly investigated and warrant further exploration in future studies. Lastly, the roles of PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24 remain underexplored in ccRCC. Clarifying their functions in tumor biology and immune interactions will require future in vitro and in vivo validation studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified and validated a four-gene signature—HPGD, PSAT1, GLOD5, and SMIM24—with strong diagnostic and prognostic relevance in ccRCC. Integrative multi-omics analysis combined with clinical correlation and immune profiling revealed that these genes are closely linked to metabolic and immune regulatory pathways in the tumor microenvironment. Among them, HPGD was identified as a key tumor suppressor. In vitro functional assays demonstrated that HPGD overexpression significantly inhibited ccRCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings provide direct biological evidence of its antitumor activity and deepen our mechanistic understanding of its role in renal cancer. The prognostic model based on these genes demonstrated high predictive accuracy and may aid in individualized risk stratification. Collectively, our findings not only enhance understanding of ccRCC pathogenesis but also offer promising targets for prognostic evaluation and therapeutic intervention. Further studies are warranted to explore the underlying mechanisms and validate these biomarkers in larger, independent cohorts.
