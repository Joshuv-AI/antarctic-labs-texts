Ungluing the Mystery of Postoperative Ileus: Enteric Glial Connexin 43 Promotes the Development of Inflammation
Authors: Keith A. Sharkey
Published: 2026

Postoperative ileus is a transient interruption of normal gastrointestinal motility that occurs in patients after abdominal surgery, complicating recovery and causing significant morbidity.1,2 Initially, activation of mechanoreceptors and nociceptors stimulates neural reflexes, leading to acute inhibition of motility, mediated by sympathetic and vagal pathways to the gut. Though short-lived, the neurogenic phase of postoperative ileus initiates activation of immune mechanisms in the wall of the gut that produce the clinically more significant second phase of postoperative ileus, termed paralytic ileus.1,2 Here, the sustained inhibition of motility causes severe emesis, intestinal distension, and loss of bowel function, which leads to an intolerance to enteral nutrition and distress to patients.1,2 Understanding what triggers and sustains the immune mechanisms of paralytic ileus is crucial to gain insights into the cause of this condition, paving the way for the discovery of effective treatments, which are currently lacking.

Over 10 years ago, Stoffels et al3 made the remarkable discovery that interleukin (IL) 1 receptor and MyD88 signaling in enteric glia led to leukocyte recruitment, inflammation, and markedly slowed small bowel transit. The development of paralytic ileus and a neuroinflammatory state in the enteric nervous system is paralleled by a switch in enteric glial phenotype from a basal state to a reactive state. Reactive enteric gliosis is a complex dynamic state involving changes in morphology, and gene expression that occurs in a disease-specific fashion.4 In models of postoperative ileus, reactive enteric glia initially upregulate expression of a variety of proinflammatory genes (eg, Il6) and then many chemokine, and immune signaling genes, the best studied of which is Ccl2, because it is the most highly expressed.5 The triggers initiating reactive enteric gliosis include ATP release, that acts via the P2X2 receptor6 and β-adrenergic signaling mediated by the sympathetic innervation of the gut.7

Despite this progress, it remained to be determined how reactive enteric glia sustain the inflammatory state in paralytic ileus. In this issue of Cellular and Molecular Gastroenterology and Hepatology, Chaudhuri et al8 examined the molecular mechanisms by which enteric glia promote paralytic ileus and have discovered that connexin (Cx) 43 hemichannels mediate the sustained neuroinflammatory response. Cx43 hemichannels are membrane channels composed of 6 Cx43 proteins that connect the cell cytoplasm to the extracellular space, permitting the release of small and hydrophilic signaling molecules.9 They open in response to inflammation, mechanical stress, and other stressors and release mediators including ATP, prostaglandins, and cytokines, thereby exacerbating inflammation.9

The authors show that in a mouse model of postoperative ileus, and in surgical specimens from human patients, there is marked upregulation in connexin gene expression, with Cx43 being the most highly expressed. Using enteric glial-specific conditional Cx43 knockout mice, the authors compared knockout mice with their littermate controls and found that glial Cx43 knockout protects against the development of the reactive phenotype and inflammation in their model of postoperative ileus. Interestingly, in the Cx43 knockout mice, there was an upregulation of protective anti-inflammatory genes such as Il4 and Il10. Modeling this condition using human enteric glia treated with IL-1β, the authors showed that pharmacological blockade of Cx43 using a mimetic peptide could block the release of proinflammatory cytokines. They extended these in vitro studies using cultured human enteric glia to show that hemichannels were activated (demonstrated using dye uptake methods) by proinflammatory stimuli known to be important in the development of postoperative ileus and that blocking Cx43 attenuated gene expression changes caused by IL-1β. Recognizing that the translation of their findings was important, the authors then studied tissues from patients early and late during a pancreatectomy, using each patient as their own control. Using western blot, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and bulk RNA sequencing, they showed a marked induction of proinflammatory genes, notably those of the IL-1 signaling pathway, an enrichment in markers of reactive enteric gliosis, as well as the induction of connexins, with Cx43 shown to be induced at the protein level. Together, this extremely comprehensive study shows that enteric glial Cx43 signaling is a critical determinant of reactive enteric gliosis, intestinal inflammation, and immune cell regulation in a mouse model of postoperative ileus, reactive human enteric glia, and surgical specimens in humans.

An interesting future direction for this research is to explore the role of the gut microbiome in triggering activation of Cx43 hemichannels. Previous studies have shown that treatment with broad spectrum antibiotics attenuates the development of inflammation in the same mouse model of postoperative ileus used in the current study.10 Because bacterial lipopolysaccharide is known to trigger Cx43 channel activation,9 it may be that Cx43 hemichannels on enteric glia represent a common pathogenic mechanism of enteric neuromuscular dysfunction, and if so, this may help explain motility disturbances associated with microbial dysbiosis.

In summary, the exciting novel findings presented by Chaudhuri et al8 suggest that glial Cx43-dependent signaling represents a key pathogenic mechanism in paralytic ileus. Because mechanical stress and surgical trauma activates Cx43 signaling, then blocking it offers a new therapeutic approach to the treatment of this important iatrogenic disorder.
