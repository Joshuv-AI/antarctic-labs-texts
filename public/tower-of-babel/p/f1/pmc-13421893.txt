Molecular basis of single-mismatch-induced nuclease-to-nickase conversion in TIGR–TasH
Authors: Ruimin Zhou, Yancheng Zhan, Yangyue Sun, Runqin Wang, Tongyao Wang, Zhikun Liu, Zhengda Shan, Xuzichao Li, Shuqin Zhang, Nan Sun, Heng Zhang, Zhiyong Yuan, Jie Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTandem interspaced guide RNA (TIGR)–Tas systems are a distinct class of RNA-guided double-stranded DNA nucleases that employ dual-spacer guide RNAs (tigRNAs) for PAM-independent target recognition. A single mismatch between the tigRNA and target DNA can convert Salicola phage CGphi29 (Sp)TasH from a double-strand nuclease into a nickase in a position-dependent manner, but the molecular basis underlying this functional switch remains unknown. Here, we combined biochemical analyses and cryo-electron microscopy to investigate tigRNA maturation and mismatched target recognition by the Nop domain of SpTasH. We show that the Nop domain is required for pre-tigRNA processing and stabilizes the mature tigRNA through extensive interactions, thereby establishing a cleavage-competent ribonucleoprotein complex. Structural analyses of SpTasH complexes bound to substrates containing single mismatches reveal that a mismatch at the 5′-most position of spacer A is readily accommodated through Nop domain-mediated stabilization of the spacer–target heteroduplex. In contrast, a mismatch proximal to the cleavage site destabilizes the heteroduplex, preventing recruitment of the corresponding HNH domain, thereby converting the complex into a nickase. Together, these findings establish the structural basis for position-dependent mismatch recognition and reveal how Nop domain-mediated tigRNA-target stabilization enables differential responses to mismatches, providing a foundation for engineering TIGR–Tas systems for genome-editing applications.

Introduction

RNA-guided systems are ubiquitous across all domains of life and achieve sequence-specific recognition through base pairing between a guide RNA and its target nucleic acid, thereby enabling programmable binding, cleavage, or modification activities [1–3]. Among these, CRISPR-Cas systems provide adaptive immunity in prokaryotes by using CRISPR RNAs (crRNAs) to recognize invading nucleic acids [4–7]. For example, in type II CRISPR–Cas systems, the crRNA:trans-activating CRISPR RNA (tracrRNA) complex associates with the Cas9 effector through repeat-derived sequences, while the spacer region of the crRNA base pairs with one strand of the target DNA sequence [8–11].

An emerging class of RNA-guided systems, TIGR–Tas systems, encode a Tas protein together with an array of tigRNAs. Based on the N-terminal architecture, Tas proteins are classified into the TasA, TasH, and TasR subtypes [12, 13]. TasH and TasR contain N-terminal HNH and RuvC nuclease domains, respectively, whereas TasA lacks such domains [13]. All Tas proteins dimerize through a central coiled-coil (CC) domain and share a C-terminal Nop domain homologous to the eukaryotic Nop56/Nop58 and archaeal Nop5 proteins, which assemble with box C/D small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) into ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes involved in rRNA processing and modification [14–16]. The Tas dimer binds a tigRNA containing conserved box C/D motifs arranged similarly to those of box C/D snoRNAs. Moreover, the Nop domain also mediates tigRNA maturation by processing the TIGR precursor transcript, either autonomously or with the assistance of host factors. For example, TasH from Salicola phage CGphi29 (SpTasH) autonomously processes tigRNAs through catalytic residues located within its Nop domain [13, 17].

The conserved box C/D motifs of the tigRNA not only mediate assembly of the Tas RNP complex but also flank two spacer regions that direct sequence-specific recognition of dsDNA targets [17]. In contrast to the single-strand base pair strategy employed by CRISPR systems, TIGR–Tas systems use a dual-spacer tigRNA, extensively coordinated by the Nop domain, to simultaneously recognize consecutive target sequences on opposite strands of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) [13, 17, 18]. This architecture establishes a unique mechanism for target search, recognition, and mismatch surveillance [13, 17, 19]. Consistent with this model, previous studies have shown that the effect of a single spacer–target mismatch on TIGR–Tas cleavage activity depends on its position and sequence context, a phenomenon also observed in CRISPR–Cas9 systems [13, 17, 20]. However, the molecular mechanism by which target mismatches influence cleavage activity remains unknown [13].

In this study, we combined mutational analysis, biochemical assays, and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structural analysis to investigate the two functions of the Nop domain: tigRNA maturation and mismatch surveillance. Our findings reveal the molecular basis of tigRNA processing and explain how mismatches within the RNA–DNA heteroduplex regulate the cleavage activity of SpTasH, providing a mechanistic foundation for the future engineering and application of TIGR–Tas systems.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

The tasH gene from Salicola phage CGphi29 (SpTasH; accession NC_020844) was polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-amplified and cloned into a T7 expression vector with a C-terminal TwinStrep-His tag. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange kit (Takara). tigRNA arrays comprising three identical repeat-spacer units were assembled by Golden Gate cloning and expressed under the constitutive J23119 promoter with an rrnB1 terminator. For RNP production, the SpTasH-TwinStrep-His cassette and its cognate tigRNA array were co-cloned into the pCDF-Duet vector via In-Fusion cloning, generating a dual-expression construct for coordinated protein–RNA assembly and purification.

Protein expression and purification

A plasmid encoding wild-type SpTasH or its mutant variants together with a 3× tigRNA array was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3). A single colony was grown overnight in 5 ml LB at 37°C, then transferred into 1 l LB and cultured at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside, and the culture was continued at 18°C for 16 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol). Cells were lysed on ice by sonication (250 W, 1 s on/3 s off, 10 min total). After centrifugation at 23 000 × g for 40 min at 4°C, the supernatant was applied to a Strep-Tactin affinity column. The column was washed with 10 column volumes of lysis buffer, and bound protein was eluted with buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM d-biotin, and 3 mM β-mercaptoethanol.

Eluted fractions were further purified by heparin affinity chromatography using a linear NaCl gradient (150–750 mM) in 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5) and 2 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). Fractions containing the SpTasH RNP were pooled and subjected to size-exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad Superdex 200 Increase column equilibrated in 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 250 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT. Protein purity and nucleotide binding were confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and urea–PAGE, respectively. Verified fractions were combined and concentrated for cryo-EM analysis, or flash-frozen and stored at −80°C in size-exclusion chromatography buffer supplemented with 20% glycerol. Apo SpTasH protein was purified following the same procedure.

Nucleic acid substrate preparation

To evaluate SpTasH nuclease activity, both fluorescently labeled and unlabeled ssDNA and ssRNA oligonucleotides were synthesized (Sangon). The 38-bp dsDNA substrate was generated by annealing the top and bottom ssDNA oligos labeled with 5′-Cy3 and 5′-Cy5 through denaturing at 95°C for 5 min, followed by slowly cooling down to 25°C at a rate of −0.1°C/s in a thermocycler. The 194-bp dsDNA substrate was generated by PCR using labeled primer pairs and a plasmid template as previously described [17]. PCR products were purified using spin columns and handled on ice or at 4°C, except during elution. Quality was confirmed by NanoDrop and 2% agarose gel electrophoresis.

dsDNA substrate cleavage assay

Wild-type or mutant SpTasH RNPs (500 nM) were incubated with fluorescent dsDNA substrates (50 nM) in cleavage buffer (25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 2 mM DTT, 0.1% bovine serum albumin, 5 mM MgCl2) at 37°C for 1 h, unless otherwise specified. Reactions were terminated by adding proteinase K and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min and heat inactivation at 95°C for 5 min. RNase A was added prior to quenching to remove tigRNA-associated cleavage products. Samples were mixed with 2× denaturing loading buffer (90% formamide, 50 mM EDTA, 0.01% bromophenol blue), heated to 95°C for 5 min, and cooled on ice. Products were resolved by denaturing urea-PAGE (10%) in 1× Tris-Boric Acid-EDTA (TBE) at 300 V for 30 min and visualized using a gel imaging system (Azure).

Pre-tigRNA processing assay

Modified pre-tigRNAs were purchased from Sangon. The pre-tigRNA processing assays were performed by incubating 50 nM various pre-tigRNAs and 1 μM Apo SpTasH for 2 h at 37°C in reaction buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT. The reactions were terminated by proteinase K treatment and resolved on 15% denaturing urea–PAGE gels.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and imaging

For both the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA and SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complexes, purified samples were diluted to 0.25 mg/ml, and 3.5 μl of each was applied to glow-discharged Au R1.2/1.3 holey carbon grids (300 mesh, Quantifoil). After a 5-s incubation, the grids were blotted for 2 s with a blot force of 2 and plunge-frozen into liquid ethane pre-cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI Thermo Fisher) at 4°C and 100% humidity.

Cryo-EM data of both SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA and SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complexes were collected on a Titan Krios G4 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 300 kV equipped with a Falcon 4 direct electron detector. Cryo-EM images were acquired at a nominal magnification of 165 000×, corresponding to a calibrated physical pixel size of 0.692 Å/pixel. Images were recorded using EPU (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a defocus range of −1.2 to −1.6 μm [21]. Each micrograph was dose-fractioned into 32 frames, with a total electron dose of ∼50 e−/Å2 per micrograph.

Cryo-EM image processing

For the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA complex dataset, 2257 micrographs were collected and processed in CryoSPARC [22]. Beam-induced motion was corrected using MotionCor2 [23], and the contrast transfer function (CTF) was estimated. Micrographs with an estimated CTF resolution below 5 Å were discarded, leaving 2196 for further analysis. Particle picking was initially performed on several hundred micrographs using blob picking, with particles ranging in diameter from 128 to 256 Å. 2D classification was then conducted to remove junk particles. The retained high-quality particles were used to train a Topaz model [24]. Using the trained model, particles were picked and extracted with a box size of 320 pixels, which was Fourier-cropped to 80 pixels (bin4), yielding 317 411 particles. Several rounds of 2D classification were performed to further clean the particle stack. Ab initio reconstruction was used to generate initial models, followed by heterogeneous refinement to improve structural features. Particles belonging to one promising class were re-extracted using 320 pixels (unbinned). Non-uniform refinement was conducted to improve the quality of this volume, resulting in a final reconstruction at 3.04 Å resolution.

For the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex dataset, 2098 micrographs were collected and processed following the same workflow described above. In addition to Topaz picking, template-based particle picking was also performed. Particles obtained from both approaches were combined, and duplicate particles were removed, yielding a total of 750 037 particles. Ab initio reconstruction generated initial volumes, followed by heterogeneous refinement to enhance map features. The best-resolved class was selected for non-uniform refinement, yielding a final reconstruction at 3.02 Å resolution.

Model building and refinement

The initial atomic model of the SpTasH protein predicted by AlphaFold3 [25] was manually divided into several segments, which were individually fitted into the cryo-EM density map using ChimeraX [26] and subsequently connected into a single polypeptide chain. Copies of this model were positioned and manually refined to trace the density in Coot [27]. The tigRNA and dsDNA were manually built de novo into the remaining density. Interactive manual adjustments were performed for both proteins and nucleic acid components to ensure correct geometries. The models were further refined using phenix.real_space_refine [28]. The quality of both final models was validated using MolProbity [29] in the Phenix suite. Data collection and model refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Results

The Nop domain of SpTasH mediates tigRNA processing

Similar to cas genes, which are located adjacent to CRISPR arrays, tas genes are found in proximity to TIGR arrays. Likewise, precursor transcripts from TIGR arrays are processed into mature tigRNAs through Tas-mediated processing, either independently or with the aid of host factors. For example, SpTasH autonomously processes precursor tigRNA (pre-tigRNA) into mature tigRNA in a metal-independent manner. Two residues within its flexible C-terminal region, Y289 and H296, have been identified as the catalytic residues responsible for SpTasH-mediated pre-tigRNA maturation [17].

Based on the structure of the SpTasH RNP complex (PDB: 9W03), several residues within the Nop domain are positioned around the 5′ and 3′ ends of the tigRNA and are therefore candidates for mediating tigRNA binding or processing, including K167, K180, H182, K233, H268, R278, R286, E292, K293, and R305 (Fig. 1A–C). To evaluate their roles in tigRNA maturation, we purified wild-type and alanine-substitution mutant apo SpTasH proteins and examined the precursor tigRNA (pre-T1) processing activity (Fig. 1D and E). A mutation on the catalytic residue H296 was also tested in parallel as a control. With the exception of K167A, all mutants exhibited markedly reduced pre-tigRNA processing activity, as observed in catalytically inactive H296A, indicating that these Nop domain residues are essential for tigRNA processing. Given their locations surrounding the tigRNA termini, these residues are likely to promote processing by facilitating tigRNA binding and stabilization rather than by directly participating in catalysis at the scissile phosphate of the edge repeat (Fig. 1E).

(D) Schematics showing fluorescently labeled or phosphorothioate (PS)-modified pre-tigRNA used in panels (E) and (F). (E) Cleavage assay evaluating the effects of Nop domain residues of SpTasH involved in pre-tigRNA processing. (F) Effect of PS-modified pre-tigRNA on SpTasH-mediated substrate cleavage. For panels (E) and (F), the gels are representative of three independent replicates.

To determine whether unprocessed pre-tigRNA affects substrate cleavage, we employed PS-modified pre-tigRNAs in in vitro dsDNA cleavage assays. Four pre-tigRNA substrates carrying PS modifications at different positions, located either at, upstream, or downstream of the scissile phosphate, were designed to block precursor processing (Fig. 1D). These pre-tigRNAs were incubated with SpTasH to assemble RNP complexes, and their dsDNA cleavage activities were compared with those of RNP complexes assembled using the mature tigRNA. PS modification substantially impaired dsDNA cleavage relative to the mature tigRNA-assembled RNP complex (Fig. 1D and F). Although we cannot completely exclude the possibility that steric hindrance introduced by the unprocessed RNA termini directly interferes with substrate binding and cleavage, these findings support a requirement for tigRNA maturation before efficient target DNA cleavage can occur. Together, these results indicate that Nop domain-mediated tigRNA maturation is essential for efficient target DNA cleavage.

Effects of single mismatches in the target dsDNA on SpTasH–tigRNA RNP activity

SpTasH was co-expressed with a 3× tigRNA array in E. coli BL21 (DE3), and the resulting SpTasH–tigRNA RNP complex was purified by affinity chromatography followed by size-exclusion chromatography (Fig. 2A and B). Based on its elution volume relative to molecular weight standards, the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP complex most likely exists as a dimer in solution (Fig. 2B). Peak fractions were analyzed by SDS–PAGE and denaturing urea–PAGE, confirming the purity of SpTasH and the presence of the mature tigRNA (Fig. 2B).

The boxed inset illustrates the architecture of a single tigRNA, comprising the left edge repeat, spacer A, loop repeat, spacer B, and right edge repeat from 5′ to 3′. (B) Size-exclusion chromatography of SpTasH. The elution volumes and molecular weights of the protein standards are indicated. (D) Time-course cleavage of PM, MM1, and MM5 dsDNA substrates over 60 min. Representative gels from three independent replicates are shown. (E) Quantification of the time-course cleavage assays shown in panel (D). Cleavage efficiency was calculated as the normalized intensity of the cleavage products divided by the total intensity (substrate plus cleavage products) within the same lane. We previously showed that a single mismatch between the spacer and target is sufficient to alter SpTasH–tigRNA RNP-mediated cleavage activity [17]. To further assess the effects of single mismatches, we performed 1-h cleavage time-course assays using PM, MM1 and MM5 dsDNA substrates, representing a perfectly matched substrate and substrates containing mismatches at positions 1 and 5 of spacer A, respectively (Fig. 2C–E).

The cleavage assays showed that the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP cleaved both target strands A and B of the PM substrate, with cleavage products accumulating over time. In contrast, when incubated with the MM5 substrate, only target strand B was cleaved. The substrate was progressively consumed, whereas the cleavage product accumulated. Cleavage of target strand A was undetectable, indicating that a single mismatch between spacer A and target strand A effectively converts the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP into a nickase (Fig. 2D and E). A similar mismatch-dependent nickase activity has been reported for the TIGR–TasR system [13]. By contrast, the MM1 substrate had only a minimal effect on the DNA cleavage activity of SpTasH–tigRNA RNP relative to the PM substrate. Both target strands remained susceptible to cleavage, with only a modest reduction in cleavage efficiency (Fig. 2D and E). Together, these results indicate that single mismatches within the spacer–target pairing region differentially affect substrate DNA cleavage (Fig. 2C–E). However, the molecular basis underlying these position-dependent effects remains unclear.

Cryo-EM structure of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA complex

To investigate the structural basis underlying the mismatch-dependent DNA cleavage activity of the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP, MM1 and MM5 dsDNA substrates were individually incubated with purified SpTasH–tigRNA RNP at 37°C for 1 h to assemble the corresponding ternary complexes (Supplementary Figs S1–S3). We first determined the cryo-EM structure of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 complex at a resolution of 3.04 Å (Fig. 3A and B, and Supplementary Figs S1 and S3A–E).

Cryo-EM structure of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA complex. Overall cryo-EM structure (A) and corresponding cryo-EM density map (B) of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA complex. (C) Schematic representation of the tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA duplex.

The cryo-EM reconstruction revealed a ternary complex comprising a SpTasH homodimer, one tigRNA, and one MM1 dsDNA substrate (Fig. 3A and B). Each SpTasH protomer comprises three distinct domains: a C-terminal Nop domain, a CC domain responsible for dimerization, and an HNH nuclease domain. The tigRNA spans both SpTasH protomers and interacts with the Nop and CC domains (Fig. 3A). The dsDNA is stably accommodated within the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP, with nucleotides from position −9 to +23 clearly visible in the resolved structure (Fig. 3C). Together, these results indicate that the MM1 mismatch does not significantly disrupt the overall positioning of the substrate or the architecture of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 ternary complex, thereby permitting productive recruitment and activation of both HNH domains.

Structural basis for mismatch accommodation at the first position of the spacer A–target heteroduplex

The A1T transversion in the MM1 dsDNA substrate introduces a single mismatch between spacer A and target strand A (Fig. 3C). Quantitative analysis showed that SpTasH–tigRNA RNP exhibited only a modest reduction in cleavage efficiency toward the MM1 substrate relative to the PM substrate during reactions lasting 20–60 min (Fig. 2D and E). To elucidate how the MM1 mismatch is accommodated by the RNP, we compared the structures of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 and SpTasH–tigRNA–PM (PDB: 9W04) ternary complexes. Structural superposition showed that the two complexes adopt highly similar overall architectures (Fig. 4A).

Structural basis for MM1 mismatch accommodation by TasH. (A) Structural alignment of the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex (PDB: 9W04) and SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA complex. Close-up views of the mismatch region between spacer A and target strand A (B), conformational changes in DNA-binding residues surrounding the mismatch (C), and active-site residues involved in substrate binding and cleavage (D) in the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM1 dsDNA complex compared with the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex.

A close-up view of the mismatch region showed that the U-T mismatch at the edge of spacer A is accommodated without causing significant conformational changes to the duplex structure, and is stabilized primarily through π–π stacking interactions with neighboring bases (Fig. 4B). Consistent with this observation, residues involved in substrate recognition and catalysis adopt conformations nearly identical to those in the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM complex, with no obvious side-chain rearrangements or perturbations of the protein–nucleic acid interface or catalytic center (Fig. 4C and D). Collectively, these results indicate that SpTasH–tigRNA RNP accommodates the MM1 mismatch by maintaining π–π stacking interactions within the RNA–DNA duplex, thereby preserving both the overall architecture of the ternary complex and the organization of the catalytic center. These structural features likely explain why the MM1 substrate is cleaved with an efficiency comparable to that of the PM substrate.

Cryo-EM structure of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex

To elucidate the structural basis of the mismatch-induced nickase activity, we determined the cryo-EM structure of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex at 3.02 Å resolution (Fig. 5A and B, and Supplementary Figs S2 and S3F–I). Notably, the HNH domains of both SpTasH protomers were not resolved, and most of the MM5 dsDNA was not visible, likely due to structural flexibility (Fig. 5A and B). Only the duplexed region adjacent to spacer B and an adjacent short unpaired segment could be modeled (Fig. 5A and B).

Cryo-EM structure of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex. Overall cryo-EM structure (A) and corresponding cryo-EM density map (B) of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex. (C) Schematic representation of the tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA heteroduplex.

The MM5 dsDNA substrate carries a C5G transversion that creates a single mismatch between spacer A and target strand A (Fig. 5C). This mismatch is likely to destabilize the RNA–DNA hybrid, thereby impairing complete R-loop formation, consistent with the absence of ordered HNH domains in the cryo-EM reconstruction.

Intermediate conformational state of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex

Compared with the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP, the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex exhibits inward shifts of both Nop domains, accompanied by lateral expansion of the tigRNA and outward movement of the two CC domains. However, these conformational changes are less pronounced than those observed in the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex (Fig. 6A). Together, these observations suggest that the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex represents a conformational intermediate between the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP and the fully engaged SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex.

(A) Structural alignment of the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex, the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex, and the SpTasH–tigRNA complex. (B) Conformations of the α4-helix residues W203 and K211 of the Nop domain in the SpTasH–tigRNA, SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA, and SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complexes. (C) Resolved dsDNA segment in the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex. (D) Residues involved in HNH domain recruitment and the corresponding interacting DNA nucleotides in the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex.

Inspection of the Nop domains revealed asymmetric conformational changes in key residues that distinguish this intermediate state (Fig. 6B). Residue W203 has been reported to stabilize the heteroduplex, whereas K211 inserts into the dsDNA, inducing local DNA unwinding [17]. In the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex, the α-helix harboring W203 and K211 in Nop A adopts a conformation resembling that observed in the PM-bound state, whereas the corresponding helix in Nop B adopts a conformation resembling that in the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP complex (Fig. 6B).

This asymmetrical conformational difference arises from the C5G mutation, which introduces a mismatch between spacer A and target strand A and thereby disrupts RNA–DNA base pairing. As a result, complete R-loop formation is impaired, rendering the upstream DNA region (positions +7 to −11 of target strand B and +13 to −11 of target strand A) flexible and unresolved in the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA structure (Fig. 6C). Consequently, the β-hairpin and wedge loop of the two Nop domains fail to approach one another, preventing closure of the substrate-binding groove (Fig. 6C and D, and Supplementary Fig. S4). This, in turn, prevents stable binding of the target DNA and proper positioning of the HNH B domain for catalytic activation.

Retained spacer B pairing enables HNH A recruitment

In the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex, the spacer B-target strand B duplex remains intact and is stably accommodated within the complex. This duplex is contiguous with the adjacent DNA duplex terminus (dT17’:dA17-dA23’:dT23), from which the opposite strand extends into a 3′ unpaired segment (dC16-dG14). Both the duplex terminus and the 3′ unpaired segment are coordinated by Nop B, where the β-hairpin and wedge loop together form a docking interface for HNH A recruitment and positioning. Although this region is not resolved in the cryo-EM map, comparison with the fully matched PM-bound structure suggests that nucleotides dA11 and dG12 within the displaced 3′ unpaired segment, together with β-hairpin residues T240 and Q241, are likely to form a platform for HNH A engagement (Fig. 6D).

By contrast, a mismatch between spacer A and target strand A disrupts RNA–DNA base pairing and precludes formation of the partial R-loop on this side, thereby impairing stable substrate accommodation within the complex and preventing effective recruitment of the HNH B domain. As a result, HNH A can still be recruited, whereas HNH B remains inactive, leading to asymmetric activation of the two HNH domains. This asymmetry provides the structural basis for the observed nickase activity, in which cleavage is restricted to target strand B, albeit with reduced efficiency relative to the fully matched PM dsDNA substrate (Fig. 2D and E).

Consistent with this interpretation, electrostatic surface representations further illustrate that the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP adopts an open conformation, the PM dsDNA-bound complex adopts a closed conformation, and the MM5 dsDNA-bound complex is captured in an intermediate state between the two (Fig. 7A). Together, these findings support a model in which SpTasH cleavage activity is governed by a conformational checkpoint determined by the stability of spacer–target pairing. Formation of a fully matched RNA–DNA heteroduplex drives productive assembly of the target-bound complex, induces coordinated conformational rearrangements in both Nop domains, and stably positions the dsDNA substrate. This conformational transition, in turn, enables recruitment and activation of both HNH domains, thereby licensing double-strand cleavage.

Mechanism of mismatch-induced nuclease-to-nickase conversion in SpTasH. (A) Surface electrostatic potential of SpTasH core complex lacking the HNH domain in three structural states: the SpTasH–tigRNA RNP complex (open state), the SpTasH–tigRNA–MM5 dsDNA complex (intermediate state), and the SpTasH–tigRNA–PM dsDNA complex (closed state). (B) Proposed model illustrating the mechanism of mismatch-induced nuclease-to-nickase conversion in SpTasH. Complete spacer–target base pairing stabilizes the cleavage-competent conformation and enables recruitment of both HNH domains for double-strand DNA cleavage. In contrast, a cleavage site-proximal mismatch destabilizes the spacer–target heteroduplex, trapping the complex in an intermediate conformation that favors asymmetric HNH domain recruitment and results in nickase activity.

Collectively, a mismatch at the first nucleotide of spacer A is accommodated without disrupting the formation of a cleavage-competent complex. In contrast, a mismatch at the fifth nucleotide of spacer A severely hinders the transition to the cleavage-competent state, trapping the complex in an intermediate conformational state and preventing HNH B-mediated cleavage of target strand A. As a result, SpTasH is converted from a double-strand endonuclease into a nickase (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

In the current study, we combined biochemical and structural analyses to uncover two functions of the Nop domain: tigRNA maturation and target mismatch surveillance. Biochemical analyses showed that the Nop domain of SpTasH not only processes pre-tigRNA, as previously reported [17], but also promotes tigRNA binding. Residues surrounding the tigRNA repeat region facilitate precursor binding and stabilization (Fig. 1E). Cryo-EM structures reveal how mismatches at distinct positions are accommodated by the Nop domain, providing mechanistic insights into mismatch surveillance. Collectively, these findings identify the Nop domain as a multifunctional domain that mediates both tigRNA maturation and target mismatch recognition, supporting a modular organization of the TIGR–Tas effector.

RNA-guided systems mediate diverse biological processes in prokaryotes, including adaptive immunity and transposition [2, 5, 30]. Their programmability has enabled the development of versatile technologies for genome editing, RNA manipulation, and molecular diagnostics, exemplified by CRISPR–Cas systems [5, 30–32]. A conserved feature of many RNA-guided systems is the requirement for guide RNA maturation before effector activation. Similar to CRISPR–Cas systems, TIGR–Tas systems require processing of precursor guide RNAs to generate mature tigRNAs [13, 17]. In CRISPR–Cas systems, this maturation can be achieved through host-assisted processing. For example, the host RNase III processes the crRNA of Cas9 systems in the presence of a tracrRNA [33], whereas most Cas12 and Cas13 systems process their own pre-crRNA autonomously [34, 35].

Guide RNA maturation therefore represents a conserved strategy for controlling the activity of RNA-guided effectors [36]. Although the mechanism of TIGR array acquisition remains unknown, our findings establish tigRNA maturation as an essential step in TIGR–Tas function [17]. Autonomous guide RNA processing has evolved through distinct catalytic architectures in CRISPR systems. Compact effectors, including TnpB and Cas13an, the ancestors of Cas12 and Cas13 proteins, process pre-crRNAs and cleave target nucleic acids using a single active site [37, 38], whereas larger effectors such as Cas12 and Cas13 employ separate active sites for the two activities. In contrast, despite its compact architecture, SpTasH also utilizes distinct catalytic centers for tigRNA maturation and target cleavage. This finding demonstrates that compact RNA-guided effectors can evolve separated RNA-processing and target-cleavage activities, expanding the known catalytic architectures of RNA-guided systems.

Our structural and biochemical analyses suggest that TIGR–Tas systems discriminate target mismatch through a distinct strategy from CRISPR–Cas systems. Off-target editing arising from the tolerance of imperfect guide RNA–target pairing is a major challenge limiting the therapeutic application of CRISPR–Cas systems. In Cas9, mismatch discrimination is primarily mediated by the REC lobe, which monitors the conformation of the guide RNA–DNA heteroduplex [39, 40]. In Cas9, R-loop formation is initiated within the PAM-proximal seed region, making mismatches in this region particularly detrimental to target recognition and cleavage, whereas PAM-distal mismatches are more readily accommodated owing to the structural plasticity of the Cas9-guide RNA complex [20, 40]. By contrast, our structural and biochemical analyses suggest that TIGR–Tas adopts a distinct pathway for target recognition and RNA–DNA annealing. Although MM1, located near the duplex terminus, was well tolerated, mismatches near the center of each RNA–DNA duplex half (MM5) markedly impaired cleavage activity, suggesting that the central region of each spacer–target duplex may function as an independent seed region.

We therefore propose that RNA–DNA annealing is initiated around the central region of each spacer, where productive base pairing nucleates R-loop formation. Under this model, RNA–DNA pairing would then propagate toward both ends of the duplex. Accordingly, mismatches within these putative seed regions would disrupt R-loop nucleation on the corresponding strand, whereas mismatches located near the duplex termini would be expected to have a smaller impact once stable RNA–DNA pairing has been established. This model is consistent with the pronounced nickase activity observed for the MM5 substrate, in which disruption of one putative seed region selectively impairs cleavage of the corresponding DNA strand while permitting cleavage of the fully paired strand. Conversely, the relatively high tolerance of MM1 may reflect the reduced contribution of terminal base pairs to the initiation of RNA–DNA annealing.

Although additional biochemical and kinetic studies will be required to directly define the pathway of R-loop formation, our findings support a model in which the two spacer–target duplexes behave as semi-independent recognition modules. Such an organization may provide an intrinsic mechanism for regulating double-strand break formation, as incomplete R-loop formation on one side could still permit cleavage of the opposite strand. This mechanism distinguishes TIGR–Tas from canonical CRISPR–Cas systems and suggests opportunities for engineering its cleavage properties. In particular, residues involved in stabilizing the RNA–DNA duplex around the proposed seed regions may represent attractive targets for tuning mismatch discrimination, whereas selective modulation of strand-specific R-loop formation could provide a rational strategy for developing TIGR–Tas variants with enhanced nickase activity or improved target specificity.
