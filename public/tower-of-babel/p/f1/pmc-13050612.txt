Persistent Hyperglycaemia and Transient Dysglycaemia in Tuberculosis: Evaluating the Bidirectional Interaction With Type‐2 Diabetes Mellitus and Clinical Outcomes
Authors: Augustine Asare Boadu, Prince Asare, Michelle Yeboah‐Manu, Stephen Osei‐Wusu, Emelia Danso Konadu, Adwoa Asante‐Poku, Yayra Klinogo, Abraham Adjei, Desmond Omane Acheampong, Jane S. Afriyie‐Mensah, Dorothy Yeboah‐Manu, Yacoba Atiase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPoor glycaemic control, indicated by a haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level of 7% or higher, increases the risk of active tuberculosis (TB) in patients with diabetes, while active TB can worsen glucose tolerance and insulin resistance, contributing to type‐2 diabetes mellitus (DM) progression. The bidirectional relationship between TB and DM is well recognised, but the evolution of glycaemic status during TB treatment, particularly the distinction between transient and persistent dysglycaemia, is not well understood in high‐burden settings.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study of 120 newly diagnosed pulmonary TB patients in Ghana, categorised as TB‐Only (n = 66), TB with established DM on metformin (TB‐DMt, n = 39), and TB with dysglycaemia not requiring pharmacological therapy (TB‐DMnt, n = 15). HbA1c was measured at baseline, 3, 6 and 9 months.ResultsTB‐DMt patients were older, had higher BMI and persistently elevated HbA1c (p < 0.001). In contrast, TB‐DMnt patients showed significant HbA1c decline during and after TB treatment, normalising by 6 months (p < 0.016). Most TB‐Only patients maintained stable euglycaemia. Transient dysglycaemia resolved in TB‐DMnt, while persistent hyperglycaemia was confined to those with pre‐existing DM (TB‐DMt).ConclusionRoutine HbA1c monitoring during TB therapy can distinguish transient dysglycaemia from true diabetes, supporting more precise risk stratification and tailored management of TB patients.

Introduction

The intersection of tuberculosis (TB) and type‐2 diabetes mellitus (DM) represents a critical global health challenge, particularly in regions burdened by both diseases. The altered metabolic environment of DM compromises host immunity against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), facilitating TB reactivation and progression (Figure 1) [1]. Sustained hyperglycaemia contributes to immunopathy in TB patients by impairing immune responses, promoting inflammation, increasing oxidative stress, altering cytokine profiles and enhancing bacterial virulence [2, 3, 4].

Interconnected mechanisms in the tuberculosis‐diabetes mellitus, glycated haemoglobin pathology. Pathophysiological effects of diabetes mellitus and persistent high blood glucose (HBG) to tuberculosis pathology. Persistent HBG can (1) increased the release of AGE and RAGE, which leads to the activation of the polyol pathway; (2) reduced intake of NADPH amidst increased release of reactive oxygen species; (3) increased sorbitol activity, activation of NF‐kB, reduced NO signalling (4) leading to increased protein glycation and oxidation; The consequences of these events are impairment of the biological metabolome leading to compromised immunity, making such individuals vulnerable to opportunistic infections such as tuberculosis.

Conversely, active TB disrupts glucose metabolism through systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, creating a bidirectional relationship where each condition potentiates the other [5, 6, 7]. This vicious cycle is particularly evident in sub‐Saharan Africa, where rising DM prevalence threatens to undermine decades of TB control progress [1, 8].

Haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), a cornerstone biomarker for long‐term glycaemic control, plays a dual role in this context. While essential for diabetes management [8, 9, 10]. Its dynamics in TB‐DM comorbidity remain poorly characterised. Emerging evidence suggests TB infection may induce chronic dysglycaemia through mechanisms like MTB‐driven visceral adipose inflammation, which disrupts glucose homeostasis and promotes insulin resistance [10, 11, 12, 13]. This process, termed metaflammation, involves chronic low‐grade inflammation linked to metabolic dysregulation [6, 14]. Key mediators like heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1) are significantly upregulated in TB patients, correlating with elevated HbA1c levels and contributing to insulin resistance through oxidative stress pathways [7, 15, 16, 17]. Such findings necessitate TB's potential to catalyse progression from euglycaemia to overt DM, particularly in metabolically vulnerable populations [17].

Recent studies reveal heterogeneous glycaemic trajectories among TB patients [10, 12, 13]. While some exhibit transient hyperglycaemia resolving with anti‐TB therapy [10]. Others develop persistent dysglycaemia associated with poor outcomes [8, 12, 13]. African and Asian cohorts demonstrate that 15%–30% of TB patients present with abnormal glucose metabolism at diagnosis, with nearly half normalising post‐treatment [10, 12, 13]. However, those with sustained dysglycaemia face 2–3 times higher risks of treatment failure and mortality [7, 13]. These observations highlight the urgent need to distinguish stress‐induced hyperglycaemia from true diabetes during TB management.

Despite these advances, critical gaps persist in high‐burden settings. The natural history of HbA1c changes during TB treatment remains poorly documented, and the clinical significance of transient versus persistent dysglycaemia is unclear. This study addresses these gaps through longitudinal HbA1c monitoring in Ghanaian pulmonary TB patients, with three objectives: (1) characterise glycaemic trajectories in TB‐Only, TB‐DM and stress hyperglycaemia cohorts/dysglycaemia not requiring pharmacological therapy (TB‐DMnt); (2) evaluate associations between HbA1c evolution and TB treatment outcomes; and (3) inform context‐specific strategies for integrated TB‐DM care in resource‐limited settings.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a prospective longitudinal cohort study, involving TB with or without dysglycaemia undergoing anti‐tuberculous and/or anti‐diabetic therapy. Study participants were prescribed standard daily oral dose (fixed‐dose combinations) consisting of isoniazid (INH), rifampin (RIF), pyrazinamide (PZA), and ethambutol (EMB) for the initial 2 months. Subsequently, they continued with INH and RIF for the next 4–6 months, the duration depending on disease progression and treatment response in accordance with WHO guidelines. TB‐DMt patients (n = 39) received metformin monotherapy (500–1000 mg BID; median 1500 mg/day; range 1000–2000 mg; pre‐study duration 4.2 years IQR, 2.1–7.8) as prescribed by their physicians, concurrent with standard anti‐TB therapy. No concomitant use of additional oral hypoglycaemic agents or insulin (0/39 patients). The study was conducted at the Department of Chest Diseases of the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH), and the Department of Bacteriology of Noguchi Memorial for Medical Research (NMIMR), University of Ghana. Plasma haemoglobin A1c was analysed using a fully automated clinical chemistry analyser (Beckman Coulter DxC 700 AU) at MDS‐Lancet Laboratories (Ghana). The study screened adults with positive GeneXpert TB results from KBTH, over a period of 3 years (October 2020–December 2024).

Study Population

We recruited clinically confirmed drug‐sensitive pulmonary TB patients in a consecutive fashion. We defined clinically confirmed cases as those with a positive GeneXpert report for MTB and X‐ray findings suggestive of TB. Individuals who met the above criteria, after consenting, were given containers to provide sputum for culture to confirm the initial diagnosis. Sociodemographic data (e.g., occupation, location, age), symptoms, medical history and other vital data were documented. A first response oral Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) test was done to determine the HIV status of each participant.

Cohorts Screening and Classification

For diabetes screening, a fasting blood glucose (FBG) test was performed using Gold‐Accu blood glucose test strips (Changsha Sinocare Inc.) using capillary blood. All 120 GeneXpert‐confirmed pulmonary TB patients underwent baseline HbA1c testing, regardless of FBG results, to enable comprehensive longitudinal monitoring (t 0: baseline; t 1: 3 months; t 2: 6 months; t 3: 9 months post‐baseline). FBG screening (t 0) was used to triage cohorts: FBG < 7 mmol/L classified TB‐Only (HbA1c 5.5% ± 0.6% confirmed); FBG ≥ 7 mmol/L patients received HbA1c confirmation. HbA1c values below 6.5% were considered non‐diabetic as described by the American Diabetes Association guidelines from 2023 (https://doi.org/10.2337/dc22‐SINT) [9] and Ugarte‐Gil et al. [8] with little modifications. Those with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% were considered diabetic (TB‐DM cohort) and were categorised into one of two groups: diabetic group consisting of those on anti‐diabetic therapy (TB‐DMt) and those with dysglycaemia not requiring pharmacological therapy (TB‐DMnt) according to the clinical judgement of the treating physician supported by monitoring of blood glucose level. Although some diabetic patients declared their status during recruitment, the HbA1c test was done to confirm their condition.

Glycaemic Classification and Rationale

For the purposes of this research, glycaemic status was initially classified using baseline HbA1c values according to ADA and WHO guidelines: normoglycaemia (HbA1c < 5.7%), prediabetes (5.7%–6.4%), and diabetes (≥ 6.5%). However, to investigate the natural history of glycaemic changes during TB and to capture transient dysglycaemia commonly observed in TB patients, we also tracked changes in HbA1c longitudinally, regardless of anti‐diabetic therapy initiation in the TB‐DM cohort. This approach is supported by recent evidence [10], which demonstrates that TB frequently induces transient elevations in HbA1c that often resolve with TB treatment alone, independent of pharmacologic intervention. Thus, for research purposes, we monitored all patients' HbA1c trends throughout TB treatment and follow‐up, regardless of their initial classification or subsequent therapy, to distinguish between persistent diabetes and temporary, infection‐related hyperglycaemia.

HbA1c Measurements and Time Points

Blood samples for the HbA1c test were collected using Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes at four time points: baseline (t 0), 3 months (t 1) and 6 months (t 2) during standard TB treatment, and 9 months (t 3), which is 3 months post‐treatment. Prior to HbA1c measurement, haemoglobin (Hb) concentration was assessed using the HemoCue 301 system (HemoCue, Angelholm, Sweden) to screen for anaemia. This step was essential because anaemia can significantly compromise the accuracy of HbA1c results, potentially leading to misinterpretation of glycaemic status. Assessing haemoglobin levels beforehand is particularly important in TB patients, who are at increased risk for anaemia, to ensure that HbA1c measurements reliably reflect true glycaemic control.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

All GeneXpert newly confirmed MTB positive patients (aged ≥18years) were screened for HIV and DM co‐infections at baseline simultaneously. In addition, the patients gave consent and agreed to be followed up during the intensive and continuous phases of TB treatment. All patients on follow‐up agreed to produce sputum, allowing blood samples to be drawn at specific time points as per the study schedule.

Pregnant women, extra‐pulmonary TB patients, HIV co‐infected cases, children below 18 years and TB patients with blood disorders like thalassaemia, haemoglobinopathy, among others were excluded from the study.

Statistical Analysis

The study results were captured, cleaned, and verified in Microsoft Access and the data was analysed using Stata statistics version 14.2 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics such as simple frequencies and proportions were used to summarise demographic characteristics. Continuous data were expressed either as mean ± standard error of the mean or SD and median ± IQR as appropriate. Inter‐cohort comparisons: Kruskal–Wallis (non‐parametric continuous), Student's t‐test (parametric), χ 2/Fisher's exact (categorical). Multivariable logistic regression (ORs with 95% CIs) adjusted for age, sex, BMI, smoking; Firth correction for sparse data. Point estimates of statistical significance were shown with 2‐tailed p values < 0.05.

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval for this study protocol was obtained from both the Scientific Committee and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (NMIMR), University of Ghana (Federal wide assurance number: FWA00001824), and the Ethics Review Committee of the Korle‐Bu Teaching Hospital. Furthermore, this study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to any data collection, written informed consent was obtained from literate participants, while consent was secured from the legal guardians of all illiterate participants. All methods and procedures adhered to established ethical standards and regulatory requirements.

The funders had no role in the study design, collection, analysis and interpretation of data, manuscript writing or decision to publish.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The cohort included 120 adults with pulmonary TB: 66 TB‐Only, 39 TB‐DMt and 15 TB‐DMnt (Table 1). TB‐DMt patients were significantly older (54 years) than TB‐Only patients (39 years, p = 0.0001). There was no significant age difference between TB‐Only and TB‐DMnt (p = 0.231) or between TB‐DMt and TB‐DMnt (p = 0.115). TB‐DMt patients had a significantly higher BMI (23.8 ± 6.4 kg/m2) compared to TB‐Only (19.6 ± 2.7 kg/m2, p = 0.0001) and TB‐DMnt (19.8 ± 2.9 kg/m2, p = 0.008). A significantly higher proportion of TB‐Only patients had a history of smoking (36.4%) compared to TB‐DMt (5.1%, p = 0.0001). TB‐DMnt had a higher smoking rate (42.9%) than TB‐DMt (p = 0.001), but no significant difference between TB and TB‐DMnt (p = 0.649).

Changes in Haemoglobin A1c During TB Treatment

Longitudinal analysis of HbA1c levels revealed distinct glycaemic trajectories among pulmonary TB patients stratified by diabetes status. At baseline, the TB‐DMt group (established DM on metformin) exhibited significantly elevated HbA1c levels (9.9% ± 2.7%) compared to both TB‐Only patients (5.5% ± 0.6%) and those with dysglycaemia not requiring pharmacotherapy (TB‐DMnt; 7.8% ± 1.8%) (p < 0.001 for all comparisons) (Figure 2A,B; Table S1). Throughout the 9‐month follow‐up, HbA1c levels in the TB‐DMt cohort remained persistently high with only minor fluctuations (t 0: 9.9% ± 2.7%) to 3 months (t 1: 9.1% ± 2.9%, p = 0.57) or to 3 months post TB treatment (t 3: 8.4% ± 2.3%, p = 0.18), consistently exceeding those of the TB‐Only and TB‐DMnt groups at 3, 6 and 9 months (all p < 0.01) (Figure 2A,B; Table S1).

Further subgroup analysis within the TB‐DMt cohort revealed divergent trajectories based on glycaemic control status. At baseline, participants classified as ‘controlled’ (HbA1c < 6.5%) numbered 8 out of 39 total participants (20.5%), with a mean HbA1c of 6.2%. This controlled group showed a gradual increase in both absolute numbers and proportions over time: 9 participants at 3 months (25.0% of 36 total), 9 participants at 6 months (33.3% of 27 total), and 11 participants at 9 months (36.7% of 30 total), with mean HbA1c levels remaining stable around 6.1%. Conversely, the ‘uncontrolled’ group (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) demonstrated both absolute and proportional decreases from baseline to 9 months. Initially comprising 31 participants (79.5% of total cohort) with a mean HbA1c of 11%, this group decreased to 27 participants (75.0%) at 3 months, 18 participants (66.7%) at 6 months and 19 participants (63.3%) at 9 months. Importantly, mean HbA1c levels in the uncontrolled group showed substantial improvement, declining from 11% at baseline to 9.7% at 9 months. At all time points, mean HbA1c values were significantly lower in the controlled group compared to the uncontrolled group (p < 0.001) (Figure 2C).

In contrast, the TB‐DMnt group demonstrated a significant decline in HbA1c over time (t 0: 7.8% ± 1.8%) to 3 months (t 1: 7.2% ± 2.1%, p = 0.001) and to 3 months post TB treatment (t 3: 6.4% ± 2.4%, p = 0.002), approaching levels comparable to the TB‐Only group by 6 months (6.3% ± 1.4% vs. 5.3% ± 0.5%, p = 0.296) and maintaining this trend through 9 months (p = 0.05) (Figure 2A,B; Table S1). The TB‐only cohort maintained stable HbA1c levels within the euglycaemic range (< 6.5%) across all time points, with only slight, non‐significant decreases from baseline to 9 months (p = 0.05) (Figure 2A,B; Table S1).

Intra‐specific trajectories (Figure 2D; Figure S1) indicated a trend of increasing HbA1c levels after the intensive phase of TB treatment in two subclassified cohorts of the TB‐DMt group: those with fairly controlled and poorly controlled HbA1c. Additionally, the TB‐DMt cohort classified as ‘well‐controlled’ exhibited a significant increase in HbA1c at baseline compared to both the TB‐Only and TB‐DMnt groups (p = 0.001) (Figure S1). However, during TB treatment (at t 2 and t 3), there was no significant difference in HbA1c levels between this group and the TB‐Only and TB‐DMnt groups (p > 0.05). Following the termination of ATMs at t 2, the ‘well‐controlled’ TB‐DMt group displayed a significant increase in HbA1c compared to both the TB‐Only and TB‐DMnt groups (p = 0.016) (Figure S1).

The Sankey plots illustrate the longitudinal transitions among different glycaemic and TB‐DM status categories over a 9‐month period (Figure 3). At baseline, most TB‐DM participants (70%, 38/54) had uncontrolled glycaemia, while only 30% (16/54) were controlled. As they resumed ATMs, the proportion of controlled participants steadily increased: 32% at 3 months, 47% at 6 months and 51% at 9 months. Meanwhile, the proportion of uncontrolled participants decreased from 70% at baseline to 49% at 9 months (Figure 3A).

Discussion

This prospective cohort study provides important new insights into the dynamic interplay between TB and DM, particularly regarding the evolution of glycaemic status during and after TB treatment in a high‐burden, resource‐limited setting. By tracking HbA1c trajectories in newly diagnosed pulmonary TB patients, we identified three main distinct patterns: persistent hyperglycaemia in those with established DM (TB‐DMt), transient dysglycaemia in a subset of TB patients (TB‐DMnt), and stable euglycaemia in the majority (TB‐Only). These clarify TB's bidirectional metabolic impact in high‐burden settings.

The progressive improvement in glycaemic control observed during TB treatment, especially among TB‐DMnt patients, likely stems from multiple interrelated factors. First, effective TB therapy reduces systemic inflammation and infection‐related stress, alleviating cortisol/cytokine‐driven insulin resistance (metaflammation) [5, 6, 7, 10]. Second, the increased clinical monitoring and healthcare engagement that occur during TB treatment can enhance diabetes management by promoting medication adherence and encouraging lifestyle modifications [3]. Third, certain anti‐TB medications may have indirect effects on glucose metabolism [1, 7], although this area warrants further research. Despite these positive trends, a substantial proportion of patients continued to exhibit uncontrolled glycaemia at 9 months, underscoring the ongoing challenges in effectively managing diabetes within this population.

In line with previous studies [8, 10, 12, 13], our findings show that TB‐DMt patients had significantly higher baseline HbA1c levels and maintained persistently poor glycaemic control throughout TB therapy, despite concurrent anti‐diabetic treatment. This persistent hyperglycaemia likely reflects the underlying metabolic challenges faced by individuals with dual TB‐DM pathology, including chronic inflammation, altered immune function and potential drug–drug interactions that complicate glycaemic management [1, 6, 7, 11]. Our subgroup analysis further revealed that while a modest proportion of TB‐DMt patients achieved improved glycaemic control, most remained uncontrolled, warranting intensified diabetes management and close monitoring in this population.

From a mechanistic perspective, chronic hyperglycaemia impairs both innate and adaptive immunity, promotes immune dysregulation through several pathways. Elevated glucose levels lead to the formation of advanced glycation end‐products (AGEs), which interact with receptors (RAGE) on immune cells, activating NF‐κB signalling pathways and sustaining a pro‐inflammatory environment characterised by increased TNF‐α and IL‐6 production but impaired Th1 responses critical for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) control [5, 15, 18, 19]. This persistent inflammation, coupled with oxidative stress and depletion of antioxidant defences, compromises macrophage and lymphocyte function, thereby facilitating MTB survival and dissemination [1, 19]. Our observation of sustained elevated HbA1c and poorer glycaemic control in TB‐DMt patients reflects this vicious cycle of metabolic and immune dysfunction, consistent with our earlier report of slower sputum conversion [20], higher relapse rates, and increased mortality in TB‐DM comorbidity [5, 7, 12, 13].

In addition to these immune‐metabolic factors, the pharmacological effects of anti‐tubercular drugs may have contributed to the persistent hyperglycaemia observed in the TB‐DMt patients. Rifampicin, a potent inducer of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, is reported to accelerate the metabolism of oral hypoglycaemic agents, reducing their therapeutic efficacy and complicating glycaemic control [1]. Moreover, rifampicin and its metabolites can increase hepatic gluconeogenesis and promote insulin resistance, further aggravating hyperglycaemia [7]. Isoniazid metabolites may impair pancreatic beta‐cell function by interfering with vitamin B6 metabolism, which may modestly diminish insulin secretion. These drug‐related metabolic effects likely compound the challenges of managing diabetes during TB treatment.

In contrast, TB‐DMnt group exhibited significant HbA1c decline to normoglycaemia by month six (despite paradoxically higher adverse TB risk than TB‐DMt [aOR 4.20 vs. 1.32, p = 0.04]). This supports the hypothesis that TB infection can induce transient hyperglycaemia through stress‐related metabolic pathways, including increased cortisol and inflammatory cytokines, which promote hepatic gluconeogenesis and peripheral insulin resistance, a phenomenon termed metaflammation [15]. The reversibility of dysglycaemia in TB‐DMnt patients suggests that many cases of dysglycaemia in TB may not represent true diabetes but rather a temporary metabolic disturbance that resolves with infection control. Our data showing HbA1c normalisation after TB treatment corroborates prior studies suggesting that dysglycaemia in TB patients may be reversible upon infection clearance, distinguishing stress‐induced dysglycaemia from chronic diabetes [10, 12, 13]. This distinction carries profound clinical implications, precluding immediate lifelong antidiabetic therapy for all TB patients with elevated baseline HbA1c. Instead, we propose an actionable protocol: FBG → confirmatory HbA1c (pre‐anaemia screened) at diagnosis, repeated at 3 and 6 months. Normalisation (HbA1c < 6.5%) obviates DM therapy with annual surveillance; persistence (≥ 6.5%) mandates prompt intensification [21, 22].

Notably, the TB‐Only cohort maintained stable euglycaemic HbA1c levels throughout follow‐up, further supporting the specificity of stress‐induced dysglycaemia to those with metabolic vulnerability or acute infection. The use of Sankey plots in this study provided a clear visualisation of glycaemic transitions, illustrating that most TB‐DMnt patients reverted to normoglycaemia, while persistent hyperglycaemia was confined to those with established DM. The greatest transitions between glycaemic states occurred during the 3–6‐month interval, coinciding with the period of highest risk for adverse outcomes. Importantly, the plots also revealed bidirectional shifts between controlled and uncontrolled glycaemic states, reflecting the complex and non‐linear trajectory of glycaemic regulation during TB treatment. These fluctuations are likely influenced by systemic inflammation, variable treatment adherence, individual metabolic differences and chronic inflammation‐induced adipose tissue dysfunction [15]. Elevated levels of heme oxygenase‐1 in TB patients have been correlated with HbA1c, supporting its contributory role in the metabolic disturbances observed [16, 23]. HO‐1 contributes to metaflammation by promoting adipocyte dysfunction and hepatic gluconeogenesis and impairing pancreatic β‐cell function, thereby exacerbating insulin resistance [15]. Targeting HO‐1 and related oxidative stress pathways may represent a novel therapeutic avenue in TB‐DM comorbidity.

Despite the use of metformin, which has known immunomodulatory and metabolic benefits via AMPK activation [24, 25, 26] glycaemic improvements in TB‐DMt patients were modest. However, the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of metformin when combined with first‐line anti‐TB drugs remain incompletely understood. Drug–drug interactions, altered absorption or changes in metabolism during TB therapy may reduce metformin's efficacy in controlling hyperglycaemia [24, 26]. Additionally, the metabolic derangements induced by TB—such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and HO‐1 mediated metaflammation—may overwhelm the glucose‐lowering and immunomodulatory capacity of standard metformin regimens [24, 27]. A key limitation is that no patients in our cohort required escalation to additional oral hypoglycaemic agents (OHAs) or insulin; in cases of more severe hyperglycaemia necessitating polypharmacy, such intensification could influence glycaemic and TB treatment outcomes, though it risks further rifampicin interactions. This challenging metabolic milieu likely contributed to the modest HbA1c reductions observed. Overall, standard antidiabetic regimens appear insufficient against TB‐associated metabolic stress, necessitating intensified or adjunctive therapies [26, 27].

Furthermore, the observed post‐treatment increase in HbA1c among ‘well‐controlled’ TB‐DMt patients highlights the potential for late glycaemic deterioration after cessation of anti‐TB therapy. This phenomenon may reflect lingering metabolic stress, immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS)‐like responses, or delayed restoration of insulin sensitivity [24, 28]. Critically, as highlighted by the UK Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), Type‐2 diabetes mellitus is inherently progressive, often requiring continuous treatment intensification over time independent of TB status. This natural disease progression likely contributes to the observed deterioration, particularly in patients with established DM [29]. It necessitates the critical need for extended glycaemic monitoring beyond TB treatment completion to identify and manage late‐onset or worsening diabetes, which could otherwise compromise long‐term patient outcomes.

Other limitations to be acknowledged include the modest sample size, particularly for the TB‐DMnt subgroup (n = 15), and limited statistical power which contributed to wide confidence intervals for adverse outcome estimates, potentially restricting generalisability. Additionally, the study did not systematically capture dynamic confounders such as nutritional status, concomitant infections, haemoglobinopathies or longitudinal changes in BMI/hepatic/renal function beyond baseline adjustments. Third, while HbA1c was used per ADA/WHO guidelines, some FBG < 7 mmol/L patients may have had undiagnosed diabetes per OGTT (gold standard), though confirmatory HbA1c and clinical judgement minimised misclassification.

Conclusion

Our findings confirm that TB‐DM comorbidity is driven by a self‐reinforcing cycle of hyperglycaemia, metaflammation and immune dysfunction, whereas TB‐induced transient dysglycaemia (TB‐DMnt) represents a reversible state of infection‐induced metabolic stress. The dynamic transitions observed in glycaemic status, particularly the persistence of hyperglycaemia in TB‐DM patients, underline the need for routine glucose monitoring using HbA1c throughout TB treatment to distinguish transient dysglycaemia from true diabetes, thereby supporting more precise risk stratification and tailored clinical management for TB patients. Future research should explore the long‐term metabolic outcomes of TB survivors, particularly focusing on those who experience stress‐induced hyperglycaemia during treatment, to assess their risk of developing DM. Additionally, studies should evaluate the impact of integrated TB‐DM care models in resource‐limited settings to improve comprehensive disease management.
