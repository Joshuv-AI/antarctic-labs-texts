Fracture resistance of posterior teeth restored by lithium silicate-based and hybrid ceramic partial coverage restorations with different preparation designs: a scoping review
Authors: Nolpapon Sirikatitham, Boonlert Kukiattrakoon, Vanthana Sattabanasuk, Khemtin Ratanasathien, Chirayu Ruengrungsom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCeramic partial coverage restorations (PCRs) are minimally invasive treatment options for complex tooth damage. This study aimed to review the fracture resistance and fracture patterns of non-endodontically treated posterior teeth restored with PCRs using various restoration types (overlay, onlay, etc.), preparation designs, and two ceramic groups (lithium silicate-based and hybrid ceramic materials).MethodsThree databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect) were used to search for specific key terms according to the PRISMA diagram. Finally, 34 articles were included for analysis. The fracture resistance and fracture patterns were summarized, and RoBDEMAT was used to assess the risk of bias.ResultsWith ≥ 1 mm restorative occlusal thickness irrespective of materials, premolars restored with PCRs exhibited vertical fracture loads ranging from 682 to 1,954 N, which were lower than those of molars with a similar condition (1,024 to 4,995 N). Molars restored with 1–2 mm-thick lithium-silicate-based overlays and MOD preparation tended to have lower ranges of vertical-load fracture resistance [lithium disilicate (LDS): 1,295-1,326 N; zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (ZLS): 2,066 − 2,189 N] compared to overlays without MOD retention [LDS: 1,406-4,995 N; ZLS: 2,177-2,738 N]. However, the effect of the MOD box was less significant in premolars. Increasing occlusal thickness tended to enhance fracture resistance, but interactions with other factors (e.g., preparation design, ceramic type, and bonded dental substrate) altered the results. Adding proximal boxes or MOD preparation increased the risk of severe fractures. Hybrid ceramics for PCRs showed promising tooth fracture loads under certain conditions, but their fracture resistance compared to LDS remained controversial.ConclusionsAlthough MOD overlays may decrease the load-bearing capacities of tooth-restoration assemblies compared to anatomic overlays in some conditions, the overall fracture loads for both restoration types always exceeded maximum bite forces. Considering other relevant factors will help clinicians design the appropriate restoration type for each clinical situation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08053-8.Keywords: Fracture resistance, Ceramic, Partial coverage restoration, Preparation design, Lithium silicate, Lithium disilicate, Hybrid ceramic

Introduction

Caries and tooth wear are common causes of significant tooth structure damage that necessitate restoration [1–4]. When the tooth structure loss in posterior load-bearing areas involves the occluso-proximal side extending beyond the buccal/lingual line angles of proximal cavities or deteriorating by at least 1 cusp, complex direct [5, 6] or indirect restorations [6] are indicated. Resin composite is widely used for direct posterior restoration, but achieving proper proximal contact and occlusal function in large restorations is not easy to accomplish [7]. Additionally, secondary caries of direct composite restorations, often caused by the technique sensitivity of adhesives related to the contaminated tooth surface [8] and dentin margin degradation [9], are the main cause of failure, which is the drawback of this direct approach. Full-coverage crowns require extensive tooth preparation [10–12], which can lead to pulpitis and may necessitate endodontic therapy [13]. Moreover, the subgingival margin of crowns always involves periodontal tissue [14], causing issues with impressions, finishing, and gingival inflammation. For these reasons, partial coverage restorations (PCRs), such as onlays and overlays, provide a minimally invasive alternative when the cavity size is appropriate.

Nowadays, the trend of esthetic PCRs supported by computer-aided design-computer-aided manufacturing (CAD-CAM) technologies has become an important treatment of choice. The development of adhesive systems for pretreated teeth and restoration surfaces (e.g., hydrofluoric acid followed by silane [15–17] for lithium silicated based ceramics; air-borne-particle abrasion followed by silane [16, 17] for resin nanoceramics), as well as resin cements [18] enables reliable interfacial bonding of these bonded ceramic materials to the tooth surface in clinical practice [4, 19]. With the bonding strategy, a paradigm shift in tooth preparation design has arisen with less concern for mechanical retention [20–23]. Additionally, some clinicians have advocated for non-retentive partial coverage restorations, if the lesions still have 100% peripheral enamel for bonding quality [20, 24]. On the other hand, some clinicians still support shallow mechanical retention from tooth defects [23, 25], which helps stabilize restorations during luting and achieve proper contact and contour. A combination of mechanical retention and interfacial bonding may be necessary for high lateral forces and partial loss of peripheral enamel.

One of the main reasons for the failure of ceramic PCRs is ceramic fractures [26, 27]. This failure has inspired researchers to focus on the factors influencing the fracture resistance of teeth restored with ceramic PCRs, such as the restoration type, preparation design (including occlusal thickness, accessory mechanical retention, etc.), and ceramic type. Some studies investigated the partial restoration’s preparation design as it seemed to have negative effects on the fracture resistance of the tooth-restoration assembly [28, 29]. However, it was not clear in detail which design had a negative or zero effect.

Lithium disilicate glass ceramics, with varying phases of lithia crystals (43–80 vol% crystalline content), as well as zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate, achieved by adding ZrO2 as a secondary nucleant [30], demonstrated superior mechanical properties to the previous generation of glass ceramic reinforced with leucite crystals [31]. Hybrid ceramic materials, known for their fast manufacturing benefits [32], are classified into resin-nanoceramic (RNC), which consists of a resin-based matrix with ceramic fillers like ZrO2, and polymer-infiltrate ceramic network (PICN) containing a porous felspathic ceramic structure infiltrated with urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA) and triethylene glycoldimethacrylate (TEGDMA) [31]. Thus, hybrid ceramics may have the advantage of being less brittle due to the positive effect of polymer combinations [31, 33]. However, the flexural strength, fracture toughness, hardness, and modulus of elasticity of PICN and RNC are lower than those of lithium silicate-based ceramics [10, 31, 32, 34]. Additionally, lithium silicate-based and hybrid ceramics, along with their pretreatment protocols, result in varying bond strengths to resin cement [35]. These mechanical properties and bond efficacy may influence the fracture resistance of teeth restored with these two types of ceramic materials.

There has been a lack of literature review to investigate the fracture behavior of posterior teeth restored with PCRs based on the restorative type (e.g., overlay, MOD overlay, and onlay), preparation design, and 2 types of ceramic materials (lithium silicate-based and hybrid ceramics). Therefore, this study aimed to review and summarize the fracture resistance and fracture patterns of non-endodontically treated premolars and molars restored with PCRs, using the three independent factors mentioned.

Methodology

This scoping review protocol was registered at Open Science Framework (OSF) via the website link (https://osf.io/j4tqu/?view_only=94a726d3cbe74081959aea573cb39b66). The search was conducted by two authors with inter-calibration, requiring consensus if there were discrepancies in article selection. The search process followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) diagram [36] (Fig. 1) and was last updated in June 2025. The three databases (PubMed, Web of Science, and ScienceDirect) were included in this review using the two search terms as follows: (1) “(onlay and fracture resistance and preparation and lithium) or (overlay and fracture resistance and preparation and lithium) or (occlusal veneer and fracture resistance and preparation and lithium) or (partial coverage and fracture resistance and preparation and lithium)” and (2) “(onlay and fracture resistance and preparation and hybrid ceramic) or (overlay and fracture resistance and preparation and hybrid ceramic) or (occlusal veneer and fracture resistance and preparation and hybrid ceramic) or (partial coverage and fracture resistance and preparation and hybrid ceramic)”.

Prisma diagram illustrates the sequence of the search strategy

After an initial search, 193 identified articles were found. The duplicated articles among the three databases were removed, leaving 113 articles remaining. Then the articles were screened by the title/abstract, leaving 61 articles for eligibility. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were determined in the PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1). One of the key exclusion criteria was studies conducted on model teeth, as the use of different substrates for bonding compared to human teeth may affect fracture resistance outcomes. The number of articles finally included was 24, plus 10 articles according to the cross-reference search. The final 34 articles were detected and divided into the subtopics of premolar PCRs (n = 9) and molar PCRs (n = 25) relative to the fracture resistance and fracture pattern. In each subtopic, all articles investigated the fracture resistance with some evaluating the fracture pattern.

The specific terms in this study included the following. An onlay refers to a PCR covering at least one tooth cusp [37]. A restoration with all-cusp coverage and a supragingival margin is called an overlay [37]. An occlusal veneer that covers all cusps is also termed as an overlay [38]. The MOD overlay refers to a conventional overlay consisting of internal mesio-occluso-distal preparation [28]. The lithium silicate-based materials are divided into two subgroups: lithium disilicate glass ceramic (LDS) and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (ZLS) [31]. The hybrid ceramics refer to two subgroups: polymer infiltrated ceramic network (PICN) and resin-nanoceramic (RNC) [31]. The review’s scope is based on independent factors related to fracture resistance and fracture pattern including: (1) restoration type (e.g., overlay, MOD overlay, and MOD onlay), (2) ceramic type (LDS, ZLS, PICN, and RNC) (3) preparation design (e.g., occlusal thickness, only anatomic reduction, proximal box, peripheral shelf, and other accessory preparations), and (4) miscellaneous factors (e.g., immediate dentin sealing and deep margin elevation).

The quality assessment of the individual studies was performed by a risk-of-bias tool for preclinical dental material research (RoBDEMAT) [39] (Table 1). This analysis consists of a 9-domain checklist classified into 4 groups of risk of bias: (1) bias in planning and allocation, (2) bias in specimen preparation, (3) bias in outcome assessment, and (4) bias in data treatment and outcome reporting [39].

Results

The risk of bias assessment

Considering Table 1 for the 34 included articles, 8 of the studies were analyzed as having a low risk of bias, while 26 studies were analyzed as having a medium risk, respectively, but no article was reported as having a high risk of bias. From the 9-domain analysis, 4 domains consisting of (1) standardization of sample and materials, (2) adequate and standardized testing procedures and outcomes, (3) adequate statistical analysis, and (4) reporting study outcomes, showed “yes” (no risk of bias in each domain) for all included articles.

The fracture resistance of premolars restored with PCRs

Overall results from included studies

As shown in Fig. 2, fracture resistance of premolars restored with PCR ranged from 350 to 1,954 N [7, 11, 40–46]. With clinical simulated aging, the vertical-load fracture resistance values of premolars bonded with three restoration types at different occlusal thicknesses were as follows: 0.5/0.6-mm LDS overlay (471-1,781 N) [40–42, 44], 1-mm LDS overlay (866-1,495 N) [44], 1.5-mm LDS overlay (1,005 N) [44], 0.5-mm LDS-MOD overlay (833–997 N) [44, 46], 1-mm LDS-MOD overlay (1,087 − 1,108 N) [44, 46], 1.5-mm LDS-MOD overlay (1,129 N) [44], 2-mm LDS-MOD overlay (963-1,361 N) [46], 0.5-mm LDS-MOD onlay (1,192 N) [46], 1-mm LDS-MOD onlay (1,055 N) [46], and 2-mm LDS-MOD onlay (837-1,182 N) [7, 46]. Without the aging protocol, the vertical-load fracture resistance values of premolars remained almost comparable to those with aging methods: 1.5-mm LDS overlay (887-1,954 N) [43], 2-mm LDS-MOD overlay (842–994 N) [45], and 2-mm LDS onlay (682–938 N) [45]. The oblique/vertical-load fracture resistance values of premolars restored with 2-mm ZLS were 624–756 N under an aging condition [11].

Types of restorations and ceramics

Only the study by Gierthmuehlen et al. [44] compared LDS-MOD overlays and LDS overlays, showing premolars with MOD overlays had higher or comparable fracture loads, depending on the occlusal thickness: 1,129 N > 1,005 N for 1.5 mm, 1,087 N > 866 N for 1 mm, and 833 N ~ 866 N for 0.5 mm (Fig. 2). Two studies [45, 46] revealed that the fracture resistance of premolars restored with an LDS-MOD overlay varied compared to an LDS-MOD onlay, depending on occlusal thickness, preparation design, and proximal box width (Fig. 2). For overlays with a 0.5 mm occlusal thickness and without MOD cavities, two studies [41, 42] found that restoration with LDS provided premolar fracture resistance comparable to that of ZLS; however, PICN restorations yielded comparable or slightly lower premolar fracture load values than LDS and ZLS.

Preparation design and other factors

Regarding Fig. 2, two studies [44, 46] found that the interaction of occlusal thickness, preparation design, and dentin substrate also influenced fracture resistance. Guess et al. [46] showed that for overlays with buccal full veneering, a 2-mm thickness (1,361 N) provided superior fracture resistance compared to 1 mm (1,087 N) and 0.5 mm (833 N) thicknesses. However, when the preparation design lacked a peripheral shelf, fracture loads were similar across occlusal thicknesses. Gierthmuehlen et al. [44] reported that for enamel-bonded overlays, premolars with a 1 mm-thick restoration (1,495 N) had a higher load capacity than those with a 0.5 mm restoration (1,279 N). When bonded to dentin and peripheral enamel, the fracture resistance of premolars restored with 1 mm and 0.5 mm overlays was similar (866 N).

A ZLS study found only a slight, statistically insignificant increase in fracture resistance with one or two peripheral shelves [11]. Additionally, a wider proximal box increased the maximum fracture load of premolars restored with MOD overlays and MOD onlays [45]. Concerning other preparation factors (Fig. 2), two studies [40, 43] advocated that immediate dentin sealing enhanced the fracture resistance of premolars restored by LDS overlays. Furthermore, overlays luted with a preheated resin composite (1,954 or 1,505 N) increased the fracture resistance of premolars than those luted with dual-cure resin cement (1,495 or 1,158 N) or flowable resin composite (1,068–887 N), regardless of whether using immediate dentin sealing [43].

The fracture pattern of premolars restored with PCRs

Figure 3 shows that premolar overlays with 2 proximal boxes (regardless of the ceramic type; LDS or ZLS) [11, 43] demonstrated a higher incidence of severe mixed fractures (75–100%) compared to the other PCR types, such as MOD onlays (0–80%) [7, 45, 46], MOD overlays (6.25–68.75%) [44–46], and overlays with buccal full veneering (0–25%) [44]. Increasing occlusal thickness led to a higher incidence of severe mixed fractures (Fig. 3), for example, overlay with buccal full veneering on enamel and dentin (1–1.5 mm = 16.67% vs. 0.5 mm = 0%) [44], MOD overlay (2 mm = 56.25–68.75% vs. 1.5 mm = 56.25% vs. 0.5–1 mm = 6.25–12.5%) [44, 46], and MOD onlay (2 mm = 37.5% vs. 0.5–1 mm = 18.75%) [46]. Besides, a greater proximal box width increased severe mixed fractures (Fig. 3), with 30% and 80% of fractures for MOD overlay and MOD onlay with full cusp-to-cusp width, compared to 10% and 60% with 3/4 intercuspal width [45].

Types of restorations and ceramics

In Figs. 4, 5 and 6, no studies have compared the fracture loads of molars restored with LDS overlays and LDS-MOD overlays within the same study. However, Farachai et al. compared the performance of ZLS overlay and ZLS-MOD overlay, finding that molars restored with overlays (2,545-2,738 N) had higher fracture resistance than those with MOD overlays (2,066 − 2,1089 N) [29]. Additionally, the load capacities of molars restored with MOD overlays (1,295-1,326 N) were statistically similar to those with MOD onlays (1,440 N), as reported in another study [63].

The superiority of LDS versus hybrid ceramic PCRs in molars remained controversial (Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7). Some studies reported higher fracture loads for LDS overlays with specific conditions [for example, 1.5-mm LDS (4,995 or 2,210 N) > 1.5-mm PICN (3,540 N) or 1.5-mm RNC (3,584 or 1,882 N)] [53, 61] and 2-mm effectively bonded LDS (4,766 or 4,676 N) > 2-mm effectively bonded RNC (3,620 or 3,593 N) [50], while others showed better performance with hybrid ceramics at reduced occlusal thicknesses of 0.5–1 mm [47, 48, 62]. The fracture-load ranking of PICN and RNC in molars also remained inconsistent [47, 48]. Ioannidis et al. found RNC (2,274 N) > PICN (1,839 N) > LDS (1,530 N) for 1-mm occlusal thickness, and PICN (1,952 N) ≈ RNC (1,941 N) > LDS (1,178 N) for 0.5-mm thickness [47]. Another study showed RNC (2,328 N and 2,092 N) > PICN (2,239 N and 1,981 N) > LDS (1,851 N and 1,191 N) regardless of occlusal thickness [48]. Additionally, LDS overlay (2,210 N) showed comparable fracture load to ZLS overlay (2,177 N) [61] (Figs. 4 and 5).

Preparation design and other factors

From Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7, several studies revealed that increasing occlusal thickness from 0.5 mm to 1-1.5 mm tended to elevate the resistance to fracture of molars restored with overlays made from LDS [47, 48, 52, 53], ZLS [67], PICN [48, 53], and RNC [47, 48, 53]. However, three studies found no difference in molar fracture loads between 0.5 mm and 1 mm overlays for LDS [51], PICN [47], and RNC [68], indicating an interaction between occlusal thickness and ceramic type in one study [47]. Furthermore, an interaction between occlusal thickness and bonded dental substrate was observed. Sesse et al. [57] found that when bonded to enamel, the fracture resistance was greatest at 0.5 mm, while bonding to enamel and dentin reversed the order to 1 mm > 0.5 mm.

Ferraris et al. [59] revealed that only anatomic occlusal reduction provided the highest fracture resistance (3,216 N) for molars restored with LDS overlays. The next most effective design was the LDS overlay with two proximal boxes (2,462 N), followed by the same design with a shoulder peripheral shelf (2,350 N) (Fig. 5). Similarly, Falahchai et al. [29], reported that ZLS overlays with only anatomic occlusal reduction had superior fracture resistance (2,738 N) compared with MOD overlays (2,189 N). A shoulder shelf reduced fracture loads for both overlay types (Fig. 6). Yli-Urpo et al. [50] found that a peripheral shelf slightly decreased fracture load when bonding was proper but slightly increased it with deteriorated bonding (Figs. 4 and 5). Alassar et al. [69] observed that RNC-MOD onlays with a shoulder finish line showed slightly greater fracture resistance of molars compared to those with a flat finish line (Fig. 7). The effect of the shoulder shelf was controversial across studies [29, 50, 59, 69], and peripheral shelves finished with a chamfer or shoulder did not affect fracture resistance [60] (Fig. 5).

Similar to premolar PCRs, immediate dentin sealing also enhanced the fracture loads of molars with overlays and MOD overlays [49, 64] (Figs. 4, 6 and 7). Dual-cure resin cement improved fracture resistance in molars restored with MOD onlays compared to self-cure resin cement [66]. Enamel pretreatment with phosphoric acid and self-etch adhesive also increased fracture resistance of molars restored with overlays compared with adhesive alone [56]. Additionally, LDS-MOD overlays with deep margin elevation (1,986 N) showed no significant difference in fracture resistance compared to overlays without this procedure (1,776 N) [65].

The fracture pattern of molars restored with PCRs

From Figs. 8, 9 and 10, MOD overlays using LDS and ZLS had no mild fractures (type A), while up to 66% of LDS overlays experienced cracks or chipping. Moreover, the LDS-MOD overlays had higher severe mixed fractures (0% from 1 study, 56.25–100% from 3 studies) [63–65, 70] compared with LDS overlays (0% from 5 studies, 8.33–100% from 10 studies) [47–56, 58–62]. In the hybrid ceramic group (Fig. 9), RNC-MOD onlays showed no mild fractures with cracks or chipping restorations (0%) [69], while mild fractures occurred in 0–40% of PICN overlays [47, 48, 53, 55] and 0-22.22% of RNC overlays [47–50, 53, 61, 62].

In contrast to the effect of proximal boxes in premolar PCRs (Fig. 3), Ferraris et al. (Fig. 9) found no considerable difference in fracture patterns between LDS overlays with proximal boxes (35.71-50%) and those without proximal boxes (42.86%) [59]. Surface pretreatment with immediate dentin sealing (IDS) interacted with the type of restorations and ceramics (Figs. 8, 10 and 11). Two studies reported an increased unfavorable fracture pattern when using IDS compared with no use, for LDS-MOD overlays (80% vs. 70%) [70] and RNC overlays (50% vs. 0%) [49]. Nonetheless, no unfavorable fractures (0%) were observed in both IDS and no IDS groups for LDS overlays [49]. Additionally, deep margin elevation of LDS-MOD overlay enhanced severe mixed fractures (100%) than the restorations without the technique (80%) [65] (Fig. 10).

Discussion

This review found the different fracture resistance values of molars and premolars restored with PCRs. Restoration and ceramic types influenced load capacity, while preparation design factors like occlusal thickness and peripheral shelf interacted with other variables to affect fracture resistance.

Restoration type (overlays and onlays)

LDS-MOD overlays provided higher or comparable fracture loads for premolars compared to LDS overlays. However, molars restored with lithium silicate-based MOD overlays tended to have lower load capacity than those restored with lithium silicate-based overlays. The discrepancy may be due to the tooth type and different preparation angles. In theory, retentive preparation (e.g., the MOD box) and sharp preparation angles typically reduce the fracture resistance of the tooth-restoration assembly [20, 29, 59]. A premolar study [44] showed that steep occlusal reduction, resembling a retentive angle, may decrease fracture resistance even in overlays without MOD retention. Conversely, molars with less steep occlusal preparation restored with anatomic overlays showed higher fracture resistance than those with MOD retention [29], supporting the theory. It may also be explained that a greater loss of tooth structure and less conservative preparation with the MOD box results in lower fracture resistance [71–73]. In addition, finite element analysis showed higher stress concentrations in MOD PCRs [28, 74], but strain gauges indicated no significant difference in coronal deformation between overlays with and without MOD retention [28]. The higher stress in MOD boxes produces a wedging effect [29] and may explain the increased severity of fractures in MOD overlays versus anatomic ones.

In our review, the fracture resistance of premolars restored with MOD overlays varied compared to those restored with MOD onlays, depending on the interacting factors. In contrast, molars restored with both types exhibited statistically similar fracture resistance [63]. However, finite element analysis revealed greater crack propagation in onlays, where significant displacement was observed, while overlays demonstrated superior performance [75].

Preparation design (occlusal thickness, peripheral shelf, etc.)

Decreasing occlusal thickness reduced the fracture resistance of teeth restored with an overlay featuring buccal full veneering (a type of peripheral shelf) [46]. Conversely, occlusal thickness showed less effect on the fracture load in an overlay without a peripheral shelf [46]. This may be explained by the fact that the angle between the occlusal plane and axial wall in the peripheral shelf design creates breaking points, exerting greater stress on ceramic PCRs [20, 46] with thinner occlusal thickness, thereby reducing the fracture resistance.

The preparation design for increasing the axial-wall height connected to the peripheral shelf improved the fracture resistance of premolars restored with MOD overlay, with the full-veneer crown demonstrating the highest fracture resistance [76]. This enhanced resistance may be attributed to the greater extent of mechanical wrapping, which often showed higher fracture resistance in numerous studies [70, 76, 77]. However, other studies examining the fracture load of teeth with overlays, MOD overlays, and MOD onlay found no significant difference across varying axial-wall heights [11] or the present or absence of a peripheral shelf [29, 63, 69]. One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the reinforcement provided by the resin adhesive and luting, which improves fracture resistance [78–80] and may mask the effects of an increased axial height or the presence of a peripheral shelf. Additionally, improper axial preparation angles may reduce the fracture resistance of the peripheral-shelf design, potentially skewing the results.

The fabrication method, ceramic type, and immediate dentin sealing

Recent studies on the flexural properties of IPS. e.max Press (heat press technique) and IPS e.max CAD (CAD-CAM technique) have shown conflicting results regarding which method provided superior outcomes [81, 82]. While CAD-CAM technology offers consistent fabrication, it introduces the risk of crack initiation during grinding [83]. However, fracture resistance is not solely determined by flexural properties. The bonded interface between tooth substrates and lithium disilicate can enhance fracture load even with lower-strength materials. One included study of this review found comparable fracture resistance between e.max Press and e.max CAD when both were employed with dual-cure resin cement, but e.max CAD performed worse with self-cure resin cement [66]. The superior mechanical properties of dual-cure composites, due to a higher degree of conversion from light curing [66, 84], may explain why dual-cure luting improves fracture resistance and masks the effect of e.max CAD.

From this review, LDS showed greater fracture resistance of teeth than PICN and RNC, with a higher occlusal thickness [53], but with lower thickness, PICN or RNC-restored teeth exhibited higher or comparable fracture resistance [47, 48, 53]. This difference may be attributed to polymer composition in hybrid ceramics, making them less brittle [33, 85]. Resin infiltration into the PICN structure can compensate for the existing flaws of the ceramic [86]. These advantages may enhance the potential of hybrid ceramics for use with thinner occlusal thicknesses. However, LDS has a significantly higher biaxial flexural strength (609 MPa) compared to PICN (193 MPa) or RNC (300 MPa) [31], allowing LDS to perform better with thicker occlusal thickness. Consistent with a study of endodontically treated teeth (not included in this review) [87], molars with an LDS overlay and standard occlusal thickness demonstrated a higher fracture load and fewer marginal gaps than those with a hybrid ceramic overlay with the same thickness. The finite element related to crack propagation and fatigue performance displayed the superior performance of LDS to hybrid ceramics [75]. Furthermore, laboratory research revealed that the hybrid ceramic material exhibited significantly more wear compared to the LDS, but it resulted in less wear on the opposing teeth [88]. Nonetheless, a meta-analysis with a follow-up period of 24 to 180 months found that LDS (98%) and hybrid ceramic onlays (99%) exhibited comparable clinical survival outcomes [89].

From the results, immediate dentin sealing (IDS) enhanced fracture resistance [43, 49, 64], likely due to improved restoration adaptation from smoothing the preparation surface, rounding internal line angles, blocking undercuts, and reducing luting cement thickness [43, 90–92]. Sealing the freshly cut dentin surface also promotes better bonding [40], thereby increasing fracture resistance.

Clinical relevance, limitations, and further studies

The overall fracture loads of teeth restored by PCRs were 350-1,954 N for premolars and 880-4,995 N for molars, while maximum bite forces were 392 N for premolars and 602 N for molars [93]. This indicates that PCRs generally withstand masticatory forces. Restoration design and material selection should consider more than just fracture resistance. Shallow retentive PCRs or those with a peripheral shelf may enhance mechanical retention and support adhesives, especially poor dentinal substrates, and under high lateral forces. They also improve seating stability [94], ensuring proper contact with adjacent teeth compared to non-retentive overlays. Clinicians should consider our fracture resistance data when choosing between non-retentive and shallow retentive designs based on the specifics of each case.

The review was limited by the scarcity of studies on ZLS and PICN in premolars; however, existing molar research provided sufficient data for certain interpretations. Future studies should compare the fracture resistance of teeth-PCR assemblies, focusing on new preparation issues (e.g., occlusal steepness, shallow retentive depth) of modern lithium-silicate ceramics (fully crystallized LDS with no sintering and ZLS). Furthermore, the fracture resistance of teeth restored with PCRs featuring shallow, conservative retentive designs should be compared to anatomical non-retentive designs, with evaluation under oblique-horizontal forces (debonding forces). Further randomized control trials are needed to compare PCRs with different preparation designs, particularly between lithium-silicate-based and hybrid ceramics.
