Safety and immunogenicity of a COVID-19–Influenza Combination nanoparticle vaccine (CIC) containing SARS-CoV-2 recombinant spike and quadrivalent influenza hemagglutinin with Matrix-M® adjuvant: A phase 1/2 clinical trial
Authors: Vivek Shinde, Wayne Woo, Anthony M Marchese, Susan Neal, Joyce S Plested, Timothy S Vincent, Mingzhu Zhu, Shane Cloney-Clark, Iksung Cho, Louis Fries, Raburn M Mallory, Gregory M Glenn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBased on viral evolution, increases in seasonal infections, and facilitation of delivery, an annual combination vaccine simultaneously targeting influenza and SARS-CoV-2 would benefit public health. The safety and immunogenicity of a COVID-19–Influenza Combination nanoparticle vaccine (CIC) containing SARS-CoV-2 recombinant spike (rS) to the ancestral strain (Wuhan) and quadrivalent influenza hemagglutinin with a Matrix-M® saponin-based adjuvant were evaluated in a phase 1/2 blinded, randomized trial. The safety, tolerability, and immunogenicity of a CIC two-dose series were assessed and compared with standalone quadrivalent influenza (qNIV) and monovalent COVID-19 (NVX-CoV2373) vaccines. Reactogenicity events, unsolicited adverse events (AEs), antiviral microneutralization responses, hemagglutination-inhibition antibody titers, anti-rS protein immunoglobulin G, and human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 receptor binding inhibition antibody responses for SARS-CoV-2 were measured. All participants had been previously vaccinated against ancestral SARS-CoV-2. From September 8, 2021, to April 22, 2022, 642 participants were enrolled and randomized into the study; 637 received at least one vaccination. After each dose, CIC formulations induced antibody responses against homologous and heterologous influenza and SARS-CoV-2 strains that were comparable with the references. The CIC vaccine group had similar incidence of solicited and unsolicited AEs as the qNIV and NVX-CoV2373 reference groups. Severe unsolicited AEs only occurred in the CIC group but were infrequent and not considered related to the study vaccine. Overall, CIC formulations displayed reference-comparable safety and immunogenicity and provided data for statistical modeling analyses to optimize influenza hemagglutinin and SARS-CoV-2 rS antigen concentrations for use in phase 2 clinical trials investigating CIC.KEYWORDS: COVID-19, influenza, combination vaccine, immunogenicity, safety, Matrix-M adjuvant

Introduction

COVID-19 remains an ongoing public health issue1 due to the viral evolution of SARS-CoV-2 and waning immunity, both leading to immune evasion.2,3 As with other respiratory viruses, such as respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza,4 it appears that SARS-CoV-2 also circulates recurrently in a predominantly seasonal pattern with variant emergence,5 requiring COVID-19 vaccine strain and variant updates to expand immunity and afford protection. These respiratory viruses continue to negatively affect public health, causing 280 hospitalizations/100,000 people in the 2024–2025 season, peaking December through March.6 The safety and effectiveness of vaccines targeting influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV have been repeatedly demonstrated, with immunization reducing infection-related hospitalizations, thus supporting seasonal vaccination campaigns.7

Surveillance of circulating seasonal influenza variants is conducted annually to help determine the seasonal vaccine strain composition. Influenza vaccines ideally induce broadly cross-reactive antibodies and potent T-cell responses against variants; the H3N2 subtype is of particular interest as it typically results in higher morbidity and mortality than other variants.8,9 As with influenza, seasonal COVID-19 vaccines are updated to afford protection against the predominant circulating SARS-CoV-2 variant(s) at that time.10 One study estimates that more than 2.5 million deaths were prevented from the inception of COVID-19 vaccines through October 2024, the majority among individuals aged ≥60 y.11 The World Health Organization and the United States (US) Centers for Disease Control both support the concurrent administration of seasonal influenza and COVID-19 vaccines.12,13 Unfortunately, while >70% of individuals in the US received the primary series of the initial COVID-19 vaccine, a third dose was administered to <20% of the population.14 Additionally, survey results reported a drop in influenza vaccine use from >60% to 54% from 2020 to 2024, the most significant decrease occurring in populations with high vaccination rates in 2020.15 Immunization declines continue and are heavily related to vaccine hesitancy (e.g., individual concerns and doubts about vaccine efficacy and safety), as well as convenience.14,16,17 At the time of this report, dual vaccination of the influenza and COVID-19 vaccines requires the administration of two separate immunizations. A single vaccine using the same vaccine platform capable of evoking or enhancing immunity against both SARS-CoV-2 and seasonal influenza, and with a positive safety profile, has the potential to simultaneously address two major public health problems at the same time, reducing the burden on the healthcare system18 and increasing adherence to vaccination.19

NVX-CoV2373 is a protein vaccine comprising SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan) recombinant spike (rS) co-formulated with the saponin-based adjuvant, Matrix-M® (Novavax, Inc., Gaithersburg, MD, USA) and has had its safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy demonstrated in two phase 3 clinical trials (vaccine efficacy 89.7% and 90.4%).20,21 The Novavax COVID-19 vaccine is the only protein-based COVID-19 vaccine approved for use in the US, indicated for individuals aged ≥65 y or 12–64 y with a high-risk condition.22 This protein-based vaccine, as well as approved mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccines, has been, and continues to be, updated seasonally to address changes in predominant variants. Some relevant technological differences between the Novavax COVID-19 vaccine and mRNA formulations are that the latter rely on lipid nanoparticles for intracellular delivery and do not incorporate adjuvant, as well as require more restrictive storage conditions (e.g., long-term frozen storage with limited stability in standard refrigeration).23

A separate phase 3 investigation reported that a Matrix-M–adjuvanted, quadrivalent influenza vaccine (qNIV) was well tolerated and demonstrated robust antibody responses (i.e., hemagglutinin inhibition [HAI]) against homologous (types A and B) influenza strains.24 The influenza B/Yamagata lineage has historically been a component of this vaccine; however, it has not been detected after March 2020, prompting transition from quadrivalent to trivalent influenza vaccine formulations in investigations initiated after this shift.25

Clinical data demonstrate that both NVX-CoV2373 and qNIV, individually, are well tolerated, with the most common adverse events (AEs) being transient, mild-to-moderate injection-site reactions (e.g., tenderness and pain) or mild systemic events (e.g., headache, muscle pain, and fatigue).20,21,24 Here, safety, reactogenicity, and immunogenicity from a phase 1/2 study are reported for various formulations of a novel COVID-19–Influenza Combination nanoparticle vaccine (CIC) containing ancestral SARS-CoV-2 rS (which was current at the time of the study) and quadrivalent influenza hemagglutinins formulated with the Matrix-M adjuvant.

Methods

Study design

This phase 1/2 study (NCT04961541) was conducted at 10 sites in Australia (Table S1) in baseline seropositive (either previously infected with or immunized against SARS-CoV-2) adult participants (aged ≥50 to ≤70 y). Study participants were randomized into one of 16 treatment groups, comprising 14 CIC treatment groups (with 12 different dose level combinations), a qNIV reference group, and a SARS-CoV-2 rS reference group (Table 1). The primary safety objective was to describe unsolicited treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) and reactogenicity (solicited AEs) profiles. Immunogenicity objectives were to assess HAI antibody responses to vaccine-homologous and antigenically drifted influenza strains and antibody responses (anti-rS immunoglobulin G [IgG] and neutralizing antibodies) to ancestral SARS-CoV-2.

Intervention

Participants in the CIC treatment groups received two doses of one of the CIC formulations, the first at d 0 and the second at d 56. The 14 CIC treatment groups comprised 12 different dose level combinations (2.5–, 7.5–, or 22.5-µg SARS-CoV-2 rS protein with 5–, 10–, 35–, or 60-µg influenza hemagglutinin [HA] per strain and 50-µg Matrix-M). Participants in the SARS-CoV-2 rS reference group received two doses of NVX-CoV2373 (5-µg rS protein plus 50-µg Matrix-M), the first at d 0 and the second at d 56. Participants in the qNIV reference group received a single dose of qNIV (60-µg HA per strain plus 75-µg Matrix-M) on d 0 and two doses of NVX-CoV2373, the first at d 56 and the second at d 70 (to ensure these participants received two doses of the COVID-19 vaccine). The compositions of NVX-CoV2373 and qNIV administered to the reference groups were the same as those evaluated in phase 3 clinical trials.20,21,24 qNIV contained recombinant HA for the following influenza strains: A/Brisbane/02/2018 (H1N1) pdm09, A/Kansas/14/2017 (H3N2), B/Maryland/15/2016 (Victoria lineage), and B/Phuket/3073/2013 (Yamagata lineage). HA and rS dose levels were selected based on design of experiments modeling26 and benefit–risk assessment to allow for the widest antigen dose range permissible. The lower end of each range was selected based on the lowest dose presumed capable of inducing an observable immunogenic response, as well as being formulated consistently in a production setting; the highest dose was selected based on previous studies.24,27

Randomization and blinding

Study participants were randomized using an interactive web response system, with randomization stratified by age (50 to <60 y vs 60 to 70 y). Study-site personnel and participants were blinded to vaccine assignment until sponsor unblinding at d 70 for data analysis. Select onsite personnel unblinded from the start of the study managed vaccine logistics, preparation, and vaccine administration. This study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Conference on Harmonization Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and applicable national and local regulatory requirements. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at each participating site. All enrollees provided written informed consent before participating.

Participants

Eligible participants were healthy adults aged 50–70 y previously vaccinated against or infected with SARS-CoV-2 at least 8 weeks before study enrollment. SARS-CoV-2 serostatus was based on vaccination documentation (e.g., vaccination card and vaccination registry) or self-reporting of prior infection. Key exclusion criteria included any ongoing, symptomatic, acute, or chronic illness requiring medical or surgical care; use of COVID-19 prophylaxis or treatment with monoclonal antibodies or antibody cocktails in the 90 d preceding the first dose of the study vaccine; history of a serious reaction to influenza vaccination or known allergy to constituents of influenza vaccines; history of anaphylaxis to any vaccine; receipt of any vaccine in the 4 weeks before administration of the study vaccine; receipt of an influenza vaccine in the 2 months before administration of the study vaccine; and chronic (>14 continuous days) administration of any immune response-modifying drug in the 6 months before the first dose of the study vaccine.

Procedures

Reactogenicity was assessed as incidence of solicited local (i.e., injection-site pain, redness, swelling, or tenderness) or systemic (i.e., fatigue, fever, headache, joint pain, malaise, muscle pain, or nausea/vomiting) AEs within 7 d of each vaccination, as reported by participants. Unsolicited AEs (including severe AEs) were collected from the time of the first study vaccination through d 70 after vaccination. Serious AEs (SAEs), medically attended AEs (MAAEs), and AEs of special interest (AESIs; includes potentially immune-mediated medical conditions and AEs relevant to COVID-19) were collected from the time of the first study vaccination through the end of the study (d 182). MAAEs were defined as AEs leading to an unscheduled visit to a healthcare practitioner.

Blood samples for immunogenicity assessments28–30 were collected before vaccination (d 0 baseline) and at predefined time points after vaccination. Serum HAI antibody responses (assayed with wild-type, virus-like particle reagents with human RBCs) were assessed at d 28 (after first dose of trial vaccine), d 56, d 70 (after second dose of trial vaccine), d 84, and d 182 against four vaccine-homologous and four antigenically drifted (A/Wisconsin [H1N1]; A/Cambodia [H3N2]; A/Hong Kong [H3N2]; B/Washington [Victoria]) influenza strains. Additionally, neutralizing antibody (MN assay with an inhibitory concentration of 50% [MN50]) responses were assayed with live wild-type virus (homologous and drifted) at the same time points. Serum IgG antibody responses measured by the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay [ELISA] to SARS-CoV-2 rS, live SARS-CoV-2 neutralization antibody responses (MN50), and human angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (hACE2) receptor binding inhibition for the vaccine-homologous SARS-CoV-2 rS antigen (prototype strain) and the Omicron BA.1 variant were also assessed.30,31

Statistical analysis

The sample size for this trial was based on clinical and practical considerations, not on a formal power calculation. With 40 individuals per vaccine group, the probability of an AE with a true incidence in that group of ≥5% being reported for ≥1 participant was at least 87%. With 560 participants receiving a CIC formulation, the probability of ≥1 CIC recipient having a TEAE with a true incidence of 0.534% was 95%. Wild-type HAI antibody-response data from the phase 3 study of qNIV24 were used to provide a reference mean fold-rise at d 28. This average was then used in an informal calculation to estimate power for detecting a two-fold difference in immune response relative to qNIV alone. This calculation showed that a sample size of 40 would provide >99% power to differentiate the immune responses between two formulations.

The full analysis set (FAS) and the safety analysis set (SAS) both included all participants who were randomized and received at least one dose of the study vaccine (regardless of missing data or protocol violations). The FAS was analyzed according to randomization group, and the SAS was analyzed according to treatment actually received. The per-protocol (PP) analysis set for immunogenicity was a subset of participants from the FAS (determined for each study visit) and included all participants who received the full prescribed regimen of the study vaccine according to the protocol, had blood collected for immunogenicity assessment for baseline and at least one post-vaccination time point, and had no major protocol violations that were considered clinically relevant to affect immunogenicity. For time points after d 56, participants must have received both doses of the study vaccine to be included in the PP analysis set.

Analysis of the safety end points was descriptive and based on the SAS. Safety data were summarized by treatment group (groups A and C were combined, as were groups H and N), across all CIC groups (all CIC), and overall (total). Summaries included solicited TEAEs over 7 d after each of the first and second doses, unsolicited TEAEs through 70 d after the first dose, and MAAEs, SAEs, and AESIs through the end of study.

The analysis of immunogenicity end points was descriptive with no formal statistical testing. The PP analysis set was used, as the results from this population were of primary interest when selecting dose and formulation for further study. For homologous and antigenically drifted influenza strains and for results from the HAI and MN assays, titer data were summarized by geometric mean titer (GMT), geometric mean fold rise (GMFR), seroconversion rate (SCR), and seroprotection rate. For influenza, SCRs were defined as proportion of participants with either a baseline reciprocal (d 0) titer of <10 and a post-vaccination reciprocal titer of ≥40, or a baseline reciprocal (d 0) titer of ≥10 and a post-vaccination titer ≥4-fold higher than the baseline titer. Anti-rS serum IgG levels were described with geometric mean ELISA units (GMEUs), GMEU ratios (GMEURs) between groups at different time points, GMFR (post- versus pre-vaccination), and SCR (proportion of participants with post-vaccination concentrations ≥4-fold higher than baseline). Two-sided 95% CIs were calculated for GMTs, GMEURs, and SCRs. The same measures were calculated for HAI/MN50 titers (GMT and GMT ratio [GMTR] for influenza and MN50/hACE2 binding inhibition for SARS-CoV-2). Titers and concentrations reported below the lower limit of quantification were set to half that limit for use in computations. All analyses were performed using SAS® (version 9.4 or higher).

Results

Participants

Between September 8, 2021, and April 22, 2022, 745 individuals were screened, of whom 642 were enrolled and randomized (Table 1; Figure 1). A total of 637 participants received at least one vaccination and were included in the SAS and FAS; 618 (97.0%) participants received a second dose at d 56, and 37/39 (94.9%) in group O received a third dose at d 70. A total of 634 participants were included in the PP analysis set; 15 (2.4%) participants discontinued from vaccination and 11 (1.7%) discontinued from the study (7 lost to follow-up, 3 withdrew [1 due to an AE of acute myocardial infarction]).

Reactogenicity

Any solicited TEAE was reported by 506 (90.7%) participants who received a CIC dose, 39 (97.5%) administered standalone NVX-CoV2373, and 38 (97.4%) administered standalone qNIV (Table 3). Of those who reported solicited TEAEs, the majority were grade 1–2 and none were grade 4. Solicited local (injection-site) events were reported by 84.3% of recipients (CIC: 468 [83.9%], NVX-CoV2373: 35 [87.5%], qNIV: 34 [87.2%]). Reactogenicity events were reported at similar frequencies after first and second doses of CIC (dose 1: 302 [54.1%], dose 2: 243 [43.5%]), NVX-CoV2373 (20 [50.0%]; 20 [50.0%]), and qNIV (22 [56.4%]; 15 [38.5%]). The most common solicited local events were injection-site pain and injection-site tenderness (Figure 2(A)). Median duration of solicited local events was similar across CIC formulations. For the CIC group overall (either dose), median duration of pain and swelling was 1 d and of tenderness and redness was 2 d. Solicited systemic events were reported by a total of 66.4% of study participants (CIC: 366 [65.6%], NVX-CoV2373: 30 [75.0%], qNIV: 27 [69.2%]) (Table 3). The most common solicited systemic events were fatigue and headache (Figure 2(B)). Overall, fever was reported in eight (1.3%) participants (CIC: 5 [0.9%], NVX-CoV2373: 2 [5.0%], qNIV: 1 [2.6%]).

Safety

There were 240/637 (37.7%) participants who reported an unsolicited TEAE during the study; 40 (6.3%) participants had an unsolicited TEAE deemed related to treatment (Table 3). The proportions of participants reporting an unsolicited TEAE were comparable for CIC (total: 37.3% [n = 208]; related: 6.3% [n = 35]) and NVX-CoV2373 (35.0% [n = 14]; 7.5% [n = 3]), and slightly higher for qNIV (46.2% [n = 18]; 5.1% [n = 2]). The most common unsolicited TEAEs reported (across all participants) were infections and infestations (8.2%), musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (7.7%), injury, poisoning, and procedural complications (6.4%), and nervous system disorders (6.4%) (Table S2). Overall, severe unsolicited TEAEs were reported for 7 (1.1%) participants (no events considered related to study vaccination), all of whom were in one of the CIC groups (7/558 [1.3%]) (Table 3; Table S3).

SAEs were reported for 15 (2.4%) participants, with similar incidence in each group (CIC: 13 [2.3%]; NVX-CoV2373: 1 [2.5%]; qNIV: 1 [2.6%]) (Table 3). The most frequent SAEs (Table S4) included cardiac disorders (overall participants: 4 [0.6%]; CIC: 3 [0.5%]; qNIV: 1 [2.6%]), musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders (3 [0.5%], all from CIC groups), and infections and infestations (overall: 2 [0.3%]; CIC: 1 [0.2%]; NVX-CoV2373: 1 [2.5%]). There were no reported events of myocarditis/pericarditis. No SAEs were assessed by investigators as related to the study vaccine, and no clear relationship with dose level among CIC formulations was found.

At least one MAAE was reported for 152 (23.9%) participants, with comparable proportions in the CIC and qNIV groups (Table S5). Four of these were considered related to study vaccination (Table 3). There were no AESIs and no deaths reported during the study. Vaccination was discontinued because of an unsolicited TEAE for two (0.3%) participants (one participant with SAEs of acute myocardial infarction and ventricular fibrillation that occurred at d 47 after the first vaccination and one participant with iron deficiency anemia that occurred at d 38 after the first vaccination), both in the CIC group. One (0.2%) participant in the CIC group was withdrawn from the study because of an SAE of acute myocardial infarction that was unrelated to the study vaccine.

Immunogenicity

Influenza HAI antibody responses

Several CIC formulations induced antibody responses (titers and fold increase from baseline) comparable with the qNIV reference (HA 60 µg, Matrix-M 75 µg, rS 0 µg) after the first dose for both homologous and drifted strains (Figure 3; Figures S1–S3). After the first dose (d 28), A/Brisbane HAI antibody titers increased relative to baseline for all CIC groups (GMT range: 81.5–141.3; GMFR range: 1.1–1.7) and the qNIV reference group (GMT: 133.9; GMFR: 1.6). Serum HAI responses at d 28 to A/Kansas were higher relative to baseline for all CIC groups (GMT: 58.1–116.2; GMFR: 2.2–4.4) and the qNIV reference group (GMT: 145.1; GMFR: 5.4). D 28 HAI responses for B/Maryland were higher than baseline for the CIC groups (GMT range: 40.7–73.7; GMFR range: 1.1–1.6) and the qNIV reference group (GMT: 65.8; GMFR: 1.8). D 28 HAI responses for B/Phuket were higher relative to baseline for all CIC groups (GMT range: 35.2–78.3; GMFR range: 1.3–2.0) and the qNIV reference group (GMT: 100.1; GMFR: 2.7). There did not appear to be a clear impact of rS dose level on influenza HAI response. For all groups, administration of a second dose in CIC groups enhanced HAI antibody responses. HAI responses assessed for drifted strains showed similar results as described for homologous strains (Supplemental Results; Figures S2 and S3).

Influenza neutralizing antibody responses

As was observed with HAI antibody responses, influenza neutralizing antibody responses (MN50) were similar to the qNIV reference after the first vaccination at intermediate to high (35 μg to 60 µg) HA dose levels for A/Brisbane (H1N1), A/Kansas (H3N2), and B/Maryland (Victoria subtype), without a dose-related response to rS level (Supplemental Results; Figures S5–S8). At d 28, neutralizing antibody responses increased relative to baseline for all CIC groups for all four homologous strains (GMT range: 63.3–1617.1; GMFR range: 1.1–4.7) and relative to the qNIV reference group (GMT range: 111.5–1935.2, GMFR range: 1.7–6.2), with a GMTR range (95% CI) of 0.44 (0.29–0.68) to 1.01 (0.75–1.36). Neutralizing antibody responses for drifted strains had similar results.

Serum anti-rS IgG responses to SARS-CoV-2

Vaccine-homologous serum anti-rS IgG responses against ancestral (Wuhan) SARS-CoV-2 were observed 28 d after the first vaccination in all CIC groups (GMEU range: 7334.6–17,807.1; GMFR range: 1.5–4.0); groups that received 22.5 μg rS exhibited responses most similar to that of the SARS-CoV-2 reference (GMEU: 15,916.6; GMFR: 3.8) (Figure S9A; Figure S10A). At d 70, anti-rS IgG responses increased further (GMEU range: 9499.5– 31,832.6; GMFR range: 1.8–7.1), although levels were below that observed for the SARS-CoV-2 reference (GMEU: 49,648.9; GMFR: 11.2). After peaking at d 70, anti-rS IgG responses plateaued, remaining elevated through d 182 in all CIC groups and the reference group. After each vaccination, responses across the CIC groups increased with increasing rS doses and exhibited interference from HA as HA dose levels increased. When compared with the SARS-CoV-2 reference group at d 28, the anti-rS IgG responses in the CIC groups to ancestral SARS-CoV-2 were generally lower (GMEUR range: 0.42–0.90), other than Group E (HA10/rS22.5/M50), which had a GMEUR of 1.05 (Figure S9B). At d 70, the responses for all CIC groups were lower than the reference group (GMEUR range: 0.24–0.64). SCRs for the CIC groups ranged from 10.8% to 62.5% (SARS-CoV-2 reference: 86.8%) at d 70 (Figure S10B).

After dose 1, anti-rS IgG responses against Omicron BA.1 were observed in all CIC groups that received 22.5-µg rS (GMEU range: 3337.6–9532.8; GMFR range: 1.6–4.5), similar to the SARS-CoV-2 reference (GMEU: 9022; GMFR: 4.7) (Supplemental Results; Figures S9(C) and Figure 10(C)). After dose 2, anti-rS IgG responses for the CIC groups (GMFR range: 2.1–8.0) were less than that observed for the SARS-CoV-2 rS reference (GMFR: 15.4). As also observed with the ancestral strain, after each vaccination, responses across the CIC groups increased with rS dose and exhibited HA interference as HA dose levels increased. The responses for all CIC groups were lower than that observed for the SARS-CoV-2 rS reference for d 28 and d 70, as indicated by GMEURs <1.0 for all groups, other than Group G (HA5/rS22.5/M50) at d 28 (GMEUR: 1.01) (Figure S9D). SCRs for the CIC groups ranged from 21.6% to 72.5% at d 70, compared with 92.1% for the SARS-CoV-2 reference group (Figure S10D).

After dose 1, SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibody responses (MN50) displayed similar trends as anti-rS IgG responses, with CIC formulations that contained 22.5-µg rS producing responses most comparable with those elicited by the SARS-CoV-2 rS reference for both the ancestral strain and Omicron BA.1. SCRs for the CIC groups ranged from 28.2% to 82.9% at d 28 and from 29.3% to 75.0% at d 70 (reference: 86.8%) (Figure 4(G)). hACE2 receptor binding inhibition

Following dose 1, hACE2 receptor binding inhibition to SARS-CoV-2 rS was observed across the CIC groups, with responses for the CIC groups that received 22.5-µg rS similar to those for the SARS-CoV-2 reference group (Supplemental Results; Figure S12). Levels of hACE2 after dose 2 returned to levels similar to those observed after dose 1 for the CIC groups but did not achieve the same level of response as the SARS-CoV-2 rS reference.

Discussion

In this phase 1/2 study, various novel CIC formulations targeting influenza and COVID-19 were well tolerated, and key factors related to antigen dosing that appear to affect immunogenicity were identified. Immunological analysis of the first and second doses of CIC at various antigen dose combinations suggests mutual interference of HA- and rS-specific humoral responses that were dependent on antigen concentration. A previous study of coadministered NVX-CoV2373 and seasonal influenza vaccines in the United Kingdom noted some immune interference; however, this did not translate to interference of vaccine efficacy.32 Interference is a clinically relevant consideration for combination immunizations,33 and these phase 1 findings with the CIC formulation can be used to guide optimization of HA and rS doses in a combined vaccine.

Some CIC formulations tested here induced antibody responses that matched standalone qNIV and COVID-19 reference vaccines (i.e., for H1N1, H3N2, B-Victoria, and rS). Following each vaccination, SARS-CoV-2 serum IgG responses across the CIC groups exhibited interference from HA as its dose levels increased, and SARS-CoV-2 neutralization and hACE2 receptor binding inhibition activities exhibited qualitatively similar trends. To generate optimal humoral immunity, it appears that higher doses of rS are needed in the CIC vaccine than the standalone rS vaccine. Conversely, a somewhat lower dose of influenza HA in the CIC formulation may elicit an influenza HAI and/or MN response, which, while not attaining the same acute peak at 28 d, could potentially provide a non-inferior area under the antibody curve over an extended period spanning the entire winter virus season, thereby providing enhanced immunity to both pathogens with a single vaccine product. A separate design of experiments approach with response surface modeling methods was conducted to determine the optimal dose levels of HA and rS antigens and examine acute responses and antibody durability throughout the season.26

The safety profiles of all investigated CIC vaccine doses were generally comparable with those of standalone NVX-CoV2373 and qNIV, with a similar incidence of solicited and unsolicited TEAEs. The first and second doses of CIC elicited similar local and systemic reactogenicity as NVX-CoV2373, with no grade 4 events reported. This suggests a favorable reactogenic profile of the CIC vaccine, in line with other studies demonstrating a lower frequency and severity of reactogenicity events with NVX-CoV2373 than messenger RNA (mRNA) COVID-19 vaccines.34,35 Severe unsolicited TEAEs only occurred in the CIC group but were infrequent and not considered related to the study vaccine.

A preplanned, exploratory substudy of a previously completed phase 3 clinical trial of healthy adults assessed participants who received a primary vaccination series with NVX-CoV2373 plus an approved influenza vaccine (n = 217) or placebo plus influenza vaccine (n = 214).32 In this substudy, coadministration of the vaccines led to a numerical reduction in anti-rS IgG titers compared with receipt of NVX-CoV2373 alone, but had no effect on HAI antibody responses compared with receipt of placebo plus the influenza vaccine.32 Furthermore, a combined administration of NVX-CoV2373 and a seasonal influenza vaccine showed higher reactogenicity than a combination placebo with the influenza vaccine. Notably, that finding was not recapitulated here after CIC vaccination, suggesting that concomitant vaccine administration had unique influenza and SARS-CoV-2 immune responses and reactogenicity compared with a single combined dose.

At the time of this report, there had been limited investigations for combination vaccines for COVID-19 and influenza. One report describes a mRNA-based formulation of a quadrivalent influenza vaccine and COVID-19 vaccine targeting the ancestral (Wuhan) strain and Omicron BA.4/BA.5 subvariant.36 Interim results from a phase 1/2 study (NCT05827926) 29 d after study vaccination observed that most participants reported mild/moderate reactogenicity, similar distribution unsolicited TEAEs with the combination as individual vaccine comparators, and positive antibody responses to homologous influenza strains. Differences in patient populations and study designs do not allow for cross-trial comparison between this mRNA vaccine study and the CIC study outcomes; however, notable features of the CIC study are investigation of immunogenicity and safety through 182 d and assessment of antibody responses against heterologous influenza strains.

Despite the inclusion of various rS and HA dose levels, this study was limited by the evaluation of a single dose level (50 μg) of the Matrix-M adjuvant. Although this concentration of the Matrix-M adjuvant is equivalent to that contained in NVX-CoV2373, it is 25 μg lower than that of qNIV. This difference may explain, at least in part, the numerical reduction in HAI antibody response against B/Phuket (Yamagata subtype) seen after immunization with the combined versus standalone vaccine. A higher dose of Matrix-M may provoke superior immunogenicity, potentially leading to further dose sparing, and warrants further investigation. Notably, prior data have shown that the 75-µg dose of Matrix-M is well tolerated in individuals aged ≥65 y.24 Due to local COVID-19 vaccine availability prior to this trial, approximately 2/3–3/4 of participants were primed with the adenoviral ChAdOx-1-S vaccine, while almost the entire remainder had received the BnT162b2 mRNA vaccine, with all treatment groups having a similar distribution (Table 2). Interestingly, after correction for d 0 baselines, the ratios of anti-S IgG GMEU responses to boosting in the two strata defined by primer were close to 1 (1.09 at d 28 and 1.26 at d 70, marginally favoring the ChAdOX-1-S recipients). Baseline titers were included in the design of experiment model and, given the similar distribution of the priming regimens across treatment groups and the near-comparable magnitude of booster-induced increases in anti-S IgG GMEU regardless of priming, we believe the insights from the design of experiment analyses should be robust.

In conclusion, these results showed that this protein-based combination vaccine targeting both SARS-CoV-2 and seasonal influenza is well tolerated and immunogenic. Although there were indications that rS and HA antigens had modest interference with each other, dose adjustments of antigen and adjuvant can optimize the CIC response. These data, and results from the aforementioned modeling study,26 support further exploration of the effect of higher rS (25 µg and 35 µg) and Matrix-M (75 µg) doses and HA doses of 30 µg to 60 µg against a changing immune history. A phase 2 (NCT05519839) formulation dose confirmation study further investigating these doses and a phase 3 study (NCT06291857) of CIC have been completed with reports in preparation.
