Genetic engineering of gut commensals: heterologous protein expression in Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145
Authors: Alba Arévalo-Lalanne, Paola Corbín-Agustí, Daniel Ramón, Javier Buceta, Juli Peretó, Marta Tortajada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Controlling membrane fluidity through fatty acid composition, culture conditions, and permeabilizing agents significantly improves transformation efficiency in recalcitrant B. animalis subsp. lactis strains without prior genetic adaptation.•Selection of optimal plasmids and promoters enables high and reproducible protein expression even in low-efficiency transformants as B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145.•Combined strain-specific physiological insights (membrane fatty acids, methylome profile, plasmid copy number) with optimized transformation protocols can overcome genetic recalcitrance in probiotic and commensal Bifidobacterium strains.Keywords: Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis, CECT8145, Genetic transformation, Electroporation, Probiotics

Introduction

Bifidobacteria is an ecologically and functionally critical genus in the maintenance of gut homeostasis (Sarkar and Mandal, 2016). Well-established as a beneficial group within gut microbiome ecosystem, bifidobacteria are progressively acquired during infancy as the immune system develops, and their decline is a landmark of aging (Arboleya et al., 2016). Several probiotic bifidobacterial strains, such as Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis DSM 15954 (Shehata et al., 2021), ATCC-SD6685 (Uusitupa et al., 2020) or CECT8145 (Martorell et al., 2016) have been characterized and their administration to humans has been shown to support immune, digestive and metabolic health, among other benefits. In particular, the strain B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT 8145 has been demonstrated to positively influence markers of metabolic health, such as adiposity, in preclinical models, including the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, Zücker and Wistar rats fed with a cafeteria diet, as well as in human volunteers with abdominal obesity (Caimari et al., 2017; Pedret et al., 2019). Notably, the effects of the strain are kept in its postbiotic, heat-treated form, due to the presence of a singular composition of lipoteichoic acid in its cell-wall (Balaguer et al., 2022).

The elucidation of the probiotic pathways underpinning the health benefits and interaction with the host requires molecular tools and methods to achieve genetic transformation of the strain. This allows the study of structure-function relationships and mechanisms of action. While the biochemical basis of B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 functionality has been identified, as well as its target pathways in the host (Amat-Bou et al., 2020; Balaguer et al., 2022; Martorell et al., 2016), the cluster responsible for lipoteichoic acid synthesis remains undetermined. To fully elucidate its mechanism of action, sequential deletion and overexpression of target genes has been applied to a few probiotic strains (Claes et al., 2012). However, the lack of effective molecular tools in Bifidobacterium makes it very challenging to study the genetic basis of their health-promoting benefits (Fukiya et al., 2018).

Indeed, bifidobacteria, part of Actinomycetota phylum, are notably recalcitrant to genetic transformation due to their high G + C content, complex nutritional requirements, sensitivity to oxygen and unstable DNA cloning (Alvarez-Martín et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2025). Their extensive restriction-modification (R-M) systems further hinder their molecular tools development, which remain minimal (Pan et al., 2022). As a result, genetic transformation of bifidobacteria results in low average efficiencies, ranging from hundreds to tens of thousands of transformants per μg DNA (Sun et al., 2012), in sharp contrast with other Actinomycetota widely used in industry, such as Corynebacterium, or probiotic lactic acid bacteria, which typically yield at least 104 CFU per μg DNA.

Within bifidobacteria, very few studies have achieved B. animalis subsp. lactis transformation, despite being one of the most established probiotic species. Previous reports focus on a selected strain (Alvarez-Martín et al., 2008; Serafini et al., 2012; M. O′Connell Motherway et al., 2014; Liu et al., 2025). Optimization is approached by heuristic adjustment of culture and electroporation conditions and by extensive previous genetic modification of the target host, either inactivating R-M systems (M. O′Connell Motherway et al., 2014a), adaptive evolution or overexpressing competence genes (Liu et al., 2025), all resulting in strain-specific strategies.

In this study, we present, for the first time, a viable and reproducible genetic transformation strategy for the B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 probiotic strain. We also analyze the potential factors hindering transformation, particularly R-M systems and the composition of the cell envelope and membrane, while optimizing the methodology. Additionally, we compare the efficiency of this approach with other probiotic strains, paving the way for successful transformation of other fastidious, industrially relevant strains.

Bacterial strains and plasmids utilized in this work.

Escherichia coli strains were sourced from commercial suppliers (Table 1) and cultured at 37 °C in LB medium with agitation or on LB agar plates. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in rotary incubators. For plasmid maintenance, growth media were supplemented with antibiotics at the following concentrations: ampicillin (Ap; 100 µg/mL), chloramphenicol (Cm; 25 µg/mL), erythromycin (Em; 150 µg/mL) or tetracycline (Tet; 100 µg/mL), according to the resistance marker encoded by the plasmid used (Table 1).

Bifidobacteria strains were retrieved by isolation from commercial nutritional supplements labeling the corresponding isolates (Table 1). All have been identified as wild type isolates, originally isolated from dairy products or infant feces (Table 1). They were routinely cultivated in commercial MRS medium (Oxoid) or in MRS plates supplemented with 1.5% (w/v) agar. MRS medium was supplemented with 0.05% wt/vol filter-sterilized cysteine (MRSC). All bifidobacteria strains were cultured at 37 °C under anaerobic atmosphere in an anaerobic chamber (BACTRONEZ-2; 5% CO2, 5% H2, 90% N2). The use of anaerobic jars for transformation purposes was avoided as recovery efficiencies were decreased, possibly due to impaired growth of transformants resulting from less stringent control of anaerobic conditions (Cox et al., 1987). Bifidobacteria growth media were supplemented with antibiotics at the following concentrations: chloramphenicol (Cm; 4 µg/mL), erythromycin (Em; 5 µg/mL) or tetracycline (Tet; 5 µg/mL), according to the resistance marker encoded by the plasmid used (Table 1).

DNA isolation and analysis

Plasmid DNA (plDNA) from E. coli was isolated using the PureLink™ HiPure Plasmid Midiprep kit (Thermo Scientific, USA), according to manufacturer’s instructions. For bifidobacterial transformants, plDNA was extracted using the GeneJET Plasmid Miniprep kit (Themo Scientific, USA). Total DNA from bifidobacteria wild type strains and transformants was isolated with the DNAeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany) for methylome analysis and plasmid copy number quantification by ddPCR, respectively. For bifidobacterial DNA extraction, an additional incubation step with lysozyme (16,000 U/mL) and mutanolysin (240 U/mL) for 1 h at 37 °C was included in the resuspension buffer. The plDNA concentrations used for transformation were quantified using the Nanodrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). More accurate DNA quantifications for downstream applications requiring high precision, including methylome analysis and plasmid copy number determination by ddPCR, were performed using the Qubit 1X dsDNA High-Sensitivity Assay kit (Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer, Thermo Fisher, USA). For screening potential transformants, 2X Taq Master Mix (Genscript, The Netherlands) was used following the standard protocols. PCR products were verified by electrophoresis in 0.5X TAE buffer on 1.5 % agarose gels and visualized using RedSafe staining with Imager 2 System (VWR).

Plasmid construction

The plasmids used in this study are shown in Table 1. All new constructed plasmids were synthesized by Genscript, using the plasmid pAM1 (6205 bp) as backbone (GenBank: LT726871.1) (Álvarez-Martín et al., 2007). Gene mrfp sequence for mCherry protein expression was retrieved from NCBI (GenBank: KP182347.1, (Russo et al., 2015), cloned under control of pTu, pGAPDH, pHUP, and pLacZ promoters. The construction was synthesized with flanking PstI and XbaI restriction sites and subsequently inserted into pAM1 (Fig. 1). The sequences of pTu (elongation factor Tu from B. longum subsp. infantis CECT4551) and pHUP (histone-like protein coding gene from B. longum ATCC15707) promoters were obtained from previous works (Landete et al., 2014; Takeuchi et al., 2002), while pGAPDH, and pLacZ promoters were extracted from CECT8145 genome. The upstream sequences of the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase encoding gene (pGAPDH) and lac operon were analyzed using BPROM tool (Softberry) to select the putative promoter sequences (Solovyev and Salamov, 2011). The complete sequences of promoters are included in Supplementary Materials (Table S1).

Transformation procedures

Unless otherwise stated, plasmids were introduced into E. coli DH10B and DH5α chemically competent cells through heat shock following manufacturer’s instructions. E. coli transformed cells were recovered in 1 mL of SOC medium (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 37 °C with 180 rpm aeration for 1 h and subsequently plated on selective LB agar medium supplemented with the appropriate antibiotic according to the corresponding plasmid-encoded resistance. For clarity, pAM1, pAM1-derivatives and pAM4 E. coli transformants were plated on LB agar bearing Ap 100 µg/mL, and pKO403_lacZ_Cm E. coli transformants on LB agar bearing Cm 25 µg/mL. All antibiotics types and concentrations used throughout the study are specified in Section 2.1. Plates were incubated aerobically overnight at 37 °C.

Bifidobacterial competent cells were prepared in MRSC medium (50 mL) inoculated with an overnight culture to a final optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.15. Unless stated otherwise (e.g. during transformation optimization experiments), the cultures were incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 3 to 4 h, until early exponential phase (OD600 of 0.3). Cells were then chilled on ice for 30 min, washed twice in precooled washing buffer (WB; 30% polyethylen glycol PEG1500, 1 mM ammonium citrate) and suspended in 225 µL of the same buffer. Electrocompetent cells aliquots (60 µL) were mixed with 1.5 µg of plDNA and stored on ice for 20 min. Electroporation was performed at 2.5 kV in 2 mm gap cuvettes, 200 Ω and 25 µF in a Gene Pulser Xcell Microbial Electroporation System (Bio-Rad, EEUU). Cells were recovered in 1 mL of MRSC broth and incubated overnight before plating onto selective MRSC agar supplemented with the appropiate antibiotic according to the plasmid-encoded resistance. For clarity, pAM1, pAM1-derivatives and pAM1 Bifidobacterium transformants were plated on MRSC agar supplemented with Em 5 µg/mL, while pNZ:TuR.mCherry and pKO403_LacZ_Cm Bifidobacterium transformants were both plated on MRSC agar supplemented with Cm 4 µg/mL. Plates were incubated for 4 to 6 days in an anaerobic chamber (BACTRONEZ-2; 5% CO2, 5% H2, 90% N2). When aurintricarboxilic acid (ATA) was used, it was added to the WB and all MRSC media at a final concentration of 1 mM. When conducting optimization of the electroporation conditions via Design of Experiments (DoE), JMP® Statistical Discovery Software was used. DoE was performed based on a L18 orthogonal array, with 3 three-level factors (plDNA concentration, electroporation voltage and resistance), and 18 randomized transformation conditions, varying plDNA concentration (0.5, 1.25, 2 µg), electroporation voltage (1.25, 1.875, 2.5 kV) and resistance (200, 300, 400 Ω) and capacitance fixed at 25 µF (Taguchi, 1987). The detailed conditions evaluated can be found in the Supplementary Materials Section (Table S2).

Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy analyses

Bifidobacterial cultures were grown in 5 mL of MRSC for wild type strains and MRSC supplemented with Em 5 µg/mL for transformant strains, inoculated to an initial OD600 of 0.15 and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 22 h (until reaching stationary-phase). The cultures were analyzed by flow cytometry in three independent experiments and by fluorescence microscopy.

For flow cytometry, 1 mL of cultures recovered at 22 h, having reached stationary-phase, were harvested, washed twice by centrifugation at 14.000 g for 1 min with 1X PBS, and finally suspended in 1 mL of 1X PBS. The analysis were performed at the Central Service for Experimental Research Support (SCSIE) of the University of Valencia (UV) using a BD LSRFortessa™ flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). Bacterial populations were identified with a blue laser (488 nm) to record forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) signals. Expression of the fluorescent protein mCherry was assessed with excitation at 561 nm and emission detection using a 610/20 nm band-pass filter. Sample acquisition was carried out with FACS Diva™ software (BD Biosciences), and data were processed using FlowJo™ software (BD Biosciences), obtaining the fluorescence per detected event.

For fluorescence microscopy, stationary-phase cultures, retrieved at 22 h of growth as described above, were used. The bifidobacteria cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 1, and subsequently serially diluted. From the five-fold diluted suspension, 2 µL were mounted on low melting-point agarose pads prepared with supplemented M9 medium (Deter et al., 2019), dried and transferred to an IBIDI µ-Dish (35 mm, low) for imaging. Imaging was performed with a Leica Thunder inverted microscope (63 × oil immersion objective, Leica DFC9000 GTC CMOS camera). Köhler illumination was applied to enhance contrast in transmitted light mode (phase contrast). Fluorophore excitation was provided by a Lumencor Spectra-X Light Engine (8 solid-state LEDs), using 575 nm light for mCherry. Filter settings were: GFP/mCherry excitation 495–517 nm / 555–591 nm and emission 500–540 nm / 602–668 nm, combined with an external filter wheel at 642 ± 40 nm to select mCherry emission. Both transmitted light and fluorescence channels were acquired with an exposure time of 150 ms with Leica LAS X software. For the fluorescence channel, the light source power was set to 25% of the nominal 500 mW output. Image analysis was performed using Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2012). Quantification of fluorescence intensity was carried out using machine-learning segmentation tool (Arganda-Carreras et al., 2017). That was trained to distinguish cells from background and generate a binary mask. Bacterial clusters and piled-up cells were excluded to prevent artifacts in fluorescence per unit area measurements. The binary masks were applied to fluorescence images, and fluorescence histograms as well as fluorescence per unit area (µm²) were calculated using a custom-made script in Wolfram Mathematica (V13.3).

Plasmid copy number analysis

Plasmid copy number was determined at Instituto de Investigación Sanitaria (INCLIVA) by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) using strain- and plasmid-specific probes. The probes were designed using the Bio-Rad commercial design tool. The chromosomal reference probe, labeled with HEX, targeted the gene encoding the acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain, a conserved single-copy gene in the B. animalis subsp. lactis genome. The plasmid-specific probe, labeled with FAM, targeted the erythromycin resistance gene encoded on the plasmid. The nucleotide sequences of the probes are listed in Table 2. Reactions were performed using a Bio-Rad C1000 Touch thermocycler with 4 technical replicates per analyzed transformant. The data were acquired on a Bio-Rad QX200 system and analyzed with QuantaSoft Software (Bio-Rad).

Analysis of cell wall components

Analysis of peptidoglycan structure, polar lipids and fatty acids were carried out at DSMZ Services, Leibniz-Institut DSMZ – Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen GmbH (Braunschweig, Germany). The required biomass was generated growing the strain in MRSC liquid medium and incubating the flasks at 37 °C in an anaerobic chamber. Cells were collected after 210 min of incubation, at OD600 0.3, in the early-log phase and after 7 h of incubation, at OD600 1.5, in the middle-log phase, and frozen until analysis. Peptidoglycan isolation and the study of its structure were performed as described in (Schumann, 2011). Cellular fatty acids were analyzed after conversion into fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) by saponification, methylation and extraction following previous published protocols (Sasser, 2017). FAME mixtures were separated by gas chromatography and detected by a flame ionization detector. In subsequent analysis, fatty acids were identified on an Agilent GC–MS 7000D system (Vieira et al., 2021). Position of single double bounds were confirmed by derivatization to corresponding dimethyl disulfide adduct (Moss and Lambert-Fair, 1989). Branched-chain fatty acid positions, cyclopositions and multiple double bounds were determined by derivatization to their 3-pyridylcarbinol and 4.4-dimethyloxazoline (DMOX) derivatives (Harvey, 1982; Spitzer, 1996; Yu et al., 1988). For data presentation, individual isomers differing only in double-bond position, branching pattern, or cyclopropane ring formation were summed and reported as the corresponding fatty acid base type. Polar lipids were extracted from freeze cells using a chloroform:methanol 0.3 % aqueous NaCl mixture and recovered from the chloroform phase, separated by two-dimensional silica gel thin layer chromatography in chloroform:methanol:water, as well as chloroform:methanol:acetic acid:water. Total lipid material was detected using molybdatophosphoric acid and specific functional groups were detected using spray reagents specific for defined functional groups (Tindall et al., 2007).

Analysis of restriction-modification (R-M) systems

R-M systems can lower transformation efficiencies, by degrading incoming DNA that lacks the host-specific methylation pattern. In order to determine if a singular R-M system was potentially impacting transformation in the bifidobacteria strains used, and particularly, the CECT8145 strain, a comparative analysis of R-M systems of the 5 strains used (Table 1) versus other B. animalis subsp. lactis strains, with complete genomes available, was conducted. Protein sequences of restriction enzymes were obtained from the REBASE database (http://rebase.neb.com, accessed October 1, 2024) (Roberts et al., 2023). Genomes of 29 B animalis subsp. lactis strains were retrieved using the NCBI Datasets tool, selecting only complete assemblies from RefSeq, and their corresponding protein FASTA files. The presence of restriction enzymes was assessed using MMseqs2 (https://mmseqs.com/v15.6f452) (Steinegger and Söding, 2017). The REBASE protein dataset was clustered with mmseqs cluster at a sequence identity threshold of 90% and a minimum bidirectional coverage of 80%. Representative sequences from each cluster were extracted for genome comparisons. Genome searches were performed with mmseqs search against the clustered REBASE database. Results were filtered to retain only the best hit for each query, based on minimum coverage of 80% and an e-value threshold of ≤ 1e-5. Data visualization was performed using the seaborn Python package (v0.12.2). Type I, II and IV R-M systems were identified across strains through their corresponding recognition sequences.

DNA methylation analysis

Similarly, in order to determine whether complex sets of methylation motifs were present, that could potentially render the strains less genetically tractable, the methylomes of different B. animalis subsp. lactis strains were sequenced and analyzed at the Genomics Service of SCSIE (UV). Briefly, single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing was performed, and methylation patterns were identified using SMRT Link (PacBio, v11.0.0.146107). The methylation motifs occurrence was analyzed using the Sequence Manipulation Suite (Stothard, 2000), with the transformation plasmids and methylation motifs sequences as input. Sequence logos were generated with WebLogo v2.8.2 (Crooks et al., 2004) using the hits obtained from the Sequence Manipulation Suite.

Results

Stepwise adjustment of transformation factors enables successful genetic modification of B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 strain.

A variety of plasmids, electroporation and incubation conditions were sequentially evaluated in order to address the transformation of B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 (Table 1). As a baseline, the conditions established by Alvarez-Martin and coworkers (2008) for multiple bifidobacteria species were used. Briefly, the CECT8145 strain was cultured overnight, recovered at OD600 0.6 in chilled sucrose-containing buffer and electroporated at 25 μF, 200 Ω, and 2 kV. Two pBC1-derived vectors, pAM1 and pAM4, as well as two plasmids referenced for bifidobacteria transformation, pNZ:TuR.mCherry, derived from pNZ8048, and PKO403_lacZ_Cm, a pKK427 derivative, were used for screening purposes. Detailed plasmid characteristics are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1. No viable transformants were obtained in the baseline conditions for strain CECT8145 (Table 3, 1st row). Subsequently, systematic optimization of electroporation parameters through a DoE strategy was applied (Taguchi, 1987), screening 18 independent conditions overall (ranging between 0.5–2 µg plDNA for plDNA concentration, 1.25–2.5 kV for voltage and 200–400 Ω for resistance, Table 3, 2nd row). More details can be found in the Materials and Methods section and in the Supplementary Materials (Table S2). This Design of Experiments did not result in viable transformants of strain either for any of the conditions assessed.

Since factors like cell-wall integrity and control of restriction-modification (R-M) systems have been shown to be key to increase transformation efficiency in other bifidobacteria (Park et al., 2019), a series of iterations using potential modulation agents of R-M systems were conducted. For this purpose, an endonuclease inhibitor (ATA), and a membrane-destabilizing agent (PEG) were applied. ATA is a general inhibitor of nucleases, including endonucleases, and it has been used to protect bacterial nucleic acids (Hallick et al., 1977). In turn, PEG is known to increase transformation efficiency in fungi, as well as in bacteria with thick cell walls such as the one present in actinobacteria like Streptomyces. While ATA addition alone did not allow to retrieve viable colonies, the combined use of PEG and ATA to the electroporation buffer resulted in a small number of transformants with both pAM1 and pNZ:TuR.mCherry (Table 2).

Additional improvement was achieved by recovering electrocompetent cells at early exponential phase, established for strain CECT8145 in OD600 0.3, as previously described by other authors for other bifidobacteria species (Li et al., 2024; Serafini et al., 2012). In these conditions, the use of PEG alone provided the highest number of transformants, when used in combination with plasmid pAM1. Therefore, the conditions selected for further experimentation included PEG electroporation buffer, no ATA addition, early exponential phase recovery at OD600 0.3 for electrocompetent cells, and the use of pAM1 plasmid backbone.

The transformation procedure established for CECT8145 strain is efficient when applied to other B. animalis subsp. lactis strains, while highlighting strain-specific variations

To further validate the method across different strains of the same species, independent rounds of transformation were performed using B. animalis subsp. lactis DSM 15954, ATCC SD-6685, DSM 10140T, and CNCM I-2494 strains with the same system (Fig. 2). Using our protocol, all strains were successfully transformed, with an average of approximately 102 CFU/μg DNA.

Substantial variation in transformation efficiencies were observed for all strains, across rounds of transformation, and between strains (Fig. 2). In general, transformation efficiencies for B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 were on the lower end of the spectrum, ranging from 1.3 CFU/µg DNA to 7.7 × 102 cfu/μg DNA (mean = 1.8 × 102 CFU/μg DNA), while other probiotic strains from the same species, such as DSM15954, exhibited efficiencies approximately 20-fold higher, ranging from 3.3 CFU/μg DNA to 5.6 × 103 CFU/μg DNA (mean = 4 × 103 CFU/μg DNA). Strain DSM15954 was also the one achieving the highest transformation efficiencies of all five strains analyzed using our protocol, close to 104 cfu/μg DNA, consistent with previous studies (Alvarez-Martín et al., 2008). Conversely, CNCM I-2494 and DSM 10140 strains provided low transformation efficiencies, barely resulting in few viable transformants per transformation round (mean = 9.5 CFU/µg DNA and 5.5 × 101 CFU/μg DNA respectively). Previous transformation experiments using other protocols reported higher transformation efficiencies for these strains (2.5 × 103 CFU/μg DNA for DSM 10140 (Pan et al., 2022); and 4.4 × 103 CFU/μg DNA for CNCM I-2494, (M. O′Connell Motherway et al., 2014)). To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first successful description of genetic transformation for the ATCC SD-6685 strain (mean = 6.9 × 10¹ CFU/µg DNA), along with B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145.

R-M systems are conserved across B. animalis subsp. lactis strains, while methylome profiles show strain-specific variations.

Given the low transformation efficiencies found in B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145, we hypothesized that a singular R-M system may be present in this strain, potentially impacting negatively the process. Therefore, we conducted an analysis of methyltransferases (MTases) and restriction endonuclesases (REases) of B. animalis subsp. lactis complete assembled genomes deposited in NCBI database, comparing them in the CECT8145 strain vs benchmark strains such as DSM15954. All analyzed bifidobacteria genomes harbor putative enzymes related to Type I, II and IV R-M systems. Among the five B. animalis subsp. lactis previously transformed strains (CNCM I-2494, DSM 10140, ATCC SD-6685, DSM15954 and CECT8145), the only difference detected is the absence in ATCC SD-6685 of a type IV enzyme, a methyl-directed restriction enzyme that specifically cleaves only modified DNA. Genome annotation indicates that this gene is a pseudogene (Fig. 3). Therefore, this analysis did not identify R-M system differences that could explain the variation in transformation efficiencies, suggesting that additional factors may underlie the observed strain-dependent variability.

From the putative enzymes identified through REBASE, the recognition sequence of the Type II REase Ban8145ORF124P (RTCAGG) was detected, encoded in the CECT8145 genome by the gene annotated as “DUF262 domain-containing protein”. The corresponding methylation motif of the type II MTase M.Ban504ORFAP (RTCAGG) was also described, encoded in the CECT8145 genome as “modification_methylase”. Together, these enzymes constitute a canonical Type II R-M system (Roberts et al., 2003; Suzuki and Yasui, 2011). In addition, the putative Type II MTase M.Ban12ORF1698P (GGWCC) was detected, encoded in the CECT8145 genome as “DNA cytosine methyltransferase”.

Similarly, in the DNA methylation analysis, the CECT8145 strain showed few differences compared to DSM 15954 and ATCC-SD6685 strains (Fig. 4A). Specifically, m4C and m6A modifications and their corresponding methylation motifs were identified, as well as an undetermined category referred as “modified base”, which encompasses methylation types that cannot be classified using this technique. The CECT8145 strain exhibited only one methylation motif associated with m4C and another with m6A, while no “modified base” motifs were detected. The 5′-RTC6mAGG-3′ methylation motif is shared by all three analyzed strains, and it is also detected in CNCM I-2494, DSM10140 and other B. animalis subsp. lactis strains with previously published methylomes (M. O′Connell Motherway et al., 2014b; Pan et al., 2022). In addition, the analysis of the genome revealed a relatively low degree of methylation in CECT8145 (0.33 %), whereas DSM 15,954 and ATCC SD-6685 showed higher levels, at 1.72 % and 3.34 % respectively.

In turn, the transformation plasmids pAM1, pNZ:Tu.mCherry, and pKO403_lacZ_Cm were analyzed to determine whether the detected methylation motifs were present in their sequences (Supplementary Material, Table S3). Among the two plasmids that yielded transformants, these motifs were detected at multiple positions along the sequences in all three B. animalis subsp. lactis strains examined. Membrane fluidity plays a critical role in B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 strain electrotransformation efficacy.

As no highly significant differences with respect to other B. animalis subsp. lactis strains had been found in R-M systems or methylation patterns that could justify the low transformation efficiencies resulting in the CECT8145 strain, several features of its cell envelope were analyzed. Different fractions of CECT8145 cell wall and membrane were analyzed to establish whether a distinctive composition could be underlying its low transformability, running a comparison between cells harvested at early and mid-exponential phases, since this factor had shown the greatest impact in transformation efficacy rates (Table 3).

Firstly, a peptidoglycan hydrolysate was produced and found to contain muramic acid (Mur) and the amino acids lysine (Lys), ornithine (Orn), alanine (Ala) and glutamic acid (Glu). Peptide analysis confirmed the following type (Vollmer et al., 2008): A3α l-Lys(L-Orn) - l-Ala3, a variant of l-Lys-l-Ala3 for both conditions, in accordance with previously described structures in bifidobacteria (Pyclik et al., 2020). Secondly, polar lipids were extracted and analyzed by thin-layer chromatography. Diphosphatidylglycerols (the most abundant), phosphatidylglycerols, phospholipids, glycophospholipids, and glycolipids were found in both samples, without significant variations in the apparent contribution of these groups between both conditions analyzed (data not shown). Finally, the composition of membrane fatty acids (FA) was analyzed as shown in Table 4.

B. animalis subsp lactis CECT8145 strain can be successfully transformed to overexpress a reporter protein.

Following our procedure, B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 strain was successfully transformed with pAM1-derived plasmids variants expressing mCherry protein under the control of promoters of different strength (pTu, pHUP, pGAPDH and pLacZ). Promoter activity was specifically assessed in CECT8145, DSM 15954 and ATCC SD-6685 strains using mCherry as a reporter protein. Among the promoters tested, pHUP showed the highest efficiency for mCherry overexpression in B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 strain, as determined in the flow cytometry fluorescence analysis (Supplementary Material, Figure S1, S2). Consistent results were obtained in DSM 15954 and ATCC SD-6685 strains, with pHUP again proving to be the most effective. The other promoters tested (pTu, pGAPDH and pLacZ), when transformants were obtained, also drove expression of the fluorescent protein but at low activity levels (data not shown). Fluorescent microscopy analysis also shows consistent expression of mCherry protein under the control of pHUP promoter in all three B. animalis subsp. lactis strains (Fig. 5).

The analysis of plasmid copy number in the mCherry transformants revealed strain-specific differences, with strains DSM 15954 and CECT8145 achieving higher values whereas strain ATCC SD-6685 ranged at half the level, in relative fluorescence activity. However, when standardized, the relative fluorescence per plasmid copy number remained consistent (e.g. at around 1000–1400 arbitray units of fluorescence auf per plasmid copy in flow cytometry), reflecting the same strength of the promoter used in all transformants (Fig. 6). Therefore, the observed differences in fluorescence intensity between strains are attributable to variations in plasmid copy number in each strain.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the genetic modification of the B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT 8145 strain, a reference probiotic and postbiotic ingredient. Rational adjustment of transformation protocols, alongside benchmarking with related strains of the same species, confirms that B. animalis subsp. lactis is generally a recalcitrant, fastidious species to genetic transformation, as previously reported (Hoedt et al., 2021). Despite transformation efficiencies varied between independent experiments, we consistently achieved values in the range of 10² CFU/µg DNA and, to our knowledge, this represents the first successful genetic modification of CECT8145 and ATCC SD-6685 strains. Although these values fall within the lower range compared to other industrially relevant actinobacteria, such as Corynebacterium or Streptomyces, they are sufficient for the reproducible generation of viable transformants using various plasmids, as well as for the successful overexpression of recombinant proteins in B. animalis subsp. lactis.

In addition, our results, in accordance with previous studies, demonstrate that minor modifications to transformation protocols can result in significant changes in transformation efficiency (Li et al., 2024), often differing by several logarithmic orders of magnitude, even when similar protocols and strains are used. Accordingly, different strains will exhibit widely different efficiencies when exposed to identical transformation conditions, highlighting the strong variability across isolates, even among those very close genetically. For example, DSM10140 displayed considerably lower efficiencies under our conditions than those previously reported by Pan and collaborators (2022), indicating that relatively small adjustments in protocol parameters, such as the composition of electrocompetent WB, can drastically affect transformation outcomes in this strain. In contrast, DSM15954 showed transformation efficiencies comparable to those previously reported (Alvarez-Martín et al., 2008), despite differences in electrocompetent WB composition and cell harvesting at higher OD600, suggesting a lower sensitivity to these parameters. CNCM I-2494 also exhibited lower efficiencies than those described in previous studies (M. O′Connell Motherway et al., 2014). These discrepancies likely reflect methodological differences between protocols. Our approach mainly addresses the physical barrier imposed by the bifidobacterial cell envelope through early-log cell harvesting (OD600 of 0.3) and the inclusion of PEG, which may facilitate plDNA uptake. However, previous studies demonstrated that R-M systems significantly limit transformation efficiency in this strain, methylation of plDNA targeting BanLI and BanLII systems increased transformation efficiencies up to 22-fold (M. O′Connell Motherway et al., 2014). These observations further highlight the strong strain dependency of genetic transformation in bifidobacteria and the importance of tailoring transformation strategies to individual strains.

Published genetic engineering studies in the genus Bifidobacterium also underscore the strain-dependent nature of transformation protocols and the particular challenge of this genus. Strategies commonly employed to facilitate transformation include the optimization of electroporation conditions, plasmid screening, and fine-tuning of R-M systems through controlled plasmid methylation (Serafini et al., 2012; Van Sinderen and Ventura, 2021). These approaches, developed through extensive trial-and-error for each specific strain to generate an optimized host, result laborious, time-consuming and are not readily transferable to other strains within the same species, creating a circular challenge for recalcitrant candidates as prior genetic modification is needed. In the case of the CECT8145 strain, modification of transformation plasmids with commercial methyltransferases had been previously evaluated by the authors, without success, together with other singular approaches including multi-walled carbon nanotubes transformation, thermal shock, conjugation and microwave-assisted transformation (Del Río Castillo et al., 2021; Dominguez and O’Sullivan, 2013).

Although B. animalis species displays an exceptionally high level of genomic identity (>99.975%), across different strains (Briczinski et al., 2009), our analysis reveals that, despite the conservation of putative R-M systems, methylome patterns differ between strains. The observed variation in DNA methylation may contribute to strain-specific phenotypic traits and influence transformation efficiency. DNA methylation is known to affect gene expression and could thereby modulate the activity of genes involved in natural competence or R-M systems, which are themselves known to impact transformation efficiency in Bifidobacterium (Liu et al., 2025; Sánchez-Romero and Casadesús, 2020). However, the CECT8145 strain showed a relatively low degree of methylation in its genome. Methylation motifs were detected in the transformable plasmids, and at least one corresponding R-M-associated REase was present in the CECT8145 strain genome. Despite this, transformants were obtained with the adjusted protocol. These facts, taken together with the relative conservation of R-M systems across the strains, suggest that R-M systems may not be the major factor accounting for low transformability in CECT8145.

Another relevant consideration is the origin of the bacterial strain. In this study, we evaluated five B. animalis subsp. lactis strains. Notably, CECT 8145, an isolate from the feces of a breast-fed infant (Martorell et al., 2016), exhibited significant transformation challenges, whereas the remaining strains, originally derived from dairy culture collections, seemed generally more amenable to genetic manipulation. It is thus plausible that commensal and dairy-adapted strains have undergone divergent evolutionary pressures in their respective environments, leading to differences in their susceptibility to genetic transformation.

In this study, we show that control of membrane fluidity is a key factor to enable genetic transformation (Ruan et al., 2015). Indeed, a higher percentage of unsaturated fatty acids was found in cells retrieved at early-log exponential phase, consistent with higher transformation efficiencies found in electrocompetent cells collected in this growth stage. It is known that the degree of FA saturation incorporated into cell membrane lipids is one determinant of its biophysical properties. Saturated FA pack tightly resulting in membranes that are more rigid and less fluid. By contrast, unsaturated FA introduce structural bends, preventing tight packing and increasing membrane fluidity, which can probably facilitate DNA uptake during electroporation (Leekumjorn et al., 2009; Mostofian et al., 2019). Enhancing membrane fluidity by harvesting cells at a physiological state with a favorable fatty acid composition can therefore improve transformation efficiency, as achieved in this work with the B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 strain. While cell density or culture age are common parameters in the optimization of electroporation protocols, fatty acid profiling can serve as a more robust and broadly applicable criterion for selecting the collection point for electrocompetent cells in diverse Bifidobacterium strains, thereby reducing the time and effort required for successful transformation as well as variability. Membrane fluidity can be also modulated through exposure to permeabilizing agents, such as PEG. While PEG is routinely employed for protoplast fusion and genetic transformation in filamentous fungi or yeasts, its use in bacteria electrotransformation remains uncommon (Gietz and Woods, 2001; Lichius et al., 2020). This work provides, to our knowledge, the first evidence of PEG-mediated enhancement of electrotransformation efficiency in bifidobacteria. In contrast with extensive and strain-specific genetic approaches focused on bypassing R-M systems, managing strain physiology through suitable culture conditions has been proven to be sufficient to allow stable transformation.

Given the challenges associated with accessing the cytoplasm of Bifidobacterium, the selection of an appropriate vector is a critical determinant of successful genetic transformation. An optimal vector ensures efficient DNA uptake, stable replication or integration, and effective gene expression, all of which are essential for genetic engineering and functional studies in these bacteria. Our results demonstrate that non-compatible replicons lead to low or unsuccessful transformation efficiencies. Although the number of Bifidobacterium-specific plasmids reported in the literature is steadily increasing, some studies still rely on vectors originally developed for lactic acid bacteria such as strains of the genus Lacticaseibacillus. Despite sharing the same ecological niche, Lactobacilli are phylogenetically distant from Bifidobacterium, and their vectors often prove unsuitable for transformation in this genus.

From a practical standpoint, the primary objective in genetically modifying a given strain is not merely to maximize the number of transformants, but to achieve effective and reproducible expression of a protein. In this context, our promoter and plasmid screening results in the B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT 8145 strain demonstrates that remarkably high expression levels can be achieved. Building on these findings, we identified a promoter capable of driving efficient expression of a fluorescent protein across multiple B. animalis subsp. lactis strains. Analysis of plasmid copy number revealed that differences in fluorescence intensity between strains depends on the plasmid copy number that can stably maintain. Consequently, effective protein expression depends not only on the choice of promoter strength and host strain, but also on the capacity of the strain to sustain a sufficient plasmid copy number (Vilanova et al., 2015). Notably, despite the low transformation efficiency of CECT8145, this strain maintained a high plasmid copy number, resulting in strong fluorescence.

Taken together, the results presented here are expected to facilitate further genetic modification of the B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT 8145 strain and, by extension, other functionally relevant Bifidobacterium strains. A viable strategy for addressing similar challenges in other recalcitrant strains involve balancing efforts between evaluating particular characteristics of the strain, such as membrane fatty acids, methylome, or plasmid copy number, and optimizing transformation protocols based on those results. The availability of broader molecular tools and strategies will enhance our ability to elucidate the mechanisms underlying probiotic functionality—an essential step toward the robust substantiation of health claims, as increasingly required by regulatory agencies (Plaza-Diaz et al., 2019).

Conclusion

We have shown that B. animalis subsp. lactis CECT8145 strain, a probiotic bifidobacteria recalcitrant to genetic transformation, can be modified as a wild-type strain, by addressing membrane fluidity, adjusting culture conditions and using permeability agents, thus avoiding extensive previous genetic adaptation of the host. An optimal selection of vector and promoters later allows to achieve reproducible and strong heterologous protein expression in this fastidious strain. This approach has been extended to other B. animalis subsp. lactis strains, supporting further transformation of other relevant probiotics and commensals to enable future structure-function studies and the elucidation of their mechanisms of action.
