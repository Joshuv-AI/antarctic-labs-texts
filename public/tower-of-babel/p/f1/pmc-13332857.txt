Impact of Donor Age, Sex, and ABO Compatibility on Outcomes After Matched Unrelated Donor Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation for Acute Leukemia: A Retrospective Single‐Center Cohort Study
Authors: Ghazaleh Kheiri, Mahtab Mashayekhi, Sahar Saeidi, Fatemeh Salemi, Mohammadreza Ostadali Dehaghi, Sahar Tavakoli, Saman Ghavamzadeh, Saeed Mohammadi, Mojtaba Azari, Davood Babakhani, Maryam Barkhordar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDonor‐related factors, including donor age, sex, and ABO compatibility, remain clinically relevant considerations in matched unrelated donor hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (MUD‐HSCT). However, their independent contribution to post‐transplant outcomes in contemporary practice is difficult to define, particularly in smaller institutional cohorts. We evaluated the association of selected donor characteristics with transplant outcomes in adult patients with acute leukemia undergoing MUD‐HSCT.MethodsThis retrospective single‐center cohort study included 85 consecutive patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML; n = 45) or acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL; n = 40) who underwent first MUD‐HSCT between 2015 and 2022. Overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival (DFS), and graft‐versus‐host disease‐free/relapse‐free survival (GRFS) were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and univariable Cox proportional‐hazards models. Relapse and non‐relapse mortality (NRM) were assessed using competing‐risk methods. Analyzes were restricted to univariable models because of limited event numbers.ResultsThe median recipient age was 27 years (range, 14–61), and the median donor age was 30 years (range, 14–73). Based on the Disease Risk Index recorded in the institutional database, 54 patients (63.5%) had high‐risk disease and 31 (36.5%) had standard‐risk disease. Donor age ≥ 30 years was not significantly associated with OS, DFS, GRFS, relapse, or NRM in either AML or ALL. ABO compatibility was also not significantly associated with these outcomes. In ALL, female donor sex was associated with inferior GRFS compared with male donor sex (HR, 3.21; 95% CI, 1.44–7.16; p = 0.004), whereas no significant association with OS or DFS was observed. No significant donor sex‐related association was detected in AML.ConclusionIn this single‐center cohort of patients undergoing MUD‐HSCT for acute leukemia, donor age and ABO compatibility were not statistically associated with major transplant outcomes. Female donor sex was associated with inferior GRFS in ALL, but this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited subgroup size, low event numbers, and absence of multivariable adjustment. Larger multicenter studies incorporating disease biology, MRD status, and transplant‐related covariates are needed to validate these observations.Keywords: acute leukemia, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, donor selection, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, unrelated donors

Introduction

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo‐HSCT) remains a potentially curative treatment for numerous malignant hematologic disorders and bone marrow failure syndromes [1, 2]. The optimal donor for HSCT is a human leukocyte antigen (HLA)‐matched sibling donor (MSD), which confers the highest survival rates [3, 4]. However, only approximately 30% of patients have an available matched sibling, making alternative donor sources, such as unrelated donors (URDs) or haploidentical (haplo) donors, essential for the majority of transplant candidates [5].

Traditionally, URD‐HSCT was associated with higher transplant‐related mortality (TRM) and lower disease‐free survival (DFS) [5]. Nonetheless, advances in donor selection and improved HLA typing technologies have significantly narrowed disparities in outcomes gap between URD and MSD transplants. Careful HLA matching at key loci (HLA‐A, ‐B, ‐C, and ‐DRB1), combined with the selection of younger donors, enhances survival in URD‐HSCT [4, 6, 7, 8].

Beyond HLA compatibility, ABO blood group matching critically influences transplant success. Evidence suggests that major ABO mismatches between donor and recipient can elevate the risk of graft failure in URD transplants. Furthermore, as the pool of HLA‐matched URDs expands, prioritizing ABO compatibility, particularly in partially matched scenarios, may further reduce complications and improve outcomes [9].

Despite these advances, optimal donor selection extends beyond HLA matching. Emerging evidence suggests donor age and sex may independently modulate immune reconstitution and graft‐versus‐leukemia effects [6], though their impact remains incompletely characterized in acute leukemia populations. In this study, we sought to evaluate the association between donor age, sex, and ABO compatibility with key transplant outcomes in patients with acute leukemia undergoing matched unrelated donor HSCT (MUD‐HSCT) at our center.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective single‐center cohort study was conducted at Shariati Hospital, Tehran, Iran, a tertiary referral center for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The study evaluated the association between selected donor‐related factors and post‐transplant outcomes in patients with acute leukemia undergoing matched unrelated donor hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (MUD‐HSCT). All consecutive eligible transplants performed during the study period were included to reduce selection bias. The study was reported in accordance with the STROBE recommendations for observational studies.

Participants and Eligibility Criteria

Adult patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) or acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) who underwent first MUD‐HSCT between January 2015 and December 2022 were included. Patients were excluded if they had received a matched sibling donor transplant, haploidentical transplant, second allogeneic transplant, or non‐myeloablative conditioning regimen. The term “adolescent and young adult” was not used in the revised manuscript because the cohort included recipients up to 61 years of age.

Data Collection and Follow‐Up

Clinical, donor, disease, and transplant‐related variables were retrospectively extracted from the institutional HSCT database and electronic medical records. Core exposure variables, including donor age, donor sex, donor–recipient sex pairing, HLA match status, ABO compatibility, remission status, graft cell dose, and Disease Risk Index (DRI), were obtained from institutional records. When follow‐up documentation was incomplete, structured phone contact with patients or family members was used only to clarify objective outcome information, such as survival status or major post‐transplant events. These data were cross‐checked against available institutional records whenever possible. Phone‐derived information was not used to define donor exposures or baseline transplant characteristics.

Disease Risk Index

Disease‐risk stratification was performed based on available cytogenetic and molecular data. In AML, patients were categorized according to ELN 2022 risk groups and subsequently grouped into standard‐risk (favorable/intermediate) and high‐risk (adverse). In ALL, high‐risk disease was defined by the presence of adverse genetic features, including Philadelphia chromosome positivity or other high‐risk cytogenetic abnormalities according to NCCN criteria. A composite disease‐risk variable was constructed combining cytogenetic/molecular risk and disease status at transplant (CR1 vs ≥ CR2).

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Research Center for Oncology, Hematology and Cell Therapy, Tehran University of Medical Sciences (IR. TUMS. HORCSCT. REC.1402.060). Written informed consent for use of clinical data in research was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians according to institutional policy. Patient confidentiality was maintained throughout data collection and analysis.

Transplant Procedure

All patients received myeloablative conditioning according to institutional protocols. Conditioning generally consisted of busulfan combined with cyclophosphamide. Busulfan was administered either orally at 4 mg/kg/day or intravenously at 3.2 mg/kg/day from day −6 to day −3, followed by cyclophosphamide 60 mg/kg/day on days −3 and −2 [10]. Busulfan formulation was determined by availability and clinical considerations. Pharmacokinetic‐guided busulfan dose adjustment was not routinely performed during the study period.

GVHD prophylaxis consisted of cyclosporine A, methotrexate, and rabbit anti‐thymocyte globulin (ATG). Cyclosporine A was started intravenously on day −2 and subsequently transitioned to oral administration when clinically feasible. Rabbit ATG was administered at a total dose of 5 mg/kg over 2 days. Methotrexate was administered in most patients at 10 mg/m2 on day +1 and 6 mg/m2 on days +3, +6, and +11. Post‐transplant cyclophosphamide was not used in this MUD cohort, as this strategy was reserved for haploidentical transplantation at our center.

Patients received institutional antimicrobial prophylaxis, including acyclovir, fluconazole, and trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole. Cytomegalovirus DNA monitoring was performed by quantitative PCR, and pre‐emptive antiviral therapy was initiated according to institutional practice.

Routine pre‐emptive relapse‐prevention therapy was not applied uniformly. Donor lymphocyte infusion or hypomethylating agents were considered selectively in patients with high‐risk clinical features, including declining donor chimerism or evidence of residual or recurrent disease. Because these interventions were individualized, infrequent, and not systematically distributed across donor‐factor subgroups, they were not incorporated into comparative statistical models. Their potential influence on relapse and GRFS is acknowledged as a limitation.

Definitions and Endpoints

The primary outcomes were overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival (DFS), and graft‐versus‐host disease‐free/relapse‐free survival (GRFS). OS was defined as the time from transplantation to death from any cause. DFS was defined as the time from transplantation to relapse or death, whichever occurred first. GRFS was defined as survival without relapse and without clinically significant GVHD events, according to standard composite endpoint principles used in allogeneic transplantation studies. Relapse and non‐relapse mortality (NRM) were analyzed as competing events. NRM was defined as death without prior relapse.

Donor age was analyzed as a binary variable using the cohort median of 30 years as the cut‐off. Donor sex was analyzed as female versus male donor. ABO compatibility was analyzed as matched versus mismatched; the mismatched category included major, minor, and bidirectional ABO incompatibilities. These mismatch categories were pooled because the limited cohort size and event numbers precluded statistically meaningful separate analyzes of ABO mismatch subtypes. Female‐to‐male transplantation was defined as transplantation from a female donor to a male recipient and was compared with all other donor–recipient sex combinations.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are summarized as median and range. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, with denominators reported where appropriate. OS, DFS, and GRFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between‐group differences were assessed using the log‐rank test. Univariable Cox proportional‐hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations between donor‐related factors and OS, DFS, and GRFS.

Relapse and NRM were analyzed using competing‐risk methods. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated with relapse and NRM treated as competing events, and group comparisons were performed using Gray's test. Fine–Gray sub distribution hazard models were used when event numbers were sufficient for model convergence. Variables with zero or near‐zero events in a subgroup were not modeled because estimates would be unstable or non‐estimable.

All analyzes were performed separately in AML and ALL cohorts because of clinically relevant differences in disease biology, relapse kinetics, and transplant outcomes. Analyzes were exploratory and restricted to univariable models because the number of events was insufficient for reliable multivariable adjustment. Important potential confounders, including DRI, remission status, HLA mismatch, graft cell dose, and post‐transplant interventions, could therefore not be simultaneously adjusted for. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyzes were performed using R software, version 4.4.2.

Results

Patient, Disease, Donor, and Transplant Characteristics

Eighty‐five patients with acute leukemia who underwent first MUD‐HSCT were included. Forty‐five patients (52.9%) had AML and 40 (47.1%) had ALL. The median recipient age was 27 years (range, 14–61), and the median donor age was 30 years (range, 14–73). Thirty‐seven recipients (43.5%) were female and 48 (56.5%) were male. Twenty‐eight donors (32.9%) were female and 57 (67.1%) were male. Baseline characteristics, are summarized in Table 1.

Disease Risk Index

DRI was assessed based on available cytogenetic and molecular data and was added to the baseline characterization of the cohort in response to the recognized prognostic relevance of disease biology in allo‐HSCT. In the institutional database, 31 patients (36.5%) were categorized as standard risk and 54 (63.5%) as high risk. Because of limited event numbers and the primary focus on donor‐related factors, DRI was not incorporated into a multivariable model. The inability to adjust donor‐factor associations for DRI is an important limitation and should be considered when interpreting the observed associations, particularly for relapse, DFS, and GRFS.

Association Between Donor Age and Outcomes

Donor age was analyzed using the cohort median of 30 years as the cut‐off. In AML, donor age ≥ 30 years was not significantly associated with OS (HR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.48–2.77; p = 0.76), DFS (HR, 1.06; 95% CI, 0.45–2.50; p = 0.90), or GRFS (HR, 1.23; 95% CI, 0.61–2.49; p = 0.57). In ALL, donor age ≥ 30 years was likewise not associated with OS (HR, 1.05; 95% CI, 0.45–2.45; p = 0.91), DFS (HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.44–2.29; p > 0.99), or GRFS (HR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.49–2.00; p = 0.98) (Tables 2 and 3).

Competing‐risk analysis also did not show a statistically significant association between donor age and relapse or NRM. In AML, donor age ≥ 30 years was associated with an NRM sub‐distribution HR (sHR) of 1.33 (p = 0.59) and relapse sHR of 0.36 (p = 0.21). In ALL, the corresponding estimates were 1.38 for NRM (p = 0.58) and 1.02 for relapse (p = 0.97). These results do not support a detectable association between donor age, dichotomized at 30 years, and major transplant outcomes in this cohort.

Association Between Donor Sex and Outcomes

Donor sex showed disease‐specific findings. In AML, female donor sex was not significantly associated with OS (HR, 1.24; 95% CI, 0.49–3.14; p = 0.65), DFS (HR, 1.17; 95% CI, 0.47–2.93; p = 0.74), or GRFS (HR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.40–1.82; p = 0.69). In ALL, female donor sex was not significantly associated with OS (HR, 1.48; 95% CI, 0.60–3.68; p = 0.40) or DFS (HR, 1.42; 95% CI, 0.57–3.49; p = 0.45). However, female donor sex was associated with inferior GRFS in ALL (HR, 3.21; 95% CI, 1.44–7.16; p = 0.004) (Tables 2 and 3).

In competing‐risk analysis, female donor sex was not significantly associated with relapse or NRM. In ALL, female versus male donor sex showed a sHR of 1.78 for NRM (p = 0.35) and 0.87 for relapse (p = 0.83). In AML, NRM was estimable with a sHR of 2.08 (p = 0.16), whereas relapse regression was not estimable because of absence of relapse events in the female‐donor subgroup.

The GRFS signal in ALL should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and potentially sensitive to small subgroup size and event distribution. Among 11 ALL recipients with female donors, first GRFS‐defining events consisted of GVHD‐related events in 7 patients (grade III–IV acute GVHD, n = 5; chronic GVHD, n = 2), relapse in 2, and death in 2. This suggests that the observed GRFS signal was driven predominantly by GVHD‐related events.

Association Between ABO Compatibility and Outcomes

ABO compatibility was not significantly associated with survival outcomes in either AML or ALL. In AML, ABO compatibility was associated with OS HR of 0.52 (95% CI, 0.17–1.57; p = 0.25), DFS HR of 0.65 (95% CI, 0.24–1.77; p = 0.40), and GRFS HR of 0.73 (95% CI, 0.33–1.62; p = 0.44). In ALL, the corresponding estimates were OS HR of 1.09 (95% CI, 0.44–2.69; p = 0.86), DFS HR of 1.02 (95% CI, 0.42–2.51; p = 0.96), and GRFS HR of 1.05 (95% CI, 0.45–2.24; p = 0.90).

Fine–Gray models did not demonstrate a significant association between ABO compatibility and relapse. The relapse sHR was 1.98 in AML (p = 0.36) and 1.85 in ALL (p = 0.28). NRM modeling by ABO status was not feasible because of insufficient NRM events in some ABO subgroups. Because major, minor, and bidirectional ABO mismatches were pooled for statistical reasons, these results should not be interpreted as excluding subtype‐specific effects of ABO incompatibility.

Female‐to‐Male Donor–Recipient Pairing

Female donor to male recipient transplantation was not significantly associated with OS, DFS, or GRFS in either disease cohort. In ALL, female‐to‐male transplantation was associated with OS HR of 0.44 (95% CI, 0.06–3.26; p = 0.42), DFS HR of 0.41 (95% CI, 0.05–3.03; p = 0.38), and GRFS HR of 1.85 (95% CI, 0.55–6.27; p = 0.32). In AML, the corresponding estimates were OS HR of 1.09 (95% CI, 0.36–3.29; p = 0.88), DFS HR of 1.03 (95% CI, 0.34–3.10; p = 0.96), and GRFS HR of 1.04 (95% CI, 0.44–2.44; p = 0.93). Competing‐risk regression for relapse and NRM in the female‐to‐male subgroup was not performed because of insufficient event numbers.

Discussion

The expanding use of unrelated donors in allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation reflects major advances in donor availability, high‐resolution HLA typing, supportive care, and transplant practice over the past two decades [6, 8, 10, 11, 12]. While HLA‐matched sibling donors remain the preferred donor source when available, several contemporary studies have shown that outcomes after matched unrelated donor transplantation can approach those achieved with matched sibling transplantation in selected settings [4, 5, 6, 10, 11]. Beyond HLA matching, donor selection often incorporates additional donor‐related variables such as age, sex, and ABO compatibility, although the independent contribution of these factors remains incompletely defined in modern MUD‐HSCT. In this study, we evaluated the association of donor age, donor sex, ABO compatibility, and female‐to‐male donor‐recipient pairing with post‐transplant outcomes in adult patients with acute leukemia undergoing MUD‐HSCT.

Importantly, the rationale for the present study differs from that of large registry‐based analyzes. Registry studies from CIBMTR and EBMT have provided the strongest evidence regarding donor selection, but they also include heterogeneous populations with broad variation in conditioning intensity, graft source, GVHD prophylaxis, and supportive‐care practices [4, 6, 7]. In contrast, single‐center analyzes, although smaller, may provide complementary insight from a more homogeneous clinical setting. Our cohort reflects a relatively uniform institutional approach to myeloablative conditioning, ATG‐based GVHD prophylaxis, and post‐transplant follow‐up. Therefore, the present study is not intended to supersede registry evidence, but rather to contextualize donor‐related effects within a contemporary real‐world transplant program.

We did not observe a statistically significant association between donor age ≥ 30 years and OS, DFS, GRFS, relapse, or NRM in either AML or ALL. These findings should be interpreted cautiously. Large registry‐based analyzes have shown that younger unrelated donors are generally associated with superior outcomes, including improved survival and lower non‐relapse mortality, supporting donor age as a clinically relevant donor‐selection variable [4, 6]. Kollman et al. reported that donor characteristics, particularly younger donor age, influence survival after unrelated donor transplantation for hematologic malignancies [6]. Similarly, more recent studies have suggested that younger matched unrelated donors may confer an advantage in selected acute leukemia populations [4]. By contrast, smaller institutional series have not always reproduced this effect consistently [7, 13]. In our cohort, the donor pool was relatively young overall, with a median donor age of 30 years, and donor age was analyzed using the cohort median rather than a higher threshold. Accordingly, our results should be viewed as cohort‐specific observations rather than evidence that donor age is no longer clinically relevant in MUD‐HSCT.

Donor sex appeared more relevant than donor age in this cohort. In ALL, female donor sex was associated with inferior GRFS. In a component‐level review of the ALL female‐donor subgroup, first GRFS‐defining events were more often GVHD‐related than relapse‐related, with grade III–IV acute GVHD in 5 patients and chronic GVHD in 2, compared with relapse in 2 and death in 2. This pattern supports a cautious interpretation of the GRFS finding and suggests that the observed association may reflect post‐transplant morbidity rather than inferior leukemia control per se. No significant donor sex effect was identified in AML. The biological plausibility of donor sex effects in allogeneic transplantation is well recognized, particularly in relation to alloimmune reactivity and sex‐antigen disparity [14, 15, 16, 17]. Prior studies have reported that donor‐recipient sex combinations, especially female donor to male recipient transplantation, may affect GVHD, relapse, and survival outcomes, although the direction and magnitude of these effects have varied across transplant platforms and disease settings [14, 15, 16, 17]. At the same time, the literature is not uniform. Some observational studies have suggested worse GVHD‐related outcomes with female donors, whereas others have found no consistent impact of donor sex after adjustment for other transplant‐related variables [13, 16, 18, 19].

The GRFS finding in ALL therefore warrants cautious interpretation. First, the relevant subgroup was small, and the event number was limited. Second, GRFS is a composite endpoint incorporating relapse, death, and clinically significant GVHD; therefore, a difference in GRFS does not necessarily imply a survival disadvantage or a consistent increase in relapse risk. Third, the present analysis was restricted to univariable models, and important confounders could not be adjusted for. Finally, parity data for female donors were not systematically available in our retrospective dataset, which limits biologically informed interpretation of donor‐sex effects, particularly for GVHD‐related outcomes [2]. For these reasons, the observed association between female donor sex and inferior GRFS in ALL should be regarded as hypothesis‐generating rather than definitive.

We also examined female‐to‐male donor‐recipient pairing and did not identify a statistically significant association with OS, DFS, or GRFS in either AML or ALL. This result is not entirely discordant with the published literature, which has been mixed in this area [7, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Some studies have linked female‐to‐male transplantation to increased GVHD, higher NRM, or inferior survival, whereas others have not demonstrated a consistent adverse effect [14, 15, 17, 19]. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in donor type, conditioning strategy, graft source, GVHD prophylaxis, patient population, and availability of parity data. In our study, the lack of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of absence of effect; rather, it indicates that such an association was not demonstrable in this cohort.

ABO mismatch was not significantly associated with OS, DFS, GRFS, relapse, or NRM in this study. ABO incompatibility remains biologically relevant in allogeneic transplantation and may affect hemolysis, erythroid recovery, transfusion burden, and graft failure, particularly in the setting of major mismatch [9, 20]. Earlier studies have reported adverse effects of ABO incompatibility in unrelated donor transplantation, including higher rates of graft failure and transplant‐related complications [9, 20]. However, the impact of ABO mismatch on survival endpoints has been inconsistent across studies, and in modern practice this effect may be mitigated by improvements in transfusion support, graft handling, and overall supportive care [7, 9, 20]. In the present analysis, ABO mismatch was examined as a pooled category including major, minor, and bidirectional mismatches. This approach was driven by limited sample size and low subgroup event counts, which precluded statistically reliable subtype‐specific analyzes. Accordingly, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence that all ABO mismatch subtypes are clinically equivalent, but rather that no statistically demonstrable association with the evaluated endpoints was identified in this cohort.

A major concern in interpreting donor‐related associations after allo‐HSCT is the potential confounding effect of disease biology. In response to this issue, Disease Risk Index (DRI) was incorporated into the revised baseline characterization of the cohort. In our dataset, 54 of 85 patients (63.5%) were classified as high risk and 31 (36.5%) as standard risk at transplantation. This is clinically relevant because disease risk strongly influences relapse, DFS, and survival after transplantation. However, despite inclusion of DRI in the descriptive baseline table, the limited number of events did not permit reliable multivariable modeling. Therefore, donor‐factor associations could not be adjusted for DRI, remission status, HLA mismatch, graft cell dose, or other transplant‐related covariates. This limitation is central to the interpretation of the present findings.

Several additional limitations should also be acknowledged. The study is retrospective and single‐center, with a modest sample size. Multivariable adjustment was not feasible because of limited event numbers, and this restricts causal inference. Measurable residual disease data were not consistently available and could not be incorporated, despite their established prognostic importance in both AML and ALL. Busulfan was administered in oral or intravenous formulations according to institutional practice, but pharmacokinetic‐guided dose adjustment was not routinely performed, introducing potential heterogeneity in conditioning exposure. In addition, individualized post‐transplant interventions such as donor lymphocyte infusion or hypomethylating agents were used selectively in a minority of higher‐risk patients and may have influenced relapse‐related outcomes and GRFS. These factors should be taken into account when interpreting the present results.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant real‐world data from a consecutive MUD‐HSCT cohort managed within a relatively consistent transplant framework. Our findings suggest that, within this institutional setting, donor age and ABO compatibility were not measurably associated with major post‐transplant outcomes, whereas female donor sex was associated with inferior GRFS in ALL. However, these observations should be considered cohort‐specific and exploratory, rather than definitive. In particular, the absence of statistically significant associations for donor age and ABO compatibility should not be interpreted as evidence that these factors are unimportant in donor selection, especially in light of larger registry‐based studies supporting their relevance [4, 6, 9].

In conclusion, in this single‐center cohort of adult patients with acute leukemia undergoing MUD‐HSCT, donor age, ABO compatibility, and female‐to‐male donor‐recipient pairing were not statistically associated with OS, DFS, GRFS, relapse, or NRM. Female donor sex was associated with inferior GRFS in ALL, but this finding should be interpreted with caution given the limited subgroup size, low event numbers, lack of multivariable adjustment, and absence of donor parity and MRD data. Larger multicenter studies with adequate adjustment for disease risk, transplant‐related covariates, and post‐transplant interventions are needed to better define the clinical significance of non‐HLA donor factors in contemporary unrelated donor transplantation [4, 6, 16].
