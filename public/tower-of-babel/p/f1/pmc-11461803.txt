Tunable optical and photovoltaic performance in PTB7-based colored semi-transparent organic solar cells integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-photonic crystals via advanced light management
Authors: Erman Cokduygulular, Caglar Cetinkaya, Serkan Emik, Baris Kinaci
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study explores the design, fabrication, and characterization of PTB7-based colored semi-transparent organic solar cells (ST-OSCs) with integrated MgF2/WO3 one-dimensional photonic crystals (1D-PCs). Integrating 1D-PCs enhanced light management by creating tunable photonic band gaps, leading to extraordinary color change and improved photon harvesting. For 1DPC5 − 425 the Average Visible Transmittance (AVT) values were 33.3%, and CIE x, y was 0.43, 0.52, for 1DPC3 − 650 AVT were 25.09% and CIE x, y was 0.23, 0.27, respectively. Thus, extraordinary color changes from blue to yellow could be achieved while keeping transparency. Thereupon, photovoltaic performance showed notable improvements, with Jsc increasing from 7.98 mA/cm2 to 9.95 mA/cm2 and 10.45 mA/cm2 for 1DPC5 − 425 and 1DPC3 − 650, respectively. By 1D-PC integration, power conversion efficiency (PCE) reached from 3.93 to 4.90% and 5.08% for 1DPC5 − 425 and 1DPC3 − 650, respectively. The Color Rendering Index (CRI) indicated successful color tuning and acceptable rendering, with CRI of 52 and 87. The study demonstrates that 1D-PC integration significantly enhances ST-OSCs’ optical and photovoltaic properties, paving the way for advanced energy-harvesting applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75119-4.

Introduction

Polymer-based semi-transparent organic solar cells (ST-OSCs) represent a significant innovation in photovoltaic technology. These cells leverage the unique properties of polymers to enhance flexibility, transparency, and efficiency in solar energy conversion. Polymer material-based OSC are easily fabricated, flexible, and can be applied semi-transparently, offering numerous advantages. Compared to inorganic photovoltaics, organic photovoltaic devices can be designed as ST-OSCs due to their unique advantages, including adjustable energy levels, low cost, tunable vibrant colors, visual comfort, lightness, transparency, and mechanical flexibility1–3. The use of ST-OSCs enables the design of energy-producing window and curtain applications for homes and vehicles, as well as greenhouses that can generate energy while transmitting the desired wavelengths of sunlight in agriculture. Semi-transparent solar cells reduce energy consumption through their transmittance and energy production3,4. At the same time, energy-generating glass applications can be developed for architectural applications by making color modifications. Furthermore, integrating ST-OSCs into technology aims to provide energy production and sustainability without compromising usability, thanks to their aesthetic integration into flexible electronic devices and wearable technologies3.

One of the advantages of ST-OSCs is their ability to selectively utilize the solar spectrum, achieving both high transparency and power conversion efficiency (PCE) through their photovoltaic and optical properties5. Recent years have seen significant breakthroughs in the PCE of opaque organic photovoltaic materials, with state-of-the-art organic photovoltaics based on non-fullerene systems achieving PCE values exceeding 19% 3,4,6. The PCE of opaque OSCs can often serve as a precursor for ST-OSCs, indicating the substantial development potential of ST-OSCs3. PCE and average visible transmittance (AVT) are considered the most crucial parameters for evaluating the performance of ST-OSCs; however, a high AVT inevitably leads to a reduction in PCE, and balancing optoelectronic performance with transparency functionality remains a significant challenge in the development of ST-OSCs7. Ideal ST-OSCs should absorb as many photons as possible outside the visible region to achieve photon-to-electron conversion while transmitting as many photons as possible in the visible region. To address this issue, researchers have made significant efforts to synthesize new materials with strong absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) and weak absorption in visible light8–10. Researchers have also focused on incorporating optical microcavities or dielectric/metal/dielectric (D/M/D) structures to enhance reflection or transmission in specific wavelength regions11–14.

Innovative designs, materials, and manufacturing techniques are continually being explored to address these limitations and make solar cells more efficient and cost-effective. Minimizing these constraints and improving the performance characteristics of solar cells can be achieved by applying various photonic structures such as plasmonic nanowires, triangular and pyramid gratings, nanostructures, and photonic crystals (PCs)15. PCs are among the best candidates for this objective. They are able to selectively manipulate the reflection and transmission spectra and improve the light interaction with the material. Related to their role, PCs can be integrated into the structure of solar cells as an add-on layer, or an active SC layer can be designed as a PC structure16.

Photonic crystals are artificial structures with spatial periodicity of dielectric permittivity. However, this facilitates both destructive and constructive interference effects for certain light wavelengths, leading to Bragg diffraction and forming a photonic band gap (PBG). In this spectral region, light cannot propagate through the material. The presence of a PBG profoundly affects light-matter interactions to enable phenomena such as trapping, waveguiding, light confinement, increased light emission, and slow photon effects17. The periodicity can vary from one to three dimensions with a period on the order of the light wavelength, categorizing photonic crystals into one-dimensional, two-dimensional, and three-dimensional photonic crystals16.

Integrating one-dimensional photonic crystals (1D-PCs) into organic solar cells can significantly enhance cell performance. 1D-PCs regulate optical properties by effectively reflecting or directing light at specific wavelengths. These structures improve light trapping within organic solar cells, leading to higher energy conversion efficiency. 1D-PCs increase absorption by allowing light to remain inside the cell longer. This improves efficiency, especially in low-light conditions. Furthermore, 1D-PCs can selectively direct light at specific wavelengths, allowing the cells to operate more efficiently across a broad spectrum. Using 1D-PCs in thin-film organic solar cells (OSCs) reduces material usage while enhancing performance, thus supporting cost-effectiveness and environmental sustainability. Additionally, 1D-PCs prevent overheating, ensuring the cells’ longevity. Heat management is especially critical for systems operating at high temperatures17,18.

It is crucial to prevent light from escaping through the active layer without being absorbed. This necessitates a comparatively thick active layer in the more common silicon solar cells19. Reflectors can reduce this thickness by reflecting light that passes through the active layer back into the active region, thereby increasing the path length for light absorption. The most basic back reflectors are metal layers, such as Al or Ag20–22. However, these metal layers have disadvantages, including loss of plasmonic resonance at the interface between the metal and absorption layers and performance degradation due to metal ion diffusion19. PCs provide controllable and improved reflections from material to encourage increased absorption, enabling different applications in thin-film solar cells. The wavelength range and intensity of the reflection depend on the PBG, which can be easily tuned through the geometrical dimensions of the PC structure. This tunability allows PCs to optimize light trapping and absorption in the active layer, contributing to more efficient and thinner solar cells16.

OSCs hold a significant place in renewable energy technologies, and integrating semi-transparent structures with one-dimensional photonic crystals (1D-PCs) expands their performance and application areas. In the future, these innovative approaches will further enhance the efficiency and durability of OSCs, providing more sustainable and economical solutions for energy production. Research and developments in this context will contribute to the more widespread use of organic solar cells in the energy sector. Studies in the literature show that 1D-PCs have been added to PTB7-based OSC structures. In a study by Betancur et al., a structure with 28% luminosity achieved a PCE of 5.6%23. In another study by Xu et al., a four-period structure with 22% AVT achieved a PCE of 5.9%. These findings demonstrate the potential of 1D-PCs to significantly enhance the performance of OSCs24. In the studies carried out to determine the theoretical photovoltaic performance parameters, PTB7-based structure using SCAPS-1D was investigated by Sharma et al. Although the hole transport layer (HTL) and electron transport layer (ETL) are different in this study and the thickness of PTB7 is high (200 nm), the theoretically obtained PCE value of 13.21% is important in terms of showing the potential of PTB7-based structures25.

Result and discussion

Adding 1D-PC to ST-OSCs enables different designs in terms of optical properties in the structure design and provides effective results in light management engineering. By periodically adding layers with different refractive indices, it is possible to alter the optical properties of the entire structure and thus design organic solar cell structures in different colors. Within the scope of this study, preliminary work aimed at designing colored organic solar cells and producing PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs with optimal properties has identified the most suitable material pair. Detailed calculations have been conducted for designed PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs.

In this study, ST-OSC structures containing 1D-PC at optimal values have been designed by modifying the optical properties of the structure with the integration of 1D-PC into the structures where maximum AVT was obtained in PTB7-based ST-OSCs in our previous studies. By developing photonic crystals with different PBG properties in the designed structures, the achievable color limits for PTB7-based ST-OSC were determined, and the exceptional optical properties were evaluated. The AVTmax obtained the maximum AVT from ST-OSC structures and was modified with PBG formed with MgF2/WO3 1D-PC system. The MgF2/WO3 structure with N periods is denoted as (MgF2/WO3)N to indicate the number of periods.

In the (MgF2/WO3)N structure, the thickness of the first layer MgF2 is d1, and the thickness of the second layer MoO3 is d2. d1 and d2 are adjusted according to the characteristics of the PBG to be designed. The dielectric constant, and thus the refractive index of the (MgF2 /WO3)N 1D-PC, changes periodically in only one dimension. The width and reflection intensity of the PBG formed by (MgF2 /WO3)N 1D-PC are related to the refractive index of each layer. Furthermore, it is desirable that the absorption of MgF2 and MoO3 is deficient and, if possible, non-existent in the wavelength range in which the PBG is desired to be formed. The difference between the refractive indices of MgF2 and WO3 provides a periodic refractive index distribution in the (MgF2/WO3)N 1D-PC structure. A higher difference allows for higher reflection in the PBG with fewer periods, thus achieving the desired optical properties with less material. Design of PTB7-based ST-OSCs integrated 1D-PC

The calculations for the SC architecture designed by integrating MgF2/WO3 1D-PC into the PTB7-based ST-OSC with maximum AVT (AVTmax) were performed using the Transfer Matrix Method (TMM). The physical principle of the calculations and the derived equations are given in the Supplementary Information. When examining the transmittance spectra of the ST-OSC AVTmax, it exhibits an almost flat spectrum in the visible region13. Therefore, the PTB7-based organic solar cell has a color very close to the D65 or AM1.5G color coordinates, i.e., it is “colorless”. Making the structure colored is possible by integrating 1D-PC systems that prohibit the transition of photons in a specific wavelength range in the semi-transparent cell structure that allows photon transition in the visible region. Therefore, 1D-PC designs were made for values above 25% AVT, considered the limit for window applications, repeating with different periods at many different Bragg wavelengths (lB). The objective was to change the colors of the structure by determining the PBG at different center wavelengths. Calculations were conducted to achieve PBG for the selected wavelengths, and thus, the thicknesses of the two dielectric materials to be used for the 1D-PC were determined. The significant difference in refractive indices between the two dielectric materials, MgF2 and WO3, that constitute the 1D-PC allows for achieving high reflection intensity even with a small number of periods. Achieving near 100% reflection intensity with fewer periods not only reduces material consumption but also lowers the error margin in production due to the reduced number of repetitions. Additionally, producing thinner and lighter ST-OSCs is feasible, and if flexible substrates are chosen, the flexibility of the cells can be further enhanced26.

The widths of the PBGs formed with the (MgF2/WO3)N 1D-PC can reach up to 200 nm, covering a wide region at the periods where reflection intensity is maximal. This consideration is crucial when selecting lB. Otherwise, a significant reduction in the transmittance of ST-OSCs would be inevitable. If lB is chosen in the middle of the visible region, the visible transmittance of the structure may decrease, rendering it opaque. Therefore, the aim is to select lB wavelengths that do not significantly disrupt transmittance, preferably near the visible region boundaries, to maintain the AVT above 25%. The reflection spectrum for different N values, i.e., different periods, of the (MgF2/WO3)N 1D-PC system when lB is selected as 425 nm and 650 nm is shown in Fig. 2. The width of the PBG for lB = 425 nm is less than the width of the PBG for lB = 650 nm. Since PBG increases linearly with lB14,27.

Optical properties of PTB7-based ST-OSCs integrated 1D-PC

Integrating (MgF2/WO3)N 1D-PC into the PTB7-based AVTmax ST-OSC structure, the electromagnetic wave is not allowed to pass through the PBG. In the inverted solar cell, electromagnetic waves entering from the bottom of the solar cell will reach the PBG without being absorbed from the active region and the wavelength part corresponding to the reflection spectrum of the PBG will be reflected to the active region. Thus, an increase in photon harvesting in the active region will occur, and the absorption properties of the structure will be improved. The effect of the increase in the absorption spectrum will increase the Jph by increasing the photon harvesting in ST-OSC. Therefore, with the increase in Jph, solar cell performance and efficiency will be improved.

The widths of PBGs formed with (MgF2/WO3)N 1D-PC are crucial in altering the optical properties and color of ST-OSC. When lB is selected in the middle of the visible region, it will disrupt the optical transmittance of the structure and cause it to approach opaque properties. Therefore, for the color change of ST-OSC, the part of the visible region whose transmittance will be obstructed should be well selected, considering the dominance of the color-matching functions. The comparison of (MgF2/WO3)N 1D-PC integrated into the AVTmax with the PBG characteristic determined by detailed calculations with the (CRIext)max and Extreme Color examined in our previous studies is given in Fig. 5a13. The (CRIext)max is the structure with the highest CRIext investigated in our previous studies13. The extreme color structure is the architecture we investigated in our previous study13, which does not contain any photonic crystal and offers the most extreme blue color achievable in PTB7-based OSC with only DMD modification. While the 1D-PC integrated structures were designed on AVTmax, different DMD thicknesses were used in (CRIext)max and Extreme Color structures. While MoO3/Ag/WO3 (10/21/55 nm) DMD structure was used for (CRIext)max, MoO3/Ag/WO3 (10/20/19 nm) DMD structure was used in the extreme color structure13.

The absorption increases with the 1DPC5 − 425 and 1DPC3 − 650 structures formed by integrating 1D-PC into the AVTmax, and it is seen that this will have a positive effect on the solar cell performance. To achieve the color change objective, the transmittance is planned and expected to decrease in the wavelength range. The transmittance spectrum given in Fig. 5b shows that the transmittance decreases for 1DPC5 − 425 and 1DPC3 − 650 structures compared to the AVTmax.

When examining the CRIext results, it is observed that the CRIext decreases with the integration of the MgF2/WO3 1D-PC into the AVTmax. Despite the periodic and compatible nature of the newly added layers to the AVTmax, a reduction in the CRIext is inevitable. As the period of the integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC increases, the number of layers increases, resulting in a decrease in the CRIext. Therefore, the obtained CRIext of 53 for the 1DPC5-425 and 84 for the 1DPC3-650 are the expected outcomes of this effect.

When the CCT results are considered, conclusions can be drawn regarding the achievable extremes of low and high CCT values. The 3632 K obtained for the 1DPC5-425 provided a lower CCT than others. 1DPC3-650 demonstrates that a high value can be reached with a CCT result of 20,697 K. Electrical properties of PTB7-based ST-OSCs integrated 1DPC

Modifying the optical properties by integrating MgF2/WO3 1D-PC into the PTB7-based AVTmax will cause a change in the electric field intensity distribution within the solar cell. These changes will affect the photon harvesting processes of the structures in which the designed 1D-PC is integrated, and the photovoltaic performance characteristics will change. The electric field intensity distribution (|E(z, λ)|²) calculated with TMM for the 1DPC5 − 425 and 1DPC3 − 650 structures obtained by integrating MgF2/WO3 1D-PC into the AVTmax investigated with light management engineering approaches according to position and wavelength is given in Fig. 9a and b.

Fig. 9Electric field density distributions of MgF2/WO3 1D-PC integrated structures (a) 1DPC5-425, (b) 1DPC3-650.

Electric field density distributions of MgF2/WO3 1D-PC integrated structures (a) 1DPC5-425, (b) 1DPC3-650.

Considering that 1DPC5-425 and 1DPC3-650 structures are 1D-PC structures integrated into the AVTmax, comparing them based on the AVTmax is appropriate. Upon examining the 1DPC5-425, as expected, the PBG centered at 425 nm is formed, and the electric field intensity increases in the low wavelength part of the visible region where the reflection is intense. Similarly, for the 1DPC3-650, the PBG centered at 650 nm causes more intense reflection in the higher wavelength part of the visible region. This is because, in this region, the electric field intensity is heightened due to the photons being reflected back into the structure. Additionally, the distribution within the solar cell is higher in the NIR region, similar to the AVTmax, in the PTB7:PC71BM layer, which is the active region.

When examining the 1DPC5-425 and 1DPC3-650 structures separately, it is observed that the electric field intensity distribution decreases as it moves toward the top layers of the periodic 1D-PC structures. This decrease is due to the gradual reduction in the number of reflected photons. Comparing the absorption spectrum changes for both structures with the AVTmax, it is evident that the electric field intensity distribution results are consistent with the absorption data. The increase in photon harvesting in the visible region has led to higher absorption, achieving the desired color change and enhancing photovoltaic performance. The alignment of electric field intensity distribution with the absorption spectrum changes supports the conclusion that integrating 1D-PC structures into the AVTmax effectively boosts optical and photovoltaic characteristics.

To quantitatively evaluate the impact of semi-transmittance properties on the photovoltaic performance in PTB7-based OSCs, the examination of EQE (External Quantum Efficiency) and QUE (Quantum Utilization Efficiency) characteristics has been applied to the structures integrated with 1D-PC. The EQE and QUE characteristics for the 1DPC5-425 and 1DPC3-650 structures, compared with other structures examined in the scope of the study, are presented in Fig. 10a and b, respectively. These figures illustrate how the integration of 1D-PC structures affects the EQE and QUE of the OSCs, providing a detailed comparison of the photovoltaic performance across different designs. This approach helps in understanding the enhancements achieved through the integration of 1D-PCs and their influence on the efficiency metrics of the solar cells.

The EQE distributions for the 1DPC5-425 and 1DPC3-650 structures exhibit a distribution similar to the previously examined structures, ranging from 300 to 800 nm. This distribution changes particularly in the short wavelength regime in relation to the AM1.5G distribution and in the long wavelength regime concerning the absorption characteristics of the PTB7:PC71BM mixture. While the AVTmax offers optical advantages, it waves photovoltaic performance compared to other structures, as previously discussed. However, integrating MgF2/WO3 1D-PC into the AVTmax achieves color change and enhances photon harvesting. This enhancement is evident from the electric field intensity distribution and absorption spectrum changes, resulting in improved EQE throughout all wavelengths. Therefore, increased photon harvesting for the 1DPC5-425 and 1DPC3-650 structures leads to improved photovoltaic performance. The distribution of this increase in the visible region is related to the wavelength range selected for the PBG. For the 1DPC5-425, with a PBG centered at 425 nm, EQE increases in the lower wavelength part of the visible region. Similarly, for the 1DPC3-650, with a PBG centered at 650 nm, the effect on EQE is more evident in the higher wavelength part of the visible region.

The improvement in EQE quantitatively shows its effect on the Jsc values calculated from the EQE distribution and, thus, on the photovoltaic performance of the devices. The Jsc of 9.88 mA/cm2 for AVTmax was 11.91 mA/cm2 and 12.07 mA/cm2 for 1DPC5-425 and 1DPC3-650 structures, respectively. The increased reflection due to the integrated 1D-PC enhances photon harvesting, which improves the EQE and subsequently leads to a rise in Jsc.

In the analysis of ST-OSCs, it is crucial to consider the photons that pass through the device without being absorbed or reflected, as the QUE parameter plays a significant role in assessing light energy utilization13. QUE not only evaluates the photon’s harvesting and transmittance properties of the device but also indirectly reflects the device’s optical usage and loss comparison. Given this context, it is essential to compare the QUE values of the structures with integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC against the AVTmax, considering their colored and semi-transparent characteristics. Examining the QUE parameter reveals that, in addition to the impact of EQE, transmittance also significantly influences the results. When comparing the 1DPC5-425 to the AVTmax, it is observed that the QUE is lower in the short-wavelength region of the visible spectrum where transmittance is low. However, beyond the 500 nm wavelength, where the impact of the 1D-PC’s PBG diminishes, the QUE increases due to the rising EQE. For the 1DPC3-650, a similar situation can be observed. The QUE is lower in the longer wavelength region of the visible spectrum, reflecting the reduced transmittance in this region. However, as the wavelength moves away from the PBG center, the QUE increases in response to the higher EQE. Overall, the regional variations in QUE values for the 1D-PC integrated structures compared to the AVTmax highlight the balance between photon harvesting and transmittance, illustrating the effectiveness of 1D-PC integration in enhancing photovoltaic performance while maintaining the semi-transparent and colored properties of the devices.

For the 1DPC3-650, a comparison with the AVTmax similarly demonstrates that changes in transmittance due to the PBG’s effect significantly influence the QUE. In the 1DPC3-650, for wavelengths greater than 600 nm, the reduced transmittance leads to a decrease in QUE compared to the AVTmax. However, in the lower wavelength region of the visible spectrum, the increase in EQE results in a rise in QUE. This clearly shows how the integration of MgF2/WO3 1D-PC impacts the optical properties and, consequently, the photovoltaic performance of the device. The strategic placement of the PBG within the visible spectrum allows for optimized photon harvesting and color changes while maintaining a balance between transmittance and efficiency.

When examining the QUE distributions for both 1D-PC integrated structures, it is evident that the QUE values are generally lower across almost the entire visible region than other structures. The impact of changes in EQE is similarly reflected in the QUE changes. The QUE values are comparable to those of other structures for the 1DPC5-425, especially in the 550–750 nm range. This indicates sufficient photon harvesting and efficient energy utilization facilitated by transmittance and reflection within this wavelength range. In the case of the 1DPC3-650, a decrease in QUE is observed in the 550–750 nm range; this is balanced by an increase in the 450–550 nm range. This balance suggests that the structure still provides efficient energy utilization, albeit with variations in performance across different parts of the visible spectrum. In summary, both 1D-PC integrated structures optimize energy usage and photon harvesting, albeit with varying degrees of efficiency across different wavelengths. This demonstrates the intricate balance between achieving desired optical properties and maintaining high photovoltaic performance.

Conclusion

This study presents the design, fabrication, and characterization of PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs with integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC. The research focused on enhancing the optical and photovoltaic performance of the ST-OSCs by incorporating 1D-PC structures, which offer tunable optical properties and improved light management. Integrating MgF2/WO3 1D-PC into the PTB7-based ST-OSCs resulted in significant improvements in light management. The designed structures exhibited tunable photonic band gaps, enhancing photon harvesting and minimizing losses. The AVTs for the 1DPC5 − 425 and 1DPC3 − 650 structures were 33.3% and 25.09%, respectively, demonstrating the ability to maintain transparency while achieving desired color changes. Integrating 1D-PCs led to improved EQE across the visible spectrum. The Jsc values increased from 7.98 mA/cm² for the AVTmax to 9.95 mA/cm² for 1DPC5 − 425 and 10.45 mA/cm² for 1DPC3 − 650, reflecting enhanced photon harvesting and charge carrier generation. PCEs of 4.9% for 1DPC5 − 425 and 5.08% for 1DPC3 − 650 were achieved, indicating a substantial improvement over the baseline structure. The study demonstrates that integrating MgF2/WO3 1D-PCs into PTB7-based ST-OSCs effectively enhances both optical and photovoltaic performance. The optimized structures achieved higher EQE, Jsc, and PCE values while maintaining desirable AVT and color properties.

The study resulted in a series of conclusions that not only can modify many optical properties of a solar cell offering a very high AVT, especially its color, but also produce a significant improvement in photovoltaic performance by integrating a functionally designed 1D-PC. It was determined that photonic crystals, especially finely tuned with light management engineering, can collectively modify all properties of the ST-OSC device without focusing on only optical or photovoltaic parameters. In this respect, the study is quite instructive, especially for the functional integration of photonic crystals into ST optoelectronic devices. These findings underscore the potential of 1D-PCs in advancing the design of efficient, colored ST-OSCs for applications in energy-harvesting windows and other optoelectronic devices. The promising results from this study pave the way for developing high-performance, aesthetically versatile solar cells that integrate seamlessly into the structure.

Material and method

Materials constituting the building architecture

In the investigated OSCs, fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO), known for its high optical transmittance and conductivity and its low work function (4.4 eV), was used as the transparent bottom contact system and window layer to prevent the reflectance28,29. For the hole-blocking layer in the OSCs, zinc oxide (ZnO), an n-type metal oxide with high carrier mobility, was employed. ZnO is widely preferred in inverted OSCs due to its excellent properties. ZnO has a conduction band energy of approximately − 4.4 eV and a valence band energy of -7.4 eV. This band structure allows electrons generated in the active region to be transported to the ZnO layer while holes are blocked28. Due to this function, ZnO is also referred to as the electron transport layer (ETL) in OSCs. Additionally, its wide bandgap offers high optical transmittance in the visible (VR) and near-infrared (NIR) regions, ensuring efficient photon delivery to the active region30–32.

In the bulk-heterojunction (BHJ) active region, the donor polymer used is poly({4,8-bis[(2-ethylhexyl)oxy] benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene-2,6-diyl}{3-fluoro-2-[(2-ethylhexyl)carbonyl]thieno[3,4-b]thiophenediyl}) (PTB7) and the acceptor polymer is a fullerene derivative with high electronegativity and superior charge transport properties, (6,6)-phenyl C71 butyric acid methyl ester (PC71BM). The BHJ PTB7:PC71BM blend is an innovative absorber system with a bandgap of 0.9 eV, an electron affinity of 3.7 eV, and high carrier mobility values33. These properties make it a frequently used material in ST-OSC structures that exhibit superior optical and photovoltaic performance34–37. In ST-OSCs’ design, the thickness optimization of the BHJ PTB7:PC71BM blend that forms the active region is crucial. This optimization is based on parameters such as good absorption characteristics, efficient exciton generation and dissociation, mechanical and environmental stability, and, most importantly, good transmittance in the visible region (VR). Almost all these parameters depend on thickness and are interdependent38. Therefore, numerous studies are in the literature on optimizing the thickness of the BHJ PTB7:PC71BM blend. Considering photovoltaic performance parameters such as open-circuit voltage (Voc), fill factor (FF), PCE, short-circuit current density (Jsc), and an important optical value like AVT, a 100 nm thick BHJ PTB7:PC71BM blend has been found to provide optimal performance for ST-OSCs34,38. Thus, the absorber layers of the ST-OSCs with the BHJ PTB7:PC71BM blend examined in this study were designed to be 100 nm thick.

Molybdenum trioxide (MoO3) was used as the hole transport layer (HTL) and inner dielectric layer. In opaque OSCs with a thick metal layer, the MoO3 layer serves solely as an HTL, while in ST-OSCs, it functions as both an HTL and the inner dielectric part of the transparent top contact in the DMD structure. The MoO3 HTL layer also prevents metal penetration into the active region in all OSCs. MoO3 possesses high hole mobility and high transparency in the visible region30,31. MoO3 has a conduction band energy of approximately − 2.3 eV and a valence band energy of -5.3 eV. These band and conductivity properties allow holes generated and subsequently separated in the active region to be efficiently transported to MoO3 while electrons are blocked28. Therefore, many studies have used MoO3 as an HTL among high-work function TCOs in the design of high-performance optoelectronic devices39,40.

A TMO-based transparent top contact structure was designed for ST-OSCs in an asymmetric MoO3/Ag/WO3 DMD configuration. In ST-OSC structures, silver (Ag) was used as the top electrode due to its relatively low absorption coefficient and high electrical conductivity compared to other commonly used metals in DMD systems and OSCs. WO3 prevents the oxidation of Ag in the asymmetric MoO3/Ag/WO3 transparent contact system. Additionally, the high refractive index of WO3 makes it an effective cap layer, providing waveguided effects for tunable optical properties. These materials and configurations were chosen to optimize the performance of ST-OSCs by balancing transparency and conductivity while protecting the active layers and ensuring efficient charge transport.

Calculation of optical spectra

The absorption, transmittance, and reflectance spectra of the designed PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs with integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC were calculated using the Transfer Matrix Method (TMM). TMM is an effective method commonly used to simulate optoelectronic devices to analyze how electromagnetic waves propagate through structures of multiple dielectric layers, such as DMDs and photonic crystals. It is a powerful technique for theoretically determining the optical characteristics of such structures. In the calculations performed using TMM, the methods and equations followed are detailed in our previous work. TMM allows for the precise analysis of the optical behavior of the ST-OSC structures, providing insights into their absorption, transmittance, and reflectance properties. Using TMM, the designed ST-OSCs’ optical performance was evaluated, ensuring that the structures exhibit the desired optical properties for efficient light harvesting and color tuning. Detailed methods and equations used in TMM calculations are provided in our previous study13. Further information on the Transfer Matrix Method and its applications in optoelectronic simulations can be found in the relevant literature39. The physical principle of the calculations and the derived equations are given in the Supplementary Information.

Average visible transmittance

The transparency properties of semi-transparent and colored optoelectronic systems are evaluated using the AVTs. These values consider the photonic response of the human eye and are determined by the transmittance characteristics in the visible light wavelength range (370 –740 nm). For the calculation of AVT, both the transmittance spectra obtained from the TMM simulations and the experimental measurements of the designed MgF2/WO3 1D-PC integrated PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs were used. The calculations and equations employed for determining AVT are detailed in our previous study13. The AVT calculation is critical in ensuring that the designed ST-OSCs not only meet the required transparency thresholds but also exhibit the desired optical performance for practical applications in semi-transparent and colored optoelectronic devices. Detailed methods and equations used in AVT calculations are provided in our previous study13.

Calculation of CIE 1931 color coordinates

In addition to AVT, another crucial characteristic of ST-OSCs is the color coordinates (x, y) on the CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram, which represent the perceived color of the device. The CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram is designed based on the photonic response of the human eye and is commonly used to determine the color properties of illuminants. The method and equations used to calculate the CIE 1931 color coordinates for the PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs with integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC are detailed in our previous study13.

The color rendering index

CRI measures how accurately a light source reveals the colors of objects in comparison to an ideal or natural light source41. CRI values range from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating better color rendering. A CRI of 100 represents the color rendering performance of standardized daylight or incandescent light sources. CRI values of 90 and above are considered excellent, while below 80 are generally deemed poor13. The methodology and equations used to calculate the CRI for the PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs with integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC are detailed in our previous study13. The CRI values for the MgF2/WO3 1D-PC integrated PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs can be accurately determined, ensuring that the color rendering properties of the devices meet the desired standards.

External quantum efficiency and quantum utilization efficiency

External Quantum Efficiency (EQE) in solar cells (SC) is related to the number of electrons and holes provided to the external circuit for each absorbed photon. It is determined by the ratio of the flow of electrons obtained from the SC to the flow of incoming photons. EQE can be calculated based on the electric field intensity distribution within the SC and the optical properties of the materials13. Quantum Utilization Efficiency (QUE) measures how effectively the absorbed photons are utilized to generate charge carriers in the SC. QUE not only considers the photon’s absorption but also the subsequent processes that lead to the generation of electric current.

Experimental details

Active layer preparation

The active regions of the PTB7-based colored ST-OSCs with integrated MgF2/WO3 1D-PC produced in this study consisted of a PTB7:PC71BM blend. The fabricated ST-OSCs have an inverted device (ID) architecture with a bulk-heterojunction (BHJ) active layer structure. Chlorobenzene (CB) and 1,8-diiodooctane (DIO) were used as solvents for preparing the PTB7:PC71BM blend. A mixture of 970 mL CB and 30 mL DIO was stirred for 1 h. Adding DIO to the chlorobenzene solution has improved surface adhesion and enhanced efficiency for the PTB7:PC71BM blend42. Ho et al. observed that a DIO concentration of 0–10% in chlorobenzene produced the best results at a 3% addition42. Therefore, a 97:3 ratio was chosen for the CB-DIO solvent. To prepare the PTB7 blend, 10 mg of PTB7 and 15 mg of PC71BM were added to 1 mL of the CB-DIO solution (97:3 by volume). The resulting 25 mg PTB7 (1:1.5 by weight) mixture was stirred with a magnetic stirrer at a constant temperature of 70 °C for approximately 18 h to ensure homogeneity.

Active layer coating

The spin coating technique was used for all film coatings of the active layer. The spin speed and duration were selected based on results from the literature for the PTB7:PC71BM (donor: acceptor) mixture43–45. A spin speed of 1500 rpm for 60 s was chosen to minimize interface defects and achieve high efficiency. The cleaned substrates were covered with 30 µL of the prepared PTB7 solution, and spin coating was performed at 1500 rpm for 60 s. After coating, the materials were left to dry slowly for approximately 12 h at room temperature in a clean room environment to prevent polymer structure degradation by thermal treatment43.

Deposition of ETL, HTL, and metal contacts

Transition metal oxide materials such as ZnO, MoO3, and WO3, which form the ETL and HTL, the metal contact material Ag, the MoO3/Ag/ MoO3 DMD transparent contacts, and the designed MgF2/WO3 photonic crystal system were deposited using the Nanovak NVTS-500 co-sputtering system. The system also features evaporation crucibles for thin-film deposition. The system has one DC and two RF sources for 2-inch targets. The target focus is a rotatable circular substrate holder that can be heated up to 600 °C. All deposition processes were conducted in a 10− 8 Torr vacuum environment.

Optical and photovoltaic characterization

The optical characteristics of the fabricated structures were determined using a Perkin Elmer Lambda 2 S UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer in the wavelength range of 300–1100 nm. A Keithley 4200 source meter and a Newport Oriel-Sol1A solar simulator with AM 1.5G illumination were used for J-V measurements.
