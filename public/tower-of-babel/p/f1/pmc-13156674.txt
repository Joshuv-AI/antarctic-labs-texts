Nonlinear associations between punishment sensitivity and problematic Internet use among adolescents: Results from cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses
Authors: Jin-Peng Wang, Jia-Qi Zhang, Ya Zheng, Kesong Hu, Sheng-Hao Zhang, Jing Xiao, Qi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model assumes that personal components, such as punishment sensitivity (PS), affect problematic Internet use (PIU) by influencing the way individuals perceive the situation. However, previous studies exploring the relationship between PS and PIU have yielded inconsistent findings. One plausible explanation for this inconsistency is the potential nonlinearity of the relationship between PS and PIU. The purpose of the present study is to examine the curvilinear association between PS and PIU among adolescents.MethodIn Study 1, we assessed 7555 participants using Behavioral Inhibition System Scales and Internet Addiction Test to measure PS and PIU. In Study 2, 506 participants were recruited to complete the Mixed Gambles Task and Internet Addiction Test to evaluate PS and PIU respectively. In Study 3, we conducted two waves of surveys within a one-year interval, with 1824 participants completing a battery of scales measuring their PS, anxiety, self-control, and PIU.ResultsThe results from Study 1 and Study 2 revealed a U-shaped association between PS and PIU. The results in Study 3 indicated that when PS was low, self-control mediated the relationship between PS and PIU. When PS was high, anxiety mediated the association between PS and PIU.ConclusionsThese preliminary results emphasize the importance of considering the nonlinear association between PS and PIU, expanding the I-PACE model. Furthermore, this study offers empirical evidence in support of tailored treatment and intervention approaches for PIU, taking into account their varying levels of PS.

Introduction

Problematic Internet use (PIU), as a prevalent mental health issue, is primarily characterized by the compulsive and continuous use of the Internet which causes detrimental consequences (Stavropoulos et al., 2017). The recent meta-analysis suggests that the global prevalence of PIU is alarmingly high with a rate of around 14.22 % (Meng et al., 2022). Moreover, PIU may also lead to repercussions, including aggression (Zhao et al., 2022) and depression (Qu et al., 2024). It is noteworthy that adolescents are vulnerable to the negative impacts of PIU, because of their specific psychological and physiological characteristics (Kuss et al., 2013). Thus, investigating the underlying mechanisms of PIU among adolescents is crucial for tailored prevention and intervention for PIU.

The Interaction of Person Affect Cognition Execution (I-PACE) model indicates that PIU arises from interactions among personality, affective responses, and cognitive processes (Brand et al., 2016). A distinctive feature of this model is its emphasis on personality traits that predispose individuals to PIU by shaping their affective and cognitive reactions (Brand et al., 2019). Within this theoretical framework, punishment sensitivity, a personality trait characterized by heightened responsiveness to conditioned stimuli associated with punishment or the termination of reward (Franken et al., 2006), shapes an individual's affective and cognitive reactions, thereby fostering PIU. On one hand, punishment sensitivity intensifies the affective reactivity toward negative life events, thereby significantly enhancing individuals' anxiety (Kubo et al., 2023; Ruckwongpatr et al., 2022). Anxiety may drive individuals to seek relief from the distress caused by negative events (Chaaya et al., 2025). Since the Internet is an effective tool in generating good feelings or avoiding negative emotional states, individuals may gradually develop a habitual pattern of using the Internet as a coping strategy to alleviate anxiety (Kuss et al., 2018). This maladaptive coping style may finally lead to PIU (Laier et al., 2018). On the other hand, punishment sensitivity may alter attention and reaction toward negative stimuli. Individuals with reduced punishment sensitivity exhibited less awareness toward potential punishment and deficits in processing negative outcomes (Brand et al., 2025). This blunted sensitivity may undermine self-control over Internet use, as these individuals may not perceive excessive use as a behavior worth regulating (Anataca et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2021; Megías-Robles et al., 2022). In turn, reduced self-control makes it difficult to inhibit excessive Internet use, thereby exacerbating PIU (Şalvarlı & Griffiths, 2022).

Numerous empirical studies have found a strong correlation between punishment sensitivity and PIU among adolescents (e.g., He et al., 2017). However, existing research has reported inconsistent findings regarding the direction of the association between PS and PIU (i.e., positive or negative correlations) are inconsistent. Some studies pointed out that adolescents with higher punishment sensitivity were more likely to develop PIU (Park et al., 2013), and this positive predictive effect was also observed during public health emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic (Kubo et al., 2023). Neuroimaging evidence further indicated that heightened punishment sensitivity promoted PIU by enhancing the activation of amygdala-prefrontal connectivity associated with emotional dysregulation, suggesting that elevated punishment sensitivity may increase emotional vulnerability and thereby increase the risk of PIU (Dong et al., 2022). Moreover, recent studies have shown that individuals with moderate levels of punishment sensitivity perform most adaptively in tasks involving self-control and reward-processing (Jean et al., 2023). This adaptive pattern reduces their risk of developing PIU (Li et al., 2019). Furthermore, other research found that adolescents with lower punishment sensitivity were more susceptible to becoming PIU. Adolescents with PIU reported lower levels of punishment sensitivity than healthy individuals (Li et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Behaviorally, youths with PIU showed reduced sensitivity to punishment in ambiguous decision-making situations, reflecting impaired responsiveness to negative outcomes (Khoury et al., 2019). Neuroimaging studies further revealed that adolescents with PIU display decreased activation in cognitive control brain regions, including the superior frontal gyrus and right anterior cingulate cortex, during loss processing (Dong et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2020). These behavioral and neural findings collectively suggest that reduced punishment sensitivity may be associated with weakened behavioral control over risky behaviors and is related to an increased risk of PIU. The discrepancies observed across these investigations underscore the intricate nature of the link between punishment sensitivity and the propensity for PIU. As outlined in the I-PACE model, PIU results from the complex interaction of personality traits with various affective and cognitive factors (Brand et al., 2019). Integrating this theoretical framework with existing empirical evidence suggests that punishment sensitivity may influence PIU through non‑linear pathways, rather than a simple linear effect. We propose a U‑shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU. This curvilinear model provides a parsimonious yet comprehensive framework that synthesizes the positive associations, the notion that moderate punishment sensitivity supports optimal adaptive functioning, and the negative relationships reported in previous studies (Frost et al., 2023).

The anxiety may play a mediating role between punishment sensitivity and PIU when punishment sensitivity is relatively high. Previous studies have found that punishment sensitivity enhances anxiety, which further causes PIU among adolescents (Park et al., 2013). One possible reason is that individuals with high punishment sensitivity are more reactive to potential negative consequences in the future, which causes anxiety (Kubo et al., 2023; Ruckwongpatr et al., 2022). In particular, adolescents encounter many stressful events, including stress from social interactions and academic pressures (Wang et al., 2025), thus more likely to feel anxious (McLaughlin & Hatzenbuehler, 2009). Once they try to relieve the anxiety by using the Internet (Liu et al., 2022), this experience increases the subjective reward expectancy of using the Internet through a reinforcement learning way (Brand et al., 2019, 2025). Therefore, they may gradually become accustomed to using the Internet to relieve anxiety in daily life (Chen et al., 2021; Chou et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2022). Consequently, high punishment sensitivity contribute to more anxious feelings, which in turn increases vulnerability to PIU.

Self-control may mediate the association between punishment sensitivity and PIU when punishment sensitivity is relatively low. Prior research suggests that individuals with excessively low punishment sensitivity may fail to recognize the risks associated with excessive internet use (Dong et al., 2011, 2013; Wang et al., 2020). Since awareness of negative consequences plays a crucial role in motivating behavioral regulation (Brand et al., 2025), a lack of risk perception may reduce self-control over Internet use (Anataca et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2021; Megías-Robles et al., 2022). Additionally, while self-control improvements in one domain can generalize to other domains (Tuk et al., 2015), this transfer effect appears weaker among individuals with low punishment sensitivity (Tuk et al., 2011), making it more difficult for them to enhance self-control ability. Given that self-control serves as a key protective factor against PIU (Brand et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026), individuals with weaker self-control tend to exhibit more severe PIU symptoms (Bağatarhan et al., 2023; Mei et al., 2016). Therefore, low punishment sensitivity may contribute to diminished self-control, which in turn increases vulnerability to PIU.

Punishment sensitivity, as a stable personality trait, reflects an individual's persistent tendency to respond to negative stimuli (Adrián-Ventura et al., 2023). In specific contexts, such as risky decision making, this trait may manifest behaviorally as loss aversion (Gianelli et al., 2022). Loss aversion is a common phenomenon in which individuals exhibit greater sensitivity to potential losses than to equal potential gains (Xu et al., 2020). In experimental settings, loss aversion can be regarded as a behavioral indicator of status-based punishment sensitivity, dynamically reflecting the immediate interaction between trait punishment sensitivity and the specific environment (Sokol-Hessner & Rutledge, 2019; Gianelli et al., 2022). Studies have also demonstrated a close relationship between trait punishment sensitivity and loss aversion (Gao et al., 2019; Gonçalves et al., 2022; Li et al., 2019). Therefore, in the present study, we adopted loss aversion as a behavioral marker of punishment sensitivity to enable methodological triangulation, thereby providing further evidence for the association between punishment sensitivity and PIU.

Overall, the current study utilized both questionnaires (Study 1) and behavioral task (Study 2) to investigate the nonlinear effect of punishment sensitivity on PIU among adolescents. Although the traditional I-PACE model posits linear associations between punishment sensitivity and PIU, prior empirical findings have been inconsistent, implying the possibility of a nonlinear pattern. We hypothesized that there was a U-shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU. Specifically, PIU would initially decline with heightened punishment sensitivity, only to subsequently increase after reaching the turning point. Furthermore, we performed a longitudinal study (Study 3) to determine whether the underlying mechanisms of punishment sensitivity enhancing PIU differed between individuals with low punishment sensitivity and those with high punishment sensitivity. According to the I-PACE model, punishment sensitivity may affect PIU through anxiety and self-control. Based on the I-PACE model and previous research, we hypothesized that, in line with I-PACE model, anxiety and self-control may mediate the relationships between PS and PIU. When punishment sensitivity was extremely high, anxiety acted as a mediator and when punishment sensitivity was extremely low, self-control acted as a mediator. Study 1

Participants and procedure

A total of 7694 students from primary (Grades 4 to 6) and secondary schools (Grades 7 to 12) were recruited from Haidian District, Shijingshan District, Daxing District, and the Economic and Technological Development Zone (ETDZ) of Beijing, China to voluntarily participate in our survey. We utilized a convenience sampling approach. At the start of the academic semester, the collaborating schools provided parents with written information sheets outlining the objectives and scope of the research, which sought to examine the students' Internet usage and identify the key influencing factors. All participants voluntarily enrolled in the study, and they and their parents signed a written consent form before the survey. Following the receipt of signed consent forms from both parents and students, the participating students were escorted by their teachers to a computer lab within the school, where they proceeded to complete a series of questionnaires. After excluding 139 participants who either failed an attention check (i.e., a question 'I never got sick') or selected the same response in all questionnaires, we were left with a final sample size of 7555 adolescents, of which 48.9 % were female. This yielded an effective response rate of 98.2 %. The age range of the participants spanned from 10 to 18 years old, with an average age of 12.43 years (SD = 2.04). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of [masked for blind review].

Measurements

Behavioral inhibition system scales

Punishment sensitivity was assessed using the BIS subscale of the Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) scale (Carver & White, 1994), and the Chinese version of this scale used in the present study has been validated (Li et al., 2008). It includes 7 items rated on a 4-point scale ranging from '1′ (not true for me at all) to '4′ (very true for me). It measures the tendency of participants to experience anxiety when confronted with cues of punishment. Higher scores indicate higher levels of punishment sensitivity. In Study 1, the Cronbach's α coefficient of the BIS was 0.78, showing good reliability.

Internet addiction test

The Young's Internet Addiction Test (IAT) was employed to assess PIU (Young, 1998), and its Chinese version used in the present study has been well validated (Lai et al., 2013). The IAT comprises 20 Likert-type items, with each item rated on a scale ranging from '1′ (not at all) to '5′ (always). Higher scores indicate a higher degree of PIU. In Study 1, the Cronbach's α coefficient of the IAT was 0.94, indicating excellent reliability.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2023). Initially, a preliminary analysis was conducted to examine the bivariate correlations between BIS scores, IAT scores, and demographic characteristics. For the main analyses, the "nls" function in R was utilized to assess the relationship between BIS scores and IAT scores. Both the quadratic model and linear model were employed to predict IAT scores based on BIS scores to clarify the disparate findings in previous studies. Additionally, gender and age were included as covariates in both models. To determine the best-fitting model, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were applied. The model with the lowest AIC and BIC values was considered the best fit, provided that BIS scores significantly predicted IAT scores in both the quadratic and linear models. In evaluating the goodness of fit, R2 was not used as it is deemed inadequate for nonlinear models (Spiess & Neumeyer, 2010). Finally, a robustness check was conducted following Haans et al. (2016) to confirm the quadratic relationship. The robustness check involved adding a cubic term to the regression model and assessing whether it improved the model fit. If the cubic term did not enhance the goodness of fit, it would support stronger evidence for a quadratic relationship.

Discussion

The results showed a quadratic relationship between BIS scores and IAT scores in adolescents, rather than a linear association. These findings supported the hypothesis that there is a U-shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU at trait level. It should be noted that previous studies measured punishment sensitivity not only by questionnaires (Li et al., 2016) but also by behavioral tasks (Yao et al., 2022). Questionnaires and behavioral tasks capture distinct aspects of punishment sensitivity. Trait punishment sensitivity, typically assessed via self-report questionnaires, reflects an individual's enduring, stable, and cross-situational personality disposition (Oka et al., 2025). In contrast, loss aversion, as measured by behavioral tasks, represents state punishment sensitivity, capturing dynamic activation within specific task contexts (Hong et al., 2023). Moreover, relying solely on personality questionnaires may induce biases related to social desirability (Pedregon et al., 2012). Therefore, incorporating behavioral tasks allows for methodological triangulation by assessing punishment sensitivity at both the subjective trait level and the objective behavioral level. This approach enables a more robust and comprehensive examination of whether the hypothesized U-shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU holds consistently across different measurement modalities. Therefore, Study 2 will employ the mixed gambles task to measure punishment sensitivity, aiming to further validate the U-shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU.

Study 2

Participants and procedure

Participants (independent of those in Study 1) were recruited from primary (Grades 4 to 6) and secondary (Grades 7 to 12) schools located in three regions of China, namely, the municipality of Beijing, Hebei, and Hainan provinces. The recruitment procedure was identical to that employed in Study 1. The sample consisted of 506 individuals, of which females accounted for 53.6 % of the participants. The age range of the participants spanned from 10 to 18 years old, with an average age of 14.02 years (SD = 2.43). Specifically, within our sample, all participants reported that they had not previously participated in a similar experiment and met the criteria of being right-handed, having normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and having no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders.

After providing their informed consent, participants were accompanied by their teachers to a computer lab, where the instructors explained the procedure for completing the questionnaires and the rules of the mixed-gambles task. Half of the participants completed the IAT before engaging in the mixed gambles task, while the other half undertook the mixed gambles task prior to completing the IAT. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of [masked for blind review].

Measurements

Mixed gambles task

We used the mixed gambles task (MGT; Tom et al., 2007) to measure loss aversion in participants. To familiarize the task, participants completed a training block with six trials before the formal block. Participants were informed that any decisions made in the mixed gambles task would be associated with the final reward, with more coins leading to greater gifts. Fig. 3A shows the timeline of one sample trial in MGT. At the beginning of each trial, a mixed gamble was presented, indicating an equal chance (50 %) to either win a certain number of coins or lose another specified number. This gamble was available for consideration for up to 6000 ms. Possible gains ranged from 10 coins to 24 coins in two increments, while possible losses ranged from 5 coins to 12 coins in one increment (Fig. 3B). Therefore, there were 64 possible combinations of gains and losses, which were presented randomly in 64 trials. The participants needed to decide by either accepting or rejecting the gamble that was presented. To encourage the participants to consider the subjective attractiveness of each gamble rather than simply following a predetermined rule, they were prompted to indicate the degree to which they accepted or rejected each gamble ("F" = strongly accept, "G" = weakly accept, "H" = weakly reject, and "J" = strongly reject). After a key press, the chosen option was highlighted with a red border for 1000 ms. Then, the next trial began after a blank screen was presented for 1000 ms. The outcome of each gamble was determined randomly with an equal chance, and the resulting number of coins was added to or subtracted from the total rewards in the program.Fig. 3The mixed gambles task. Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2023). In the preliminary analysis, we calculated λ from the mixed gambles task to estimate the participants' loss aversion. The acceptance rate was computed based on the strong/weak responses of each participant were converted into accept and reject categories ("strongly accept" and "weakly accept" to accept, "strongly reject" and "weakly reject" to reject). Next, a logistic regression was performed to compute the regression coefficients for the gains and losses variables. The participants' decisions (accept or reject) were entered into the model as the dependent variable, while the magnitudes of the potential gains (bgains) and losses (blosses) were entered as the independent variables. Then, the formula λ = -blosses/bgains was employed to compute λ (Tom et al., 2007). A smaller λ value represents lower loss aversion. In the main analyses, the "nls" function in R was used to assess the relations between λ and IAT scores. Both the quadratic model and linear model with λ were run to predict IAT scores. Furthermore, gender and age were conducted as covariates in both models. Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were utilized to select the most suitable model, which had the lowest AIC and BIC values. This selection was made when λ demonstrated significant prediction of IAT scores in both quadratic and linear models. The λ values were square root-transformed. Lastly, the same robustness check was conducted as in Study 1.

Discussion

The results of Study 2 revealed a quadratic relationship between λ and IAT scores among adolescents. Aligning with prior research (Yao et al., 2020), IAT scores were observed to be higher for adolescents with low λ as compared to those with moderate levels of λ. It is suggested that adolescents with low λ might underestimate the potentially severe repercussions of excessive Internet use (Wang et al., 2020), which could contribute to the development of PIU. Conversely, those who demonstrated high λ also showed elevated IAT scores compared to their moderate λ counterparts, echoing the outcomes observed in Study 1. In summary, the evidence from both Study 1 and Study 2 verified the hypothesis that the effect of punishment sensitivity on PIU in young populations exhibited a more complex, nonlinear pattern. However, the underlying mechanisms of the U-shaped relationship remain. We conducted Study 3 to directly test the hypothesis we proposed in the Introduction section.

Study 3

Participants and procedure

The initial sample comprised 1881 participants from primary (Grades 4 to 6) and secondary schools (Grades 7 to 12) in Beijing municipality, Hebei, and Hainan provinces in China. The recruitment procedure followed the same protocol as that in Study 1. They completed self-report questionnaires in June 2023 (Time 1) and again in June 2024 (Time 2). After excluding 57 participants who did not complete the whole questionnaires, the final sample size was 1824 individuals aged 10 to 18 years (M = 12.86, SD = 2.68).

Measurements

Behavioral inhibition system scales

The BIS subscale used in Study 3 remained the same as in Study 1. In Study 3, for the first and second assessments, Cronbach's α values at Time 1 and Time 2 were 0.87 and 0.79, respectively.

Internet addiction test

The measurement was consistent with Study 1 and Study 2. In Study 3, the Cronbach's α at Time 1 and Time 2 were 0.95 and 0.96.

Generalized anxiety disorder scale

The Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) was used to evaluate anxiety (Spitzer et al., 2006), and its Chinese version used in the present study has been validated (Zeng et al., 2013). The GAD-7 has 7 items with four options ranging from '0′ (not at all) to '3′ (nearly every day). Higher scores indicate higher levels of anxiety. In the present study, the Cronbach's α of the scale at Time 1 and Time 2 were 0.93 and 0.94.

Brief self-control scale

The self-control was assessed by the Brief Self-Control Scale (SCS; Morean et al., 2014), which has been translated and validated for use in Chinese populations (Luo et al., 2021). In the present study, we utilized the 'impulse control' subscale of SCS. This subscale contains 4 items, with each item rated on a scale ranging from '1′ (not at all) to '5′ (always). The impulse control subscale measured the ability to resist short-term rewards and temptations, with higher scores indicating greater ability to control impulsivity (Luo et al., 2021; Morean et al., 2014).The Cronbach's α of the impulse control subscale in the present study was 0.80 and 0.79 at Time 1 and Time 2, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed in Mplus version 8.3. We first divided the participants into two groups based on the empirically derived vertex. Using the same nonlinear regression model as in Study 1, the results showed that in Study 3, the quadratic term was –2.91 and the linear term was 0.09. Based on the quadratic function, the vertex was calculated as –1 * (–2.91) / (2 * 0.09) = 16.16. Accordingly, participants were split into two groups: the left half of the U-shaped group (LU group), in which individuals had BIS scores below 16.16, and the right half of the U-shaped curve (RU group), in which participants exhibited BIS scores above 16.16. Cross-lagged panel models for half-longitudinal designs were employed. For both the RU and LU groups, the longitudinal mediating effect of anxiety and self-control in the relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU was tested, respectively. Additionally, gender and age were included as covariates in all mediation models. Across the four models, statistical significance of the indirect effect was evaluated using bootstrapped confidence intervals based on 5000 resamples. A significant mediating effect was supported if the 95 % bootstrapped confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero (Yin et al., 2023).

Discussion

The results of Study 3 supported the hypothesis that although both individuals with high and low punishment sensitivity tended to have higher levels of PIU, there were different mechanisms underlying these two conditions. When punishment sensitivity was extremely high, individuals felt more anxious. Then, individuals might use the Internet to release anxiety (Liu et al., 2022). On the contrary, people with extremely low punishment sensitivity fail to be aware of the potential risks of excessive Internet use, reducing their level of self-control. Thus, they were prone to be addicted to the Internet (Mei et al., 2016).

General discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the nonlinear relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU among adolescents. We measured punishment sensitivity in adolescents using the BIS scale and the MGT task respectively, and built regression models to predict PIU from punishment sensitivity. The results showed that there was a U-shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU, implying that adolescents were more prone to develop PIU when punishment sensitivity was either extremely high or extremely low. Moreover, when punishment sensitivity was extremely high, anxiety played a mediator role between punishment sensitivity and PIU. When punishment sensitivity was extremely low, self-control mediated the punishment sensitivity and PIU. These results enrich and expand the I-PACE model, underscoring the nonlinear impact of punishment sensitivity as a person component of PIU. Additionally, the findings also provide implications for healthcare providers. Mental health practitioners and educators should attach importance to adolescents with either excessively high or low punishment sensitivity and provide appropriate guidance for these specific groups to prevent and mitigate the occurrence of PIU.

The present study found that adolescents with high punishment sensitivity were more prone to have PIU than adolescents with moderate levels of punishment sensitivity, and anxiety mediated this pathway. These results were in line with the previous studies. Some cross-sectional studies (Meerkerk et al., 2010) and longitudinal designs (Kräplin et al., 2022; Xue et al., 2023) demonstrated that punishment sensitivity positively predicted PIU. This relationship has also been confirmed among adolescents (Liu et al., 2022). The I-PACE model provides a framework for explaining this phenomenon (Brand et al., 2019). According to the I-PACE model, adolescents with high punishment sensitivity tend to experience anxiety (Park et al., 2013) and worry (Talisman & Rohrbeck, 2025) in daily life. To alleviate this psychological distress, adolescents may seek relief, and the Internet often serves as an accessible and immediate tool to escape from stressful events or negative emotions (Chou et al., 2015; Gao et al., 2022; Ruckwongpatr et al., 2022). This successful avoidance, in turn, increases the likelihood of using the Internet as a primary coping method in the future, thereby enhancing the levels of PIU (Laier et al., 2018). This proposed pathway is further corroborated by recent large-scale studies. For instance, blocking life trouble is the core symptom of PIU among adolescents (Wang et al., 2024). However, in the long run, excessive Internet use may lead to more negative events (e.g., academic problems and complaints from parents), allowing adolescents to frequently experience anxiety in daily life (Zhang et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the withdrawal symptoms of problematic Internet use may make individuals feel anxious when they end their use of the Internet (Romano et al., 2017). Adolescents with high punishment sensitivity are sensitive to anxiety. Thus, they may spend more time on the Internet to avoid anxiety, producing a vicious circle that continuously develop PIU (Brand et al., 2016; Erhel et al., 2024).

The present study showed that compared with adolescents with moderate punishment sensitivity, those individuals with low punishment sensitivity were more likely to fall into PIU, and self-control played a mediator in this process. These results were consistent with some previous studies. For example, individuals with PIU generally showed less sensitivity to punishment (Wang et al., 2020), and they were less responsive to potential real-life punishment that they might face due to excessive Internet use (Dong et al., 2013). At the same time, such people were also insensitive to immediate negative stimuli during Internet use (Cho et al., 2022). The I-PACE model offers a potential explanation for the negative correlation between punishment sensitivity and problematic internet use. On the one hand, adolescents with low punishment sensitivity exhibit an attentional bias that leads them to pay less attention to cues associated with punishment and the deprivation of rewards (Kreuze et al., 2020; Serrano-Ibáñez et al., 2019). This bias makes them more likely to overlook the potential negative consequences of excessive Internet use and to fail in controlling their time spent online, thereby contributing to the development of PIU (Brand et al., 2016). On the other hand, individuals with low punishment sensitivity tend to exhibit reduced responsiveness to negative outcomes (Vargas et al., 2019). This is reflected in lower error-correction rates following negative feedback (Brand et al., 2025), and reduced physiological arousal in response to negative stimuli (Yoon et al., 2015). As a result, they may fail to control their time spent online even when facing negative consequences (Feldmann et al., 2021). In summary, low punishment sensitivity appears to impair both the identification of and the responsiveness to negative stimuli, thereby diminishing self-control and, in turn, exacerbating PIU.

The I-PACE model emphasizes the strong connection between personality traits and PIU (Brand et al., 2016, 2019). Most previous studies assumed that the relationships between personality traits and PIU were linear (e.g., Marengo et al., 2020). However, previous studies on the association between punishment sensitivity and PIU showed inconsistent results. Some researchers found that individuals with high punishment sensitivity were more prone to be addicted than individuals with low punishment sensitivity (e.g., Kubo et al., 2023), while other studies pointed out that people with low punishment sensitivity were more likely to use the Internet excessively (e.g., Nikolaidou et al., 2016). The present study proposed that the nature of the link between punishment sensitivity and PIU might exhibit a U-shaped pattern, rather than a linear pattern that was assumed by previous studies. This point suggested that there might be a nonlinear association between specific personality traits and PIU, expanding the understanding of the relationship between personality traits and PIU by the I-PACE model. Moreover, punishment, as a general predisposing variable, has a potential effect on many types of addictive behaviors (Brand et al., 2019). It is noteworthy that the relationships between punishment sensitivity and other addictive behaviors such as alcohol dependence are also inconsistent. Some studies found that the correlation was positive while others found a negative correlation (Logge et al., 2023). Future research could explore whether the relationship between punishment sensitivity and other addictive behaviors also presents a U-shaped pattern to further enrich and extend the I-PACE model.

While the present study offers robust evidence for U-shaped relationships between punishment sensitivity and PIU, the relatively small effect sizes observed in the mediating models may limit the direct practical applicability of the findings. Nevertheless, such small effect sizes are not uncommon in longitudinal research on adolescent PIU, as these models often account for complex patterns influenced by multiple confounding variables (Liu et al., 2025). Importantly, the statistical significance of these effects supports the stability and reliability of the proposed mediating mechanisms, suggesting that the findings may still offer some meaningful implications. For mental health providers, punishment sensitivity, as a relatively stable personality factor, could provide us with some potential interventions for PIU among adolescents (Brand et al., 2016). To be specific, for students with high sensitivity to punishment, focusing on reducing the anxiety they experience in daily life (Park et al., 2013), and guiding them to establish an adaptive coping style with pressure (Dong et al., 2024), may be a way to reduce their time spending on the Internet. On the other hand, for students with low punishment sensitivity, mental health providers could try to focus on their attentional bias and make them fully aware of the possible negative consequences caused by problematic Internet use, reducing their risk of developing PIU (Xu et al., 2021). In addition, educators and parents are suggested to pay more attention to students' Internet use, especially in cases where students exhibit high or low sensitivity to punishment. Once the students show "stay online longer than intended" several times, it implies that they may be at risk of getting addicted to the Internet (Wang et al., 2024). Professional help and targeted prevention and intervention should be sent to these students in time. It is crucial to reiterate, however, that these practical suggestions are drawn from statistically significant but relatively small effect sizes. Therefore, they should be viewed as preliminary directions rather than definitive clinical guidelines. Future research with larger, more diverse samples and experimental designs is necessary to determine whether interventions targeting these specific mechanisms can produce clinically meaningful changes in adolescents' PIU.

It is worth noting that the present study also has some limitations. Firstly, while the study focused on adolescents due to their heightened vulnerability to PIU (Wang et al., 2024), it remains unclear whether the observed U-shaped relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU generalizes to other age groups such as adults and children. Future research should examine whether similar patterns emerge across different developmental stages. Secondly, the study's two-wave design, though informative, limits the ability to formally test mediation pathways or disentangle between-person and within-person effects. To address this, future studies could incorporate additional time points and employ advanced analytical methods, such as random intercept cross-lagged panel models (Muthén et al., 2024), to better distinguish intra-individual dynamics from inter-individual differences and directly assess indirect effects. Thirdly, it should be noted that socioeconomic status (SES) may play a confounder in the relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU. On one hand, SES may be associated with punishment sensitivity, as individuals with lower SES tend to report higher levels of punishment sensitivity (White et al., 2022). On the other hand, SES may also influence PIU. For instance, higher parental education levels negatively predict adolescents' PIU (Fadl et al., 2025). Adolescents with high versus low SES exhibit different core symptoms of PIU, suggesting that SES altered the underlying mechanisms in the development of PIU (Zhuo et al., 2025). Therefore, the observed correlations between punishment sensitivity and PIU may be partially explained by variations in SES. Future studies should consider SES as a covariate to further validate the associations identified in the present study. Fourthly, the present study treated loss aversion as a behavioral indicator of punishment sensitivity given their conceptual overlap. Punishment sensitivity (assessed via the BIS) reflects stable personality trait related to sustained responsiveness to punishment or aversive stimuli (Oka et al., 2025). In contrast, loss aversion measured by the mixed gambling task represents a state like behavioral sensitivity to losses relative to equivalent gains (Hong et al., 2023). Neuroimaging evidence indicates shared neural substrates between the two constructs, supporting their similarity (Arioli et al., 2023; He et al., 2026). Some behavioral studies have also found a positive association between BIS based punishment sensitivity and task-based loss aversion (e.g., Gao et al., 2019). Nevertheless, the present study did not simultaneously assess both measures to directly examine their association and distinctions. Future studies should administer both the BIS scale and the mixed gambling task within the same sample to quantify their correlation. Furthermore, factor analysis may be adopted to identify a common latent factor underlying punishment sensitivity and loss aversion (Huang et al., 2024), enabling tests of whether this general negative sensitivity factor exhibits a U-shaped association with PIU. Lastly, the assessment of the severity of PIU in this study mainly relied on students' self-reports. However, considering that Internet addicts may hide the duration of time online (Young, 1998), it may not fully and accurately reflect students' PIU solely based on a self-reported questionnaire. Therefore, future studies could consider integrating data reported by parents, teachers, and others to more comprehensively identify and assess PIU among adolescents (Shiyun et al., 2023).

The present study investigated the nonlinear relationship between punishment sensitivity and PIU by self-reporting and behavioral measurement. The results were consistent with the hypothesis, revealing a U-shaped association between punishment sensitivity and PIU, meaning that adolescents were more likely to fall into PIU when punishment sensitivity was extremely high or extremely low. The findings not only enrich the I-PACE model's understanding of the relationship between personality traits and addiction but also provide new insight for early identification and intervention of PIU in adolescents.
