Assessment of apical debris extrusion with three nickel–titanium rotary systems using conventional and lyophilization-based quantification methods
Authors: Nishmitha N. Hegde, Shruthi Attavar, Avishikta Banerjee, Niranjan Harikrishna, V. Chaithra Lakshmi, Manikandan Ravinanthanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Apical extrusion of debris during root canal preparation is inevitable and may contribute to postoperative pain and periapical inflammation. The extent of extrusion varies with instrument design and motion. ProTaper Next (PTN), TruNatomy (TN), and HyFlex EDM (HEDM) systems feature distinct geometries and tapers aimed at improving shaping efficiency while reducing extrusion. The conventional Myers and Montgomery model for debris quantification lacks precision in moisture control, whereas lyophilization (freeze-drying) offers a novel, potentially more accurate alternative.Aim:The aim is to compare apically extruded debris using rectangular cross-section file system (PTN), parallelogram cross-section file system (TN), and variable cross-section file system (HEDM), and to assess lyophilization as a quantification method.Materials and Methods:Ninety extracted single-canal premolars (curvature <5°) were randomly assigned to two main groups (n = 45). Group 1 measured debris using the Myers and Montgomery model, while Group 2 used lyophilization. Each group was subdivided into three subgroups – rectangular cross-section file system, parallelogram cross-section file system, and variable cross-section file system. Canals were irrigated with 2 mL bidistilled water after each file sequence, and extruded debris was quantified per the respective method.Results and Conclusion:All samples exhibited debris extrusion. Parallelogram cross-section file system (TN) produced the least extrusion, though differences were not statistically significant. Lyophilization demonstrated reliability and precision for debris quantification.

INTRODUCTION

Debridement, disinfection, and obturation are basic endodontic concepts that aim to preserve dentition and create a milieu supportive of periradicular healing. Instruments and irrigants are employed for cleaning and shaping the root canal space. Dentin chips, pulp tissue, bacteria, and irrigants are pushed inadvertently to the periradicular area during this operation, which causes unfavorable effects, such as inflammation, postoperative discomfort, and delayed periapical healing. According to reports, apical debris extrusion (ADE) can cause severe problems 1.4%–16% of the time. Hence, the prevention of apical transportation of debris is of paramount importance for the success of the treatment.[1]

Physical aspects of tooth anatomy, such as canal angle and working length (WL), as well as mechanical aspects such as instrumentation procedures, motion kinetics, and the number and kind of instruments, can influence the amount of debris extrusion.[2]

Clinicians routinely employ rotary endodontic instruments to contour root canals, facilitating efficient cleaning and shaping. These systems utilize mechanical forces to remove infected pulp tissue and contaminated dentin. However, the mechanical action of these instruments generates corresponding reaction forces on the canal walls and root dentin. Depending on the instrument’s geometry, size, and alloy composition, different rotary systems may induce varying magnitudes of stress within the root structure, potentially contributing to dentinal defects or apical microcrack formation.[2]

Root canal shaping has become more efficient and predictable with the advent of nickel–titanium (NiTi) rotary instruments, which enhance shaping precision while reducing procedural errors. Owing to their superior cutting efficiency, reduced preparation time, and lower incidence of postoperative discomfort, NiTi systems are now widely preferred by clinicians. During instrumentation, radicular dentin is cut and enlarged through continuous interaction between the rotating file and canal walls. The high rotational speeds (>300 rpm) generate transient but repetitive contact points, leading to localized stress concentrations within the dentin. When the WL extends beyond the optimal limit, canal preparation alone can significantly weaken the root structure, increasing the risk of apical microcracks or splits. Consequently, certain highly active rotary NiTi systems may inadvertently extend beyond the apical foramen, further predisposing the root to apical fracture formation.[3]

Biologically, all the sources of irritants must be removed from the root canal space. Effective cleaning and shaping of the root canal system (RCS) with rotary instrumentation and appropriate irrigation techniques contribute significantly to the success of endodontic therapy. However, during this process, irrigants and debris containing necrotic tissue, microorganisms, pulpal remnants, and dentinal particles may be extruded beyond the apex into the periapical tissues, potentially causing postoperative inflammation and subsequent treatment failure.[45]

ADE has been reported with various instrumentation and irrigation protocols, with the amount of extrusion differing according to the shaping technique employed. Variations in canal preparation methods can influence the extent of debris forced beyond the apical foramen, even when instrumentation is confined short of the apical terminus. The majority of NiTi rotary systems employ a crown-down technique with a push–pull or pecking motion, which inevitably leads to a certain extent of ADE. However, advancements in instrument design, alloy technology, and kinematics are expected to exert a favorable effect by minimizing the quantity of debris extruded during canal preparation.[5]

Since both irrigants and debris are extruded apically, the liquid must be allowed to evaporate and be stored in an incubator. The irrigant used in the current study was bidistilled water, which prevents any weight gain due to the development of sodium hypochlorite crystals. Quantification of the debris extruded using this methodology tends to be greater than that achieved with double-distilled water.[5]

There have been significant advancements in developing NiTi rotary instruments in recent years. This continuous improvement in the manufacturing processes aims to produce more efficient files, possessing flexibility, efficiency, safety, and simplicity.

ProTaper Next (PTN) is a comprehensive file system characterized by its unique offset design with rectangular cross-section, which generates a mechanical wave-like motion throughout the entire length of the instrument. This decreases the amount of contact between the file and dentin, hence enhancing debris removal and flexibility.[6]

TruNatomy (TN) is a single-file system with a parallelogram cross-section that has the advantage of preserving the pericervical dentin due to its instrument design, and hence, there’s a need to compare its efficiency with the existing rotary systems.[7]

HyFlex EDM (HEDM) is also a single-file system with variable cross-section that operates in continuous rotation and incorporates a variable cross-sectional design along its cutting length – triangular coronally, trapezoidal in the middle third, and quadrangular apically. Owing to this geometry, the file maintains four points of contact with the canal walls, thereby enhancing cutting efficiency. However, this leads to limited space for debris elimination.[89]

This study aims to compare the amount of apically extruded debris after preparation of root canals using the rectangular cross-section file system, parallelogram cross-section file system, and variable cross-section file system. The lyophilization technique is also assessed as a method for quantifying ADE.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was reported as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory studies in Endodontology 2021 guidelines. The study was conducted in the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences, and Central Research Laboratory, Nitte (Deemed to be University) after obtaining the Institutional Ethical Clearance (ABSM/EC/98/2021).

Sample preparation

Ninety noncarious mandibular premolars with complete root formation and radiographically showing a single root canal with <5° curvature were included in the study. The sample size was calculated based on the study conducted by Schneider., 1971.[10] Teeth with deep caries and caries involving the pulp, more than a single canal, fractured teeth, nonvital teeth, open apex, and resorption were excluded from the study. Coronal access was achieved using Endo-access burs, and apical patency was achieved with a size 15 K-file.

The WL was established by subtracting 1 mm from the length of the initial instrument (size 15 K) at the apical foramen.

Sample grouping

Ninety extracted single-canal premolars with canal curvature <5° were randomly assigned to two main groups (n = 45 each) based on the method of apical debris quantification:

Group I – Myers and Montgomery method, and Group II – Lyophilization method.

Each main group was further subdivided into three subgroups (n = 15) according to the rotary instrumentation system used: Subgroup A: Rectangular cross-section file system (PTN), Subgroup B: Parallelogram cross-section file system (TN), and Subgroup C: Variable cross-section file system (HEDM).

Experimental setup

In Group I, debris and irrigant were collected following the Myers and Montgomery model.[11] Each tooth was mounted in a preweighed Tarsons tube serving as the debris collection chamber. Elastomeric impression material sealed the cervical region, and a 27G venting needle equalized air pressure during instrumentation. Receptor tubes attached to the apical elastomeric plug collected the extruded debris and irrigant. Following instrumentation, tubes were incubated at 37°C for 7 days to ensure complete drying before final weighing. The apparatus was concealed to maintain operator blinding.

In Group II, debris was collected using the lyophilization method. Extruded material in receptor tubes was freeze-dried under vacuum, allowing precise quantification by removing all moisture while preserving solid debris.

Irrigation

Following the use of each file, the canals were irrigated with 2 mL of bidistilled water delivered through a TN irrigation needle positioned 1 mm short of the WL. Debris adhering to the root surface was rinsed into the tube with 1 mL bidistilled water.

Debris evaluation

The extruded debris was gathered in preweighed Eppendorf tubes. Each tube was weighed three times on a microbalance (Sartorius Japan KK-BSA224S-CW; accuracy 0.00001 g), and the mean weight was recorded.

For Group I, tubes were incubated at 37°C for 7 days, and for Group II, samples underwent lyophilization. The net apical debris weight (AED) was calculated as the difference between pre- and postinstrumentation mean weights.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software version 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, United States) was used to analyze the data. Descriptive statistics were done using mean and standard deviation since the data followed the normal distribution curve. Parametric analysis and an independent sample t-test were used for data interpretation; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

All ninety samples included in this study exhibited measurable ADE, confirming that debris extrusion is an inevitable consequence of root canal instrumentation regardless of the system or quantification method employed. The mean and standard deviation of extruded debris for each subgroup were calculated, and intergroup comparisons were made using independent sample t-tests with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Comparison between quantification methods

A highly significant difference was observed between the conventional Myers and Montgomery method and the lyophilization method across all three rotary systems (P = 0.000). The mean amount of debris extrusion recorded using the lyophilization method was substantially greater than that obtained through the conventional drying model, indicating higher sensitivity and accuracy of lyophilization in detecting minor variations in debris mass [Table 1].

Intergroup statistical analysis revealed a significant difference between the parallelogram cross-section file system and the variable cross-section file system (B-C, P = 0.001), indicating that the parallelogram cross-section file system produced significantly less apical debris compared to the variable cross-section file system. Although the intergroup variations were not universally significant, a consistent trend of reduced debris extrusion was observed with the parallelogram cross-section file system, suggesting that its design and single-file kinematics contribute to more efficient coronal transportation of debris.

DISCUSSION

Apical extrusion of debris and irrigant during root canal cleaning and shaping is a critical factor influencing treatment outcomes. Studies have shown that dentin particles, necrotic tissue, bacteria, and irrigation solutions can be forced into periapical tissues during instrumentation, eliciting an inflammatory response. While tissue reactions are more pronounced when instrumentation extends beyond the apex, even short-of-apex extrusion can provoke postoperative inflammation. Therefore, minimizing the amount of apically extruded debris is essential to reduce adverse postoperative responses.[12]

The amount of apically extruded debris can vary depending on the preparation technique and the design of the file system;[13] however, all instrumentation methods tend to propel intracanal contents toward the periapical tissues. The cross-sectional design and motion of rotary files facilitate the collection of dentinal debris into the flutes and its coronal transportation toward the orifice, thereby reducing the likelihood of debris compaction within the canal.[14]

Variations in ADE among mechanical systems are influenced by differences in cross-sectional design, cutting blade geometry, taper, tip configuration, usage principles, flexibility, alloy composition, number of files employed, kinematics, and overall cutting efficiency.[1516]

Siqueira highlighted the delicate balance between microbial aggression and host defenses. Apical extrusion of microorganisms during biomechanical preparation disrupts this balance, exposing periradicular tissues to additional irritants and eliciting an immediate inflammatory response aimed at restoring equilibrium between the root canal and periapical space. The severity of this reaction depends on the quantity and virulence of the extruded bacteria. Consequently, minimizing the amount of apically extruded debris is essential to reduce postoperative inflammation and promote favorable treatment outcomes.[17]

The debris extruded to the periapical area contains both organic and inorganic tissues, which could initiate the inflammatory reactions and postoperative pain, and flare-ups.[4]

The present study confirmed that ADE occurs with all three rotary systems, consistent with previous reports that some extrusion is unavoidable during biomechanical preparation of the root canal. In this study, the parallelogram cross-section file system (TN) extruded the least debris, followed by the rectangle cross-section file system (PTN). In contrast, the variable cross-section file system (HEDM) showed the highest extrusion. These findings align with previous studies reporting that file design and motion influence debris extrusion, but complete elimination remains impossible. Yılmaz Çırakoglu and Özbay[18] and Mustafa et al.[19] observed similar trends, with TN producing the least amount of apically extruded debris, although differences with PTN were statistically insignificant.

The design characteristics of the files help explain these observations. TN and PTN have similar apical tapers with a regressive design that increases coronally to maintain a 0.8 mm diameter of the flute. The TN Orifice Modifier, with its modified triangular cross-section, offers conservative orifice relocation compared to the PTN XA Opener, potentially minimizing coronal dentin removal. The TN system features an offset parallelogram-shaped cross-section with two 85° cutting edges and a 24° helical angle in its active portion, ensuring that only one cutting edge contacts the canal wall at a time, thereby minimizing the engagement area.[7] This contributes to reduced apical extrusion of debris.

In contrast, PTN files rotate in a unique asymmetrical motion and feature an offset rectangular cross-section, contacting the canal wall at only two points.[20] This reduces engagement, limits undesirable taper lock, allows more cross-sectional cutting and debris removal, and facilitates production of larger dentin chips.[18]

HEDM utilizes a continuous rotation motion and has a variable cross-section along its entire cutting length. The coronal third has an almost triangular cross-section, the middle third a trapezoidal design, and the apical third a quadratic cross-section. The file maintains four points of contact with the canal walls, enhancing cutting efficacy; however, limited flute space may restrict coronal debris clearance, potentially explaining the slightly higher extrusion observed in this system. In addition, the HEDM preparation file has 5% taper, and the 25 finisher file has a variable taper, which may further contribute to differences in ADE.[821]

Debris quantification was performed using both the conventional Myers and Montgomery model and the novel lyophilization technique. Both methods reliably detected extrusion; however, lyophilization provided more precise and reproducible measurements by eliminating moisture-related variability inherent to incubator drying. Freeze-drying preserves all solid debris while removing water completely, allowing accurate assessment of net AED. This advantage is particularly useful when evaluating subtle differences among rotary systems.[22]

Given the variability in experimental setups and outcomes in debris extrusion studies, critically evaluating methodology is essential. Comparative assessments, such as the current study, improve understanding of the influence of file design and instrumentation technique on extrusion and guide researchers in selecting appropriate methods. Outlining the benefits and limitations of each methodology also encourages the development of experimental designs that better reflect clinical conditions, enhancing the translational relevance of in vitro findings.

CONCLUSION

All three rotary systems produced ADE, with the parallelogram cross-section file system demonstrating a trend toward lower extrusion, likely due to its single-file design and variable taper. The rectangle cross-section file system and variable cross-section file system showed higher debris extrusion, influenced by their sequential instrumentation and cross-sectional design. Furthermore, the lyophilization method proved to be a reliable and precise alternative to the traditional Myers and Montgomery model for quantifying apical debris. These findings emphasize the clinical relevance of selecting rotary systems that facilitate coronal debris removal and highlight lyophilization as a robust tool for in vitro evaluation of ADE.
