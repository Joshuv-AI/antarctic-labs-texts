AABA Task Force on the Ethical Study of Human Remains Recommendations: Proposal for the Management and Oversight of Community Partnership and Ethical Stewardship of Human Remains
Authors: Benjamin M Auerbach, Fatimah L C Jackson, Shamsi Daneshvari Berry, Michael L Blakey, Jennifer Caldwell, Carter Clinton, Joseph L Graves, Joseph B Jones, Ellen M Lofaro, Ripan S Malhi, Carmen V Mosley, Phoebe R Stubblefield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEthically responsible and culturally acceptable management, study, and stewardship of legacy skeletal and other human remains currently held and managed in scientific institutions is a longstanding concern that, over the length of these collections' existence, has been exiguously addressed. Most recently, the ethical treatment of legacy collections of individuals from the African American community in the United States has been especially highlighted. The American Association of Biological Anthropologists (AABA) created a Presidential Task Force to address these concerns about legacy collections in 2022 by drafting practices and recommendations for policies to be adopted by the AABA and sibling organizations.We report on the first ever convergent analysis of research priorities and perspectives on these topics from the communities of biological anthropologists and a national cross‐section of African Americans. Based on the surveys and discussions with these communities, all groups expressed a desire to enter a mutual, formal partnership where descendant communities are empowered to make decisions about the study and disposition of legacy collections. Our recommendations focus on promoting dialogue between parties involved through partnerships where desired. To make this possible, institutions should inventory and determine provenance of remains in legacy collections, ascertain the identity of descendant communities, and contact those communities using guidelines we provide. We argue that a default position taken by researchers is that no research need occur without the explicit consent of relevant descendant communities or communities of care. Examples of successful community partnerships are provided, along with new practices in ethical engagement with descendant communities.Keywords: bioethics, community partnership, descendant community, legacy collection, science communication

Terminology Used in This Document

Community of Origin

In determining the identity of human remains, the community of origin comprises all individuals to whom the interpretations about the study of those remains apply. This includes but is not limited to those who are genetically and culturally related directly to the individuals whose remains are under investigation. This provides opportunities for groups to self‐identify their association with the remains of any individual or group of individuals.

Community of Care

We use a definition for community of care that aligns with the working definition established by the American Association for Anatomy Legacy Anatomical Collections Task Force. Essentially, these are individuals and groups who have a more intimate role in the oversight and stewardship of human remains. These can include members of descendant communities represented in local collections, researchers, collections managers, and administrators. Those in this community are advocates for the individuals whose remains are housed in collections, providing unique and essential perspectives that give context for decisions made about the care and potential study of those remains. As noted by the American Association for Anatomy Task Force, the “community of care could include individuals representing cultural communities, historical communities, ethical interests and/or genetic relatedness.”

Community Partnership

The guiding principles of community partnership involve transparency, cultural awareness, and clear and regular communication between communities. These communities include but are not limited to the community of origin, descendant community, community of care (or ethical clients), scientific community, and other interested parties. Under ideal circumstances, the parameters of the partnership should be determined by all participants at the inception of communication with regular updating. Community partnerships can encompass different arrangements between parties. At one end of the range, research taking place within community partnership may be community‐led, in which the community instigates or guides the research agenda. At the other, studies are driven by the academic researchers in consultation with members of communities of origin and care to ensure ethical concerns are incorporated into the research program.

Descendant Community 1

A descendant community is a group of people whose ancestors were interred at a cemetery, had lived at a particular historic site, or whose remains have become part of a legacy collection. A descendant community is the social community descended from that of the deceased. These include both those who know their specific familial genealogical connection to the deceased and those who do not. The definition of descendant community is sufficiently broad to encompass those without definite lineal ties to a single site, but whose family histories are included in its surrounding region, reflecting the fact that the family ties and ethnic associations descendant community represents often crossed site boundaries. The descendant community is the primary decision maker in the absence of identifiable lineal descendants. “Descendant community” combines the definition of the community of origin with that of a community of care.

Informed Consent

Informed consent is a voluntary process. It is the process of describing a research project to a potential participant, in sufficient detail, that the participant knows the risks, benefits, future scope of the research, and their own choices regarding participation. This process is ongoing throughout the scope of research and beyond its conclusion. Any individual giving consent to take part themselves or on behalf of others is a participant, not a subject, through appropriate application of an informed consent procedure. The informed consent process serves to mitigate harm to individuals contributing themselves or permitting their ancestors to take part in research, and if enacted in good faith, establishes a relationship between living participants and researchers that lessens or prevents objectification.

Informed consent is essential in the stewardship of all legacy collections, though there is variation in its solicitation and implementation. In an anatomical gift program, for example, living donors and next of kin can engage in the informed consent process. Individual state laws may provide other individuals, such as agents acting as medical power of attorney or legally authorized representatives, to engage in the informed consent process for the purposes of anatomical gifting. In the context of managing a legacy collection of human remains, it may not be possible to obtain informed consent from these groups, and so descendant communities or communities of care should be consulted.

Legacy Collection

These are defined as collections of human remains that were acquired as part of anatomical, anthropological, or other scientific research or teaching endeavors. Individuals whose remains and tissues are in these collections generally did not have the opportunity to provide informed consent to have their remains and tissues preserved in perpetuity in the collections. Likewise, communities of origin did not generally provide permission for the acquisition, preservation, or study of these remains.

Sibling Organizations/Associations

While this proposal is written for the Executive Committee of the AABA and the membership of the AABA, some of the guidelines and proposals herein will impact and potentially draw on the resources of other professional organizations. These include, but are not limited to, organizations that have also formed task forces to address the management and study of human remains and tissues housed in legacy collections. Some of these sibling organizations include the American Anthropological Association, the American Association of Anthropological Genetics, the American Association for Anatomy, the American Association for the Advancement of Science (Section H), the Association of Black Anthropologists, the Society for American Archaeology, and the Society for Historical Archaeology.

Introduction 2

The collection and storage of human remains—bodies and tissues—for the purposes of study in anthropology, anatomy, and in the biological sciences has a long history that is in many ways inextricable from the origins of these disciplines (Hrdlička 1914; Hoyme 1953; Stocking Jr 1988; Biedler 1992; Campanacho et al. 2021; Robbins Schug et al. 2025). These remains were taken from archaeological and medical contexts, as well as opportunistically from the recently deceased, often without the permission or knowledge of the communities or individuals to whom they belonged. As reviewed elsewhere (Cornwall et al. 2024), informed consent for the collection of human remains was uncommon until the latter half of the twentieth century. Thus, the legacy collections of human remains that are held in museums, universities, and other institutions present a series of ethical concerns in a modern context. This is a context wherein researchers seek unambiguous policies and practices that account for the wishes of the deceased or, in the absence of this knowledge, the preferences and views of their contemporary communities, families, and descendant communities.

Ethical considerations thus underlie the framework for expected practices in the collection, storage, and disposition of human remains. Essentially, ethical guidelines seek respect for the dead and their communities (Wilkinson 2014). Furthermore, as advocated by the Recommendations for the Management of Legacy Anatomical Collections by the American Association for Anatomy Legacy Anatomical Collections Task Force (Cornwall et al. 2024), these considerations may be best informed through the principles of bioethics (Beauchamp and Childress 2019). Three principles are especially relevant: beneficence to society, justice and equitable distribution of burden, and the minimization of harm, especially with respect to the deceased and their communities. Any policies adopted by professional associations considering a desire to be ethical, then, must consider and seek to balance these principles. Moreover, policies need to provide flexibility in structuring the management of legacy collections while being engaged with the fundamental pillars of bioethics.

The Task Force members have used this framework as we have debated and developed a proposed set of guidelines and practices for addressing the burgeoning concern among anthropologists about legacy collections. This report presents recommendations to assist both the leadership of the American Association of Biological Anthropologists (AABA) and its members in operationalizing ethical practices in the management and scientific study of human remains and tissues that are part of legacy collections. The purpose of this proposal is to provide (1) a general framework for the management and oversight of these collections with respect to descendant communities, communities of origin, and communities of care, and (2) options for how the AABA and its sibling organizations can create mechanisms for regulation and resources for best practices, with particular emphasis on the stewardship of African American remains.

The following document is intended to provide guidance and suggestions for future steps in light of current knowledge. We acknowledge that, much as changing circumstances led to the creation of our Task Force, additional events in the future will be cause for continued examination and revision to both guiding principles and oversight. Our ensuing recommendations, therefore, incorporate flexibility and we include advice about the aspects of these recommendations that the AABA Executive Committee should plan to regularly review, revise, and amend when necessary.

Formation and Original Plan for the Task Force

Between May and July 2021, the AABA Executive Committee convened a Task Force on the Ethical Study of Human Remains. The charge to the Task Force was to develop a set of practices and recommendations for the study, management, and disposition of human remains in legacy collections. As then‐chair of the Committee for Community Partnership (formerly the Repatriation Committee), an ad hoc AABA committee that served as a liaison with the national NAGPRA office and members of the association, Dr. Benjamin Auerbach was contacted to chair the committee, also based on his years of experience working with human skeletal remains in collections globally as well as experience in collections management. Dr. Fatimah Jackson was shortly after asked to serve as a co‐chair based on her experience in the New York African Burial Ground, the Cobb Collection, and biomedicine.

The Task Force co‐chairs (Auerbach and Jackson) established early in the process of setting up the Task Force that developing guidelines for all legacy collections of human remains would be onerous given differences in laws and attitudes across the globe with respect to collections, as well as the breadth of communities and descendant communities that would need to be consulted in developing guidelines. Instead, we, the Task Force, decided to pursue the concerns raised with respect to one large group of communities: African Americans in the United States. This choice was made because many of the motivating instances cited as unethical—in that they failed to meet bioethical standards—involved African and African American remains in United States collections. Perhaps the most prominent of these was the unethical use in a publicly accessible online course of the remains of two victims of the Philadelphia MOVE bombing in 1985 (Kassutto 2021). The co‐chairs proposed that the work of the Task Force center on the African American community once the full Task Force was convened in August 2021, and all members assented to this approach.

Proposing to focus on developing guidelines with respect to African Americans in legacy collections, we planned a two‐stage approach. The first stage was to survey the membership of the AABA and a random sample of self‐identifying members of the African American community. We used the responses to these surveys as data for the second stage, which was to develop guidelines and then test through public meetings and small groups the ideas motivating these recommendations within both the AABA community and African American communities, the latter in the form of a large national set of focus groups. To complete the first stage, an initial Task Force was established, comprised of individuals who had varied expertise in ethics, identity, and the study of human remains: Drs. Michael Blakey, Shamsi Berry, Alan Goodman, Joseph Graves, Lance Gravlee, Ellen Lofaro, Ripan Malhi, and Charlotte Roberts. Once the initial surveys were conducted, the Task Force membership was expanded in April 2022 to bring in researchers with extensive experience working with BIPOC communities in their research: Drs. Jennifer Caldwell, Carter Clinton, Joseph Jones, Carmen Mosley, and Phoebe Stubblefield. These two groups of the Task Force membership, following the input provided through surveys and focus groups, worked to develop the recommendations we present here.

The overarching perspective of the Task Force is that our recommendations should result in efforts toward restorative justice for the descendant communities that have been affected by the creation of legacy collections. In this spirit, the Task Force regards the actions and reforms suggested in this document and those of sibling organization task forces to be an opportunity to establish and repair relationships with descendant communities. Namely, this is a chance for the biological anthropology community to reflect on the harms carried out in the absence of meaningful community partnerships that involve ongoing informed consent and collaboration with respect to decisions about the management of and research on remains in legacy collections. The changes that we advocate herein may be a challenge for our scientific community as well as for the responsive descendant communities. But we also regard that such challenges provide opportunities for growth and positive change, enhanced mutual understanding, and improved future collaborations.

This document follows on the Recommendations for the Management of Legacy Anatomical Collections by the American Association for Anatomy Legacy Anatomical Collections Task Force (Cornwall et al. 2024), the December 2023 Online Report of the Commission for the Ethical Treatment of Human Remains by the American Anthropological Association (Agarwal et al. 2024), and the Human Remains Task Force Report to the Secretary of the Smithsonian Institution (Smithsonian 2024). The leadership of the AABA Task Force has been in regular communication with the task forces of our sibling associations. To ensure that policies and best practices in the management and disposition of human remains in legacy collections (defined below) are universal among sibling associations, the AABA Task Force will recommend the adoption of the recommendations of the Anatomy Task Force as a basis for the guidelines we present herein, though our guidelines add to the foundation provided in the Legacy Anatomical Collections recommendations and, unlike the conclusions of our sibling organizations, we include insights specifically procured from the African American community on their preferences for the disposition of their ancestral remains. We highlight the importance of restorative justice, of obtaining the informed consent of descendant communities, and the need for mutual education between all individuals in a community of care for a legacy collection. These are elements not addressed in the Anatomy Task Force recommendations. Outside of these additions, the guiding principles and guidelines of the American Association for Anatomy align closely with those of the AABA Task Force and provide an essential structure on which we provide the implementation guidance here.

Recommended Steps of Action To Be Taken by Institutions and Individuals

As detailed in the Recommendations for the Management of Legacy Anatomical Collections by the American Association for Anatomy Legacy Anatomical Collections Task Force (hereafter “Anatomy Recommendations”) (Cornwall et al. 2024), the ethical guidelines for the management of legacy collections focus on community partnership as a desired outcome for the management of human remains collections. Their recommendations establish a sequence of steps toward this goal that the AABA Task Force likewise advocates are put into practice. In summary, these steps are:

Inventory. Individuals or committees should make an inventory of all human remains held in an institution's legacy collections using available records. Inventory consists not only of documenting what remains are present, but provenance of those individuals. Provenance data includes identifying data, such as the origins of the remains (who acquired them, whether they were donated, etc.) and, most importantly, the communities to which the remains may be traced. The community includes past as well as identifiable extant descendant communities and communities of origin. In completing an inventory, individuals should be mindful of associating individuals with existing communities with a goal of being able to engage in community partnership (see ‘Overarching and Guiding Paradigm’ below). We also acknowledge that identification of such communities may not be possible, and in these circumstances a community of care should be assembled (see 'Community Communication and Partnership Guidance' below). This initial list should not be seen as an opportunity to do further research before working with the related communities, and should be non‐destructive in nature (observational only). It should be noted that the definition of inventory in this document differs from the “itemized list” called for under the 2024 revisions to NAGPRA (Title 43 CFR 10§10.1 and Title 43 CFR 10§10.10).

Management and Care Plan. Upon completion of inventories, those who administer collections should plan to develop a management plan that outlines a procedure for the maintenance and care for human remains in the collection. The first step in developing these plans is reaching out to descendant communities and communities of care, as appropriate, and to set up a consultation with members of those communities. The intention of consultation is to ascertain the wishes of the community with respect to the care, study, and long‐term status of the remains, and to develop a partnership if it is desired. If no particular community can give or deny access, informed consent cannot be achieved. The order of who can give consent should be (1) lineal descendants, if not (2) descendant community, if not (3) communities of care. Decisions may be informed by members of the research community, but they cannot override the decisions of descendant communities or communities of care. See 'Recommended Requirements and Timelines for Inventory and Management of Legacy Collections' below.

Long‐term Care and Stewardship of Remains. The stewardship of remains should be determined in consultation with all interested parties. Should the descendant community or community of care determine that remains should be returned to the care of that community, arrangements should be made at the guidance of that community. An overarching goal of determining the long‐term status of remains is to minimize burden on communities of origin or descendant communities while avoiding unfunded mandates on institutions that currently hold human remains.

The Task Force provides further advice about these steps at the end of this document. This advice includes recommendations about timelines for completion of inventories, the components of inventories, and roadmaps for establishing contact with communities and maintaining community partnerships. We also suggest measures for ensuring compliance with these recommendations and guidelines for use of collections while the inventory process is underway.

Overarching and Guiding Paradigm

In both the Preliminary Report (Auerbach and Jackson 2022) and in public forums, the AABA Task Force on the Ethical Study of Human Remains has advocated for community involvement and partnership to become the standard for researchers who wish to study human remains. Under this paradigm, researchers are encouraged to identify communities of origin and communities of care. The purpose of this identification is to recognize the agency of these communities in deciding what types of research are desired or acceptable, if any, that make use of the remains of individuals who did not or could not provide explicit consent for the use of their remains or tissues in anthropological or biological study.

We have focused on community partnership and consultation as our main recommendation in response to survey results of both members of the African American community and the membership of the AABA. These results were reported in the Preliminary Report of 18 November 2022. Furthermore, the outcomes of a follow‐up survey of over 2000 members of the nationwide African American community conducted from late 2023 to early 2025 were elemental in the formation of our recommendations here. In both the initial survey and later expanded survey, African Americans interviewed in focus groups stated their concern about the scientific community's apparent lack of self‐policing with respect to the stewardship and ethical treatment of human remains. However, African Americans also expressed a collective desire to engage with members of the scientific community in making decisions about those human remains. Members of the AABA community who completed the online survey expressed a majority interest in communicating with and forming partnerships, when desired, with communities of origin and descendant communities. In both the scientific and African American communities, most members who supported community engagement and partnership also stated that they had no guidelines or roadmaps for such interaction.

In response to these findings, the AABA Task Force set out to provide examples of how to perform community partnered research by hosting sessions where multiple members of the association who are already performing community‐engaged and community‐led research presented their approaches. These included the Presidential Plenary panels at the 2022 and 2023 AABA Annual Meetings, an online Q&A session in November 2022, and a listening session at the 2023 AABA Annual Meeting. Through these sessions, individuals shared their experiences to help others learn about useful approaches to engaging with communities when those communities wish to participate. We recommend that the AABA create resources using the experiences of members engaged in community partnered research to model ways in which other researchers can establish connections with communities, as well as a set of general best practices for engaging with communities of origin. We provide recommendations for these practices and highlight them through the community partnerships engaged by members of the Task Force.

We also recommend that individuals read the Anatomy Recommendations in full, as they provide important background and context for our own deliberations as biological anthropologists. In summary, the Anatomy Recommendations emphasize the importance of cultural awareness and transparency in guiding both ethical and legal management of legacy collections. The Anatomy Task Force has further recommended that institutions develop oversight committees that include participation from communities of origin, and that these committees develop protocols to manage the use, storage, and long‐term status of legacy collections. As noted in the online survey of the AABA community in the spring of 2022, however, the implementation of this guidance for institutional oversight committees is a fundamental challenge. We discuss this in more detail in the next three sections and provide recommended solutions in the ‘Mechanisms to Aid Management and Provide Oversight of Legacy Collections’ section.

Before discussing these challenges, we should also note that the AABA Task Force recommends that members of all sibling associations, including the AABA, adopt a default position that no research need occur without the explicit consent of relevant descendant communities or communities of care. This is in opposition to what often has been a controlling paradigm in anthropology and biology, which is that a study should be undertaken regardless of community support, partnership, or interests so long as there is support from the scientific community. This is a major change in the approach to research, but it has important advantages that may strengthen studies and enhance their validity and reach; descendant communities may have unique knowledge that enhances research questions, and consultation with those communities before research provides opportunities for members of the AABA to calibrate analyses to incorporate and address the interests of descendant communities or communities of care. From an ethical standpoint, as expressed in detail in the Anatomy Recommendations, researchers are strongly encouraged to seek input from descendant communities, communities of origin, or communities of care (in order of importance) before engaging in research to determine what those communities prefer with respect to the long‐term status and study of human remains. The AABA Task Force recognizes that this, too, presents a significant logistical concern to the scientific community that complicates existing standards of research but that, in the larger perspective, improves the power and cultural relevance of research.

Research Among the Descendant African American Community

In the United States, African American communities can be found in virtually every state and vary significantly in size, composition, and culture. Some are urban, some rural; some are affluent, others impoverished. Within the broad categories, there are further subdivisions based on factors such as religion, ethnicity, and immigration history.

As introduced above, the Task Force decided early in its activities to focus on the attitudes, concerns, and input from communities that constitute the broader descendant African American population in the United States. To obtain information from such a vast and heterogeneous set of communities the Task Force decided that the long‐term goal for the work under our charge would be a national survey conducted through small discussion groups. Before we could conduct this national survey, we sought input from a smaller sample of African Americans.

Preliminary Survey of the African American Community in 2022

The final questions for the preliminary survey were selected by members of the Task Force to gauge the awareness of African Americans who were outside of the academy, especially the fields of anthropology and biology, of the concerns that motivated the creation of the AABA Task Force. Moreover, the survey sought to understand preferences of individuals who belong to the descendant community of the individuals whose remains are held in collections. Given the opportunity, would they want to be involved in determining the study and long‐term status of human remains? What guidelines would they want in place with respect to the study, storage, and potential return of those individuals whose remains are held by institutions? The Task Force sought to ask questions that addressed these topics without assuming familiarity with the situation or the degree of involvement desired by individuals who were surveyed.

In early 2022, Dr. Jackson and students trained in basic survey methods opportunistically assessed 538 self‐identified African American adults in the greater Washington, DC area. In this preliminary survey, these individuals were systematically asked five main questions:

Are you aware that appropriated African American remains are in the possession of U.S. university labs and museums?

Results of this initial survey were reported in the Preliminary Report to the AABA Executive Committee (Auerbach and Jackson 2022). As reported therein, the African American responses were heterogeneous, with respondents showing general wariness about the trustworthiness of scientists to be responsible stewards of human remains as well as a focus on the importance of consent by communities of origin and descent (as we define them here). While most of these initial respondents did not advocate for the return of remains, they did advocate for more control over the access to remains with descendant community input and self‐policing within the scientific community, both from professional associations and through funding agencies (like the National Institutes of Health). Respondents emphasized the lack of community voices and agency in making decisions about the study, collection, and long‐term status of the remains of African American individuals. They wanted minoritized groups to be better informed and their voices to be heard. A sample of these responses is provided in Appendix I.

The Task Force examined these responses against the experiences of some of our members who had been pursuing community‐engaged and community‐participatory research years prior to the formation of the Task Force. As noted above, Drs. Jackson and Blakey worked with the African American community in the excavation and study of the New York African Burial Ground. Dr. Clinton has likewise worked with grave soils from the burial ground. Dr. Jones has served as both a researcher and community member in the East Marshall Street Well Project at Virginia Commonwealth University, where remains of African Americans were found in an abandoned well on the Medical College of Virginia campus. Dr. Stubblefield has been involved in the excavations of sites and burials associated with the 1921 Tulsa Race Massacres. Dr. Caldwell has worked with the Gullah‐Geechee peoples of Carolina coastal regions to study identity and genetics. In these varied contexts, African American communities echoed many of the concerns raised by the initial survey respondents, and in all these contexts community participation was fundamental in making decisions about topics of study and the long‐term status of remains. This further informed the direction of the Task Force in the development of these guidelines and a general roadmap for community engaged research.

A concern about the initial survey of the African American community is that it was conducted within one small region of the United States and thus may reflect the attitudes of communities in that region. Thus, while the Task Force worked on developing recommendations and guidelines, Dr. Jackson guided an expanded survey of members of the African American community from locations across the United States to determine the universality of what was learned from the initial survey. We report a summary of the outcomes of these surveys here.

Expanded National Survey of Descendant African Community From 2023 to 2025: Methods

As there are numerous African American communities across the United States, each with its own unique characteristics, dynamics, organizational structure, and historical specifics, Dr. Jackson used the protocol of ethnogenetic layering (Jackson 2008; Caldwell and Jackson 2021) to identify and study specific regional micro‐ethnic groupings of legacy African American peoples. A standardized protocol was developed by Dr. Jackson and, after training 52 graduate and undergraduate students at Howard University in Focus Group Research management, these students returned to their hometowns to conduct the focus groups. From late 2023 through 2024, and until early 2025, these students used opportunistic sampling to organize focus groups of 5–7 adult participants at 14 different locations throughout the United States. All respondents were over 18 years of age and respondents agreed to give their comments anonymously. Participants were not financially compensated for their participation in the study. All data was collected by Howard University graduate and undergraduate students during their winter and spring vacations in their hometown environments.

Two thousand seven hundred sixteen self‐identified African American individuals were included in the survey. Focus groups were initiated across the United States in 35 states. Both urban and rural communities were studied, and males and females were represented in equivalent proportions. Expanded National Survey of Descendant African Community From 2023 to 2025: Results

In the extensive surveys of the African American community, it became obvious that the community is aware of and has cogent opinions on many of the important issues surrounding the disposition of their ancestors' remains. African American opinions extend to contemporary as well as historical samples. Some of the explicit ethnographic comments are presented below with respect to core central questions and key insights of this study.

Central Question 1: How should an adequate inventory be made and maintained by scientists working on historic human materials?

Key Insight : Biological anthropologists should follow strict ethical guidelines and protocols when taking an inventory of African American skeletal and other (biological) remains. Once we explained who biological anthropologists are and what we do as natural scientists, participants expressed an expectation that we had rules that we followed in our custodianship of African American skeletal and other biological materials. Considerations About Demographic Characteristics of African American Participants

The United States has a large African American population of over 46,000,000 individuals with distinctive communities of significance in many cities. Many of these cities have been important centers for African American culture and civil rights activism. The selection of locations within which to conduct our focus groups was ultimately opportunistic and depended upon the willingness and abilities of student survey leaders to conduct focus groups during their vacation breaks from university training. Ultimately, the team collected complete information from 2716 self‐identified African American adults. Dr. Jackson and her team tried to collect a representative sample of the African American community so that the Task Force's insights would reflect the regional variation that exists in African American perspectives and viewpoints. We endeavored to document the voices of both men and women in rural, urban, and suburban communities from 18 to 89 years of age. Furthermore, data collection focused on capturing the viewpoints of rural African Americans since these areas often have unique socioeconomic, historical, and cultural characteristics. Individuals from these areas often make up the bulk of inadequately identified African American skeletal remains in university and museum collections (de la Cova 2019; Williams and Ross 2022). Missing from the roster of rural areas surveyed were participants from South Carolina, North Carolina, and Virginia. The geographical sites we surveyed tended to disproportionately represent urban areas with large African American populations. Missing from our list, however, was input from the sizable African American communities in Philadelphia, PA, Jackson, MS, Richmond, VA, and Gary, IN, though perspectives from Washington, DC, and areas of Virginia and Maryland near Washington DC were likely reflected in the preliminary survey from 2022. We also made a conscious effort to include individuals from varied economic and educational backgrounds in our focus groups.

Discussion: Most Salient Concerns of the African American Descendant Community

The establishment and maintenance of trust between descendant communities and researchers is a fundamental theme that emerges from the survey as well as from the experiences of Task Force members who have engaged in partnered research. Trust comes in many forms, from clear communication about the goals and outcomes of research to the continual acknowledgement of agency to community of origin or descendant community members. Participants showed in their responses that recognition of their wishes with respect to stewardship of remains as the final decision is essential in building trust and being ethical. The wishes of communities should be recognized and discussed with transparency, and where there may be conflict with the goals of researchers, dialogues should be undertaken with an understanding that there are differences in implicit and explicit power in the relationship between descendants and those who manage legacy collections, excavate sites, or regulate access to remains. We see these themes emerge in the responses to each of the questions asked in the survey, as we summarize below.

How Should an Adequate Inventory Be Made and Maintained by Scientists Working on Historic Human Materials?

Participants articulated repeatedly that biological anthropologists should follow strict ethical guidelines and protocols when taking an inventory of African American skeletal and other remains. This includes the following steps:

Obtaining proper permission and transparent, informed consent from relevant authorities, such as descendant communities, before beginning any research;

Obtaining proper permission and transparent, informed consent from relevant authorities, such as descendant communities, before beginning any research;

Using standardized methods to systematically document and inventory the skeletal remains;

Using standardized methods to systematically document and inventory the skeletal remains;

Maintaining detailed records of each individual in the inventory, including information on age, sex, ancestry, and any cultural or historical context that may be relevant;

Maintaining detailed records of each individual in the inventory, including information on age, sex, ancestry, and any cultural or historical context that may be relevant;

Respecting the cultural and religious beliefs of the descendant communities and handling the remains with care and sensitivity;

Respecting the cultural and religious beliefs of the descendant communities and handling the remains with care and sensitivity;

Communicating findings and conclusions transparently and respectfully to relevant stakeholders, including descendant communities and academic colleagues.

Communicating findings and conclusions transparently and respectfully to relevant stakeholders, including descendant communities and academic colleagues.

Ultimately, the goal of taking an inventory of African American skeletal and other remains is to promote respect, understanding, and collaboration between biological anthropologists and descendant communities while also contributing to a broader agenda of scientific knowledge and research.

How Should Scientists Communicate With Descendant Communities About the Analysis of Their Ancestors' Remains?

When communicating with descendant communities about the analysis of their ancestors' remains, participants indicated that scientists should follow a culturally sensitive and respectful approach. Participants provided key guidelines for effective communication, from their perspectives. Most respondents suggested that scientists should take the time to establish trust with descendant communities through open and transparent communication; the scientists must establish trust and build relationships. Building respectful relationships is essential for successful collaboration. It is essential that researchers involve descendant communities in decision‐making, and so scientists should actively involve descendant communities in the decision‐making process regarding the analysis of their ancestors' remains. This includes seeking African American community input on research goals, methodologies, and ethical considerations. Scientists must use accessible language, communicating their findings with terminology that is clear and free of technical jargon. Each of the communities we contacted indicated their desires to be a part of the research process and to understand what is being done to their ancestors and why. This ensures that information is easily understood by members of the descendant community. Scientists must also acknowledge the centrality of cultural perspectives and beliefs, respecting and acknowledging the breadth of African American cultural perspectives and beliefs among members of the descendant communities. This includes recognizing the spiritual, emotional, and historical significance of their ancestors' remains.

As part of the research protocol, scientists should offer opportunities for feedback and discussion. Scientists should provide opportunities for descendant communities to ask questions and participate in conversations about the analysis and significance of their ancestors' remains. This two‐way dialogue fosters mutual understanding and collaboration. At the same time, scientists must prioritize confidentiality and privacy of descendant communities when discussing sensitive information related to the analysis of ancestors' remains. Clear protocols should be in place to ensure data protection and respect for individuals' rights. Given the adverse history African Americans have had with the American state, many participants expressed anxiety about the potential for harmful results from the analysis of their ancestors' biological materials. By following culturally relevant ethical guidelines, scientists can effectively communicate with descendant communities about the analysis of their ancestors' remains in a respectful and collaborative manner. This approach fosters understanding, trust, and mutual respect between researchers and descendant communities.

Who Should Control the Access to, Storage of, and Analysis of African American Ancestral Remains?

Balancing the control of ancestral remains between scientists and descendant communities involves a delicate and complex process that requires collaboration, communication, and respect from all parties involved. Participants indicated some key considerations for achieving a balanced approach. Scientists and representatives of descendant communities should engage in shared decision‐making processes regarding the handling, analysis, and repatriation of ancestral remains. Both parties should have equal opportunity to provide input and have their voices heard in the decision‐making process. For this to be successful, there must be honesty and transparent communication to maintain trust and address any preexisting distrust. Both parties should communicate openly and respectfully about their perspectives, concerns, and expectations regarding the control of ancestral remains. Scientists should respect the cultural beliefs, practices, and values of descendant communities when handling ancestral remains. This includes acknowledging and honoring spiritual, religious, and cultural protocols related to the treatment and repatriation of remains. The only means for ensuring this is adherence to established ethical guidelines and protocols when working with ancestral remains. This includes following legal requirements, research standards, and institutional policies that govern the treatment and analysis of human remains, in addition to the development of mutually agreed‐upon protocols and standards.

Collaboration and partnership between scientists and descendant communities can help foster mutual respect, understanding, and trust. By working together as equal partners, both parties can ensure that the control of ancestral remains is shared in a fair and equitable manner. In cases where descendant communities wish to repatriate ancestral remains for reburial, scientists should support and facilitate this process. Repatriation is an important step in recognizing the rights and wishes of descendant communities to control the treatment and stewardship of their ancestors' remains. By following these guidelines and principles, scientists and descendant communities can work together to achieve a balanced and respectful approach to the control of ancestral remains. This collaborative effort promotes mutual understanding, trust, and cooperation between all parties involved.

What Safeguards Can Be Put in Place to Prevent the Scientific Exploitation of African American Biological Remains?

To prevent scientific exploitation of African American biological remains, several safeguards should be put in place according to survey participants. These safeguards include, as a start, informed consent; prior informed consent should be obtained from descendant communities or relevant stakeholders before any research or analysis is conducted on African American biological remains. Another safeguard is ethical review. Desire was expressed for research involving African American biological remains to undergo ethical review to ensure that the research is conducted in a responsible and respectful manner.

A common theme was systematic collaboration with descendant communities. Scientists should actively engage and collaborate with descendant communities in all stages of research involving African American biological remains. This includes seeking input on research design, methods, and interpretation of results. Participants emphasized the importance of research cultural sensitivity and respect. Researchers should demonstrate cultural sensitivity and respect for the beliefs, values, and traditions of descendant communities when working with African American biological remains. This includes acknowledging the historical context of these remains and the potential for trauma or harm associated with their study. A number of participants listed the importance of data protection and confidentiality. Measures should be in place to protect the privacy and confidentiality of data obtained from African American biological remains. Researchers should ensure that data is handled securely and in compliance with relevant data protection regulations. Another safeguard that was frequently mentioned in focus groups was that of ultimate repatriation and reburial. If requested by descendant communities, African American biological remains should be repatriated and respectfully reburied in accordance with local cultural practices and beliefs. Throughout our inquiries among African Americans, the need for appropriate education and outreach was stressed. Researchers should engage in educational outreach activities to raise awareness about the ethical and social implications of studying African American biological remains. This can help foster understanding and support within both the scientific and African American communities. By implementing these safeguards, scientists can help prevent the exploitation of African American biological remains and ensure that research is conducted in a responsible, ethical, and respectful manner. As emphasized above and throughout all responses, collaboration, transparency, and cultural sensitivity were highlighted as key principles that should guide research involving African American biological remains.

Concerns of the Biological Anthropology Community

With the documentation of concerns and guidance from a sample of the descendant African American community, we now turn our attention to the attitudes and concerns of the biological anthropology community. In 2022, the Task Force conducted an anonymous survey of the AABA membership that focused on the kinds of research conducted using human remains, the provenance of remains held in legacy collections, and attitudes about community partnership in researching human remains. Survey results were presented in an interim report published in November 2022. The raw data for the survey results are available in a CSV file attached to this report as a Supporting Material, along with an R script that contains metadata and analytical code. Because individuals included information that could be used for identification (e.g., what samples they studied) in their open written responses, these responses are not included with the Supporting Material in the interest of protecting individual privacy.

A total of 402 members of the AABA responded to the survey (roughly 20% to 25% of the total membership depending on year), the majority of whom were individuals currently in or retired from faculty positions (57.2%), followed by graduate students (24.4%), professionals in non‐academic or government positions (8.7%), and postdoctoral fellows (6.0%) (3.7% did not specify). Participants in the survey could select multiple fields of study. The majority engaged in research related to bioarchaeology (53.5%), skeletal biology (43.8%), human evolutionary studies (35.3%), forensic anthropology (27.9%), and human biology (22.1%). Of those surveyed, the majority also indicated that they have performed non‐destructive research of human skeletal remains, most of which were from archaeological or forensic contexts. Fewer than 20% of respondents have studied human skeletal remains identified as African American, and most of those who did identified the use of three collections: Terry (National Museum of Natural History), Hamann‐Todd (Cleveland Museum of Natural History), and The University of Tennessee's Donated Collection. We conclude that the survey respondents are a good reflection of the membership engaged with the study of human skeletal remains, with less representation by individuals who study nonhuman primates, human biology, or engage in anthropological genetics.

One concern raised within the Task Force is that a “silent” but nontrivial constituency of the AABA membership does not share the attitudes conveyed in survey responses (summarized below) and did not participate in the survey. Anecdotally, members of the Task Force have heard via conversation or email from fewer than ten members of the AABA who disagreed with the premise of the survey and chose not to complete it. While taking into account the breadth of opinions held by members of the AABA is important in deciding how to address the ethical stewardship of legacy collections, the Task Force members concluded that we could only rely on systematically collected data to understand concerns held by professional members within the AABA. For this reason, we focus on a summary of the results of the survey here.

Contents of Legacy Collections

In the 2022 AABA survey, a majority of respondents reported that their legacy collections of human remains were skeletal in nature. Of the 402 respondents, 35.3% (142) reported that they managed collections of unknown or undocumented provenance. In most of these cases (57%), remains of unknown provenance were used in teaching, though since the survey was conducted members of the AABA have reported to the Task Force about their efforts to remove remains of unknown provenance from the classroom when logistically possible. Almost all respondents to the survey noted that remains of unknown provenance were acquired through third‐party organizations (such as biological supply houses), or the records of acquisition have been lost.

A minority of respondents reported that their institution curates skeletal remains of African Americans (15.7%, or 63 respondents). As the survey was anonymous, it is not known how many institutions are represented by these 63 respondents. However, the members of the Task Force do not have reason to think, based on the survey, that African Americans are widely represented in legacy skeletal collections in North America. Respondents did note in open comments that the nature of the survey precluded other remains that may be identified as those from Black, Indigenous, or People of Color (non‐African American) individuals from being counted. Given the focus of the Task Force on addressing the stewardship of African American remains in establishing guidelines for the management of legacy collections, this was an intentional decision.

While the survey gave some indication of how human remains in legacy collections have been used and who they represent, we do not know what groups are represented within the various institutional collections managed by biological anthropologists. A clear concern that emerges from the survey is that missing provenance information is a nontrivial occurrence across legacy collections. In addition, while a majority of respondents reported that they either did (n = 63) or did not (n = 167) have African American skeletal remains in their collections, approximately 43% of respondents indicated that they did not know if they did or did not respond to the question. We have good reason to think that this is, in part, a product of a lack of standardized and systematic inventories for all legacy collections that include provenance.

Existing Procedures for Obtaining Permission to Study Remains and Community Partnership

In conducting the survey, the Task Force was interested in knowing how researchers currently seek permission to study human remains held in legacy collections. This was, in part, to gauge how common members of the AABA use community partnered approaches toward pursuing research. In more than 70% of cases, researchers have obtained permission solely from the institution that curates the legacy collection. This has been the standard by which researchers obtain permission to study human skeletal remains in the discipline.

In the survey results, between 20% and 40% of respondents who engaged in research using human skeletal remains have on one or more occasions obtained permission through consultation with descendant communities, including formal organizations and Native Nations, as well as from families or donors. Thus, while not the standard by which researchers obtain permission, some members of the association do seek consent from descendant communities when researching human remains with known provenance. Some of this is mandated through NAGPRA, though respondents indicated in open questions that they sought community input for remains of individuals who descended from groups other than Native Americans. These interactions most often took the form of correspondence with members of the community, either through mediators (e.g., Tribal Historic Preservation Officers, government representatives, or curators) or direct to community leaders. In some cases, researchers also gave presentations or held listening sessions with communities before research was conducted, after research was completed, or both. Co‐authorship or direct collaborations with community members was rare but does also occur (nine respondents noted they had directly collaborated with or co‐authored with members of the descendant community).

What researchers do with respect to community engagement and what their attitudes are about it contrast in the results from the AABA survey. Of the 402 respondents, 301 completed questions about attitudes using a Likert scale. Most overwhelmingly support the need to determine provenance (93.7%) and get consent from descendant communities (92%) before conducting research, finding these as essential or of moderate importance. Fewer researchers think that it is at least moderately important that descendant communities should be consulted in planning and interpretation of results (87.7% and 78%, respectively). The survey, then, indicates that there is a desire to engage with descendant communities among members of the AABA, though respondents noted that this desire is context‐dependent and should not be mandated universally.

There is therefore a disconnect between how members of the association would like to conduct their research with respect to descendant community engagement and how they currently are expected to proceed to conduct research. This was openly evident during the Task Force's 2023 AABA listening session, where participants were presented with scenarios in which human remains were to be studied and community partnership was an option. Participants indicated frustration in not having a roadmap in how to engage in community partnered research or clear guidance on what is the “appropriate” ethical approach to studying human remains in legacy collections. Participants expressed a desire to have a series of steps that should be taken with options of how to proceed given the context of their research.

Association of Remains With Descendant Communities

One of the prominent concerns raised in the open responses to the 2022 survey, and again in listening sessions and personal communication with the Task Force, has been the issue of associating human remains with living descendant communities. As we noted in the definitions above, descendant communities need not be associated by lineal or genetic association. Genetic association has the potential to be restrictive in cases where lineal descent cannot be determined or descendants are not extant, or, alternatively, expansive to many descendants (e.g., see Harney et al. 2023). The Task Force notes that genetic association may be used as one method of making associations between human remains and living groups but should not be the sole criterion or an exclusionary criterion against other sources of information, such as historical documentation, oral histories, and archaeological evidence.

Members of the AABA who responded to the survey noted that the method of identification for purposes of research may not correspond well with identifying descendant communities or communities of origin, especially within contexts where culturally associated contextual information or self‐identifying information was not recorded and may be subjective (such as archaeological contexts without culturally definable information). For example, the provenance of human remains in a legacy collection may be known, but the remains were recovered at a time when mortuary information or context was not also recorded or their association with remains has been lost. Another concern raised was ambiguity in the relationship of an ancient culture to living descendant communities, though the Task Force notes that such ambiguities can be resolved through consultation with a community of care.

Ultimately, the AABA survey and subsequent communication with the membership has demonstrated that while the practice of engagement with descendant communities is desired, there are important factors that encumber its practice. These include:

Uncertainty in the provenance of individuals in legacy collections;

Uncertainty in the provenance of individuals in legacy collections;

Concerns about how to link provenanced human remains to descendant communities or communities of origin; and

Concerns about how to link provenanced human remains to descendant communities or communities of origin; and

Best practices for conducting research with respect to the wishes of descendant communities

Best practices for conducting research with respect to the wishes of descendant communities

Members also noted that in many cases institutional collections, especially those not used in research but instead in teaching, are managed by teaching faculty or staff who do not have resources for tracking down provenance information, determining descendant community identity, or communicating with communities. Even in institutions with curators and collections managers, performing these activities requires more resources than are required for the maintenance of the collections. An important conclusion from these sentiments is that, even if a good set of guidelines is determined for ascertaining provenance and descendant community engagement, the means for operationalizing such guidelines may be limited. The implications and proposed solutions for these findings are presented below.

Challenges in Instituting Management Recommendations

From the African American surveys, we know that there is an expectation that the ethical stewardship of legacy collections of human remains from these communities will invest in collaboration, transparency, and cultural sensitivity. Members of the AABA likewise aspired to these ideals, as reflected in the association survey and subsequent discussions. As outlined above, though, members of the AABA have expressed concerns about two sets of challenges: (1) identifying and ethically interacting with descendant communities, and (2) having resources for the oversight and management of legacy collections. We unpack the nature of these concerns here, in brief, to provide a basis for the ensuing sections in which we present potential solutions that may be adopted by the AABA and its sibling organizations. The challenges of implementing the best practices emerging from the Anatomy Recommendations will be idiosyncratic by individual, institution, and collection. Thus, the discussion here is provided to illustrate general issues, which the Task Force seeks to resolve in the form of three options that create mechanisms for regulation and provide resources for instituting the procedures set out in the Recommended Steps above.

We can look to the solutions proposed by sibling organizations for guidance about how to address the first challenge: identifying descendant communities and engaging in ethical discourse about the use and stewardship of legacy remains. The Anatomy Recommendations present a solution through engagement with communities of care to establish ways to identify and reach out to descendant communities, or to work in their stead when descendant communities are unclear. In the Anatomy Recommendations, communities of care should include members who represent communities of origin or descendant communities for individuals whose remains are in a collection. For example, if a legacy collection in Fairfax County, Virginia, were to include remains of African Americans from other locations (e.g., other locations in Virginia) but do not have an identified descendant community (e.g., the descendant community no longer exists), then members of the Fairfax County African American community would be asked to participate as members of the community of care for those remains. By participating in the community of care, the Fairfax County African American community would be able to voice their concerns and preferences, ideally becoming involved as partners should researchers seek permission to study the remains.

On its merits, this is a reasonable recommendation that matches the spirit of community partnership that the AABA Task Force has promoted. It would also address the main concerns of the African American community as raised in the national survey. However, we must recognize some complications to this scenario:

We assume that provenance for the remains in the collection is unambiguous and well documented. The achievement of good provenance may involve archival research, which may not be available given restrictions on time and resources for individuals within the institution housing the remains. Nonetheless, individuals are encouraged to determine the origins of human remains, as accurate inventories are essential in the new guidance.

The Task Force encourages that such individuals be incorporated into institutional committees that oversee the communication with descendant communities.

Assuming the concerns above are addressed, there is no universal process for reaching out to and engaging with descendant communities. This is because the methods for facilitating contact and dialogue depend on the community and will occur in steps and at a pace determined by members of that community. But engagement with descendant communities can be achieved and should always be the goal for ethical stewardship of collections. In summary, we here highlight the concerns raised in (1) identifying the provenance of human remains, (2) ascertaining the community or communities of origin that would be approached about the use, management, and disposition of those remains, and (3) developing dialogues with and working toward partnerships with those communities. Though multiple examples exist for how individuals within the anthropological community have established and developed relationships with communities of origin, these are particular to each circumstance (see vignettes presented by Drs. Caldwell and Clinton in Appendix II). While the Task Force provides a generalized roadmap, the burden remains with individuals and institutions to develop these relationships and navigate the cultural intricacies involved.

This relates further to the complication of resource availability. Resources are necessary to determine provenance and to inventory collections of human remains and tissues, establish oversight on their usage (ideally in partnership with descendant communities), and manage collections. We note that resources include, but are not limited to, time for individuals to perform these duties, adequate training for individuals to complete these practices, and financial support to make them possible. Training may be the most easily addressed, as workshops held by the AABA and sibling organizations, both in person at conferences and perhaps online, could help individuals and institutions understand the measures necessary for executing the practices outlined herein and in the Anatomy Recommendations. Further training would be necessary for individuals to learn how to approach descendant communities and to develop partnerships with their members. This assumes that individuals have adequate time, especially in academic positions, to carry out the training as well as the ensuing duties. Based on conversations with members of the AABA community, lack of training and time have been reasons why some collection managers have elected to indefinitely suspend access to their collections by researchers and interested parties from potential communities of care or origin. Concomitant with this is the unequal distribution of financial and institutional support of collection managers across universities and museums, where some places have the ability to hire individuals trained in collections management and to staff positions for inventory and maintenance, while in other institutions such duties ostensibly fall to the responsibilities of a single individual without additional financial or other compensation.

Despite these difficulties—or rather, because of them—the Task Force strongly encourages the AABA Executive Committee to commit to helping members of the AABA and sibling associations address these difficulties so that the practices outlined in the Anatomy Recommendations may be implemented widely. It is the opinion of the Task Force that reliance on individual effort will result in a patchwork of implementation, where some institutions have well‐developed communities of care and oversight of collections while others struggle to comply with maintaining inventories. Such disparities would be the product of unequal resource allocation as outlined above; based on the AABA membership survey results provided in the Task Force Preliminary Report (Auerbach and Jackson 2022), we assume that most members of the association wish to adopt practices in line with those we have summarized. Without staffing, training, and time, however, members of the scientific community will struggle to achieve the goals of more ethical management, study, and decisions about the long‐term status of human remains in legacy collections.

Mechanisms to Aid Management and Provide Oversight of Legacy Collections

To address these challenges, the Task Force has developed three potential mechanisms that could lead to minimizing patchwork implementation of our recommendations. We present these ideas in detail below—the Committee, the Office, and the Agency—starting with the option that requires the fewest resources: the Committee. All three provide opportunities to equalize resources among institutions and normalize processes for establishing and supporting communities of care. We also note at the outset that these three options are not mutually exclusive, and with some minor changes to reduce duplicate responsibilities or efforts, could work together.

Option 1: Standing Oversight Committee

This option constitutes the lowest cost commitment of the AABA and sibling organizations. The AABA would establish a standing committee to provide oversight and guidance to individuals and institutions. This committee would be tasked with organizing and leading regular workshops and other forms of training for members of the association in archival research, methods for determining provenance, community partnership and outreach, and human remains and tissue inventory. The committee would also serve as a central point of communication for members of the association to share concerns, challenges, and information about legacy collections. In this role, the committee would have an overarching perspective about how legacy collection inventory, management, and long‐term status is occurring within the association, and thus be able to identify common problems and solutions, sharing these as necessary to help all members better achieve legacy collection stewardship. In addition, ethical concerns and poor stewardship of collections could be brought to the attention of this committee, which would investigate and report unethical behavior to the Executive Committee of the AABA. (Without a change in bylaws, however, we note there is no mechanism by which bad actors can be censured or policed by the AABA.) Under ideal circumstances, sibling associations would also form such committees, which could then coordinate their efforts. We note that the existing Committee for Community Partnership could be redeveloped to perform the role of this proposed committee.

Structure

We envision this to be a standing committee of the AABA, with a chair and multiple members. The members of the committee should include both current members of the AABA as well as community of care members who have worked with biological anthropologists in partnered projects. Depending on the nature of the commitment, the AABA may want to consider the chair of this committee to be an elected office, which would be part of the Executive Committee. Given the amount of oversight, communication, and training to be provided by this committee, it would need subcommittee chairs to oversee its activities.

Organizational Commitment

As noted in the general description of this option, this committee would require the fewest resources by the AABA. It does ask the association to create and staff a standing committee indefinitely and to provide resource support for training and communication. If sibling organizations choose to create their own standing committees, there would be an incentive to establish regular communication between committee chairs to share information and resources.

Resources and Personnel

To staff this committee would require at least a dozen individuals, which would provide redundancy and enough individuals to carry the training and communication burden charged to the committee without overwhelming its membership. All personnel would need to be trained in the processes outlined in these recommendations and the Anatomy Recommendations, and ideally would have experience in community partnered research and public outreach. As suggested in ‘Structure’ above, the committee may need subcommittee chairs, namely for training and for oversight.

Financial Implications

The financial burden of this committee would be limited to resources necessary to provide training for members at regular intervals throughout the year. It would be further beneficial if this committee could oversee the selection of small grants to individuals or institutions to offset the costs of resources associated with inventory, archival research, or community outreach. Such resources could potentially include funding for temporary hires, especially at institutions that do not have resources to support their employment.

Potential Benefits

This committee would benefit the association by providing a structure for regular, centralized, and standardized training in all aspects of legacy collection stewardship. Moreover, the committee would serve as a central point of communication among members, and in this role would be able to identify and address common concerns, questions, and procedural problems. Committee members would give members counsel for developing communication with descendant communities and community partnerships, provided by roadmaps that could be applied across the association. Finally, the committee would be a central location for individuals to report bad actors or non‐compliant institutions and could potentially investigate these reports. Investigation by the Committee would expose the AABA to legal liability, and so investigation may not be an activity taken by the Committee but instead be delegated to the Office or Agency (see below).

Potential Drawbacks

While this committee would be a significant improvement in training, centralization, and oversight, it would have limited authority and thus restricted reach. There is no mechanism by which to require members of the AABA to complete training, even when those members are stewards of legacy collections. Thus, it is possible only a limited number of individuals will avail themselves of the training necessary to execute the best practices outlined above and in the Anatomy Recommendations.

Finding enough members of the association who have adequate experience and training to participate in the committee, and who have time and interest to be on the committee, may be a challenge. This may be overcome by making the chair of the committee an elected position based on experience (this is an option, not a recommendation) to give that individual authority to recruit to the committee and to provide intensive training to any members who are interested in serving regardless of personal experience. Ideally, as community partnerships and communities of care become more common, these staffing issues may decrease over time.

Interactions with the committee will be voluntary, and so what the members of the committee learn about concerns and problems from the membership may reflect only a small portion of the AABA community. Our experience on the Task Force has been that AABA members have only approached us when they want to influence policy or react to actions taken by the Task Force. Members are generally reticent to police each other, let alone report violations. Often, members also do not seek help or resources because they are unaware of their availability or do not have the time or means to seek the aid. Though these are generalizations, they reflect the fact that no standing committee can compulsorily ask members to engage in its activities.

Option 2: An Inter‐Association Organizational Office

This option is an intermediate mechanism, requiring cooperation among associations but also distributing responsibility, resources, and experience so no one organization would be tasked with training and oversight of members in the stewardship of legacy collections. In many respects this option is similar to the standing committee outlined in Option 1, with additional duties and abilities given the additional resources and reach associated with an Inter‐Association Office. This Office would oversee and organize training for individuals who are members of the participating associations, provide guidance and roadmaps for community partnership, and give resources to groups or individuals to help them meet best practices for managing legacy collections.

There are some notable additional resources and duties the members of the Office would be equipped to perform. As an inter‐organizational entity, the Office would potentially have more resources to grant to individuals for offsetting costs associated with inventory and community outreach. It may likewise be bestowed more authority to police individuals and institutions that fail to comply with best practices. By drawing on the expertise of individuals across organizations, the Office would have more flexibility and perhaps tax individual members of the Office less by distributing duties than is possible with the Committee in Option 1. We discuss these details below.

Structure

The Inter‐Organizational Office would be structured to have either a chair or set of co‐chairs. If a single chair were chosen, the position could rotate between participating sibling organizations. Co‐chairs may be selected from different organizations. Like the Committee in Option 1, the chair/co‐chairs would oversee the activities of the Office. Main duties for the Office would be similar to those of the Committee: training of individuals and institutions in how to provenance and complete inventories; maintenance of a central repository of inventories and information about collections; and oversight of community partnerships and outreach to descendant communities. Given the breadth of disciplines and institutions that would benefit from the activities of the Office, subcommittees and subcommittee chairs would be necessary to oversee aspects of training, oversight, and aid for community partnerships. Importantly, the Office would also serve as an advocate for best practices and would be an entity that individuals could cite as a source of information and guidance to institutions (museums and universities).

Depending on organizational commitment and support, the Office would also have an option to act as an inter‐organizational space for keeping track of concerns and difficulties encountered by institutions and communities of care/origin. That is, the Office option could be given authority to help monitor the activities of members of participating organizations, addressing a common concern voiced by members of the African American community in the Task Force surveys. Such an Office would be given the ability to hear and respond to complaints submitted by communities and by individuals, especially when institutions may not have structures in place internally to adjudicate these concerns (or, if the structures are present, do not respond to complaints). This would require oversight by the Executive Boards of participating organizations and may require some restructuring to support the Office's actions. Whether the Office has authority to respond to complaints or would pass them along with recommendations to the leadership of organizations would need to be determined.

Finally, the Office could be a centralized entity for providing resources for individuals and institutions that lack resources for collection inventory, management, and community partnership. Pooling resources from multiple sibling organizations would allow for the Office to offer small competitive grants to offset costs associated with these activities. The Office would have a breadth of individuals with experience and expertise in community partnership, and would be available to help individuals navigate the roadmap for establishing these partnerships. By having a national or international scope, the members of the Office would be equipped to observe commonalities among groups or situations that might make them applicable to different institutions and individuals.

Organizational Commitment

The Office would require sibling organizations to enter a partnership for an indefinite period of time and a high degree of inter‐organizational cooperation. Sibling organizations would need to provide more individuals and financial resources to make the Office work efficiently. It may be necessary to hire one staff member to oversee correspondence and to direct information to the relevant parties within the Office (see personnel below). Creating such an inter‐organizational entity would likewise have the potential need to revise bylaws, so its establishment may take a couple of years.

Resources and Personnel

Whether the Office has a chair or co‐chairs, it will require timely and effective communication both internally and with organizational leadership and members. To this end, it would be ideal for the Office to have at least one full‐time staff position for a manager, a position that would receive all correspondence and direct it to the appropriate parties. These parties would be subcommittees within the Office, specializing in training, outreach, oversight, granting, descendant community communication, and community partnership. In addition, the Office would require equal participation by members across sibling organizations. This would mean that each organization would have a number of seats within the Office, and that these positions would need to be filled through appointment, election, or both. Subcommittees would, likewise, be comprised of a mixture of individuals from multiple organizations. This distributes duties and ensures that no one organization has disproportionate authority or commitment in the Office.

Financial Implications

The financial commitment would be equally or proportionally divided among participating sibling organizations, depending on what those organizations determine to be the more fair option. As noted above, organizations would be potentially committed to supporting one staff position (a manager), funds for competitive need‐based grants (used for the same purposes as the Committee in Option 1), and the development and maintenance of an inter‐organizational website.

Potential Benefits

Compared with an AABA standing committee, the Office would both afford more reach across members and institutions represented within sibling organizations while also minimizing redundant efforts or committees among those organizations. The Office would provide a centralized entity for communication, training, and policing among the organizations. This also would minimize potential confusion among scientists and members of communities of care about who to consult, as well as give a centralized organization for communities of origin to engage beyond individuals or institutions. (This assumes that these communities would want to contact such an organization. The national survey of descendant African American communities indicates that many of these would like to participate with such an organization, but we cannot generalize this to all descendant communities, and so this can only be determined by each case).

If given authority, the Office would have the additional benefit of policing best practices, as well as being the potential entity for updating those guidelines based on experience without having to convene new task forces within sibling organizations. Oversight in the Anatomy Recommendations is left to the discretion of leadership of the American Association for Anatomy and its sibling organizations separately, which could lead to inconsistent measures and methods for enforcing the guidelines. By having a single inter‐organizational Office, these idiosyncratic responses would be avoided. Having a centralized Office also makes sure that all individuals and institutions abide by the same rules and practices.

Potential Drawbacks

The Office will have many of the same drawbacks detailed in Option 1. Having enough trained members to participate in the Office as committee members will present possible staffing issues, at least early in the process. With 2 years of planning and organization, however, this may afford time to identify and train individuals to take the initial roles in the Office. Beyond staffing, the same concern about voluntary interactions with the Office among organizational members and communities of origin will continue to be a concern. Creating a culture of expectations among members and normalizing interactions among descendant communities and professional organizations will occur if undertaken purposefully over time, but this remains a central concern with both Options 1 and 2.

As with any added complexity, creating an inter‐organizational entity invites logistical and political elements. To develop this Office would require a strong, shared vision among organizations and their memberships. While inter‐organizational cooperation already occurs through joint sessions at conferences, shared awards, and overlapping memberships, creating a shared office that would be staffed by members of sibling associations is largely unprecedented. For this reason, unknown pitfalls and issues will undoubtedly arise in the creation and maintenance of the Office. Nonetheless, because of its many benefits, we strongly advocate that the AABA Executive Committee and membership pursue this option in the near term.

Option 3: A National Agency for the Stewardship of Legacy Human Remain Collections

The Agency model would encompass all the duties and operations outlined for the Office (and therefore also the Committee) options outlined above, with two major additional sets of duties: (1) active “desks” for staff who specialize in outreach and partnerships with specific descendant communities; and (2) enforced oversight and implementation of the guidelines for the inventory, management, use, and disposition of legacy collections. This is the most ambitious and longest‐term option for creating a mechanism to support and oversee the implementation of our recommendations and practices for the use and stewardship of legacy collections of human remains. It calls for the creation of a semi‐independent entity, a National Agency, which would greatly expand on the duties and support offered by the Committee (Option 1) and Office (Option 2) ideas. It would have a Board of Directors on which the leaders of the sibling organizations and their Legacy Collection Committee chairs would serve and provide oversight.

Given the complexity of developing an Agency, we on the Task Force regard this to be a long‐term option that would follow either the Committee or the Office models. We recommend that the Agency start on a limited scope, with only a few regions and/or communities of origin represented among the “desks” (described in more detail below under Structure), in part to make sure the organizational structure is functional and to prevent too rapid a growth of the Agency. If the maintenance and flexibility in management and use of human remains in legacy collections desired by both communities of descendants and scientists is to be realized, we on the Task Force argue that this option may be the best long‐term solution, though it comes with significant resource requirements and organizational commitments.

Structure

The National Agency would be created as a semi‐independent entity apart from the AABA and sibling associations. By semi‐independence, the Agency should be overseen by the leadership and membership of the sibling associations but have autonomy to carry out its charge. Its function will be to provide training to associations and their members in all aspects of legacy collection inventory & provenance, management, use, and most of all, community partnership. The Agency would also, in regular interaction with sibling organizations, maintain and continue to develop best practice guidelines.

As discussed in the Challenges section above, unequal resources among institutions remain a major concern of members in the AABA, along with training. The Agency would provide a solution for this problem by centralizing resources that could democratize access among individuals. This would be addressed by the creation of staffed “desks.” For example, there could be a desk for African American communities of New England, and another desk for African American communities of the Pacific Northwest. Thus, there would be regionalized specialists who focus on developing connections with and maintaining communication with leaders and members of specific descendant communities. These would be proactive staffed positions, where individuals (or teams of individuals) with training could establish and maintain ongoing communication and outreach with potential or known communities. When a collection manager or a researcher wishes to work with a legacy collection provenanced to a region and/or community, that manager or researcher would contact the Agency, who would identify the desk(s) that in turn would help make the connection between the member of the scientific community and the appropriate descendant community or communities of origin. As the Agency staff member in charge of a desk has already developed connections with the descendant community under ideal circumstances, this would aid connections between researchers and communities to establish community partnerships when desired by the descendant community. The Agency could also operate to build trust with local communities and work to advocate for them. In the long term, some of the “desks” in the Agency may be staffed by members of communities of origin.

The Agency would oversee training of professionals who either manage or use legacy collections. This would encompass the same kinds of training outlined for Options 1 and 2 above. Because it would also be the main hub for interactions between researchers and descendant communities, the Agency could also require researchers and managers to have completed training before being eligible to work with these communities. There could also be opportunities for the Agency to provide training to members of communities of origin and care.

Thus, the Agency would have divisions, each with its own manager. One division would oversee the “desks” and their staff. Another division would be in charge of training. Other divisions might include review and maintenance of guidelines, enforcement and oversight of guidelines, and public engagement and relations. Thus, the Agency, at its fullest manifestation, would require extensive staffing and likely a physical office.

To oversee the “desks” and training staff, the Agency would require officers similar to those found in other not‐for‐profit organizations (e.g., a CEO, a CFO, and managers for each division). These individuals would report to the Board of Directors, which again would be comprised of leadership from the sibling organizations. The right to hire and dismiss all officers of the Agency would be reserved by the Board of Directors. The Board of Directors would also perform annual audits of the Agency.

Organizational Commitment

Given the complexity of the Agency's structure, the AABA and sibling organizations would need to make a long‐term commitment to serve as the Board of Directors and oversight for the activity of the Agency. Such organization also would require more resources and bandwidth than the leadership of many associations has available. Thus, changes in organizational structure, or the formation of a formal not‐for‐profit company would need to occur to make the Agency model work. For these reasons, the Task Force again strongly recommends the gradual development and growth of the Agency should this model be adopted.

Resources and Personnel

As noted above, the Agency option requires a large number of personnel and resource commitment to operate. At minimum, the Agency would need a CEO or Director, a CFO, and managers for each of the divisions, in addition to at least one person (and ideally multiple individuals with different specialties) to staff each “desk.” Additional staff would need to be hired to train, maintain an internet presence, and serve as community outreach. Thus, even at the smallest size (i.e., one “desk”), the Agency would require a staff of around 12 individuals: leadership (2), managers (4), trainers (2–3), an internet technology specialist (1), a public relations and media person (1), and one “desk” specialist.

Financial Implications

The financial impact of an Agency option is orders of magnitude greater than Option 1 or Option 2 above. To fund the Agency, the AABA and sibling associations would need to seek external funds from the U.S. Government (which may present both regulatory and political impediments), from independent funding organizations, or private capital. All sources of financing for the Agency would need to be carefully considered. For the Agency to be effective and trusted, it would need to not be beholden to the requirements of private investors, governmental agencies, or foundations. Thus, the AABA and sibling organizations would need to proceed with care before committing to any funding source. This may be the greatest impediment to executing the Agency option (see Drawbacks below), outside of hiring well‐qualified staff.

Potential Benefits

The Agency provides all the benefits of the Office (Option 2) with respect to centralization, non‐redundancy, and oversight. It also adds the benefit of providing a centralized process by which researchers would be connected with descendant communities and develop partnerships with them under the guidance of trained individuals who already have established relationships with those communities. The Agency also protects the agency of those communities of origin, as they would have a central entity with which to work in addressing concerns. This would address both concerns raised as challenges earlier in this document: it provides resources and training for individuals who may not have time or access within the restrictions of their positions and institutions. It would also provide oversight and possible policing, much as the Office (Option 2), to make sure individuals are engaging with communities respectfully, with cultural awareness, and without being exploitative. Thus, the Agency, if organized with transparency and community engagement, would address many of the concerns raised by the African American community in the Task Force surveys. Such centralization and oversight are missing with Option 1.

In addition, having an Agency might develop a normalized protocol in which individuals know they must work through the Agency to establish community partnerships. While individuals might be able to develop those partnerships without an Agency, only those researchers with the desire, resources, and knowledge to pursue them would engage in community partnered consultation. This overcomes the problem for Options 1 and 2 in which interaction with the Committee or Office would be voluntary. Having an Agency also addresses the identification and cultural awareness gaps that managers of legacy collections face, especially if they are managing many populations. An Agency would not replace or circumvent the institutional committees or boards set out in the Anatomy Recommendations but rather enhance their work by giving an organization for them to contact and use as a resource. The Agency, then, provides synergistic opportunities that improve the ease by which individuals and institutions would be able to follow the best practices for legacy collections.

Potential Drawbacks

The greatest drawbacks for the Agency are financial and staffing. As explained in the Financial Implications above, to fund the Agency would likely require millions of American dollars, money that would have to be raised from various sources. We on the Task Force are wary of investments with restrictions or that lack autonomy (with Board of Directors oversight) for the Agency. Thus, funding will be a major hurdle to be addressed. Finding responsible and well‐trained individuals to serve as the leadership and as “desk” agents for the Agency also poses a problem. This may be alleviated over time if the Office (Option 2) model is in place, as training and experience by individuals within the Office would naturally yield individuals who could be leaders in the Agency. Individuals with experience in community partnered research could also be hired to fulfill the “desk” agent roles, especially if they are familiar with and ideally established rapport with multiple descendant communities within a region.

Beyond these concerns, like any bureaucratic solution, the Agency does introduce many layers of oversight and control beyond individual professional associations. By only adding a single additional layer in the form of “desk” agents, though, the Agency would not visibly add significant administrative burden to scientists and managers and members of communities of origin/care. Oversight of the Agency would be direct from the AABA and sibling associations, which would ensure that the Agency minimizes impediments to the connections necessary between communities. Thus, while it is an ambitious proposal, the Agency would have long‐term benefits for all parties and stakeholders in legacy collections.

One option that the Task Force recommends the AABA and sibling organizations consider is that these three options be implemented over time in order of ascending complexity. That is, the Committee could be created and staffed upon adoption of these recommendations. Negotiations among associations could lead to the creation of an Office within a couple of years, if no sooner. The Agency will take some time to create but could be the long‐term goal for the AABA and sibling associations.

Recommended Requirements and Timelines for Inventory and Management of Legacy Collections

Building on the three organizational options, the AABA needs to set specific requirements for the completion of inventories and the management of collections, including outreach to communities. Here we provide details about the process of collection inventory and management. Accountability and enforcement will be important to the uniform, successful implementation of the many changes suggested in these guidelines. The Task Force recommends that inventory completion follows a set timeline, staggered using criteria presented below. Inventories will have a uniform structure, and filing of inventories should occur in a centralized, publicly accessible location though safeguards should be taken to protect sensitive information (e.g., individual identifiable information). Roadmaps for community outreach and partnership are provided to help members of the AABA determine a process for engaging in collection management. We recognize that with actual implementation, adjustments to these guidelines will be necessary. Such adjustments could be carried out by the Committee (Option 1 in the previous section). The Task Force does not recommend that the AABA address adjustments with an ad hoc committee, such as another task force.

Inventory Components

Inventories should provide information that maximizes data about the nature of human remains in collections and their origins while also respecting sensitive information. For example, while some scholars have advocated for the naming and identification of individuals within collections, some communities may not wish this information to be made available. Because of this, inventories should err in favor of maximizing basic information that will be useful for connecting to communities and for descendant communities to make decisions about engagement with institutions. Inventories should leave out additional details that might be more sensitive for those communities to request directly, and only then, upon request, seek to address those gaps in knowledge.

A sample form for inventories is provided as a supplementary spreadsheet submitted with this report (see Supporting Information). If the AABA adopts these recommendations, they may choose to use this form or modify it. Any form, however, should be identical for all institutions and individuals. We are aware that some information requested on the sample spreadsheet may not be accessible for all constituents of the AABA and sibling associations, and so there should be flexibility in information required. Components to include in inventories should include:

Identifying name for a collection (e.g., “Fairfax County cemetery”). The more specific this identifying group is, the easier it will be for others to locate it when it is placed into a centralized database. For example, including dates, geographic information (that comply with archaeological protection and antiquities acts), and historical names would be good conventions in providing specificity.

For example, if communities would prefer that individuals are stored with ceremonial objects or are held within a type of container, this should be included as information on the inventory if it is known.

Inventory Completion Timeline

The Task Force recommends that individuals and institutions that are stewards of legacy collections establish a priority list for the completion of inventories. We recommend a phased approach to completion of inventories. Collections not under active study should be given priority over those that are actively under study at the time of the adoption of these recommendations. A moratorium on research should be in place once the inventory process has started until consultation with communities of origin, descendant communities, or communities of care has determined the collection can again be made available and the circumstances under which research may proceed. This prevents further disenfranchisement of these communities in favor of scientists while giving opportunities for researchers to work with communities to determine research directions that meet mutual objectives. If a moratorium is not possible, an explicit plan should be put into place to minimize research until inventory and consultation are completed. As stated above, the default position of collections managers should be that no research need occur. By prioritizing parts of a collection that are not under active research, this will have the benefit of fast‐tracking completion of inventories while minimizing impacts on ongoing or existing research. Where communities of origin or descendant communities are already involved in making decisions about a research collection, inventory of that collection should take place in consultation with the wishes of that community, ideally through a committee or participation in a community of care.

The Task Force recognizes that completion of an inventory will be faster for well‐documented and studied collections, as well as those of smaller size, compared with collections that have no recorded provenance or are large. Likewise, institutions that have full‐time staff to manage collections and that have more resources will expedite inventories compared to institutions where one or no individuals are tasked with collection management. Given these challenges, the Task Force recommends that a staggered timeline for inventory completion is adopted based on the size of collections and resource availability. All individuals or institutions that are stewards of collections of human remains should provide details to the centralized entity or organization, such as the Committee or Office that will be the central location for inventory submission and organization.

The Task Force also recognizes that, while the timelines need to be consistent, they may need to be adjusted in consultation with the broader association. Specific timelines will need to be determined by the AABA leadership in consultation with members of the AABA community. Some flexibility should be allowed for institutions that can document delays due to lack of information to determine provenance or logistical hurdles. These extensions would need to be determined by a centralized entity or organization.

Upon completion of an inventory, forms should be filed with a centralized entity that provides access to the information from the inventory in a searchable database. The AABA and sibling associations should plan to host these inventories if possible. These databases provide communities of origin and descendant communities with access to information, as well as accountability for the completion of inventories to institutions and individuals. In addition, it establishes the information necessary for the next stage of the collections management process: consultation with communities of origin/care and descendant communities. We note the posting of inventories alone does not constitute outreach and engagement with descendant communities. Institutions will need to be proactive in reaching out to descendant communities or communities of origin and to enter into a bidirectional dialogue (see ‘Community Communication’ below).

Legacy Collection Management Guidelines

Legacy collections will need to be maintained both during and after the inventory process is completed, and until descendant communities or communities of care are able to make decisions about the study and long‐term allocation of those collections. Outside of the explicit wishes of descendant communities or, in their absence, the community of care, completions of inventories should not be used as a proviso for an institution or individuals to reinstitute research or dispose of remains. Though the Task Force understands that the continued maintenance and housing of collections while awaiting the input of descendant communities places a burden on institutions and collection managers, it is unethical to determine the conditions for study or stewardship of remains without descendant community input.

Broadly, standards of management and maintenance of legacy collections should follow the ethical and legal guidelines of institutions and organizations in which those collections are held. At minimum these should include secure housing of remains with identification information in association with the remains (e.g., inventory identification numbers on boxes associated with completed inventories). Respect for remains should be a priority at all times and managers should not make assumptions about the wishes of those whose remains are in legacy collections or of their communities.

Ultimately, management of collections should be undertaken at the guidance of descendant communities or communities of care. Researchers and institutions should respect the cultural beliefs, practices, and values of descendant communities when handling and housing ancestral remains. This includes acknowledging and honoring spiritual, religious, and cultural protocols related to the treatment of remains. In addition, scientists and descendant communities should adhere to established ethical guidelines and protocols when working with ancestral remains. This includes following legal requirements, research standards, and institutional policies that govern the treatment and analysis of human remains.

Community Communication and Partnership Guidance

As explained above, following the completion of inventories, contacting and communicating with descendant communities is a key aspect of our recommendations. Given the differences in structure, leadership, and wishes of each community, the specifications we present here are meant to give guiding principles and generalized steps that researchers and institutions should follow in contacting and communicating with descendant communities. Examples of successful partnerships with descendant communities are provided in Appendix II. We also provide in this section a series of citations to studies that provide good examples of how members of the AABA community have already engaged with communities in the process of their research.

There will be instances when descendant communities cannot be determined, in part because of the lack of provenance documentation, lost historical context, or ambiguity about descendant community identity. There may also be cases in which descendant communities do not respond to communication by researchers or institutions after a good faith effort. What constitutes a good faith effort and reasonable time for response is situational, but we provide guidance in this determination below. In these cases, we also provide guidance below for the creation of communities of care to make decisions about the management, research, and long‐term status of legacy collections.

Before proceeding with the principles and general guidance, we recommend that the leadership of the AABA and sibling organizations develop a positive messaging campaign to the membership about interactions with descendant communities. This could include detailed examples of successful descendant community outreach and partnership. Based on the Task Force survey of the membership and subsequent sessions, we know that many members of the AABA have not directly worked with descendant communities outside of third parties or intermediaries (e.g., Tribal Historic Preservation Officers, NAGPRA committees, and curators), and so our recommendation to the community to work toward descendant community communication and partnership will lead to some uncertainty and anxiety about how to proceed. Providing centralized resources to the membership is therefore of utmost importance in the successful implementation of the recommendations we provide.

Guidelines for Contacting and Communicating With Descendant Communities

The determination of what communities constitute descendants or communities of origin is dependent on the context and provenance of a given legacy collection of human remains. As we outline above in the “Inventory Components” subsection, under ideal circumstances descendant communities will be ascertained from existing records about the procurement of remains. For example, if remains were exhumed from a recorded cemetery, it may be possible to track which community or communities used that burying ground. Collection managers should avoid rushing to identify descendant communities and instead thoroughly investigate provenance. As we noted before, use of DNA or other molecular methods is not recommended as a means of determining information about origin unless advocated for by a community of care, which includes input from individuals representing cultural communities, historical communities, and ethical interests. Good documentation of all steps taken in the process of identifying a descendant community is essential. Descendant communities should know that these records exist when they are contacted, and the records should be made available at their request.

There is no single roadmap for contacting and communicating with descendant communities. This is because the methods of communication, the frequency of conversation, and the steps for developing trust and dialogue will vary by context. Members of the scientific community should be aware that there may be multiple groups within a descendant community with differing attitudes about a legacy collection. There may be cultural sensitivities and concerns about which members of the scientific community should be aware. Often the best way to learn of these is through initial communications with descendant communities, though not all communities will be forthcoming, at least at first. For this reason, we encourage all collection managers to engage with individuals who have already established a rapport and trust with a given descendant community, such as archaeologists, cultural anthropologists, or sociologists. These researchers may know who might serve as a representative from the community if one is already established. Ideally, members of the scientific community could learn from the community directly about their preferences for representation, methods of communication, and topics of concern. Local institutional committees (including communities of care), either within one institution or shared among multiple nearby institutions, will be essential in guiding this process. We encourage the organization of such committees to help with inventory, management, and outreach.

Regardless of the specific circumstances of communication and needs of a descendant community, we now know of best practices that emerged from our national survey of descendant African American communities. Measures should be in place to protect the privacy and confidentiality of data obtained from human biological remains. Researchers should ensure that data is handled securely and in compliance with relevant data protection regulations. In addition to clear communication about the kinds of data used to identify legacy collections of human remains, as well as all available information about their provenance, researchers should engage in educational outreach activities to raise awareness about the ethical and social implications of studying human remains. This can help foster understanding and support within both the scientific and broader communities. If requested by descendant communities, human remains should be repatriated and respectfully reburied in accordance with cultural practices and beliefs. By implementing these practices as safeguards, respondents to the national survey suggested that scientists can help prevent the further exploitation of human remains and ensure that research is conducted in a responsible, ethical, and respectful manner. Collaboration, transparency, and cultural sensitivity are key principles that should guide research involving human remains.

The outcomes of dialogue with descendant communities should not be predetermined. We have advocated for, at minimum, participation by descendant communities if not collaboration (see Colwell‐Chanthaphonh and Ferguson 2008, 10–14) when desired by the communities. However, all parties involved in these dialogues should be open to changing goals and preferences as more information is made available and as the requests of the descendant community evolve. Collection managers and members of the scientific community should therefore engage with descendant communities with a framework that encourages flexibility and continuous dialogue (again, as desired by the descendant community).

The Task Force advocates for scientists to enter conversations with descendant communities without apprehension but instead with an outlook that, as such dialogue has been absent throughout much of the history of research using legacy collections, communication with descendant communities and communities of origin provide novel opportunities when desired. In the AABA membership survey, some respondents expressed concern about future loss of information about human variation should legacy collections be closed to research. Community partnerships do not preclude future research but rather have the potential to enhance it or take it in new directions. Moreover, as set out through this document, the concern of the Task Force has been on the ethical stewardship of legacy collections. Outreach and communication with descendant communities is an essential necessity to meet the bioethical standards and goals of restorative justice that have been our guiding paradigm. African Americans surveyed across the United States stated their desire to determine what happens to their ancestors' remains explicitly and should not be ignored. Furthermore, many examples of community‐partnered studies in biological anthropology, archaeology, and cultural anthropology have demonstrated that research that bridges the interests of all parties is often an outcome of such partnerships.

Readers should be familiar with precedent for how other researchers have engaged with descendant communities. We highlight examples from members of the Task Force in Appendix II. Furthermore, biological anthropologists and human biologists have engaged in partnerships with descendant communities in various contexts, and reports of their methods are instructive (e.g., Blakey 2010; Turner and Andrushko 2011; Ravenscroft et al. 2015; Boutin 2019; Clinton and Jackson 2021). Multiple publications in archaeology have examined best practices for collaborations with descendant communities (see, for example, McDavid 2002; Atalay 2012; Colwell 2016; Franklin and Lee 2020; Atalay and McCleary 2022; Montgomery and Fryer 2023; Gilmore et al. 2024), though many of these have focused on partnerships with First Nations and Native American communities. As these communities have protective laws (e.g., NAGPRA) and political organizational structures for interfacing with researchers, examples of community engagement and communication with them are unlikely to be applicable to most African American or other descendant communities. Though, they are still examples of good practices and ethical standards of collaboration and participation as well as cautions for exploitation in the name of partnership (La Salle 2010). Researchers should be aware of ongoing development and refinement of community partnered research and ethical practices within molecular (DNA) research fields (e.g., Tall Bear 2013; Claw et al. 2018; Wagner et al. 2020; Tsosie et al. 2021; Kowal et al. 2023). Outside of anthropology, fields like public health (Teufel‐Shone et al. 2019; Lucero et al. 2020) and sociology (Banks et al. 2015; Cross et al. 2015; Fahlberg 2023; Keighley et al. 2023; Fahlberg et al. 2025) have worked within the community‐based participatory framework or similarly named frameworks. Beyond publications, direct communication and sharing of experiences among researchers—ideally with coordination under the Committee or Office models—will likewise be essential for continued growth and success in implementing the community partnered approach.

How Long Should One Wait for a Response From a Descendant Community?

This is a common question that has been asked both within the Task Force and in conversations with members of the academic community. As with other matters that involve descendant communities, this will depend on the community. For example, whether a community has an existing structure for representation and outreach will have an impact on both communication with and response time from the community. A good faith effort that involves multiple invitations for dialogue should be made with an understanding that some communities may not be prepared to engage in a conversation at the time. Some communities may need to be contacted through more than one modality; researchers should not assume that all communities have access to email, for example. Collection managers and members of the scientific community should also not assume a lack of communication is a sign of disinterest or an implicit permission for the researchers to do as they desire without input from the community. Communities of care may be consulted after attempts to contact a descendant community have been exhausted within an explicit set of guidelines (see the next section), but researchers and institutions should always give descendant communities priority in determining how to manage and determine the disposition of legacy collections.

Guidelines for Communities of Care: When to Implement Them and How They Should Operate

The Anatomy Recommendations outline the development of communities of care within institutions, defining their membership as “advocat[ing] on behalf of the individuals represented in the collections, hav[ing] unique perspectives on elements important to legacy collections, and […] providing context surrounding the proper treatment of these individuals' remains and contributing to decision‐making about their care” (Cornwall et al. 2024: 10). Thus, communities of care serve as a body for discussion of ethical approaches to the care and disposition of individuals whose remains are held in collections. There are two broad circumstances under which communities of care should be constituted: general implementation and management of these guidelines, especially among institutions with fewer resources; and in cases where provenance data do not allow for identification of a descendant community or where the descendant community cannot be engaged in the process of determining management and stewardship. The Task Force recommends that members of the AABA look toward formally organized communities of care within institutions as a method for instituting the recommendations approved by the Executive Committee. Communities of care could be formed within larger institutes or among groups of regionally proximate institutions, in the case of smaller sized colleges or units where there may be fewer resources or individuals who could serve as members of the community.

Who serves as part of a community of care will vary by location and context, but the persons or groups who serve should have some investment in the oversight, management, and care of human remains. Ideally, communities of care consist of a group of individuals both within the institution and members of communities from outside the institution. Members of descendant communities should be welcomed as part of communities of care and their participation actively supported. As noted in the Anatomy Recommendations, not all stakeholders (e.g., curators, administrators, researchers, and custodians of remains in addition to descendant communities) need to participate in communities of care, but communities of care should represent a breadth of those who have a connection with the management as stewardship of human remains within collections. We also note that every effort should be made to have good representation of local and descendant communities, and that care should be taken to avoid tokenism in the assembly of such communities.

We recommend that communities of care should be consulted when the descendant community cannot be. There are many circumstances in which a descendant community may not be reachable even under the best efforts of individuals and institutions. A lack of specific provenance information about human remains is one circumstance cited as a concern by members of the AABA community in both the online 2022 survey and in subsequent conversations. For example, as noted by Agarwal (2024), skeletal collections from India were acquired through third‐party individuals and organizations, such as biological supply houses, wherein provenance information about individuals was purposefully obscured or removed before they were made available for purchase by universities and other institutions. Thus, the descendant community may not be determinable. In other cases, multiple communities might be interested parties and thus the logistics for consulting all communities transparently and in a coordinated manner could prove onerous for small organizations, colleges, or institutions. Communities of care could provide both a resource for determining how to reach out to descendant communities (especially in place of the Office or Agency should these not be organized) and an initial group to decide on the best approaches to take with respect to the management and custodianship of legacy collections. The specific charge to communities of care should be made at the local level, with flexibility in their organization so that the composition and meeting structure of the community of care address the needs of the local communities, institution, and legacy collection.

Members of the AABA should take caution about abuses that could arise within and in institutional interactions with communities of care. Descendant community groups should be invited to participate within communities of care and be compensated or reimbursed for their time, though our surveys of members of the African American communities across the United States show that there is skepticism and some distrust of the very institutions that hold legacy collections of human remains. Thus, efforts will need to be made to provide a genuine democratic environment within communities of care, where the power differences of those in the institutional community against those of the community outside the institution are transparently addressed and all members are heard. Moreover, communities of care should not be used in lieu of good faith efforts to contact and work with descendant communities, or to rubber stamp the wishes of administrative or legal stakeholders within institutions without proper partnership or consultation with descendant communities. Likewise, communities of care should be recognized in ways that they have support by administrators and power to make recommendations about the management of human remains when descendant communities legitimately cannot be consulted. The Task Force recommends the AABA create a formal system of communication for communities of care with any model of organization adopted (Committee, Office, or Agency) for accountability and oversight, and to provide a mechanism by which abuses of communities may be shared with the association.

Special Consideration: Legacy Collections Used for Instruction

A final common concern has been raised in the survey of the AABA membership, in public forums with the AABA membership, and in direct communication: how should we proceed with use of human remains in instruction? These legacy collections are not exempt from our Task Force's recommendations (or the Anatomy Recommendations), but the minimization of harm posits a conflict between education and continued exploitation of communities that never had the opportunity to give consent. Members of the AABA want to continue to use real human remains to teach osteology, especially with respect to pathologies and fragmentary identification. Yet continued use of these remains without knowledge and consent of descendant communities violates the recommendations for ethical practice that we have outlined above, and furthermore perpetuates the harms highlighted by members of our national survey of African American communities.

The Task Force proposes a few options to address these concerns:

Institutions that have the financial means could consider purchase of cast models of human remains for teaching whole element morphology and identification. The Task Force also realizes that this may only punt the problem to future debate, as these bone models were taken from scans or casts of human remains where individuals or communities did not have the opportunity to give consent for their use. Thus, the AABA may want to pursue a campaign to ask businesses that produce these models to create them from remains obtained with clear consent by donors, their families, or descendant communities for use in education.

There are a growing number of academic institutions that have accumulated collections of human remains with explicit consent by the individual or their families for the use of their remains in education and research. As these collections continue to expand, the Task Force sees them as a potential source of remains for the purposes of education. Final Recommendations

The Task Force members thank the leadership of the AABA for giving us the time to investigate and deliberate to develop these recommendations. As many concerns have been addressed in this document, we highlight here our primary advice to the AABA and sibling organizations:

Legacy collections of human remains represent an ongoing area of concern with respect to bioethics. How the AABA membership decides to proceed with policies and guidance in the management and disposition of human remains must balance the beneficence to society in their study, justice and equitable distribution of burden to descendant communities as well as among members of the society, and the minimization of harm, especially with respect to the deceased and their communities. We must address through new policies and actions the continued trauma to descendant communities, which may view the study of remains as both a source of knowledge but also persistent exploitation. The members of the AABA cannot decide for a descendant community how they regard the continued stewardship of remains, their study, or the kinds of questions that could be asked; making these decisions without the active partnership of descendant communities or ignoring their wishes removes agency and perpetuates the problems that led to the assembly of the Task Force in the first place. This perspective is already present within the membership of the AABA, based on survey results and discussions, but messaging should be made clear about what the ethical standards of the AABA are with respect to community partnership and participation.

However, given the enormity of legacy collections across the globe, the Task Force advocates for the adoption of the Office model, where multiple sibling associations may provide collective resources and oversight to aid the academic community with the process of restoring legacy collections to descendant community input and agency.

These recommendations may be treated by the leadership of the AABA as a living document, in that there will be concerns about the execution and operation of our guidance that we cannot anticipate until they are implemented. For this reason, we strongly encourage that the AABA make the Committee for Community Partnership (CCP), or another similar committee, a standing committee and to charge that committee with regular review of the implementation of the Task Force guidelines. To this end, we recommend that the AABA Executive Committee or the CCP regularly conduct surveys of the AABA membership about their experiences in implementing these guidelines. The CCP may also undertake surveys of descendant communities, much as we pursued within the Task Force among members of the African American community, to gauge efficacy and issues that arise from the implementation of these guidelines. Partnerships with cultural anthropologists and sociologists will be important toward this goal.

In conclusion, the Task Force hopes that the AABA and sibling associations take this moment as an opportunity to change a foundational legacy for the discipline into a new approach for conducting our science. This new approach can be built on participation of descendant communities and communities of origin, through open dialogue, and through transparency. Only through respect, communication, and collaboration can we minimize harm, begin the process of repairing relationships between communities, and promote ethical studies of human remains where desired. Through the approaches we advocate, the work of biological anthropologists and sibling fields will be able to evolve and take on questions heretofore unexplored.

Questions and Summary of 2022 Survey Results From the African American Descendant Community in Washington, D.C.

(1) Are you aware that appropriated African American remains are in the possession of U.S. university labs and museums?

Our first question in the pre‐survey was designed to determine if our participants were familiar with the issue of U.S. university labs and museum possession of appropriated African American remains. Once we explained the meaning of appropriated, uniformly the responses were anger followed by a need for empowerment. Once respondents cooled off (since for many this was the first time that they had been formally informed of the extent of the disposition of African American biological remains) we were able to move on to the second question.

(2) How should the remains be handled?

In this second question, participants expressed a broad range of responses. No one suggested doing nothing. Rather, most saw the appropriation of African American skeletal remains and other biological materials as part of a long, centuries‐old tradition among white Americans of taking that which did not belong to them for their own purposes and use. With time, the responses to how the remains should be handled became more sophisticated and detailed. What emerged was a strong desire to gain some benefit for the living from the deaths and mistreatment of our ancestors.

(3) Should guidelines exist to control scientists’ access to these samples?

Most participants in the preliminary survey assumed that scientists had some novel set of guidelines that they at least purported to follow. Participants were aware of the role of bioethics in scientific research. Most participants assumed that physical anthropologists followed these guidelines although many participants suggested that different (bioethical) rules existed for African American materials versus European American materials. Participants felt that, as members of the African American community, they should be involved in the augmentation of such guidelines.

(4) Who should be consulted to set up these guidelines?

Participants strongly felt that members of the affected communities should be consulted to set up bioethical guidelines since there are nuanced cultural differences among African American communities in different parts of the United States. Participants indicated that guidelines should be locally specific and approved by the actual descendants rather than people unaffiliated with the affected African American community.

(5) How should these guidelines be enforced?

Participants were adamant that the police not be involved in the enforcement of bioethical guidelines on scientists in possession of African American skeletal and other materials. However, participants did feel that guidelines need to be enforced, perhaps by university administrators or through the scientists' professional organizations. One participant referred to this as a need for “checks and balances” among scientists.

Vignettes About Descendant Community Engagement and Community Partnership in Research

Community Partnership in Research Conducted by Dr. Jennifer Caldwell

My research with descendant communities has consistently demonstrated that meaningful involvement must be embedded at all stages of study design, implementation, and collection management. Roadmap elements: Rules/best practices.

In cardiovascular genetic research among the Gullah Geechee, we adopted community‐defined “rules of engagement” to enhance trust and participation. This included co‐developing research questions with community leaders, ensuring transparency around data and specimen use, and delivering tangible community benefits such as health education and capacity building. These practices proved vital in overcoming historical distrust and improving recruitment and retention (Caldwell and Jackson 2024).

In Louisiana, the Linking GAINS study and the Genesis AI project further illustrate how descendant community perspectives can guide ethical frameworks, informed consent processes, and governance models for data stewardship. Linking GAINS integrates cultural values and community‐led advisory structures into genetic research, while Genesis AI demonstrates how community voices can shape emerging digital tools to avoid bias and ensure cultural relevance.

Across these projects, several roadmaps have emerged: beginning with listening and co‐setting priorities; creating shared governance and advisory structures; co‐developing consent and data/specimen policies; ensuring transparent benefit‐sharing; using culturally appropriate communication; investing in infrastructure and local capacity; and maintaining relationships beyond project completion. Together, these practices underscore that descendant communities are not just participants but partners within research.

Challenges remain, including power imbalances, funding timelines misaligned with community expectations, and concerns about long‐term stewardship of genetic and cultural data. However, transparency, reciprocal benefit, and education can mitigate many of these challenges.

A recurring lesson from the Gullah Geechee work is that descendant communities must have agency over how data and collections are used and safeguarded for future generations.

In line with these experiences, I recommend that funding agencies, IRBs, and institutions explicitly require descendant community involvement across all stages of research and collection management. Dedicated resources must be provided for engagement activities, and community compensation should be considered a core budget item. Standards for stewardship should reflect community values and establish long‐term mechanisms for shared authority.

Community Partnership in Research Conducted by Dr. Carter Clinton

I am an African American scholar from Brooklyn, New York, and my connection to the New York African Burial Ground is personal. Those ancestors could be my not so distant kin. This background shaped my research at the New York African Burial Ground and now informs how I carry projects forward and enter community spaces in North Carolina, with duty, pride, humility, and rigor. I carry the weight of history into every conversation, and I measure success not only in scientific outputs but also in the strength of relationships, the clarity of understanding, and the respect shown to ancestors and their descendants.

I did not seek out the first phone calls. Community members from Hillsborough, North Carolina and Wendell, North Carolina reached out to me after they watched my National Geographic video. They asked a direct question that deserved a direct answer. Could we apply our non‐destructive genomic protocol to their local historical burial sites, believed to hold the remains of African American ancestors, to learn about kinship, genetic genealogy, health related genetic markers, and ancestral origins. I listened, then I agreed to meet in person, because this work only moves forward with trust, clear expectations, and shared authority.

From the outset the communities led the engagement. They defined the initial questions. They set the pace. They approved the approach. Their autonomy is not symbolic, it is operational. Every major decision flows through a community advisory board that includes elders, church leaders, local historians, and family representatives. We meet regularly to review goals, consent language, data handling, and timelines. When priorities evolve, the research design evolves with them. If a question does not serve their needs, it does not drive our analyses.

Our communication model is intentional and two‐way. We hold open town halls in accessible community venues with childcare, food, and time for discussion. I begin with a plain language overview of what burial soil based genomic analysis can and cannot answer. I explain the safeguards that protect ancestor dignity and present‐day families. We then shift to questions, concerns, and new ideas from the room. The advisory board captures action items, and I commit to specific follow up by named dates. Between town halls, we use regular updates by email, printed flyers at churches and community centers, and small group check ins to maintain momentum and accountability.

The scientific foundation is non‐destructive, and I stand by that standard. We carefully collect samples of burial soil, leaving skeletal remains undisturbed. Field procedures respect site boundaries, religious practices, and local protocols. Chain of custody is recorded from the moment of collection to the laboratory bench. This approach safeguards ancestor rest and still allows rigorous analysis.

In the laboratory, we use cutting edge molecular techniques that match the gravity of the questions. Whole genome sequencing of human DNA, metagenomic profiling, and identity by descent analysis provide complementary views of genetic ancestry, kinship, and health markers associated with diseases that disproportionately affect African descended populations. Identity by descent signals help us evaluate relatedness across burials, which informs family linkages that oral histories have preserved for generations. Metagenomic profiles and targeted panels permit cautious evaluation of genetic variants that intersect with known health disparities, while avoiding speculation beyond the data. Throughout, we validate pipelines, document parameters, and use controls appropriate to complex environmental samples.

Information sharing does not end with a journal article. We produce community friendly reports with clear visuals, short videos that can be shared on phones, slide presentations for churches and community day meetings, and lay language summaries for households. Each town hall includes a results segment that explains what we know, what is uncertain, and what we are doing next. I also schedule one on one sessions for family members who want help interpreting specific findings. These conversations are confidential and paced to match individual comfort.

This work has emotional and intellectual impact. Standing at a historical burial site beside descendants who don’t know the names, the stories, or the histories, I feel both the weight of responsibility and the clarity of mission. I am honest about limitations, including the fact that soil‐based data will never replace the lived histories of families or the authority of community memory which is why we incorporate oral interviews into our study. I am also clear about the promise. Thoughtful use of genomic tools can support documentation of kinship, illuminate ancestral origins at a population level, and provide insights that can inform health education and advocacy.

What matters most is the relationship. These partnerships in Hillsborough and Wendell are sustained and forward looking. We build agreements that center stewardship, we protect privacy, and we plan together for how findings will be used. The communities decide how results appear in public forums, who speaks at events, and how credit is shared. My role is to bring technical rigor, transparent communication, and an unwavering respect for their cultural beliefs. Their role is leadership. Together, we are demonstrating that community centered genomics in North Carolina can honor ancestors, empower descendants, and produce knowledge that serves the public good.
