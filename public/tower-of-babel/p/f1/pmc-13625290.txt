Empagliflozin-pretreated mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles facilitate cardiac repair via AGGF1
Authors: Qianwen Chen, Qingqing Gu, Jun Wei, Li Deng, Ye Deng, Jingyi Wang, Jianya Huang, Tingting Sun, Lu Pan, Wenjun Mao, Qingjie Wang, Ruxing Wang, Ling Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrent treatment modalities can only reap a limited efficacy in reversing myocardial damage and promoting functional recovery in patients with myocardial infarction. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore new therapeutic approaches to enhance cardiac repair.MethodsMSCs were pretreated with empagliflozin to obtain EMPA-EVs, with non-pretreated MSC-EVs serving as controls. In vitro experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of the two types of EVs on macrophage phenotypic transformation, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and vascular endothelial cell lumen formation. In vivo experiments, a rat model of myocardial infarction was constructed, and EMPA-EVs, non-pretreated MSC-EVs, or phosphate-buffered saline were administered via myocardial injection. Post-treatment assessments included left ventricular ejection fraction, myocardial infarction area, and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in myocardial tissue. Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of mortality globally, with the annual related deaths having increased by approximately 60% over the past three decades. In today’s global population expanding and meanwhile aging, the burden of ischemic heart disease continues to escalate, with 254 million cases reported in 2021 [1]. As the most serious manifestation of ischemic heart disease, acute myocardial infarction (AMI) typically results from sudden occlusion or abrupt reduction in coronary blood flow, and culminates in irreversible ischemic necrosis of the affected myocardium.

Timely revascularization and pharmacological interventions can partially preserve cardiac function following infarction, but cannot enable the regeneration of necrotic cardiomyocytes [2]. Stem cell therapy has emerged to overcome this issue, but its application is hindered by several events, including immune rejection, a poor survival in ischemic microenvironments, and a limited capacity of cardiomyogenic differentiation. Increasing evidence suggests that the regenerative effects of stem cells are primarily mediated by paracrine factors that exert pleiotropic effects on such processes as cardiac regeneration, metabolic homeostasis, inflammation, angiogenesis, and fibrosis [3, 4].

Among various paracrine mediators, extracellular vesicles (EVs)—nanosized, lipid bilayer-enclosed particles—have shown a mediatory effect on intercellular communication. EVs are rich in bioactive molecules derived from their parent cells, including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [5], and serve as key effectors in cell-to-cell signaling [6]. Notably, EVs possess low immunogenicity and cytotoxicity, a strong tissue-penetrating capacity, and a high feasibility of bioengineering modifications [7]. Accumulating evidence supports the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis [8], AMI [9, 10], ischemia/reperfusion injury [11, 12], and heart failure [13, 14].

Empagliflozin (EMPA), a selective sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, has demonstrated robust cardioprotective and renoprotective effects beyond its glucose-lowering activity [15, 16]. The cardioprotective effect of EMPA involves molecular regulation on multiple processes, including apoptosis, oxidative stress [17], inflammation [18], autophagy [19], and myocardial fibrosis [20]. Previous studies have also reported that pharmacological pretreatment can enhance the efficacy of EVs [21–23]. These findings inspired us to systematically investigate the reparative effects of EMPA-pretreated MSC-derived EVs (EMPA-EVs) against myocardial infarction.

Methods

Cell culture and drug pretreatment

Human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (RRID: CVCL_A9JT), rat cardiomyoblast cell line H9c2 (H9c2 (2 − 1), RRID: CVCL_0286), human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, RRID: CVCL_2959), and mouse monocyte-macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (RRID: CVCL_0493) were obtained from commercial suppliers. All the cells used were identified and free from mycoplasma contamination before the experiment, and were cultured in accordance with the instructions. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. H9c2, HUVECs, and RAW264.7 cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Culture medium was refreshed every 1–3 days, and all cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. In the hypoxia experiment, the cells were placed in a hypoxia incubator (1% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 94% N₂) at 37 °C for subsequent culture.

In preliminary experiments, the optimal concentration and duration of EMPA (Beyotime, SD2411, purity ≥ 98%) were determined using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Vazyme, A311) and JC-1 (5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethyl-imidacarbocyanine iodide, Beyotime, C2003S) staining. To induce a pro-inflammatory phenotype, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Beyotime, S1732) for 24 h. In formal experiments, the cells were treated with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), MSC-EVs (1 µg/µL), or EMPA-EVs (1 µg/µL).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. MSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a consistent density. Upon reaching 50% confluence, the cells were exposed to various concentrations of EMPA for 24–48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added into each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Mitochondrial membrane potential assessment

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was evaluated using JC-1 dye. At a high MMP, JC-1 aggregates in mitochondria and emits red fluorescence; at a low MMP, JC-1 remains in a monomeric form emitting green fluorescence. Cells were seeded in confocal dishes, treated under experimental conditions, and stained with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min. Mitochondrial polarization was evaluated according to red-to-green fluorescence ratios under a fluorescence microscope.

Additionally, the TMRE MMP assay kit (tetramethyl rhodamine ethyl ester, Beyotime, C2001S) was used to detect mitochondrial dysfunction. A reduction in TMRE fluorescence indicated MMP loss and mitochondrial permeability transition, characteristics of early apoptosis.

Identification of MSC surface markers

MSCs were analyzed by flow cytometry for surface markers. The cells were harvested and then incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (CD45-FITC, CD44-FITC, CD31-PE, CD105-PE, CD34-APC, CD73-APC; 1:100; eBioscience) at 4 °C for 30 min. Samples were analyzed using BD FACS Aria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, China) to confirm MSC identity.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured via EdU (5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine, Vazyme, C10310) incorporation. After treatment, cells were stained with EdU working solution and Apollo fluorescent dye to visualize DNA synthesis, and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 for nuclei. The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated under a fluorescence microscope.

Cell migration assay

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in the complete medium until the cell monolayer showed a 100% confluence. A straight wound was created in the center of the cell layer using a 200 µL pipette tip, and then gently washed with PBS to remove cell debris. The medium was replaced with basal medium containing drugs or corresponding controls, and the cells were further cultured for 12 h. The areas of the scratch before and after culture were compared under a microscope to assess cell migration ability.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase Staining

After cell fixation, SA-β-Gal (senescence-associated β-galactosidase, Beyotime, C0602) staining was performed in a CO₂-free incubator at 37 °C overnight. The cumulative optical density values of the positive staining areas were observed and counted under an optical microscope to evaluate the degree of cell senescence.

Cell cycle analysis

One stage of cell cycle can be indicated by the DNA content in cells. The distribution of the cell cycle can be analyzed by propidium iodide (PI) staining combined with flow cytometry. Cells were collected and fixed with absolute ethanol, and then cell cycle staining solution (sharebio, SB-C6031) was added and incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. The flow cytometry histograms were analyzed using FlowJo software to calculate the proportions of cells in G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases.

Transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

MSCs were divided into EMPA (500 nM) pretreatment group and vehicle (DMSO) control group, with three independent biological replicates per group. Total RNA was isolated using Magzol reagent and DNase I digestion. Samples with RNA integrity values > 8 were further processed. mRNA was enriched using Oligo (dT) magnetic beads according to the NEBNext® Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module. The library was constructed through mRNA fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and low-cycle enrichment. The final library was subjected to quality control and paired-end sequencing with a read length of 150 bp. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using edge R, and their overall distribution was presented by volcano plots. Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs using a cluster profiler.

Isolation, identification and uptake of EVs

MSCs were pretreated with EMPA or control until the confluence reached approximately 80%, and then cultured in EV-free complete medium for 48 h (EV-free fetal bovine serum was purchased from VivaCell). After discarding the culture supernatant, cell debris and impurities were removed by sequential differential centrifugation at 300 g for 10 min, 2000 g for 10 min, and 10,000 g for 30 min (4 °C). The supernatant was then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 g for 90 min to obtain crude EV pellets. To eliminate residual protein contaminants, the crude EVs were resuspended in PBS and washed via an additional ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 90 min. Finally, the purified EVs were resuspended in 100 µL PBS and stored at −80 °C. The total protein content of EVs was determined using a BCA kit (bicinchoninic acid assay, Keygen, KGP2101). The absorbance was detected at 562 nm using a microplate reader, and the total protein concentration of each sample was calculated from the standard curve. The morphology of EVs was observed by transmission electron microscopy, and total particle concentration of EVs were evaluated by nanoparticle tracking analysis after each batch of samples had been diluted to an appropriate concentration. The particle-to-protein ratio was calculated as the total particle concentration divided by the corresponding total protein concentration. The surface markers Tetraspanin-Associated Protein 1 (CD81), Cluster of Differentiation 63 (CD63), and Tumor Susceptibility Gene 101 (TSG101) of EVs were detected by Western blot.

To verify their uptake ability, EVs were labeled with the red fluorescent dye Dil (1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate, Beyotime, C1036), and the unbound dye was removed. Then, the EVs were co-incubated with H9c2 rat cardiomyocytes, HUVECs, and mouse monocyte - macrophage leukemia cell line RAW 264.7 cells for 24 h. Subsequently, the cytoskeleton was stained with Actin-Tracker Green-488 (Beyotime, C2201S), and the nuclei with DAPI (4′,6 - diamidino −2 - phenylindole, Beyotime, C1005). The uptake was visualized and analyzed by confocal microscopy.

Quantification of empagliflozin by UPLC-MS/MS

EMPA concentrations in culture supernatant, EMPA-MSC pellets and EMPA-EVs were quantified via validated UPLC-MS/MS MR assay. All samples were subjected to methanol extraction combined with three cycles of freeze-thaw plus ice-bath ultrasonication to fully disrupt EV lipid bilayers and release intravesicular components prior to quantification. Chromatographic and mass spectrometry parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Serial EMPA standard solutions were prepared for calibration curve establishment, and compound concentration was calculated based on validated external standard formulas. Samples with signals below the lower limit of quantification were defined as undetectable.

Apoptosis detection

H9c2 cells were seeded in 6-well plates, cultured overnight, treated with PBS or EVs and subjected to hypoxia/serum deprivation (H/SD) stimulation. The cells were collected and stained with annexin V-FITC and PI (Keygen, KGA1102). The apoptotic cell ratios were detected by flow cytometry, and the analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

Tube formation assay

To evaluate the effect of EVs on the angiogenic ability of endothelial cells, HUVECs were starved for 12 h, uniformly seeded in 24-well plates pre-coated with matrigel (absin, abs9491), and cultured in fresh medium containing PBS or EVs for 12 h. The tubes were imaged, and the tube length, branch nodes, and ring structures were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Macrophage polarization detection

To investigate the regulatory effect of EVs on macrophage polarization in an inflammatory environment, RAW264.7 cells were induced by LPS (1 µg/mL, 24 h) and treated with PBS or EVs. Anti-mouse cluster of differentiation 80 (CD80) antibody (Invitrogen, 12–0801-82) was added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After fixation and membrane disruption, the same incubation with anti-mouse cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206) antibody (Invitrogen, 17–2061-82) was carried out. The proportion of M1-type and M2-type macrophages were detected by flow cytometry. Un-treated RAW264.7 cells were used as M0-type control.

Total RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the centrifugal column method (Beyotime, R0027). cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription using HisyGo RT Red SuperMix (Vazyme, RT101). RT-qPCR was performed using ChamQ Blue SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q312). The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. Primer information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Nitric oxide (NO) content detection

Inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) catalyzes the production of NO that plays a crucial role in the development of inflammatory macrophages. The inflammatory level in RAW264.7 macrophages was evaluated using a NO content detection kit (Beyotime, S0021S). The cell culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, and added with Griess Reagent I and II. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm, and the concentration of nitrite, an indirect product of NO, was calculated using a standard curve.

Construction of myocardial infarction (MI) animal model and in vivo tracing of EVs

All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing Medical University (No. IACUC-2308024). Male sprague-dawley (SD) rats aged 6–8 weeks were randomly divided into groups. A permanent ligation of the left anterior descending artery was performed to establish the MI model. The pale anterior wall of the left ventricle and weakened myocardial contraction indicated a successful modeling.

EVs were diluted to a working concentration of 1 µg/µL based on the measured protein concentration. Immediately after the surgery, 50 µL of PBS, EVs (approximately 6 × 10⁹ particles per rat), or DiR (1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide, UELandy, D4006) labeled EVs were injected into multiple points in the myocardium at the infarct border zone. On the first day after the surgery, the rats were randomly selected from the surgery group and the sham group, and their hearts were collected, cut into slices approximately 2 mm thick, and stained with TTC (2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride, Beyotime, C0651). Red areas indicated viable myocardium, while white areas indicated infarcted regions and could indicated the success or failure of model construction. The biodistribution of DiR-EVs was tracked from day 1 to day 12 after surgery using an in vivo imaging system.

Histological analysis and cardiac function assessment

On the third day after surgery, immunofluorescence staining was performed on tissue sections to detect the pro-inflammatory macrophage marker iNOS and the pro-reparative macrophage marker cluster of differentiation 163 (CD163). Immunohistochemical staining was used to detect the expression of cytokines interleukin-1β and the pro-reparative macrophage marker CD206.

On the 28th day after surgery, M-mode echocardiography was used to record cardiac parameters and determine left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS). Tissue samples were stained with Masson’s trichrome, Sirius Red, and HE to assess infarct size, fibrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration. At the same time, the liver, spleen, lung, and kidney of rats in each group were collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for HE staining to observe histopathological changes and assess the in vivo biosafety of EMPA-EVs.

Western Blot

For extracellular vesicle samples, purified EV pellets were resuspended in pre-chilled lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails, followed by incubation on ice for 1 h. For cell samples, cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed with the same lysis buffer on ice for 15 min. After incubation, all lysates were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 10 min at 4℃, and the supernatants were collected as total protein extracts. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Keygen, KGB2101). Total protein was mixed with loading buffer and denatured at 95℃ for 5 min. Equal amounts of total protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, IEVH07850). After blocking with 5% skim milk, the following primary antibodies were added: CD81 (AF2428, Beyotime), CD63 (AF1471, Beyotime), TSG101 (AF8259, Beyotime), B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2, 26593-1-AP, Proteintech), Bcl-2-associated X protein (BAX, 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), β-actin (20536-1-AP, Proteintech), iNOS (80517-1-RR, Proteintech), arginase-1 (ARG-1, 16001-1-AP, Proteintech), angiogenic factor 1 (AGGF1, 11889-1-AP, Proteintech), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), AKT (60203-2-Ig, Proteintech), and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT, 28731-1-AP, Proteintech). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies, enhanced chemiluminescence (Vazyme, E422) was used for detection.

Protein sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

MSC-EV and EMPA-EV were isolated and extracted, then enzymatically digested for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis. DIA data were analyzed using the DIA-NN software with a non-prespecified spectral library strategy, and matched against the UniProt human protein database. Protein quantification was standardized by the MaxLFQ algorithm. Significantly differentially expressed proteins (SDPs) between groups were defined as those with fold change > 1.2, Student’s t-test P < 0.05, and Benjamini-Hochberg correction q < 0.05. The overall distribution of proteins was visualized using a volcano plot. Gene Ontology functional enrichment analysis was further conducted.

Plasmid transfection

When the cell confluence reached 60%, the human AGGF1 overexpression plasmid (AGGF1-OE, RefSeq: NM_018046.5) and empty vector negative control (NC-OE) were transfected into MSCs using riboFECT™ CP transfection reagent. Meanwhile, small interfering RNA targeting AGGF1 (siAGGF1) and non-targeting negative control siRNA (siNC) were transfected into EMPA-preconditioned MSCs. The AGGF1-OE plasmid, siAGGF1 and their corresponding negative controls were obtained from Origene (SC127530, China) and RiboBio (China). Transfection was performed using riboFECT™ CP transfection reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. The culture supernatants were then collected for subsequent EV isolation. The target sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Statistics

Continuous variables were compared between two groups using the independent sample t-test. For the comparison among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance was used. All data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

EMPA pretreatment enhances MSCs proliferation and inhibits senescence

Flow cytometry showed high expression of CD44, CD105 and CD73 (positive rate > 95%), but not expression of CD45, CD31, or CD34 in MSCs cultured in vitro (Fig. 1a). CCK-8 and mitochondrial membrane potential detection results indicated that a treatment with 500 nM EMPA for 48 h could significantly enhance the viability of MSCs and maintain mitochondrial function (Fig. 1b-d). Therefore, this dosage was established as the standard for subsequent experiments. EdU staining and scratch assays found that compared with the control group, EMPA-pretreated MSCs (EMPA-MSCs) presented significantly stronger abilities to proliferate and migrate (P < 0.01; Fig. 1e-f, i-j). Flow cytometry cycle analysis revealed that the proportion of cells in the G0/G1 phase increased significantly in the EMPA group (Fig. 1g, k). SA-β-GAL activity detection showed that EMPA could significantly inhibit cellular senescence (Fig. 1h, l). RNA sequencing analysis indicated that in EMPA-MSCs, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly enriched in processes such as cell cycle, senescence regulation, and DNA replication, and signaling pathways including Hippo and AKT (Fig. 1m-o). The above results consistently confirm that EMPA can promote the proliferation and inhibit the senescence of MSCs.

Preparation and characterization of extracellular vesicles

EVs were isolated from MSCs supernatants using differential centrifugation (Fig. 2a). Transmission electron microscopy revealed that both groups of EVs exhibited a typical cup-shaped morphology, and nanoparticle size detection indicated that the size and concentration distribution of EVs were similar between two groups (Fig. 2b-c). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in total protein concentration, and particle-to-protein ratio between MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs (Fig. 2d-f, Supplementary Table 4). Western blot analysis showed that specific marker proteins, such as TSG101, CD63, and CD81, were expressed in both groups of samples (Fig. 2e). After incubating Dil-labeled EVs with H9c2 cells, HUVECs, and RAW264.7 cells for 24 h, respectively, the fluorescent signal was localized in the perinuclear cytoplasmic region (Fig. 2f-h), confirming that EVs had been effectively internalized by the cells. These results suggest that the EVs were successfully extracted, and they can be absorbed and taken up by target cells, and also distribute in the cytoplasm to exert biological functions.

Quantitative UPLC-MS/MS was performed to determine EMPA concentrations in culture supernatant, EMPA-preconditioned MSCs and EMPA-EVs. The quantitative results showed that EMPA reached approximately 0.2 µg/mL in cell culture supernatant, and the intracellular EMPA level was around 0.05 ng per 10⁶ MSC cells, confirming successful drug uptake by MSCs during preconditioning. Notably, EMPA signals were undetectable and below the lower limit of quantification in EMPA-EVs (Supplementary Fig. 1a–e, Supplementary Table 5), effectively ruling out the possibility that EMPA itself directly exerts pharmacological effects.

EMPA-EVs inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promote angiogenesis, and regulate macrophage phenotypic transformation

Based on the established therapeutic efficacy ranges from in vitro cardiovascular studies (ranging from 2 to 100 µg/mL) [24, 25, 26], we selected EVs at a concentration of 50 µg/mL in cell-based experiments. Previous studies have confirmed that extracellular vesicles derived from MSCs can protect cardiomyocytes from ischemia and hypoxia by inhibiting apoptosis [27]. In our in vitro experiments, H9c2 cardiomyocytes were treated with H/SD and co-cultured with MSC-EVs or EMPA-EVs for 24 h. Flow cytometry showed that the rates of apoptotic cardiomyocytes in both MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs groups were significantly lower than that in the PBS group (Fig. 3a-b). Western blot analysis revealed that compared with the PBS group, the expression of pro-apoptotic protein BAX was significantly down-regulated, while the expression of anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly up-regulated in both MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs groups (Fig. 3c, f-g). JC-1 and TMRE detections confirmed that both MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs groups showed significantly increased mitochondrial membrane potential, compared with the PBS group (Fig. 3d-e, h-i). Notably, EMPA-EVs were significantly superior to MSC-EVs in inhibiting apoptosis and improving mitochondrial function (P < 0.001), suggesting that EMPA-EVs have a stronger anti-cardiomyocyte apoptosis effect.

Under serum starvation conditions, compared with PBS, either MSC-EVs or EMPA-EVs significantly increased the proportion of EdU-positive HUVECs (Fig. 3i, m) and enhanced cell migration ability (Fig. 3k, n). In addition, compared with PBS, EVs significantly increased the number of tube-forming junctions and grids of HUVECs and promoted the formation of tubular structures (Fig. 3l, o). Meanwhile, quantitative analysis found that EMPA-EVs were significantly superior to MSC-EVs in increasing cell proliferation, migration and angiogenesis indicators (P < 0.001), suggesting that EMPA-EVs have a stronger ability to promote angiogenesis.

RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS to establish an inflammatory model. Flow cytometry showed that both EV groups significantly reduced CD80⁺ M1 macrophages and increased CD206⁺ M2 macrophages, with the most prominent effect in the EMPA-EV group (Fig. 4a-f). The opposite changes in CD80⁺ and CD206⁺ populations confirm that EMPA-EVs promote macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that compared with PBS, both EVs treatments repressed the expression of pro-inflammatory genes, including iNOS, tumor necrosis factor -α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), but boosted the expression of pro-repair genes, including ARG-1 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) (Fig. 4g-k). Supernatant detection showed that the release of NO after EVs treatment was significantly reduced, compared with that in the PBS group (Fig. 4l). Similarly, Western blot analysis indicated that compared with PBS, both EVs treatments significantly decreased iNOS expression and increased ARG-1 expression (Fig. 4m-o). Our experiments revealed that EMPA-EVs had a significantly stronger effect than MSC-EVs in inhibiting pro-inflammatory phenotypic transformation.

EMPA-EVs promotes myocardial repair through shifting macrophages from pro-inflammatory to reparative in rats

To investigate the cardioprotective role of EMPA-EVs, we established a myocardial infarction model in SD rats by permanently ligating the left anterior descending coronary artery. Thirty minutes after model establishment, the model rats were divided into the control group treated with PBS and the experimental groups injected with MSC-EVs or EMPA-EVs (Fig. 5a). Consistent with validated EV doses from past cardiovascular studies, 1–5 × 10⁸ particles [28, 29] or 5 µg per mouse [30] is the standard intramyocardial dose for myocardial infarction models, while a therapeutic dose of 10–50 µg per rat [24, 26] has been established in preclinical rat models. We therefore selected 50 µg EVs in 50 µL for intramyocardial injection, aligning with literature-validated effective doses. At 24 h after model establishment, TTC staining revealed clearly demarcated infarcted areas in the myocardial tissue, confirming the successful establishment of the model (Fig. 5b-c). Meanwhile, DiR-labeled EVs exhibited strong fluorescence signals in the infarcted myocardium, indicating that EVs can be effectively retained at the injured site after intramyocardial injection (Fig. 5d).

The results showed that, compared with the PBS control group, the LVEF and LVFS in the MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs groups were significantly increased (Fig. 6a-c), indicating that EVs could effectively improve myocardial systolic function. AGGF1 encapsulated in EMPA-EVs At least partially mediates their cardioprotective effects

To further elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms through which EMPA-EVs promote cardiac repair, we performed 4D proteomic analyses on MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs. Hierarchical clustering revealed that the protein profiles of EMPA-EVs were markedly distinct from those of MSC-EVs (Fig. 7a). A volcano plot demonstrated that there were 49 differentially expressed proteins between the MSC-EVs and EMPA-EVs groups (P < 0.05, fold change > 1.2), with 30 proteins up-regulated and 19 down-regulated (Fig. 7b). Compared with the down-regulated proteins and the overall proteome (Supplementary Fig. 2a–b), the enrichment analysis of biological processes associated with up-regulated proteins was more consistent with the anticipated research direction. Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in biological processes such as negative regulation of inflammatory response, negative regulation of T cell activation, negative regulation of lymphocyte activation, negative regulation of leukocyte cell-cell adhesion, regulation of endothelial cell proliferation and some potentially related biological processes. (Fig. 7c). It is worth noting that AGGF1 (a vascular growth factor with a G domain and FHA domain) is a potent vascular growth factor and is gradually emerging as a new type of anti-inflammatory factor in the fields of cardiovascular and nervous systems. The function of AGGF1 is highly correlated with the biological direction we are exploring, and the expression level of this protein in EMPA-EVs is more than twice that of MSC-EVs in proteomics analysis. To further investigate whether the highly expressed AGGF1 in EMPA-EVs mediates the anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and pro-angiogenic effects of EMPA-EVs, we first validated the efficiency of AGGF1 overexpression and knockdown in MSCs via RT‑qPCR and Western blot. RT‑qPCR analysis revealed that AGGF1 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in the AGGF1-OE group compared with the NC-OE group (Fig. 7f). Western blot further confirmed the marked upregulation of AGGF1 protein expression in the AGGF1-OE group (Fig. 7h-i). Three independent siRNA sequences targeting AGGF1 were screened, and siAGGF1−2 with the optimal silencing efficiency was selected for subsequent experiments (decreasing AGGF1 mRNA to approximately 32% of the siNC group) (Fig. 7g). Western blot analysis further verified that siAGGF1−2 markedly downregulated AGGF1 protein expression (Fig. 7j-k). Subsequently, EVs were isolated from these genetically modified MSCs and co-cultured with macrophages, cardiomyocytes, and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), respectively, to explore the role of AGGF1 in EMPA-EVs-mediated cardioprotective effects.

In the LPS-induced M1-type macrophage model, Western blot results showed that compared with the control, MSC-EVs overexpressing AGGF1 (MSC-EVsAGGF1−OE) significantly inhibited iNOS expression, increased ARG-1 level, and enhanced AKT phosphorylation. This effect was reversed by the AKT inhibitor Ipatasertib, as shown by the rebounded expression of pro-inflammatory factors (Fig. 7l-m, p-q). The anti-inflammatory effect of EMPA-EVs silencing AGGF1 (EMPA-EVsiAGGF1) was weakened, and AKT phosphorylation was inhibited (Fig. 7n-o). The anti-inflammatory effect of EMPA-EVsiAGGF1 was salvaged after treatment with AKT agonists, evidenced by a significant enhancement of AKT phosphorylation (Fig. 7r-s). In conclusion, in an inflammatory environment, EMPA-EVs turn macrophages from pro-inflammatory M1 to pro-repair M2 by activating the AKT pathway through AGGF1.

Further experiments were conducted to explore the functional effects of EVs rich in AGGF1 on cardiomyocytes and endothelial cells. MSC-EVsAGGF1−OE significantly improved the mitochondrial function of H9c2 cells under hypoxia/serum deprivation conditions (Fig. 8a-b), and inhibited their apoptosis (Fig. 8e-f), which was further verified at the protein level, accompanied by the upregulation of a p-AKT level (Fig. 8i-j). EMPA-EV siAGGF1 significantly discounted the protective effect of EMPA-EVs, as displayed by their weaker mitochondrial function (Fig. 8c-d) and anti-apoptotic ability (Fig. 8g-h), as well as a lower level of p-AKT (Fig. 8k-l). MSC-EVs AGGF1−OE significantly enhanced the tube-forming ability (Fig. 8m-n) and proliferative activity (Fig. 8q-r) of serum-starved HUVECs, while EMPA-EVsiAGGF1 inhibited the angiogenic (Fig. 8o-p) and proliferative ability (Fig. 8s-t) of EMPA-EVs. Previous studies have found that AGGF1 activates the FAK-Src-AKT angiogenesis pathway through interacting with the functional angiogenic domains of integrins α5 and β131, while AKT phosphorylation synergistically promotes the angiogenesis by enhancing endothelial cell viability and lumen-forming ability [32]. In Western blot analysis, we also found that MSC-EVsAGGF1−OE significantly upregulated the expression of p-AKT and its downstream anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 (Fig. 8u-v). EMPA-EVsiAGGF1 significantly inhibited the protein levels of p-AKT and Bcl-2 (Fig. 8w-x).

Discussion

Myocardial infarction, a cardiovascular emergency with high morbidity, disability and mortality, has a complex pathogenesis often involving acute coronary occlusion that leads to ischemic and hypoxic necrosis of cardiomyocytes, triggering cardiac dysfunction or even heart failure [33]. Existing measures, such as reperfusion and drug therapies, have significantly improved patients’ short-term prognosis [34], but post-MI issues, such as excessive inflammatory responses, massive cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and insufficient angiogenesis, still hinder cardiac repair and functional recovery [35, 36], underscoring the necessity of exploration for safe and effective new strategies.

This study systematically investigated the role of EMPA-EVs in myocardial repair, with the main findings including: (1) EMPA pretreatment enhances the proliferation of MSCs and inhibits their senescence; (2) EMPA-EVs inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promote angiogenesis, and regulate macrophage phenotypic transformation; (3) EMPA-EVs shift macrophages from pro-inflammatory to reparative in rats after MI; and (4) AGGF1 encapsulated in EMPA-EVs at least partially mediates their cardioprotective effect (Fig. 9f). These findings not only refresh the understanding of the roles of EMPA and extracellular vesicles in myocardial repair, but also provide experimental basis and theoretical support for the development of EMPA-EVs-based new therapies for myocardial infarction.

The biological activity and function of MSCs, as the primary donor cells for EVs, directly determine the quality and therapeutic efficacy of their secreted EVs. In the present work, we demonstrated that EMPA preconditioning remarkably remodels the intrinsic phenotype of MSCs: it enhances MSC proliferation and inhibits cellular senescence, accompanied by the regulation of cell cycle. Correspondingly, EMPA-EVs acquire enhanced functions: they suppress cardiomyocyte apoptosis, facilitate angiogenesis and modulate macrophage polarization, leading to improved cardioprotective effects.

Beyond their direct involvement in tissue repair, MSCs also participate in intercellular communication via EV-mediated transfer of bioactive molecules, such as growth factors, cytokines, lipids, and RNAs [5, 37]. In various pathological conditions, including brain injury [38], acute lung injury [39], inflammatory bowel disease [40], liver fibrosis [41], acute kidney injury [42], and bone injury [43], MSC-derived EVs contribute to immune modulation, tissue protection, and regenerative processes. Recent advances in EV engineering, including small molecule pretreatment [21], hypoxic preconditioning [44], nanoparticle modification [45], and modulation of EV cargo [46], can ensure a higher therapeutic efficacy and a targeted delivery [47]. Our findings suggest that by activating signaling pathways, such as Hippo and AKT, to regulate cell cycle and senescence-related genes, EMPA can be introduced to improve the in vitro expansion of MSCs and extend their active lifespan, thereby offering a possibility for large-scale production of highly active EVs.

During the early phase of MI, necrotic cardiomyocytes release damage-associated molecular patterns that can rely on Toll-like receptors to activate macrophages and polarize them toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. This polarization elevates the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, along with reactive oxygen species [48]. While this response facilitates the clearance of necrotic tissue, the silence of adequate anti-inflammatory factors may sustain inflammation and impair tissue healing. In the acute and subacute stages of MI, the enrichment of IL-4 and TGF-β in the cardiac microenvironment, along with Galectin-9 secretion by damaged cardiomyocytes, promote macrophage polarization towards the reparative M2 phenotype via PI3K/AKT signaling [49]. M2 macrophages then release anti-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-10 and ARG-1, to resolve inflammation, promote angiogenesis, and remodel extracellular matrix [50]. Persistent or excessive inflammation can lead to cardiomyocyte apoptosis and infarct expansion, thereby impeding the healing process. Thus, the transition from M1 to M2 macrophages is expected to facilitate myocardial recovery. Here, our study revealed the multiple roles of EMPA-EVs in inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promoting angiogenesis, and regulating macrophage phenotypic transformation, all rendering their advantages as a candidate therapy for myocardial repair. Compared with non-pretreated MSC-EVs, EMPA-EVs bring with comprehensive effects on the pathological processes after MI by synergistically regulating inflammatory balance, cell survival, and angiogenesis. In particular, they drive the transformation of macrophages to set up a favorable microenvironment for tissue repair, which may particularly explain their superiority over traditional MSC-EVs.

Despite ongoing advancements in clinical management of AMI, long-term cardiac recovery is still challenged by irreversible cardiomyocyte loss, microvascular dysfunction, and progressive ventricular remodeling. In the present rat model of MI, EMPA-EVs improved cardiac contractile function, reduced fibrosis, and transformed the immune microenvironment, further verifying their therapeutic potential in vivo. The increase in left ventricular ejection fraction and reduction in fibrosis area directly reflected the restorations in cardiac structure and function, while the anti-inflammatory transition of macrophage phenotypes allowed a long-term inhibition on disease progression. These results support EMPA-EVs as a novel multi-targeted therapeutic tool for MI, with anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and pro-regenerative properties.

Our results further support that AGGF1, a component enriched in EMPA-EVs, mediates cardioprotective effects by activating the AKT signaling pathway across various cell types. AGGF1 (formerly VG5Q) is a novel angiogenic regulator originally identified as the first disease-causing gene in Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome, a rare congenital vascular disorder characterized by capillary malformation, venous abnormality, and tissue hypertrophy [51]. As a potent angiogenic regulator, AGGF1 exhibits stronger pro-angiogenic activity than vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in endothelial cells, and specifically binds to integrin α5β1 to trigger the downstream FAK-Src-AKT signaling cascade [31]. Mechanistically, the AGGF1/PI3K-AKT axis serves as the core signaling pathway for its biological functions, independent of the VEGF pathway [52]. Beyond angiogenesis, AGGF1 exerts therapeutic effects by activating autophagy [53], maintaining vascular integrity [52], and producing anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway [54, 55]. In cardiovascular disease models, AGGF1 acts as a critical protective factor: it attenuates myocardial infarction and ischemia-reperfusion injury by promoting angiogenesis and inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis [52, 56] and ameliorates cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure by alleviating cardiomyocyte apoptosis caused by endoplasmic reticulum stress [57]. Interestingly, the therapeutic benefits of AGGF1 have also been observed in models of ischemic retinopathy [58], diabetic vascular dysfunction [59], cachexia-induced muscle wasting [60], cerebral ischemic injury [61], and pulpitis [55], advocating its broad anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic capabilities.

Of note, the present study focuses on EMPA-preconditioned MSC-EVs with remodeled endogenous cargo, rather than engineered EVs designed as drug delivery nanocarriers. As verified by UPLC-MS/MS, EMPA was undetectable in purified EVs, indicating that MSCs were mainly phenotypically modulated during EMPA incubation, and no intentional drug loading into EVs occurred. Functionally, the cardioprotective capacities of EMPA-EVs are at least partially mediated by the upregulation of AGGF1 in EV cargo. As widely acknowledged, therapeutic cargo loading strategies are categorized into passive loading and active loading. Passive loading relies on the spontaneous incorporation of the drug into the vesicles during the vesicle formation process, either by interacting with the parental cells or by directly co-culturing with the EVs; this approach is simple but suffers from low efficiency, poor controllability, and non-specific packaging [62]. In contrast, active loading achieves direct cargo encapsulation into isolated EVs by enhancing membrane permeability through sonication, electroporation, heat shock, or detergent treatment, with higher efficiency and better dosage control [63, 64]. Collectively, these methods aim to develop EVs as targeted drug delivery systems, which is a distinct research direction beyond the scope of the current study.

This study has certain limitations: first, commercial cell lines cannot fully simulate the physiological characteristics of primary cardiomyocytes, and the use of primary cardiomyocyte or organoid models could enhance the reliability of in vitro studies. Second, proteomics suggests the presence of pleiotropic cardioprotective proteins in EMPA-EVs, but their specific functions have not been verified, and the downstream effector molecules and regulatory networks of the AGGF1/AKT axis still require in-depth analysis. Moreover, the current research is based on animal models, and the pharmacokinetic characteristics, optimal dosage and long-term safety of EMPA-EVs in humans need to be further verified by preclinical and clinical studies.

Conclusion

EMPA-EVs exert therapeutic effects against MI by upregulating AGGF1, underscoring their potential as a cell-free therapeutic strategy. Future research should optimize their preparation and delivery to explore the translational potential of EMPA-EVs in the treatment of MI.
