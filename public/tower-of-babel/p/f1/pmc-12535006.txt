PtHSF5, a HSF transcription factor from Pinellia Ternata positively regulates heat tolerance in Arabidopsis
Authors: Qiujie Chao, Jing Peng, Qian You, Chen Bo, Ru Wang, Yanfang Zhu, Yongbo Duan, Xingkui Tao, Jianping Xue, Tao Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPinellia ternata (Thunb.) Breit., a traditional Chinese medicinal herb, frequently experiences ‘sprout tumble’ under heat stress during growth, significantly compromising its yield potential. While heat shock transcription factors (HSFs) are known to play critical roles in plant thermotolerance regulation, the functional characterization of HSFs in P. ternata remains unexplored.ResultsIn this study, we identified a heat-inducible PtHSF5 that exhibits tissue-specific expression dominance in petioles and leaves. Subcellular localization analysis revealed nuclear confinement of PtHSF5, which notably lacks intrinsic transcriptional activation capability. Transgenic Arabidopsis overexpressing PtHSF5 demonstrated enhanced seed germination rates, primary root elongation, and seedling survival under thermal stress, indicating its positive regulatory role in thermotolerance. Mechanistic investigations revealed that PtHSF5 overexpression significantly increased enzymatic activities of key antioxidant enzymes - peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) - while effectively reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation under heat stress. Transcriptome profiling further demonstrated PtHSF5-mediated regulation of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway genes, suggesting its potential role in enhancing ROS scavenging capacity through modulation of this metabolic pathway, thereby improving heat stress adaptation.ConclusionThis study provides novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying heat stress adaptation in P. ternata and establishes PtHSF5 as a promising candidate for improving thermotolerance in medicinal plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07370-4.

Background

With the intensification of global climate change, environmental variations are exerting increasingly significant impacts on plant growth and development [1]. Temperature fluctuation stands out as one of the most direct environmental factors affecting plant physiology. Under the influence of greenhouse effects, it is predicted that high-temperature stress will become more intense and frequent, with global temperatures projected to rise by 1.8–4.0 °C by the end of this century [2]. Increased temperatures have significant adverse effects on plant growth. For example, high temperatures accelerate plant water loss, diminish photosynthetic capacity, and affect plant growth and metabolism [3]. Zhao et al. (2017) have indicated that each 1 °C increase in temperature could lead to a reduction of over 3% in crop yields [4].

To survive, plants have evolved complex and highly efficient regulatory networks to combat heat stress, with heat shock factors (HSFs)-mediated transcriptional regulation playing a pivotal role in plant thermotolerance responses [5–7]. Numerous HSF family members have been identified across plant species, including 82 in Triticum aestivum (wheat) [8], 26 in Oryza sativa (rice) [9], and 26 in Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) [10]. Functioning as terminal components in the stress-responsive gene expression signaling cascade, HSF transcription factors mediate the activation of heat-responsive genes and extensively participate in regulating plant adaptation to thermal stress [6, 7]. Research demonstrated that four AtHSFA1 subclass genes (AtHSFA1a/b/d/e) in Arabidopsis govern over 65% of heat stress-inducible genes, with the quadruple mutant exhibiting hypersensitivity to moderate-to-high temperature conditions [11]. Furthermore, over-expression of TaHSFA6f in Triticum aestivum (wheat) improves its heat tolerance [12], whereas White clover HSFB2a negatively regulates thermotolerance in Arabidopsis [13]. Notably, Li et al. (2024) demonstrated that ZmHSF4 and ZmHSF20 function as positive and negative regulators, respectively, in maize heat stress responses. Intriguingly, ZmHSF4 operates downstream of ZmHSF20, collectively forming an interconnected regulatory network for maize thermotolerance adaptation [14].

Pinellia ternata, a herbaceous plant of the Araceae family, is widely distributed in China, South Korea, and Japan, with a long history of medicinal use [15, 16]. The tuber of P. ternata serves as its medicinal organ, containing various bioactive compounds including alkaloids, polysaccharides, organic acids, flavonoids, and sterols [17, 18]. These constituents exhibit pharmacological effects such as antitussive, antiemetic, antitumor, and antiviral activities [19]. With the continuous discovery of its expanding medicinal properties, the market demand for P. ternata has been steadily increasing. However, its growth is particularly temperature-sensitive, the plant exhibits ‘sprout tumble’ phenomenon when ambient temperatures exceed 30 °C, which severely constrains yield improvement [20]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying heat-induced ‘sprout tumble’ will facilitate variety enhancement of P. ternata through molecular breeding approaches. Tian et al. (2022) demonstrated that heterologous expression of the PtsHSP17.2 gene from P. ternata in tobacco significantly enhanced thermotolerance in transgenic plants, suggesting its potential role in heat resistance regulation [21]. Functional validation through overexpression lines and gene-edited knockdown mutants of PtSAD confirmed its role as a negative regulator in high-temperature stress signaling [22]. Recent studies identified PtWRKY2 and PtNAC66 as positive regulatory transcription factors involved in heat stress responses [23, 24]. And omics-based studies have revealed that long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs) also participate in regulating the plant’s response to heat stress [25, 26]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying heat stress adaptation in P. ternata remain poorly characterized.

A recent research systematically identified 22 PtHSF genes through genomic analysis and performed comprehensive bioinformatics characterization [27]. Expression profiling prioritized PtHSF5 for functional investigation of heat stress responses in Arabidopsis thaliana. Transcriptomic profiling further elucidated its regulatory network governing thermotolerance. These findings provide novel insights into heat stress adaptation mechanisms in P. ternata, establishing a foundation for future molecular breeding strategies.

Results

PtHSF5 encodes an HSFA subclass member in P. ternata

Based on reported information regarding the HSF family in P. ternata, the heat-inducible gene PtHSF5 was isolated from this species [27]. This gene contains a 1065-bp open reading frame encoding 354 amino acids, with a predicted molecular weight of 38.99 kDa and an isoelectric point of 4.78. Phylogenetic analysis classified PtHSF5 within the HSFA subfamily, demonstrating close evolutionary relationships with AtHSFA2 (Figure S1).

Expression profiling of PtHSF5 in P. ternata

Quantitative real- time PCR (qRT-PCR) based tissue-specific expression profiling revealed that PtHSF5 is ubiquitously expressed across all examined tissues of P. ternata, with relatively low expression levels in floral tissues and the highest expression observed in petioles, approximately three-fold higher than in floral tissues. Intermediate expression levels were detected in leaves and roots (Fig. 1A). Under heat stress, PtHSF5 expression was significantly induced, reaching a 23-fold increase within 1 h of treatment compared to the control. To determine the subcellular localization of PtHSF5, transient expression assays were conducted in tobacco epidermal cells using GFP-tagged constructs. To assess the transcriptional activation activity of PtHSF5, yeast two-hybrid assays were performed using recombinant plasmids (pGBKT7-PtHSF5), a positive control (pGBKT7-53 + pGADT7-T), and a negative control (pGBKT7). All transformed yeast strains grew on single dropout medium (SD/-Trp), but only the positive control grew on triple dropout medium (SD/-Trp-Leu-His), indicating that PtHSF5 lacks intrinsic transcriptional activation activity (Fig. 2B).

PtHSF5 enhances thermotolerance in Arabidopsis

To investigate the function of PtHSF5 in heat stress response, wild type (WT) and three independent PtHSF5-overexpressing Arabidopsis lines (OE-1, OE-2, OE-4) were selected for phenotypic analysis (Fig. 3A). Under normal conditions, seed germination rates were indistinguishable between WT and transgenic Lines, approaching 100%. However, under heat stress, germination rates declined in all genotypes, with WT showing reductions to 76.8% (2 days) and 11.5% (3 days), while transgenic lines exhibited significantly higher germination rates than WT (Fig. 3B, C). Root elongation assays revealed that transgenic Lines exhibited an average 11.2% increase in root length compared to WT under non-stress conditions. PtHSF5 enhances reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity under heat stress

As shown in Fig. 5A, heat stress intensified the staining intensity of both DAB and NBT. No significant differences in staining patterns were observed between WT and transgenic Arabidopsis lines under normal conditions. Further investigation of antioxidant enzyme activities demonstrated comparable peroxidase (POD) levels across all lines under non-stressed conditions, while catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities were significantly elevated in transgenic lines relative to WT (Fig. 5B). Heat stress induced increased activities of all three enzymes across genotypes, with transgenic lines exhibiting substantially higher POD, CAT, and SOD activities than WT following stress treatment. These results suggest that PtHSF5 may mitigate heat-induced ROS accumulation through enhanced activation of key antioxidant enzymes, particularly POD, CAT, and SOD.

PtHSF5 modulates expression of heat stress-responsive genes in Arabidopsis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which PtHSF5 enhances thermotolerance in Arabidopsis, transcriptomic sequencing analysis was performed on WT and transgenic line OE1 materials exposed to 42 °C stress for 2 h. Comparative transcriptomic analysis identified 179 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the OE1 Line, comprising 101 upregulated and 78 downregulated genes (Fig. 6 A). KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed significant enrichment in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, which enriched 7 genes (Fig. 6B). Simultaneously, GO enrichment analysis was performed on the DEGs, revealing a total of 38 enriched categories across three major classifications: biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. Notably, among these pathways were the “response to stimulus” and “antioxidant activity” categories (Fig. 6 C). The “response to stimulus” pathway included 56 genes, while the “antioxidant activity” pathway contained 5 genes (Table S1 and S2). Discussion

To cope with environmental stresses, plants have evolved sophisticated and efficient regulatory networks, in which transcription factor-mediated regulation plays a crucial role [3, 28]. HSFs, one of the most prevalent transcription factor families in plants, are extensively involved in diverse stress responses including heat shock, salinity, drought, and oxidative stress [6, 7]. With the persistent global temperature rise, heat stress has emerged as a critical limiting factor affecting the development of the P. ternata industry [15, 20]. Recent studies have identified several transcription factors (PtWRKY2, PtNAC66, PtMYB77, and PtHD-Zip6) participating in the regulation of heat stress responses in P. ternata [23, 24, 29, 30]. Specifically addressing HSFs, You et al. have identified 22 HSF members in P. ternata, categorized into HSFA, HSFB, and HSFC subfamilies [27]. However, their functional roles in regulating heat stress responses in this species remain uncharacterized.

One of the HSF members, PtHSF5, was cloned for functional analysis under heat stress. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that PtHSF5 is closely related to Arabidopsis AtHSFA2 and is classified as a Class A HSF member. Ogawa et al. demonstrated that overexpression of AtHSFA2 significantly enhanced basal and acquired thermotolerance in transgenic lines, whereas its mutant lines in Arabidopsis exhibited markedly reduced heat tolerance [31]. The expression of HSFs in many plants is either upregulated or downregulated under heat stress. For instance, the transcriptional level of HSFA2 in wheat is strongly induced by heat stress [32], while the expression of HSFC1a in rice is suppressed under similar conditions [9]. Consistent with the expression patterns of Class A HSF members in other species, PtHSF5 expression was significantly induced by heat stress, suggesting its potential role in regulating the heat stress response in P. ternata.

Heat stress typically inhibits plant growth and accelerates senescence. This study demonstrated that PtHSF5 enhances seed germination rate and primary root elongation in Arabidopsis under heat stress conditions while improving survival rates, suggesting PtHSF5 may regulate thermotolerance responses in P. ternata. The observed functions of PtHSF5 align with previous reports on Class A HSF members in other species [31, 33, 34], further supporting the critical regulatory role of Class A HSFs in plant heat stress responses. Abiotic stress in plants triggers excessive ROS accumulation, leading to oxidative damage [35, 36]. HSFs participate in ROS signaling regulation, with a study showing AtHSFA7b can induce ROS scavenging to reduce accumulation and enhance salt tolerance in Arabidopsis [37]. Notably, heat stress-induced sprout tumble in P. ternata is closely associated with ROS accumulation. Our findings reveal that PtHSF5 reduces ROS accumulation in transgenic lines under heat stress while increasing POD, CAT, and SOD enzymatic activities. These results indicate PtHSF5 likely enhances thermotolerance through mediating ROS signaling pathways.

Under various environmental stresses, plants enhance their stress responses by modulating multiple metabolic pathways. The phenylpropanoid pathway plays a crucial regulatory role in plant growth, stress resistance, and ROS scavenging [38]. Xu et al. (2022) demonstrated that OsGRP3 enhances drought tolerance in rice through modifying phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and promoting lignin accumulation [39]. Additionally, phenolic compounds in Festuca brachyphylla are induced by heat stress, with elevated phenolic levels contributing to improved thermotolerance [40]. Our findings revealed that PtHSF5 significantly induces enrichment of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway genes in Arabidopsis under heat stress. This observation aligns with Fu et al. (2022), who identified PhHSF19 as a regulator of phenylpropanoid synthesis in Petunia hybrida [41]. We hypothesize that PtHSF5 may enhance Arabidopsis thermotolerance through regulating phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, which is consistent with Wang et al.‘s (2024) findings demonstrating phenylpropanoid pathway involvement in heat stress responses in Rosa hybrida [42].

The genes enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, particularly AT2G37130, AT2G38380, and AT2G38390 are broadly involved in regulating diverse stress responses in plants and modulate ROS accumulation under adverse stress conditions [43–45]. In the PtHSF5 transgenic lines, these genes demonstrated significant heat stress-induced up-regulation, while PtHSF5 exhibited marked suppression of SRG2 expression, a senescence-associated gene in Arabidopsis [46]. We hypothesize that PtHSF5 may enhance ROS accumulation in P. ternata under thermal stress through transcriptional regulation of phenylpropanoid biosynthetic genes. This mechanism potentially decelerates senescence progression and consequently improves thermotolerance adaptation in P. ternata.

Conclusions

This study identified a heat-induced HSFA member, PtHSF5, in P. ternata, which localizes to the nucleus. Functional analysis demonstrated that overexpression of PtHSF5 enhances thermotolerance in Arabidopsis, as evidenced by improved seed germination, primary root elongation under heat stress, along with increased seedling survival rates and antioxidant enzyme activities. RNA-seq analysis revealed 179 DEGs, with significant enrichment of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway genes. These findings suggest that PtHSF5 may enhance heat tolerance by regulating the expression of genes in this pathway and reducing ROS accumulation under thermal stress. To our knowledge, this represents the first functional characterization of an HSF member in P. ternata under heat stress, providing valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying thermotolerance in this medicinal plant.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and experimental treatments

The original P. ternata, identified by Prof. Jianping Xue (Huaibei Normal University), were obtained from the wild without any specifically permissive requirement. Uniform-sized P. ternata tubers preserved in the laboratory were planted in nutrient soil-filled trays and cultivated in a greenhouse under controlled conditions: temperature maintained at 25 °C, Light intensity of 35 µmol·mm-²·s-¹, and a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark. Upon reaching the flowering stage, flowers, leaves, petioles, and roots were separately collected. Additionally, flowering-stage plants were subjected to 42 °C heat stress. Whole-plant samples were collected at 0, 1, 4, 12, 24, and 48 h post-treatment. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates. Collected tissues were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

WT Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0 ecotype) and transgenic lines (T3 generation homozygous plants) were cultivated at 23 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a Light intensity of 50 µmol·m-²·s-¹. Heat stress treatments (42 °C) were applied to assess seed germination, root elongation, and seedling survival rates, following the methodology described by Liu et al. (2024) [23].

Construction of the PtHSF5 overexpression vector and generation of Transgenic Arabidopsis plants

Based on the 1,065 bp full-length coding sequence (CDS) of PtHSF5, specific primers PtHSF5-F and PtHSF5-R (Table S1) were designed for PCR amplification. The amplified CDS was sequenced, and error-free clones were ligated into the pCAMBIA1301 vector via KpnⅠ and Hind III restriction sites to generate the PtHSF5 overexpression vector. The constructed vector was introduced into WT Arabidopsis using the Agrobacterium-mediated floral dip method. Mature seeds from transgenic plants were collected, surface-sterilized, and germinated on 1/2 MS medium containing 50 mg·mL−1 hygromycin B for selection of positive transformants. Homozygous T3 transgenic lines were obtained through successive generations of screening.

Subcellular localization analysis

The full-length PtHSF5 CDS was amplified using primers PtHSF5-GFP-F and PtHSF5-GFP-R (Table S1) and subsequently cloned into the pCAMBIA1305-GFP vector via homologous recombination to generate the recombinant plasmid 35 S-PtHSF5-GFP. Both the recombinant plasmid (35 S-PtHSF5-GFP) and the empty vector (pCAMBIA1305-GFP) were introduced into Agrobacterium strain GV3101 and transiently expressed in epidermal cells of Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via agroinfiltration. After 48 h of incubation, GFP fluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Motic PA53 FS6, Hong Kong SAR, China).

Transcriptional activation activity assay

The PtHSF5 CDS was amplified with primers PtHSF5-BK-F and PtHSF5-BK-R (Table S1) and cloned into the pGBKT7 vector to generate the recombinant plasmid PtHSF5-pGBKT7. Yeast strain Y2HGold co-transformed with pGBKT7-53 and pGADT7-T served as the positive control, while yeast transformed with empty pGBKT7 vector was used as the negative control. For the experimental group, PtHSF5-pGBKT7 and empty pGADT7 were co-transformed into yeast. Transformed yeast strains were plated on SD/−Trp and SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His media respectively, and incubated at 30 °C for 3–5 days. Transcriptional activation activity of PtHSF5 was evaluated based on colony growth.

ROS staining and antioxidant enzyme activity assays

Three-week-old WT and transgenic Arabidopsis plants were subjected to 42 °C heat stress. Leaves of uniform size were collected before and 6 h after stress treatment for ROS staining and enzyme activity analysis. H2O2 and O2- accumulation were assessed via DAB and NBT staining, respectively. SOD, POD, and CAT activities were measured using methods described by Xu et al. (2023) [47].

Gene expression analysis

Semi-quantitative PCR was performed to detect PtHSF5 expression in WT and transgenic Arabidopsis lines using Arabidopsis Tubulin (AtTUB) as the internal reference gene, following protocols outlined by Xue et al. (2017) [48]. qRT-PCR was employed to analyze PtHSF5 expression in P. ternata and stress-responsive genes in Arabidopsis. The 18 S ribosomal RNA gene (Pt18SrRNA) [49] and AtTUB served as reference genes for P. ternata and Arabidopsis, respectively. Three biological replicates were performed for all experiments. Primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

RNA-seq analysis

Three-week-old WT and OE1 transgenic Arabidopsis Lines were subjected to a 2-hour heat stress treatment at 42 °C. Whole plants were collected before treatment and after 2 h of stress, with three biological replicates for each condition. The collected samples were sent to Genepioneer Biotechnologies Company (Nanjing, China) for RNA library construction and sequencing. Sequencing data were normalized using fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM) mapped reads. Significantly DEGs were identified based on expression levels across samples, applying thresholds of fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 with a p-value < 0.05.

Phylogenetic analysis

The HSF protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana, maize, and tomato were all downloaded from the NCBI database. Phylogenetic tree analysis of PtHSF5 and HSF proteins from the above-mentioned species was conducted using MEGA11 software and the Maximum Likelihood method, with the Bootstrap value set to 1000.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted with three biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA were performed using SPSS statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) to analyze differences between groups. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied, with asterisks (*) or different letters indicating statistically significant differences.
