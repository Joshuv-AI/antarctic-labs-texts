Caregiver Holding, Not Vocalizing, Supports Real‐Time Vagal Regulation
Authors: Megan Micheletti, Anna Madden‐Rusnak, Kaya de Barbaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCaregivers play a critical role in infant physiological regulation. Lab‐based studies show that more responsive mothers have infants that are better regulators, indexed by a decrease in infant respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) during standardized stressors. However, little is known about the specific caregiving behaviors that lead to physiological regulation. This study leverages wearable sensors and ecologically valid observations of mother and infant behavior to identify how spontaneous caregiving behaviors influence real‐time mother and infant parasympathetic regulation following infant distress. N = 41 mother‐infant dyads (mean age 4.6 months) were recruited from an urban North American city. Infants and mothers wore ECG monitors while being video recorded participating in daily routines around their home for 1 hour. Trained RAs annotated infant crying and mothers' regulation behaviors, including holding and speaking to their infant. Contrary to laboratory findings, mothers did not respond immediately to their infant's crying in ecologically valid home interactions, with most response latencies around 10 s and some up to 2–3 min. Additionally, not all caregiving behaviors had effects on real‐time physiological regulation. Mothers holding their infant, but not vocalizing, produced significant time‐locked decreases in real‐time mother and infant RSA, corresponding to regulation. Physical touch, but not vocalizations, may scaffold mother and infant physiological regulation in the context of unprompted infant distress. Specific caregiving behaviors actively shape in‐the‐moment physiological arousal and regulation “in the wild.”Keywords: holding, infant crying, parenting, regulation, respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA)

Introduction

Infant distress vocalizations, including fussing and crying, are important signals that work to elicit caregiver attention, proximity, and soothing (Bornstein et al. 2017). Over the first year of life, infants begin to develop their own emotion regulation and coregulation skills through a series of coordinated, sensitive interactions with their caregiver that function to regulate the infant's emotional state (Bernard et al. 2013; Ekas et al. 2018). Caregivers who are more sensitive to their infant's distress cues have infants that develop stronger social‐emotional adjustment and more positive, long‐term mental health outcomes (del Carmen et al. 1993; Leerkes et al. 2009; McElwain and Booth‐LaForce 2006). However, relatively less is known about the specific physiological pathways by which caregiver behavior may influence infant and caregiver physiology and, in turn, scaffold real‐time infant emotion regulation.

Research indicates several parenting behaviors, conserved across cultures and mammalian species, that are associated with infant regulation or reductions in infant distress (Bornstein et al. 2017). Namely, mother's vocalizations directed to her infant (Jahromi et al. 2004) and picking up and holding her infant (Hunziker and Barr 1986; Jahromi et al. 2004) are the two most common parenting strategies for reducing infant distress (Bornstein et al. 2017). Close physical contact is shown to be one of the most effective interventions for terminating infant crying (Bell and Ainsworth 1972), and caregiver touch attenuates infant distress and the infant physiological stress response during stressful situations, like the still‐face paradigm (Feldman et al. 2010). Soothing touch in particular, like stroking, rocking, patting, and bouncing, promotes physiological regulation during the still‐face paradigm (Van Puyvelde et al. 2021) and in unstructured, ecologically valid home settings (Madden‐Rusnak et al. 2025). More broadly, caregiver touch, warmth, and smell are argued to lay the foundation for mammalian attachment (Hofer 2006). In rodents, maternal licking and grooming in infancy have long‐lasting epigenetic effects on brain structure and function (Champagne 2008). Despite the volume of work illustrating that caregiver vocalizing and holding work to support infant regulation (Mesman et al. 2016), how they shape physiological regulation in the moment remains less clear.

In addition to overt behaviors that support infant emotion regulation, infants and caregivers also engage in specific physiological changes to modulate infant arousal. On the shortest timescale, the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) modulates its activity in accordance with changes in the environment. Changes in vagal tone, a PNS response measured via heart rate variability (HRV) or respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), reflect vagal regulation of the heart when an individual is confronted with a challenge (Porges 2007). Vagal withdrawal is indexed by a decrease in RSA during challenging situations, theorized to allow for a shift in focus from internal demands to generating coping strategies in order to control affective arousal (Porges 2007). Consistent with the polyvagal theory, a moderate decrease in RSA in response to a stressor has been associated with better emotion regulation in young children both concurrently (Morris et al. 2020) and prospectively (Bazhenova et al. 2001; Dale et al. 2011).

Much of the knowledge on infant RSA change in response to a stressor comes from the Face‐to‐Face Still Face (FFSF) paradigm, widely used to study infant reactivity and emotional competence (Jones‐Mason et al. 2018; Tronick et al. 1978). In this controlled experimental task, caregiver and infant first engage in a 2‐min face‐to‐face interaction (play). Next, the caregiver is instructed to stop communicating with the infant and maintain a “still face” for 2 min (still‐face). Then, the caregiver and infant can resume normal face‐to‐face interaction for 2 min (reunion). Results of a large meta‐analysis reveal that on average, infants show a decrease in RSA during the still‐face relative to play, theorized to reflect a stress response and shift in focus from homeostasis to generating coping strategies (Jones‐Mason et al. 2018).

Only recently has evidence emerged from ecologically valid home settings to confirm a similar pattern of vagal withdrawal in response to spontaneous or unprompted stressors. During naturalistic episodes of infant crying in the home, infant RSA also decreases following cry onset (Madden‐Rusnak et al. 2023). However, this study did not control for the possible effect of caregiving behaviors on real‐time RSA change. Thus, although there is evidence that decreases in RSA track behavioral distress and reflect an infant's need to actively engage with the environment, we do not know the effect of real‐time caregiving behaviors on the timing and magnitude of this RSA change.

It is particularly important to study the effects of caregiving behaviors on RSA change in the home because the most common, culturally preserved caregiver responses to infant distress, that is, picking up and holding or vocalizing to the infant, are by design greatly constrained in lab‐based paradigms. Most lab‐based studies constrain natural parenting behaviors when they seat infants and caregivers across from each other in static positions and give instructions not to touch (Adamson and Frick 2003; Tronick et al. 1978). This means that most of the past work on real‐time infant regulation is focused on caregiving behaviors like gaze, vocalization, distraction, or affect changes (Crockenberg and Leerkes 2004; Beebe et al. 2008). These are not common to all cultures (Abu Salih et al. 2023; Bornstein et al. 2015) and may not be representative of how mothers actually regulate their children. Additionally, in structured lab tasks, most caregivers respond to their distressed infant within 3–5 s (Beebe et al. 2008, 2010; van Egeren et al. 2001). At home, these response latencies may be longer and more variable given that caregivers have competing demands on their attention (Edemekong et al. 2023) or may be physically farther away from their infants when they become distressed (Salo et al. 2022).

In addition to studying naturalistic, unconstrained behavior in the home, we are also motivated to examine specific behaviors. Other research has analyzed caregiving responses to infant distress via global ratings, such as maternal sensitivity (Augustine and Leerkes 2019). While such averages or global ratings are valuable, they obscure the temporal effects of specific caregiving behaviors on real‐time regulation (Abney et al. 2025). In the present study, we prioritize examining the effects of specific behaviors (over global ratings) to understand the temporal structure by which specific behaviors (i.e., holding, vocalizing) influence physiological regulation.

Given that there is little work on real‐time physiological regulation, multiple possibilities exist for the direction of dynamic RSA change in response to caregivers' regulatory behaviors. That is, whether caregiving behaviors lead to decreases or increases in infant RSA following infant distress is unclear. Following the vagal regulation hypothesis, one possibility is that we observe an initial decrease in infant RSA following a caregiver's regulatory behavior, as this behavior is actively supporting the infant's engagement with the environment in order to cope (Ostlund et al. 2017). Alternatively, an initial increase in infant RSA could be observed, as this is theorized to reflect an infant's orientation to their social partner (Porges and Furman 2011). Empirical research linking observations of actual parenting behaviors to physiological reactivity in the context of negative infant signals is limited, and we have found no studies to date that examine the link between specific parenting behaviors and physiological regulation following distress in the home.

In addition to infant patterns of RSA change around distress, caregivers also show physiological changes in response to their infant's distress. During structured interactions where infants express distress and caregivers are allowed to respond (e.g., during an infant arm restraint or novel toy task where caregivers can respond to their distressed infant), there is a greater RSA decrease when mothers report higher levels of infant‐oriented cry processing, indicating that they were more tuned into their infant's cues (Leerkes et al. 2016). However, this evidence is from a structured task, and it remains unclear the extent to which regulatory behaviors are associated with time‐locked decreases or increases in mother RSA.

Perhaps most importantly, as regulatory behaviors, physiological arousal, and emotion regulation occur at the real‐time timescale, methods that allow for moment‐by‐moment assessment are necessary to capture those fluctuations (Davis et al. 2018). For example, estimates of RSA that aggregate responses over 30 s or longer are likely averaging out critical information about the multidirectional relationship between physiological and behavioral regulation. For example, emerging evidence suggests that the traditional RSA decrease during the still‐face phase is better characterized by a decrease that starts during play and then increases over the course of the still‐face episode. Although the averages show a significant difference between episodes (i.e., higher in play, lower in still‐face), they miss the precise timing of this change across conditions (Abney, daSilva, and Bertenthal 2021). Leveraging a continuous measure of RSA is necessary to capture dynamic change and reveal patterns of vagal reactivity time‐locked to caregiving behavior.

In the present study, we leverage high‐density wearable‐sensor based ECG measures collected from infants and caregivers as they move freely around their home to produce a dynamic RSA time series for analysis. Broadly, drawing on polyvagal theory and a dynamic biobehavioral model of infant‐caregiver emotion regulation, we aim to evaluate the behaviors contributing to physiological changes in infants and caregivers during naturalistic episodes of distress in the home.

Aims and Hypotheses

First, we aimed to characterize caregiver responses to infant distress in the home, including the types and latencies of responses. We hypothesized that in the home setting, where caregivers are likely to have competing demands and infant distress is not being intentionally provoked by an experimental task, we will see variability in whether and how caregivers respond to their infants. The second aim was to identify the behavioral mechanisms that influence infant RSA during naturalistic infant distress. During infant distress, we examined the effects of caregiver holding and vocalizations on infant RSA levels. We hypothesized that the onset of each regulatory behavior would predict a time‐locked decrease in infant RSA, suggesting that regulatory behaviors lead to decreases in infant RSA that allow for infant coping. The third aim of this study was to identify the behavioral mechanisms that lead to changes in mother's RSA during naturalistic episodes of infant distress. We hypothesized that the onset of specific caregiver regulatory behaviors will predict a time‐locked decrease in caregiver RSA, suggesting a pathway by which parenting behaviors are associated with changes in RSA that reflect active engagement and responsiveness to infant distress cues.

Methods

Participants

Participants in the current study were 87 mothers and their infants. Dyads were recruited from Austin, TX via convenience sampling using Facebook, online university event calendars, and fliers posted in community health centers. Mothers ranged in age from 18 to 43 years (M = 31.2). Fifty mothers identified as White non‐Hispanic, 15 as Hispanic, 9 as White Hispanic, 5 as Black, 1 as Asian, and 4 as more than one race. Fifteen percent had a high school diploma or less, 13% had attended but not completed college, 28% had a 4‐year college degree, and 33% had a graduate degree. The majority (84%) of mothers were married or living with a partner. Annual family income ranged from less than $25,000 to over $125,000; median = $75,000–$99,999. Thirty‐nine percent of mothers were not employed outside of the home, 18% were employed part‐time, 42% were employed full‐time, and 1% were employed full‐time and had a second job. Infants were 4.5 months on average (SD = 2.6, range = 1.1–10.4 months).

Out of the N = 87 dyads that completed an introductory session, n = 2 dyads were excluded because no video was collected due to video camera error and n = 9 were excluded for not having sensor data, including: mother did not agree to wear the sensor (n = 2), mother did not consent for child to wear the sensor on their chest and instead selected an ankle placement (n = 1), or a motion‐only sensor was being piloted on mother and infant (n = 6). An additional n = 5 dyads were excluded for this analysis because a sensor synchronization protocol was not completed on video. This missing data was due to experimenter error (i.e., not synchronizing the sensors on video) and was disproportionately clustered at the beginning of data collection. This resulted in a usable dataset of N = 71.

Procedure

One to two research assistants conducted a videotaped, 90‐min introductory session in the home of each dyad. The study was conducted in English or Spanish depending on the household's primary language. Research assistants obtained written informed consent from legal guardians and configured the dyad's wearable devices. Caregivers and infants wore a Movisens EcgMove4, a combined 1024 Hz ECG and activity sensor, on their chest (movisens GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany). Caregivers wore the sensor on their chest under the right breast via hypoallergenic foam gel electrodes (VersaTrode A10005‐3%–7%, New York, United States). Infants wore the sensor in the center of their chest on the nipple line using hypoallergenic, gentle‐adhesive, foam gel pediatric electrodes (TenderTrode A10001, New York, United States). Following a sensor synchronization protocol, sensors were applied to mother and infant. One research assistant then followed the infant continuously for the duration of the session, keeping both infant and mother on video. Mothers and infants completed a 5‐min free play session (M = 5.2, SD = 0.91 min) and a short reactivity task (M = 3.57, SD = 1.27 min) in which infants were presented with novel stimuli. For the remainder of the session, mothers were given an open‐ended opportunity to walk the research assistants through daily routines in their home, including breastfeeding, diaper changing, preparing a meal, doing household chores, or putting their baby to sleep if tired. On average, videos were 51 min long (range = 36–71 min).

Measures

Naturalistic Infant Distress

Episodes of infant distress were annotated from video data by research assistants using Mangold INTERACT (Mangold International GmBH, Arnstorf, Germany). Similar to existing coding schemes (Hubbard and Van IJzendoorn 1991), crying and fussing within 5 s were combined and collapsed into a single category labeled “distress.” Distress labels were further categorized as “crying” or “fussing” depending on the intensity of distress. A distress episode was labeled as crying if loud, harsh, rhythmic, open‐mouthed cries were detected at any point. A distress episode was labeled as fussing if the entire episode consisted of negative vocalizations less intense than crying, including whining and whimpering.

Regulatory Behaviors

Research assistants annotated caregiver holding and vocalizations from videos using Mangold INTERACT. We only coded for regulatory behaviors that overlapped with an infant distress episode. All physiological analyses include only the first instance of each regulatory behavior that started after distress onset and before distress offset. Regulatory behaviors were not mutually exclusive, that is, talking to and holding the infant could co‐occur.

Holding the infant was defined as any instance of picking up and fully supporting the infant's weight while stationary, seated, or moving. Holding annotations of the same type within 10 s were combined. Coders attained a kappa score of 0.844 on identifying holding, indicating strong interrater reliability (Bakeman and Quera 2011).

Vocalizations were defined as any caregiver speech directed to the infant. Vocalizations included talking as well as “shushing,” but not vegetative sounds like sneezing or breathing. Vocalizations of the same type within 10 s were combined into the same episode. When two caregivers were present, both caregiver vocalizations were annotated. Strong interrater reliability was achieved with a kappa score of 0.745 (Bakeman and Quera 2011).

Physiological Data Processing

Infant and caregiver ECG were collected at a sampling rate of 1024 Hz and R–R intervals were extracted using Movisens DataAnalyzer (movisens GmbH, Karlsruhe, Germany). Kubios HRV software (Kubios Oy, Kuopio, Finland, v. 3.4.3) was used to remove artifacts across the period of the introductory visit during which sensors were on mother and infant. A threshold‐based artifact correcting algorithm was used to compare every IBI value against a local average interval, obtained by median filtering the IBI time series. If an IBI differed from the local average by more than a “very low” threshold of 0.45 s, the interval was identified as an artifact. Artifacts were corrected with interpolated values using a cubic spline interpolation. We excluded mother (n = 6) and infant (n = 5) data with more than 10% of beats needing correction, at which point the data has too many errors and does not allow for appropriate editing (Dollar et al. 2020). Average beats corrected was 2.1% (range = 0–9.9%). Following automatic beat correction, we deleted beats greater than or equal to 200 or less than 20 beats per minute (Rodriguez‐Linares et al. 2008). We excluded n = 10 mother data with more than 10% of beats greater than or equal to 200 or less than 20 beats per minute. Average beats deleted was 2.0% (range = 0%–9.8%).

Dynamic RSA

Next, dynamic RSA was calculated from the cleaned IBI time series using a technique validated in developmental samples by Abney, daSilva, Lewis, and Bertenthal (2021). To account for any deleted beats, the RSA method resampled and interpolated the IBI time series using cubic spline. Beats‐per‐minute and RSA were calculated and filtered using child‐ or adult‐normed parameters. The HF band pass filter was set to 0.15–0.4 Hz for mothers and 0.24–1.04 Hz for infants (Huffman et al. 1998). A 15‐s sliding window (updated every 200 ms) of RSA values was computed to obtain a continuous RSA timeseries for the entire session. The RSA timeseries for mother and infant were aligned so that each window of RSA covered the same period of time.

Data Analysis

Multimodal Data Synchronization

To conduct time‐locked analyses, we aligned video and ECG data streams. During the introductory session, a “marker” was established across data streams by a research assistant. In full view of the video camera, both infant and mother Movisens were oriented in the same direction and placed into the closed fist of one hand. Next, the research assistant vertically raised their hand before forcefully bringing it down to strike a flat surface two times, then moved their hand horizontally in a zig–zag direction for 5 s, followed by two forceful vertical hits again. This marker was selected because it produced a distinct motion and visual pattern that could be observed in the Movisens acceleration data as well as the video data. Data streams were synchronized to the millisecond around the first vertical hit at the point of contact with the flat surface. This made it possible to compare video annotations to sensor data with high temporal precision.

Event‐Based Analysis of RSA Change

After aligning data streams, we conducted a time‐locked event‐based analysis to examine the effect of each regulatory behavior on infant and mother RSA (Gatzke‐Kopp et al. 2022). Trials included the 30 s prior to regulatory behavior onset and up to the 75th percentile of regulatory behavior duration (i.e., approximately 20 s for vocalizations and 180 s for holding). Plotting to the 75th percentile gives us the opportunity to show what happens once a regulatory behavior begins and over the course of the behavior for most participants, without limiting interpretability due to low sample size at the tail of the curve. For each trial, the mean value of the baseline (the 5 s prior to regulatory behavior onset) was calculated. This value was subtracted from the pre‐event and post‐event parts of the signal to get RSA change relative to regulatory behavior onset. Next, we averaged all trials for each participant, (“participant average”), and plotted the mean signal of all participants together, or the “grand average”.

Significance Testing

To determine whether RSA change following the regulatory behavior was significantly different from chance changes in RSA, we conducted bootstrapping to obtain 95% confidence intervals for randomly selected portions of infant and mother RSA that did not include the regulatory behavior of interest (Abney, daSilva, Lewis, and Bertenthal 2021). Consistent with existing work (Abney, daSilva, and Bertenthal 2021; Madden‐Rusnak et al. 2025), by using a bootstrapping analysis we can identify when RSA change fell above or below the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval, which indicates RSA change that is beyond the threshold of chance alone. Visualizations are used to indicate to readers the pattern of RSA change following events of interest (i.e., caregiver holding or vocalizing) and where it differs significantly from chance RSA change, as determined by the permutation analysis.

Results

Infant Distress: Descriptive Results

N = 41 infants had instances of distress recorded on video (58% of N = 71 infants). Compared to infants who did not display distress, infants with distress were not significantly different across age (t = 0.15, p = 0.438), gender (X 2 = 1.9, p = 0.165), or average RSA across the entire recording (t = 1.0, p = 0.315). Infants displayed multiple distress episodes per video, so distress vocalizations within 30 s were combined to prevent overlap between regulatory behaviors and the onset of a new distress episode. Figure 1 illustrates the combined distress vocalizations for each participant. Of those infants who displayed distress, infants had 4 distress episodes on average (median = 3, SD = 3, range = 1–15). The mean length of distress durations was 47.2 s (SD = 60.7 s). Figure 2 shows that both fuss and cry data were right skewed. Infants engaged in more fussing than crying during 1 hour of observation. On average, cry durations (M = 70.7, SD = 69.2 s) were longer than fuss durations (M = 25.4, SD = 22.9 s).

For the analyses below, we decided to include distress during tasks (n = 15) as well as open‐ended observation (n = 125). When analyzed separately (i.e., considering only distress that occurred outside of tasks), the main pattern of RSA results still held, so we include distress from both contexts to increase our sample size.

We did not conduct a formal a priori power analysis because this study involved collection of high‐density, within‐person physiological and behavioral data in the home, for which population effect sizes are not well characterized. Given the within‐subjects design, with N = 41 participants and on average 4 trials per participant, this study is powered to detect medium‐to‐large effects of regulatory behaviors on RSA (approximately d ≥ 0.40) with 80% power at alpha = 0.05 and within‐person correlations of 0.4 (Cohen 1988).

Regulatory Behaviors: Descriptive Results

Out of the 140 recorded distress episodes for N = 41 participants, after the distress episode started, mothers responded to 27.9% (n = 39) by picking up and holding the infant and 72.1% (n = 101) by vocalizing to the infant. As responses were naturalistic (i.e., mothers were free to respond to their infant continuously and without any constraints), we also observed regulatory behaviors start before the distress onset and continue during it. For example, a mother may have been holding her infant for 2 min before the infant started crying, and then continued holding the infant while they remained crying. Out of 140 distress episodes, 36.4% (n = 51) had a caregiver already holding the infant and 9.3% (n = 13) included a vocalization that had started prior to the distress and continued through it. Taken together, this means that during infant distress, 81.4% of the time mothers vocalized to the infant and 64.3% of the time mothers held their infant.

In addition to variability in the type of response, we also observed variability in latency to respond to infant distress. Table 1 summarizes the latencies to respond to fusses and cries. Most vocalizations started within 4 s of infant distress onset, and holds started within 40 s. Figure 3 shows mothers' latency to respond to their infant's fusses and cries. Mothers responded to fusses faster than they did for cries, however, some latencies to fusses and cries were up to 30–60 s, which falls far outside of the traditional lab‐based definition of “contingent” responses within 3–5 s.

Physiological Change Around Regulatory Behaviors

Infant

We evaluated the effects of holding and vocalizing on infant RSA (Figure 4). When a caregiver held their distressed infant, infant RSA significantly decreased from 90 to 180 s after holding onset. In contrast, the effect of a caregiver's first vocalization to the infant in the context of distress produced significant increases in RSA from 8 to 24 s after vocalization onset. To examine overlapping behaviors, we split up caregiver's vocalizations into those that did and did not overlap with simultaneous holding. Vocalizations with and without simultaneous holding both produced significant increases in RSA within 7–9 s (Figure 5). Taken together, vocalizations do not appear to be as supportive as physical touch/holding for scaffolding infant physiological emotion regulation as indexed by decreases in RSA.

Infant RSA change relative to regulatory behavior onset during infant distress.

Caregiver

Figure 6 shows the effects of infant distress and regulatory behaviors (i.e., holding and vocalizing) on caregiver RSA. After infant distress onset, caregiver RSA change was not significantly different from chance alone. We also tested the effects of holding and vocalizing on a mother's own RSA (Figure 6). When a caregiver picked up and held her distressed infant, this produced a significant time‐locked decrease in her RSA from approximately 120–140 and 160–180 s after holding onset. When a caregiver first vocalized to her distressed infant, her RSA change remained relatively stable and did not differ from chance alone.

Caregiver RSA changes relative to infant distress and regulatory behavior onset.

We also observed patterns of RSA immediately prior to behavior onset. Although not statistically significant, for all caregiving responses, the RSA prior to behavior onset was negative, meaning that caregiver RSA values were lower in the 30 s prior to behavior onset relative to the 5 s prior. It is possible that caregivers were anticipating or tuned in to their infants' cues (i.e., showing a lower relative RSA) prior to their behavioral response. This would be more consistent with the polyvagal theory: that caregivers are in fact preparing physiologically to provide support before doing so. These changes may be associated with the onset of cognitive or attentional processes rather than the timing of the external motor behaviors we coded, like talking or holding. However, this pattern was not significantly different from chance alone in our sample, reflecting the need for additional research to test this hypothesis using naturalistic data.

Discussion

This study provides the first direct hypothesis test that caregiver regulatory behaviors produce time‐locked changes in infant and caregiver RSA. Unlike most previous studies (Jones‐Mason et al. 2018), we leverage observations from naturalistic distress episodes in the home, affording us access to ecologically valid variability in types and latencies of caregiving responses to infant distress. Previous research suggests that infants of more sensitive mothers have more vagal withdrawal in response to a stressor, which predicts better long‐term emotion regulation (Morris et al. 2020), and that when mothers are allowed to actively respond to infants, both mother and infant RSA decreases (Moore et al. 2009). However, the specific behaviors underlying these associations have not been previously examined. It is critical to study the impact of caregiving behaviors on infant and mother vagal reactivity in order to understand how parenting behaviors structure infant emotion regulation in real time.

Broadly, our descriptive results showed that parents respond to infant distress at home in ways that are consistent with the literature (Bell and Ainsworth 1972; Bornstein et al. 2017; Joosen et al. 2013), but unique in type and latency relative to responses observed in laboratory sessions. We saw caregivers respond to infants by touching and holding their infants as well as talking to their infants. Only 6% of infant distress episodes did not produce any recorded caregiver response at all. Latency of caregiver responses to distress varied in the home and were overall much longer than contingent responses observed in laboratory settings. While most distress episodes led to a caregiver vocalizing or picking up and holding the infant within 10 s, some latencies were upwards of 2 min, consistent with other behavioral observations of caregivers in the home with latencies up to 2–3 min (Bell and Ainsworth 1972). Sometimes caregivers did not respond to a cry until 2 min after it started; if studied during the traditional 2‐min reunion of the face‐to‐face still face, these responses may not have been recorded. More broadly, contingencies of 3–5 s commonly observed in face‐to‐face laboratory observations (e.g., van Egeren et al. 2001) are much less likely to occur in naturalistic home interactions, representing only 14% of caregivers' response latencies to infant distress. Additionally, we found that caregivers were not quicker to respond to cries relative to fusses. Although caregivers may interpret cries as more intense and therefore in need of a more immediate response, it was also possible that quicker responses to fusses prevented them from escalating into cries. Future work should investigate what factors predict response latency in naturalistic settings, like the presence of other children in the home or the physical distance between a caregiver and infant.

Our results also extend the growing literature on the dynamics of vagal reactivity in infants and caregivers. Across studies, the function of vagal withdrawal as understood by the polyvagal theory is widely agreed upon: RSA decreases when the PNS withdraws, which allows heart rate to increase and supports efforts to interact or disengage with others in order to soothe (Porges 2007). However, the timing and behavioral correlates of vagal reactivity remain unclear across studies, with findings varying across settings, contexts, paradigms, subsamples, and measurement approaches (Jones‐Mason et al. 2018). Broadly, our results lend support to the polyvagal theory, but also raise new questions about the timing and function of real‐time RSA change across naturalistic distress.

As reported in recent work, infants show the expected RSA decrease following distress onset, theorized to reflect their behavioral expressivity and engagement with the environment in order to cope (Madden‐Rusnak et al. 2023). The current work provides additional detail as to the specific caregiving behaviors associated with changes in infant RSA. We found that caregivers holding their infant produced time‐locked decreases in RSA from 90 to 180 s after holding onset. This is consistent with the hypothesis that caregivers' holding behaviors scaffold decreases in infant RSA that allow for coping (Ostlund et al. 2017). Caregiver vocalizations, with and without simultaneous holding, led to significant increases in RSA. Increases in RSA in the context of distress could reflect a lack of resources for coping. In other work, however, increases in RSA (or higher baseline RSA levels) are interpreted as an orienting response to a social partner (Bradshaw and Abney 2021; Porges and Furman 2011). One consideration is that the prior work examining orienting responses takes place in neutral or positive contexts, where we observe this increase in RSA directly following vocal responses to infant distress. Thus, it is possible that the interpretation of this change in RSA may differ depending on the emotional context prior to and following the vocalization.

That holding and vocalizations produced different effects on RSA was surprising as we hypothesized that both modalities of behavior would produce decreases in RSA. However, it is possible that physical touch and vocalizations act differently on physiology in the context of infant distress. Existing observational work has shown that talking to an infant is less effective at terminating infant crying relative to physical touch (Bell and Ainsworth 1972). Furthermore, the effects of holding on regulation have precedent: in a randomized controlled trial, mothers randomized to carry their infant more had infants who cried and fussed almost 43% less overall (Hunziker and Barr 1986). Our findings suggest that caregiver holding is associated with time‐locked decreases in infant RSA, indicating one pathway by which parenting behaviors shape infant physiology. By contrast, the effect of caregiver vocalizations on infant RSA—and how to interpret this time‐locked increase in RSA—requires further research.

Mothers in our sample did not show the expected RSA decrease following infant distress onset. We hypothesized that mothers' RSA would decrease following the onset of regulatory behaviors to her distressed infant, especially in the home setting where she can actively engage with and soothe her infant. There is some evidence that mother's RSA may increase during the first 15 s of the face‐to‐face still face before decreasing, theorized to reflect mothers' own process of regulation (Oppenheimer et al. 2013; Ostlund et al. 2017), but our dynamic measure of mother RSA following infant distress did not reveal any RSA change that was different from chance alone. Additionally, caregiver holding produced a significant decrease in mother's RSA, while vocalizations produced no change. That vocalizations did not produce a decrease in RSA is inconsistent with the theory that vagal withdrawal indicates active engagement with the environment, that is, soothing a distressed infant. One possibility is that the directionality of physiology and behavior are reversed: changes in physiology may predict the onset of regulatory behavior. Although insignificant, for all caregiving responses, mother's RSA prior to the behavior onset was negative. Mothers may be tuning in to their infants' cues as they prepare to respond, leading to a decrease in RSA before they behaviorally respond. Mothers' RSA changes may also occur in tandem with cognitive or attentional processes that precede regulatory behaviors, which we were unable to capture in our paradigm. Additional work is required to assess these possibilities and confirm the robustness of this pattern.

Limitations

This study leveraged wearable ECG sensors in combination with objective behavioral annotations to examine the relationship between parenting behaviors and physiological regulation in the home. We also calculated a continuous measure of RSA to capture the real‐time dynamics of RSA around distress and regulatory behaviors. In the context of these strengths, the current study also has a number of limitations.

First, not every infant in our sample showed distress during a one‐hour introductory visit (only about 58%). Although this lends itself to the ecologically valid nature of our data, we do not have cry data from nearly 30 infants. Longer recordings of parent and child behavior collected with wearable audio and physiology sensors (de Barbaro et al. 2023), potentially in combination with automated cry detection algorithms to minimize annotation burden (Yao et al. 2022), could capture spontaneous infant crying for more infants across more contexts.

Second, although we argue that parenting behaviors were more naturalistic in the home compared to a laboratory session, we also acknowledge that research assistants were present in the home for the current study. As a result, they may have interfered with typical routines or patterns of caregiver responding. Wearable audio sensors or less obtrusive video recordings (e.g., portable security cameras temporarily mounted to the walls) are promising strategies for capturing even more naturalistic parenting behavior (de Barbaro and Fausey 2022; Sullivan et al. 2021).

Third, we did not code for every regulatory behavior observed and instead focused on the highest‐frequency caregiving behaviors. For example, we did not code for parent distraction or affect changes, mutual gaze, or infant self‐regulatory behaviors like mutual gaze or gaze aversion which have been examined in prior works (Crockenberg and Leerkes 2004; de Barbaro et al. 2023; Jahromi et al. 2004). As it was difficult to ascertain both mother and infant gaze on our videos, we decided not to code for gaze behaviors that may have been regulatory in nature. There are behaviors missing from the present analysis (e.g., gaze, breastfeeding, facial affect) that could provide additional layers of context to the results.

Lastly, we did not code for maternal sensitivity or separate participants into “suppressors” or “non‐suppressors” based on their patterns of RSA suppression. This was guided by our motivation to identify the effects of specific behaviors on RSA, regardless of global maternal sensitivity or average pattern of responding. However, we also recognize that we are measuring the effect of specific behaviors on RSA over the course of just 1 hour in the life of an infant and caregiver, with a history of patterns and behaviors preceding this sampling frame. Future work should collect repeated, longitudinal measures of parenting behaviors in combination with physiology to make sense of within‐person variability in mother and infant behavior, as well as the long‐term implications on infant regulation.

Conclusion

The present study advances our understanding of how the biological substrates of emotion regulation are structured in infancy via ecologically valid parenting behaviors occurring in free‐flowing, unprompted home interactions. At home, caregivers vary in how long it takes to respond to infant distress, with most latencies around 10 s and some up to 2–3 min. Caregivers also vary in how they respond to their infant following distress onset. These sources of variability are not present in structured regulation tasks, but they have implications for real‐time physiological regulation. By examining dynamic RSA change within mother–infant dyads, we reveal the process by which caregiver holding, but not vocalizing, scaffolds infant emotion regulation via vagal withdrawal. This study provides initial evidence that parenting behaviors can shape dynamic physiological arousal “in‐the‐wild.”
