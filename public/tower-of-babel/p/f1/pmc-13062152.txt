Local shear mechanical behavior of rock joint under constant normal load
Authors: Wanlin Wang, Haichun Ma, Changlin Dai, Jiazhong Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe shear mechanical behavior of joint surfaces is crucial for the safety and stability of rock engineering. Under constant normal load (CNL) conditions, the boundary element method is employed to calculate the distribution of normal contact forces. By combining the “dip angle” with the Patton model, a shear force calculation program analyzes the shear results for each discrete element. This approach allows for the investigation of microscopic phenomena during the shear process and provides insights into the effects of variations in load and shear displacement. Changes in load and shear displacement influence both the shear forces and the shear zones. The shear zones primarily expand and contract around their original regions. At local points of fracture shear, the patterns of shear force exhibit a differential effect, indicating that regions with higher shear stress are more prone to shear failure. This study provides a reference for understanding the variation of shear forces and shear zones during the shear process and offers a new perspective for investigating the mechanisms and patterns of shear failure.Keywords: Rock joint, Local shear stress, Constant normal load, Dip angle, Boundary element methodSubject terms: Engineering, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Fractures are common discontinuities in rock masses that significantly reduce mechanical strength and provide pathways for coupled physical processes. Shear failure is the dominant failure mode associated with rock fractures1. The shear characteristics of rock joints have been widely studied, with particular attention to roughness parameters and peak shear stress. In 1966, Patton introduced a shear model based on Coulomb’s law of friction, establishing the relationship between shear force, normal stress, internal friction angle, and dip angle2. Subsequently, Ladanyi and Maksimovic et al., proposed their own shear model3–5. In 1977, Barton introduced a method for predicting shear strength in rough joint surfaces by utilizing the joint roughness coefficient (JRC) as a morphological parameter. This coefficient characterizes the effect of surface roughness on shear strength. Barton proposed that JRC could be estimated either through visual comparison with ten standard profiles or by back analysis of shear test results6. Previous research on rock shear models primarily focused on two-dimensional analyses. However, recent advancements in technology have ushered in a new era of research, moving beyond two-dimensional approaches to explore three-dimensional characteristics of joint surfaces. The development and use of advanced scanning devices have enabled detailed analysis of three-dimensional parameters, enhancing the understanding of rock joint shear characteristics7–9. Grasselli discretized joint surfaces into a limited number of triangles, introduced concepts such as potential contact area () and threshold dip angle () to quantify rock joint surface parameters, and proposed corresponding shear strength equations and roughness parameters based on experimental results7–10, which have been widely recognized. Due to the widespread shear failure and great damage in practical engineering, many scholars have conducted extensive research on the shear characteristics of rock joint surfaces. Three-dimensional topography scanning experiments were conducted on rock joints with different surface morphologies, and a functional relationship between the apparent dip angle and the initial contact area ratio was established11. Due to the random distribution of contacts, it is difficult to relate the contact area to a single contour. Only by analyzing the entire surface, rather than just a section, can the influence of roughness on the shear behavior of rock joints be properly understood12. A novel method was proposed to quantify the area and extent of damage during the shear of joint surfaces13. This method has good application prospects for studying the evolution of surface roughness parameters related to joint shear mechanics. The relationship between parameter variation and the shear behavior of the joint is investigated, and a shear stress–shear displacement constitutive model of the joint is established. The joint surface geometry and aperture distribution during the shearing process provide the fundamental basis for joint shear behavior. Based on the microscopic understanding of rock heterogeneity, a micromechanical model was proposed to study the deformation and failure process of rock14. The material properties in the rock are assumed to obey Weibull distribution, and the heterogeneity of the rock at the microscopic level is taken into account. Elastic damage mechanics was used to describe the constitutive relationship of the microstructure, finite element method was used as the basic stress analysis tool, and maximum tensile strain criterion and Mohr-Coulomb criterion were used as the damage threshold. A simple method similar to applying a fracture model is used to track the fracture propagation process and the interaction between multiple fractures. A large number of studies have been conducted on rock joint strength, joint height and dip angle, shear velocity, dilation angle, bonded-particle models, fracture aperture, and seepage characteristics15–22.

The method of numerical simulation technology to study shear process has been widely paid attention to23,24. A numerical method was proposed to determine the contact area of rock joints under combined normal and shear loading conditions. The approach requires only three-dimensional surface coordinates prior to shearing and is implemented via direct shear simulations using a cohesive particle model within a discrete element framework25. The locations of the contact regions observed in the simulation are in good agreement with those determined by the proposed method.To characterize the roughness of the joint surface, including the contact area, the concept of an “effective and non-effective micro-slope angle” was defined based on the apparent inclination of triangular elements relative to the shear direction. Bahaaddini used the discrete element program PFC2D to simulate the shear characteristics of rock joints. The results of the numerical model are in good agreement with Patton26, Ladanyi, Archambault, Barton and Choubey models. In 2014, PFC2D was used to study the influence of joint length on the shear characteristics of rough rock joints, and it was found that the mobilization of coarse particles with different sizes at different scales led to the observed scale effect27. Progress has also been made in the study of fracture contact, rough degradation and damage, shear-flow coupling characteristics, and size effects28–31.

Although considerable research has focused on peak shear strength, relatively little attention has been given to the spatial distribution of local shear stress and contact deformation of joint surfaces. To address this gap, a numerical model for shear behavior was developed based on previous studies. Using scanned surface data, the model evaluates the magnitude and distribution of shear stress during the shearing process under low-stress conditions (shear dilation). The results provide a theoretical basis for understanding shear failure mechanisms in rock masses.

Simulation calculation model of rock joint shear

Under the action of shear load, the convex body tilted towards the shear direction begins to undergo elastic deformation, and the areas tilted against the shear direction are separated from each other, forming holes perpendicular to the shear direction9. This is also confirmed by the fracture permeability experiment results of Yeo et al.,20 and Gentier et al.,( 1997).

The contact area of the joint is mainly concentrated around the protrusion inclined to the shear direction, and the steeper the protrusion is, the easier it is to contact during the shearing process. PFC results also support these experimental observations25. On this basis, it can be assumed that during rock shear, parts inclined in the direction of shear contact and are subjected to shear forces, while parts opposite the direction of shear do not contact and are not subjected to shear forces. Park defined the concept of ‘effective and non-effective micro-slope angles’ based on the apparent inclination of the triangular element to the shear direction25. The concept is extended to calculate the contact area and dip angle.

Figure 1 illustrates the shear profile of rock joints, with the joint plane subjected to shear in the direction of . As shear displacement changes, the pores between the upper and lower joint planes also vary. Based on these pore variations, the fracture surface can be classified into contact and non-contact regions. The contact area undergoes deformation and damage due to normal and shear stresses, while the non-contact area remains unaffected by forces and does not experience deformation or damage. Thus, during the shear process, the contact area, normal stress, and shear stress evolve, leading to changes in deformation and eventual failure.

Basic principles of computing programs

Firstly, the data of the rock sample’s joint surface are recorded using a scanning instrument. The scanner has a scanning interval of 0.1 mm, and a three-dimensional model of the joint surface is then established.

Secondly, the boundary element method (BEM) is employed to solve the contact and stress problems of the joint surface. In three-dimensional space, only the boundary surface is discretized, significantly reducing the dimensionality of the problem. This reduction leads to smaller linear systems, lower memory requirements, and more efficient computations. The benefits of this approach are particularly pronounced when dealing with unbounded domains32,33. Compared to the finite element method, the boundary element method offers several advantages, including the use of fewer elements, higher calculation accuracy, faster computation speed, and better capability in handling complex boundaries. A single-loop iteration scheme based on the conjugate gradient method is employed to determine the actual contact area and contact pressure distribution. This method reliably converges for any rough surface and utilizes a two-dimensional multi-stage multiple summation algorithm to calculate surface deflection and subsurface stress. The efficiency of this approach is notable, as the contact solver can converge even when the contact region is highly fragmented34,35. First, the loads at each contact point are calculated using the boundary element method. The shear forces are then determined based on the relationship between load and shear force in the Patton model. Changes in dip angles are primarily calculated by updating the dip angles corresponding to each contact point during the displacement process. The threshold setting is based on the premise that this study considers only deformation without causing failure. During the calculation, the stress in some contact shear regions may exceed the failure strength; however, since the program does not account for failure, this may result in abnormal stresses. Therefore, considering the model size and physical properties, thresholds are established for the magnitude of deformation and shear strength. If a point exceeds the threshold, the excess is evenly redistributed among the remaining points. The normal stress corresponding to a normal deformation of 0.001 mm is set as the threshold, as this deformation results in an angle change of 0.6°. These changes in normal deformation and dip angle influence the distribution of normal and shear stresses. The shear strength value is used as the threshold for shear stress.When both normal stress and shear stress exceed their respective thresholds, the stress redistribution caused by deformation is taken into account, and the shear stress is recalculated accordingly. The normal stress exceeding the threshold is evenly distributed across all contact points, and the shear stress is then recalculated using Patton’s equation. Result analysis and discussion

Three-dimensional model of shear force distribution

In the pre-processing stage, the raw data is imported, and the surface data is converted into matrix form. Since the surface scan may be rotated relative to the horizontal due to the scanning process, rotating the surface data is often the first pre-processing step after data import. Next, the smallest (and ) values are set as the new origin, as scans are typically obtained in an absolute reference system. Additionally, the raw data is often trimmed in both the and directions because scanned or synthetically generated data frequently exhibits flaws at the outermost points38. Since the scanning interval is 0.1 mm, the joint surface is divided into square grids with side lengths of 0.1 mm. On this basis, modeling and calculation are carried out.

Figure 5 illustrates the model of the joint surface of the rock sample after scanning and reconstruction, which serves as the basis for subsequent calculations. Figure 6a depicts the magnitude and distribution of shear stress when a constant normal load of 1.4 MPa is applied to the rock sample, with a shear displacement of 0.1 mm, while Fig. 6b shows the corresponding magnitude and distribution of normal stress. In Fig. 6a, it is observed that most areas experience little to no shear force, with values ranging from 0 to 21.7 MPa. In a few regions, shear force exceeds 20 MPa, though the vast majority remains below 15 MPa. Areas with high shear stress are more susceptible to shear failure, indicating that protective measures should be considered for these regions in practical engineering applications.

The effect of shear displacement on the magnitude and distribution of shear stress

Figure 7 illustrates the distribution of shear stress. Panels (a) and (b) show the magnitude and distribution of shear stress at shear displacements of 0.2 mm and 0.4 mm, respectively, under a constant load of 1.4 MPa. The figures reveal that the shear stress distribution area remains largely consistent across the different displacements. The majority of the area exhibits low shear stress. Despite changes in the ‘effective region’ (where the upper and lower surfaces coincide) during the shearing process, the most accessible part of the joint surface continues to be the raised areas. Conversely, regions opposite to the shear direction remain difficult to contact during movement. As a result, the shear force distributions in Fig. 7a and b are relatively similar, reflecting the overlapping nature of the shear stress patterns.

The magnitude and distribution of force on the joint surface are significantly influenced by its roughness3,15,23. As shear behavior occurs, the contact state of the joint surface changes dynamically, necessitating a dynamic assessment of the roughness’s impact on surface forces. Treating roughness as a static constant can lead to erroneous judgments about the stress state. Consequently, the ‘effective roughness’—defined as the roughness of the area where the upper and lower surfaces overlap—emerges as a critical factor affecting rock stress.

With increasing shear displacement, the ‘effective roughness’ changes, resulting in variations in shear stress across the affected area. This variation is evident when comparing Fig. 7a and b, with Fig. 7c illustrating the change in shear stress in the Y direction, ranging from 0.3 to 44.7 mm. As shear displacement increases to 0.2 mm, the shear stress in the corresponding region varies, with most regions showing a change in shear stress between − 1 and 5.53 MPa. This range demonstrates that the joint surface stress remains in a dynamic state throughout the shear process, aligning with the variations in shear stress caused by changes in ‘effective roughness’.

This trend of shear stress is further illustrated in Fig. 8. While the maximum shear stress remains unchanged at 25.7 MPa from Fig. 7a to b, it shows a slight decrease from 25.8 MPa to 25.7 MPa in Fig. 8. This indicates that shear stress variation does not follow a consistent trend but is instead unpredictable. This unpredictability underscores the importance of real-time analysis of roughness, which is a key objective of this simulation. The simulation aims to study the evolution of mechanical behavior during the dynamic shear process and to predict potential shear failure regions throughout the shear process. Additionally, it seeks to monitor changes in shear stress, thereby providing valuable insights for understanding and managing the shear behavior of rock joint surfaces.

The shear stress variation trend of grid nodes

In order to better explore the change of shear stress in the shearing process, the shear stress at different nodes is randomly extracted and the ‘dip angle’ of the point is compared with the normal stress. The shear stress curve was classified and sorted, and 4 points were selected in each case. By observing the trends of the curves in the figure, we can gain further insight into the mechanical behavior and its influencing factors during the shearing process. We can predict that the shear behavior of different points may include: one is no shear, with the shear displacement, and the shear appears; the second is the initial shear force, with the shear displacement, and then shear disappear; three is possible constant shear, the shear force becomes larger, smaller, or fluctuating; and four may not shear. We also discuss the types of constant contact at different height levels.

It is assumed that the strength of the rock joints is sufficient, so the shear stress curves of the joints under each load condition are expected to follow the same trend when deformation is neglected. The damage caused by shearing stress was ignored in the simulation calculation, the change of normal height Z of joint surface will cause the change of ‘dip angle’. Therefore, for a given joint surface, the change of normal stress should be the source of subsequent changes. In Fig. 12 shear stress curves at these nodes are basically parallel. By comparing the normal stress values of the points near each sampling point, it is not difficult to find that the normal stress between these points and the surrounding points is generally less than 20 MPa, and it does not change much (less than 3 MPa) during the whole shear process. Therefore, for the region with small normal stress and little change, the normal deformation can be ignored, and the ‘dip angle’ is basically unchanged, resulting in a more regular change of shear stress at the same node.

In general, shear of a rock joint plane is a complex process. The roughness of the joint surface affects the magnitude and distribution of normal stress, which in turn influences the deformation of the joint surface, leading to changes in the magnitude and distribution of shear stress in a mutually interacting process. For a given joint surface, normal stress can be regarded as the source of this series of effects, and its influence, along with joint surface deformation, on the trend of shear stress is considered comprehensively. When normal stress is low and there is little variation compared to surrounding points, the shear stress changes in a regular manner. Shear stress changes irregularly in areas with high normal stress and large gaps compared to the surrounding region, where height differences often vary more drastically.

The variation of shear forces at different points is closely related to the expansion and contraction of the shear band around its original location. It is precisely the dynamic changes of the shear band that induce the dynamic variation of shear forces at different points within the shear zone. Points located in regions of high stress, at the edges of the shear band, or at areas of high vibration exhibit more intense and irregular fluctuations. In contrast, points situated within the interior of the shear band, away from stress concentration zones, generally display more regular variations.

Based on the research of many scholars, combining the concept of ‘dip angle’ and boundary element method, this paper presents a method to simulate the magnitude and distribution of shear stress in the shearing process. The novelty of this method is that it can calculate the shear stress of each region, which is helpful to better grasp the failure region of the joint surface, and has important reference significance for engineering practice. There are also the following shortcomings in the study.

Shear failure is not considered in the simulation calculation. Even under low stress conditions, the rock surface will inevitably be damaged to a certain extent, which will affect the surface roughness and normal stress. This study assumes that no damage will occur, which may cause some degree of error.

Joint surface scanning accuracy and mesh division have effects on shear calculation. The scanning accuracy of joint surface will affect the calculation of roughness. Due to the limitation of scanning accuracy, the scanning interval in this study is 0.1 mm, and the grid is also divided into 0.1 mm in the calculation process. Higher scanning accuracy can build more accurate joint surface models, and subsequent calculations will be more accurate. Whether Patton equation is suitable for calculation, shear failure will reduce the dip angle, which is ignored. When the shear stress is calculated using Patton equation, the calculated result will be larger than the actual value.

Conclusions

The failure of a rock mass often begins locally, gradually affecting the stability of the entire rock mass. Studying the local behavior of fissure shear can provide a better understanding of the shear fracture process of the rock mass. Due to the variety of parameters related to joint surface roughness, the magnitude and distribution of normal and shear stresses change with shear displacement and applied load, making it very difficult to predict shear behavior accurately. Understanding the influence of joint surface roughness and normal stress on shear behavior helps to more accurately characterize the mechanical behavior during the shear process. The proposed method provides a detailed approach to study the shear characteristics of rock joint surfaces.

According to the simulation results, the variation of shear stress in the shearing process can be explained well. The distribution of shear stress is influenced by many factors, among which normal stress and surface roughness are the most significant. In general, under the condition of low stress, the increase of load will lead to the increase of normal stress, shear stress and the expansion of the area under shear action.

Under the same load condition, an increase in shear displacement results in the expansion and contraction of the original shear region, without sudden emergence of a new shear region.

The variation trend of local shear stress remains consistent under different load conditions in the relatively flat region with low normal stress. However, the local shear stress changes irregularly when the joint surface fluctuates violently and the normal stress is large and unevenly distributed.

This paper presents a new dynamic research perspective for each shear region to investigate the variations in shear stress caused by changes in the joint plane, normal stress, normal deformation, and other factors affecting shear behavior. This approach enables a more detailed and comprehensive understanding of shear processes and allows for the prediction of shear-induced damage.
