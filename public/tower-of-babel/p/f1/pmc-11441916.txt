Effect of “He Tiao Du Ren An Shen Acupuncture” on restless leg syndrome: A randomized trial
Authors: Xin-Rui Guan, Ying-Hua Chen, Jun-Feng Li, Xiao-Qing Su, Wei Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to observe the clinical effects of “He Tiao Du Ren An Shen Acupuncture” (HTDRAS Acupuncture) for treating restless leg syndrome (RLS).Methods:We randomly divided 66 RLS patients into 2 groups: the observation group received “He Tiao Du Ren An Shen Acupuncture” and the control group received conventional acupuncture. All participants were treated once a day, 6 days a week, with 1 day off, for a total of 1 month. Clinical effectiveness of the 2 groups was compared, neurotransmitter levels, the International Restless Leg Syndrome Scale and the Hamilton Anxiety Scale were assessed in both groups.Results:The curative effect in the observation group was better than that in the control group (P < .05). After treatment, the expression of 5-hydroxytryptamine in the observation group was higher than in the control group (P < .05). The International Restless Leg Syndrome Scale and Hamilton Anxiety Scale scores in observation group were lower than those in control group (P < .05).Conclusion:“He Tiao Du Ren An Shen Acupuncture” for RLS is significantly effective and safe. It can effectively improve the levels of 5-hydroxytryptamine in RLS patients, alleviate clinical symptoms and reduce anxiety. This treatment has a high clinical application value and is worthy of clinical promotion.

1. Introduction

Restless leg syndrome (RLS) is a common neuromotor disorder characterized by uncontrollable nocturnal leg motor impulses, which can be accompanied by abnormal sensations such as stinging, deep soreness, itching, and insect infestation.[1] The incidence rate of RLS in China ranges from 1.2% to 5%, and it can occur at any age. The average age of onset is between 30 and 40 years old.[2] With the increase in life pressure, the incidence rate of the disease also shows an increasing trend year by year, and RLS has also been found in children in recent years, with a prevalence of 1% to 4%.[3] The main clinical manifestation of RLS is an uncontrollable urge to move the legs, which can be relieved after activity and worsens during rest. The symptoms often appear or worsen in the evening or at night, and are relieved in the morning. RLS has a circadian rhythm and is accompanied by discomfort in the legs.[4]

Acupuncture, a representative therapeutic method in Traditional Chinese Medicine, offers a safe, simple, efficient, and side-effect-free approach to alleviating the symptoms of RLS.[5] Traditional Chinese Medicine theory posits that RLS patients often suffer from an excess of Yang and a deficiency of Yin, with clinical manifestations such as a crawling sensation, numbness, and an inability to keep the legs still at night, which can be attributed to Yin deficiency with Yang hyperactivity and Yin’s failure to restrain Yang. The life activities and mental states of the human body are the external expressions of Yang function; an imbalance between Yin and Yang can lead to symptoms like restless leg and anxiety due to mental disorders.

“He Tiao Du Ren An Shen Acupuncture” (HTDRAS Acupuncture) is a therapeutic approach formulated by Professor Chen Yinghua from the First Affiliated Hospital, Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine, drawing on extensive clinical experience and integrating traditional Chinese medical theories. It has yielded considerable clinical benefits in treating mental disorders. This method leverages the significant role of the Ren and Du meridians in addressing mental illnesses stemming from imbalances in the body’s organs, Qi, Blood, Yin, and Yang. By strategically selecting acupoints along the Ren and Du meridians and employing specific needling techniques to adjust and harmonize the body’s internal balance of Qi, Blood, Yin, and Yang, it effectively treats mental disorders.

2. Research methods

2.1. Ethical approval of research protocol

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethic Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine for the prospective, randomized, controlled trial (Project Number: HZYLLKT202407801). Written informed consent was obtained from patients or their guardians prior to participation in this study. Both patients and researchers were blinded.

2.2. Patients

Patients with climacteric insomnia from November 2023 to April 2024 were enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria: Participants must meet the diagnostic criteria for RLS, be aged 18 to 65 years with no gender restrictions, have a disease duration of at least 6 months, score between 14 and 35 on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), and have a Hamilton Anxiety Scale (17-item) total score under 7. They must not have used related treatment medications or antianxiety drugs within the past month and must voluntarily participate in this study by signing the informed consent. Exclusion criteria: Participants are excluded if they have serious physical organic diseases that cause secondary RLS and anxiety, such as hyperthyroidism or multiple sclerosis; are pregnant; have cognitive impairments, serious mental illnesses, or depression including anxiety associated with mental disorders; have abnormal coagulation function; have overdosed on stimulant medications, sedative-hypnotic drugs, or are experiencing withdrawal from antianxiety drugs; or have participated in other clinical trials within the past 3 months. According to the above criteria, total number of 66 cases were enrolled and assigned to control group and observation group by the random number table method, with 33 patients in each group.

2.3. Treatment method

Control group received conventional acupuncture treatment with the following acupoints selected: Baihui (GV20), left and right Sishencong (EX-HN1), Yintang (EX-HN3), Shenmen (HT7, bilateral), Neiguan (PC6), bilateral, Yanglingquan (GB34, bilateral), Zusanli (ST36, bilateral), Sanyinjiao (SP6, bilateral), Xuanzhong (GB39, bilateral), and Chengshan (BL57, bilateral). Specific methods: after routine disinfection of acupoints, the patient is in a sitting position. Conventional acupuncture is performed using needles that are 0.30 mm in diameter and 40 mm in length. The depth of acupuncture varies: Baihui (GV20) is horizontally punctured at 15 to 25 mm, left and right Sishencong (EX-HN1) at 15 to 25 mm, Yintang (EX-HN3) is punctured downward at 15 to 25 mm after pinching the skin, Shenmen (HT7) is directly punctured at 15 to 20 mm, Neiguan (PC6) at 15 to 20 mm, Yanglingquan (GB34) at 25 to 30 mm, Zusanli (ST36) at 25 to 30 mm, Sanyinjiao (SP6) at 27 to 32 mm, Xuanzhong (GB39) at 15 to 25 mm, and Chengshan (BL57) at 25 to 35 mm. After insertion and obtaining Qi at these acupoints, a method of tonifying and sedating is applied. The needles are manipulated once every 10 minutes, for about 1 minute each time, for a total of 3 times, amounting to 30 minutes. Acupuncture is administered once daily, with 6 consecutive treatments per week and a rest day, over a period of 4 weeks.

Observation group received HTDRAS Acupuncture with the following acupoints selected: Baihui (GV20), Yintang (EX-HN3), Shenting (DU24), 3 Ning Shen points, Tanzhong (CV17), Zhongwan (CV12), Sun’s abdominal area one, Shenmen (HT7, bilateral), Neiguan (PC6, bilateral), and Taichong (LR3, bilateral). Specific methods: the patient, in a supine position, is disinfected with 75% alcohol. Baihui (GV20) is punctured with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle at a 30° angle to the scalp, approximately 15 mm deep. Yintang (EX-HN3) is punctured downward with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle, approximately 15 mm deep. Shenting (DU24) is punctured upward with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle, 15 to 25 mm deep. The 3 Ning Shen points are punctured upward with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle, 25 to 40 mm deep. Tanzhong (CV17) is punctured downward with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle, approximately 15 mm deep. Sun’s abdominal area one is punctured downward with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle at a 15° angle to the skin, approximately 30 mm deep. Zhongwan (CV12) is punctured downward with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle at a 150° angle to the skin, approximately 30 mm deep. The acupoints on the Governing Vessel (Du Mai), including Baihui (GV20), Yintang (EX-HN3), Shenting (DU24), and the 3 Ning Shen points, use a twirling reinforcing and reducing technique. The acupoints on the Conception Vessel (Ren Mai), including Tanzhong (CV17), Sun’s abdominal area one, and Zhongwan (CV12), use a twirling reinforcing technique. The other acupoints are punctured with a 0.30 mm × 40 mm needle, 15 to 25 mm deep, using a tonifying and sedating technique. The needles are manipulated once every 10 minutes, for about 1 minute each time, for a total of 3 times, amounting to 30 minutes. Acupuncture is administered once daily, with 6 consecutive treatments per week and a rest day, over a period of 4 weeks.

2.4. Outcome measures

The general data of patients were collected and analyzed upon admission. The severity of RLS and the clinical efficacy of both groups were evaluated using the International Restless Leg Syndrome Scale (IRLS) both before and after the treatment. The IRLS consists of 10 assessment items, each offering 5 response options. The scoring for each item varies from 0 to 4 points, resulting in a total score that ranges from 0 to 40. A higher score suggests a greater severity of RLS. The scale classifies the severity into categories: scores of 1 to 10 indicate mild, 11 to 20 indicate moderate, 21 to 30 indicate severe, and 31 to 40 indicate very severe symptoms. Clinical response rate = (IRLS score after treatment ‐ IRLS score before treatment)/IRLS score before treatment × 100%. A clinical response rate of ≥75% is considered recovery; ≥50% and <75% is considered effectiveness; ≥25% and <50% is considered improvement; <25% is considered ineffectiveness. The overall clinical efficacy is calculated as the sum of the recovery rate, effectiveness rate, and improvement rate. Before and after treatment, the serum 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) levels and HAMA scores were assessed in both groups.

2.5. Statistical methods

SPSS 25.0 software was applied to analyze study data. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation, and comparison between groups was performed by independent sample t-test. Categorical data was reported by frequency and percentage, and was analyzed with chi-squared test. P < .05 was considered significant.

4. Discussions

RLS, identified as both a neurological condition and a sleep disorder, is characterized by a marked discomfort and urge to move the legs. This urge is particularly heightened during periods of inactivity and tends to intensify at night.[6] The nocturnal symptoms of RLS can cause significant disturbances to sleep, potentially resulting in chronic sleep deprivation.[7] Such deprivation poses an increased risk for various long-term health conditions, including cognitive decline, dementia, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus.[8] The psychological effects of managing RLS are multifaceted, encompassing depressive symptoms, a higher likelihood of suicidal ideation and self-harm, impediments to learning, and a diminished sense of motivation.[9,10]

Acupuncture, an ancient therapeutic modality within traditional Chinese medicine, works by balancing the functions of the Zang-fu organs and the flow of Yin and Yang, thereby serving to prevent and treat a variety of illnesses. In recent years, as research into RLS within the field of Chinese medicine has deepened, it is increasingly believed that the condition’s pathogenesis involves a deficiency of Yin and an excess of Yang, with Yin being unable to restrain Yang. Based on this understanding of the pathogenesis, Professor Chen Yinghua has suggested the HTDRAS Acupuncture method. This approach aims to adjust and harmonize the Du and Ren Meridians, thereby alleviating restlessness and promoting tranquility of both mind and body.

In this study, the HTDRAS Acupuncture method was utilized in treating patients diagnosed with RLS for the initial time, and its therapeutic effectiveness was evaluated. The study outcomes demonstrated that the observation group exhibited a more pronounced improvement compared to the control group, suggesting that the application of HTDRAS Acupuncture is notably efficacious in managing the symptoms of RLS. A study suggests that serotonin (5-HT) receptors may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of RLS, are associated with dopamine receptors, and could potentially become a new target for RLS treatment.[11] In this study, the level of 5-HT was obviously increased in observation group after treatment, it may be beneficial that acupuncture can regulate the activity and content of neurotransmitters by regulating the function of the neuroendocrine system. After treatment, it was found that IRLS scores and HAMA scores in observation group were lower than that of control group, suggesting that HTDRAS Acupuncture possesses the potential to ameliorate clinical symptoms and alleviate anxiety among patients. In recent study, acupuncture therapy has been proved to be effective and safe on RLS.[12,13]

This is probably due to the fact that Baihui (GV20) is known for its sedative effects, which promote the regulation of vital essence and enhance the circulation of Blood and Qi in the cranium. Shenting (DU24), known as “the home of the spirit,” induces tranquility. Yintang (EX-HN3) affects the Du Meridian, harmonizing the functions of the heart and kidneys, and enriching the brain’s marrow. The “Ning Shen” points, with 1 situated on the Du Meridian, are associated with the frontal brain region and work to stimulate the brain and calm the spirit. Tanzhong (CV17), as the convergence point for Qi of the 8 extraordinary meridians, alleviates chest congestion and supports cardiopulmonary health. Zhongwan (CV12), the meeting point for the viscera, regulates the spleen and stomach to enrich Qi and Blood. Sun’s abdominal area one, located on the Ren Meridian, corresponds to the brain’s frontal pole and effectively addresses mental disorders. Shenmen (HT7), the original point of the heart meridian, boosts heart Qi and enhances mental clarity. Neiguan (PC6), a connecting point of the pericardium meridian, calms the heart and spirit. Taichong (LR3) regulates emotions by balancing Qi and Blood, soothing the liver, and providing relief from depression.

This research presents a potent therapeutic strategy for managing RLS. Nonetheless, it encounters certain constraints. Primarily, the constrained sample size and sole-source nature of this prospective inquiry limit the universality of its results. Consequently, an expansive array of supplementary prospective studies is essential to substantiate the findings. Additionally, deeper investigation is warranted to uncover the underlying processes that lead to the amelioration of clinical manifestations.

5. Conclusions

HTDRAS Acupuncture for RLS is significantly effective and safe. It can effectively improve the levels of 5-HT in RLS patients, alleviate clinical symptoms and reduce anxiety. This treatment has a high clinical application value and is worthy of clinical promotion.
