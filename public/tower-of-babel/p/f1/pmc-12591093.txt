Dysregulation of astrocyte-secreted pleiotrophin contributes to neuronal structural and functional deficits in Down syndrome
Authors: Ashley N Brandebura, Adrien Paumier, Quinn N Asbell, Tao Tao, Mariel Kristine B Micael, Sherlyn Sanchez, Nicola J Allen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYNeuronal dendrite patterning and synapse formation are tightly regulated during development to promote proper connectivity. Here, we identify downregulation of pleiotrophin, a secreted molecule enriched in astrocytes, as a major contributor to dendrite and synapse deficits in the Ts65Dn mouse model of Down syndrome (DS). We find overlapping deficits in neuronal dendrites and spines in Ts65Dn mutant and pleiotrophin knockout mice. We show that targeting pleiotrophin overexpression to astrocytes in adult Ts65Dn mice in vivo can rescue dendrite morphology as well as restore spine density, synapse number, and plasticity deficits in the hippocampus. Moreover, pleiotrophin promotes dendrite outgrowth in cultured Ts65Dn neurons through Alk-Akt signaling. Our findings highlight dysregulated astrocyte protein secretion as a contributing factor to DS and identify pleiotrophin as a plasticity-inducing molecule that can be used to promote proper circuit connectivity, importantly, at later stages of development after typical periods of circuit refinement have been completed.

INTRODUCTION

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common form of genetic intellectual disability, caused by trisomy of human chromosome 21 (HSA21).1 Individuals with DS have neurodevelopmental changes that result in hyperactivity, intellectual disability, and memory impairments.2 Individuals with DS have an altered trajectory of brain growth during development, where decreased brain size becomes evident during the second trimester of gestation.3 Defects in neurogenesis are prominent in DS, but importantly, the altered rate of brain growth during the second trimester mirrors the time frame for neuronal maturation, dendrite growth, and synapse formation,4,5 suggesting that in addition to neurogenesis deficits, there are also broad changes to synaptic circuitry due to impaired dendrite branching that contribute to reduced brain volume. Histological studies in humans have identified changes to dendrite morphology in Layer V pyramidal neurons in the visual cortex (VC), which have shorter and less complex dendrites than those of neurotypical individuals.6 Additionally, decreased dendritic spine density is reported in pyramidal neurons of the motor cortex, VC, and the hippocampus.7-9

The establishment of neuronal morphology and synapse formation is intricately regulated by astrocytes.10 For example, astrocytes produce extracellular matrix molecules that guide dendrite arborization11 and a large repertoire of proteins secreted from astrocytes promotes the formation and maturation of excitatory and inhibitory synapses.12-21 With respect to DS, an in vitro study demonstrated that DS astrocytes have decreased levels of the synaptogenic protein thrombospondin1 (Tsp1) and that adding Tsp1 to the culture media could partially recover neuronal spine density deficits. Furthermore, transcriptional and mass spectrometry screens of DS astrocytes have identified a multitude of astrocyte-secreted proteins that are altered in DS.22,23 Together, these studies demonstrate that astrocyte-secreted factors contribute to circuit aberrations observed in DS.

As highly secretory cells, astrocytes are a promising target for delivering secreted molecules into the extracellular space to modify dendrite arborization and synapse density in neurodevelopmental disorders such as DS. In the current study, we set out to test this approach by using viral-mediated overexpression of a candidate protein, pleiotrophin (Ptn). We identified Ptn from a prior mass spectrometry screen of altered secreted proteins from cultured primary cortical astrocytes isolated out of the Ts65Dn mouse model of DS.23 Ptn emerged as a top candidate due to its high levels of brain expression during neonatal development when neurite outgrowth and synapse formation are occurring,24 its strong enrichment in astrocytes during development,25 and its substantial downregulated secretion from primary mouse astrocytes in the Ts65Dn mouse model of DS.23

The roles of Ptn in neurite outgrowth during brain development have been extensively characterized. Ptn has a high affinity for heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate sidechains in the extracellular matrix, thereby strongly promoting neurite outgrowth from neonatal neurons in vitro.24,26 In vivo Ptn is expressed in neuroepithelial and radial glial cells during the developmental stages of early neuronal migration and neurite extension, with enrichment in the axonal migration pathways.27 In addition to neurite outgrowth, Ptn has modulatory effects on synapses and plasticity. Interestingly, Ptn knockout (KO) mice have a lowered threshold for long-term potentiation (LTP) in the Schaffer collateral pathway, while pressure injection of Ptn into hippocampal slices or mice genetically overexpressing Ptn has impaired LTP.28,29 This body of literature demonstrates that Ptn has essential roles in the establishment of neural connections and modulation of synaptic plasticity. In addition to regulating neurite outgrowth and synaptic plasticity under homeostatic conditions, Ptn has regenerative and neuroprotective capacities in injury contexts. For example, Ptn promotes dendrite arborization after cortical prick injury and axon regeneration after spinal cord injury,30 improves adult neurogenesis deficits in the hippocampus in mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease and multiple sclerosis,31,32 and promotes survival of dopaminergic neurons after amphetamine-induced injury33 and in the 6-OHDA model of Parkinson’s disease.34,35

In the present study, we characterized dendrite and spine phenotypes in Ptn KO mice, in parallel with the Ts65Dn mouse model of DS, and found highly overlapping phenotypes. We then tested whether targeting Ptn overexpression to astrocytes could ameliorate the circuit aberrations observed in the VC and the hippocampus of Ts65Dn mice, finding positive effects. Importantly, these results are achieved with Ptn delivery at adult stages after aberrant circuitry has developed, indicating that Ptn can reverse impairments after developmental windows have closed. In the future, these findings may be translatable to other neurodevelopmental disorders with similar circuit aberrations.

RESULTS

Ptn expression is developmentally regulated and is decreased in Ts65Dn astrocytes in vivo

We previously performed transcriptomics and proteomics on cultured cortical astrocytes isolated from the Ts65Dn mouse model to identify mRNA and secreted protein changes in DS astrocytes that may contribute to neurodevelopmental phenotypes (Figure S1A). The Ts65Dn model has a partial trisomy of mouse chromosome 16 that is homologous to the distal region of HSA21 and highly recapitulates the neurodevelopmental impairments that occur in individuals with DS.2,36,37 From this study, we identified that the candidate molecule Ptn showed significant downregulation compared to wild-type (WT) astrocytes at both the secretory and transcriptional levels (Figures S1B and S1C). Based on these findings, we investigated using single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) whether Ptn expression is reduced in DS astrocytes in vivo. We took a holistic approach and quantified Ptn expression in astrocytes, as well as neurons and other glial cell types, including oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and microglia, at postnatal day (P)14 in WT vs. Ts65Dn Mutant (Mut) VC (example images in Figures S1D-S1G). Downregulated Ptn expression was found in Mut S100β+ astrocytes as well as Cspg4+ OPCs (Figures S1H-S1I), with no significant changes in microglia or neurons (Figures S1J-S1K). Adding translational relevance, we found that Ptn is also downregulated in several, but not all, publicly available gene expression datasets from human DS brain tissue (Table S1). For those studies having single-cell resolution, downregulation of Ptn is typically observed in non-neuronal cells, including astrocytes, with one study showing upregulation in inhibitory neuron subsets.38-43 To determine which cell type(s) are the major contributors of Ptn during neurotypical development, we quantified the relative levels of Ptn in astrocytes, OPCs, microglia, and neurons. We found astrocytes have the highest Ptn levels at P14, followed by OPCs, with ~2- and ~6-fold less in neurons and microglia, respectively, compared to astrocytes (Figures S2A and S2B). We validated this finding using a published single-nucleus RNA-sequencing study from the developing mouse VC (Figure S2C).44 Altogether, these findings confirm that Ptn is downregulated in astrocytes in vivo in the Ts65Dn mouse model and that astrocytes are the major cellular contributors of Ptn at P14.

We characterized the spatiotemporal expression profile of Ptn during neurotypical development to gain insight into its functions in the developing brain. The VC and the hippocampus have well-characterized time frames for neural circuit formation that occur within the first month of development in the mouse, and are both regions affected in DS.6,8,9,45 In the VC, dendrite outgrowth is prominent from birth,46 synapse formation is high from P7,47,48 synaptic maturation begins ~ P14,49 and circuit stabilization is achieved by ~ P120 (Figure 1A). Published astrocyte RiboTag RNA-sequencing datasets of the VC50 and the hippocampus51 show that Ptn has peak expression levels during periods of synaptogenesis and synaptic maturation (Figure 1B). We validated Ptn expression in the VC and hippocampus at P14 using smFISH (Figure 1C). In the VC, we compared the levels of Ptn at P7, P14, and P30 in GFP+ astrocytes from the Aldh1L1-EGFP mouse line. Ptn was most highly expressed at P7 and P14 during synapse formation and maturation in cortical Layer I and Layer II/III, followed by significant developmental downregulation at P30 when most synapse formation is complete (Figures 1D and 1E). In the hippocampus, Ptn is upregulated from P7 to P14 and then significantly downregulated by P30 in both the CA1 and the dentate gyrus molecular layer (DGML) (Figures 1F and 1G). To further examine the spatial expression of Ptn, we examined the VC at P14 when Ptn is highest. Given that other astrocyte-derived factors, such as glypican 450 and chordin-like 1,15 have spatial enrichment patterns across cortical layers, we quantified Ptn expression by cortical layer using Glast and Tubb3 to label astrocytes and neurons, respectively. Correlating with previous reports of Ptn as an upper-layer cortical astrocyte marker,50 we found Ptn was expressed significantly higher in Layer I astrocytes compared to Layer IV, with similar expression levels in Layer II/III (Figures S3A and S3B). Likely due to the low levels of Ptn in neurons, a strong spatial pattern of Ptn was not observed (Figure S3C).

Astrocyte-secreted Ptn promotes dendrite outgrowth and branching

Given that Layer V pyramidal neurons have dendritic outgrowth and branching impairments in humans with DS6,9 and that pyramidal neuron dendrites project to upper layers where Ptn is highly expressed,50 we asked if Ptn can induce dendrite outgrowth in pyramidal neurons in vitro. This was approached by culturing cortical astrocytes from WT or Ptn KO mice to generate astrocyte conditioned media (ACM) that was used to treat cultured cortical neurons. We first validated using smFISH that Ptn KO mice29 do not have detectable Ptn (Figure S4A). In our experimental design, WT neurons were grown in minimal media (MM; no ACM), ACM from WT astrocytes (WT ACM), ACM from Ptn KO astrocytes (Ptn KO ACM), or Ptn KO ACM + recombinant Ptn (Rescue ACM; Figure S4B). We added recombinant Ptn at 50 ng/mL after determining a physiological range of Ptn secretion from WT cultured astrocytes (Figure S4C; See STAR Methods). Dendrites were labeled with Map2, combined with nuclear Ctip2 to identify dendrites of Layer V pyramidal neurons (arrowheads in Figure S4D).52 Neurons cultured with WT ACM showed enhanced dendrite outgrowth compared to MM, but Ptn KO ACM failed to induce dendrite outgrowth to the same extent. When neurons were exposed to Ptn KO ACM + recombinant Ptn protein, the total dendrite length was partially recovered (Figure S4E). We performed Sholl analysis to determine if there were significant differences in branching patterns as a measure of dendritic complexity. We observed that WT ACM induced more complex branching of dendrites compared to Ptn KO ACM. When Ptn was added to the KO ACM, dendritic complexity was partially recovered (Figure S4F). Importantly, in these experiments, the neurons were WT, and the loss of Ptn was only from the astrocytes. Altogether, these experiments identify that astrocyte-derived Ptn functions to promote dendrite outgrowth and branching.

Ptn KO mice have overlapping deficits with DS Ts65Dn Mut mice in measures of dendrite outgrowth, dendrite branching, and spine density alterations in vivo

We next assessed whether Ptn KO mice have dendrite outgrowth deficits in vivo, and how this compares to Ts65Dn mice. To do this, we crossed the Ptn KO and Ts65Dn mice to a fluorescent reporter line expressing yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) under the Thy1 promoter (Thy1-YFP), resulting in sparse labeling of Layer V pyramidal neurons.53 Total dendrite length, dendritic complexity, and spine density were analyzed in the VC at P30 and P120 to represent developmental and mature timepoints. We first confirmed that Layer V neurons from Ts65Dn Mut mice have decreased dendrite length. We found significant reductions in dendrite length compared to WT at both P30 and P120 (Figures 2A and 2B). Sholl analysis showed decreased dendritic complexity at both ages (Figures S5A and S5B). We next performed the same quantifications in the Ptn × Thy1-YFP line to see if Ptn KO mice have similar phenotypes as the Ts65Dn Mut mice. We found a significant reduction in total dendrite length at P30, with dendrite length normalizing to WT levels by P120 (Figures 2C and 2D). Similarly, Sholl analysis revealed decreased dendritic complexity in Ptn KO mice at P30 that recovered by P120, indicating neurodevelopmental delay (Figures S5C and S5D).

We analyzed spine density in Ptn KO and Ts65Dn mice in Layer V pyramidal neurons. Ts65Dn Mut mice showed no difference at P30, with a significant decrease at P120 (Figures 2E and 2F). Similarly, Ptn KO mice showed a significant decrease in spine density at P120 (Figures 2G and 2H). Spine morphology was assessed using previously published criteria for immature filopodia, thin, and long thin spines, as well as more mature stubby and mushroom morphologies.16,54 No significant changes in spine morphology were observed at P30. At P120, we observed an increase in the percentage of filopodia spines in Ts65Dn Mut mice, with Ptn KO mice showing the same trend (Figures S5E and S5F). The parallel phenotypes observed in Ptn KO and Ts65Dn Mut mice demonstrate that Ptn contributes to dendrite growth as well as spine formation during neurotypical development and suggest that decreased Ptn in Ts65Dn Mut astrocytes may contribute to the neuronal phenotypes observed.

Ptn KO and Ts65Dn mice have deficits in cortical synapse formation

As Ptn expression peaks during the time of synapse formation, we investigated the role of Ptn in developmental synapse formation by comparing synapse number in the VC of WT vs. Ptn KO mice.29 We used antibodies against the vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (Vglut1) and the alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor subunit GluA2 to label pre- and post-synaptic puncta of intracortical synapses. Due to the spatial enrichment of Ptn in the upper cortical layers during development, we focused our analysis on synapses in Layer II/III VC. At P30, we found a significant decrease in GluA2 and colocalized puncta compared to WT, with no change in Vglut1 puncta (Figures 3A and 3B). These impairments remained at the young adult P120 timepoint (Figures 3C and 3D). We also examined thalamocortical synapses using Vglut2 with GluA2 colocalization in Layer I and found that Ptn KO mice have a significant decrease in Vglut2, GluA2, and colocalized puncta at P30 and P120 (Figures 3E-3H).

Given that Ptn is decreased in the VC of Ts65Dn Mut mice, we next investigated if Ts65Dn Mut mice also have synapse impairments in this region. We found that Ts65Dn Mut mice have a significant decrease in colocalized Vglut1-GluA2 puncta at P30 that persists at P120 (Figures S6A-S6D). We did not detect any changes in thalamocortical synapses at either age (Figures S6E-S6H). These results demonstrate that Ptn has roles in the development of VC synaptic circuitry and that Ts65Dn Mut mice, which have reduced Ptn levels in VC, also have synaptic deficits in this region, albeit not fully overlapping phenotypes.

Ptn overexpression rescues dendrite and spine phenotypes in DS Ts65Dn Mut mice

The overlapping neuronal alterations observed in Ts65Dn Mut and Ptn KO mice provide a rationale to investigate whether overexpressing Ptn in Ts65Dn Mut mice can attenuate the neuronal deficits. Our cell enrichment quantification showed that Ptn is most highly expressed in astrocytes (Figure S2), suggesting that this cell type may be the best option to deliver Ptn to the system. We developed viral constructs to drive the expression of Ptn or the control spaghetti monster fluorescent protein (smFP; non-fluorescent-modified GFP55) in astrocytes using the minimal GfaABC1d (Gfap) promoter (Figure S7A). The PHP.eB serotype of adeno-associated virus (AAV)56 with retro-orbital delivery route was used to achieve brain-wide targeting (Figure S7B). We quantified these viral tools for penetrance and specificity in the VC and the hippocampus. In the hippocampus, hemagglutinin (HA) labeling was observed throughout the DG, CA3, and CA1 (Figures S7C-S7C′). HA staining colocalized with the S100β astrocyte marker but not neuronal nuclear antigen (NeuN)-labeled neurons for both the Ptn and smFP constructs (Figures S7C′-S7D). Penetrance of hippocampal astrocyte transduction was similar for both constructs, targeting an average of 67.7% and 65.7% of astrocytes for smFP and Ptn, respectively (Figure S7E). Furthermore, these constructs had high specificity with minimal (<1%) off-target transduction of neurons (Figure S7F). The VC had similar penetrance and specificity (Figures S7G-S7J).

We used these viral tools to determine if increasing levels of Ptn in Ts65Dn Mut mice can improve circuit defects. In these experiments, we had a four-group design: WT mice with smFP control (hereafter referred to as “WT”), WT mice with Ptn overexpression (“WT Ptn”), Ts65Dn Mut mice with smFP control (“Ts65Dn”), and Ts65Dn Mut mice with Ptn overexpression (“Ts65Dn Ptn”). The virus was injected at P60, after the dendrite phenotypes had developed, based on our previous characterization in Figure 2 (Figure 4A). We next performed histological analysis to assess if Ptn overexpression impacts total dendrite length, dendritic complexity, and spine density in Layer V VC neurons, as described in Figure 3. Representative filament tracings from reconstructed dendrites are depicted in Figure 4B. We found Ts65Dn dendrites to be significantly less complex compared to WT by Sholl analysis, reproducing our earlier findings. Importantly, dendritic complexity and total dendrite length were fully recovered to WT levels in Ts65Dn Ptn mice. We did not observe significant changes to dendritic complexity or total length in the WT Ptn group (Figures 4C-4E).

We next assessed spine density, finding that dendrites in Ts65Dn mice had reduced spine density compared to WT, again confirming our previous findings (Figure 2). Spine density in Ts65Dn Ptn mice was fully recovered to WT levels. WT Ptn dendritic spine density was not significantly altered from WT (Figures 4F and 4G). Due to our earlier findings that Ts65Dn mice have an increased percentage of filopodia spines, we next assessed whether Ptn overexpression affects filopodia abundance. The percentage of filopodia was not altered in Ts65Dn Ptn mice compared to Ts65Dn, but interestingly, we did detect a significant increase in filopodia spines in the WT Ptn group compared to the WT control (Figure 4H).

We next asked if Ptn would have similar effects on dendrite and spine morphology in other brain regions, focusing on the hippocampus, as previous reports demonstrate that Ts65Dn mice have dendrite length and spine density deficits in the hippocampal granule neurons.57,58 Thus, we focused our analyses on the DGML. The Thy1-YFP line does not exhibit sparse labeling in the hippocampus,53 which prevented accurate reconstruction of dendrites for morphological analysis. Therefore, we utilized a sparse viral labeling strategy to express GFP under the synapsin promoter (Syn-GFP) in a separate cohort of mice (Figure 5A). We performed filament reconstruction of the granule neurons in the same manner as in the cortical analysis (Figure 5B). We failed to detect a significant decrease in the complexity of Ts65Dn granule neuron dendrites compared to WT, but we note a strong trend. We did observe that Ts65Dn Ptn dendrites had significantly increased complexity compared to Ts65Dn dendrites (Figures 5C and 5D). When assessing total dendrite length, Ts65Dn dendrites were significantly shorter than WT dendrites, and Ptn fully recovered the dendrite length deficit (Figure 5E). Finally, we observed a significant reduction in spine density in Ts65Dn mice compared to WT, which was fully recovered with Ptn overexpression (Figures 5F and 5G). There were no changes in dendritic complexity, total dendrite length, or spine density in the WT Ptn group (Figures 5C-5E and 5G). In contrast to the VC, we did not observe differences in the percentage of filopodia spines between any of the groups (Figure 5H). These findings demonstrate that increasing the levels of Ptn in Ts65Dn Mut mice can restore neuronal dendrite length/complexity and spine density deficits in two distinct brain regions affected in DS.

Ptn promotes dendrite outgrowth through the Alk-Akt axis

After demonstrating that Ptn can improve dendrite outgrowth in cortical and hippocampal neurons, we next wanted to test the mechanism by which Ptn exerts these actions. Multiple Ptn receptors have been reported in the literature, including anaplastic lymphoma kinase (Alk) and protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type zeta1 (Ptprζ).59-61 Ptn was demonstrated to bind to Ptprζ, resulting in inhibition of its phosphatase activity, thereby resulting in increased levels of phosphorylated/activated Alk receptor.60,62 Furthermore, it was shown in vivo that neural stem cell-derived Ptn promotes dendritic complexity in newly born granule neurons via activation of the Alk-Akt axis63 (Figure S8A). We tested whether Ptn is acting through this same pathway to promote dendrite outgrowth in the context of DS. We first validated in neuronal cultures that 30 min of Ptn treatment stimulates phosphorylation of Akt by using western blotting to quantify pAkt Ser 473 to total Akt ratios. We found a significant increase in pAkt/total Akt ratios in the Ptn treatment condition (Figures S8B-S8D). Next, we utilized an in vitro culture system to test if inhibition of Alk-Akt signaling could block the dendrite outgrowth-inducing effects of Ptn on Ts65Dn neurons. In these experiments, ACM was generated from WT or Ts65Dn mice, and Ts65Dn Mut neurons were grown in the following conditions: minimal media (MM; no ACM), WT ACM, Ts65Dn Mut ACM, Ts65Dn Mut ACM + recombinant Ptn, and with Ts65Dn Mut ACM + Ptn + the Alk inhibitor TAE684 (Figure 6A). Map2 was used to quantify the dendrite length. We found that WT ACM induced dendrite outgrowth compared to MM in Ts65Dn Mut neurons, while Ts65Dn Mut ACM failed to promote the dendrite outgrowth. Addition of recombinant Ptn to Ts65Dn Mut ACM restored the ACM-induced dendrite outgrowth, but when TAE684 was added to the Mut ACM + Ptn media, Ptn failed to recover dendrite outgrowth (Figures 6B and 6C). Thus, we find that Ptn is promoting dendrite outgrowth in Ts65Dn neurons via the Alk-Akt signaling axis.

Ptn overexpression improves excitatory synapse phenotypes and synaptic plasticity deficits in DS Ts65Dn mice

To assess if Ptn can impact circuit-level changes in Ts65Dn mice, we assessed synapse number, synaptic plasticity, and behavioral performance. We ran WT, WT Ptn, Ts65Dn, and Ts65Dn mice through a behavioral battery (Figure S9A). However, we failed to detect robust behavioral deficits between WT and Ts65Dn groups in open field (Figures S9A-S9F), spontaneous alternation, and fear conditioning assays (Figures S10A and S10C-S10G). We did observe an increase in the number of arm entries in the Y maze in Ts65Dn mice compared to WT, suggesting hyperactivity in this assay, but Ptn did not improve this measure (Figure S10B). Overall, given the lack of behavioral differences between WT and Ts65Dn groups, we would not expect to see the effects of Ptn in Ts65Dn mice.

We next investigated if Ptn improved the synapse number, as we observed that Ptn can recover spine density deficits (Figures 4 and 5), but not all spines represent a functional synapse.64 We performed immunohistochemical staining for pre- and post-synaptic markers in the VC. We found no significant changes among genotype or treatment groups in Layer V where the pyramidal neuron cell bodies are located (Figures S11A-S11D). In Layer II/III, we saw moderate improvements in Vglut1 and colocalized puncta numbers in Ts65Dn Ptn compared to Ts65Dn, which did not reach significance (Figures S12A-S12D). Overall, we found stronger synaptic deficits in Ts65Dn mice compared to WT in the hippocampus DGML. We found that Ts65Dn mice have a significant reduction in Vglut1 compared to WT. Vglut1 puncta numbers were restored in the Ts65Dn Ptn group (Figures 7A and 7B). GluA2 and colocalized puncta were not significantly different between WT and Ts65Dn groups (Figures 7C and 7D). The reduced Vglut1 in the DGML is consistent with prior reports.57,65 We also assessed inhibitory synapse number in the DGML and found no changes between WT and Ts65Dn groups, consistent with a previous report,58 and also, no changes with Ptn overexpression (Figures S13A-S13D). Overall, these results show that Ptn can improve deficits in excitatory presynaptic Vglut1 puncta numbers in the DGML.

We next asked if Ptn is sufficient to impact the functional circuitry. Previous reports demonstrate that Ts65Dn mice have impairments in DG synaptic potentiation after high-frequency stimulation (HFS) of the middle molecular layer (MML), representing deficits in a form of short-term plasticity (STP).66,67 We performed stimulation and recording in the MML (Figure 7E). This electrode placement resulted in the typical paired pulse depression expected for this region66 (Figure S13E). We analyzed the paired pulse ratio and did not observe any significant differences between genotype or treatment groups (Figure S13F). We confirmed that after HFS, Ts65Dn mice fail to exhibit an STP response, whereas the STP response in Ts65Dn Ptn mice appears similar to WT (Figures 7F and 7G). We quantified the synaptic potentiation and found that Ts65Dn Ptn mice had a full recovery to WT levels. Synaptic potentiation was similar in WT Ptn and WT control groups (Figures 7F, 7G, and S13G). These results highlight the ability of Ptn to promote the correction of presynaptic puncta density and plasticity deficits in the hippocampal circuitry.

DISCUSSION

We find that Ptn is decreased in the Ts65Dn mouse model of DS, and through characterization of Ptn KO mice, we identify important neurodevelopmental phenotypes that are mirrored in Ts65Dn Mut mice. We demonstrate that overexpressing Ptn in astrocytes, the main cellular contributor of Ptn during development, rescues neuronal dendrite morphology and spine density in both the cortex and the hippocampus of Ts65Dn mice. Ptn overexpression also improves excitatory synapse number and synaptic potentiation in the hippocampus. These improvements are seen when delivering Ptn to Ts65Dn mice after phenotypes are already established in adult mice, showing reversibility outside of critical periods of developmental plasticity.

Roles of Ptn in neurotypical development

In the present study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of the spatiotemporal expression profile of Ptn during development. We find Ptn is expressed at high levels during periods of dendrite branching and synapse formation,47,48,68 and that Ptn KO mice have altered dendrite morphology and deficits in intracortical and thalamocortical synapse number. It should be noted that the thalamocortical synapse reduction may arise due to impaired navigation of these long-range projections to the target innervation zone. Previous reports describe that Ptn induces axonal outgrowth of thalamic neurons in vitro, and Ptn is expressed in vivo in the embryonic cortical subplate/intermediate zone, the migration path for destined thalamocortical projections.27,69,70 Genetic loss of Ptn specifically at postnatal timepoints will give insight into whether impaired axon guidance plays into this phenotype.

It is interesting that Ptn KO dendrites are shorter and less complex than WT dendrites at P30, but levels are comparable to WT by P120. This is in contrast to findings in the Ts65Dn Mut mice, where dendrite morphology remains impaired at P120. These findings suggest that in the case of genetic loss of Ptn, there may be other compensatory factors that are able to recover dendrite outgrowth. For example, midkine is a closely related paralog of Ptn that has overlapping, but not identical, functions.71 It is worth noting that in our previous mass spectrometry study, midkine secretion from Ts65Dn astrocytes was also downregulated,23 suggesting that the co-delivery of these factors to Ts65Dn mice should be investigated in the future to determine if this shows a potentiated response.

Ptn as a modulator of circuit activity

A main finding of this study is that overexpression of Ptn in Ts65Dn mice can recover deficits in STP plasticity in the DG. Interestingly, in previous reports, the DG STP phenotype was attributed to increased presynaptic GABA release and improved with GABA receptor antagonists.66,67 We show the recovery of the excitatory synapse number with Ptn. This is not necessarily contradictory to previous reports, and it should be investigated in the future whether Ptn alters the excitatory-inhibitory balance.

Others have reported changes to circuit activity in the hippocampus, which are Ptn dose-dependent. Ptn KO mice have a reduced threshold for LTP, but conversely, pressure injection of excess Ptn into hippocampal slices impairs LTP.28,29 These results suggest that the levels of Ptn may signal to the system to increase or decrease synaptic activity. We did not check CA1 LTP, and it would be interesting to test if the output of the DG to CA3 or CA1 is altered. Although there were no significant changes, we did note consistent trends toward reduced dendrite branching and spine density with Ptn overexpression in the WT context, along with an increase in filipodia spines in the cortex (Figures 4 and 5). These findings warrant future investigation into the effects Ptn may have on the circuitry when expressed above physiological levels. There are reports demonstrating that overexpression of Ptn in WT mice potentiates microglial reactivity in an acute inflammation model.72-74 Therefore, it will be necessary in the future to consider what levels of Ptn expression and duration of expression might be beneficial at different developmental stages, in different brain regions and different injury/disease contexts.

Roles of Ptn in the context of DS and other neurodevelopmental disorders

We identified Ptn as a candidate molecule by mining our recently published dataset describing transcriptomic and proteomic changes to astrocytes from several neurodevelopmental disorders.23 In addition to DS, Ptn protein was found at significantly decreased levels in the secreted protein fraction from Rett syndrome astrocytes, with a strong trend toward reduction in fragile X syndrome astrocytes. Ptn levels have also been reported as downregulated in schizophrenia organoids.75 This suggests that Ptn deficiency may be common to neural circuit impairments observed across multiple neurodevelopmental disorders. The ability of Ptn to induce circuit plasticity in adulthood is relevant with respect to therapeutic potential, particularly in DS, where translation from preclinical models to humans has often failed due to the need for early intervention with the approaches tested.5,76,77

Astrocytes as a target for circuit rewiring in neurodevelopmental disorders

We previously published that the astrocyte transcriptome and secreted protein repertoire are robustly altered in DS.23 Ptn was one candidate we chose to follow up on, but it is certainly not the sole factor accounting for the circuit aberrations. This is evident in the data showing that Ptn KO mice have a delayed dendrite branching phenotype that eventually normalizes to WT levels by P120, whereas the Ts65Dn dendrites remain impaired. Our previous study showed that Ts65Dn astrocytes upregulate the secretion of potentially damaging factors, including a ligand for phagocytic receptors, Gas6, and activators of the complement system, such as C3.23 Thus, it is likely a mixture of downregulation of dendrite outgrowth and synapse-inducing factors, as well as upregulation of damaging factors that results in the observed phenotypes. Given the roles of astrocytes in regulating the critical periods of plasticity,15,78 these cells are attractive therapeutic targets for delivering extracellular matrix and secreted synapse-modulating proteins to rewire brain circuitry in disordered contexts, and this study provides evidence of the feasibility of this approach.

Limitations of the study

Here, we used the Ts65Dn mouse model of DS to investigate whether Ptn overexpression could recover circuit aberrations. Ts65Dn mice do not perfectly recapitulate the genetics of DS, as their trisomic region contains an additional ~60 genes which are not homologous to HSA21.79 However, the T65Dn mouse model strongly recapitulates many of the neurodevelopmental phenotypes observed in humans and has a freely segregating chromosome that mimics the human condition, which is a particular strength of this model.4 It is notable that we did not detect strong behavioral phenotypes in our study. Other DS mouse models, such as Dp16, are reported to have strong behavioral deficits in learning/memory tasks, but Dp16 mice poorly recapitulate neurodevelopmental phenotypes.80,81 In the future, alternative mouse models of DS can be used to evaluate the effects of Ptn across models. Another limitation is that we tested the effects of overexpressing Ptn only in astrocytes. It is not known whether Ptn produced by other cell types has the same effects. Finally, we report that Ptn promotes dendrite outgrowth via stimulation of the Alk-Akt pathway, which is reported to be downstream of inactivation of the Ptprζ phosphatase activity.62 However, it should be noted that cortical neurons express low levels of Ptprζ compared to other protein tyrosine phosphatase (RPTP) receptors such as Ptprσ.25 An alternative mechanism of Ptn stimulation of neurite outgrowth is through sequestration of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans from Ptprσ, which reverses inhibition on neurite outgrowth.30 Whether Ptn binds directly or indirectly to Ptprζ, or another RPTP receptor, cannot be determined from this study and will be a focus of future work. However, it is clear that Ptn increases phosphorylation of Akt in neurons, and blocking activation of the upstream Alk receptor ablates the dendrite outgrowth-promoting effects of Ptn.

In conclusion, we present evidence that Ptn is an astrocyte-secreted protein that promotes development of neuronal dendrite morphology and synaptic circuitry during neurotypical development. We demonstrate that Ptn is downregulated in astrocytes from a commonly used mouse model of DS, and use viral-mediated overexpression to fully rescue dendrite and spine deficits, restore excitatory synapse number, and functionally improve hippocampal synaptic potentiation. This work opens investigative avenues for future astrocyte-based therapeutics and Ptn as a plasticity-inducing molecule that may be relevant to multiple neurodevelopmental disorders.

METHOD DETAILS

Primary astrocyte cultures

The cortices were dissected and meninges removed in ice-cold DPBS. The cortices were cut into ~1mm3 pieces using a razor blade to allow for optimal enzymatic digestion. Papain digestion was performed according to the kit instructions (Miltenyi MACS Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit 130-092-628). Digestion was performed using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (Miltenyi 130-096-427) using the 37C_NTDK_1 protocol provided by Miltenyi with a total time of 22 min. Cells were centrifuged at 4C 300 g for 10 min and then resuspended in AstroMACS buffer and passed through a 70-μm basket filter to remove clumps. Astrocytes were subsequently isolated using the Miltenyi MACS Anti-ACSA-2 Microbead kit (Miltenyi 130-097-678) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with slight modifications to deplete myelin debris and microglial cells prior to positive astrocyte selection. Myelin was depleted using a 10-min incubation at 4C with anti-myelin beads (Miltenyi 130-104-257). The MS magnetic separator column was then used to deplete the myelin from the cell suspension via magnetic separation. The flow-through was collected and centrifuged at 4C 300 g to remove excess beads. Microglia depletion was performed using anti-CD11b beads (Miltenyi 130-093-634) for an incubation period of 10 min at 4C. The MS magnetic separator column removed the microglia from the cell suspension via magnetic separation. The flow-through was collected and centrifuged again at 4C 300 g to remove excess beads. The supernatant was collected and subsequent positive selection for astrocytes was performed. First a 10-min incubation with FcR blocking beads was used to minimize any nonspecific binding. ACSA2-conjugated beads were then used to positively select for astrocytes during a 15-min incubation period at 4C (FcR and ACSA2 beads in kit, Miltenyi 130-097-678). The MS column was used to magnetize the ACSA2-beads and collect the astrocytes. The magnetized astrocytes were plunged into a new tube with AstroMACS buffer and centrifuged again at 4C 300 g to remove excess beads. Cells were counted using Trypan Blue solution (Gibco 15250-061) and plated in 6-well plates (Falcon 353046) coated with poly-D-lysine (Sigma P64047) at a density of 40,000–60,000 cells per well. Astrocytes were grown to confluency over 7 days in vitro (DIV) in astrocyte growth medium containing: 50% DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific 11960044), 50% Neurobasal (Thermo Fisher Scientific 21103049), Penicillin-Streptomycin (LifeTech 15140-122), Glutamax (LifeTech 35050-061), sodium pyruvate (LifeTech 11360-070), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (Sigma A8199), SATO (containing transferrin (Sigma T-1147), BSA (Sigma A-4161), progesterone (Sigma P6149), putrescine (Sigma P5780) and sodium selenite (Sigma S9133)), and heparin binding EGF like growth factor (HbEGF,5 ng/mL; R&D systems 259-HE/CF). Astrocyte cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37C/10% CO2.

Collection of astrocyte conditioned media

The growth media was removed from the 6-well plates after 7DIV, and the wells were washed three times with warm Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS; HyClone SH30264.01). Astrocytes were then cultured in minimal low protein conditioning media containing: 50% DMEM, 50% Neurobasal, penicillin-streptomycin, glutamax, sodium pyruvate, and HbEGF. The culture was maintained in a humidified incubator at 37C/10% CO2 for 4DIV. The conditioned media was concentrated using Vivaspin-6 centrifugal concentrators with a molecular weight cut-off of 10 kDa (Sartorius VS0602). Protein concentration in the astrocyte conditioned media (ACM) was assessed using a Qubit 4 fluorometer (Invitrogen Q33238) and the Qubit protein assay kit (Invitrogen Q33211). The ACM for neuronal dendrite outgrowth assays was stored at 4C for no longer than 1 week before use. The ACM used for the neuronal dendrite outgrowth assays was produced from either WT astrocytes or Ptn KO astrocytes in Figure S4 or from WT or Ts65Dn Mut astrocytes in Figure 6.

Primary neuron cultures

The cortices were dissected and meninges removed in ice-cold DPBS as described above. Papain digestion was performed the same as for primary astrocyte isolation. Cells were centrifuged at 4C 300 g for 10 min and then resuspended in cold DPBS and passed through a 70-μm basket filter to remove clumps. Neurons were subsequently isolated by biotin-conjugated magnetic bead depletion of non-neuronal cells using the Miltenyi Neuron Isolation Kit according to the manufacturer instructions (Miltenyi 130-115-389). Depletion with the non-neuronal antibody cocktail was performed for 5 min at 4C followed by centrifugation at 300g (4C) for 10 min. Anti-biotin beads were then used to remove the non-neuronal cells during a 10-min incubation at 4C. The cell suspension was applied to the Miltenyi LS column and the flow-through (now a neuron-enriched cell suspension) was collected. Trypan Blue was used to count cells and the neurons were plated on glass coverslips (12 mm diameter, Carolina Biological Supply 633029) coated with poly-D-lysine and laminin (R&D 3400-010-01) in 24-well plates (Falcon 353047) at a density of 40,000–60,000 cells per well. Neurons were cultured in media containing 50% DMEM, 50% Neurobasal, Penicillin-Streptomycin, Glutamax, sodium pyruvate, N-acetyl-L-cysteine, insulin (Sigma I6634), triiodo-thyronine (Sigma T6397), SATO, B27 (containing L-carnitine hydrochloride (Sigma C0283), ethanolamine (Sigma E9508), D(+)-galactose (Sigma G0625), putrescine (Sigma P5780), sodium selenite (Sigma S9133), corticosterone (Sigma C2505), linoleic acid (Sigma L1012), lipoic acid (Sigma T1395), progesterone (Sigma P8783), retinyl acetate (Sigma R7882), retinol (Sigma 95144), D,L-alpha tocopherol acetate (Sigma T3001), bovine albumin (Sigma A4161), catalase (Sigma C40), glutathione (Sigma G6013), insulin (Sigma I6634), superoxide dismutase (Sigma S5395), transferrin (Sigma T5391), triiodol-l-thyronine (Sigma T6397)), and forskolin (Sigma F6886). Cortical neuron cultures were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37C/10% CO2.

Western blotting to determine Ptn concentration

Western blotting was used to determine the physiological range of Ptn secretion from astrocytes. For determination of the physiological range of Ptn secretion from cultured astrocytes, four independent culture experiments were used. The ACM total protein concentration was assessed using the Qubit Protein Assay kit and ACM was loaded into the wells at inputs of 3 μg, 6 μg and 9 μg. Recombinant human Ptn protein was added into the wells at: 0.1 μg, 0.2 μg and 0.3 μg. The ACM and recombinant protein samples were heated in reducing loading dye (Thermo 39000) at 55C for 45 min. The samples were separated on 4–12% Bis-Tris gradient gels (Invitrogen NW04122BOX) at 80V for 1.5 h in MOPS SDS buffer (Invitrogen B0001.02). Proteins were transferred to PVDF membrane (Millipore IPFL00010) at 100V for 1 h in an ice bucket using Tris-Glycine buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific 28363) with 20% methanol (Fisher A412-4). The membrane was blocked in blocking buffer containing 0.1% Casein (BioRad 1610782) in Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS, BioRad 105300272) for one hour at room temperature on a shaker. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4C in blocking buffer with anti-Ptn antibody (1:500, goat, R&D Systems AF-252-PB). The next day the membrane was washed three times for ten minutes each in TBS with 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma P9416) followed by secondary antibody (diluted 1:20,000 in blocking buffer +0.1% Tween, donkey anti-goat 680, Invitrogen A21084) incubation for one hours at room temperature on a shaker. The membrane was washed three times for ten minutes each in TBS and the bands were visualized using the Odyssey Clx Infrared Imager (Li-Cor) and the band intensity was analyzed using Image Studio software (Li-Cor). The band intensities for WT ACM inputs were compared as ratios to the known input of recombinant Ptn to get an estimate of the fraction of Ptn protein of total protein in the ACM. These experiments yielded a range of 0.01–0.048 μg of Ptn in 1.5 μg of WT ACM, with an average of 0.025 μg Ptn. Based on this range, the final value of 0.025 μg Ptn was chosen for the in vitro experiments. Molecular weights were visualized using Precision Plus All Blue Protein standard loading dye (BioRad 1610373).

Treatment of neuronal cultures

Ptn KO ACM experiments

Primary neurons isolated from WT mice were cultured for 4DIV in either MM, WT ACM, Ptn KO ACM or Ptn KO ACM +0.025 μg recombinant human Ptn (50 ng/mL; Rescue ACM, R&D Systems 252-LP-050). 1.5 μg/well of ACM was added to MM for the conditions receiving ACM treatment. Ptn stimulation to evaluate Akt signaling

WT neurons were cultured 3DIV in minimal media. Ptn (50 ng/mL) was added for 30 min and neurons subsequently lysed in RIPA (Thermo Fisher Scientific 89901) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific PI78441). Proteins were denatured and gel electrophoresis and transfer performed as described above. Membranes were stained with antibody against phosphorylated Akt Ser 473 (rabbit at 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology 4060S) and beta actin (mouse a 1:5000; Sigma Aldrich A1978) or beta actin with total Akt antibody (rabbit a 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology 2938S). Dendrite outgrowth assay/immunocytochemistry

The cover slips with neurons were washed three times with warm DPBS followed by 20 min fixation with room temperature 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Electron Microscopy Sciences 50980487) diluted in PBS. Coverslips were washed three times with room temperature DPBS and subsequently blocked/permeabilized in a solution of 50% goat serum (Life Technologies 16210072)/50% antibody buffer (containing 150 mM sodium chloride (Sigma S5886); 50 mM Trizma base (Sigma T6066), 100 mM L-lysine (Sigma L5501), 1% bovine serum albumin (Sigma A2153), pH 7.4) and 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma T9284). The blocking step was performed for 30 min. Coverslips were then washed once in PBS and incubated overnight at 4C with antibodies against Map2 (1:2500, chicken, EnCor Biotechnologies CPCA-Map2) and Ctip2 (1:500, rat, Abcam ab18465) for Ptn KO ACM experiments, or Map2 alone for Ts65Dn Mut ACM experiments. The next day coverslips were washed three times in PBS and incubated for two hours with appropriate secondary antibodies (goat anti-chicken 488, 1:500, (Thermo Fisher Scientific A-11039) and goat anti-rat 555, 1:500, (Thermo Fisher Scientific A-21434)) in a solution of antibody buffer with 10% goat serum. Coverslips were washed three times in room temperature PBS and then stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific 62247) for 5 min. Excess DAPI was washed off with one PBS wash and then coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using Fluoromount G (Southern Biotech 0100-01). Imaging was performed with a 20× objective on a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope. Z-stacks were collected with 1.0 μm steps and a total of 5 steps. The maximum intensity projection images were used to assess total dendrite length and complexity. For each condition a total of 3 coverslips (technical replicates) were imaged per experiment. Five to ten images were acquired for each coverslip in each experiment. For Ptn KO ACM experiments, Ctip2+ cells in the image were selected for quantification if the entirety of its dendrite labeling was captured in the image. Quantification of total dendrite length and Sholl analysis for dendritic complexity was performed in Imaris v9.2.0 (Oxford Instruments) using the filament tracing tool. Sholl analysis was performed with a resolution of 10 μm. For Ts65Dn Mut ACM experiments all cells with Map2 labeling in the image were selected for quantification of total dendrite length if their dendrites were fully captured in the image. Four independent experiments were performed in each dataset (Figures S4 and 6).

Cloning of plasmids for viral vectors

Untagged mouse Ptn cDNA clone in a PCMV6-Kan/Neo vector was obtained from oriGene (Accession No. NM_008973, Origene MC204839). The pCAG_smFP-HA vector was obtained from Addgene (#59759). Ptn and smFP-HA were separately inserted into pZac2.1 GfaABC1d-tdTomato vector obtained from Addgene (#44332) after removing tdTomato sequence. The In-Fusion HD cloning kit (Takara 638909) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The GfaABC1d-tdTom vector was linearized at the NheI and XbaI (New England Biolabs R3131S, R0145S) restriction sites for three hours at 37C and the linearized vector was purified from an agarose gel and cleaned using Nucleospin columns (Macherey-Nagel 740609.50). The Ptn cDNA was PCR amplified and an HA tag added to the C terminus using the following primer sequences with 15–16 bp overlapping sequences to the linearized vector:

Forward (5′ to 3′): ctcactataggctagcatgtcgtcccagcaatatca

Reverse (5′ to 3′ ): caggagaagatgctggattatccgtatgatgttccggattatgcatagtctagagtcga

The PCR product was subsequently gel purified and cleaned with the Nucleospin columns. The In-Fusion HD cloning enzyme premix was used to insert the Ptn-HA sequence into the vector.

The smFP-HA was cloned into the GfaABC1d-tdTom vector using the same procedure. The primer sequences used for smFP are as follows:

Forward (5′ to 3′): ctcactataggctagcatgtacccttatgatgtgcccgattatgct

Reverse (5′ to 3′ ): gtacgatgtcccggactacgcttaatctagagtcgacccgg

Stellar competent E. Coli cells (Takara 636763) were then transformed with the plasmids via heat-shock according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Bacteria were screened using carbenicillin resistance (100 μg/mL; Teknova C2130). The colonies were expanded in an overnight LB culture. The DNA was purified using an endotoxin-free plasmid MaxiPrep kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen 12362).

Viral vector generation

Plasmids were sequenced for accuracy using Eton Sequencing before packaging into AAV at the Salk Institute Viral Vector (GT3) core facility. Viruses were packaged as AAV PHP.eB serotype to allow for systemic retroorbital delivery56 in order to obtain brain-wide transduction of astrocytes.

Retroorbital virus injections

Mice at P60 were anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen by constant flow through a nose cone. Gfap-smFP and Gfap-Ptn was injected via retroorbital administration at a dose of 1.0E12 viral genome copies in a total injection volume of 160 μL. In the cohort for hippocampus rescue, hSynapsin-GFP (Addgene 50465, a gift from Bryan Roth) was added to the solution at 1.0E10. The viruses were diluted in DPBS just prior to injection. Mice were allowed to recover from anesthesia and then returned to the housing room.

Tissue collection and preparation for histology

Mice were deeply anesthetized with intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of 100 mg/kg Ketamine (Victor Medical Company 1699053) and 20 mg/kg Xylazine (Victor Medical Company 1264078) and transcardially perfused. For fixed tissue collection, mice were perfused first with room temperature PBS followed by 4% PFA. The brains were rapidly dissected and post-fixed overnight in 4% PFA (Electron Microscopy Sciences 19208) at 4C. After 24 h the brains were moved to 30% sucrose (Invitrogen 1503-022) for 24–48 h. The brains were then embedded in TFM (Electron Microscopy Sciences 72593) and frozen in a dry ice/ethanol slurry and stored at −80C until use.

Single molecule fluorescent in situ hybridization

smFISH was used to characterize the spatiotemporal pattern of Ptn expression in astrocytes, confirm loss of Ptn in Ptn KO mice and differences in Ptn mRNA expression levels in astrocytes, neurons, OPCs and microglia in WT and in Ts65Dn mice. FISH was performed with the RNAScope Fluorescent Multiplex Detection v1 kit (ACD 320851) or v2 kit (ACD 323100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for fixed tissue (protocols ACD 320535-TN and ACD 323100/RevB-UM, respectively).

Characterization of developmental Ptn expression

These experiments utilized paraformaldehyde fixed tissue and the RNAScope v1 kit. Sagittal sections of brain were sliced at 16 μm using a cryostat (Hacker Industries OTF5000). Sections were obtained from Aldh1L1-EGFP mice at P7, P14 and P30. An epitope retrieval step was performed at 95C for 20 min using the ACD epitope retrieval buffer. Slides were then washed once in PBS and dehydrated in 100% ethanol before beginning protease treatment. Protease III was used for P7 samples and Protease IV was used for P14 and P30 samples. All protease treatment was performed for 30 min at 40C in the hybridization oven. Mus musculus Ptn probe (ACD 486381) was hybridized for 2 h at 40C. Amplification steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions without deviation. Ptn probe was conjugated to the 550-channel. After completion of the probe labeling, immunohistochemical labeling was performed to amplify the transgenic GFP signal in astrocytes. Briefly, slides were blocked and permeabilized in blocking buffer containing 10% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were then incubated overnight at 4C with anti-GFP antibody (1:500, chicken, Novus Biological NB100-1614) diluted in an antibody dilution buffer containing 5% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton. The next day slides were washed in PBS three times for 5, 10 and 15 min. Secondary antibody was applied (1:500, goat anti-chicken 488) for 2 h at room temperature in antibody dilution buffer. Slides were washed three times for five, ten and fifteen minutes. DAPI was applied (1:1000) for five minutes. Slides were washed once in PBS and then coverslips (24 × 60 mm and 1.5 thickness, Fisher 12541037) were mounted with Fluoromount G. Five independent experiments were performed. Imaging was performed using a 40× objective on a Zeiss LSM 700 confocal microscope. Laser and gain intensities were maintained as constant for each image. Tiled z-stacks were obtained from the visual cortex and the hippocampus with 0.4 μm z-steps and a total of 12 steps. Three slices were imaged for each mouse. Layer I and Layer II/III in the cortex, and CA1 and ML, in the hippocampus were segmented out as separate images in FIJI using the DAPI channel. Images were quantified using a custom macro in FIJI that masks the astrocytes (cell body with proximal processes) and measures the thresholded mRNA signal within the mask of each cell as previously described.21 The threshold was maintained as constant for each image within an experiment. This analysis was performed on maximum intensity projections. Example images in Figure 1 show the maximum intensity projection for each timepoint.

Confirmation of Ptn KO

Coronal sections of 16 μm were obtained from WT and Ptn KO mice at P30. Epitope retrieval, FISH and immunohistochemical labeling for S100β was performed on fixed tissue in the same manner as described above using the RNAScope v1 kit. Images were obtained on the LSM 700 confocal microscope using a 40× objective. Tiled z-stacks were collected from the visual cortex with 1 μm z-steps and a total of 7 steps. Constant laser and gain settings were used for WT and KO pairs. Images shown are maximum intensity projections.

Spatial and cell type-enrichment analysis

Sagittal sections of brain were sliced as described above and processed with the RNAScope v2 kit. A pair of WT and Ts65Dn Mut was always processed together in the same hybridization experiment. Slides were baked at 55C and then treated with hydrogen peroxide for 8 min. Slides were washed two times for two minutes each in distilled water. Protease III treatment was applied for 12 min at 40C in the hybridization oven. After protease treatment, the hybridization, amplification and fluorophore conjugation steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions without modification. Ptn probe was always conjugated to the Opal 570 dye (Akoya Biosciences SKU FP1488001KT). For comparison of Ptn expression in the different brain cell types, Opal 570 was added at 1:2000. A probe for Mus musculus Slc1a3 (ACD 430781-C2) was used to label astrocytes, Mus musculus Cspg4 (ACD 404131-C3) to label OPCs, Mus musculus Tubb3 (ACD 423391-C3) to label neurons and Iba1 antibody (rabbit at 1:250; Wako Fujifilm 016–20001) for microglia. The cellular marker mRNA probes were conjugated to fluorophore at 1:1500. 20x z stack images were used to tile the visual cortex (20 z-steps at 1 μm increments, 8 tiles) to visualize spatial enrichment patterns of Ptn within different cortical layers in WT astrocytes and neurons. The levels of Ptn expression were quantified within the cell body masks using a custom macro in FIJI as described above. To quantify levels of Ptn expression between cell types in neurotypical development, 40x z stack images were taken in Layers I-III of VC at a step size of 1.0 μm and a total of 6 z-steps. Individual cells were rendered for volumetric analysis using the surface tool in Imaris v9.9.9. The signal was thresholded by intensity to create surface masks of astrocytes, neurons, OPCs and microglia in the different images and disconnected components manually unified for accuracy. A mask was created for the Ptn signal within the cell volume surface. The volume of Ptn was compared between WT astrocytes, neurons, OPCs and microglia and normalized to the cell volume. We found the 3D volumetric analysis gave a more accurate representation of the Ptn signal within each cell type compared to using collapsed z-stacks in FIJI as described above for the developmental analysis of Ptn in astrocytes. This is due to the nature of the glial cell processes overlapping with and physically touching other cell types. We note that this analysis is slightly biased due to microglia labeled with Iba1 antibody having more extensive process labeling compared to the neurons, astrocytes and OPCs, which might lead to an underestimation of the Ptn levels in microglia. Therefore, we validated our cell enrichment analysis with a published single nucleus RNA-sequencing dataset from the VC at the same age,44 which showed highly similar relative levels of Ptn expression between the cell types. To quantify differences in Ptn expression between WT and Ts65Dn Mut mice the same dataset was used for neurons, OPCs and microglia. However, the 1:2000 concentration of the fluorophore necessary to visualize Ptn in neurons and microglia, which express the transcript at lower levels, was saturating in the astrocytes. Therefore, we used the fluorophore at 1:5000 to label Ptn in astrocytes. In these hybridizations we used the S100β antibody (rabbit at 1:500, Abcam 52642) as the astrocyte cell label because it allowed for a more accurate surface rendering of the volume of a typical astrocyte, due to S100β antibody more efficiently filling the astrocyte processes in comparison to the Glast mRNA probe. 40x z-stacks were taken and surface rendering performed as described above. The volume of Ptn within each astrocyte was compared between WT and Ts65Dn Mut mice. The volume was normalized to cell size and the Mut value expressed as a ratio of the WT for each pair within a hybridization.

Validation of viral constructs

The penetrance and specificity of the GfaABC1d-Ptn-HA and GfaABC1d-smFP-HA viruses were quantified. Viruses were injected and expressed for two months before collection of the brains. Coronal sections of 16 μm were obtained from mice injected with each virus for validation of viral penetrance and specificity in the cortex. Sagittal sections of 16 μm were used for validation in the hippocampus. TFM was removed with a PBS wash. Slices were blocked and permeabilized in blocking buffer (10% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton) for one hour at room temperature. Overnight staining with antibody dilution solution (5% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton) was performed at 4C. Antibodies for the astrocyte markers Sox9 (cortex, rabbit at 1:1000, Abcam ab185966) and S100β (hippocampus, guinea pig at 1:250, Synaptic Systems 287004) were used to check for penetrance of the virus labeled with anti-HA antibody (1:500, rat, Sigma 11867423001). For the cortex, the neuronal marker NeuN (1:500, rabbit pre-conjugated to 647, Abcam ab190565) was used in separate tissue sections and co-labeled with anti-HA antibody to check for off-target transduction. In the hippocampus S100β and NeuN were used on the same sections. The next day slices were washed three times in PBS with 0.2% Triton for 5, 10 and 15 min. Secondary antibody labeling was performed in 5% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton for two hours at room temperature. Appropriate secondary antibodies were used (1:500, goat anti-rabbit 488, Thermo Fisher Scientific A11034; 1:500, goat anti-rat 555, Thermo Fisher Scientific A21434; 1:500, goat anti-guinea pig 488, Thermo Fisher Scientific A11073). Washes in PBS with 0.2% Triton were performed three times for 5, 10 and 15 min. DAPI was applied (1:1000) for 5 min followed by a PBS wash. Coverslips were mounted with Fluoromount G. Quantification of viral penetrance and specificity was performed in three to four mice for each virus. Images were obtained on an LSM 700 confocal microscope using a 20× objective. Tiled z-stacks in the visual cortex were acquired with 2.0 μm z-step increments and a total of 8 z-steps. The z-stacks in the hippocampus were acquired with 1.0 μm z-step increments and a total of 15 z-steps. Two to three slices/mouse were imaged. The images were stitched and maximum intensity projections were created in Zen software. The images were then imported into FIJI for cell counting analysis using the cell counter plug-in. The number of Sox9+ or S100β+ cells were counted in each image to obtain the total number of astrocytes and then the number of HA + astrocytes was counted to get a percentage of total astrocytes expressing HA. The off-target expression in neurons was quantified as the percentage of HA + cells that were NeuN+. An overview image of an entire sagittal brain slice was acquired at 10x to demonstrate expression pattern throughout the brain with the retroorbital administration route (Figure S7B).

Ex vivo dendrite and spine analyses

Fixed tissue coronal slices of 50 μm thickness were obtained from the Ptn x Thy1-YFP and Ts65Dn x Thy1-YFP lines or Ts65Dn mice injected with hSyn-GFP virus. These preparations were used for the characterizations of total dendrite length, dendritic complexity, spine density and spine morphology of Layer V pyramidal neurons and hippocampal DG granule neurons. TFM was removed with a PBS wash and coverslips were mounted using Fluoromount G. Triangular pieces of parafilm were cut and placed at the top right and bottom left corners of the slides to prevent the coverslip from crushing the thick tissue and obscuring spine morphology. All subsequent analyses were performed blinded to genotype and treatment.

Dendrite length and complexity analysis

Imaging was performed on an LSM 700 confocal microscope. Six to eight independent experiments were performed for Ptn WT vs. KO, Ts65Dn WT vs. Mut and the 4-group rescue experiments (WT smFP, WT Ptn, Ts65Dn Mut smFP and Ts65Dn Mut Ptn). Laser settings and gain were maintained constant within each experiment. Tiled z-stacks of the visual cortex or hippocampus were obtained to include at least 3–5 labeled cells per image. Images were obtained using a 20× objective. The z-steps were obtained at 0.5 μm increments with a total of 60 steps. Three slices/per mouse were imaged for each experiment. Zen Blue 3.3 was used to stitch the images and the stitched z stack images were then imported into Imaris where the filament tracing tool was used to manually trace individual dendrites. Dendrites were traced if they were completely contained in the z stack and did not appear to be cut through the z-dimension. Imaris outputs total dendrite length as the sum of all individual processes for a given filament (dendrite). Sholl analysis was performed using 50 μm increments from the cell body. Imaris outputs the number of branches intersecting with the Sholl circles as a function of distance from the cell body. Quantifications for total dendrite length and Sholl analysis were performed on 15–30 dendrites/mouse for a total of 120–180 dendrites/group analyzed. The data is analyzed as the individual mouse averages for greater statistical stringency. Images shown in Figure 2 are maximum intensity projections. Filaments shown in Figures 4 and 5 were exported as TIFF files representing the scene from the Imaris analysis file.

Spine density and morphology analysis

Imaging of dendritic spines was performed on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope with Airyscan imaging modality. Six to eight independent experiments were performed for each dataset. Laser settings and gain were maintained constant within each experiment. Images were obtained using a 63× objective with 2.0 zoom. The z-steps were obtained at 0.17 μm increments and the number of steps for each image was determined on an individual dendrite basis to encompass the entire dendrite. In the cortex, dendrites were chosen for imaging based on the following criteria: 1.) the cell body resided in Layer V and the dendrite reached to the upper layers (Layers I-III), 2.) the primary branchpoint on the dendrite resided in the upper layers, and 3.) the dendrite in the image could be resolved from surrounding dendrite branches when focusing through the z stack. In the hippocampus, granule neuron dendrites were imaged from the primary branchpoint in the molecular layer with the qualification that it could be resolved from other surrounding dendrites. For each individual mouse, five separate dendrites were imaged that were sampled from at least two separate tissue sections. The images were Airyscan processed in Zen and exported as TIFF files. These images were imported into NeuronStudio for spine density and morphology analysis. Spine heads were labeled within NeuronStudio and the spine head width and neck length measurements were exported. These measurements were used to assign spine morphology based on the classifications described previously.16,54 Quantification of spine density (number of spines/μm) was performed on the average of analyzed dendrites/mouse for statistical stringency. Spine morphology quantifications were performed on the total of all dendrites analyzed/mouse and presented as a percentage of total spines for each morphological category. Images presented in Figures 2, 4, and 5 are maximum intensity projections.

Immunohistochemical synaptic analysis

Fixed tissue coronal slices of 16 μm thickness were obtained from the Ptn, Ts65Dn, and Ts65Dn x Thy1-YFP lines (YFP-ve progeny). TFM was removed with a PBS wash. Slices were blocked and permeabilized in blocking buffer (10% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton) for one hour at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted in antibody dilution solution (5% goat serum in PBS with 0.2% Triton) and stained overnight in a humidified chamber at 4C. The antibodies used for excitatory pre-synaptic puncta were Vglut1 (1:2000, guinea pig, Millipore AB5905) and Vglut2 (1:2000, guinea pig, Millipore AB2251-I). Excitatory post-synaptic puncta were labeled with GluA2 (1:400, rabbit, AB1768-I). For inhibitory synapses, an antibody against pre-synaptic Vgat (guinea pig at 1:500, Synaptic Systems 131 004) and an antibody against post-synaptic gephyrin (rabbit at 1:500, Synaptic Systems 147 008) was used. The next day the slides were washed in PBS with 0.2% Triton three times for 5, 10 and 15 min. Appropriate secondary antibodies were used in antibody dilution solution. Secondary antibodies were applied for two hours at room temperature followed by three washes of 5, 10 and 15 min in PBS with 0.2% Triton. DAPI was applied (1:1000) for five minutes followed by a PBS wash. Coverslips were mounted with Fluoromount G. For the characterization experiments (Figures 3 and S6) GluA2 was labeled on the 488-channel (1:500, goat anti-rabbit 488) and Vglut1/2 were labeled on the 594-channel (1:500, goat anti-guinea pig 594, Thermo Fisher Scientific A11076). For the rescue experiments, Vglut1 was labeled on the 488-channel (1:500, goat anti-guinea pig 488, Thermo Fisher Scientific A11073) and GluA2 was labeled on the 647-channel (1:500, goat anti-rabbit 647, Thermo Fisher Scientific A21245). In these stainings, anti-HA antibody (1:500, rat) was added to confirm viral transduction within each mouse and HA was labeled on the 555-channel (1:500, goat anti-rat). In separate sections, Vgat was labeled on the 488-channel (1:500, goat anti-guinea pig 488) and gephyrin on the 647-channel (1:500, goat anti-rabbit 647). Stainings in these datasets were collected over 5–8 independent experiments. Images were obtained using a 63× objective with 1.2 zoom on an LSM 880 microscope. The laser settings and gain were maintained constant within each experiment. Z-stacks of 0.4 μm increments were obtained separately in Layer I for Vglut2/GluA2 and Layer II/III for Vglut1/GluA2 with a total of 10 z-steps per image. The same objective, z-increments and total thickness were used for imaging of Vglut1/GluA2 excitatory and Vgat/Gephyrin inhibitory synapses in the DG ML. Three slices/per mouse were imaged for each experiment. Quantification of colocalized puncta was performed as previously described using the Imaris spots tool and the colocalization algorithm.15,16,21 Spot sizes were set to 0.4 μm for Vglut1, Vgat and GluA2 puncta, 0.3 μm to gephyrin puncta and 0.5 μm for Vglut2 puncta. A colocalization distance of 0.7 μm was used. Variability between individual staining experiments necessitated the data for Ptn KO or Ts65Dn Mut mice to be normalized to the WT within each experiment. In the rescue cohort, data points are normalized to the average of the WT condition. Images shown in Figures 2, 7, S6, and S11-S13 are single z planes.

Behavioral analysis

Behavioral testing was performed in the Salk Institute’s In Vivo Scientific Services (Animal Resources Department). Behavioral tests were assayed in the following order with 1–2 days rest in between each test: open field, spontaneous alternation Y maze and fear conditioning. All tests were performed in the morning between the hours of ZT1 to ZT5.5. Brain tissue was collected immediately after fear conditioning. All behavioral tests were performed blinded to genotype.

Open field

Mice were habituated to the testing room for thirty minutes prior to initiation of the test. The room was lit by indirect lighting of ~30 lux. Activity Monitor software (Med Associates) was used to assess the locomotor activity over a ten-minute period. Mice were placed into the open field chamber (43.2 × 43.2 × 30.5 cm; Med Associates Inc. ENV-515S-A) facing the outer plexiglass wall. Motion data was collected with Activity Monitor v5.92 software (Med Associates Inc.) and analyzed. At the conclusion of the test, mice were returned to their home cage and placed back into the housing facility. Analysis was performed using Activity Monitor v6.02 (Med Associates Inc.). Data was analyzed as 5-min blocks across the ten-minute test duration. The total distance traveled between the 4 groups tested (WT smFP, WT Ptn, Ts65Dn Mut smFP and Ts65Dn Mut Ptn) was assessed, as well as the percentage of time in center bins vs. peripheral bins.

Spontaneous alternation Y maze

Mice were habituated to the testing room for thirty minutes prior to testing. The room was lit by indirect lighting of ~30 lux. The mouse was placed at the distal end of the starting arm (Arm A) and allowed to explore freely in any of the three arms (Arm A, Arm B, Arm C) over a testing period of eight minutes. The dimensions of arms were 17.0 cm × 6.5 cm x 23.0 cm. After the completion of the testing period the mouse was returned to its home cage. ANY-maze v7.2 software (San Diego Instruments) was used for motion tracking and automated analysis. The percentage of correct alternations was quantified as well as the total number of arm entries during the test duration. A correct alternation is defined as a triad of three entries without a repeat entry into a previous arm in the triad.

Fear conditioning

Mice were habituated to the testing room for one hour prior to testing. The room was lit by indirect lighting of ~30 lux. The fear conditioning protocol used in this study is a two-day protocol. On the first day, mice were placed into the conditioning chamber (24.0 × 30.0 × 21.0 cm, Med Associates Inc. VFC-008) for a 3-min habituation period before being exposed to a series of foot shocks that are paired to an acoustic tone. The tone (90 dB/5000 Hz) is presented prior to each foot shock. The tones were presented at 180, 300 and 420 s for 30-s durations. The foot shocks were delivered directly after termination of the tone at 0.5 mA for two seconds. The mice learn to associate the foot shock with the tone and typically exhibit a freezing behavior. The mice were kept outside of the testing room in an anteroom to avoid pre-exposure to the acoustic tone. On the second day, the mice were tested in two separate paradigms. The first paradigm was used to test the contextual memory of the chamber. The mice were placed into the same chamber configuration as on the first day during the training period. The test duration was three minutes. In this paradigm there was no foot shock or tone and the mice exhibited freezing behavior simply in response to the contextual memory of the chamber. After the contextual memory paradigm, the mice were tested in a novel context and with the auditory cue and distinct visual, tactile and olfactory cues from the previous chamber. This paradigm tests the memory association of the auditory cue with the foot shock. The test duration was three minutes. The tone was presented three times at 20, 70 and 120 s for a duration of 30 s. Video Freeze software v.2.07.0.106 (Med Associates) was used for data analysis. Freezing was defined as a minimum motion threshold of 18 over 15 frames (0.5 s). The freezing behavior was quantified as the percentage of total time freezing within the test periods for the same context and novel context paradigms. For the learned association paradigm on the first day, the freezing behavior was quantified as the percentage of time freezing within defined periods (pre-shock, after first shock to second shock, second shock to third shock, and remainder of time after third shock).

Electrophysiology

Acute hippocampal slices preparation

Slices were prepared from 4-month-old animals injected with Gfap-Ptn or Gfap-Smfp. Animals were deeply anesthetized by injection with a 2.5% Avertin solution and immediately decapitated. The hippocampi were extracted, enclaved into a block of 4% agar and cut into 350 μm transverse sections using a Leica VT1000s vibratome. The brain dissection was performed in physiological temperature (37°C), with choline chloride-based dissection solution composed of (in mM): 93 choline chloride, 2.5 KCl, 2 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 20 HEPES, 5 Sodium L-ascorbate, 25 D-glucose and 24 NaHCO3. Slices were then placed in a recovery chamber containing HEPES artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) composed of (in mM): 5 HEPES, 93 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 5 Sodium L-ascorbate, 25 D-glucose and 24 NaHCO3. Slices recovered for 10 min at 37°C and then for at least 2 h at room temperature before recordings were performed until 6–7 h after slicing. Solutions were supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate and continuously equilibrated with carbogen (95% O2/5% CO2).

Recording of evoked field potentials

Slices were placed in a recording chamber and perfused with carbogen-saturated aCSF composed of (in mM): 126 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.2 MgCl2, 2.5 CaCl2, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 11 D-glucose and 25 NaHCO3, supplemented with 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Under visual control, the stimulating electrode (tungsten concentric bipolar microelectrode WPI #TM33CCINS-B) was inserted into the middle molecular layer (MML), and the recording electrode (borosilicate glass pipette; Harvard Apparatus #BS4 64–0805, ~0.5 MΩ of resistance, filled with 2 M NaCl) was inserted into the MML ~300 μm apart from the recording electrode. For synaptic potentiation studies, we reproduced the protocol described by Kleschevnikov and colleagues.66,67 Testing stimuli (duration 100 μs, currents 60–100 μA) evoked field responses with amplitudes of 70–80% of maximum. To ensure that responses were from the MML, we first apply paired stimuli with an intra-stimulus interval of 50 ms producing paired-pulse depression responses. The paired-pulse ratio was calculated by dividing the amplitude of the second response by the amplitude of the first response (Figure S13F). Baseline field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) response was then recorded until stable for at least 10 min. Synaptic potentiation was induced by tetanizations consisting of 4 trains of stimuli (0.5 s at 100Hz) applied with a 10 s interval, and post tetanus fEPSP were recorded for 50 min. Recordings were performed using Axon Digidata 1550A and a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices) and sampled at 10 kHz. Analyses were performed using Clampfit 10.7 (Molecular Devices) with a 0.5 kHz post-hoc filter. The initial slope of fEPSP was measured at latencies 0.1–0.9 ms after the stimulation artifact. The evolution of fEPSP initial slope over time was represented as percentage of the average baseline response (Figure 7G).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analysis and data visualization was performed in Graphpad Prism 10. All tests were two-tailed. Data was tested for normality using either Shapiro-Wilk or Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. For normally distributed data, t tests (or one-sample t tests) were used to compare two groups, one-way ANOVAs were used to compare three groups, and two-way ANOVAs were used to compare multiple groups and treatments. If data was not normally distributed, pairwise comparisons were performed using Mann Whitney Rank-Sum test (or one-sample Wilcoxon Signed Rank sum) and multiple comparisons were performed using Kruskal-Wallis on ranks. For synaptic potentiation experiments, we performed Repeated Measures two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s correction for multiple comparisons, comparing each timepoint to the last datapoint of the baseline right before tetanization. All bar graphs and line graphs represent mean ± SEM. Figures were generated using Adobe Illustrator 2022 and Biorender.
