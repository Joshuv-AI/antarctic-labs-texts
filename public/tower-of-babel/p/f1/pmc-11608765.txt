Gender, BMI, and Age‐Related Variations in Lower Limb Alignment Parameters and CPAK Phenotypes in Chinese Patients with Knee Osteoarthritis
Authors: Songlin Li, Xi Chen, Sen Liu, Hongjun Xu, Yang Yu, Shanni Li, Zhaojing Yin, Yiyang Du, Miaomiao Zhang, Peilai Liu, Wenwei Qian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesResearch on the distribution of and the variation in coronal plane alignment of the knee (CPAK) in the Chinese osteoarthritis population is limited. We aimed to establish the CPAK classification based on the characteristics of lower limb alignment in the Chinese osteoarthritis population. We also investigated variations in lower limb alignment parameters and CPAK phenotypes based on gender, body mass index (BMI), and age.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on a total of 944 knees diagnosed with osteoarthritis in 479 patients from January 2017 to December 2023. A scatterplot was used to describe the distribution of the CPAK classification, and the differences in lower limb alignment parameters and the CPAK classification were compared across genders (male, female), ages (middle‐aged/<65 years, elderly/≥65 years), and BMI categories (normal/<25 kg/m2, overweight and obese/≥25 kg/m2) using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test.ResultsThe average arithmetic hip‐knee‐ankle angle and joint line obliquity (JLO) were −3.03° ± 5.69° and 174.45° ± 4.29°, respectively. There was a higher prevalence of constitutional varus alignment in males and the overweight or obese group, while constitutional valgus alignment was more common in females and the normal BMI group (p < 0.05). Additionally, females had a greater apex distal JLO than males (p < 0.05). There were no statistically significant differences in lower limb alignment parameters among different age groups (p > 0.05). Although there were variations in alignment parameters across different genders and BMI categories in the knee osteoarthritis population, the predominant CPAK classifications were type I (38.03%), followed by type II (20.02%) and type IV (17.06%).ConclusionThe most common CPAK types were I, II, and IV, and they were not influenced by gender, BMI, or age, indicating that the CPAK classification can reliably reflect constitutional alignment. A better understanding of native alignment variability can aid in providing patient‐specific recommendations when considering orthopedic alignment strategies.Keywords: arthroplasty, CPAK, knee alignment, knee osteoarthritis

Introduction

Mechanical alignment (MA) is the acknowledged principle of successful alignment in total knee arthroplasty (TKA).1, 2 Optimizing prosthesis longevity, knee joint function, and overall patient satisfaction is the primary objective of TKA. However, reports indicate dissatisfaction rates as high as 20% among patients who have undergone traditional TKA.3, 4 While the causes of dissatisfaction are numerous, alterations in limb alignment may be significant. 5 The distribution of lower limb alignment across the population is diverse, with many individuals exhibiting varus or valgus alignment following skeletal maturation. 5 Restoring neutral alignment for these individuals may be unnatural, which could then affect patient satisfaction.

Classification of the coronal plane alignment of the knee (CPAK) provides a simple yet comprehensive system for describing lower limb alignment. 6 This classification has found no differences in the distribution of CPAK types between patients with knee osteoarthritis and healthy individuals. The CPAK method uses the arithmetic hip‐knee‐ankle angle (aHKA) and the joint line obliquity (JLO), based on the lateral distal femoral angle (LDFA) and the medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA). The aHKA can be categorized as varus, neutral, or valgus constitutional limb alignment, and the JLO can be categorized as apex distal, neutral, or apex proximal. These categories combine to form nine distinct phenotypes of CPAK. Patients with CPAK types I, II, and IV may achieve a better intraoperative balance with kinematic alignment (KA) than those with MA during TKA, providing a reference for surgeons when formulating preoperative plans. 6 The CPAK classification is gradually being accepted by scholars in an increasing number of countries, although there is limited research on this classification in China at present.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

The Asian population has a higher prevalence of varus limb alignment and a broader distribution of lower limb alignment.12, 14 Hsu et al. analyzed the distribution of CPAK types in 214 healthy knee joints in Taiwan, China. 15 However, their sample size was limited, and data on the distribution of CPAK types among individuals with osteoarthritis were lacking. Subsequently, Gao et al. and Liu et al. measured alignment parameters on long leg radiographs and CT scans and summarized the distribution of CPAK types in a limited sample size of patients with osteoarthritis. 8 , 16 There is also limited research on whether the distribution of CPAK types varies among different genders, BMI categories, and age groups. The objectives of this study were to explore the distribution of CPAK in a large Chinese osteoarthritis population and investigate whether there are differences in lower limb alignment parameters and CPAK types based on gender, BMI, and age.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by our institutional ethics committee (I‐24PJ1013). We retrospectively collected clinical data of patients who underwent primary TKA or unicompartmental knee arthroplasty at our institution from January 2017 to December 2023. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) rheumatoid arthritis; (2) traumatic arthritis; (3) spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee; (4) history of lower limb surgery; (5) extra‐articular deformity; (6) Paget's disease of bone; (7) tuberculosis of the knee; (8) hemophilic arthroplasty; (9) severe bone defects (Ahlback grade ≥3); (10) poor quality radiographs; (11) absence of long leg radiographs; (12) unilateral knee osteoarthritis.

Ultimately, a total of 479 patients totaling 944 knees were included in this study (see Figure 1 for details). The inclusion and exclusion processes were carried out by two orthopedic physicians who reviewed the medical records and imaging data.

Flowchart of the study.

Radiological Measurement

All patients underwent weight‐bearing, standing, and hip‐to‐ankle digital radiography. Hip‐to‐ankle digital radiography was formed by stitching three consecutive images using the Philips Medical Systems (Philips Healthcare, North Ryde, Australia). Lower limb alignment parameters were measured by an experienced orthopedic physician using the PACS system. Before the measurement, the physician checks whether the patella is facing forward, whether the proximal l fibular head is excessively covered or exposed, and whether the asymmetry of the intercondylar is used to rule out flexion deformities. The mechanical femoral axis was defined as the line connecting the center of the femoral head to the apex of the intercondylar notch at the distal femur. The mechanical tibial axis was defined as the line connecting the midpoint of the tibial spines to the midpoint between the medial and lateral malleolar surfaces. The femoral joint line was defined as the line between the lowest points on the medial and lateral condyles of the femur, while the tibial joint line was the line drawn across the most distal articular surface of the lateral and medial plateaus. The LDFA was defined as the mechanical femoral axis and femoral joint line, while the MPTA was defined as the medial angle formed between the mechanical tibial axis and the joint line of the proximal tibia. A schematic diagram of the measurements is shown in Figure 2. After the measurements were completed, 100 randomly selected radiography images were measured by another orthopedic physician to assess inter‐rater reliability.

CPAK Classification

The CPAK classification is determined by two independent parameters: aHKA and JLO. 6 The aHKA is defined as the MPTA minus the LDFA. The aHKA is not affected by joint space narrowing or tibiofemoral subluxation and can be used to assess constitutional alignment in the coronal plane. A negative aHKA indicates a varus constitutional alignment, whereas a positive aHKA indicates a valgus constitutional alignment. JLO is defined as the MPTA plus the LDFA and can be used to assess the obliquity of the knee joint line relative to the ground when standing with both feet. When the joint line is extended to the midline and intersects below the level of a horizontal joint line, it is referred to as the apex distal, where the JLO is less than 180°. If the joint line intersects the midline above the joint line plane, it is referred to as the apex proximal, and the JLO is greater than 180°. A JLO of 180° indicates that the joint line is approximately neutral. The three subgroups of aHKA combined with the three subgroups of JLO form a matrix, creating a nine‐square grid. This grid represents the nine phenotypes of CPAK.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). A scatterplot was used to describe the distribution of CPAK classifications in the population. Continuous data were described using mean and standard deviation (SD), while categorical data were described using percentages. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to compare the consistency of measurements between two observers, using a two‐way mixed‐effects model with absolute agreement. The continuous data conforming to the normal distribution were expressed as mean and SD, and the independent sample t‐test was used for comparison between groups. The chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare the differences in lower limb alignment parameters and CPAK classifications across different genders (male, female), age groups (middle‐aged/<65 years, elderly/≥65 years), and BMI categories (normal/<25 kg/m2, overweight and obese/≥25 kg/m2). A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 944 knees from 479 patients were included in the study, where all knees were diagnosed with osteoarthritis. The cohort consisted of 101 males and 378 females, with an average age of 67.64 ± 6.43 years and an average BMI of 26.77 ± 3.44 kg/m2. Demographic information is detailed in Table 1. The ICC of the LDFA (0.92; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.89–0.95) and the MPTA (0.83; 95% CI, 0.65–0.92) were in satisfactory agreement (ICC >0.75). The results measured by the first orthopedic physician were used for statistical analysis. In this osteoarthritis population, the average LDFA was 88.74 ± 3.62, the MPTA was 85.71 ± 3.50, the aHKA was −3.03° ± 5.69°, and the JLO was 174.45° ± 4.29°.

CPAK Classification in the Chinese Osteoarthritis Cohort

In this Chinese cohort study, 56.14% of knees had a varus constitutional alignment, 28.18% had a neutral alignment, and 15.68% had a valgus constitutional alignment. Additionally, 68.75% of knees had an apex distal joint line, 29.45% had a horizontal joint line, and 1.80% had an apex proximal joint line. Type I CPAK classification was the most common phenotype (38.03%), followed by type II (20.02%) and type IV (17.06%). Type IX had the lowest proportion, with only two knees classified under this type. A detailed CPAK classification for Chinese patients with osteoarthritis in this study is shown in Figure 3.

Distribution of CPAK Classification between Genders

Female patients had a lower LDFA than male patients (88.54 ± 3.76 vs. 89.52 ± 2.93, respectively, p < 0.05), with no significant difference in MPTA between the two groups (85.71 ± 3.61 vs. 85.71 ± 3.06, respectively, p = 0.988). Both groups predominantly exhibited varus alignment, with 55.01% of female patients and 60.51% of male patients having varus alignment. Only 7.18% of male patients exhibited valgus constitutional alignment, while the proportion of female patients with this alignment was 17.89% (p < 0.05). Regarding JLO, both groups had mostly apex distal joint lines and horizontal joint lines, but the proportion of apex distal joint lines was higher in females (71.16% vs. 59.49%, p < 0.05), while the proportion of horizontal joint lines was lower in females (26.84% vs. 39.49%, p < 0.05). The predominant CPAK types in females were I, II, and IV, while in males they were I, IV, and II. Notably, the proportion of CPAK IV was higher in males than in females (27.18% vs. 14.42%, p < 0.05). Conversely, CPAK III was more prevalent in females than in males (12.28% vs. 4.62%, p < 0.05). There were no type IX phenotype in the male group. The differences in lower limb alignment parameters and CPAK classification between males and females are shown in Table 2 and Figure 4.

Distribution of CPAK Classification between Different BMI Groups

The LDFA of individuals with normal BMI was lower than that of overweight or obese individuals (88.21V° ± 3.73° vs. 89.00° ± 3.55°, respectively, p < 0.05). The MPTA was higher in individuals with normal BMI than in those who were overweight or obese (86.40° ± 3.38° vs. 85.38° ± 3.51°, respectively, p < 0.05). Both groups had predominantly varus constitutional alignment. However, the proportion of varus constitutional alignment was lower in individuals with normal BMI than in those who were overweight or obese (47.56% vs. 60.28%, p < 0.05), while more patients with a normal BMI had valgus constitutional alignment than patients who were overweight or obese (21.50% vs. 12.87%, p < 0.05). Both groups had mostly apex distal joint lines and horizontal joint lines (p > 0.05). In terms of the CPAK classification, both groups were found to have mostly CPAK type I, followed by type II and type IV. However, the proportion of CPAK type I was lower in individuals with normal BMI than in those who were overweight or obese (31.27% vs. 41.29%, p < 0.05). Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that types I, II, and IV were the most common CPAK phenotypes, while type IX was the least common in Chinese osteoarthritis population. Furthermore, types I, II, and IV predominated across genders, BMI groups, and age groups, suggesting that this CPAK classification can more reliably reflect constitutional lower limb alignment. Additionally, based on previous findings, patients with CPAK types I, II, and IV are more likely to achieve knee balance with KA TKA than with MA TKA, 6 suggesting that KA TKA may be suitable for most of the Chinese osteoarthritis population.

Bellemans et al. proposed the concept of constitutional varus, suggesting that lower limb alignment exhibits varus constitutional alignment after skeletal maturity in most individuals. 5 The parameter of aHKA was proposed by MacDessi et al., which is considered predictive of lower limb alignment before the onset of arthritis.6, 17, 18 These parameters can be used to represent a simple and comprehensive measure of the CPAK. The boundaries of the CPAK classification are defined as one SD (rounded to the nearest integer) of aHKA and JLO. In this study, the SD of aHKA was 6°, and the SD of JLO was 4°, which were significantly greater than the SDs of 2° and 3° reported by MacDessi et al. 6 This discrepancy could be attributed to the inclusion of healthy individuals without OA in the population studied by MacDessi et al. On the other hand, this difference also indicates that there is greater variability in coronal plane lower limb alignment and JLO in the Chinese population.

Distribution of CPAK Classification between Genders

Huber et al. found that types III and VI, representing valgus constitutional alignment, were more common in females, while types I and IV, representing varus constitutional alignment, were more common in males. 19 Similarly, Steele et al. found that females were more likely to have valgus alignment than males (34.1% vs. 17.2%, p < 0.01) and less likely to have apex proximal joint line than males. 20 In this study, we also found a higher proportion of valgus alignment in female patients. Overall, both male and female patients most commonly exhibited types I and II followed by type IV. However, due to a higher proportion of horizontal joint lines in male patients, the proportion of type IV was higher in males. Conversely, the proportion of valgus alignment was higher in females, with more females exhibiting apex distal joint lines, resulting in a higher proportion of type III in females than in males. According to Hueter–Volkmann's law, increased stress inhibits bone growth, while reduced stress accelerates bone growth.21, 22, 23 Therefore, during walking and physical activities, increased load due to the varus torque at the knee joint may lead to the development of varus alignment. This results in inconsistent growth epiphysis development between the medial and lateral sides, with delayed growth on the medial side and accelerated growth on the lateral side. Bellemans et al. found that increased physical activity during adolescence can lead to more knee varus. 5 Since male individuals typically engage in more physical activity than female individuals, male lower limb alignment tends to be more varus and neutral, while valgus alignment is more common in females. Lu et al. demonstrated that more dynamic changes in the hip‐knee‐ankle angle, femoral bowing angle, distal femoral valgus resection angle, MPTA, and joint‐line convergence angle occur with age progression in female patients than in males. 24 This may also explain the wider range of CPAK classifications observed in female patients.

Distribution of CPAK Classification in Different BMI Groups

Obesity contributes to osteoarthritis and is considered a risk factor for poor outcomes after TKA.25, 26 Huber et al. found that in populations with a high BMI, females tend to have more varus alignment of the lower limbs. 19 Patients with a high BMI are at increased risk of medial cartilage damage, and the exacerbated wear of the medial cartilage and subchondral bone can lead to an increased degree of varus alignment in the femur and tibia (decreased MPTA, increased LDFA, and increased aHKA), resulting in a greater degree of varus constitutional alignment. Our study also found that the aHKA of overweight or obese patients is higher than that of normal BMI patients. The overweight or obese group also had a higher proportion of varus constitutional alignment than the normal BMI group (60.28% vs. 47.56%, p < 0.05). This may explain why the proportion of CPAK type I is higher in overweight or obese individuals than in normal BMI individuals. Our study may provide new avenues for further research on the impact of weight on bone morphology, and the prognosis of TKA.

Distribution of CPAK Classification in Different Age Groups

Hwang et al. found that, in healthy Asian populations, HKA and LDFA gradually increase with age, and the incidence of varus >3° also increases with age. 27 Asian lifestyles, including squatting, sitting on the floor, or sitting cross‐legged, may contribute to the development of varus across the lifetime. 28 This may also explain why the varus alignment in our study is greater than in other regions. 7 MacDessi et al. found no differences in the CPAK classification between a cohort of young healthy individuals and a cohort of older individuals undergoing knee arthroplasty. 6 An 8‐year follow‐up study of 48 patients (60 knees) found that, while there were significant changes in MPTA, aHKA, JLO, JLCA, and HKA with the progression of osteoarthritis, there were no differences in the CPAK classification, supporting the idea that the CPAK classification reflects the constitutional alignment since skeletal maturity and is not affected by aging. 29 Our study also found no significant differences in the CPAK classification between middle‐aged and elderly populations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study represents the largest sample size to date for the CPAK classification in a Chinese population. And we are unaware of any studies that have characterized the gender, BMI, and age‐related variations in CPAK phenotypes and related lower limb alignment parameters in Chinese patients with knee osteoarthritis. However, there are still some limitations of this study. Firstly, we only included individuals with knee osteoarthritis, so the results may not apply to populations of individuals with healthy knees. Future research should investigate alignment classification in healthy individuals and compare the differences between healthy individuals and individuals with osteoarthritis. Secondly, the study only compared alignment parameters and CPAK classifications between middle‐aged and elderly populations, as well as between individuals with normal BMI and those who are overweight or obese. We did not further subdivide age groups or BMI categories. Thirdly, the study population was predominantly female, which is consistent with the epidemiology of knee osteoarthritis in the Chinese population.

Conclusion

Despite differences in alignment parameters among genders, BMI, and age groups, CPAK types were most commonly types I, II, and IV, indicating that the CPAK classification can reliably reflect constitutional alignment. A better understanding of the CPAK classification and native alignment variability can provide a theoretical basis for the implementation of TKA with personalized alignment. In the future, this can serve as a foundation for further exploration of the most suitable alignment strategies in TKA for different classifications within the Chinese osteoarthritis population.
