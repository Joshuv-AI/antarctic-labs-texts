Comparing computable type 2 diabetes phenotype definitions in identifying populations of interest for clinical research
Authors: Priyanka D Sood, Star Liu, Rita R Kalyani, Hadi Kharrazi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSignificant variations exist in computable phenotype definitions to identify patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) using electronic health records (EHRs). These variations cause challenges in identifying T2D populations for clinical research. To address these challenges, this study compares the variations in common phenotypes in identifying patients with T2D using EHRs.MethodsA retrospective data analysis was performed using clinical data extracted from EHRs of 207 813 adult patients captured 2017–2019. Multiple T2D phenotypes were used: (1) Surveillance, Prevention and Management of Diabetes Mellitus, (2) Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW), (3) eMERGE Northwestern Group, (4) Durham Diabetes Coalition (DDC) and (5) a definition developed by a panel of experts at Johns Hopkins.ResultsEach phenotype definition identified a different T2D population with a unique composition of demographics and clinical features. Although the identified patients overlapped across phenotypes, only 22.7% (47 326) of the population was commonly identified across all definitions. Of the phenotypes, DDC identified the greatest number of patients with T2D (139 832, 67.3%), while CCW had the highest mean age (65.3 years), the highest percentage of black patients (35%) and the highest mean Charlson comorbidity score of 2.96. DDC identified patients with T2D with the lowest means of inpatient (0.64) and emergency room (1.06) visits.ConclusionOur study highlights the complexity of computable T2D phenotypes in translating commonly agreed T2D clinical definitions when applied against retrospective EHR data. Our findings provide an understanding of using appropriate phenotypes to identify, enrol and analyse T2D populations of interest using EHR data.

Introduction

The population of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) is growing rapidly in the USA.1 More than 25% of the US healthcare costs are associated with the chronic condition of T2D.2 Effective prevention and management of T2D are key to limiting the growing cost of diabetes, which reached US$237 billion in direct medical costs in 2017.2 Clinical guidelines have recommended aggressive standards for improving diabetes self-management and quality of care for patients with T2D.3 4 Nonetheless, to improve disease management and reduce the burden of healthcare utilisation for populations with T2D, robust mechanisms are increasingly needed to identify T2D populations using clinical data captured in electronic health records (EHRs).57

Clinical data captured in EHRs are routinely used to retrospectively identify patients with T2D on a population level. However, despite the existence of gold-standard clinical definitions of patients with T2D,8 variation in translating such definitions into common queries using EHR data has been a challenge.9 To address these variations, computable phenotype definitions have been proposed to harmonise and standardise the retrospective identification of patients with specific conditions, such as T2D, using clinical data extracted from insurance claims or EHRs.10 Commonly agreed computable phenotypes can improve the identification of populations with T2D, their comorbidities and their healthcare utilisation outcomes such as inpatient (IP) admission, emergency room (ER) visits and outpatient (OP) visits. Moreover, the variations in defining computable T2D phenotypes may lead to potential demographic considerations when identifying patients with T2D in different racial and ethnic groups.

Several publicly available computable T2D phenotypes include varying criteria to identify populations with T2D; however, a gold standard computable definition is still missing. These phenotypes may include inclusion/exclusion criteria on diagnosis (Dx), medication (Rx) or laboratory (Lx) codes, care pathways, time periods (eg, number of days) or sequence of events. Some examples of the validated and commonly used T2D phenotypes include Surveillance, Prevention and Management of Diabetes Mellitus (SUPREME-DM; SDM),11 Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Chronic Conditions Data Warehouse (CCW),12 eMERGE Northwestern Group (eMERGE)13 and Durham Diabetes Coalition (DDC).14 In addition to these well-established, consensus-driven phenotypes, individual academic medical centres have also developed custom phenotype definitions of populations with T2D for internal operational purposes.15

Recent literature has discussed the challenges in identifying populations with T2D using EHR data.5 6 To overcome these challenges, researchers have measured and reported the validity of pre-existing phenotypes on individual patient level (eg, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values)7; however, these approaches do not necessarily address the population level effect of using common phenotypes in identifying patients with T2D. For example, a patient with T2D can be identified using blood test.16 Yet, Lx data (eg, blood test results) may not be the only defining criteria to identify patients with T2D in data warehouses that lack Lx data. Hence, it is crucial to understand how various data types (ie, Dx, Rx vs Lx) and data sources (eg, EHR vs claims) can impact the identification of patients with T2D.

Clinical data extracted from EHRs is being increasingly used for T2D clinical research such as retrospective data analysis and determining population eligibility for clinical trials. However, research that assesses the impact of different T2D phenotype definitions on identifying populations with T2D is currently lacking. Using appropriate T2D phenotypes can enhance the identification of patients with T2D for clinical trials, population health management efforts and healthcare outcomes research for populations that may or may not have specific data types. For example, the T2D population identified using a data source that only contains Dx codes may be different from another data source that includes Dx, Rx and Lx codes. Thus, it is pertinent that healthcare researchers understand the effect of T2D phenotype variation and its potential impact on clinical research.

Currently, studies that assess the challenges of computable phenotypes in identifying patients with T2D using EHR data are lacking. To address some of these challenges, this study compared and analysed how each T2D phenotype definition may result in identifying different sets of populations with T2D using retrospective EHR data.

Methods

Data source and study population

A retrospective secondary data analysis was performed using data extracted from Johns Hopkins Medical Institute’s (JHMI) EHR data warehouse. The query used to extract this initial dataset (ie, ‘raw data’) incorporated all inclusion criteria used by the T2D phenotypes; however, it excluded all the exclusion criteria used by these phenotypes (online supplemental Appendix A). Approximately 208k adult patients were identified by the initial data cut who visited JHMI’s OP settings at least once over 3 years (2017–2019). The raw EHR data included demographic and clinical data. Demographic data included age, sex (biological), race (white, black, Asian) and ethnicity (Hispanic/Latino, non-Hispanic/Latino). Clinical data included the International Classification of Disease, 10th Revision (ICD-10) Dx codes (Dx),17 RxNorm Rx codes18 and LOINC Lx results codes.19 Since JHMI hospitals and clinics are primarily located in Maryland, most of the study population resided in Maryland (81.4%).

T2D phenotype definitions

Four commonly used computable T2D phenotype definitions (ie, CCW, DDC, SDM, eMERGE) and an expert-driven definition from Johns Hopkins (ie, Hopkins) were assessed. Patients with type 1 diabetes, if any, were excluded from all phenotypes used. SDM identifies patients with diabetes for public health interventions and epidemiological studies. eMERGE identifies patients with T2D for genotype–phenotype correlation studies. DDC identifies patients with T2D to reduce morbidity and mortality rates on a community level. CCW identifies patients with diabetes using Medicare and Medicaid claims data. And, lastly, the Hopkins T2D phenotype was specifically designed to discern patients with T2D for healthcare disparity research. Although these phenotype definitions have originally been developed for different objectives, all phenotypes attempt to identify patients with diabetes in large clinical data warehouses.

Since not all T2D phenotype definitions were defined with the most current ICD codes, DDC, SDM and eMERGE definitions were converted from ICD-9 to ICD-10.20 Additionally, not all phenotype definitions with Rx inclusions had the list of specific RxNorm codes that should be used for the Rx (eg, DDC and SDM do not list the specific RxNorm codes of the T2D Rx). RxNorm codes were identified using the National Library of Medicine’s RxNav tool.21 For this study, only those RxNorm codes were selected that are associated with the primary ingredient(s) of the Rx names in the T2D phenotype definition.

Of the four common T2D phenotype definitions, CCW only included Dx codes with a reference period of 2 years. Definitions of DDC, SDM and eMERGE included a series of detailed care pathways with Dx and Rx codes, as well as Lx results during specified periods. The Hopkins definition did not have pathways and included all three data types of Dx, Rx and Lx codes with wide eligibility criteria to identify patients with T2D. See online supplemental Appendix A for additional details for each phenotype.

Variables

The dependent variable included the degree of overlap in count and identification of patients with T2D across phenotype definitions. Independent variables included demographics (eg, age, sex, race, ethnicity, location), data types of T2D Dx, Rx and Lx, and the Charlson comorbidity index.22

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data analysis was conducted on the T2D population identified using different phenotypes. The χ² test was used for categorical variables, and an analysis of variance test was used for continuous variables to assess statistically significant differences in patient demographics. The distribution and overlap of the study population by each data type (ie, Dx, Rx and Lx) were assessed across each of the T2D phenotype definitions. The distribution of health utilisation events (ie, IP, ER and OP visits) was also assessed for each phenotype definition. The overall findings were showcased as descriptive data tables and Venn diagrams. Structured query language queries were written for data extraction. All visualisations and statistical analyses were conducted using R.23

Results

Our initial study population included 207 813 patients identified using all inclusion criteria across the T2D phenotypes (while excluding the exclusion criteria) from 2017 to 2019. Table 1 shows the overall demographics of our study population (ie, raw data cut from the database). Of the study population, the mean age was 62.4 years, with 51.3% (106 704) females, 31.8% (66 073) blacks and 89.9% (186 785) Non-Hispanic/Latinos. Most of the study population included residents from Maryland (81.4%, 169 215). DDC phenotype identified the most patients (67.3%; 139 832) with T2D, with a median age of 65 years. The distribution of sex was similar across all phenotypes. The percentages of the black population (35%, 27 952) and Non-Hispanic/Latino (91.1%, 72 817) identified by CCW were comparatively higher than other phenotype definitions. Across all phenotypes, the majority of the identified T2D populations resided in Maryland (more than 80%). All demographic differences detected between T2D phenotypes were statistically significant except for the category of ‘other’ sex (p values not shown in table 1).

Discussion

Despite the growing prevalence of T2D in the USA, a harmonised and widely adopted phenotype to identify patients with T2D in clinical data sources is lacking. Indeed, significant variations exist in common phenotype definitions to identify patients with T2D using EHR data. These variations cause challenges in identifying T2D populations for clinical research, epidemiological studies or population health efforts. Our study provides an in-depth understanding of the effects of variations in T2D phenotype definitions used to identify and compare populations of interest for T2D studies. Our analysis revealed novel results that may be beneficial to the underlying data used for T2D clinical research, particularly to determine patient eligibility, recruitment strategies and enrolment in clinical studies. Each phenotype definition may only apply to specific interventions in particular healthcare settings. The findings from our study can also support policy changes for efforts related to T2D population health management, data management strategies and disease reporting and analysis.

The variations in each phenotype definition are evident from our findings (table 1). Although the ratio of black and Hispanic/Latino populations is similar across each of the common T2D phenotype definitions, each phenotype identified a slightly different set of populations within these racial and ethnic groups. The majority of the T2D population identified by each phenotype were older adults (ie, mean age ranging between 63.3 and 65.5 years). This may be indicative of higher utilisation of healthcare services by older populations, hence higher representation in the EHR data. CCW had the highest Charlson comorbidity score across all phenotypes. This is likely due to CCW capturing generally older populations who often have more comorbidities, including Dx codes used to calculate the comorbidity score. This may be correlated with the highest utilisation of IP, OP and ER visits (table 2), particularly in the older population as CCW has the highest mean age of 65.3 years.

Our results show similarities between DDC and Hopkins phenotypes, and between SDM and eMERGE phenotype definitions. DDC and Hopkins phenotype definitions identified the most patients with T2D in our study population and had the lowest Charlson comorbidity scores across all phenotypes, although attributable to slightly younger age (table 2). Similar phenotypic patterns were also apparent for demographics and healthcare utilisation rates of IP, OP and ER visits. This may be due to similarities and differences in the underlying definition criteria used by these phenotypes to identify patients with T2D. Notably, both DDC and Hopkins definitions were created for regional populations. DDC was created for a community-based project in North Carolina to identify and reduce T2D morbidity and mortality rates; similarly, Hopkins’ definition was created for community-level healthcare disparities research purposes in Maryland. Phenotype definitions of SDM and eMERGE also showed similar results, likely due to similarity in their underlying definitions and inclusion/exclusion criteria.11

Although SDM and eMERGE had similarities in their definitions and measures of descriptive statistics, the populations identified across both definitions were different (figure 1). This reflects how each phenotype definition is clinically different and may identify a unique population; hence resulting in significantly different outcomes for a given clinical study. Furthermore, the identification of the T2D study population may be dependent on the types of data available. Each phenotype definition shows a significantly different cluster of the population given the presence or absence of Dx, Rx and Lx data types (figure 2). These findings could suggest using certain phenotypes over others given the availability of the underlying data types. For example, clinical research studies that rely heavily on Dx codes may consider CCW as it only relies on Dx for identifying patients with T2D. Alternatively, such studies may implement DDC if the availability of Dx data exceeds that of Rx and Lx, as many patients were identified using Dx codes. Moreover, studies may also consider internal definitions (eg, Hopkins) that were created for operational purposes which may or may not prove to be substantial to define the denominator population.

This study signifies the role of phenotype definitions in selecting different T2D populations; however, our results have some limitations. First, the generalisability of the results is limited to healthcare settings with similar underlying population compositions as that of the population represented in JHMI’s EHR data. The methodology offered in this study, however, can be replicated in other health settings and for other chronic conditions with multiple phenotype definitions. Second, results presented in this study are limited to 3 years of data availability using EHR data and do not translate to other periods using other data sources such as claims data. Third, we combined all inclusion criteria used across all phenotypes to generate the initial raw data set and did not use a gold-standard T2D population (eg, a data set reviewed manually by clinicians) due to the large population size. Similarly, we did not compare the effect of other existing definitions internal to JHMI (eg, patients identified by clinicians for clinical trials) to identify the underlying T2D population. Future studies may evaluate and compare the accuracy of the studied phenotypes with a manually identified gold standard T2D population. Fourth, terminology mapping from ICD-9 to ICD-10 and compiling RxNorm codes may have introduced inconsistencies in the inclusion and exclusion criteria used in some of the phenotypes. For example, some Dx codes may have been left out when converting the phenotype definitions from ICD-9 to ICD-10 Dx codes. For medications, our data sources included National Drug Code (NDC) and RxNorm codes, but given the one-to-many relationship between NDC and RxNorm codes, we only selected the first match to be included in the study. Fifth, we did not seek to construct additional phenotype definitions of T2D, such as to include clinical standards around insulin deficiency, insulin resistance or T2D definitions with obesity. Finally, this study does not infer any minimum demographic or hospital utilisation threshold needed to make a meaningful epidemiological assessment of patients with T2D using EHRs.

Future work to enhance the study results should include assessing the impact of phenotype definitions in identifying populations with T2D using claims data and comparing it with EHR data. Online supplemental Appendix C and D show preliminary data comparing T2D phenotype definitions using claims data versus using both claims and EHR data. Future research should also assess the effect of phenotype definitions in predicting healthcare utilisation (eg, IP, OP and ER visits) using both EHR and claims data.2426 As evidenced by demographic variations, future work should examine the interactions of demographics and diagnostic variables across phenotypes. Additional efforts are needed to evaluate and compare the effect of induced data quality issues (ie, completeness, accuracy and timeliness) on EHR data, and subsequently the identification of patients with T2D using different phenotype definitions.27 28 Moreover, future work should investigate the potential effect of variations in computable phenotypes to identify various subtypes of T2D (ie, clinical variations such as insulin deficiency, insulin resistance, mixed clinical phenotypes with or without obesity).8 Additional research is also needed to analyse potential bias in healthcare utilisation prediction models that may result from using different T2D phenotype definitions.29

Conclusion

Given that researchers often use EHR data to identify eligible patients with T2D for enrolment in clinical trials or population health studies, it is crucial to understand how the selection of T2D phenotypes can affect the study population of interest, and therefore, study outcomes. Our study shows that the performance and robustness of T2D phenotypes are significant considerations; therefore, using common computable T2D phenotype definitions against EHR data and a comprehensive set of data types (ie, Dx, Rx and Lx) may result in the selection of different T2D populations. Clinical researchers, especially diabetes researchers, should be aware that each computable phenotype definition was established with specific goals and for different populations. Although patients with T2D overlap across multiple computable T2D phenotypes, researchers need to note the phenotyping nuances and their potential effect on the selected T2D populations and subsequently research outcomes using EHR data.
