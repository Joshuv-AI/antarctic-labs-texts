Effect of zinc oxide-doped hydroxyapatite nanoparticles on the friction reduction and antibacterial properties of orthodontic ceramic brackets and stainless-steel wires
Authors: Saira Ikram, Saad Liaqat, Sandleen Feroz, Faiza Sharif, Khaled Fahmi Fawy, Naveed Ahmad, Umar Nishan, Fahad Alkhtani, Nawshad Muhammad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to assess the friction reduction and antibacterial properties of orthodontic brackets and wires coated with ZnO-doped HAP nanoparticles. ZnO-doped HAP nanoparticles were characterized with SEM, FTIR, and XRD analysis. After characterization, ZnO-doped HAP nanoparticles were coated onto orthodontic brackets and wires employing the dip coating method. The samples were then divided into four groups, control group Z0 (uncoated wires and brackets, and HAP only) and experimental group Z5(5%ZnO+HAP), Z10 (10% ZnO+HAP), Z15 (15% ZnO+HAP). The prepared samples were then subjected to mechanical and antibacterial testing. Mechanical properties such as friction resistance and microhardness improved with the coating of ZnO-HAP nanoparticles. The lowest friction was observed for the Z15 group (7.81 ± 1.10 N) while the highest was observed for the control group Z0 (21.25 ± 0.92 N). Friction force decreased with coating and with increasing concentration of ZnO nanoparticles in the composites in the order of Z0 > Z5 > Z10 > Z15. Microhardness of the brackets and wires improved with the coating, with the highest microhardness values observed for groups Z10 and Z15 of 2253 ± 93.7 and 2239 ± 123.1, respectively. The hardness of the wires also improved with the coating with the lowest value observed for the uncoated Z0 (351 ± 45.17). Agar well diffusion test showed an inhibition zone of 11.3 ± 0.57 mm, 15.3 ± 0.57 mm, 14.6 ± 1.15 mm, and 15.1 ± 1.14 mm for Z0, Z5, Z10 and Z15, respectively. The result of this study showed that zinc oxide-doped hydroxyapatite nanoparticle coating improved the mechanical and antibacterial properties of orthodontic brackets and wires.

Introduction

The recent advancements in the field of biomaterials have resulted in achieving the predictable clinical outcomes for orthodontic intervention to successfully address the problems related to malalignment, malocclusion, crowding, and spacing. The tooth movements involved in orthodontic treatments use sliding mechanics. It involves the movement of the tooth by slipping the orthodontic wire made of different alloys over the orthodontic brackets and molar tubes [1]. The advantage of this approach includes the reduced chair time and three-dimensional control of movements [2, 3]. However, the generation of friction between the wires and brackets is commonly experienced by orthodontists. To overcome this resistance, a large amount of force is required, which can result in loss of anchorage and tipping of teeth [4, 5]. The primary factor that contributes to the sliding mechanics of tooth movement is friction resistance. Currently, this friction reduction is the major challenge for materials scientists, as approximately 60% of the applied force to the teeth during the orthodontic treatment is dissipated due to friction [6].

Surface properties of a material play a pivotal role as the primary interface between the material and its surrounding environment, significantly influencing its overall performance and functionality [7–9]. These key surface characteristics, including roughness, chemical composition, energy, and topographic features, dictate crucial interactions with biological tissues, fluids, and mechanical forces [10, 11]. These surface properties govern fundamental biological responses such as cell adhesion, protein adsorption, and bacterial colonization, ultimately impacting biocompatibility, antibacterial efficacy, and long-term clinical success of biomaterials. Surface characteristics also affect corrosion resistance, wear behavior, and frictional performance. Thus, tailoring surface attributes provides a strategic avenue for enhancing material functionality without altering the bulk properties, making surface engineering an essential focus in the development of advanced materials for diverse applications [7, 12].

Previously, in orthodontics, the area of focus was on the bulk characteristics of brackets and archwires. However, there has been growing interest among clinicians to work collaboratively with materials scientists to develop some novel surface modifications aimed at improving clinical efficiency and patient outcomes. Despite ongoing debate regarding which bracket type generates the least friction, there is general agreement that stainless steel (SS) arch wires, due to their smooth surface, exhibit lower frictional resistance during sliding mechanics [13]. Resistance to sliding depends on several factors, such as wire and bracket material, relative clearance between bracket and wire, their surface characteristics, using various ligating methods, and biological factors like the presence or absence of saliva, etc. Methods for reducing friction resistance include using self-ligating brackets and archwires, using archwires of different alloys, and employing ligatures of different materials, such as polymeric ligature, Teflon-coated ligature, and SS sintered ligature to reduce the friction produced in sliding mechanics [14]. Recently, several novel methods for friction reduction have been employed. This involves the application of nanoparticle coating on the surface of archwires and brackets to significantly improve their properties, such as hardness and friction reduction. As advent of nanotechnology improved the quality of orthodontic treatment, it is crucial to investigate novel means to reduce friction resistance [15].

Nanotechnology has garnered significant advancements in the field of biomedicine to address key limitations associated with routine clinical procedures. Nanoparticles are ultrafine units with sizes measured in nanometers (nm; 1 nm = 10−9 m) [16]. Since nanoparticles have small dimensions and increased overall surface area, they can be used as a dry lubricant to diminish the resistance generated due to friction amongst the orthodontic brackets and wires. Nanoparticles released from the coating act as a solid dry lubricant within the orthodontic brackets and wires, thus decreasing the friction coefficient [17, 18].

The application of ZnO has been extensively explored in many areas, such as catalysis, gas sensors, and lubricants [19]. In the field of biomedicine, ZnO nanoparticles have been incorporated in various formulations due to their broad-spectrum antimicrobial and anti-fungal properties, low cost, chemical stability, and biocompatibility. Furthermore, they have shown promising results in promoting wound healing, bone regeneration, and as a smart carrier in drug and gene delivery systems [20, 21]. However, for successful clinical application, the key limitations associated with their applications are dose-dependent cytotoxicity and aggregation tendencies, which can affect bioavailability. Moreover, ensuring long-term biosafety and achieving precise control over particle size, dispersion stability, and surface functionalization remain critical challenges in translating ZnO-based systems into routine biomedical use [21, 22].

ZnO nanoparticles act as a spacer, thus eliminating direct contact between the surface irregularities of orthodontic wires and brackets [23]. Beroozian et al. conducted a study to estimate the outcome of friction reduction generated within the orthodontic brackets and wires by coating them with spherical ZnO nanoparticles. The author found that there was a considerable reduction in friction generated among SS wires and porcelain brackets when the porcelain brackets were coated using zinc oxide nanoparticles [8]. Similarly, Kachoei et al. calculated the amount of friction reduction after coating nickel-titanium (Ni-Ti) wires with ZnO nanoparticles. The author concluded that there was a 21% reduction in friction with ZnO nanoparticle coating. Furthermore, the coated wires showed improved antibacterial activity against Streptococcus mutants [24].

Similarly, the development of early caries lesions, often known as white spot lesions, is another limitation associated orthodontic treatments in addition to friction generation. It has been reported by several studies that orthodontic treatments employing wires and brackets frequently resulted in plaque deposition. This plaque accumulation then leads to an increase in the concentration of acidogenic bacteria such as Streptococcus mutants and Lactobacillus, which in turn lowers the pH and demineralizes the enamel surfaces to initiate early caries lesions [25]. These early carious lesions form in the area close to the vicinity of orthodontic wires and brackets [26]. The strategies to prevent the development of early caries lesions include the application of high-fluoride-containing dentifrices, fluoride varnishes, fluoride gels, and fluoride mouth rinses [27, 28]. Similarly, the Chlorhexidine-containing mouthwashes have been used as an effective means to inhibit early caries during fixed orthodontic treatments [14]. Moreover, the dental sealants and filling materials such as composite fillings and glass-ionomer cement have been employed for treating white spot lesions [29]. Several studies have supported the use of xylitol gums on a daily basis to effectively inhibit the early caries lesion formation [30, 31].

Hydroxyapatite is a potent remineralizing agent with excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, and osteoconductivity, making it an ideal material for diverse biomedical applications, such as bone grafts, dental implants, and tissue engineering scaffolds [32, 33]. It is a biologically active compound with a composition comparable to enamel and dentine [33, 34]. Several studies have reported the remineralizing potential of HAP to treat carious lesions [26, 33]. Huang et al. showed that deposition of nano-hydroxyapatite on the tooth surface resulted in preventing the early carious lesions [26]. Thus, adding ZnO nanoparticles to the coating of hydroxyapatite on brackets and wires could prevent the early carious lesion/white lesions that are formed around the orthodontic brackets and wires during treatment. Ramazanzadeh et al. investigated the bactericidal effects of ZnO and copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticle coatings on orthodontic brackets against Streptococcus mutans, aiming to reduce the risk of early carious lesion development during orthodontic treatment. Their findings demonstrated a significant reduction in bacterial counts within two hours in the experimental groups coated with CuO-ZnO and ZnO-CuO, compared to the uncoated control group. However, this study did not discuss the effect of coating on the frictional properties of the material [35].

This in vitro study is motivated by previous research demonstrating the potential of ZnO nanoparticles to reduce friction between orthodontic brackets and arch wires, as well as their well-documented antibacterial properties [24]. Despite these promising findings, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding coatings that simultaneously address both frictional resistance and antimicrobial efficacy in fixed orthodontic appliances. To address this, our project explores a novel surface modification approach by applying a smart coating of ZnO-doped hydroxyapatite (HAP) nanoparticles onto ceramic brackets and stainless-steel (SS) wires. This dual-function coating is designed not only to minimize frictional forces during sliding mechanics but also to inhibit bacterial colonization and reduce the risk of early caries development during orthodontic treatment. By targeting both mechanical and biological challenges, this study aims to advance the development of multifunctional orthodontic materials with improved clinical performance.

Methodology

ZnO nanoparticle synthesis

ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized by a reflux precipitation method under controlled conditions (temperature, pH, time). Analytical grade reagents such as zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Sigma Aldrich), ethylene glycol (Sigma Aldrich), and hexamine were used as a precursor. The first 0.5 Molar stock solution of zinc nitrate was prepared in distilled water. The working solution was prepared by taking 20 ml of stock solution and 680 ml of distilled water in a round-bottom flask, positioned in a heating mantle for heating. To this, hexamine was added to the working solution and heated for 1 h. The solution turned milky, and after boiling, showed precipitation of nanoparticles. The solution was quenched in ice-chilled water and filtered with vacuum filtration using micropore filter paper. The precipitant obtained on the filter paper was dried in an oven for 1 h and then calcined in the furnace at 600 °C to remove any water molecules.

Preparation of ZnO doped HAP nanoparticles using ball milling process

Commercially sourced HAP nanoparticles (Sigma-Aldrich) were combined with laboratory-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles to prepare HAP/ZnO nanocomposite mixtures with varying ZnO content. The composites were formulated at three ZnO weight percentages: 5%, 10%, and 15%, relative to the mass of HAP.

For each formulation, 5 g of HAP nanoparticles were weighed and mixed with ZnO nanoparticles as follows:5% ZnO: 0.25 g of ZnO nanoparticles were mixed with 5 g of HAP.10% ZnO: 0.5 g of ZnO nanoparticles were mixed with 5 g of HAP.15% ZnO: 0.75 g of ZnO nanoparticles were mixed with 5 g of HAP.

Nanoparticles were doped with hydroxyapatite in a ball milling machine (RETSCH, Germany). After mixing in desired proportions, ZnO and HAP nanoparticles powder mixture was added to a vessel containing zirconia balls. Distilled water was used as a mixing medium. The vessel was rotated at a constant milling speed of 300 rpm for 3 h [36]. After milling, the vessels were emptied, and the prepared nanocomposite powder was placed in a beaker for drying in an oven. The dried doped nanoparticles were stored in an airtight container for subsequent application and characterization.

Coating of wires and brackets

Stainless steel wires (SS orthodontic wires, Orthoform Ovoid), and ceramic brackets (Acclaim Roth) were used for this study. Before coating, SS wires, and ceramic brackets, they were treated with acid to make the surface rough. For ceramic brackets, hydrofluoric acid was used. Brackets and wires were immersed in 20% HF acid for 60 s, then rinsed with distilled water and then desiccated in air. Once the wires and brackets were treated with acid, they were coated with ZnO-doped HAP nanoparticles. For the coating purpose, nanoparticles were first dispersed in an ethanol solution. The brackets were then dipped in the ethanol solution containing nanoparticles. For efficient coating of nanoparticles, the brackets were dipped twice and then dried with hot air using a blow dryer.

Before coating, the wires were cleansed with running water to wash away any dust particles. The wires were then dipped in the ethanol solution containing nanoparticles and left immersed in the NPS solution for 30 min. After this, the wires were carefully removed from the solution and hung in the air with the help of paper clips. They were again over-layered with the solution of NPs using a dropper and further desiccated with hot air using a blow dryer.

Characterization

In this in vitro study, the prepared specimens were subjected to characterization using the following analytical techniques.

Scanning electron micrography (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX)

The surface morphology of ZnO, HAP, and doped ZnO+HAP was analyzed using a Scanning Electron Microscope (JEOL-5910, Japan). The images were taken at 1000X, 2500X, 10,000X, and 25000X. Elemental composition of the samples and the pattern of distribution of different elements in the samples EDX mapping was performed. It was performed with a Scanning Electron Microscope with EDX Analyzer (Jeol, Japan, JSM IT 100).

Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometry (FTIR)

Infrared spectroscopy (Shimadzu, FTIR-8400S, IR prestige 21) of the samples was performed in a range of 400–4000 cm−1. A Background scan was obtained first and then scanned with the rate of 10 in the region of 400– 4000 cm−1. The obtained data were evaluated using Origin software and the spectra peaks were matched with the literature.

X-Ray diffraction

Sample crystallinity of ZnO, HAP, and doped ZnO-HAP was determined with an X-ray diffractometer (JEOL XRD, JDX-3532) in a range of 2θ = 10°–70°. The step size was 0.02°and the time taken per step was 2 s. Differac EVA software was used for analyzing the XRD spectra of the samples.

Assembly of brackets and wires

Brackets were attached to aluminum plates using a light-cure bonding agent (Heliostit Orthodontic) purchased from the local market (Fig. 1). The surface of the aluminum plate was first made rough with sandpaper. Then bonding agent was applied, and the bracket was carefully placed at zero angles. It was then cured for 20 s with Curing light (woodpecker DTE light) until the complete set of bonding agents was. The coated wires were passed within the brackets and held with orthodontic ligatures. Friction test

A friction test was conducted with the Universal Testing Machine (Shimadzu AG-IS) (Fig. 1) following the experimental setup described by Tidy for frictional force measurement between orthodontic brackets and wire [37]. A single orthodontic bracket was fixed rigidly to an aluminum plate, which was mounted on the lower grip of the testing machine. A straight segment of orthodontic wire was passed through the bracket slot and clamped firmly at both ends in the upper grip of the machine, ensuring the wire was held taut and immobile during testing. The sliding motion was generated by moving the aluminum plate and bracket at a constant rate of 0.5 mm/sec (30 mm/min) relative to the stationary wire, thereby isolating frictional forces at the single bracket-wire interface. Artificial saliva was applied dropwise to the bracket-wire contact area throughout each test to simulate oral conditions.

Microhardness test

The hardness of the specimens was assessed with a Vickers microhardness tester (Shimadzu-M microhardness tester), following the procedures outlined in ASTM E384-22. The surface of both coated and uncoated SS wires was indented with a force of 500 g for 50 s, while the force used for ceramic brackets was 100 g for 50 s. Vickers hardness value was measured by changing the unit from kgf /mm2 to GPa by using the formula HV = 1857.4[P]/[d2][kgf/mm] = 18.2 [P] / [d] [GPa] [38].

P in the formula represents the load in grams, while d represents the diagonals of indentations of a diamond pyramid. Five measurements were taken on each sample, and then the average of the samples was calculated.

Antibacterial test

Antibacterial activity was assessed with a well diffusion test. The experimental groups comprise prepared samples containing different concentrations of ZnO nanoparticles, i.e., 5%, 10%, and 15%, whereas the samples in the control group only contain HAP nanoparticles. First 0.5 McFarland1–2×108 CFU/mL suspension of Streptococcus mutans (S.mutans) bacteria was taken and spread over the entire surface of the agar plates. S. mutans are recognized as the principal pathogenic bacteria due to their ability to metabolize a wide variety of carbohydrates to rapidly produce acids as by products and robust biofilm formation. This resulted in the demineralization of tooth surfaces, which leads to the formation of cavitated lesions [39]. Following inoculation, the agar plates were incubated at 37 C° for 24 h. Wells measuring 6–8 mm in diameter were then created using a sterile metal borer. A volume of 100 μL of each test material was introduced into the wells, and the plates were further incubated for another 24 h under the same conditions. Antimicrobial activity was assessed by calculating the zone of inhibition around the test materials [40].

In the agar well diffusion method, three experimental groups of ZnO-incorporated HAP (5%, 10%, and 15%) were tested against the control group (HAP only). Discussion

Orthodontic brackets and wires have been extensively used for the treatment of malocclusion. Despite their long-standing use, prominent issues associated with their application are excessive friction between brackets and wires and plaque-induced gingival inflammation. In this study, we aimed to mitigate these challenges by coating ceramic brackets and SS arch-wires with ZnO-doped HAP nanoparticles. The outcomes were evaluated through a combination of morphological, structural, mechanical, and antibacterial analyses.

Ceramic brackets are widely used in esthetic orthodontic treatments but are known to generate higher frictional forces compared to metal brackets, which can impede tooth movement efficiency and cause treatment delays. SS archwires are standard in clinical practice due to their mechanical strength, predictability, and common pairing with ceramic brackets in everyday orthodontics, making them an appropriate choice for evaluating modifications intended to address practical clinical challenges. Thus, the ceramic brackets and (SS) arch wires were used in our study as they represent clinically relevant orthodontic materials prone to frictional challenges. Furthermore, they provide an ideal substrate for evaluating the effect of ZnO-doped hydroxyapatite nanoparticles on both friction reduction and antibacterial properties.

SEM analysis revealed that the synthesized ZnO nanoparticles had a rod-like morphology with diameters ranging from 0.5 to 2 μm and lengths below 10 μm. These findings are consistent with the previous reported studies [44, 45]. HAP particles exhibited a planar pentagonal or flake-like structure, consistent with findings by Sedihghi et al. [46]. After ball milling, a significant size reduction was observed for ZnO rods (50–90 nm), and HAP particles (<50 nm in diameter). This reduction in the particle size has been mainly caused by the high-energy ball milling, resulted in a repetitive collison between the ball and the particles leading to mechanical fragmentation [47].

SEM images of the coated brackets and wires confirmed uniform deposition of ZnO-doped HAP, with the flake-like structures conformally covering the bracket and wire surfaces. Elemental mapping using EDX further validated the homogeneous distribution of Zn, Ca, P, O, and C within the coatings, with increased Zn levels correlating with an increasing ZnO content in the composites.

The results of FTIR analysis supported the successful incorporation of ZnO into the HAP matrix. The characteristic peaks corresponding to the stretching vibration of P-O were observed at ~1058 cm−¹ [41]. The peaks recorded at 1382 and 790 relate to the stretching vibration of CO32−, which is indicative of carbonated HAP [48].

Similarly, in the ZnO spectra, the broad peak observed at 3367 can be attributed to the presence of hydroxyl group (OH) [42], and the peak at 1425 is indicative of the angular deformation of ammonium ion. The region at 1508 is due to the stretching vibration of water molecules. However, the peak at 629 is due to the presence of the Zn-O bond [43]. The region at 920 corresponds to the stretching of the O-H bond of carboxylic acid (OH) [49]. In the 5% ZnO spectra, the small peak at 3464 is indicative of the OH of water molecules, indicating the presence of water. However, the strong peaks at 1061 and 958 are ascribed to the vibration of the P-O bond, indicative of the PO43– ion hydroxyapatite [50]. In the 10% and 15% ZnO spectra, the small peaks observed at 3464 and 3460 correspond to the presence of water molecules. Also, the strong peaks at 1060 and 957, 1074 and 956 are all the major peaks indicative of the presence of PO43− [41]. The regions at 672 and 667 are all indicative of Zn-O bonds [43].

XRD analysis confirmed the crystallinity and phase purity of the synthesized materials. The HAP spectrum showed diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 24° (102), 27° (210), 31° (211), 33° (112), 35° (300), 47° (222), and 49° (213). All these diffraction peaks observed in our current study has been mentioned by Chandrasekar et al. [51]. The peaks observed in the ZnO spectrum at 2 thetas are 32° (100), 34° (002), 36° (101), 47° (102), 56°(110), 63° (103), 67° (200), 69°(112), and 77° (200). All these are the major characteristic peaks of ZnO nanoparticles [52]. Similarly, all the tested composites showed a consistent HAP peak while ZnO-related peaks became more distinct with increasing dopant concentrations, indicating successful doping without phase separation.

Nanoparticles have been used in different studies to decrease the generation of friction within orthodontic brackets and wires [53]. ZnO nanoparticles were used to improve the friction reduction properties of orthodontic brackets and wires [24, 54]. In this study, we employed ZnO-doped HAP nanoparticles on orthodontic brackets and wires to improve mechanical properties such as friction reduction and microhardness properties.

In our study, we observed that mechanical properties such as friction reduction and hardness improved after coating and increasing the concentration of ZnO nanoparticles in the samples. The improvement in friction resistance properties could be attributed to its nanoparticle size and rod-shaped morphology of ZnO. A study conducted by Cinar et al. showed that the coefficient of friction depends on particle shape [55]. The sample that contained spherical and irregular particles had a higher coefficient of friction than the samples with rod-shaped and flake-like particles. In our study, both rod-shaped ZnO and planar pentagonal structure of HAP might have contributed towards a significant reduction in friction.

Similarly, ZnO nanoparticle filler has been used to increase the microhardness properties of amalgam filling [56]. A study reported by Birru et al. showed that ZnO nanoparticle fillers increase the hardness of epoxy composites [57]. Thus increase in the hardness of the coated brackets and wires could be attributed to improving the hardness properties of ZnO nanoparticles.

Interestingly, it has been observed that the antibacterial efficacy did not proportionally increase with higher ZnO concentrations beyond 5%. This may be attributed to the limited availability of ZnO at the surface due to encapsulation within the HAP matrix, thus reducing its direct interaction with bacterial cells. This deviation from the previously reported studies highlights the importance of particle exposure at the biointerface [66].

While this study successfully demonstrated that ZnO-doped hydroxyapatite coatings can enhance the mechanical and antibacterial properties of orthodontic brackets and wires, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the findings are based entirely on in vitro experiments, which do not fully replicate the complex oral environment. Variables such as salivary flow, enzymatic activity, dynamic mechanical forces, and biofilm formation over time may alter the coating’s performance. Second, although antibacterial activity was observed, the effectiveness plateaued at higher ZnO concentrations, possibly due to encapsulation of ZnO within the HAP matrix, limiting its surface availability for bacterial interaction. Third, cytotoxicity and long-term biocompatibility of the coatings were not assessed in this study. Finally, the durability and wear resistance of the coatings under simulated orthodontic stress conditions were not evaluated. Future in vivo studies and extended mechanical testing under simulated clinical conditions are needed to validate these promising results and optimize the coating composition for clinical application.

Conclusion

Zinc oxide-doped hydroxyapatite (ZnO-HAP) nanoparticles were successfully synthesized and employed as a coating material for orthodontic brackets and wires to enhance their mechanical and antibacterial properties. FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of characteristic functional groups associated with both ZnO and hydroxyapatite. XRD spectra revealed distinct peaks corresponding to ZnO and HAP, validating the crystalline structure and successful doping. SEM analysis demonstrated rod-shaped ZnO nanoparticles and planar, pentagonal HAP particles, with clear evidence of ZnO-HAP composite formation. Doping resulted in a reduction in particle size, indicating improved dispersion and surface interaction. The coated samples exhibited increased microhardness with rising ZnO concentrations, following the order: Z0 < Z5 < Z10 < Z15. Frictional force testing showed a significant decrease in resistance for coated brackets and wires compared to the control group, with values decreasing progressively as ZnO concentration increased (Z0 > Z5 > Z10 > Z15). These results collectively suggest that ZnO-HAP coatings effectively improve the antibacterial properties, mechanical strength and frictional performance of orthodontic appliances, offering promising implications for clinical use.

However, to ensure the successful translation of this smart technology for the well-being of mankind, it is crucial to conduct long-term in vivo studies in the future to further investigate the performance and durability of the coated orthodontic appliances under physiologically relevant oral conditions in animal models.
