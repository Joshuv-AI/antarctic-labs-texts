Non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in non-diabetic kidney disease: a narrative review of mechanisms and clinical advances
Authors: Fei Luo, Jingjia Hu, Hongliang Fang, Xianfa Li, Caifeng Zhu, Ziyang Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Significant advances have been made in elucidating the role of non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (ns-MRAs) in non-diabetic kidney disease (NDKD). As a key downstream effector of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS), pathological overactivation of the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) amplifies inflammatory and fibrotic signaling, thereby contributing to proteinuria, podocyte injury, and progressive damage to both the glomerular and tubulointerstitial compartments. Mechanistic studies suggest that ns-MRAs exert multi-level protective effects by targeting the inflammation–fibrosis axis, including preservation of podocyte structure and function, attenuation of acute kidney injury-to-chronic kidney disease transition, improvement of endothelial dysfunction, and suppression of cardiac and renal fibrosis. Clinically, the phase III FIND-CKD trial has provided direct randomized evidence that finerenone slows estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline in adults with proteinuric CKD without diabetes, while a prespecified exploratory analysis further supports its potential relevance in glomerular diseases. Real-world studies in IgA nephropathy and membranous nephropathy also suggest antiproteinuric effects when finerenone is added to renin–angiotensin system inhibition. Moreover, co-administration with sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors may confer complementary antiproteinuric effects, although hyperkalemia remains an important safety consideration requiring appropriate monitoring. Collectively, current randomized and observational evidence supports consideration of ns-MRAs in selected patients with proteinuric NDKD, while highlighting the need for subtype-specific interpretation, serum potassium monitoring, and precision-stratified treatment strategies.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing global public health concern and a major cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and cardiovascular events [1]. Among the causes requiring renal replacement therapy, non-diabetic kidney disease (NDKD) accounts for a significant proportion, including IgA nephropathy (IgAN), membranous nephropathy (MN), and hypertensive kidney disease [2, 3]. Despite the heterogeneity in etiology, persistent proteinuria and chronic renal inflammation and fibrosis are the common pathological foundations of these conditions.

Recent epidemiological data further emphasize the clinical importance of NDKD. Globally, CKD affected an estimated 788 million adults in 2023 and remains a major contributor to mortality and disability [1]. Within the NDKD spectrum, primary glomerular diseases are particularly important in East Asian populations, where IgAN represents the most common biopsy-proven primary glomerulonephritis, with reported rates ranging from 6.3% to 24.7% among renal biopsy cohorts [4]. These epidemiological data underscore the need for disease-modifying strategies that address residual renal risk beyond conventional supportive care.

Despite the proven benefits of renin–angiotensin system (RAS) inhibitors and sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors as guideline-recommended foundational therapies [5–7], a substantial proportion of patients with CKD remain at residual risk, characterized by persistent proteinuria and progressive renal function decline.

The mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), a key downstream effector of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS), is involved not only in the regulation of sodium and water balance but also, when excessively activated, can directly induce inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis, thereby exacerbating renal injury [8, 9]. Traditional steroidal MR antagonists, such as spironolactone, have been shown in early studies to reduce proteinuria [10]. However, long-term use of steroidal MRAs in CKD is constrained by hyperkalemia and, for spironolactone, off-target endocrine adverse effects.

In recent years, the development of non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (ns-MRAs) has provided new therapeutic strategies for targeting MR. Finerenone has demonstrated cardiovascular and kidney benefits in CKD associated with type 2 diabetes, although hyperkalemia remains an important safety consideration [11–13]. More recently, the phase III FIND-CKD trial extended randomized evidence to adults with proteinuric CKD without diabetes, showing a modest but statistically significant slowing of eGFR decline [14]. In parallel, disease-specific observational studies in IgAN and MN have reported antiproteinuric signals that require cautious interpretation because of their nonrandomized designs [15, 16]. Recent reviews have also highlighted the evolving mechanistic and clinical rationale for MR antagonism in NDKD [17].

This review provides an overview of MR activation–mediated renal injury, with a focus on underlying mechanisms, and highlights recent advances in ns-MRAs for NDKD, as well as future research directions and therapeutic potential.

Methods

This narrative review was conducted to synthesize mechanistic, preclinical, and clinical evidence regarding non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (ns-MRAs) in non-diabetic kidney disease (NDKD). A structured search of PubMed/MEDLINE was performed for studies published from January 2015 to June 2026; seminal earlier publications were also considered when they provided essential mechanistic or clinical context. Search terms included combinations of ‘chronic kidney disease’, ‘non-diabetic kidney disease’, ‘IgA nephropathy’, ‘membranous nephropathy’, ‘hypertensive nephropathy’, ‘mineralocorticoid receptor’, ‘aldosterone’, ‘inflammation’, ‘fibrosis’, ‘renal injury’, ‘mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists’, ‘non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists’, ‘finerenone’, ‘esaxerenone’, ‘spironolactone’, and ‘eplerenone’. Reference lists of relevant primary studies and recent reviews were also screened manually. Eligible evidence included randomized trials, prospective or retrospective clinical studies, observational cohorts, pharmacological studies, animal experiments, and mechanistic studies relevant to MR activation or ns-MRA therapy in CKD. Studies focused exclusively on diabetic kidney disease were included only when they informed pharmacology, safety, or combination-treatment concepts potentially applicable to NDKD. Case reports, non-renal outcome-only studies, duplicate or invalid reports, and articles without sufficient methodological detail were excluded. Because this is a narrative review rather than a systematic review, meta-analysis was not performed. Evidence was organized using a descriptive hierarchy based primarily on study design and endpoint robustness; this hierarchy was not intended to constitute a formal assessment using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Importantly, a prospective Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-level record-by-record screening log was not maintained during the original narrative review process. Therefore, exact screening-stage counts could not be reliably reconstructed retrospectively and were not imputed. Mineralocorticoid receptor and its antagonists

Pathological activation of MR and kidney injury

MR is a ligand-activated nuclear transcription factor with cell type–specific expression in the kidney. In addition to distal tubular epithelial cells, MR is expressed in multiple non-epithelial cell types, including podocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, supporting its roles in glomerular filtration barrier homeostasis and immune–inflammatory regulation [18, 19].

Under physiological conditions, MR maintains sodium and water homeostasis by regulating target genes including serum- and glucocorticoid-regulated kinase 1 (SGK1) and the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) [20, 21], while its relative specificity for aldosterone is conferred by 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2) [22]. However, in podocytes, fibroblasts, and inflammatory cells, low 11β-HSD2 expression renders MR susceptible to nonselective activation by circulating cortisol, thereby promoting inflammatory and fibrotic responses [18]. In addition, MR activation can occur independently of aldosterone: even when aldosterone levels are suppressed by renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, factors within the CKD microenvironment, such as oxidative stress, inflammatory mediators, and high salt load, can sustain MR activation in a ligand-independent manner via Rac1-dependent pathways [23].

MR overactivation contributes to disease progression through both hemodynamic and inflammatory mechanisms. Hemodynamically, it aggravates disturbances and promotes glomerular hypertension by enhancing sodium and water retention, inducing intrarenal vasoconstriction, and impairing endothelial function [20, 21, 24]. In parallel, MR overactivation drives inflammation by upregulating pro-inflammatory mediators such as monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), activating the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathways [25, 26], and promoting macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype [27–29], ultimately leading to a sustained inflammatory response. Against this backdrop, the pro-inflammatory milieu further drives fibrogenesis. MR activation enhances the expression of profibrotic mediators, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), thereby facilitating fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix accumulation, culminating in glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis [30–32]. Taken together, targeting MR represents a promising strategy to attenuate the inflammatory and fibrotic pathways that drive CKD progression, with therapeutic potential that may extend beyond conventional RAS blockade.

Pharmacological differences between steroidal and non-steroidal MRAs

Conventional steroidal MR antagonists, exemplified by spironolactone and eplerenone, can reduce proteinuria when added to background therapy in CKD [10]. Their use is nevertheless limited by safety and tolerability considerations. Spironolactone interacts with androgen and progesterone receptors and can cause gynecomastia, menstrual disturbances, and other endocrine adverse effects, whereas eplerenone has greater MR selectivity but lower receptor-binding potency [33]. Both agents can impair distal potassium excretion and increase hyperkalemia risk, particularly in patients with reduced eGFR or concomitant RAS inhibition [34, 35].

Non-steroidal MRAs have emerged as a structurally distinct approach to MR blockade. Finerenone and esaxerenone exhibit high MR selectivity with limited affinity for other steroid-hormone receptors and therefore have little propensity for the sex-hormone-related adverse effects associated with spironolactone [36, 37]. These pharmacological differences do not eliminate hyperkalemia risk, and direct clinical comparisons with steroidal MRAs in NDKD are lacking.

Finerenone has a relatively short plasma half-life and a distinct tissue-distribution and receptor-binding profile, whereas esaxerenone has a longer half-life and sustained antihypertensive activity [38]. Experimental studies indicate that different MR ligands can produce distinct receptor conformations and transcriptional co-regulator interactions, potentially influencing inflammatory and fibrotic gene programs [36, 39, 40]. However, these mechanistic differences should not be interpreted as proof of superior clinical efficacy or lower hyperkalemia risk in NDKD in the absence of direct comparative trials.

To date, no head-to-head randomized controlled trial has directly compared steroidal and non-steroidal MRAs specifically in NDKD populations. Consequently, apparent differences between these drug classes are inferred from pharmacology and separate clinical development programs rather than direct comparative evidence. Spironolactone may remain an effective and inexpensive option in selected patients but is limited by endocrine adverse effects and hyperkalemia; eplerenone reduces off-target endocrine effects but has less extensive NDKD outcome evidence. The principal practical strengths of ns-MRAs are high MR selectivity, minimal sex-hormone receptor activity, and, for finerenone, an expanding randomized evidence base in proteinuric CKD without diabetes. Nevertheless, treatment selection should be individualized according to kidney function, baseline serum potassium, background RAS inhibition, comorbidity, access, and monitoring capacity rather than assuming class superiority (Table 1).

Mechanistic basis of ns-MRAs in NDKD

NDKD represents a heterogeneous group of diseases, yet its progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) converges on common pathogenic pathways characterized by persistent proteinuria, chronic renal inflammation, and fibrosis. By antagonizing MR signaling, ns-MRAs exert pleiotropic effects across multiple levels, including immune modulation, glomerular protection, attenuation of tubulointerstitial injury, and cardiovascular protection. Modulation of the immune microenvironment

ns-MRAs modulate both innate and adaptive immunity, exerting anti-inflammatory effects while regulating specific immune cell subsets. In innate immunity, MR activation in myeloid cells promotes macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Barrera-Chimal et al. [41] demonstrated that finerenone treatment or myeloid-specific MR deletion promoted a shift toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype and attenuated post-ischemic inflammation and fibrosis. Aldosterone-driven proliferation of resident renal macrophages through the MR/colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1) pathway was inhibited by esaxerenone [42], while finerenone also modulated complement C5a–C5aR1 signaling in macrophages [43].

In adaptive immunity, MR signaling has also been implicated in T-cell-mediated renal injury. In a deoxycorticosterone acetate (DOCA)-salt model, finerenone reduced renal infiltration of retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor γt-positive (RORγt+) γδ T cells [44]. Esaxerenone reduced renal CD8 + T-cell infiltration and altered sodium-chloride cotransporter (NCC) phosphorylation in another experimental setting [45]. At the transcriptional level, activated MR can participate in a p65/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) complex that promotes IL-6 expression, whereas esaxerenone interfered with this activity in experimental systems [46]. Collectively, these findings support multi-level immunomodulatory effects of MR blockade, although their relevance across heterogeneous NDKD subtypes remains to be established.

Protection of intrinsic glomerular cells

Podocytes are essential for maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier. Under pathological conditions, MR activation has been linked to podocyte injury, leading to cytoskeletal reorganization and apoptosis, thereby disrupting the filtration barrier and exacerbating proteinuria.

Using single-nucleus RNA sequencing in a DOCA-salt-induced hypertensive injury model, Abedini et al. [47] showed that finerenone reversed maladaptive podocyte gene expression and restored adhesion-related pathways under comparable blood-pressure control, consistent with a podocyte-protective effect partly independent of hemodynamic mechanisms.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key contributor to podocyte injury. In a Western-diet model of obesity and insulin resistance, finerenone attenuated albuminuria, ultrastructural podocyte injury, and mitochondrial abnormalities, with experimental evidence implicating estrogen-related receptor γ (ERRγ) and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathways [48]. The same study also identified changes in inflammatory and matrix-remodeling programs, supporting a multifactorial protective effect in that model.

Rapid podocyte loss activates adjacent parietal epithelial cells (PECs), promoting proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Lazareth et al. [49] showed that PEC-specific MR signaling contributes to this process through the heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor/epidermal growth factor receptor (HB-EGF/EGFR) axis and that steroidal MR blockade attenuates injury. Together, these findings support a potential role for MR antagonism in progressive glomerular lesions, although direct confirmation in human NDKD remains limited.

Tubulointerstitial protection and antifibrotic mechanisms

Tubulointerstitial fibrosis represents a common final pathway in progressive kidney disease. Lattenist et al. [50] demonstrated that finerenone attenuated the AKI-to-CKD transition after ischemia-reperfusion injury, with reductions in tubular injury, oxidative stress, and fibrotic remodeling. Single-nucleus analyses in a separate study further suggested partial restoration of tubular metabolic programs and attenuation of maladaptive intercellular signaling [47]. MR signaling in vascular smooth muscle cells (SMCs) also contributes to ischemia-reperfusion injury. In mice, SMC-specific MR deletion or finerenone treatment attenuated renal injury in association with reduced Rac1–guanosine triphosphate (GTP) formation and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase-related oxidative stress; SMC-specific Rac1 deletion produced a similar phenotype [51]. Validation in a porcine model further supported the translational relevance of this pathway.

Across unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO) and ischemia-reperfusion models, finerenone produced greater reductions in selected fibrotic readouts than empagliflozin in the reported experimental settings [52]. This comparison should be interpreted as model-specific preclinical evidence rather than proof of general clinical superiority over SGLT2 inhibition.

Cardiovascular protection in CKD

Cardiovascular complications are highly prevalent in CKD, and experimental studies also support cardiovascular effects of MR blockade. In a 5/6-nephrectomy CKD rat model, finerenone reduced proteinuria and attenuated left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and myocardial fibrosis, with associated changes in endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) signaling [53]. Consistent findings were reported in a genetic CKD model, where finerenone improved vasodilator responses and Akt/eNOS phosphorylation [54].

Mechanistically, endothelial MR overactivation has been linked to vascular dysfunction. In obesity-associated hypertension, finerenone or endothelial-specific MR deletion reversed selected microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3)/mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) abnormalities associated with impaired vasodilation and vascular remodeling [55]. Finerenone also attenuated neointimal remodeling after vascular injury [56].

In models of CKD-related vascular calcification, spironolactone reduced aortic calcification and osteogenic markers [57, 58]. Because these studies involved a steroidal MRA, they support a potential role for MR signaling in vascular calcification but do not establish an equivalent clinical effect of ns-MRAs. Direct clinical evidence in NDKD remains limited.

Mechanistic rationale for combination therapy

Combination therapy with ns-MRAs and established CKD treatments is supported by complementary mechanistic rationale. In a mouse model of Alport syndrome, ramipril, empagliflozin, and finerenone together improved survival and renal injury measures more than less intensive regimens, while transcriptomic analyses suggested suppression of residual inflammatory and fibrotic programs [59]. In models of hypertension-induced end-organ damage, low-dose finerenone plus empagliflozin also improved selected cardiorenal outcomes [60]. These data support preclinical complementarity, but they should not be interpreted as proof of clinical synergy in all NDKD populations. Clinical evidence for ns-MRAs in NDKD

In patients with CKD associated with type 2 diabetes, finerenone has established cardiorenal outcome evidence from the phase III FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD programs [11, 12]. With publication of FIND-CKD, direct randomized evidence is now available in adults with CKD without diabetes receiving background RAS inhibition [14].

The evidence discussed below is organized using a descriptive hierarchy to aid interpretation rather than a formal GRADE assessment. Tier 1 comprises phase III randomized evidence (FIND-CKD and prespecified analyses of randomized participants); Tier 2 comprises phase II randomized trials and small randomized crossover studies; Tier 3 comprises prospective or retrospective observational clinical studies; Tier 4 comprises preclinical animal or cell-based studies; and Tier 5 comprises exploratory in vitro biomarker findings, including Klotho-related experiments. A higher tier reflects study design strength but does not imply uniform certainty for every NDKD histological subtype or outcome.

FIND-CKD: Phase III evidence in NDKD

FIND-CKD (NCT05047263) is the first phase III randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of finerenone in patients with CKD without diabetes. The trial randomized 1,584 adults receiving RAS inhibitor therapy who had an eGFR of 25 to < 90 mL/min/1.73 m² and albuminuria, defined as a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) of 200 to ≤ 3,500 mg/g, to receive finerenone 10 or 20 mg once daily or placebo. The study population was mainly composed of patients with chronic glomerulonephritis, including IgAN, and hypertensive or ischemic nephropathy, reflecting the etiological heterogeneity of NDKD. The primary outcome was the total eGFR slope, assessed as the mean annual rate of change in eGFR from baseline to month 32 [61].

Finerenone significantly slowed kidney function decline compared with placebo: the mean annual eGFR change from baseline to month 32 was − 3.3 mL/min/1.73 m² in the finerenone group and − 4.0 mL/min/1.73 m² in the placebo group, corresponding to a between-group difference of 0.7 mL/min/1.73 m² per year (P < 0.001). Finerenone also reduced the risk of the prespecified composite kidney or cardiovascular outcome, defined as a reduction from baseline of at least 57% in eGFR, kidney failure, hospitalization for heart failure, or cardiovascular death (HR 0.77; 95% CI, 0.60–0.99). The hazard ratios were 0.78 for the kidney composite and 0.60 for the cardiovascular composite, although the confidence intervals crossed unity. Hyperkalemia was the most common adverse event and occurred more frequently with finerenone than with placebo (17.0% vs. 13.3%); however, discontinuation of the trial regimen due to hyperkalemia was uncommon (1.5% vs. 0.1%), as was hospitalization due to hyperkalemia (0.9% vs. 0.6%) [14].

A prespecified exploratory analysis of the same trial further evaluated 903 participants with investigator-reported glomerular diseases, including IgA nephropathy (IgAN), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and membranous nephropathy (MN). In this subgroup, the between-group difference in total eGFR slope was 0.73 mL/min/1.73 m² per year (95% CI, 0.22–1.24), UACR was reduced by 42% at month 12 (95% CI, 35%–48%), and the exploratory composite of kidney failure or sustained ≥ 40% eGFR decline favored finerenone (HR 0.74; 95% CI, 0.57–0.97) [62]. These findings strengthen the relevance of finerenone to proteinuric glomerular disease; however, this analysis derives from the same parent FIND-CKD trial and should not be regarded as an independent confirmatory randomized trial. Moreover, analyses of individual glomerular subtypes were exploratory and less precise for smaller disease groups.

Overall, FIND-CKD provides the strongest direct clinical evidence to date for finerenone in proteinuric NDKD. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. The primary endpoint was total eGFR slope rather than a hard kidney endpoint, patients with very low-grade albuminuria or extreme nephrotic-range proteinuria were underrepresented, and treatment effects may differ across heterogeneous NDKD subtypes. Further disease-specific analyses and longer-term studies are therefore needed to clarify which patients derive the greatest benefit and whether eGFR-slope improvement translates into sustained reductions in kidney failure.

Real-world evidence in IgA nephropathy and membranous nephropathy

Primary glomerular diseases are a major component of NDKD. Beyond the randomized evidence summarized above, disease-specific clinical data for IgAN and MN are derived mainly from prospective or retrospective observational studies, consistent with recent discussions of the potential role of MRAs in nondiabetic CKD and glomerular disease [63].

In IgAN, antiproteinuric effects of finerenone have been reported across several studies. A small prospective study observed a maximal reduction in proteinuria of approximately 40% over 2–8 months [64], while retrospective cohorts reported response rates of approximately 62% [65] and a 6-month UACR reduction of approximately 42% [16]. In a multicenter observational study of 178 patients, finerenone was associated with a 45.1% reduction in median UPCR at 6 months, with stable eGFR and no signal of uncontrolled hyperkalemia [66]. A propensity score-matched study likewise reported greater proteinuria reduction with finerenone plus RAS inhibition than with RAS inhibition alone [67].

Larger retrospective analyses have provided additional evidence. In a cohort of 382 patients with IgAN, finerenone use was associated with a higher rate of complete renal remission and a lower risk of renal endpoints; greater remission was also observed among patients receiving concomitant SGLT2 inhibitor therapy [68]. Another retrospective study of 76 patients reported greater proteinuria reduction with finerenone plus an SGLT2 inhibitor than with either monotherapy, without an apparent increase in hyperkalemia during short-term follow-up [69].

In MN, a retrospective cohort of 20 patients reported reductions in UPCR and UACR of approximately 42% and 44%, respectively, at 6 months [70]. A larger multicenter real-world cohort reported a 58.5% reduction in proteinuria over 6 months, with greater reductions among patients receiving concomitant immunosuppressive therapy; renal function remained broadly stable and no serum potassium values > 5.5 mmol/L were reported [71]. In another real-world study of 43 patients with NDKD, nearly half of whom had MN, median 24-hour urinary protein excretion decreased by 2.46 g, while eGFR and serum potassium remained stable during short-term follow-up [72].

Overall, evidence from IgAN and MN suggests a potential antiproteinuric role for finerenone in selected patients. However, most available studies are nonrandomized, have relatively small sample sizes and short follow-up, and rely mainly on surrogate endpoints such as UACR or UPCR reduction rather than hard kidney outcomes. Selection bias, residual confounding, heterogeneity in background therapy, and incomplete adverse-event ascertainment remain important limitations. These findings should therefore be confirmed in adequately powered, disease-specific randomized trials with longer follow-up and clinically meaningful kidney outcomes. Combination therapy with ns-MRAs and SGLT2 inhibitors

With SGLT2i now a key component of CKD therapy [5], interest in combination with ns-MRAs has increased. In NDKD, preliminary evidence is emerging.

A phase 2b randomized controlled trial (MIRO-CKD) evaluated the ns-MRA balcinrenone in combination with dapagliflozin in CKD patients with proteinuria. Compared with dapagliflozin monotherapy, combination therapy reduced UACR by an additional 22.8% and 32.8% at the 15 mg and 40 mg doses, respectively, with a greater reduction observed at the higher dose. Hyperkalemia occurred slightly more frequently in the combination groups, although no serious adverse events were reported. These findings support further evaluation of the short-term efficacy and safety of this combination [73].

Another open-label randomized crossover trial in 20 patients with NDKD evaluated finerenone (20 mg/day) in combination with dapagliflozin (10 mg/day) over 8 weeks. UACR was reduced by 36%, exceeding that observed with either monotherapy and approaching the expected additive effect [74]. The ROTATE-3 study, although involving the steroidal MRA eplerenone rather than an ns-MRA, also suggested complementary albuminuria-lowering effects of combined SGLT2 and MR blockade [75].

In IgAN, combination therapy has also shown greater antiproteinuric effects than either monotherapy in observational studies [68, 69], supporting further evaluation of combined ns-MRA and SGLT2 inhibitor therapy in primary glomerular disease.

From a physiological perspective, SGLT2 inhibition induces glycosuria and osmotic diuresis and increases distal tubular sodium delivery and tubular flow, changes that may favor distal potassium secretion [76]. This effect may partially counterbalance the potassium-retaining effect of MR blockade but does not eliminate hyperkalemia risk or the need for appropriate monitoring of serum potassium and kidney function.

Evidence in patients with hypertension and CKD

Esaxerenone is an ns-MRA approved in Japan for hypertension, with emerging evidence in patients with hypertension and concomitant CKD.

In patients with hypertension and moderate kidney dysfunction, dose-escalation studies evaluated esaxerenone titration from 1.25 mg/day to 2.5–5 mg/day. Target dosing was achieved in most participants, including those receiving concomitant RAS inhibitors. After 12 weeks, clinically relevant blood pressure reductions were observed, while transient hyperkalemia occurred in a minority of patients receiving combination therapy and renal function remained broadly stable [77].

Overall, these dose-escalation data support the feasibility of esaxerenone use in patients with hypertension and CKD. However, the available evidence is derived mainly from hypertension-focused studies rather than dedicated NDKD outcome trials, and extrapolation to heterogeneous NDKD populations should remain cautious.

Safety considerations and management of hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia remains the principal safety concern associated with ns-MRA use in CKD. In FIDELIO-DKD, FIGARO-DKD, CONFIDENCE, and FIND-CKD, hyperkalemia occurred more frequently with finerenone-based therapy than with control therapy, although most events were managed through protocol-guided monitoring, temporary interruption, or dose adjustment [11–13]. In FIND-CKD, hyperkalemia was reported in 17.0% of participants receiving finerenone and 13.3% receiving placebo, whereas permanent discontinuation and hospitalization due to hyperkalemia were uncommon [14]. Available NDKD real-world studies have not identified severe hyperkalemia as a dominant short-term safety signal, but these observations should be interpreted cautiously because most cohorts were retrospective and involved selected patients [15, 16, 71, 72].

Before initiation of finerenone, baseline eGFR and serum potassium should be assessed. In general, 20 mg once daily may be considered when eGFR is ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m², whereas 10 mg once daily with cautious titration is preferred when eGFR is 25–59 mL/min/1.73 m². Routine initiation is not recommended when eGFR is < 25 mL/min/1.73 m², and use in such patients should be limited to exceptional individualized circumstances under specialist supervision. Serum potassium and kidney function should generally be reassessed approximately 4 weeks after initiation or dose adjustment and thereafter according to individual risk [78].

No hyperkalemia-management algorithm has been prospectively validated specifically for NDKD. Accordingly, the following approach should be regarded as a pragmatic, risk-based framework rather than a disease-specific standard. For serum potassium levels of 5.0–5.5 mmol/L, immediate discontinuation is not invariably required; reversible contributors should be reviewed, including dietary potassium excess, potassium supplements, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, dehydration, acute kidney injury, and other potassium-raising medications. Early repeat testing is appropriate, and dose escalation should generally be avoided. Dose reduction or temporary interruption may be considered when potassium continues to rise, recurrent elevations occur, or additional risk factors are present.

For serum potassium > 5.5 mmol/L, temporary interruption of the ns-MRA should generally be considered while the result is confirmed and reversible causes are addressed. Depending on the clinical context, measures may include adjustment of contributing medications, use of an appropriate diuretic, correction of metabolic acidosis, and consideration of potassium binders such as patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate. Reinitiation at a lower dose may be considered only after serum potassium has returned to an acceptable range and kidney function is stable [78].

Monitoring intervals should be individualized. Patients at higher risk—including those with lower eGFR, higher baseline serum potassium, recent dose changes, intercurrent illness, or concomitant RAS inhibition—may require reassessment earlier than the routine 4-week interval and more frequent follow-up until values stabilize. Severe hyperkalemia, rapid potassium elevation, electrocardiographic abnormalities, or associated symptoms require urgent clinical evaluation. These thresholds should be interpreted as a pragmatic, protocol-informed framework rather than a validated NDKD-specific algorithm.

Disease heterogeneity, special populations, and future perspectives

Although NDKD subtypes share convergent pathways involving proteinuria, inflammation, and fibrosis, they differ substantially in initiating mechanisms, histological lesions, and the relative contributions of vascular, immune, podocyte, and tubulointerstitial injury. This heterogeneity may influence the magnitude and pattern of response to ns-MRAs. For example, IgAN with active inflammatory lesions or high-risk Oxford MEST-C (mesangial hypercellularity, endocapillary hypercellularity, segmental sclerosis, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and crescents) features may differ biologically from predominantly chronic sclerotic disease, whereas hypertensive nephrosclerosis may derive benefit mainly through vascular, endothelial, and hemodynamic mechanisms. In MN, podocyte injury and nephrotic-range proteinuria are central, and ns-MRAs are more appropriately considered adjunctive antiproteinuric and antifibrotic agents than disease-specific immunomodulators. Therefore, apparently similar antiproteinuric responses across NDKD should not be interpreted as evidence of identical disease biology or uniform treatment effects [3, 14, 18, 68, 71, 72].

The potential relationship between MR blockade and Klotho also requires cautious interpretation. Although Klotho is generally considered a renoprotective and anti-aging factor, recent in vitro data suggest that different MR antagonists may exert divergent effects on Klotho expression, with finerenone, spironolactone, and eplerenone showing distinct regulatory profiles in renal cell lines [79]. The clinical relevance of these findings remains uncertain because the available evidence relies on limited cell-line models, lacks validation in human renal tissue, and has not been examined in prospective biomarker cohorts. It is also unclear whether the observed effects are cell type–specific, dose dependent, exposure dependent, or influenced by compensatory signaling. Accordingly, Klotho-related findings should currently be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence limiting the clinical use of finerenone. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal measurements of circulating and urinary Klotho together with MR-activation markers, inflammatory mediators, fibrosis biomarkers, and clinical outcomes.

Special populations require particular caution. In patients with advanced CKD, especially those with eGFR < 25 mL/min/1.73 m², evidence remains insufficient and hyperkalemia risk is higher. Routine use of ns-MRAs in this population cannot currently be recommended; individualized use, if considered, should occur under strict specialist supervision with intensive monitoring. Older adults may also be more vulnerable because of multimorbidity, polypharmacy, fluctuating kidney function, and reduced physiological reserve.

Kidney transplant recipients represent another clinically relevant but understudied population. Available finerenone evidence derives from a very small single-center retrospective cohort. Kahvecioglu et al. screened 1,750 kidney transplant recipients and compared 15 patients receiving finerenone with 15 matched controls. Finerenone was associated with an approximately 40% reduction in proteinuria at 6 months among patients who tolerated therapy, without significant deterioration in eGFR or serum creatinine, although early potassium increases and the small retrospective design require substantial caution [80]. Importantly, long-term hard allograft outcomes, rejection risk, interactions with calcineurin inhibitor exposure, and sustained safety have not been established. Routine use in transplant recipients therefore cannot currently be recommended.

Following FIND-CKD, future research should move from a uniform drug-class perspective toward a precision-stratified framework. Priority areas include disease-specific subgroup analyses, histological activity and chronicity scores, baseline proteinuria, eGFR category, MR-activation biomarkers, fibrosis burden, and background use of RAS and SGLT2 inhibitors. Prospective studies should determine whether benefits observed on eGFR slope translate into sustained reductions in kidney failure and other hard clinical outcomes, while also evaluating patient-reported outcomes, cardiovascular events, safety in high-risk populations, and cost-effectiveness. Such work may help identify which NDKD subtypes and clinical phenotypes are most likely to benefit from ns-MRA therapy and how these agents can be integrated into individualized CKD management. Recent cardiorenal reviews have similarly emphasized the expanding therapeutic context of finerenone and the need to define its role across broader CKD phenotypes and combination-treatment strategies [81].

Conclusions

Accumulating evidence supports MR activation as an important contributor to hemodynamic, inflammatory, vascular, and fibrotic injury in NDKD. Preclinical evidence suggests that ns-MRAs exert anti-inflammatory, antifibrotic, and cardiovascular effects, while current clinical evidence supports their consideration in selected patients with proteinuric NDKD.

Clinically, FIND-CKD provides phase III randomized evidence that finerenone modestly slows eGFR decline in adults with proteinuric CKD without diabetes receiving background RAS inhibition [14]. A prespecified exploratory analysis of the same parent trial further supports its relevance to glomerular diseases [62], while observational studies in IgAN and MN provide supportive antiproteinuric signals. However, therapeutic responses are likely to vary across NDKD subtypes and should be interpreted in relation to baseline proteinuria, kidney function, histological activity and chronicity, background therapy, and hyperkalemia risk.

Accordingly, ns-MRAs may have a role in selected patients with proteinuric NDKD under appropriate serum potassium and kidney function monitoring. Longer-term studies are needed to determine whether eGFR-slope benefits translate into durable kidney outcomes and to define benefit-risk profiles in advanced CKD, older adults, kidney transplant recipients, and other understudied populations. Precision-oriented trials and biomarker studies may help identify which NDKD phenotypes are most likely to benefit and how ns-MRAs should be integrated with RAS and SGLT2 inhibition.
