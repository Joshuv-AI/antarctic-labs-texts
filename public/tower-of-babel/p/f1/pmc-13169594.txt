Efficacy of group counseling in enhancing social adaptation of adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation
Authors: Hai Zhu, Zongkui Zhou, Huihui Ke, Ju Yao, Ruxue Yan, Zeyong Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents relocated for poverty alleviation are prone to social maladaptation, which may adversely affect their personal development and social stability. Enhancing their social adaptation is therefore of considerable significance. This study designed and implemented a group counseling program to examine its effects on the social adaptation of these adolescents.MethodsThis study recruited a total of 300 relocated adolescents experiencing social adaptation difficulties. These participants came from urban districts, county seats, and towns in Z City, Guizhou Province, China. The intervention group (n = 151) received an 8-week group counseling program targeting social adaptation, while the control group (n = 149) received no intervention. The Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents, the Personal Growth Initiative Scale–II., and the Future Expectations Scale were administered before and after intervention.ResultsAt baseline, no significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in total scores of social adaptation, personal growth initiative, or future expectations. After eight weeks, the intervention group scored significantly higher on all three scales compared with the control group. No significant pre–post changes were found in the control group, whereas the intervention group showed significant improvements from pre- to posttest.ConclusionThe structured group counseling program significantly enhanced the social adaptation of adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation, enabling them to adjust more effectively to new environments and new lives after relocation.Keywords: Adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation, Group counseling, Social adaptation, Personal growth initiative, Future expectation

Introduction

Relocation for poverty alleviation is a resettlement policy implemented in China under the national poverty eradication initiative to address the developmental dilemma of “a land that cannot support its people.” This policy represents a significant instance of what the global literature terms “Development-Induced Displacement and Resettlement (DIDR)” [1, 2], a phenomenon affecting millions worldwide due to infrastructure projects, urban development, and environmental programs [3]. In China, According to the Guizhou Provincial Bureau of Ecological Migration, during the 13th Five-Year Plan period, 1.92 million people were relocated across the province, the highest among all provinces and municipalities in China, with more than 300,000 of them being school-aged children. This large-scale resettlement creates profound challenges for the affected individuals, particularly the young. After leaving their familiar places of residence, relocated households face changes in living environments and production patterns, requiring a period of adjustment to their new lives. Compared with adults, children with immature physical and psychological development encounter greater challenges in adapting. Adolescents aged 11–18, whose development is unbalanced and highly influenced by multiple factors, are particularly vulnerable to social maladaptation in new environments [4].

Social adaptation reflects the dynamic process of interaction between individuals and their social environment. Successful adaptation enables individuals to maintain a harmonious and balanced relationship with the outside world [5]. It has a significant effect on physical and mental health [6], serves as a key indicator of individual development [7], and is crucial for adolescents’ future growth. Maladaptation not only compromises adolescents’ current health and academic progress but may also increase their risk of behavioral problems [6], hindering both personal development and social stability. Therefore, improving the social adaptation of adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation is important for both individual well-being and broader social stability. Previous studies have shown that social adaptation is shaped by multiple factors, including individual attributes such as self-esteem, self-efficacy, self-control, and self-concept [8–10], as well as environmental factors such as relationships with family and school [8, 11–13].

The determinants of social adaptation among relocated adolescents share commonalities with those of general children and adolescents but are also shaped by the unique experiences of relocation. Relocation represents a special form of population migration, and research on migrant and mobile populations offers useful insights. Studies on migrant children suggest that social adaptation is influenced at the individual, family, school, and societal levels. Psychological qualities such as identity, self-esteem, and resilience are positively associated with adaptation [14, 15]. Favorable family economic conditions and close parent–child interactions also promote adaptation [16]. Family-level factors such as gendered division of labor may also shape adolescents’ behavioral adaptation, including bullying perpetration [17]. In rural Chinese contexts, grandparenting styles in skipped-generation families may also play a significant role in adolescent psychological outcomes [18]. At the school level, peer interactions, teacher–student relationships, and teacher support exert significant influence [19, 20]. At the societal level, institutional safeguards, public attitudes, and community services further shape adaptation [21, 22]. However, relocation for poverty alleviation differs from ordinary migration in its policy-driven nature, large-scale collective resettlement, and establishment of new settlement communities. These features may create distinct challenges, including changes in household economic conditions and a lack of belonging in newly established communities.

Bandura’s theory of reciprocal determinism proposes that individual internal factors, behaviors, and the environment exert mutual influence, suggesting that social adaptation is shaped by both intrinsic personal factors and extrinsic environmental conditions [23, 24]. Similarly, ecological systems theory emphasizes that individual development is nested within multiple layers of environmental systems [25]. Relocation disrupts the original microsystem, mesosystem, and exosystem, producing substantial changes in family, school, and community contexts, and requiring individuals to actively adjust to new systems. Accordingly, the design of intervention programs should focus on both the individual in the new environment and the interactive processes within it. While ecological systems theory emphasizes the importance of multiple interacting systems, enhancing individual agency and internal resources is a foundational prerequisite for adolescents to successfully navigate and leverage their changing microsystems [26]. Before adolescents can effectively utilize support from family, school, or community, they must possess the internal capacity to recognize, seek out, and engage with these external resources. Therefore, this study focuses on strengthening the individual’s internal resources. This approach aims to equip adolescents with enhanced adaptive capacities, including proactive behavioral orientations and positive cognitive outlooks, and foster a supportive peer-group microsystem through group counseling, thereby providing a stronger foundation for them to proactively interact with and benefit from the broader family and school systems.

Group counseling, using the group as a medium, provides participants with opportunities and settings for self-exploration and growth [27]. It emphasizes developmental processes, adopts a growth-oriented perspective, and facilitates the stimulation of individuals’ intrinsic motivation [28]. Empirical evidence indicates that group counseling is an effective therapeutic and educational approach for groups experiencing shared developmental challenges and similar psychological difficulties [29, 30], with particular advantages in school settings. Because of its emphasis on peer support and collective growth, group counseling is especially well suited to assisting adolescents who face common maladaptation pressures after relocation. As relocation for poverty alleviation is a collective experience affecting this population, a group-based intervention is theoretically superior as it mirrors this communal change. It provides participants with a temporary surrogate microsystem that is a supportive peer group sharing similar experiences. This enables them to learn adaptive strategies and to strengthen their confidence and ability to cope with new environments. This study therefore employs group counseling as an intervention to improve the social adaptation of adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation.

Evidence also indicates a positive association between social adaptation and personal growth initiative [31]. The higher the level of personal growth initiative, the better the adaptation to the surrounding environment [32]. Adolescents’ degree of social adaptation is also closely related to their expectations for the future; those with higher expectations are more likely to achieve healthy development [33]. On this basis, this study hypothesizes that group counseling promotes better adaptation to new environments and new lives among relocated adolescents by enhancing their personal growth initiative and future expectations. Accordingly, activities designed to strengthen these two psychological qualities were incorporated into the group counseling program, and the study examined their changes along with their association with improvements in social adaptation.

From the perspective of intervention pathways, most existing studies have focused on enhancing specific traits such as hope [34] or abilities such as problem-solving [35] to indirectly promote social adaptation. Few programs have been developed with the direct goal of improving social adaptation. Regarding intervention targets, most studies have examined special groups such as children with hearing impairment [35] or individuals with autism [36]. Although findings show that group counseling can improve the mental health of migrant children [37] and enhance their social adaptation [38], targeted interventions for adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation in China remain scarce. This study therefore developed a group counseling program based on the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents [39]. Compared with other social adaptation scales, this instrument is more specific in assessing the adaptation of relocated adolescents. A program based on its dimensions allows for more targeted intervention in both pathways and target groups.

In summary, this study has two primary aims. First, it aims to design a group counseling program to improve social adaptation in adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation, using the dimensions of the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents as a framework. Second, it aims to test the effectiveness of this program in improving adolescents’ social adaptation, while also examining its effects on personal growth initiative and future expectations, thereby providing a preliminary exploration of its underlying mechanisms.

Methods

Participants

Adolescents are primarily concentrated in the upper grades of primary school through senior high school. The Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents was administered in seven schools in Z City (three primary schools and four secondary schools), covering students from Grade 4 of primary school to Grade 12 of senior high school. A total of 3,565 questionnaires were distributed, and 3,536 valid responses were collected, yielding a response rate of 99.19%. The required sample size was calculated using G*Power software, with effect size set at a medium level (f = 0.25), significance level α = 0.05, and power 1 – β = 0.95. The result indicated that a minimum of 210 participants was required.

Students were ranked from lowest to highest based on their total social adaptation scores. The group leaders then conducted interviews in sequence based on this ranking, and, following the principle of voluntary participation, selected adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation who demonstrated social adaptation difficulties and expressed motivation and determination to make changes. Motivation was assessed during these interviews using two standardized screening questions: “How interested are you in participating in activities that could help you adapt better to your new environment?” and “How much effort are you willing to invest in such activities?” Responses were rated on a 3-point scale, and only adolescents indicating at least moderate motivation were included. This selective sampling approach was adopted for ethical and practical considerations: ensuring that participants were willing to engage in a psychological intervention aimed at promoting change, which is essential for both participant safety and intervention integrity in a voluntary counseling context. It also aimed to enhance participants’ commitment and reduce dropout rates.

These included students resettled in urban districts, county seats, and towns. The selected students from the same school were randomized into either the intervention group or the control group. During the study, 16 participants withdrew, and 300 students were ultimately included (see Fig. 1). The intervention group included 151 participants (74 boys, 77 girls), and the control group included 149 participants (74 boys, 75 girls). The distribution of boys and girls across educational stages and regions is shown in Table 1. Because the number of relocated adolescents at the senior high school level in towns and urban districts was very small, this subgroup was not included.

During the study, 16 participants withdrew (see Fig. 1), resulting in a final sample of 300 students. Measures

Social adaptation scale for relocated adolescents

The Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents [39] contains 30 items across five dimensions: self-development, self-identity, interpersonal cognition, proactive communication, and behavioral control. Self-development refers to the emotional experience generated by evaluating one’s own value and abilities. Self-identity refers to the degree to which relocated adolescents recognize their identity during adaptation and integrate into new groups. Interpersonal cognition reflects the ability to establish and maintain positive interpersonal interactions and awareness of the importance of social behaviors. Proactive communication refers to the willingness and ability to engage actively with others. Behavioral control refers to the ability to regulate physical or verbal actions that may harm others. Each item is rated on a 5-point (1–5) Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of social adaptation. In this study, the overall internal consistency coefficient of the scale was 0.90, and the coefficients for the five subscales were 0.82, 0.77, 0.79, 0.74, and 0.73, respectively.

Personal growth initiative scale–II

The Personal Growth Initiative Scale–II [40, 41] consists of 16 items across four dimensions: readiness for change, planfulness, using resources, and intentional behavior. Responses are rated on a 6-point (0–5) Likert scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of personal growth initiative. In this study, the overall internal consistency coefficient of the scale was 0.95, with coefficients for the four subscales of 0.89, 0.87, 0.83, and 0.78, respectively.

Future expectations scale for adolescents

The Future Expectations Scale for Adolescents [42, 43] consists of 14 items across four dimensions: occupational expectations, academic expectations, family expectations, and expectations of personal well-being. Each item is rated on a 5-point (1–5) Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting more positive expectations for the future. In this study, the overall internal consistency coefficient of the scale was 0.93, with coefficients for the four subscales of 0.86, 0.87, 0.83, and 0.78, respectively.

Procedure

All participants were first assessed using the three instruments: the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents, the Personal Growth Initiative Scale–II, and the Future Expectations Scale for Adolescents. The intervention group then participated in group counseling sessions lasting 1.5 h per week for eight consecutive weeks, while the control group received no intervention during this period. After the intervention, both groups were reassessed with the same three scales. Group counseling sessions were conducted by leaders with backgrounds in mental health education and relevant practical experience. Each session was facilitated by two leaders: one primary leader and one co-leader.

The intervention was delivered by four leaders, all postgraduate students in psychology. They worked in pairs, coordinating their schedules to cover the sessions across the participating schools, as shown in Table 2. Prior to intervention, all leaders completed a standardized training based on the intervention manual, which covered group facilitation skills, ethical guidelines, and crisis management. To ensure intervention fidelity, leaders were required to log the completion of core activities and note any adaptations made after each session. Furthermore, they participated in weekly online group supervision meetings led by a senior supervisor. These meetings served to address implementation challenges, ensure procedural consistency, and provide ongoing professional support.

The counseling groups were structured, homogeneous, and developmental in nature. Considering differences between primary and secondary school students, some units were adapted according to educational stage. The primary goal of the counseling program was to enhance the social adaptation of relocated adolescents. Activities were designed based on the five dimensions of the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents: self-development, self-identity, interpersonal cognition, proactive communication, and behavioral control. In addition, drawing on the principles of positive psychology, activities were incorporated to strengthen participants’ personal growth initiative and future expectations, with the aim of fostering positive psychological resources that would support adolescents’ long-term adaptation and development.

The overall objectives of the program were: (1) to help members recognize and accept themselves, strengthen motivation, and persist in their efforts; (2) to help members learn effective communication and improve self-control, thereby facilitating personal growth; and (3) to help members enhance social adaptation and instill a sense of hope for the future. Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 23.0. Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated to assess the internal consistency of the questionnaires. Before the formal analysis, independent-sample t tests were conducted to confirm homogeneity between the intervention and control groups. Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was then applied to examine both main effects and interaction effects between the two groups. To assess potential school-level clustering effects, we calculated Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs) for the three outcome variables at posttest using linear mixed models with school as a random intercept. The ICCs were small and non-significant for social adaptation (ICC = 0.04, P = 0.25), personal growth initiative (ICC = 0.03, P = 0.34), and future expectations (ICC = 0.06, P = 0.19), indicating that school-level clustering did not substantially violate the independence assumption of MANOVA.

Ethics statements

This study adhered to ethical standards for scientific research and was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Teacher Education, Zunyi Normal University (Approval No. ZYSYJY-2023001). Written informed consent was obtained from all adolescent participants and their parents before the study, with a clear explanation of the research purpose, procedures, and principles of data confidentiality. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Throughout the intervention and data collection, participants’ physical and psychological well-being and personal privacy were safeguarded. All data were used solely for the purposes of this study.

Results

Homogeneity test of variables before intervention

Independent-sample t tests were conducted to examine the homogeneity of the intervention and control groups. No significant differences were found between the two groups in total scores on the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents (t = − 1.05, P > 0.05), the Personal Growth Initiative Scale–II (t = − 1.57, P > 0.05), or the Future Expectations Scale for Adolescents (t = − 1.32, P > 0.05).

Social adaptation

Data analysis revealed that at pretest, no significant differences were observed in social adaptation total scores across educational stages (primary school, junior high school, senior high school) (F(2, 297) = 2.73, P > 0.05). However, significant differences were found across regions (urban districts, county seats, towns) (F(2, 297) = 6.41, P < 0.01). Therefore, educational stage was excluded from further analysis, and region was included as a covariate in the subsequent analysis of covariance.

Discussion

The results of this study showed that the social adaptation of adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation in the intervention group improved significantly following group counseling, whereas no significant changes were observed in the control group. After eight weeks of intervention, the social adaptation level of the intervention group was also significantly higher than that of the control group. These findings suggest that social adaptation can be enhanced through intervention and that group counseling is an effective approach for improving social adaptation, consistent with previous research [44, 45]. The effectiveness of group counseling for relocated adolescents may be attributed to its alignment with the relocation context. As relocation often ruptures existing social networks, the group served as a temporary surrogate microsystem that provided a supportive peer environment where adolescents shared similar experiences. This provided immediate belongingness and mutual understanding, buffering the stress of adapting to new surroundings.

Analysis of the subdimensions of the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents revealed that, with the exception of behavioral control, scores in self-development, interpersonal cognition, self-identity, and proactive communication increased significantly in the intervention group and were significantly higher than in the control group after counseling. This indicates that participation in group counseling provided adolescents with opportunities and contexts for interaction and mutual support, thereby enhancing positive emotional experiences and awareness of proactive communication. These experiences helped them integrate more effectively into new groups and environments after relocation and strengthened their sense of self-identity.

By contrast, no significant improvements were observed in behavioral control. Several factors may explain this. First, the effects of behavioral control training are often reflected in neural mechanisms [46] and behavioral indicators [47], which require specialized instruments for measurement. The use of self-report questionnaires in this study may have limited the ability to detect changes in this domain. Second, improvement in behavioral control generally requires repeated and long-term training [48], whereas the effects of short-term interventions are limited [46]. In this study, only one unit of the counseling program focused on behavioral control, with insufficient practice and feedback, which likely accounts for the absence of significant improvement in this dimension.

Analysis of the Personal Growth Initiative Scale–II and the Future Expectations Scale for Adolescents further showed that relocated adolescents improved in both personal growth initiative and future expectations after counseling. The enhancement of these positive psychological qualities holds potential for supporting their long-term adaptation and development. Previous studies have shown that when individuals’ expectations for the future and levels of initiative increase, their adaptation to the surrounding environment improves [33, 49]. This aligns with the perspective of positive psychology, which posits that individuals with positive psychological traits adapt more readily to their environment [50]. Adolescents capable of self-initiated and proactive change [51] report higher life satisfaction, fewer negative emotions such as depression [52, 53], and greater psychological well-being [54]. Adolescents who hold higher expectations for their future in domains such as work, study, and personal life are also more likely to achieve healthy development [33].

Beyond confirming the overall improvement in outcome variables, the correlation analyses revealed a deeper layer of intervention effect: the intervention group exhibited significantly stronger linkages between social adaptation and both personal growth initiative and future expectations. This strengthened correlation pattern suggests the intervention synchronized these developmental assets, rather than demonstrating a causal pathway. The particularly strong association between social adaptation and personal growth initiative in the intervention group implies that group counseling helped participants develop a more integrated set of adaptive resources. However, correlations do not establish causation. This finding provides preliminary evidence that personal growth initiative and future expectations may be important factors associated with social adaptation, but whether they serve as mechanisms through which group counseling promotes adaptation requires formal mediation analysis.

Theoretically, this finding aligns well with Bandura’s reciprocal determinism. The counseling group created a supportive microsystem (environment) in which successful interactions (behavior) enhanced confidence and growth initiative (personal factors), which in turn promoted further adaptive engagement. The particularly strong association between social adaptation and PGI in the intervention group implies that the intervention may have helped relocated adolescents translate their enhanced internal drive more effectively into concrete adaptive strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, participants were selected based on their motivation to change, resulting in a sample of motivated rather than general relocated adolescents, thus limiting external validity and potentially inflating effect estimates. Second, the use of a no‑treatment control group instead of an active control group cannot rule out attention or expectation effects, nor does it allow us to determine whether the program is more effective than regular group activities. Third, the intervention primarily focused on individual-level processes, with limited engagement of family or school systems, which are central to the ecological framework invoked. Finally, the study did not examine causal mediation pathways or long‑term effects of intervention.

Future research should include more representative samples and employ active control groups to verify intervention effectiveness. Moreover, integrating multi-system components could enhance the ecological validity and impact of the intervention. Specifically, given the limited improvement in behavioral control, future interventions may benefit from a hybrid design combining group counseling with behavioral drills or teacher/parent training. Longitudinal studies with mediation analyses are also needed to examine long-term effects and underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

Using the Social Adaptation Scale for Relocated Adolescents as a framework, this study designed and implemented a structured group counseling program. The results demonstrated that the program significantly improved the overall social adaptation of adolescents relocated for poverty alleviation, enabling them to adjust more effectively to new environments and new lives after relocation. The intervention also enhanced participants’ personal growth initiative and future expectations, enabling them to adjust more effectively to new environments and new lives after relocation. The improvement of these two positive psychological qualities provides a foundation for supporting long-term adaptation and development in the new context.
