A 65-year-old woman presented to our emergency department with gait ataxia, memory impairment, and blurred vision. Two weeks earlier, she had developed fever, headache, and dizziness, diagnosed at a local clinic as a presumed viral infection. Her dizziness and unsteadiness progressed, leaving her unable to perform daily activities. Her medical history included open-angle glaucoma with mild visual field impairment. She had a 33-pack-year smoking history, did not use alcohol, and did not have dietary restrictions.

The patient was afebrile with stable vital signs and a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14 (Eye Opening 4, Verbal Response 4, Motor Activity 6). Neurologic examination showed flutter-like oscillations and opsoclonus (Appendix 1, video 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251204/tab-related-content), and she had scanning speech. Testing of her cerebellum indicated dysmetria with movement decomposition during the finger-to-nose test. She had seated postural instability with intermittent generalized myoclonus (Appendix 2, videos 2 and 3, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251204/tab-related-content). Her Romberg test was negative, and gait testing was limited by unsteadiness. She did not have nuchal rigidity, sensory deficits, or autonomic dysfunctions.

Initial laboratory screening and noncontrast brain computed tomography (CT) performed in the emergency department were unremarkable. Suspecting a cerebrovascular event or encephalitis, the emergency physician ordered brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which was also unremarkable. Her lumbar puncture showed lymphocytic pleocytosis (117 [reference < 5] × 109/L; 100% mononuclear leukocytes). Cerebrospinal fluid protein was 0.35 (reference 0.15 to 0.8) g/L, and glucose was 3.4 (reference 2.3 to 3.9; serum glucose 5.6) mmol/L.

Upon admitting the patient to our neurology service, we repeated the neurologic examination, which suggested cerebellitis, and ordered studies to exclude infectious and metabolic causes for her symptoms. Cerebrospinal fluid cultures, infectious testing, serum HIV tests, and syphilis tests were negative. We excluded thyroid dysfunction and vitamin deficiency. Autoimmune and immune-mediated encephalitis-associated antibodies were not detected.

Chest radiography suggested right hilar lymphadenopathy (Figure 1). Contrast-enhanced CT showed a right lower lobe nodule with hilar lymphadenopathy (Figure 2A, Figure 2B). Bronchoscopic biopsy confirmed small cell lung cancer (SCLC). We obtained a serum paraneoplastic antibody panel, which was positive only for anti-Sry-like high-mobility group box (SOX) 1 antibodies. We diagnosed anti-SOX1 antibody-positive paraneoplastic neurologic syndrome (PNS) associated with limited-disease SCLC (LD-SCLC).

In hospital, the patient’s best-corrected visual acuity was 0.3/0.1, which had decreased from 0.6/0.2 at 3 months before symptom onset. Objective retinal testing performed by the ophthalmologist showed abnormalities in both eyes, consistent with her visual symptoms (Appendix 3, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251204/tab-related-content), and they diagnosed cancer-associated retinopathy (CAR), in addition to her PNS.

The patient’s neurologic symptoms worsened during her hospital stay (Figure 3). Her Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) was 20/30, and her modified Rankin Scale (mRS) was 4, both indicating substantial cognitive impairment and severe functional disability. On day 10, we initiated intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; 400 mg/kg/d for 5 d) and intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg/d for 3 d). She received concurrent chemoradiotherapy for LD-SCLC (4 cycles of carboplatin–etoposide and hyperfractionated radiotherapy, 45 Gy/30 fractions). Her neurologic symptoms transiently improved but relapsed within a week, prompting repeat intravenous methylprednisolone (day 19) and subsequent oral maintenance therapy.

At discharge (day 37), the patient’s mRS was 1 and MMSE was 29/30 (33 d after baseline). Her sitting balance had normalized and her gait was steady (Appendix 4, videos 4 and 5, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251204/tab-related-content). During the second chemotherapy, her best-corrected visual acuity improved to 1.0/0.1, and repeat ophthalmologic testing recovered (Appendix 3). After 4 cycles, contrast-enhanced CT showed complete resolution of the hilar lymphadenopathy (Figure 2C, Figure 2D). She also reported that a “mist had lifted” from her vision and she had regained independence, including being able to file her tax return. Six months later, she remained free from symptoms while tapering prednisolone.

Discussion

Paraneoplastic neurologic syndromes are neurologic disorders characterized by immune-mediated damage to the nervous system that occurs distant from the site of malignancy or metastasis.1 They are thought to arise when tumour cells ectopically express proteins normally restricted to neurons or glia (onconeural antigens), triggering tumour-specific antibodies and cytotoxic T-cell responses.1 Through cross-reactivity or molecular mimicry, these immune responses inadvertently target the nervous system and cause neurologic injury.1 The mechanisms underlying loss of immune tolerance remain unclear.

Although symptomatic PNS is rare, affecting approximately 0.01% of all patients with cancer, it occurs more frequently in certain malignancies1 and is most strongly associated with SCLC (3% to 5%).2 Other associated cancers include ovarian and breast cancers, non–small cell lung cancer, lymphoma, and thymoma.1,3

The manifestations of such syndromes are highly diverse, depending on whether immune-mediated injury affects the central nervous system (CNS), peripheral nerves, neuromuscular junction, or muscle.1,2 In our patient, acute cerebellar ataxia predominated, with mild cognitive impairment and opsoclonus–myoclonus. This constellation suggests a complex overlap of paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration (PCD), paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis, and paraneoplastic opsoclonus–myoclonus.1,2 Coexistence of multiple paraneoplastic syndromes has been reported.3

When acute cerebellar ataxia is the initial presentation, sudden-onset PNS may be misdiagnosed as cerebral infarction.4 The differential diagnosis of acute cerebellar ataxia is broad (Table 1); similar considerations apply when limbic encephalitis or opsoclonus–myoclonus accompanies the presentation.4 In patients with pleomorphic neurologic manifestations, PNS should be considered, but they are largely diagnoses of exclusion because no symptom is pathognomonic. When a malignancy is known or suspected, PNS refers to neurologic dysfunction unexplained by direct tumour involvement or other cancer-related causes (coagulopathy, treatment-related neurotoxicity, metabolic derangements, or infection).1,2 Accordingly, a stepwise approach that first excludes common and urgent causes is essential. Even after an appropriate workup, the underlying tumour may remain occult for months or years, underscoring the need for continued surveillance.1,4

Onconeural antibodies are implicated in PNS, with anti-neuronal nuclear antibody type 1 (anti-Hu) and Purkinje cell antibody type 1 (anti-Yo) among the most prevalent.1,2 SOX1 is an intracellular antigen expressed in neural cells, particularly cerebellar Bergmann glia.3 Autopsy studies suggest that neurologic injury in anti-SOX1-associated disease is primarily mediated by cytotoxic T cells.5 Clinical phenotypes also vary by antibody specificity. 1 Anti-SOX1 is most often detected in SCLC.3 Among patients with cancer and anti-SOX1 positivity, Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome was most frequent (30%, 33/116), followed by PCD, paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis, and neuropathy.3

The clinical utility of serial anti-SOX1 antibody monitoring is uncertain, and testing is used mainly for diagnosis. Rarely, anti-SOX1 antibodies become undetectable after tumour resection, paralleling PNS symptom improvement.6 Although we did not repeat antibody testing, future serial measurements may help monitor treatment response and oncologic stability.

Cancer-associated retinopathy causes acute or gradual visual impairment due to an autoimmune response in which circulating antibodies target retinal proteins, similar to PNS pathophysiology. 7 In our patient, anti-SOX1-associated cellular immunity may have contributed to retinal injury, potentially mediated by cytotoxic T cells. However, we confirmed only that anti-recoverin antibodies were negative; other reported antiretinal antibodies (e.g., α-enolase, neurofilament proteins, and heat shock protein 70), which we did not assess, may have been involved.7 Cancer-associated retinopathy is most frequently associated with SCLC, followed by breast cancer, gastrointestinal cancers, and hematologic malignancies.7 Its clinical presentation is characterized by symptoms similar to those of retinitis, such as photosensitivity, and ring scotomas.7 Visual symptoms typically precede the detection of these malignancies.7

Treatment involves immunotherapy alongside tumour management; however, outcomes often result in stabilization or slight symptom amelioration.3,8 Prognosis is particularly poor in syndromes affecting the CNS, especially when multiple neurologic systems are involved.3,8 Previous studies suggest that greater neurologic severity at treatment initiation predicts poorer outcomes, with patients with an mRS 3 to 4 typically showing minimal improvement or stabilization.8,9 Our case involved a patient with severe and diverse CNS symptoms who had declined to an mRS score of 4 before treatment initiation.

Nevertheless, the patient achieved near-complete neurologic recovery after treatment. Her MMSE scores also improved. Urgent intervention after symptom onset may have been crucial in this outcome. Previous reports suggest that earlier initiation of immunotherapy in PCD may be associated with better outcomes, whereas delays may be linked to poorer treatment response.8 Notably, in PNS affecting the CNS, brain MRI findings have been reported to show the onset of atrophy in the affected regions once the disease duration exceeds approximately 3 months.8,9 Furthermore, prolonged disease duration can lead to brain atrophy in the affected regions, resulting in irreversible changes.3,8 Our patient received treatment approximately 3 weeks after symptom onset, and no degeneration was evident.

Early immunotherapy may help limit neurologic injury in severe cases even before a definitive diagnosis. High-quality evidence supporting routine IVIG or high-dose methylprednisolone before anticancer therapy is lacking. However, small case series and reports suggest that initiating immunotherapy as early as possible may improve neurologic outcomes. 1,8 In our patient, improvement closely followed early immunotherapy, which supported a role for prompt immune modulation and an immune-mediated pathogenesis related to tumour antigens.1

For CAR, early intervention is also considered crucial, and treatment is likely effective for similar reasons as in PNS.7 Although no standardized therapy exists, small case series and reports describe variable benefit from systemic corticosteroids (including high-dose methylprednisolone), IVIG, and other immunosuppressants such as azathioprine and cyclosporine.7 In this case, the patient’s visual recovery was likely possible because retinal structure and photoreceptor function remained largely preserved despite the disease. Early imaging and electrophysiologic testing suggested that the damage was not irreversible, which served as a strong predictor for a positive prognosis.10

This case highlights the importance of diagnosing PNS in patients with rapidly progressive or atypical neurologic symptoms, particularly when malignancy is suspected based on clinical background or when tumour screening identifies a neoplasm. Early immunosuppressive therapy may help prevent irreversible neurologic injury, even before a definitive diagnosis.
