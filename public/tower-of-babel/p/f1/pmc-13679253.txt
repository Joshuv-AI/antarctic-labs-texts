ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate prediction of melting points for pure molecules remains a significant challenge in predictive chemistry, with implications across various scientific fields, including materials science, drug discovery, and separations chemistry. Traditional methods, such as group contribution (GC) techniques, have shown limited success due to the complex relationship between molecular structure and melting point. In this study, we present a data-driven machine learning (ML) approach to predict the melting points of organic compounds, leveraging both 2D and 3D molecular descriptors. Our results indicate that ML models can significantly improve melting-point predictions, providing a robust tool for the scientific community. Scientific contribution Our detailed analysis on melting point prediction, along with SHAP explainability, reveals the top influencing features for the prediction. The P2MAT application we developed as part of this study can predict both melting and boiling points from a SMILES string. P2MAT is available as an easy-to-install, user-friendly GUI for maximum outreach to the scientific community. Introduction

An important and long-standing goal in predictive chemistry is the development of an accurate melting point model that is generally applicable to a wide range of molecules. Many decades of work have been invested in this endeavor, but thus far, none are sufficiently accurate to make useful and confident predictions for compounds without a measured melting point. Indeed, an improved understanding of melting point would be useful across large areas of science, including, but not limited to, materials for energy applications [1–3], drug discovery [4, 5], organic hydrogen getters [6, 7], ionic liquids [8, 9], and separations chemistry [10, 11] as well as the prediction of properties such as viscosity [12]. Accurate predictions of melting points are difficult because large differences in melting points can arise from small changes to molecular structure. Existing melting point models fall broadly into two categories: those that leverage group contribution (GC) theory and those that use machine learning (ML) techniques. Krzyzaniak et al. [13] used the solid–liquid phase transition equation, Tm=ΔHmΔSm, to develop a melting point model. The enthalpy of melting (ΔHm) was estimated using GC methods, and the entropy of melting (ΔSm) was estimated using an equation that depends on the rotational symmetry number and the molecular flexibility number of the target molecule. Using a database of 596 melting points containing only aliphatic non-hydrogen-bonding molecules, their method was found to have a root mean square error (RMSE) of 34.3∘C. Subsequent papers from this group have suggested that the largest source of error comes from the estimate of ΔSm. The model was later expanded to include aromatic and aliphatic rings using a modified expression for the molecular flexibility number, yielding a comparable average absolute error (AAE) of 33.2∘C [14]. Estimating ΔSm is challenging and limits the utility of methods that make use of the phase transition equation. Marrero and Gani [15] developed a different GC method that accounts for the chemical environment around a chemical group via the use of second-order and third-order group contributions. Second and third-order group contributions are corrections to the original, first-order contribution. Special emphasis was given to improving the prediction of cyclic groups (both aliphatic and aromatic). When both second and third-order groups were used, the AAE was reduced to 20.22∘C (down from 24.9∘C when only first-order corrections were used). ML methods that incorporate molecular descriptors can serve as an alternative to GC-based methods. For example [16], used a series of ML methods incorporating 2D and 3D features. 2D features are chemical descriptors that encode topological information, whereas 3D features encode 3-dimensional shape and functionality information. Several approaches were used, in which different sets of databases, different ML methods, and different sets of features were used. These approaches achieved an MAE of 25–29∘C. [17] used the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center (CCDC) database, which contains 58, 810 unique crystal structures with associated melting points (Zhu 2023). Some of these crystal structures are duplicates, but are considered unique by the authors because they are different enough. The authors argue that because their goal is to generate 3D descriptors from the crystal structures, these duplicates are worth keeping in the dataset. The authors went on to train their melting point model by using Deep learning [18], XGBoost [19], and Random forest methods [20], using 2D, 3D, and 2D + 3D descriptors. The best-performing model was trained using XGBoost with only 2D descriptors. This model achieved an MAE of 28.1∘C.

Here, we report the development of several melting point ML models trained using a database of 19, 811 data points [21]. The models differ based on the ML technique (linear regression [22], deep learning, etc.), the use of 2D structural Padel features vs. 2D + 3D structural Padel features, and the degree of feature engineering used. Errors are quantified using 5-fold validation. Explainability is incorporated into the best-performing model via the SHAP algorithm [23], and a discussion of the accuracy of the best-performing melting point model on subsets of chemically relevant molecules (molecules containing only C, H, and O, for example) is given.

Graphical user interface

The application window (GUI Layer), shown in Fig.1, is built using PyQt5 [25]. To keep the Qt event loop responsive during predictions, all blocking tasks are handled in a QThread subclass called PredictionWorker. This worker communicates with the main thread using four specific Qt signals: progress(int), result(object), invalid_smiles(list), and error(str). When the application starts, it loads the visible properties and the enabled state of each checkbox from a single config.json file. This setup allows users to change properties without needing to recompile or repackage the application.

Molecular descriptor computation

Molecular features are computed using PaDEL-Descriptor [26], a Java-based cheminformatics tool that generates both 2D and 3D descriptors from SMILES strings. The padelpy Python wrapper calls PaDEL through the system’s Java runtime, producing up to about 1,240 numerical descriptors per molecule. To process large query sets efficiently, SMILES strings are submitted to PaDEL in batches of 128 molecules by default. A divide-and-conquer fallback strategy ensures that if a batch call fails, the batch is split in half until individual molecules are processed. This way, one problematic structure does not stop the analysis of the remaining molecules. The temporary .smi input and .csv output files created during each PaDEL call are stored in the operating system’s temporary directory and are deleted right after they are read, whether the call succeeds or fails.

SMILES validation

Input SMILES strings are checked for structure using RDKit [27] before calculating any descriptors. Specifically, Chem.MolFromSmiles processes each candidate string. A return value of None or any exception indicates an invalid structure. Invalid SMILES strings are sent back to the caller and shown to the user without interrupting predictions for the other molecules.

Path resolution and packaging

Resource paths for models, feature lists, CSS, and images are managed by the helper.resource_path utility. This tool checks during runtime whether the application is running as a standard Python script or as a PyInstaller-frozen binary (sys._MEIPASS). This method provides consistent and dependable access to assets in both deployment modes.

Method

The modeling pipeline (Fig. 2) was created as a clear and fully reproducible framework. It included preprocessing methods, feature selection techniques, and machine learning algorithms across three chemically defined datasets: CHO, CHON, and FULL. Each dataset went through three input transformation methods: no transformation, StandardScaler, and Quantile Transformer. Four feature selection approaches were applied: Whole (no feature removal), Boruta [28], Pearson correlation [29] filtering, and principal component analysis (PCA) [30]. The resulting feature sets trained six regression models: Ridge [31], ElasticNet [32], Extra Trees [33], LightGBM [34], XGBoost [19], and a deep neural network (DNN) [35], resulting in a total of 216 distinct model configurations. For each configuration, we optimized hyperparameters independently using Bayesian search [36] to ensure fair comparison. The best-performing base learners were then combined into a two-level stacking ensemble [37]. Out-of-fold predictions served as meta-features for a Ridge regression meta-learner. This approach created a final model with lower generalization error than any individual model. We also conducted an applicability domain analysis using the Williams plot [38]. This analysis identified response outliers and high-leverage compounds, assessed the structural coverage of the training set, and evaluated the reliability of predictions on the test set. Finally, to understand the predictions of the top-performing tree-based models and identify the molecular descriptors that mainly drive changes in property prediction, we calculated Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) [23] using the TreeExplainer algorithm. This analysis provided precise feature attributions for each compound while meeting the key game-theoretic principles of efficiency, symmetry, and additivity.

Data transformation

Standard Scaler Transformation

In this pipeline, StandardScaler was employed as one of three input transformation methods, together with no transformation and QuantileTransformer. To prevent data leakage, it was fitted exclusively to the training set. This approach centers each molecular descriptor to a mean of zero and scales it to unit variance. Consequently, models that are sensitive to feature gradients, such as Ridge, Elastic Net, and deep neural networks (DNNs), are less likely to be disproportionately affected by descriptors with large numerical ranges.

Quantile Transformer

The Quantile Transformer was adopted as an alternative to the Standard Scaler to better accommodate the skewed, heavy-tailed distributions frequently present in computed molecular descriptors, including topological indices and connectivity counts. Instead of scaling features to unit variance, this method transforms each feature’s marginal distribution into a uniform or approximately normal distribution. Consequently, it offers increased robustness to outliers, which might otherwise distort variance estimates and negatively influence the performance of distance-based or gradient-based machine learning algorithms.

Feature selection

Boruta

Boruta feature selection approach [28] was designed to identify all features that provide meaningful information about the target variable, rather than stopping once a minimal sufficient subset had been found. In the current pipeline, XGBoost was used as the base estimator, configured with 100 estimators and tree_method=“hist” on the CPU. The algorithm was allowed to run for up to 100 iterations. In each iteration, shadow versions of all features were generated by permuting their values, and the model was trained on the combined set of original and shadow features. A feature was considered confirmed if its importance exceeded the maximum importance of the shadow features at the 100th percentile, which served as a stringent selection criterion. To reduce unnecessary computation, early stopping was applied after 20 consecutive rounds without change. Both confirmed and tentatively confirmed (weakly confirmed) features were retained according to the support criterion. This procedure yielded compact feature subsets ranging from 78 to 127 features, depending on the dataset (e.g., 101 for CHO and 127 for FULL). Overall, this represented approximately a 90% reduction from the original ∼1,100 to 1, 200 descriptors while preserving the most informative variables.

Correlation-coefficient

Feature selection was performed using a two-stage Pearson correlation [29] filter applied exclusively to the training set. In the first stage (termed relevance filtering), features (X) were retained only when the absolute Pearson correlation between each feature and the target variable (Y) exceeded 0.05. This step eliminated descriptors with little linear association with Y. In the second stage (referred to as redundancy reduction), the remaining features were ranked in descending order according to their correlation with the target and then pruned greedily. A feature was removed if its correlation with any previously accepted feature was greater than or equal to 0.85. This procedure reduced multicollinearity while preserving the most informative representative from each related feature cluster. The final subset was of moderate size and low redundancy, comprising approximately 306–389 features across the datasets.

PCA

Principal component analysis (PCA) [30] was used as a feature selection strategy to transform the scaled molecular descriptor space into an orthogonal, lower-dimensional representation. The method was configured to retain the smallest number of principal components (PCs) required to explain at least 90% of the total variance in the training set, while also enforcing a minimum of 10 components to prevent cases in which only a few PCs captured nearly all of the variance. As a result, between 84 and 113 PCs were retained, depending on the dataset, representing a substantial reduction from the original 1,100–1,200 descriptors. Although PCA reduces collinearity and noise by discarding low-variance directions, it also decreases interpretability because each component represents a combination of the original descriptors. More importantly, because PCA is an unsupervised method that maximizes variance rather than predictive relevance, it can weaken the relationship between features and target variables. This limitation was reflected in the observed model performance.

Data split

To minimize structural overlap between training and test sets and reduce the risk of information leakage in model evaluation, a chemistry-aware cluster-based split was employed in place of a simple random partition. Morgan circular fingerprints [39, 40] (radius 2,2048 bits) were computed for all valid compounds using RDKit’s MorganGenerator. Pairwise Tanimoto distances [41, 42] were then computed in parallel across all compound pairs, and the Butina algorithm [43] was applied to cluster compounds at a similarity threshold of 0.8 (distance cutoff 0.2). The resulting clusters were randomly shuffled and greedily assigned to the test set until approximately 20% of the dataset was reached, ensuring that structurally similar compounds always reside in the same partition. The quality of the split was assessed by computing three Tanimoto similarity distributions: pairwise within-train, pairwise within-test, and the maximum cross-set similarity of each test compound to its nearest training neighbor. A low mean cross-set similarity and a small fraction of test compounds with nearest-neighbor similarity ≥0.8 confirm that the cluster-based partition effectively separates the chemical spaces of the training and test sets. Machine learning models

Ridge regression

Ridge regression [31] is a regularized extension of ordinary least squares (OLS). It is designed to reduce multicollinearity and limit overfitting by adding an L2 penalty to the loss function. It aims to minimize the residual sum of squares while enforcing a constraint on the squared Euclidean norm of the coefficient vector:

where α≥0 is the regularization hyperparameter. The closed-form solution, w^=(X⊤X+αI)-1X⊤y, always exists. This holds true even when X⊤X is singular, which improves numerical stability in high-dimensional descriptor spaces. As α increases, the coefficient magnitudes shrink toward zero. This reduces model variance, but it also increases bias. Unlike Lasso, Ridge keeps all features and applies the penalty evenly across correlated predictors instead of selecting one variable arbitrarily. In this study, Ridge Regression is the linear baseline for comparing the nonlinear models. We optimized the regularization strength, α, using Bayesian hyperparameter optimization over the range [10-4,103] with a log-uniform prior. This approach reflects the wide dynamic range usually seen for this parameter in high-dimensional QSAR settings.

ElasticNet

Elastic Net regression [32] combines the L1 penalty from Lasso and the L2 penalty from Ridge regression within a single optimization framework. This allows for variable selection and coefficient shrinkage to happen at the same time.

where α controls the overall regularization strength and ρ∈[0,1] (the l1_ratio) determines the balance between the two penalties. When ρ=1, Elastic Net becomes Lasso. When ρ=0, it becomes Ridge. The L1 component encourages sparsity by shrinking irrelevant coefficients to exactly zero, which provides implicit feature selection. The L2 component stabilizes the solution when there are groups of highly correlated descriptors. This is common in molecular fingerprint spaces, where similar fragments create correlated binary bits. Elastic Net was chosen over Support Vector Regression (SVR) [44] because it has a much lower computational cost on large datasets, training in seconds instead of minutes. It also fits well with high-dimensional, sparse descriptor matrices. Both α and ρ were optimized together using Bayesian search.

Extra trees regressor

The Extra Trees (Extremely Randomized Trees) [33] algorithm is an ensemble learning method that builds a large number of unpruned decision trees and averages their predictions. It expands on the Random Forest framework [20] by adding another layer of randomness. In Random Forest, the optimal split threshold for each candidate feature is chosen. In contrast, Extra Trees randomly selects thresholds from the range of each feature. Specifically, at each node, K features are picked at random, and for each feature (fk), a threshold (tk) is sampled uniformly from ([min(fk),max(fk)]). The split ((f^,t^)) that maximizes variance reduction is selected from this random set. This dual randomization of both features and thresholds further reduces the correlation among trees compared to Random Forest. It reduces ensemble variance while only slightly increasing bias. In practice, Extra Trees usually trains two to four times faster than Random Forest, as it avoids the computationally heavy threshold search during node splitting. In high-dimensional molecular descriptor datasets, this random splitting method acts as an effective form of implicit regularization. It helps prevent individual trees from overfitting to correlated fingerprint features. The main hyperparameters, such as the number of estimators, maximum tree depth, minimum samples needed for splitting and for leaf nodes, and the proportion of features considered at each split, were optimized using Bayesian search.

LightGBM

LightGBM [34] is a gradient boosting framework created by Microsoft. It delivers top performance on tabular data through two main innovations compared to traditional gradient boosting machines (GBMs) [45]. The first innovation is Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS). It keeps all instances with large gradients or high errors, while randomly selecting instances with small gradients. This approach reduces data volume while maintaining the most important training signals. The second innovation is Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB). This method finds sparse features that rarely take on nonzero values at the same time and merges them into a single bundle, which decreases the effective number of features.

Tree construction uses a histogram-based method. This bin’s continuous feature values into discrete buckets. As a result, the computational cost of finding splits drops from the expensive O(nlogn) needed by traditional GBMs to O(B), where B is the number of bins. Importantly, LightGBM uses a leaf-wise growth strategy. At each step, it splits the leaf that offers the greatest reduction in loss, no matter the tree’s depth. This approach usually leads to deeper, uneven trees that achieve lower training errors with fewer trees compared to the level-wise growth used by XGBoost and scikit-learn’s GBM. However, it needs careful regularization with parameters such as min_child_samples, num_leaves, and max_depth to avoid overfitting.

The model trains using the gradient and Hessian of the loss function, which is Mean Absolute Error in this study. It also supports parallel split finding across CPU cores through OpenMP.

XGBoost

XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) [19] is a scalable, regularized gradient-boosting implementation that builds on the classical GBM framework by adding L1 and L2 regularization terms directly into the tree-building goal. At iteration t, the regularized objective function is defined as:

XGBoost finds the optimal split by using a second-order Taylor expansion of the loss function, which includes both the gradient gi and the Hessian hi. In this study, we use the histogram-based tree method. This method employs the same histogram binning strategy as LightGBM and improves the efficiency of finding splits in large datasets. Unlike LightGBM’s leaf-wise growth approach, XGBoost uses level-wise tree growth. It builds each tree to a fixed depth before going further, which usually leads to more balanced trees. This more cautious exploration of the hypothesis space may help with generalization on smaller datasets.

Deep neural network

The deep neural network (DNN) [35] used in this study is a fully connected feed-forward regression model. It is implemented in PyTorch and wrapped in a scikit-learn compatible interface to help integrate with the Bayesian hyperparameter optimization pipeline. The network architecture (shown in Fig. 4) includes nlayers repeated blocks. Each block consists of a linear projection, batch normalization, a nonlinear activation function, and dropout regularization. This is followed by a single linear output neuron.

Deep Neural Network architecture diagram. The network consists of nlayers fully-connected blocks, each containing a linear layer, Batch Normalization, a non-linear activation function, and dropout regularisation, followed by a single linear output neuron for regression

Batch normalization is applied to each pre-activation in the mini-batch to reduce internal covariate shift and allow for higher learning rates. The activation function, (σ), is chosen through hyperparameter optimization from (ReLU), (Leaky ReLU), and (tanh). The network is trained using the Huber loss, which acts quadratically for small residuals and linearly for large residuals. This improves robustness to outliers caused by polymorphism or measurement inconsistencies in the dataset. Optimization is performed with Adam, using a learning rate sampled from [10-4,10-2] under a log-uniform prior. Learning rate annealing is carried out with ReduceLROnPlateau. Early stopping with a set patience helps prevent overfitting. To take advantage of Apple hardware, the model is assigned to the Metal Performance Shaders (MPS) backend when available. If MPS is not available, it falls back to CUDA and then to the CPU. To make Bayesian hyperparameter optimization more manageable, a two-stage training strategy is used. First, candidate configurations are evaluated with a lower budget of 50 epochs and a patience of 10 during the search phase. Then, the best-performing configuration is retrained from scratch using the full budget of 200 epochs and a patience of 20.

Stacking ensemble

The stacking ensemble (shown in Fig. 5) is a two-level meta-learning architecture designed to combine the strengths of LightGBM, XGBoost, Extra Trees, and a deep neural network (DNN) into a single model that has a lower generalization error than any individual component. In the first level, each base learner is trained separately using its own tuned hyperparameters, feature selection method, and data transformation strategy. This results in four unique predictors with different error profiles. To give the meta-learner an unbiased training signal and avoid data leakage, out-of-fold (OOF) predictions are created through five-fold cross-validation. Each base model is re-instantiated with its best saved hyperparameters and trained on four folds of the training data. It then generates predictions for the remaining held-out fold. This method results in a prediction matrix Z∈Rntrain×4, where each column zk=[y^1(k),…,y^n(k)]⊤ holds the OOF predictions from base model k. In the second level, a Ridge regression meta-learner is trained on Z.

Stacking ensemble architecture diagram. The ensemble consists of four base learners (LightGBM, XGBoost, Extra Trees, DNN) that generate out-of-fold predictions on the training set to train a Ridge regression meta-learner. At inference time, the base learners produce predictions on the test set, which the meta-learner combines to produce the final output

L2 regularization in the ridge meta-learner prevents the model weights from leaning toward a single base learner. This helps use complementary information from all four contributors. During inference, the four fully trained base models make predictions for the test set. These predictions are then arranged into a matrix, Ztest∈Rntest×4, and passed through the fitted meta-learner to get the final prediction. To avoid unnecessary computation when adding new base models or retuning the meta-learner, the out-of-fold and test predictions for each base model are saved to disk as NumPy arrays and reloaded in future runs. Only predictions from new models are recalculated. The stacking framework does not depend on the feature spaces used by the individual base models. Each model applies its own fitted scaler and feature selector internally. This makes the approach strong when different feature-selection methods create descriptor subsets of varying sizes.

Outlier detection

Outlier detection used the Williams plot method, a common applicability domain analysis technique recommended by the OECD [46] guidelines for QSAR model validation [38]. In this study, the Williams plot was created in the meta-feature space of the stacking ensemble. This four-dimensional space is defined by the out-of-fold predictions from the base models, which include LightGBM, XGBoost, Extra Trees, and DNN. It was not built in the raw descriptor space.

For each compound, two values were calculated: leverage (h) and standardized residuals. Leverage measures how much a compound impacts the centroid of the training set. It was determined using the formula hi=xiT(XTX)-1xi, where X is the OOF meta-feature matrix. The leverage threshold, h∗=3(k+1)n, sets the boundary for the applicability domain, where k is the number of base models, and n is the training set size.

Standardized residuals were calculated by scaling the prediction errors of both training and test compounds with the standard deviation of the training residuals. This step ensures a uniform scale across data splits. Three types of outliers were identified: (i) response outliers, for which |standardized residual|>3, indicating possible measurement errors or incorrect SMILES; (ii) leverage outliers, for which h>h∗, representing compounds outside the structural applicability domain; and (iii) bad leverage points, which meet both criteria, indicating the most questionable compounds.

Model explainability

Model explainability was evaluated using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [23], a framework for understanding machine learning model predictions. Based on cooperative game theory, SHAP values quantify how much each feature contributes to an individual prediction. In this study, we calculated SHAP values for the top-performing base learners, LightGBM and XGBoost, as well as for the Ridge meta-learner in the stacking ensemble. We used TreeExplainer for the tree-based models and LinearExplainer for the Ridge model. We determined global feature importance by averaging the absolute SHAP values across the test set. The SHAP beeswarm plot provided a comprehensive summary of the ensemble model’s behavior. In this visualization, each row represents a feature, ranked by mean absolute SHAP value from most to least important. Each dot corresponds to a single compound in the test set. The horizontal position of each dot shows the SHAP value: points to the right of zero indicate that the feature increased the predicted values, while points to the left indicate a decrease. Dot color indicates the raw feature value, with red for high values and blue for low values. This reveals whether a feature has a direct effect or a varying impact across compounds. This analysis identified the molecular descriptors that most significantly influenced prediction and showed how their effects differed among compounds.

Uncertainty quantification

Two complementary measures of uncertainty were calculated for the stacking ensemble on each test set. First, we measured disagreement among the base models by computing the standard deviation of predictions from the four base learners (LightGBM, XGBoost, ExtraTrees, and DNN) for each test compound [47]. This metric acts as a per-sample indicator of uncertainty since compounds with greater disagreement between the base models are expected to have larger absolute prediction errors. We checked this relationship by calculating the Spearman rank correlation (ρ) [48] between the standard deviation of the base models and the absolute test error.

Second, we generated split-conformal prediction intervals using the out-of-fold (OOF) training residuals as the calibration set [49, 50]. The nonconformity score for each training compound was defined as si=|yi-y^iOOF|. We calculated the conformal quantile at a desired coverage level α as qα=quantile(s,⌈(n+1)α⌉/n), where n is the number of training compounds. We defined the prediction interval for a test compound as [y^-qα,y^+qα]. This method offers a distribution-free coverage guarantee: if the calibration set is properly specified, the proportion of test compounds whose true melting points fall within the interval approaches α as n increases. We evaluated empirical coverage at nominal levels of 50%,60%,70%,80%,90%,95%, and 99% to check interval calibration across the entire confidence spectrum.

Description of physics-based methods of boiling point

A variety of methods are available for estimating boiling points, each with its own set of pros and cons. Coefficient-based methods, such as DIPPR-based methods, rely on experimental data to regress and determine a set of coefficients that can then be used to calculate vapor pressure via a governing equation (“Design Institute for Physical Properties" 1996). Those coefficients can then be used to determine the temperature at which the vapor pressure equals 1 Atm, yielding the boiling point. When available, coefficient-based methods provide high-quality estimates of the boiling point. However, not all molecules have associated coefficients, and if experimental data is not available, those coefficients cannot be determined. Group contribution methods offer an alternative to coefficient-based methods. Group contribution methods assign a value to each chemical group. These values are obtained by regression from a wide range of boiling-point data. By applying these values to a working equation, boiling-point estimates within a few degrees of experimental measurements are often possible. The Joback method is a popular method for estimating boiling points. Based on a set of 438 molecules, the Joback method has an average absolute error of 12.9 °C [51]. Brown and Stein improve on the Joback method by adding a correction term [52]. Based on a dataset of 6, 584 boiling points, the Brown-Stein method has an absolute average error of 20.4 °C. Group-contribution methods are easy to implement and can provide reasonable boiling-point estimates. They work best for molecules similar to those found in the database used in regression. Group contribution methods do not have values for every possible group and cannot be applied to molecules that lack a represented chemical group. If the desired group is unavailable, other methods must be used. More rigorous, physics-based methods, such as the Equation of State (EoS), have been proven to provide very accurate vapor pressures while often running on just a laptop’s resources. Examples here include the Saft-gamme Mie, SAFT-VR, Peng Robinson, and Cubic Plus Association (CPA) [53]. Like coefficient-based methods, EoS methods require experimental data to estimate parameters. However, this is not an impediment, as experimental data using a variety of different physical properties are usually available.

Results and discussion

This section provides a detailed overview of the QSAR melting-point prediction pipeline (considering 2D features) across three chemical-space datasets: CHO, CHON, and Full. Similar analyses of the melting point (considering 3D features) and the boiling point are presented in the supplementary document.

Dataset

Three separate datasets were created from the curated experimental melting-point database to evaluate model performance across chemical spaces with increasing diversity. The CHO subset includes compounds made only of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. It represents aliphatic and aromatic oxygenates, such as alcohols, ethers, aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids. The CHON subset builds on the CHO set by adding nitrogen-containing compounds, which include amines, amides, imines, nitro groups, and heterocyclic structures. The Full dataset includes all compounds, regardless of their elemental makeup. This dataset contains halogenated, sulfur, phosphorus, and metal-containing molecules, along with CHO and CHON compounds.

Data partitioning

The complete dataset was divided into 15, 428 training compounds and 3, 860 test compounds using Butina clustering with a Tanimoto similarity threshold of 0.7. In the training set, the mean pairwise Tanimoto similarity was 0.142, and the median was 0.107. In the test set, the corresponding values were 0.139 and 0.105. Across both sets, the mean maximum similarity between each test compound and its nearest training neighbor was 0.121, with a median of 0.094 and a maximum of 0.698. No test compound showed a nearest-neighbor similarity that met or exceeded the 0.7 threshold. These results (shown in Table 3) indicate that the cluster-based split effectively reduced structural overlap between the training and test sets, which helps prevent information leakage during model evaluation.

Across all three datasets, several consistent patterns appear. Gradient-boosted tree ensembles, specifically LightGBM (LGBM) and XGBoost, have the lowest mean absolute errors (MAEs) among the individual models in every instance. Although ExtraTrees does not match the performance of LGBM or XGBoost, it consistently outperforms all linear regressors. The deep neural network (DNN) shows the most variability across feature-selection strategies. It performs poorly when trained on the full raw feature set, with MAE values as high as 43.57 K on the Full dataset. However, it becomes competitive with ExtraTrees when using Boruta-selected features, achieving 29.41 K on Full and 30.70 K on CHO. This pattern likely reflects the DNN’s need for a more compact and less collinear input space. Lastly, PCA-based feature selection consistently yields the highest MAE across all models. This indicates that the principal components explaining the greatest variance in the descriptor matrix are not the most informative for predicting the target property.

Explainability of stacking ensemble model

Figure 6E shows the importance of each feature and how they influence the predicted melting point based on SHAP analysis. The results reveal patterns that make sense both chemically and physically. In each plot, features are listed from top to bottom according to their mean absolute SHAP values, and each dot represents an individual compound. The horizontal position of a dot reflects its SHAP contribution to the predicted melting point. Positive values increase the prediction, while negative values decrease it. The color of the dot indicates the raw feature value, with red signifying high values and blue indicating low values.

In the CHO dataset, the top features, especially the molecular distance edge descriptor MDEO-11 [54] and the average simple path index ASP-1 [55], shows the key role of global molecular structure and specific atomic connections in determining activity. Next in importance, van der Waals volume-weighted spatial autocorrelation descriptors like ATSC1v and AATS2v suggest that the arrangement of steric bulk significantly affects target interactions [55]. The model’s predictive ability is also strongly influenced by thermodynamic solvation parameters from Abraham’s Molecular Linear Free Energy Relationship descriptors, including MLFER_E, MLFER_A, and MLFER_S, which capture polarizability and hydrogen-bond acidity [56]. Additionally, local electronic environments, as indicated by electrotopological state indices such as SHBint2 and SsOH [57], along with molecular flexibility, as characterized by the fraction of rotatable bonds (RotBFrac) [55], provide a clearer picture of ligand-binding potential. Overall, the SHAP profile for the CHO dataset shows that model predictions mainly rely on large-scale structural connectivity, then steric distribution and solvation thermodynamics.

In the CHON dataset (Fig. S1 (E) in the supplementary document), which includes nitrogen in the chemical space, the feature ranking shifts significantly toward descriptors associated with hydrogen bonding and electronic effects. MLFER_E moves to the top position, while maxHBint2, defined as the highest intramolecular hydrogen bond at a two-bond distance (r = +0.92), and SHBd, the total of hydrogen-bond donor E-state values [57], ranked second and third, respectively. This trend highlights the strong role of nitrogen-mediated donor-acceptor interactions in crystal cohesion. In the Full dataset (Fig. S2 (E) in the supplementary document), MLFER_S, which reflects Abraham’s dipolarity/polarizability, outshines all other features, with an average SHAP value of 11.4 K and a range from −42 to +22 K. This prominence mostly comes from the large polarity differences between ionic and zwitterionic compounds, like sulfonates and phosphates, compared to nonpolar hydrocarbons that define this broader chemical space.

Across all three datasets, MLFER_E, MLFER_S, and MLFER_A consistently rank among the most important predictors, reinforcing the theoretical importance of linear free-energy relationships in melting-point prediction [56]. In contrast, the fraction of rotatable bonds (RotBFrac [55]) is the only descriptor that consistently shows a negative SHAP direction, ranking 10th, 5th, and 6th in the CHO, CHON, and Full datasets, respectively. Increased flexibility affects the entropic penalty during crystallization, lowering the melting temperature regardless of elemental makeup. Overall, this SHAP-based profile supports the models’ physical interpretability, showing a shift from macro-structural connectivity to steric distribution and solvation thermodynamics, and finally to local electronic environments. It also offers molecular-level insight for designing compounds with specific melting points. Section 1.3 of the supplementary document describes each feature presented in the three datasets using our SHAP analysis.

To better understand the performance differences, given the overlap in training data, we measured structural similarity between our training set and each external dataset using two metrics derived from Morgan circular fingerprints (ECFP4; radius = 2, 2048 bits) implemented in RDKit [27]. For each compound in one dataset, we identified the highest Tanimoto coefficient with any compound in the other dataset using bulk similarity search. We defined the mean maximum Tanimoto similarity (MMTS) as the average of these maximum values per compound. This serves as a continuous measure of chemical space overlap that does not depend on a threshold. We defined the leakage fraction (LF) as the percentage of compounds whose nearest-neighbor Tanimoto similarity was at least 0.8, which is the near-duplicate threshold used for scaffold-based clustering [43]. Both metrics were calculated in both directions and summarized as symmetric scores: MMTS=(MMTSA→B+MMTSB→A)/2 and LF=(LFA→B+LFB→A)/2. Higher values for either metric indicate greater structural overlap, suggesting a higher likelihood of information leakage.

Overall, P2MAT surpassed the published model in six out of seven external comparisons, and the MMTS and LF values help clarify the observed results. On the dataset from McDonagh et al. [14] (MMTS = 0.623, LF = 34.05%), P2MAT lowered the reported MLR error from 33.20 to 22.20 K, marking an improvement of roughly 23%. For the large MDPI dataset from Karthikeyan et al. [58] (4, 173 compounds; MMTS = 0.685, LF = 45.13%), the MAE decreased from 32.60 to 22.20 K and the RMSE from 49.30 to 35.65 K, while R2 stayed similar at 0.68. On the drug-compound dataset from Modarresi et al. [59] (MMTS = 0.574, LF = 34.94%), the RMSE dropped from 42.00 to 32.08 K, and R2 increased from 0.66 to 0.80. When tested on the large OCHEM dataset used by both Tetko et al. [60] and Sivaraman et al. [16] (21, 840compounds; MMTS = 0.842, LF = 66.01%), P2MAT achieved an RMSE of 35.10 K, compared to Tetko’s 38.00 K, and an R2 of 0.83, compared to Sivaraman’s 0.77. The strong performance likely stems from the high overlap with our training set. P2MAT performed best on the dataset from McDonagh et al. [61] (1, 100 compounds; MMTS = 0.665, LF = 42.21%), where the RMSE improved from 41.30 to 27.43 K and R2 rose from 0.75 to 0.90. Notably, this dataset is a subset of the compounds used to train P2MAT, which suggests that the high LF reflects direct overlap with the training set rather than genuine external generalization. The only exception was the dataset from Bergström et al. [62] (277 compounds; MMTS = 0.415, LF = 10.26%), which includes structurally constrained, drug-like molecules and had the least overlap among the benchmark sets. On this out-of-distribution dataset, P2MAT produced an RMSE of 48.86 K and an R2 of 0.03, consistent with its limited structural similarity to our training data. Together, these results indicate that P2MAT generalizes well across chemically diverse external datasets, while instances of poorer performance relate closely to lower structural overlap with the training set.

Discussion

The stacking ensembles achieved test MAEs of 25.3–28.7 K and R2 values of 0.70–0.83. This performance puts them within the 2–35 K range reported for similar QSAR and deep-learning models predicting melting points in the literature [58, 62, 63]. Across all 12 dataset and feature-selection combinations, the stack consistently improved MAE by 2–4% compared to the best individual base model. This confirms that the Ridge meta-learner effectively uses the complementary strengths of the four base learners. The significantly lower R2 for CHON (0.701) compared to CHO (0.829) indicates that nitrogen-containing compounds introduce extra structural variability. This variability includes intramolecular hydrogen bonding, tautomerism, and resonance delocalization in heterocycles that fixed 2D PaDEL descriptors cannot fully capture. Incorporating graph neural network embeddings that respond to electronic structure represents a logical way to improve further.

Together, the SHAP and applicability-domain analyses create a physically sound and practically useful uncertainty framework. SHAP rankings show that the Abraham LFER descriptors (MLFER_E, MLFER_S, and MLFER_A) are the most influential predictors across all three datasets. This supports the theoretical basis of linear free-energy relationships for melting-point prediction. In contrast, RotBFrac is the most consistent negative predictor. This indicates that molecular flexibility generally lowers the melting point by increasing the crystallization entropy penalty, regardless of elemental composition. For deployment, the Williams-plot applicability-domain boundary (hi>h∗) and conformal prediction intervals provide two complementary levels of uncertainty communication. In-domain predictions are linked to a well-calibrated 90% interval of ±53 to 60 K and have an expected MAE of 23–27 K. However, out-of-domain queries (3.9% of test compounds in each dataset) must be interpreted carefully because structural extrapolation introduces an additional systematic error of about 2–3 K.
