Forgotten tricuspid valve? In cases of severe regurgitation, I prefer early surgery
Authors: Nicola Buzzatti, Francesco Maisano, Ottavio Alfieri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfter decades of neglect, awareness of the clinical importance of tricuspid regurgitation has recently increased, partly due to the expansion of transcatheter therapies. Historically, tricuspid valve surgery has been associated with high operative mortality, primarily because patients were referred late, often presenting with right ventricular dysfunction and end-organ damage. However, more recent reports demonstrate excellent surgical outcomes when procedures are performed in appropriately selected patients without right ventricular impairment or organ dysfunction. In the setting of severe tricuspid regurgitation, early referral for correction of the valvular defect—before the onset of right ventricular dysfunction—is essential to achieve low operative mortality and favourable long-term prognosis.Keywords: Tricuspid regurgitation, Tricuspid valve surgery, Tricuspid valve replacement, Tricuspid valve repair

Introduction

The description of the tricuspid valve as the ‘forgotten’ cardiac valve is no longer appropriate, as numerous scientific studies published in recent years have clarified the natural history of tricuspid valve disease, the indications for repair or replacement, and the associated risks and expected outcomes. Moreover, the new European guidelines provide reasonably detailed recommendations on the subject, although several grey areas remain.1

Within this evolving framework, there is now sufficient evidence to support a treatment pathway that reasonably incorporates surgical intervention at an early stage of the disease.

Natural history

Over the past two decades, multiple lines of evidence have demonstrated that tricuspid regurgitation independently exerts a negative impact on patient survival, with increasing severity correlating with poorer outcomes. In patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation managed medically, 5-year survival falls below 50%, and may be as low as 14% at 10 years.2,3

Severe tricuspid regurgitation is initially well tolerated from a haemodynamic standpoint in most cases. In more advanced stages, however, persistent volume overload leads to right ventricular remodelling. Ventricular dilatation is followed by dysfunction, marking a turning point at which patient management becomes significantly more challenging. Symptoms frequently persist despite diuretic therapy, and patients develop venous congestion, reduced forward flow, and consequent end-organ damage.

Right ventricular function plays a pivotal role in tricuspid regurgitation, as it ultimately determines symptom burden, operative risk, and long-term prognosis.4

A growing disease burden

The prevalence of tricuspid regurgitation increases with age. Moderate-or-greater tricuspid regurgitation is observed in approximately 4% of individuals older than 75 years. Despite this substantial prevalence, tricuspid regurgitation has long remained undertreated, owing to its seemingly benign early course and historically disappointing surgical outcomes.3

Greater awareness of the natural history of the disease, however, has recently drawn renewed attention to the importance of timely treatment.

During the past decade, transcatheter procedures have also been extended to the tricuspid valve. The advent of transcatheter technologies has introduced the possibility of treating patients who would otherwise remain untreated, offering lower procedural risk and substantially increasing interest in tricuspid pathology.

A recent analysis examined changes in the management of tricuspid regurgitation over the past 12 years, comparing the period before the introduction of transcatheter therapies into routine clinical practice with the period thereafter.5

A significant increase in the number of patients referred for treatment was observed (+122%), with absolute increases in all groups (surgery, transcatheter therapy, and medical therapy). Notably, the proportion of patients undergoing corrective intervention (surgical or transcatheter) increased significantly (from 72% to 81%, P < 0.001), while the proportion managed medically decreased (from 28% to 19%, P = 0.046) (Figure 1). Thus, not only were more patients referred overall, but a higher proportion were ultimately treated compared with the past.

These trends are clearly linked to the availability of less invasive transcatheter options capable of treating high-risk or inoperable patients who would otherwise remain untreated, as well as to increased disease awareness within the cardiology community.

Given the evolving landscape of therapeutic options for tricuspid regurgitation, it has become essential to determine when patients should be directed towards conventional surgery and when transcatheter therapy is the more appropriate strategy.

Surgical risk: past and present

Historically, mortality following tricuspid valve surgery was high, averaging approximately 8–10%.6 In these cohorts, patients typically presented for surgery with severe symptoms, right ventricular dysfunction, hepatorenal impairment, and ascites.

Many factors associated with advanced stages of disease have long been recognized as predictors of increased postoperative mortality. However, only in recent years has a specific risk model been developed capable of accurately predicting mortality following tricuspid valve surgery: the Triscore.7 (Table 1). The Triscore has proven reliable in estimating in-hospital mortality among patients undergoing tricuspid procedures, enabling stratification according to individual risk profiles: ≤5% mortality for low Triscore (≤3), 8–14% for intermediate Triscore (4–5), and >22%—rising to over 65%—for high Triscore (≥6).

Right ventricular function is particularly critical, representing the most important determinant of the heart’s ability to tolerate surgery. Assessment of right ventricular performance in the presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation may be deceptive, as the ventricle appears to function better than it truly does owing to its work against a low-pressure chamber (the right atrium). Once regurgitation is corrected, the ventricle may struggle to accommodate the sudden increase in afterload and maintain adequate cardiac output—a phenomenon known as afterload mismatch.

In the past, patients were often referred for surgery only very late in the course of the disease, typically when symptoms persisted despite optimal diuretic therapy, often many years after onset. By this stage, the right ventricle was frequently markedly remodelled and dysfunctional, substantially contributing to high operative mortality. This elevated surgical risk, in turn, discouraged early referral within the cardiology community, perpetuating a self-reinforcing cycle.

In recent years, several studies have demonstrated a significant reduction in mortality following tricuspid valve surgery. The most recent data from the STS Registry report a mortality rate now below 5%.8 This improvement reflects several contributing factors: advances in operative techniques (including beating-heart and minimally invasive thoracotomy approaches in reoperative cases), more rigorous patient selection, the introduction of transcatheter therapies, and increased awareness within the cardiology community of the clinical importance of tricuspid regurgitation—leading to earlier referral and treatment in less advanced disease stages.

Indeed, outcomes are markedly improved when patients undergo surgery in a better clinical condition and with preserved right ventricular function. Sala et al.9 reported a postoperative mortality of 0% among patients without right ventricular dysfunction or right-sided heart failure, compared with 16% among those with moderate-or-greater dysfunction and symptomatic right-sided failure (Table 2).

These findings, consistent with Triscore-based risk stratification, are highly encouraging and underscore the value of surgery as an excellent therapeutic option when performed at an early stage of disease.

Efficacy of interventions: surgery vs. transcatheter therapies

Surgery remains the gold standard for effectively reducing tricuspid regurgitation. While valve replacement abolishes regurgitation completely, durable repair requires careful anatomical selection. In most cases, surgical tricuspid repair consists of annuloplasty aimed at reducing annular dilatation and restoring leaflet coaptation in the context of functional disease. In selected patients with leaflet tethering or complex anatomy, adjunctive techniques such as edge-to-edge repair may be combined with annuloplasty, achieving excellent outcomes (recurrence of ≥3+ tricuspid regurgitation at 16 years: 3%).10

It is important to recognize that the majority of patients who ultimately undergo surgery receive valve replacement rather than repair, largely because they are referred too late—often with long-standing disease, significant ventricular remodelling, and leaflet retraction, all of which limit the feasibility of repair. Replacement, in turn, carries a higher risk of right ventricular afterload mismatch and an increased likelihood of requiring permanent pacemaker implantation. Earlier referral would allow many patients to undergo valve repair, thereby avoiding replacement.

When replacement is required, bioprosthetic valves are most commonly used due to the low-pressure environment of the right heart, which promotes slower structural degeneration but increases thrombogenic risk. Ten-year freedom from structural valve deterioration has been reported at approximately 70%.11 Mechanical prostheses remain an option in younger patients and those with prior operations.

At present, surgical efficacy in reducing tricuspid regurgitation exceeds that reported for the most widely used transcatheter repair technologies, which are associated with high rates of residual regurgitation (>50% with ≥2+ and >15% with ≥3+).12 Transcatheter valve replacement may improve efficacy, but its applicability is currently limited by anatomical constraints and, similar to surgery, by the risk of right ventricular afterload mismatch.

To date, no randomized clinical trials have directly compared surgery with transcatheter therapies for tricuspid regurgitation. Recent retrospective analyses show markedly different rates of residual regurgitation in surgical vs. transcatheter patients—≥2+ in 3.4% vs. 65%, and ≥3+ in 0% vs. 30%, respectively.5

With respect to long-term survival, it is now well established that correction of tricuspid regurgitation—either surgically or via transcatheter intervention—confers a survival benefit compared with medical therapy alone.13

Following tricuspid valve surgery, as with in-hospital mortality, the clinical stage at presentation—particularly in terms of right ventricular dysfunction and end-organ damage—is crucial. Among asymptomatic patients with annular dilatation but preserved right ventricular function, 5-year mortality following surgery has been reported at 0%. This increases to 12% in patients with mild symptoms and mild right ventricular dysfunction, and reaches 40% in those with right-sided heart failure and moderate-to-severe right ventricular dysfunction.14

In series reporting 10-year outcomes, overall survival is approximately 40%. Notably, among patients with a low Triscore (≤3)—typically younger individuals with fewer symptoms, minimal organ damage, and preserved right ventricular function—surgery provides a substantial survival advantage compared with medical therapy (72% vs. 44%; HR 0.27, CI 0.20–0.37, P < 0.0001). This benefit is no longer evident for Triscore ≥4, which identifies older patients with significant organ dysfunction and advanced right ventricular impairment.

More specifically, when feasible, tricuspid repair confers the greatest 10-year survival benefit compared with both replacement and medical therapy for all patients with low (≤3) and intermediate (4–5) Triscore values. A rational therapeutic pathway

In light of the available evidence, a rational therapeutic pathway for patients with tricuspid regurgitation places low-risk, high-efficacy surgery as the preferred option in the early stages of disease.

The most recent European Society of Cardiology Guidelines2 recommend surgery for patients with severe tricuspid regurgitation who are symptomatic, or asymptomatic but already exhibiting signs of right ventricular remodelling. In all cases, surgery is advised only when there is no severe right or left ventricular dysfunction and no severe pulmonary hypertension.

Despite recent progress in disease awareness, many patients still present too late for optimal surgical intervention. In the presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation, irrespective of symptoms, it is essential to treat patients before right ventricular dysfunction develops. Early intervention is crucial to minimizing operative mortality and achieving the best long-term outcomes.

In the era of transcatheter technologies, it is more important than ever that each patient undergoes an individualized evaluation by a dedicated Heart Team—including a cardiologist, cardiac surgeon, interventionalist, and imaging specialist—to determine the most appropriate therapeutic strategy.

Conclusions

After decades of neglect, awareness of tricuspid regurgitation has recently increased, driven in part by the emergence of transcatheter technologies.

Tricuspid valve surgery, historically associated with high mortality owing to late referral, has demonstrated excellent in-hospital and long-term outcomes when performed before the onset of right ventricular dysfunction and end-organ damage. A new dedicated risk model, the Triscore, has been developed to assist in selecting surgical candidates, and surgery remains the first-line option for patients in the early stages of disease.

In the setting of severe tricuspid regurgitation, timely referral for surgical correction is essential in order to achieve optimal outcomes—specifically, low operative mortality, absence of residual regurgitation, and favourable long-term prognosis.
