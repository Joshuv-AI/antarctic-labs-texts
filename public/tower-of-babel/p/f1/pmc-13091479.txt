Microbial transmission among giant panda, sympatric wildlife, and domesticated animals
Authors: Hairong He, Shuting Liu, Han Han, Hong Zhou, Yuxiang Chen, Huawei Tian, Minghua Chen, Wei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe gut microbiome of wild animals is subject to complex influences, including cross-species transmission that risks host and environmental health. This study used 16S rRNA sequencing to profile microbial communities in giant pandas (GPs), sympatric wildlife, domesticated animals, and environmental samples from panda habitats. Results revealed significant differences in microbial community structure at both phylum and genus levels. Source Tracker analysis indicated that red pandas (RPs) serve as the primary potential microbial source for GPs (72.17%). However, the two species exhibited distinct patterns in acquiring microbes from other groups: GPs were more susceptible to potential transmission by aerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria compared to RPs. Water and soil showed distinct microbial assembly patterns as sinks, reflecting their divergent physicochemical properties. These findings demonstrate that dietary specialization and environmental exposure collectively shape the gut microbiome, offering insights for managing microbial interactions and habitats.

Introduction

The microbiome is often regarded as the host’s “second genome,”1 forming a highly dynamic ecosystem2 that profoundly influences host health, development, and adaptability.1,3 As with any ecosystem, a fundamental question remains: What ecological processes shape the diverse composition of microbial communities? Increasing evidence indicates that microorganisms are influenced by factors such as the host’s genetic background4 and diet.5 Simultaneously, microorganisms are also controlled by external transmission sources, such as different host individuals and the dynamic exchange networks formed between shared host environments.6,7,8 Moreover, such phenomena can directly influence the nature and frequency of microbial transmission between hosts.9 Therefore, understanding the dispersal and colonization patterns of microorganisms is key to comprehending the dynamics and functions of the microbiome.

Horizontal transmission of microorganisms among hosts and between hosts and their environment presents a key ecological process governing microbial community structure. Studies in humans and non-human primates reveal that direct contact during social activities may alter gut microbial composition, with the degree of sharing being proportional to contact opportunities10; Moeller et al. further demonstrated that social behavior shapes microbiome structure, reinforcing the idea that animal sociability facilitates microbial exchange.11 Moreover, the role of the environment in microbial transmission should not be underestimated. Humans and animals inhabiting the same geographic area often harbor convergent microbial profiles due to shared environmental exposure,12 and such overlapping habitats can effectively promote cross-species microbial dispersal.13 In summary, microbial transmission pathways represent a phenomenon driven by multiple factors, and understanding the underlying transmission mechanisms is crucial for microbial assembly.

The giant panda (GP) (Ailuropoda melanoleuca), as a flagship conservation species,14 faces multiple anthropogenic pressures, including habitat fragmentation, tourism, and livestock grazing.15,16 These activities not only directly encroach upon their living space but may also alter the native microbial community and introduce exogenous microorganisms. Moreover, GPs share their range with numerous wild mammals, such as the nationally protected species takin (Budorcas taxicolor) and forest musk deer (Moschus beresovska). as well as resource competitors such as wild boar (Sus scrofa) and tufted deer (Elaphodus cephalophus).17,18 Through fecal and urinary contamination of shared environments such as water and bamboo, these species, which coexist with GPs in limited habitats, represent an indirect transmission threat. Meanwhile, vector organisms such as ticks and mosquitoes may directly transmit pathogens such as Babesia during blood-feeding on different hosts.19 Together, these findings suggest that the GP’s microbiome may be exposed to a horizontal transmission network woven by multiple species.

In the Meigu Dafengding Nature Reserve (Figure 1), GPs and sympatric species (e.g., red pandas (RPs) and Chinese muntjac) not only share highly similar daily activity rhythms (temporal niche overlap) but also compete for trophic niches,20 primarily due to bamboo-dominated diets. Furthermore, human activities such as livestock grazing have degraded habitats, potentially forcing pandas to expand their ranges and increasing spatial niche overlap with other wildlife.21 These intensified multi-dimensional competitive interactions likely foster closer interspecies contact, thereby creating enhanced opportunities for cross-species microbial exchange. Therefore, this study addresses the central scientific question: What is the structure of the microbial transmission network linking GPs, sympatric animals, domesticated animals, and the environment within their ecosystem? To what extent and in what patterns do these microorganisms from different sources contribute to the GP and its key homologous species, such as the RPs? To address this issue, this study employs 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing technology combined with microbial source tracking to: (1) analyze differences in microbial community composition among GPs, RPs, sympatric animals, domesticated animals, and environmental samples and (2) quantify the direction of microbial transfer, contribution ratios, and potential causes of differences among these groups. This study provides a key case for understanding microbial flows in complex ecosystems and offers scientific basis for GP habitat management, disease risk early warning, and conservation strategy development.Figure 1Study area and sampling point distributionGP, giant panda; RP, red panda; SA, sympatric animals; and HA, domesticated animals. Results

Microbial community composition characteristics

We conducted community structure analysis at the phylum and genus levels for all samples. At the phylum level (Figure 2A), microbial communities exhibited distinct environmental gradient changes. The soil (SL) group was dominated by the Acidobacteria (36.64%) and Proteobacteria (36.01%), displaying typical soil microbial characteristics. In the GP and RP groups, Proteobacteria dominated absolutely (49.11%–51.66%). The domesticated animal (HA) group exhibited a typical gut microbiota structure, with an exceptional enrichment of Firmicutes (68.67%) accompanied by Bacteroidetes (16.09%). This pattern is consistent with that of omnivorous mammals such as pigs and dogs.22 At the genus level (Figure 2B), this differentiation became more pronounced: The SL group was dominated by soil-associated bacteria such as Subgroup 2_norank (16.67%); the GP and RP groups showed enrichment of Pseudomonas (28.00–29.00%); while the HA group was dominated by core gut bacteria including Clostridium (5.59%) and Lactobacillus (4.41%). Analysis of variance (Figures 2C and 2D) further confirmed significant differences in community structure at both phylum and genus levels among the groups. Microbial transmission pathway traceability analysis

Microbial source tracing analysis revealed significant differences in microbial input pathways and contributing bacterial genera between groups. Specifically, an exceptionally high proportion of microbial source contribution was observed between the GP and RP groups (GP→RP: 68.25%; RP→GP: 72.17%), dominated by genera such as Pseudomonas and Pedobacter. However, differences exist in other potential sources: For GP, microorganisms potential sources originating from SA accounted for 8.88%, with the primary contributing genera being Rhodoferax (14.57%), Pseudomonas (10.05%), and Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (9.76%); In RP, the major genera originating from SA were Pseudomonas (15.32%), Flavobacterium (8.91%), and Massilia (7.62%). The SA group exhibited both overlapping and distinct patterns. Notable differences were also observed in the potential contributions from the HA group. Within the GP group, the gut microbiome community from HA samples was dominated by the dominant species Clostridium sensu stricto 1 (55.51%); In the RP group’s HA samples, the gut microbiome community centered on Bacteroides (35.10%) and was enriched with a series of common gut symbionts and fermentative bacteria including Romboutsia (11.87%), Megamonas (5.54%), and Faecalibaculum (3.09%) (Figures 3A and 4A). The sample communities in the GP group, potentially derived from the WT group, exhibited a simple structure dominated by a limited number of bacterial genera. The dominant species were Leuconostoc (43.84%) and Oligoflexus (33.33%). with Roseiarcus (11.11%) as the third most abundant genus. Other detected genera, such as Escherichia-Shigella and Pseudomonas, exhibited relative abundances below 0.50%. In contrast, the community structure of the RP group potentially originating from WT was more complex and diverse, lacking a single dominant genus. These were primarily composed of the marker genera UCG-005 and Clostridium sensu stricto 1. The potential source WT was mainly represented by Prevotella_9, Lactiplantibacillus, and other bacteria. The 38.28% contribution is mainly attributed to environmental and aquatic-associated bacterial genera enriched in the RP gut, such as Pseudomonas, Pedobacter, and Flavobacterium (Figure 4B).

In environmental samples, the WT group’s microbes mainly originated from unknown sources (52.65%) and the SL group (31.95%), with minor contributions from RP, SA, HA, and GP groups. Significance test for key microbial transmission pathways

To assess the reliability of the proposed microbial transmission pathways, we performed nonparametric tests to compare the differences in contribution rates from specific source groups to different target groups. The Kruskal-Wallis test combined with Dunn’s post-hoc analysis revealed that when GP served as the source, its contribution rate to the RP group (M = 0.682) was significantly higher than to the HA group (M = 0.043, p < 0.0001) and SA group (M = 0.157, p < 0.0001) (Figure 6A). Conversely, when RP served as the source, its contribution rate to the GP group (M = 0.722) was significantly higher than to the SA group (M = 0.383, p = 0.0134) and WT group (M = 0.062, p = 0.00088) (Figure 6B). Furthermore, the contribution patterns of HA and SA as sources also exhibited specific target preferences. Discussion

Host selection drives microbial community assembly: A pattern of divergent colonization in a shared environment

Our findings reveal that while GPs and RPs exhibit high similarity in gut microbial composition, their microbial communities also differ significantly from those of sympatric and domesticated animals. Moreover, the proportion of microbial sources varies markedly depending on the host species. This phenomenon strongly suggests that the host’s intestinal environment serves as a selective filter for the temporary colonization of microorganisms, and the intensity of this filtering varies depending on the host’s dietary habits and physiology.23 Previous studies have demonstrated that dietary shifts can rapidly reshape gut microbiota across diverse hosts,7 and species with similar feeding habits tend to harbor more similar gut microbiomes.24,25 A case in point is provided by recent research on leaf-eating Presbytis monkeys, which found an increase in human disturbance-related (HDR) microbial species, further demonstrating that factors such as diet, host biology, and human-altered environments can collectively shape the composition and function of gut microbiota in these primates.26 We therefore speculate that the limited pattern of source contribution between GPs and sympatric animals reflects their highly specialized, bamboo-based diet. In contrast, the greater pattern of source contribution observed in RPs likely stems from their broader dietary habits, which supports higher plasticity in their gut microbiome.27

The high degree of microbial overlap between GPs and RPs may be attributed to several factors. These include their similar diets centered on bamboo, shared habitat space, and potential behavioral interactions.28,29 In contrast, the significant divergence between the GP’s microbiome and that of its co-habitat animals likely stems from the extreme intestinal environment. This environment is shaped by its highly specialized diet. The panda’s diet is characterized by high cellulose and low protein content. It is coupled with its short and simple digestive tract. Together, these create an acidic environment unfavorable for obligate anaerobes but conducive to aerotolerant and facultative anaerobic bacteria.30 This is consistent with the results of the GP group microbial community from the potential source SA group that we observed. This structure significantly impedes the colonization of relevant microbial communities,31 thereby excluding microbes derived from other animals. A similar pattern was observed in the HA group. There, the majority of the potential contribution (55.51%) was attributable to the genus Clostridium sensu stricto. This genus constitutes the core microbial community responsible for cellulose digestion in GPs.32 Additionally, the HA group showed an increase in bacterial taxa that include opportunistic or potential pathogens, such as Brevundimonas, Acinetobacter, and Escherichia-Shigella, all of which are aerobic or facultative anaerobic organisms.33,34,35,36,37 Their enrichment is widely considered a microbial indicator of captive or anthropogenically disturbed environments.38,39,40 These bacteria potentially originate from water sources or contact surfaces within domestic environments. This suggests that domesticated animals may serve as significant external factors transmitting pathogenic microorganisms to the GP gut.

In contrast, RPs consume a more diverse diet that includes bamboo leaves, shoots, fruits, and vegetables.41 Their intestinal environment more closely resembles that of herbivores, and it features a longer cecum.27 Their gut microbiota varies with food sources. It exhibits seasonal dynamics42 and potentially greater “versatility.” This shapes a more open ecosystem with weaker selective pressures. This ecosystem is capable of accommodating and integrating a wider range of microorganisms from sympatric animals and the environment. Our results revealed that the predominant potential sources in the SA group were Pseudomonas (15.32%), Flavobacterium (8.91%), and Massilia (7.62%). These are typical environmental and aquatic bacteria.43,44,45 In the HA group, the gut microbiota structure exhibited a relatively typical mammalian profile marked by dominant genera such as Bacteroides (35.10%) and Romboutsia (11.87%).46 These bacteria are strict anaerobes and key degraders of dietary fiber and complex polysaccharides,47,48 commonly found in the guts of livestock and other mammals. The RPs possess a longer caecum and a broader diet. which provides a gut environment rich in diverse plant polysaccharides.49 Consequently, its gut can receive and integrate these allochthonous anaerobic bacteria. Conversely, the GP has a short and acidic gut specialized for digesting bamboo cellulose. This adaptation results in a narrow functional niche and a strong chemical barrier that effectively filters out such generalist, foreign bacteria.31 This contrast underscores that the RP gut microbiota is more diverse.

In contrast, the GP gut microbiota shows selective enrichment of specific microbial taxa. Despite convergent diets, there is a high degree of microbiota overlap between GPs and RPs. However, they exhibit distinct modes of acquiring microbes from other animal sources. This divergence suggests that the RP’s gut ecosystem may act as a critical microbial hub in the sympatric habitat. It could initially uptake and accumulate pathogens from wild and domestic animals. This may subsequently increase the risk of indirect transmission to the GP’s relatively secluded gut. Such transmission would occur through shared habitat and food resources such as bamboo. This dynamic could pose a potential cross-species infection threat to endangered sympatric species such as the GPs. However, confirming the role and efficiency of host filtering in this process will require further evidence. Future studies should therefore integrate intestinal physicochemical measurements or metagenomic functional data, thereby validating the differences in intestinal filtering mechanisms between GPs and sympatric species.

The Differentiated Role of Environmental Media as Microbial “sinks”

In addition, we compared the roles of soil (SL) and water (WT) as potential microbial sources. Results indicated that water acts as a more effective medium for potential microbial transmission than soil. A core bacterial community—including Hafnia-Obesumbacterium, Rhodoferax, Massilia, and Pseudomonas—was shared among different potential source groups (GP, RP, and SA) in water samples. Most of these core taxa are facultative anaerobes, capable of flexibly switching metabolic modes across different environments.50 Furthermore, water samples were enriched with bacterial taxa that include opportunistic or potential pathogens, such as Acinetobacter (33.33%) and Prevotella_9 (25.65%) from the HA group, and Yersinia (1.78%) from the RP group. Members of these genera have been documented in the literature to possess traits that facilitate survival in host environments, such as resisting immune clearance and utilizing host nutrients.51 This grants them a physiological advantage when invading and colonizing established gut communities.52 Consequently, their colonization success after ingestion is significantly higher than that of bacteria solely adapted to soil environments.

Meanwhile, the ease with which water acquires microorganisms from other potential sources also depends on its own physical structure. Soil is a highly structured porous medium where microorganisms typically spread by adsorbing onto soil particle surfaces. This mode of transport severely limits microbial mobility, and the extent of adsorption depends on factors such as particle texture and heavy rainfall.53,54,55 Water bodies, in contrast to soil, are homogeneous, flowing liquid structures where microorganisms can migrate long distances via water currents.56 Furthermore, soil is a medium with higher biodiversity than water bodies. Studies indicate that soil microbial community diversity negatively correlates with the survival space of invasive microorganisms.57 In summary, the fundamental reason why water bodies serve as more effective conduits for microbial transmission than soil lies in the combined effects of physical structure, chemical environment, and ecological competition. Soil represents a highly competitive and exclusionary environment where microbial transmission relies on water bodies, which function as highly mobile and readily colonizable transmission media. This understanding is crucial for comprehending microbial movement within ecosystems and assessing health risks to animals exposed to environmental contaminants.

Therefore, the differences in the physicochemical properties of water and soil dictate their distinct roles in the microbial transmission network. Within the context of animal gut microbiota exchange focused on in this study, the highly mobile water is more likely to act as an extensive, long-distance ‘transmission pathway,’ widely dispersing microorganisms from habitats of domestic and sympatric wild animals. This process may introduce allochthonous microorganisms, including pathogens, into the activity ranges of endangered species such as the GPs.58,59 In contrast, soil functions more such as a local ‘buffer zone.’ Its strong adsorption capacity, while limiting the widespread diffusion of microorganisms, may also lead to the formation of high-concentration microbial reservoirs at localized points (e.g., fecal deposition sites).60,61 These reservoirs can impose more direct, though spatially limited, impacts on the gut microbiota of animals through direct contact (such as during foraging or digging). Consequently, when assessing and managing cross-species microbial transmission risks, it is imperative to recognize that these two media shape fundamentally different risk profiles.

Based on the findings of this study, we gain important insights into the interactions among host specificity, environmental exposure, and microbiome assembly. The shared habitat and partially overlapping diets of GPs and RPs drive the formation of a unique pattern of convergence and divergence in their gut microbiomes. Such convergence may not only broaden their ecological niches for utilizing shared food resources (e.g., bamboo) but also actively reshape the composition and function of the environmental microbial pool through ongoing microbial exchange. Furthermore, the distinctly different potential transmission patterns observed between soil and water highlight the need to deepen our understanding of aquatic transmission mechanisms. Human activities such as grazing and farming may facilitate microbial dispersal via water between the environment and animals. Therefore, when developing microbial safety strategies for GP populations, special attention should be paid to the key role of RPs—as core participants in the microbial transmission network—and water should be monitored as a critical pathway for the spread of microorganisms, including pathogens. This approach will help establish a more comprehensive framework for ecological health risk assessment and prevention.

Conclusion

This study reveals that despite high microbial sharing between GPs and RPs due to their overlapping habitats, their gut ecosystems adopt distinct ecological strategies toward microbes from sympatric (SA) and domesticated animals (HA). The GP gut is dominated by oxygen-tolerant or facultative anaerobic bacteria, along with core cellulolytic taxa, reflecting strong host-specific filtering. In contrast, the RP gut more readily enriches bacteria from aquatic environments, such as Pseudomonas, Hafnia-Obesumbacterium, and Yersinia. Furthermore, water bodies, as a more effective potential source of microbial transmission, allow facultative anaerobes that have already established themselves in the environment to more easily re-establish, whereas soil microbial communities are less likely to serve as potential sources of transmission. Non-parametric tests comparing contribution rates from specific source groups to different target groups revealed significant differences in microbial contributions to GP and RP. Moreover, HA and SA sources exhibited distinct target preferences in their contribution patterns. These findings highlight that microbial transmission is not only shaped by adaptive strategies but also exhibits clear directionality and target specificity. Thus, we recommend incorporating a water safety-centered early-warning system for pathogen transmission into environmental monitoring programs to protect the ecological health of wildlife.

Limitations of the study

Although this study systematically reveals the interactions between hosts and environments in microbial assembly, several limitations remain: First, while 16S rRNA gene sequencing effectively resolves microbial community structure, it inherently lacks resolution for species identification (particularly at the species level) and functional prediction. Future adoption of metagenomic sequencing will enable more precise elucidation of specific microbial functions and their transmission mechanisms. Second, the study primarily relied on cross-sectional data, which can reveal microbial community differences across habitats but struggles to dynamically capture real-time changes in microbial communities. Long-term longitudinal tracking would help elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics of microbial transmission. Additionally, while the classification of environmental samples (e.g., WT and SL) holds ecological significance, our study did not further differentiate internal heterogeneity within these categories. For instance, different water bodies (e.g., streams and stagnant ponds) or soil types may exhibit distinct microbial retention and transmission capacities, which were not quantified here. This limitation suggests that the specific influence of such environmental factors on microbial transmission warrants more detailed future investigation. Finally, while source-tracking analysis identified microbial transmission pathways, the experimental design remains observational and fails to directly validate specific microbial transmission capabilities and colonization mechanisms through controlled experiments. These limitations point to future research directions, including adopting multi-omics approaches, conducting long-term monitoring, and integrating quantitative analyses of environmental factors to more comprehensively elucidate microbial interactions between wildlife and their environments.

Method details

Study area and sample collection

Meigu Dafengding National Nature Reserve is located in the northeastern part of Meigu County, Liangshan Yi Autonomous Prefecture, Sichuan Province. Its geographical coordinates are 102°52′— 103°20′E and 28°30′— 28°50′N. This area provides habitat for a number of rare animals, including giant pandas, red pandas, and takin. However, long-term dependence on local natural resources and livestock farming by residents has led to severe degradation of wildlife habitats, including those of giant pandas.62 Studies indicate that grazing has become one of the primary disturbances in protected areas, accounting for 71.13% of all disturbances.63

All samples in this study were collected within the reserve. We collected animal feces, soil, and water samples using the line transect method in both grazing and non-grazing areas, obtaining a total of 108 fresh feces samples: 67 samples were from giant pandas and their sympatric wildlife, while 41 were from domesticated animals. Detailed sample information is provided in Table S1. Fecal samples were collected as fresh specimens within 5 days whenever possible. Disposable PE gloves were worn throughout the process, with new gloves changed for each sample to prevent cross-contamination between specimens. Soil and water samples were collected around the feces, yielding a total of 33 soil samples and 8 water samples (Figure 1). Soil samples were collected using a five-point method, centered on fresh fecal deposits within a 1-meter radius. After clearing loose surface debris, topsoil (0-5 cm depth) from each point was collected and combined to form a single composite sample per site.64 The processed soil was stored in sterile plastic bags. Water samples were collected from locations where direct animal activity (e.g., visitation or defecation) was observed, using sterile bottles.65 All samples were transported on dry ice to Mingke Biotechnology (Hangzhou) Co., Ltd. for full-length 16S rRNA sequencing.

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

This study employed Illumina paired-end sequencing of the full-length 16S rRNA gene (V1-V9), amplified using the universal bacterial primers 27F(5’-AGRGTTTGATYNTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R(5’-TASGGHTACCTTGTTASGACTT-3’), to characterize the microbiome in environmental samples.66 First, total DNA was extracted using the FastLee® DNA Kit. After quality control via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, barcoded specific primers were used for polymerase chain reaction(PCR) amplification of the full-length region. This was performed using TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase (TransGen AP221-02) on an ABI GeneAmp® 9700 PCR system. Each sample was amplified in three technical replicates, with cycle numbers optimized in preliminary experiments and maintained consistently. The product was subjected to 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, recovered from the gel using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit, eluted with Tris-HCl, and confirmed by re-electrophoresis. The recovered products were precisely quantified using the QuantiFluor™-ST Blue Fluorescence Quantification System (Promega) and then mixed in equimolar ratios. The library was generated by connecting Illumina Y-adapters, magnetic bead purification, 8 cycles of enrichment PCR, and NaOH denaturation to produce single-stranded DNA templates. Final sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform with 250 bp/300 bp paired-end reads.

Statistical analysis

This study conducted systematic bioinformatics analysis on Illumina paired-end sequencing raw data. First, quality control filtering was performed using Trimmomatic to obtain high-quality reads. FLASH was then employed to merge paired-end reads into contigs based on overlap. Usearch software was then utilized to remove chimeras against the gold database.67 Finally, reads were oriented and separated into high-quality data for each sample based on barcode and primer sequences (consensus confidence ≥ 0.8). Subsequently, the UPARSE software package was employed to perform operational taxonomic unit(OUT) clustering at 97% similarity,68 and the Uclust classifier was used to annotate species for the OTU representative sequences.69

Perform microbial source tracking analysis using Source Tracker to calculate the contribution ratios of different pollution sources to the receiving point samples,70 and visualize the results using the ggplot2 package. This Bayesian method estimates the proportional contributions of known source microbial communities to sink samples, based on the key principle of linear mixture modeling solved via a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm.71 A critical feature is the inclusion of an “unknown” source to account for unsampled origins and to evaluate the completeness of the source sample set. The applicability of the method requires that the source sample set is representative, with sufficient biological replicates per source category, and that all sequence data are processed uniformly into an OTU abundance table for input. Since the contribution rate data did not meet the normality assumption, the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to evaluate overall differences among multiple source groups. Where the overall test was significant (p < 0.05), Dunn’s post-hoc test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction was used for pairwise comparisons. Data were visualized using box plots overlaid with scatter points, supported mainly by the ggplot2, rstatix, and ggsignif packages. Bar plots depicting microbial composition at the phylum and genus levels were also generated using ggplot2. All statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.5). The map of the Meigu Dafengding National Nature Reserve was generated with ArcGIS 10.8.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0). For comparisons of microbial proportional contributions among multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. Statistical significance was set at ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. All data are presented as mean ± SEM. Exact sample sizes (n) and statistical test results for each experiment are indicated in the figure legends.
