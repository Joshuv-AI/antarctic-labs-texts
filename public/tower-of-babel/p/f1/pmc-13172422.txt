Multidimensional data analysis and classification using SMIAL
Authors: Aline Knab, Shannon Handley, Xiaohu Xu, Max Mackevicius, Ayad G Anwer, Jared M Campbell, Yuan Tian, Ewa M Goldys, Akanksha Bhargava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLabel-free imaging enables multidimensional data acquisition across spectral and temporal domains and is increasingly used in life sciences. However, users working with multidimensional label-free data often lack the programming expertise needed to build reproducible image analysis pipelines. Existing software require custom programming or switching between fragmented platforms for feature generation and classification tasks. In this work, we introduce SMIAL, an open-source graphical user interface (GUI) software available as a stand-alone package runnable as a 64-bit Windows executable that enables end-to-end machine learning (ML) workflows for multichannel 2D imaging data without requiring programming skills. Users can input multichannel image stacks directly in SMIAL or work with pre-processed multidimensional data via feature tables or pre-trained ML models. Additionally, SMIAL supports parameter saving and reloading to ensure reproducibility across its pre-processing, segmentation, feature generation, feature selection and classification panels. We demonstrate SMIAL’s utility across three representative applications of label-free, multispectral imaging: melanoma detection, tracking mitochondrial response to rotenone treatment and non-invasive assessment of food quality. Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Machine learning

Introduction

Advances in high-throughput microscopy have transformed life science imaging by enabling multidimensional data generation with emerging applications in signature-based screening1,2, label-free diagnostics3–5 and disease progression3,6. Label-free imaging methods provide significant advantages over conventional microscopy in clinical settings by removing the need for extrinsic labelling, which is often impractical due to time constraints and potential sample toxicity3,7,8. For example, multispectral or hyperspectral methods capture intrinsic optical properties of biological samples (e.g. autofluorescence, absorption, reflectance) using a wide range of excitation and emission wavelengths and often produce large volumes of multichannel imaging data9,10. These data are inherently multidimensional as they capture signals across spatial, spectral and temporal domains. The data complexity holds immense potential for new clinical applications but also presents significant challenges for interpretation and analysis.

Image-based machine learning has emerged as a powerful tool for extracting meaningful patterns from complex datasets, particularly in disease diagnosis2,11–15. Despite these advances, there is limited access to advanced computational software and programming expertise is often required to apply them effectively. Existing image-analysis software often lacks support for multidimensional, label-free data and requires significant programming knowledge. Even basic pre-processing of multispectral data can be challenging, as signal-to-noise often varies across imaging channels. Additionally, pre-processing parameters must typically be tuned on a per-channel basis and may require adjustment with each new image acquisition protocol. Effective tools must support feature extraction across spatial, spectral and temporal dimensions, while also offering dimensionality reduction and feature selection techniques to mitigate overfitting risks16,17. Furthermore, selecting an appropriate classification strategy requires consideration of factors such as supervised versus unsupervised learning, classifier types, evaluation metrics and the ability to manage multiple classes and class imbalances18.

While several machine learning-based software have been developed for microscopy data, many fall short in supporting multidimensional label-free analysis. Commercial software such as Columbus (PerkinElmer), MetaXpress (Molecular Devices), Incucyte (Sartorius), and Prediktera offer proprietary toolkits, but their high costs and closed-source nature limit accessibility. Open-source software developed in academic settings has improved image-analysis workflows18, yet they often require programming knowledge to adapt for label-free data19–21 or lack the infrastructure to build end-to-end machine learning pipelines for multispectral datasets11,22–25. CellProfiler11, while being widely used for feature extraction from labelled data, offers limited customisation for multidimensional feature selection and classification. Tools such as Advanced Cell Classifier26 and CellXpress23 support some aspects of multidimensional classification but lack robust, channel-specific tuning of pre-processing parameters and feature extraction capabilities. Others, such as AVHYAS27 and spectraAI28, are designed for spectral data analysis and only offer limited pre-processing options. Additionally, their machine learning modules are primarily suited for semantic segmentation and image denoising, making them less suitable for image-based profiling. Moreover, the fragmented nature of these tools requires users to switch between multiple platforms to complete a full classification pipeline, increasing the risk of errors and reproducibility.

To address these limitations, we introduce SMIAL, an open-source software for multidimensional data analysis and classification. The software comprises six key panels: Load Images, Segmentation and Masks, Pre-processing, Feature Generation, Data Cleaning and Statistics and Feature Analysis. This modular design enables users to build end-to-end reproducible machine learning pipelines without requiring programming knowledge. Additionally, SMIAL can input different types of multidimensional data, including 2D multispectral and temporal images, feature tables (e.g. from 3D images or labelled microscopy data), and pre-trained machine learning models. Furthermore, data classification could be carried out using imaging features in different data dimensions, including intensity, morphology, texture and time. We demonstrate SMIAL’s capabilities through three workflows for (1) label-free melanoma detection using cellular autofluorescence, (2) tracking mitochondrial response to treatment using longitudinal autofluorescence imaging and (3) hyperspectral imaging assessment of fruit quality. Overall, SMIAL provides powerful, accessible and reproducible workflows to analyse and classify multidimensional data for life science applications from disease detection and monitoring to non-invasive food quality assessment.

Conducting cell-based multi-class classification for melanoma detection

Figure 2 illustrates the SMIAL pipeline used to build a multi-class supervised classification model based on the cellular autofluorescence of melanoma cells and healthy controls. Multispectral images from three cell types were analysed: A375 amelanotic melanoma cells, COLO679 melanoma cells and 142BR healthy fibroblasts. Each dataset contained 29 spectral imaging channels at a spatial resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels (1 pixel = 0.41 μm) acquired across an excitation range of 345–505 nm29.

Using the Load Images panel (Fig. 2a–c), the multispectral datasets for each cell type were loaded and assigned the corresponding class labels COLO679, A375 or 142BR. Hand-drawn binary masks created from brightfield images covered 471 cells for 142BR, 570 for COLO679 and 844 for A375 cells. These masks were overlaid onto the corresponding autofluorescence images within the Segmentation and Masks panel to confirm segmentation accuracy (Fig. 2d–i).

The Pre-Processing panel enhanced cellular autofluorescence by applying image smoothing, background subtraction and calibration (Fig. 1j–o). The HyRes (Hyperspectral Restoration using Sparse and Low-Rank Modelling) image smoothing algorithm30 improved the SNR by an average of 16% across channels. Background signal was subtracted from all channels using a reference water image acquired under the same conditions as cells (Fig. 2j, k, l). Image calibration was performed using the Calibration subpanel based on images of a calibration fluid containing NADH and Flavins31 and a calibration factor obtained from corresponding spectrofluorometer measurements. Pre-processed images (Fig. 2m, n, o) showed higher SNR compared to raw images (Fig. 2j, k, l).

Using the Feature Generation workflow, 1436 features per cell class were extracted, including 15 intensity and 11 texture features per spectral channel, and 8 morphology features. It enabled direct visual comparison of selected features across cell types (Fig. 2p), and inter-feature correlations within each class (Fig. 2q). Applying the Data Cleaning and Statistics panel reduced the total feature set to 59 (Fig. 2r, s, t). First, all rows with missing values or invalid entries (e.g. NaN) were removed. A statistics filter using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test (with p > 0.0001) removed one intensity feature (Fig. 2s). Subsequently, a correlation filter using Spearman correlation (with r = 0.9) was applied, which resulted in the removal of an additional four morphology features, 304 texture features and 1067 intensity features (Fig. 2t).

Figure 2u–w illustrates the use of the Feature Analysis panel to distinguish between the three cell types. The multi-select algorithm in the Feature Selection subpanel identified the top ten highly ranked features across Maximum Relevance and Minimum Redundancy (MRMR), Chi-square, Laplacian, ReliefF and Gini-Index methods. This was followed by principal component analysis (PCA) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) transformation. A logistic regression classifier using default hyperparameters with 10-fold cross-validation showed cell group clusters on a linear discriminant plot (Fig. 2u), visualised using the Performance Visualisation subpanel. High area under the curve (AUC) values in both the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and precision-recall curves (AUC = 0.98 for 142BR, 0.98 for COLO679, and 0.99 for A375) indicated strong model performance in distinguishing fibroblasts from the two melanoma cell lines (Fig. 2v). Average F1 score (0.93), sensitivity (0.93) and specificity (0.97) across cell types supported the high model performance, as assessed through the Performance Metrics subpanel.

The estimated mean absolute SHAP values revealed the most influential features for melanoma classification, particularly in identifying amelanotic A375 melanoma cells. These included the ratio of mean intensities between channel 16 and channel 18, channel 12 and channel 22, and the cellular major axis (Fig. 2w). Channel 12 to channel 22, with respective excitation/emission wavelengths of 377/414 nm and 451/575 nm, are tentatively attributed to the native fluorophores NAD(P)H and flavin proteins. This suggests a role for cellular redox states in classification, consistent with previous studies, albeit using different channel combinations29. Conversely, the intensity ratio between the mean intensity of channel 7 and channel 24 contributed the least to the classification.

Retinal h1RPE7 cells were treated with 10 µM rotenone and mitochondrial responses were assessed using autofluorescence multispectral imaging at 24, 48 and 72 h post treatment. Untreated cells from the same cell line served as controls and were imaged using the same protocol.

Data display tools in the Load Images panel revealed dynamic changes in mitochondrial autofluorescence at the single cell level in both treated and untreated cells (Fig. 3a–d). Using the Segmentation and Masks panel, ilastik32-generated binary masks were overlayed onto the high-resolution autofluorescence images of mitochondrial networks, providing a visual comparison of segmentation performance (Fig. 3e, f). Median filters of sizes 5 (Fig. 3g), 10 (Fig. 3h), and 20 (Fig. 3i) were tested in the Pre-processing panel to reduce noise while preserving the mitochondrial structure. Filter sizes greater than size 5 reduced visibility of fine structures (Fig. 3h, i) and were excluded from further analysis to avoid loss of relevant biological features.

The Feature Generation panel extracted 18 intensity, 19 morphology and 14 textural descriptors of mitochondrial structure and function (Fig. 3j). Additionally, temporal changes were quantified using the Time subpanel to capture mitochondrial activity in the treated group at 24, 48 and 72 h relative to the untreated control group at 24 h (Fig. 3k–m). While mean intensity of treated mitochondria networks increased by 1.75 times within 72 h (Fig. 3l), the rate of change of angular second moment (homogeneity) declined more rapidly from 24 to 48 h (2 × 10−2/h) than from 48 to 72 h (6 × 10−3/h) (Fig. 3m). These changes corresponded with mitochondrial network fragmentation (a morphological indicator) which increased by 20.1% at 24 h, decreased slightly at 48 h, and peaked at 21.2% by 72 h (Fig. 3k). Similarly, circularity steadily decreased over time from 10% at 24 h to 6% by 72 h (Fig. 3k). Applying the Data Cleaning and Statistics panel reduced the total feature set from 1182 features to 21. First, all rows with missing values or invalid entries (NaN/Inf/empty) were removed, followed by a statistics filter using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test (with p > 0.0001), removing 884 features. Subsequently, a correlation filter using Spearman correlation (with r = 0.9) was applied, removing an additional 277 features.

Using the Feature selection subpanel, the multi-select algorithm identified the top ten highly ranked features across MRMR, Chi-square, Laplacian, ReliefF, Gini Index, Bhattacharya distance, entropy, t-test, Wilcoxon and ROC method. From the Classification subpanel, an LDA classifier was implemented using 5-fold cross-validation and the default hyperparameter settings. Performance was first evaluated within individual feature categories: intensity features achieved an AUC of 0.77 ± 0.02, while morphology and texture features yielded lower AUCs of 0.51 ± 0.05 and 0.50 ± 0.03 respectively (Fig. 3n). Incorporating temporal features significantly enhanced classification performance, resulting in an AUC of 0.83 ± 0.03, F1 score of 0.82 with 91.22% cells correctly identified, indicating that time-based features were more discriminative than static descriptors alone. Combining all feature types (intensity, morphology, texture and time) led to a slight further improvement, achieving an AUC of 0.85 ± 0.07.

Data display tools via the Load Images panel enabled seamless visualisation of all three hyperspectral imaging data (Fig. 4a–c). Binary masks were generated within SMIAL based on high reflectance contrast between object and background pixels using the Segmentation and Masks panel. Segmentation parameters included: contrast scale (0.1), binary threshold (0.12), fill holes (connectivity 8), remove pixel components (100), and smooth (15). Comparing SMIAL-generated masks with hand-drawn masks (Fig. 4d–f) showed high segmentation performance with a mean F1 score of 0.98 and intersection over union (IoU) of 0.96 across all classes. Precision was 0.96 (overripe), 0.97 (ripe) and 0.96 (unripe), while recall remained consistently high at 0.99 for all classes (Fig. 4g).

The Feature Generation panel extracted 6515 features per class, including intensity, morphology and texture features. Analysis of intensity-based features revealed that mean and maximum reflectance (Fig. 4h, i) overlapped across classes. While wavelengths between 500 and 600 nm showed small differences between classes in terms of contrast (Fig. 4j) and kurtosis (Fig. 4k), single-feature analysis appeared insufficient for class separation.

The Classification subpanel in the Feature Analysis panel was used to assess how resampling strategies affect classification performance due to the class imbalance caused by the overrepresentation of the unripe fruit class. Three strategies were tested, including ADASYN upsampling, SMOTE upsampling and random downsampling. Classification was performed using 5-fold cross-validation, z-score normalisation, Gini-index based selection of top five features, PCA and LDA feature extraction, polynomial support vector machine (SVM) classifier with default hyperparameters. Among the resampling strategies, SMOTE upsampling achieved the highest AUC (Table 1). Classification performance was highest for the ripe class with an AUC of 0.76 ± 0.05 (Fig. 4l) and a specificity of 0.71 ± 0.15 (Fig. 4m). Overall, these results demonstrate SMIAL’s ability to handle high-dimensional hyperspectral data, perform segmentation on high SNR reflectance images and handle class imbalance in a supervised classification pipeline.

Discussion

Label-free imaging methods are increasingly used in both biomedical and non-biomedical contexts, generating high-resolution, multichannel images that capture complex spatial, spectral and temporal phenomena. However, there remains a lack of freely available machine learning software that enables custom data analysis and classification pipelines for diverse 2D imaging datasets without requiring significant programming expertise. To address this gap, this study introduces SMIAL, an open-source software package for multidimensional data analysis and classification. SMIAL provides an intuitive GUI with structured, numbered panels for image loading, segmentation, pre-processing, feature generation, data cleaning, statistical analysis, feature selection and classification. To enhance usability and reduce barriers for research adoption, SMIAL’s user manual provides workflow diagrams that guide users in implementing each panel and developing custom pipelines suited for their applications. Additionally, the GUI includes progress indicators and informative error messages for troubleshooting to further improve the user experience.

SMIAL’s broad support for diverse inputs, including autofluorescence, transmittance, reflectance, and labelled microscopy images, makes it a usable, workflow-driven software for a range of optical imaging applications. Additionally, its ability to input feature tables further extends its utility beyond traditional image analyses. The integration of several visualisation options in each panel allows users to conveniently assess and improve data quality prior to developing machine learning models. Furthermore, it is possible to evaluate generalisability and model performance on new datasets using pre-trained classification models. SMIAL’s parameter saving and reloading capability makes it easier to develop reproducible image-analysis pipelines, with each panel exporting detailed parameter logs for transparency and repeatability. The integration of custom calibration formulas34, batch-processing capabilities for segmentation and pre-processing, and multi-select optimisation in feature selection and feature analysis makes SMIAL advantageous in high-throughput data environments.

SMIAL is well-suited for extracting diverse feature types, including morphological, intensity-based, texture and temporal features from multidimensional imaging datasets. A key advantage is its ability to spatially map extracted features onto the original images, allowing users to compare results across classes. The feature analysis panel enables users to select an optimal subset of features, reducing the risk of overfitting the classification model. Users can leverage this capability to develop ensemble methods35 by aggregating subsets obtained from multiple feature selection algorithms or spatial scales36, thereby improving classification performance.

SMIAL offers a competitive advantage over alternatives such as Autogluon37 with its large collection of classifiers, enabling users to select the most effective model for their specific data and problem38. While many classifiers may achieve high AUC values, as demonstrated with the example of the melanoma autofluorescence dataset, significant differences may emerge when comparing multiple performance metrics, such as F1 score, sensitivity, and balanced accuracy. SMIAL provides performance metrics for both the training and test datasets, allowing users to monitor potential overfitting where a model performs well on training data but fails to generalise as indicated by lower performance metrics on the test data39.

The challenge of handling unbalanced datasets40 is addressed by allowing upsampling or downsampling methods where significant differences occur between class observations. Users can assess classification performance through various metrics, including accuracy in predicting each class for both the training and test sets. For example, it enables the measurement of false negatives for each class, where a low number of false negatives indicates good classification. This allows users to determine if the classifier is disproportionately favouring the majority class, leading to high misclassification in the minority class.

We demonstrated how SMIAL enabled longitudinal monitoring of cellular response to treatment, while identifying that cellular morphology and texture features were significantly impacted by 24, 48 and 72 h of exposure. This capability could be useful in investigating cellular mechanisms of drug resistance in cancer41–43, reproductive medicine44,45 and neurological diseases using SMIAL. For example, SMIAL could be used to perform a comparative assessment of conventional features vs temporal features to predict a patient’s response over time46.

SMIAL’s pipeline, encompassing image pre-processing, segmentation, feature extraction, dimensionality reduction and classification, mirrors typical image-analysis scenarios in early disease diagnostics47–49. This further opens avenues for easily integrating machine learning with label-free techniques in this direction. For example, Bemando et al.50 applied machine learning techniques to identify risk factors of coronary heart disease and developed an early diagnosis tool using tabular patient data with attributes associated with cardiovascular diseases. Similarly, SMIAL could be used to select features for data separation and make predictions in data classification51.

While this study showcased applications on single-cell imaging data, SMIAL can also input feature tables obtained from other types of data, such as vascular imaging data36 and data from translational imaging studies52. This includes data from in-vivo techniques53, multiscale54 and multimodality approaches55, image-based computational models56 or other specialised temporal feature extraction toolkits57.

Despite its strengths, SMIAL has several limitations. Currently, the feature generation capability is limited to 2D data or importing feature tables externally generated from 3D data. Future iterations could include the ability to generate three-dimensional features. Additionally, SMIAL cannot handle imaging data requiring motion tracking before feature extraction, but this could be addressed by generating features from other software, such as CellCognition58 and BioImageXD59, which can then be imported into SMIAL. The Data Cleaning and Statistics panel currently handles missing observations by prompting their removal rather than applying automatic imputation strategies (e.g. median or mean substitution).In addition, the classification tool is currently more focused on supervised methods than unsupervised methods. While deep learning is not enabled in this version, SMIAL could be used as a tool to interpret features obtained through these models via feature correlation60. Furthermore, currently, metadata detection is limited to the image input size and number of channels, not displaying detailed acquisition parameters.

By combining SMIAL’s reproducible and comprehensive data analysis capabilities with an intuitive and interpretable interface, we aim to reduce barriers to adoption for non-specialists and promote the development of novel machine learning workflows for microscopy image analysis across diverse research disciplines.

Methods

SMIAL was developed in Matlab 2024a and is freely available as a stand-alone package runnable as a 64-bit Windows executable, that does not require a Matlab installation (https://github.com/EwaGoldys/SMIAL.git). The full source code is also provided for direct use within MATLAB environments.

Load Images panel

The Load Images panel can input multichannel spectral and temporal imaging data. It is compatible with common file formats, including .mat, .tiff, .jpg, .png and can read basic metadata, including image dimensions, number of channels, spatial and temporal resolution. A user-friendly data viewing capability is built in to access different types of visualisations suited for multichannel images, including one image at a time display, 3D rendered surface plots and intensity profiling using line plots.

Segmentation and Masks panel

The Segmentation and Masks panel allows users to define regions of interest using an intensity threshold-based segmentation, which is particularly suitable for high SNR images (e.g. reflectance or stained data). To generate a binary mask, the tool provides options to enhance image contrast, apply smoothing, fill holes, remove small components, and perform morphological closing. Additionally, images can be segmented in a batch-processing mode for efficient analysis of large datasets. For low-contrast imaging data, the panel offers flexibility by allowing the import of binary masks generated using external segmentation tools32,61. Users can also utilise the mask-overlay tool to visually assess the segmentation quality on their original image. When ground-truth masks are available, the panel provides quantitative metrics to evaluate segmentation performance, including IoU, F1 score, precision and recall.

Pre-processing panel

The Pre-processing panel provides a toolkit for image-denoising, background subtraction and image calibration for multispectral imaging data. It allows tuning parameter settings on a per-channel basis. The panel can also import parameters from a previous session to aid with workflow reproducibility. Additionally, a Quality subpanel is included that can evaluate the performance of the pre-processing method using various metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), mean squared error (MSE), etc. (see Manual Section 5.4 for a complete list). Additionally, users can pre-process multiple files using the batch-processing option.

Image denoising: the image denoising option consists of mean and median filters, wavelet-filters such as the biorthogonal Cohen–Daubechies–Feauveau (CDF9/7)10, spline (bior) wavelets62 and a pre-trained grayscale denoising neural network (DnCnn). Additionally, First Order Roughness Penalty Denoising (FORPDN) method63 and Hyperspectral restoration (HyRes)64 method are included specifically for multispectral data (see Supplementary Table 1).

Background subtraction and calibration: this option allows users to import their own reference files to perform background subtraction and image calibration31. The built-in method then subtracts the background and calibrates the imaging data according to Eq. 1.

Here, p represents the pixel index and c the specific imaging channel, y is the original image (or pre-processed image if previously performed), ybkg is the background image, ycal represents the calibration image and fc is the calibration factor for each channel (see Manual Section 5.3 for details). Depending on their requirements, users can edit the calibration formula or avail other options to further reduce the background, such as setting the median intensity of predefined background regions to zero, subtracting a background image achieved by Gaussian convolution, bottom-hat, or top-hat filtering. Each parameter setting can be fine-tuned and visually assessed using the line plots and 3D surface plots.

Feature generation panel

This panel enables the generation of a feature table consisting of intensity, morphological, and texture features (see Manual Section 10.1 for a complete list of available features). To analyse temporal imaging data, this panel can additionally compute the difference, fold change, percentage change, percentage difference and rate of change in the intensity, morphology, and texture features between time points. Additionally, a feature table import option is available for users to use their own features or update them with those generated with SMIAL. Furthermore, multiple visualisation aids provided by the panel can help users compute feature distributions or overlay feature values on the underlying images.

Data cleaning and statistics panel

This panel’s comprehensive suite of analysis options including for:

Removal of outliers: this function removes outliers outside of a user-defined upper and lower thresholds (e.g. 1st and 99th percentiles). It also provides an option to remove negative intensity values and undefined values (or empty rows in a feature table).

Feature redundancy analysis: this functionality provides several criteria for assessing and removing redundant features, including zero entropy, zero standard deviation, insignificance, and pairwise Pearson or Spearman (for normally distributed or non-normally distributed data, respectively) correlation below a user-specified threshold. Additionally, it provides an option to remove observations or features containing undefined values (NaN), infinity values (e.g. division by zero) or empty values.

Feature analysis panel

With this panel, users can either build a new classification model or load a previously built SMIAL model (i.e. a pre-existing classification model) to test new data. The panel can implement both feature selection and classification using a range of standard methods for feature normalisation, selection, dimensionality reduction and supervised classifiers.

Feature normalisation: feature normalisation can be achieved with the following parameter settings: z-score, value normalisation, robust scaling and unit vector transformation.

Feature selection and dimensionality reduction: Feature selection can be performed using 15 supervised methods with an option to enter the desired number of features and a recommendation that this number does not exceed the square root of the smallest class size. The following methods are available: statistical feature selection including parametric (t-test, ANOVA) and non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon, Kruskal–Wallis), filter-based methods including Maximum Relevance and Minimum Redundancy (MRMR)65, Chi-Square test66, Gini index67, Laplacian68, ReliefF69, particle swarm algorithm based on Fisher’s distance70,71, Pearson ANOVA29, and entropy, Bhattacharyya, and ROC that ranks features based on class separability criteria72. Wrapper methods are also available, including forward feature selection73. A manual feature selection is also available to specify which features should be included in further analysis. Additionally, the panel can implement a multi-select function that ranks features based on their scores across multiple feature selection methods. Dimensionality reduction can be performed using methods such as PCA74,75, LDA, and sparse filtering76.

Classification: the classification subpanel is designed to analyse the features either generated from the feature generation panel or independently provided to it. It offers several classifiers for binary and multi-class classification, including eight supervised classification methods: linear SVM77, polynomial SVM78, k-nearest neighbours79, LDA79, logistic regression80, naïve bayes81, quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA)82 and random forest83. Two unsupervised classification methods are available: kMeans84 and fuzzy c-means clustering84, where the user can specify the number of clusters present in the data or determine it automatically. SMIAL supports hyperparameter tuning for all except QDA and the multi-classifier (comparison of all supervised classifiers). When enabled, hyperparameter optimisation is applied to each fold during k-fold cross-validation, ensuring the most suitable parameters are selected for each training subset. In cases of significant class imbalance, the classification panel provides upsampling methods including multiple adaptations of the synthetic minority over-sampling technique (SMOTE) and adaptive synthetic sampling (ADASYN) for the minority class, or random downsampling for the majority class to enhance classification, thus preventing a bias towards the majority class85.

A major challenge in machine learning is overfitting and poor generalisability. To address this, a choice of three validation methods is available: hold-out validation, k-fold cross-validation and leave-one-out validation86. In hold-out validation, the data is split into a training and a test dataset. The training data is used to build the classification model, while the test data assesses the classifier’s performance by applying the model to unseen data. The k-fold validation method partitions the data into k equal subsets termed as folds87,88, alternating between training on k-1 folds and testing on the remaining fold87,88. The resulting performance values from each iteration are averaged to show the algorithm’s overall predictive capabilities. Leave-one-out is similar to k-fold validation, but with k equal to the total number of objects in each class, and therefore, only suitable for small sample sizes87.

Following classification, there is a range of model performance metrics, including confusion matrix, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, diagnostic odds ratio, balanced accuracy, percentage of correctly labelled samples, false positive rate and negative and positive predicted value. Additionally, the panel computes a ROC curve and the corresponding AUC and precision-recall curve89.

Data acquisition

Cell data cell culturing: The h1RPE7 cell line (human retinal pigment epithelial cells, passage 18) was obtained from the European Collection of Cell Cultures (ECACC) and purchased from Cellbank Australia (Westmead, NSW, Australia). Retinal cells were subcultured and maintained in a complete culture medium comprising Ham’s F-10 Nutrient Mix (ThermoFisher, cat no. 11550043), (20%) heat inactivated foetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher, cat no. 10100147) and (1%) puromycin dihydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, product no. P8833). Melanoma cell lines COLO679 (87061210) and A375 (88113005) and fibroblast cell line 142BR (90011806) were obtained from CellBank Australia (Westmead, NSW, Australia). Melanoma A375 cells were subcultured and maintained in a complete culture medium comprising DMEM (high glucose, pyruvate, no glutamine; Gibco 10313021), FBS (15%, Gibco 26140-079) and (1%) Antibiotic-Antimycotic (ThermoFisher, cat no. 15240062). COLO679 cells were cultured in a complete medium comprising RPMI 1640 with L-Glutamine (Gibco), FBS (10%), and (1%) Antibiotic-Antimycotic. 142 BR fibroblasts were cultured in a medium comprising Minimum Essential Medium Eagle (EMEM + non-essential amino acids, Sigma-Aldrich M5650) combined with 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, 22400089), foetal bovine serum (15%), and (1%) Antibiotic-Antimycotic.

Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator and passaged when cells reached a confluency of 80%. Cells were washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (ThermoFisher, cat no. 14190250) and trypsinised with TrypLE (GIBCO, Australia, cat no. 12604013). Following incubation with trypsin for 5 min at 37 °C CO2, a complete medium was added to trypsinised cells. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in the complete medium. Cells were subcultured in 35 mm glass dishes (~105 cells/dish in 1 mL complete medium) a day prior to treatment. Upon adherence to the glass dish, retinal cells were treated with 10 µM rotenone. Rotenone was prepared by initially supplementing 10 mg rotenone (Sigma-Aldrich, product no. R8875) with 2.5 mL DMSO. Subsequently, 10 µL of this solution was added to 10 mL of cell medium, and the cells were exposed to 1 mL of this solution. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and 72 h. A h1RPE7 control group was similarly seeded into 35 mm glass dishes with 18 mm wells and # 1.5 cover slip bottoms (Cell E&G, USA, and # GDB0004-200) at the same density.

Imaging: Glass dishes with retinal cells in 1 mL of Hanks Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, ThermoFisher, product no. 88284) were used for autofluorescence imaging. Hyperspectral autofluorescence imaging of cells was performed at a spatial resolution of 0.09 µm. The Andor iXon Ultra 888 EMCCD (ANDOR, Oxford Instruments) was used, with a 150x oil (NA 1.45) objective (UAPON150XOTIRF, Olympus). The microscope system (Olympus FV3000, Shinjuku, Tokyo, Japan) is equipped with LEDs and fluorescent filter cubes of various excitation and emission wavelength ranges. The system was operated below −65 °C. The image acquisition parameters for imaging retinal cells are summarised in Supplementary Table 2.

Image pre-processing: retinal cell images were smoothed using a median filter with a parameter of 5 before applying flattening and calibration. Hyperspectral autofluorescence images of the fibroblast cell line 142BR and melanoma cell lines COLO679 and A375 were pre-processed using the HyRes algorithm. Cell masks were generated using Ilastik5,32 for retinal cell data and hand-drawn for the melanoma data. Additionally, these images were calibrated29.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical testing was measured using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test for two-group comparison (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001) and Spearman correlation. To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were conducted using a minimum of three independent imaging dishes. For the melanoma dataset analysis, a total of 471 cells from the 142BR line, 570 cells from COLO679, and 844 cells from A375 were imaged. For the mitochondrial network analysis, the following sample sizes were analysed: 1945 networks from 24-h control, 1889 from 24-h rotenone-treated, 2522 from 72-h control retinal cells, and 1793 from 72-h rotenone-treated retinal cells.
