Anticholinergic Burden at Admission and Discharge Among Geriatric Inpatients: A Single-Center Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Ayesha Akhtar, Fatima Balquis, Shazia Jamshed, Zirwa Asim, Palanisamy Sivanandy, Mohamed Izham Mohamed Ibrahim, Adeel Aslam, Mudassar Iqbal Arain, Ayesha Iqbal, Wajid Syed, Mahmood Basil A Al-Rawi, Naji Alqahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnticholinergic medications are widely prescribed in older adults and contribute to cumulative anticholinergic burden (ACB), which is associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Hospitalized geriatric patients frequently experience multimorbidity and polypharmacy, increasing the risk of elevated ACB. Data from low- and middle-income settings, particularly at transitions of care, remain limited.ObjectiveTo assess prescribing patterns of medications with anticholinergic properties and quantify anticholinergic burden at admission and discharge among geriatric inpatients, and to evaluate its association with polypharmacy and clinical predictors.MethodsA retrospective observational cohort study was conducted in the cardiology and nephrology departments of a tertiary care hospital in Islamabad, Pakistan, from January 2021 to December 2022. Patients aged ≥60 years with complete medication records were included. Anticholinergic burden was measured using the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) scale.ResultsA total of 1100 patients were analyzed (median age 65 years). Polypharmacy increased from 49.5% at admission to 84.5% at discharge (p<0.001). The proportion of patients with ACB ≥1 rose from 36.1% to 43.6% (p<0.001), and the median ACB score increased from 1 to 2 (p<0.001). Heart failure, renal disorders, higher comorbidity count, and longer hospital stay were independently associated with higher ACB at discharge. Male gender was associated with lower odds of ACB.ConclusionAnticholinergic burden and polypharmacy are common and increase during hospitalization among geriatric patients. Targeted medication review at admission and discharge may help reduce cumulative anticholinergic exposure.

Introduction

Global population ageing has led to a substantial increase in the proportion of individuals aged 65 years and older worldwide.1 Older adults frequently present with multimorbidity, which often necessitates polypharmacy—commonly defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications—for appropriate disease management.2 Although polypharmacy is frequently clinically justified, it is associated with an increased risk of adverse drug events, drug–drug interactions, and cumulative anticholinergic burden (ACB).3 Age-related physiological changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, together with multimorbidity and prolonged medication exposure, further heighten susceptibility to medication-related harm in this population.4 Increased permeability of the blood–brain barrier with advancing age may facilitate central nervous system penetration of anticholinergic agents, thereby increasing the risk of cognitive impairment and delirium.4

Anticholinergic medications are commonly prescribed for a wide range of indications but exert their effects through blockade of muscarinic receptors, leading to adverse outcomes such as constipation, dry mouth, urinary retention, dizziness, confusion, cognitive impairment, and falls.5 The cumulative effect of exposure to multiple anticholinergic medications is quantified as anticholinergic burden, which more accurately reflects clinical risk than exposure to individual agents alone. Several tools have been developed to quantify ACB, including the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) Scale, the Anticholinergic Risk Scale (ARS), and the Duran Scale.‎6

However, variability among these instruments can influence prevalence estimates and associations with clinical outcomes. Recent systematic reviews and cohort studies have demonstrated the clinical relevance of ACB in hospitalized older adults, with higher ACB scores consistently associated with adverse outcomes such as delirium, cognitive decline, and mortality.7 Notably, studies comparing multiple ACB instruments have shown that certain scales exhibit stronger predictive validity for cognitive outcomes in hospitalized elderly populations.8

Previous research indicates that up to 46.9% of older adults are exposed to medications with anticholinergic properties,9 while international studies report wide variability in prevalence during hospitalization. For example, studies have shown that between 81.5% and 90.2% of hospitalized older adults receive at least one medication with anticholinergic properties during their inpatient stay.10 Despite increasing recognition of anticholinergic burden in high-income healthcare settings, evidence from low- and middle-income countries remains limited, including Pakistan. In particular, data describing anticholinergic prescribing patterns among hospitalized older adults in cardiology and nephrology services are scarce.‎11 These departments typically manage patients with high medication burdens, including cardiovascular agents and renally cleared drugs that may accumulate in older adults and contribute to increased anticholinergic exposure. Given Pakistan’s rapidly ageing population and rising prevalence of chronic diseases, understanding anticholinergic prescribing practices in hospitalized elderly patients is essential to inform safer medication use and guide future interventions.

Methodology

Study Design

A retrospective observational cohort study was conducted to evaluate the real-world associations between anticholinergic burden and polypharmacy among geriatric inpatients. Retrospective study design could give us a snapshot of prescribing trends and discrepancies with anti-cholinergic guidelines in a limited time that’s why this study design was preferred.

Study Period

Study was conducted from Jan 2021–Dec 2022 retrospectively in the cardiology and nephrology departments.

Study Setting

The study was conducted at a Joint Commission International (JCI)-accredited private tertiary care facility in Islamabad, Pakistan. It is a quaternary care hospital with cutting edge technology and healthcare advancements to provide quality healthcare. As it is a JCI accredited hospital so everything is guideline based and appropriate, yet we found a gap for improving anti-cholinergic prescribing.

Population and Sampling

Our targeted population was patients receiving anti-cholinergic therapy in the cardiology and nephrology departments and they meet the elderly criteria. Time base sampling technique was used for the author’s convenience. Medical records were reviewed from nephrology (initial n=652; exclusions n=152; final n=500) and cardiology (initial n=1108; exclusions n=508; final n=600) departments.

Patient Selection Criteria

In-patients of nephrology and cardiology at the department of Shifa International Hospital were included. Consecutive patients aged ≥60 years with complete medical record of the prescribed medications and diagnosed with at least one chronic condition like hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular and chronic kidney diseases (CKD) were also included. Exclusions comprised Alzheimer’s/severe dementia, active malignancy, transplant list status, severe trauma/end-stage organ failure/chronic pain/critical illness/ICU/palliative care, and those whose medication data could not be evaluated at discharge or in-hospital death. Patients with missing or incomplete electronic medical records were also excluded from the study.

Ethical Approval

Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and Ethics Committee of Shifa International Hospital and Shifa Tameer-e-Millat University (IRB# 078–24). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki. Patient consent form is not required for retrospective study in Pakistan. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout the study. All medical records were anonymized prior to analysis, and no identifiable patient information was collected, stored, or reported.

Data Collection

The data was collected directly from the medical record of patients by reviewing the patient record file from medical record to obtain the information of socio-demographics details including gender, age and weight. Details of comorbidities such as hypertension, Diabetes Mellitus, Chronic Kidney Diseases and others diseases which were reported based on physician diagnosed conditions. These comorbid conditions were chosen based on the burden of diseases among elderly in Pakistan.

Measuring Anticholinergic Burden

Anticholinergic burden refers to the cumulative exposure to anticholinergic effects resulting from the use of one or more medications with anticholinergic properties. In this study, it was determined using the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden Scale (ACBS), as described by Boustani and colleagues. The ACBS was selected because it is one of the most widely validated tools internationally, allowing easier comparison of our findings with the existing literature.

According to this scale, medications are classified into three categories based on their anticholinergic activity: scores of 1, 2, and 3. A score of 1 is assigned to drugs with minimal anticholinergic activity, a score of 2 to drugs with definite anticholinergic effects, and a score of 3 to drugs with strong anticholinergic activity and a high potential to contribute to overall anticholinergic burden.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 27). To assess the normality of continuous variables, the Shapiro–Wilk test was utilized. Since several continuous variables exhibited non-normal distributions, these data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%).

Polypharmacy was analyzed as a binary variable (yes/no) based on a predefined threshold of five or more medications (≥5). Anticholinergic burden was measured using the total Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden (ACB) score and was further categorized into two groups: those with anticholinergic burden (ACB score ≥1) and those without burden (ACB score = 0). Changes between admission and discharge were analyzed using paired statistical tests. Specifically, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied to compare paired ACB total scores, while the McNemar test (with continuity correction) was used to compare paired binary outcomes, including polypharmacy status and anticholinergic burden status. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To identify predictors of anticholinergic burden at both admission and discharge, univariable logistic regression analyses were initially performed to estimate crude odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Variables that showed statistical significance (p-value < 0.05) were subsequently included in multivariable logistic regression models to obtain adjusted ORs and their corresponding 95% CIs. The results of the models are reported as both crude and adjusted ORs, along with their respective 95% CIs and p-values. No imputation was carried out since the dataset contained no missing values for the primary variables included in the analyses.

Discussion

In this study, we identified three major findings. First, both polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden were common at admission and rose substantially by discharge. Second, the median total ACB score rose from admission to discharge, and the proportion of patients with any anticholinergic burden also rose. Third, diagnosis category, comorbidity burden, length of stay, and polypharmacy status were all independent predictors of anticholinergic burden.

The prevalence of anticholinergic burden in our population is comparable to that of previous studies ‎9 obtained the same results in his study. We observed a substantial rise in both polypharmacy and anticholinergic burden from admission to discharge, with polypharmacy rising substantially and ACB burden rising significantly but less substantially. Our results are consistent with findings of a study ‎12 these observations and suggest that discharge prescribing practice may influence the cumulative anticholinergic burden. The shift in the distribution of ACB scores to higher categories at discharge is important in the light of previous outcome studies. Several cohort studies and comparative scale analyses have reported the same findings. Although our study did not investigate clinical outcomes, the shift in the distribution of ACB scores. ‎13

Diagnosis-specific associations were also observed. Heart failure and renal disorders were independently associated with higher odds of anticholinergic burden at both admission and discharge, whereas ischemic heart disease was associated with lower odds after adjustment at admission. Prior studies have similarly reported that multimorbidity clusters and specialty service type are associated with differences in anticholinergic exposure.14 Longer length of hospital stay was associated with higher odds of anticholinergic burden at discharge. This association has also been described in prior inpatient studies. ‎15 From a clinical perspective, our findings support the potential value of structured medication review at both admission and discharge, with specific attention to cumulative anticholinergic exposure. Prior interventional and deprescribing studies suggest that pharmacist-led medication reconciliation and targeted review of anticholinergic agents can reduce exposure and may improve selected outcomes, although high-quality randomized evidence remains limited.‎16 Several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective single-center design limits causal inference and generalizability. Second, anticholinergic burden was estimated using one validated scale; different scales may yield different burden estimates and associations. Third, drug-to-ACB classification is subject to potential misclassification, which could bias estimates of exposure. Fourth, we did not include medication dose, duration, or adherence, which may modify clinical risk. Fifth, clinical outcomes such as delirium, falls, or readmissions were not analyzed, so outcome associations cannot be inferred. These limitations can be addressed in future prospective, multicenter studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that anticholinergic burden and polypharmacy are common among geriatric inpatients in cardiology and nephrology services and tend to increase between admission and discharge. Anticholinergic burden was associated with multiple comorbidities, multiple diagnosis, and length of stay. These findings add data from an LMIC regional setting and support the need for prospective studies and targeted medication review strategies to evaluate whether reducing anticholinergic burden improves patient-centered outcomes.
