The burden of ovarian cancer attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia from 1990 to 2021: an analysis of the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Yuanyi Yu, Wei Zhang, Ting Deng, Na Li, Jianchi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOvarian cancer (OC) ranks among the deadliest gynecological malignancies worldwide. Occupational asbestos exposure has been linked to an elevated risk of OC. Asia accounts for a substantial share of global asbestos consumption; nevertheless, regional analyses focusing on OC burden attributable to occupational asbestos exposure under the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework remain scarce.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure across Asia and delineate its temporal trends from 1990 to 2021.MethodsData on all-age deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), age-standardized rates, and crude rates of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure among Asian females were retrieved from the 2021 GBD dataset and its comparative risk assessment framework for subsequent burden analyses. The Age-Period-Cohort (APC) model was adopted to characterize long-term trends of the disease burden, although decomposition analysis was performed to quantify the respective contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological shifts.ResultsBetween 1990 and 2021, the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia declined from 0.41 per 10,000 population to 0.37 per 10,000 population, with an estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) of − 0.29 (95% CI − 0.52, − 0.06). Meanwhile, the age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) decreased from 7.89 per 10,000 population to 6.74 per 10,000 population (EAPC = − 0.50, 95% CI − 0.66, − 0.33). Over the study period, the total number of deaths and DALYs increased by 161.10% and 131.38%, respectively. Marked regional disparities were observed: ASMR and ASDR trended upward in South and Southeast Asia, declined in high-income Asia–Pacific regions, and stayed relatively stable across East Asia, with Central Asia bearing the highest overall disease burden. In terms of age distribution, OC burden increased with advancing age, peaked in the 70–74 age group and then declined gradually. Both crude mortality rate (CMR) and crude DALY rate (CDR) exhibited rising tendencies among the elderly population. APC analysis revealed that the age effects for mortality and DALY rates increased among middle-aged and older adults but decreased in the advanced age groups. Period effects presented an overall upward trend, whereas cohort effects declined generally. Decomposition analysis identified population aging and population expansion as the primary drivers of the growth in absolute disease burden, and epidemiological transitions exerted a mitigating effect on the overall OC burden.ConclusionsBased on estimations from the GBD 2021 framework, the age-standardized burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure has fallen across Asia. However, the absolute burden continues to climb due to population growth and aging, accompanied by prominent regional heterogeneity. Targeted interventions regarding data surveillance, exposure regulation, and occupational health policies are therefore urgently required for future prevention and control.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00998-4.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer (OC) is one of the highly aggressive gynecological malignancies, often marked by nonspecific symptoms that frequently lead to late-stage diagnosis and poor prognosis [1, 2]. As recorded in the latest global cancer burden data from 2022, 324,398 individuals were newly diagnosed with OC resulting in 206,839 deaths[3], ​​positioning it as the eighth most prevalent cause of​​ cancer fatalities among women[4]. Asia, with its vast territory, large population, and significant disparities in economic development, exhibits a complex pattern of OC burden. A previous ecological study across Asia using GLOBOCAN 2012 data indicated that China, India, Indonesia, Japan and Pakistan recorded the largest number of new OC cases in Asia in 2012, collectively accounting for approximately 76% of all cases on this continent. By contrast, the highest age-standardized incidence rates were observed in Singapore, Kazakhstan, Brunei, Armenia, and Japan. This study also demonstrated a significant positive correlation between the age-standardized incidence rate of OC and the Human Development Index (HDI) (r = 0.143, p = 0.006), although no significant correlation was detected between the age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) and HDI (r = 0.005, p = 0.975) [5]. Accordingly, population composition, cancer registration quality, diagnostic capacity, access to treatment and socioeconomic development status should all be taken into account when interpreting the disparities in OC burden between high-income economies such as Japan and Singapore and certain developing regions in South and Southeast Asia. Further investigation into risk factors associated with OC is essential to formulate effective prevention and intervention measures.

Asbestos is a fibrous mineral widely utilized in construction, manufacturing, textile production, and daily consumer goods due to its flame-retardant and insulating properties [6]. Long-term occupational exposure to asbestos poses substantial hazards to human health. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified all types of asbestos as Group 1 carcinogens. Malignancies with conclusive causal links to asbestos exposure encompass malignant mesothelioma, lung cancer, laryngeal cancer, and OC. Malignant mesothelioma can develop in serous tissues including the pleura, peritoneum, pericardium, and tunica vaginalis testis [7, 8]. Asbestos fibers primarily enter the human body via the respiratory tract and migrate to the abdominal cavity through lymphatic vessels or mucosal tissues. These fibers are implicated in chronic inflammation of ovarian tissues [9], which may subsequently elevate the risk of OC development. Meta-analyses have indicated that women with occupational asbestos exposure face approximately a twofold higher risk of receiving an OC diagnosis or dying from this disease [10]. A body of existing studies further corroborates the association between asbestos exposure and OC [11, 12].

Over the past decades, numerous Asian nations have undergone rapid industrialization. Booming sectors such as construction, manufacturing and mining have sustained high asbestos consumption for an extended period [13]. Relevant investigations have confirmed that Asia has emerged as the world’s leading asbestos-consuming region, accounting for roughly two-thirds of the global total consumption [14]. Although jurisdictions including Japan, South Korea, and Singapore have gradually imposed restrictions or full bans on asbestos use, asbestos mining, importation, production, and application persist in several other Asian countries. Previous literature has identified China and India as major consumers and importers of asbestos across the continent [14, 15]. Given the long latency period of asbestos-related disorders, the adverse health outcomes stemming from historical occupational exposure will continue to manifest for decades to come. As one of the malignancies associated with asbestos exposure, OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure has not yet undergone systematic burden assessment in Asia. In this context, evaluating the OC burden linked to occupational asbestos exposure across Asia using the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) estimation framework can deliver evidence for the formulation of regional prevention and control strategies.

This study adopted the 2021 GBD dataset to explore temporal trends, age-specific distribution patterns, and underlying driving factors of the OC burden attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia from 1990 to 2021. The findings aim to provide evidence-based references for regional OC prevention and control as well as occupational exposure governance.

Methods

Data acquisition

We obtained data from GBD 2021 (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/, accessed November 20, 2024). GBD 2021 spans 204 countries and territories over the period 1990–2021, generating comprehensive burden estimates for 371 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors, encompassing data on disease outcomes, including cancers, as well as records of exposure to relevant risk factors and their attributable disease burden. Data extracted from GBD 2021 were as follows: (1) all-age deaths, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), ASMR, and age-standardized DALYs rate (ASDR) of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure among females across Asia, its five subregions (Central Asia, High-income Asia Pacific, South Asia, East Asia, and Southeast Asia), and the 34 corresponding countries and territories from 1990 to 2021; (2) the number of deaths, total DALYs, crude mortality rate (CMR), and crude DALYs rate (CDR) stratified by age groups.

GBD 2021 applies a standardized modeling protocol to enhance the internal consistency and cross-regional and cross-temporal comparability of estimates. The Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm) is primarily used to compute mortality from specific causes, although non-fatal outcomes are modeled via DisMod-MR 2.1. As a Bayesian meta-regression tool, DisMod-MR 2.1 synthesizes multi-source data derived from registries, surveys, published literature, and surveillance systems. On this basis, the Comparative Risk Assessment (CRA) framework of GBD is adopted to quantify the disease burden attributable to various risk factors. For occupational asbestos exposure, the asbestos impact ratio (AIR) is employed to indirectly infer occupational asbestos exposure patterns across nations and time periods. Combined with relative risk (RR) values from existing epidemiological studies, the population attributable fraction (PAF) is calculated to estimate OC deaths and DALYs attributable to occupational asbestos exposure.

It should be noted that the GBD database does not provide individual-level raw measurements of occupational exposure by country, occupational category, or year. For regions and countries lacking direct occupational exposure surveillance, cancer registration, or cause-of-death reporting data, estimates of disease outcomes, exposure levels, and attributable burden rely on standardized modeling, covariate adjustment, spatiotemporal extrapolation, and uncertainty propagation. Accordingly, the exposure indicators and attributable burden presented in this study represent population-level estimates generated under the GBD modeling framework, rather than directly measured individual occupational exposure or causal judgements for individual cases. Uncertainty intervals for GBD estimates are produced through posterior sampling, and the 95% uncertainty interval (UI) provided by GBD is reported in the present study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

The temporal trends in the burden of OC from 1990 to 2021 were measured with age-standardized rates (ASRs) and estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs). To further examine the temporal trends of ASRs, a linear regression model was constructed by fitting the natural logarithm of ASRs against calendar years. The model is specified as: Y = α + βX + ε, where Y = ln (ASR), X = year, β indicates positive or negative trend, and ε denotes the error term. If the 95% CI of the EAPC includes 0, ASR trend is considered steady.

This study analyzed deaths, DALYs, ASMR, ASDR and EAPC of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure across Asian regions, countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 to evaluate temporal trends in disease burden. Age-specific distributions of the corresponding OC burden in Asia were additionally presented for 1990 and 2021.

APC analysis

The APC model, built upon the Poisson distribution, is applied to characterize temporal trends of disease burden stratified by age, period, and cohort. The age effect reflects disparities in OC mortality and DALYs rates attributable to occupational asbestos exposure across distinct age groups. The period effect captures shifts in social conditions, historical contexts, diagnostic practices, and exposure environments that affect all age groups concurrently. The cohort effect indicates variations in exposure backgrounds and disease risks among different birth cohorts, which are determined by subtracting age from the observation period. A linear correlation exists among age, period, and cohort, leading to inherent non-identifiability in the APC model and hindering fully independent estimation of the three effects [16].

The Intrinsic Estimator (IE) proposed by Yang, Fu and colleagues enables the estimation of age, period and cohort effects under specific constraints and yields relatively robust parameter outputs. Nevertheless, the IE approach cannot fundamentally resolve the non-identifiability issue stemming from the linear dependence among age, period and cohort in the APC model, so relevant results should be interpreted as population-level trend evidence. In this study, two-factor and three-factor models were constructed. The two-factor models included age-period, age-cohort, and period-cohort models, which were fitted via Poisson log-linear regression. The three-factor model referred to the APC model, whose parameters were estimated using conventional constrained Poisson log-linear estimators and the IE algorithm [17].

In the APC model with the IE method, age, period and cohort were grouped into consecutive 5 year intervals. Age was divided into 16 groups (20–24 years, 25–29 years, …, 95 + years), period into 6 groups (1992–1996, 1997–2001, …, 2017–2021), and birth cohort into 21 groups (1897–1901, 1902–1906, …, 1997–2001). These groupings were used to estimate the age, period, and cohort effects on OC mortality and DALYs rates attributable to occupational asbestos exposure. The outputs of the APC model primarily describe long-term trends and variations in RR at the population level. Given that the estimates rely on age, period, and cohort grouping schemes, as well as the assumptions of the IE method, these results shall not be used alone for individual-level causal inference.

Decomposition analysis

To identify the primary drivers of changes in the absolute burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure from 1990 to 2021, decomposition analysis was applied to quantify the contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological shifts to variations in deaths and DALYs. Each factor was assessed independently while holding the other two constant. The Das Gupta method was used to decompose changes in deaths and DALYs associated with occupational asbestos-related OC across Asia and its five subregions. This decomposition analysis reflects the contributions of demographic and epidemiological changes to absolute burden trends under counterfactual scenarios, and does not represent causal effect estimates for specific policies or individual exposure alterations.

Results

Temporal trends in the burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia, 1990–2021

East Asia exhibited relative stability in both measures, with EAPCs of -0.05 (95% CI − 0.24, 0.13) and -0.10 (95% CI − 0.27, 0.07). Discussion

This study adopted the comparative risk assessment framework of the GBD 2021 to evaluate the burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia from 1990 to 2021. Notably, the attributable burden reported herein is estimated via GBD models. It reflects regionally comparable trends under established model assumptions and available data, rather than direct measurements of individual occupational exposure or causal determination for individual cases. Overall, ASMR and ASDR declined across Asia, whereas the absolute number of deaths and DALYs continued to increase, indicating divergent trends between age-standardized rates and absolute burden. Such disparities may be jointly driven by population size, age structure, regional disparities, socioeconomic development, and variations in data sources.

The coexistence of decreasing ASRs and increasing deaths and DALYs is not contradictory. Adjusted by standard population structures, ASMR and ASDR primarily reflect long-term changes in disease burden per capita. In contrast, the absolute counts of deaths and DALYs are affected by both population expansion and population aging. Between 1990 and 2021, the growth and aging of female populations in Asia enlarged the pool of elderly individuals at high risk of OC, which may be a major contributor to the rising deaths and DALYs. Previous GBD studies have also demonstrated that age-standardized OC rates may decline, yet population growth and demographic shifts can still drive up the absolute burden[18–20]. Consistent with these findings, our decomposition analysis confirmed that population growth and aging were the leading drivers of increased deaths and DALYs. Meanwhile, the gradual restriction or ban on asbestos use, together with strengthened occupational safety management and exposure surveillance in some countries, has likely reduced occupational asbestos exposure levels, which helps explain the downward trend in age-standardized attributable burden[21, 22].

Regional disparities can be interpreted from three perspectives. First, age distribution shapes absolute burden. Regions with a larger proportion of elderly populations tend to have higher OC-related deaths and DALYs. Prior GBD-based decomposition analyses have also identified population growth and aging as key factors driving the rising absolute burden of cancers, particularly gynecological malignancies, in Asia[23, 24]. Second, disparities in the history of asbestos use and regulatory policies lead to varying long-term occupational exposure backgrounds. For instance, countries and regions including Japan, South Korea, and Singapore have progressively restricted or banned asbestos, while some nations in South and Southeast Asia still face challenges such as high costs for asbestos substitution, inadequate occupational safety regulation, and insufficient exposure monitoring[14, 15, 25, 26]. Third, socioeconomic development affects the quality of cancer registration, pathological diagnosis, accessibility to treatment, and capacity for occupational disease identification[27]. It should be noted that no routine screening program has been proven effective in reducing OC mortality among the general population. The United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) does not recommend routine OC screening for asymptomatic women without known high-risk genetic syndromes. Therefore, the advantages of high-income regions in OC burden control are mainly manifested in stronger diagnostic capacity, superior cancer registration quality, reliable pathological confirmation, wider treatment access, and comprehensive disease management[28]. Furthermore, regional gaps cannot be simply attributed to differences in asbestos exposure. OC risk is also associated with age, genetic susceptibility, family history, reproductive and hormonal factors, obesity, and other conditions[29, 30]. Additional occupational and environmental exposures may also correlate with OC risk[31, 32]. These factors often coexist with asbestos exposure across different occupational groups. Accordingly, the regional disparities observed in this study represent comprehensive estimated differences under the GBD attributable framework, rather than direct consequences of variations in asbestos exposure alone.

Our results revealed that the overall burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure increased substantially with age across Asia, peaked in the 70–74 years age group, and then declined gradually. CMR and CDR increased markedly among the oldest age groups. Asbestos-related diseases generally have a long latency period[21, 33]. The peak burden in the 70–74 years group may correspond to occupational asbestos exposure occurring in the mid-to-late twentieth century. Earlier research indicated that Asia accounted for approximately 33% of global asbestos consumption between 1971 and 2000[21]. In addition, advancing age is accompanied by gradual deterioration of immune function, which impairs the body’s ability to monitor and eliminate tumor cells[34]. Meanwhile, a heavier comorbidity burden and diminished physiological reserve may elevate the risks of OC-associated death and disability[35], partly explaining the elevated CMR and CDR in elderly populations.

The APC model illustrated complex temporal dynamics of OC burden attributable to occupational asbestos exposure. In terms of age effect, the RRs of death and DALYs from OC generally increased with age, which aligns with the age distribution findings and confirms aging as a critical demographic factor driving the attributable burden upward. The decline in both risks among the oldest populations may be attributed to competing mortality, increased comorbidities, and uncertainties in diagnosis and cause-of-death attribution among the elderly[36, 37]. A rising period effect was observed for both mortality and DALYs rates, with the RR of mortality climbing from 0.30 in 1992–1996 to 3.10 in 2017–2021. This trend may be linked to the peak asbestos consumption in Asia from the late twentieth century to the early twenty-first century and the long latency of related diseases[21]. It may also be influenced by improvements in diagnostic capabilities, as well as changes in the comparability, completeness, validity of cancer registry data and quality of cause-of-death coding[38, 39]. The cohort effect showed a continuous downward trend, with the relative mortality risk dropping to 0.01 for the 1997–2001 birth cohort. This is likely because later birth cohorts had fewer opportunities for occupational asbestos exposure, shorter exposure duration, and have not yet reached the peak age for disease onset, alongside the implementation of asbestos control policies in multiple countries. For example, South Korea banned the use of crocidolite and amosite in 1997 and all forms of asbestos after 2009[22, 26], while Japan launched a full phase-out of asbestos in 2002[25]. Given the non-identifiability issue of APC models arising from linear correlations among age, period, and cohort variables, these results should be interpreted as population-level trend indications.

From a public health perspective, sustained attention is required for the burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia. Despite the overall decline in age-standardized burden, population growth and aging may keep the absolute number of deaths and DALYs at a high level in the future[40]. Promoting asbestos substitution, refining occupational safety standards, strengthening occupational exposure surveillance, and facilitating the sharing of cancer registry data can support more accurate assessment of health risks associated with occupational asbestos exposure and the development of regional prevention and control strategies[41, 42]. Considering the long latency of asbestos-related disorders, the disease burden stemming from historical exposure will persist for a long time even in countries that have enforced asbestos bans[33].

This study has several limitations. First, all analyses were based on the modeled estimates from GBD 2021, rather than direct measurements of individual occupational exposure or paired case-exposure data. Asian countries vary greatly in the completeness of cancer registration, cause-of-death reporting, occupational disease monitoring, and occupational exposure assessment. For regions with sparse data, GBD relies on spatiotemporal modeling, covariates, and extrapolation to generate estimates. Therefore, cross-country comparisons and rankings should be interpreted with caution, and these results are more suitable for describing regional trends and generating public health hypotheses[43, 44]. Second, this study adopted overall estimates of occupational asbestos exposure, without distinguishing between chrysotile, amphibole asbestos, and other types. Stratification by exposure intensity, duration, or specific occupational groups was also not feasible. Divergent asbestos regulations and occupational exposure standards across countries may compromise the comparability of risk estimates. Third, the PAF was calculated based on relative risk estimates from existing epidemiological studies. OC risk is modulated by reproductive factors, obesity, genetic predisposition and other occupational or environmental hazards. Previous studies have suggested elevated OC risks linked to certain occupations and workplace exposures[32]. Hence, this study cannot fully quantify the impacts of other occupational exposures, occupational categories, co-exposure patterns, and unmeasured confounders on asbestos-attributable burden estimates. Fourth, both the APC model and decomposition analysis are population-level descriptive approaches. Subject to model assumptions, grouping strategies, and ecological bias, they cannot be used to infer individual-level risks or causal effects of specific policies. Future research should integrate detailed occupational exposure surveillance, data on asbestos type and intensity, occupational categorization and high-quality cancer registry data to achieve more precise assessment of OC attributable burden.

Conclusion

Based on the modeling estimates and comparative risk assessment framework of GBD 2021, the overall age-standardized burden of OC attributable to occupational asbestos exposure in Asia declined from 1990 to 2021. Nevertheless, the absolute numbers of deaths and DALYs kept rising driven by population growth and aging, with substantial heterogeneity across regions and countries. APC analysis and decomposition analysis indicated that age structure, period changes, birth cohort disparities, and demographic factors jointly shaped trends in this attributable burden. Given uncertainties regarding data sources, exposure assessment, and model extrapolation in GBD estimates, the findings should primarily serve as a reference for regional public health trend monitoring and occupational exposure management. Future efforts should strengthen surveillance of occupational asbestos exposure, cancer registration, and occupational disease data sharing, and advance asbestos substitution policies, so as to more accurately evaluate and gradually reduce the OC burden attributable to occupational asbestos exposure.
