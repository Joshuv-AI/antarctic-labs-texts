Continuing problem of alcohol misuse and amplified health risk behaviors
Authors: Alice M Ellyson, Frederick Rivara
Published: 2026

Alcohol has long been shown to be an important risk factor for both the occurrence of trauma, as well as complications and higher mortality among those injured. The large national study by Brock and colleagues1 in this issue of the journal adds another consequence of drinking: lower helmet use among bicyclists. There was a clear dose effect of intoxication with helmet use, with 45.9% of sober cyclists wearing helmets compared with 9.9% of cyclists with solely alcohol intoxication, and only 6.2% of cyclists who were intoxicated by both alcohol and drugs. These findings coincide with other behaviors (eg, firearm use, multiple sexual partners) that illustrate the use of alcohol in amplifying risks of adverse health outcomes in multiple domains.2

Helmet use is important, with estimates that it can reduce the risk of brain injury by as much as 88%;3 it is effective in crashes that involve motor vehicles and can also protect against facial injury.

The findings of this study are even more important now with increased e-Bike and e-Scooter use in cities across the country and around the world.4 The data used in the Brock report were for 2017–2023; the use of motorized micromobility devices has skyrocketed since then. Many of these devices are rented for short rides, markedly reducing the likelihood of helmet use. A recent meta-analysis reported that only 2.4% of patients were reported to be wearing helmets.5 Their use not only poses dangers to riders but also to pedestrians, since users often ride on sidewalks. E-Bikes and E-Scooters attain higher speeds than pedal cyclists, which results in higher energy forces in the event of crashes, generating a higher risk of severe injury compared with pedal cyclists.6 In addition, nearly one-third of e-scooter riders were intoxicated at the time of injury.5

Does alcohol intoxication “cause” helmet non-use? Yes and no. Impairment by alcohol and/or drugs can certainly affect judgment and make individuals less likely to wear a helmet. On the other hand, individuals who have chronic substance abuse are less likely to engage in preventive health behaviors and thus would be less likely to engage in helmet use, regardless of intoxication.7

There are a number of important messages for this study. First, asking patients about helmet use and screening for substance misuse are critically important in trauma patients. Second, the rise in the use of powered micromobility devices and the corresponding lower use of helmets demands that different interventions be developed, such as interlock breathalyzer devices on them, and require that manufacturers and cities be held at least partly responsible for the injuries incurred.
