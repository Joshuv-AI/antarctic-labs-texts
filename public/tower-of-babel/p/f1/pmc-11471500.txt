Cryptococcus neoformans osteomyelitis of the radius treatment with antifungal calcium sulphate: A case report and literature review
Authors: Yanwei Li, Jinhui Zhang, Lianchi Lü, Haisen Hong, Qingfen Wang, Changqing Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCryptococcus neoformans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen widely distributed in nature, commonly found in soil and bird droppings such as pigeons. Cryptococcal osteomyelitis is usually found in immunocompromised patients, in an unusual condition Cryptococcus invades the bone. It usually occurs as a part of disseminated infection and is rarely isolated, and isolated cryptococcal osteomyelitis of the radius is rarer.Case presentationWe report a case of a 41-year-old woman with no underlying disease who presented with progressive distal swelling of the left forearm. The patient was initially suspected by local doctors of a malignant bone tumor. For further diagnosis, we performed a computed tomography-guided biopsy of the radius and found granulomatous inflammation with massive macrophage infiltration and a specific stain showing mycosis. Genetic testing revealed a high abundance of Cryptococcus neoformans. We performed debridement of the radius and filled the bone defect with antifungal calcium sulfate. Fluconazole was taken orally for 3 months after operation. No recurrence was found in the 15-month follow-up.ConclusionIt is the second case of Cryptococcus osteomyelitis as the only manifestation of extrapulmonary disease since 1973. It should be a safe and effective method to eliminate dead space, release antifungal drugs for a long time and increase bone stability by filling bone defect with antifungal drugs and calcium sulfate bone powder.

Cryptococcus osteomyelitis is an opportunistic infection that can lead to systemic fungal disease. It mainly occurs in patients with immunocompromised individuals such as HIV infection, malignant tumors, solid organ transplantation, connective tissue disease, and immunosuppressive therapy. Cryptococcus osteomyelitis mainly involves the spine, ribs, femur, tibia, clavicle, pelvis, and humerus, with the flat bone and metaphysis of long bone more common [21,44,81,109]. Generally, it is believed that Cryptococcus neoformans enter the human body mainly through the respiratory tract to cause pulmonary cryptococcosis, which is pulmonary and neurotropic [93]. Most cases involve a single bone lesion at a time. Osteomyelitis caused by cryptococcosis is mostly caused by Cryptococcus neoformans dissemination through the bloodstream, and can also be caused by a wound infection. The incidence of cryptococcosis in the immune normal population is very low, only about 1/100,000 [9], and only 164 cases have been reported in the literature since 1973 (Table 1). Moreover, the radius is a rare site of occurrence, and the first case of Cryptococcus osteomyelitis of the radius was reported in 2011 [104]. Case presentation

A 41-year-old female was admitted to the hospital due to swelling in her left forearm for over two weeks. The swelling in the distal part of her left forearm appeared without any obvious cause, and she did not experience any pain, skin ulcers, chills, fever, or other discomforts. The patient was initially treated with cephalosporin antibiotics for infection at a local hospital, but there was no significant improvement. She did not have any history of respiratory or gastrointestinal tract issues in the past month. Six months ago, she underwent surgery for uterine fibroids at the same local hospital. Additionally, two to four years ago, she received drug treatment, including ‘interferon,’ for viral hepatitis B.

Physical examination: The patient's general appearance was slightly thin, with a normal body temperature. Cardiovascular, pulmonary, and neurological examinations showed no significant abnormalities. The swelling in the distal dorsal area of her left forearm measured approximately 6cm × 3cm × 1cm. There were no visible bruises or varicose veins, and the skin temperature was slightly increased. The area was tender to touch, but there was no pain when pressing longitudinally.

Laboratory examination: white blood cell (WBC) 7.69 × 10 9/L, lymphocyte ratio 18.3 % (20.0%–50.0 %), high sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP) 7.95 mg/L (normal value 0.00–5.00 mg/L), procalcitonin-ER (PCT-ER) 0.02 ng/ml (0–0.1 ng/ml), erythrocyte sedimentation rate 36 mm/h (normal value 0–26 mm/h). Alkaline phosphatase 170.9 U/L (normal value 45.0–125.0). The results of immunology included HIV, syphilis and hepatitis C, were negative.

Imaging manifestations: X-ray showed irregular bone density decrease, cortical thinning, no periosteal reaction, a little callus growth at the ulnar edge, swelling, and increased density of surrounding soft tissue (Fig. 1). Following the identification of the pathogenic microorganism, the left radial osteomyelitis resection and antibiotic bone powder filling were performed. The radius defect was about 8cm × 1.2cm × 1.0cm after resection. To create a dough-like filler in the defect cavity, 1g of voriconazole, 10 cc of calcium sulfate bone powder (Bayside, UK@), and 5.5 ml of sterilized water for injection were combined. Finally, the patient was diagnosed with Cryptococcus neoformans osteomyelitis of the left radius and treated with fluconazole consolidation therapy for 12 weeks (oral fluconazole 200 mg bid). During the follow-up period, the erythema of the distal forearm gradually decreased. At the follow-up 3rd and 6th postoperative months, the patient's swelling had completely resolved and ESR, CRP, and WBC count were normal. Discussion

As a conditionally pathogenic fungus, Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii are two common species, among which Cryptococcus neoformans infection is the most common. Cryptococcus neoformans is widely distributed in nature and is commonly found in soil and bird feces such as pigeons. It is generally believed that Cryptococcus neoformans infection occurs mostly in immunocompromised hosts. However, with the improvement of diagnosis and treatment, more and more Cryptococcus neoformans infections in immunocompetent hosts have been reported in recent years, and osteomyelitis caused by Cryptococcus neoformans has no age preference [41].

The incidence of Cryptococcus infection is not high. By searching all English articles in PubMed since 1973, including the keywords “Cryptococcus” or “Cryptococcus” and “bone”, “bone” or “osteomyelitis”, excluding diffuse disease (more than 3 sites), we identified a total of 160 cases of Cryptococcus osteomyelitis, of which 31 cases occurred in the rib, followed by 24 cases in the spine (see Table 1). Isolated Cryptococcus osteomyelitis as the only manifestation of extrapulmonary disease is uncommon, usually associated with disseminated disease and immunodeficiency, while isolated cryptococcal osteomyelitis in the radius is even rarer in the literature.

The patient, in this case, had a dozen or so chickens in her own home, and pigeons were kept in the house of a neighbor a few dozen meters away. We suspect that the skin barrier around the lesions on the forearms or hands of the patient was compromised during poultry rearing, and therefore cryptococcal infection may have occurred through transdermal transmission into the radial bone marrow after contact with soil contaminated by birds such as pigeons. In addition, the patient was treated with interferon for chronic viral hepatitis B for 2–4 years before the onset of the disease, and it remains to be seen whether this contributed to the patient's immunocompromised status.

Antifungal therapy is widely acknowledged to be crucial in the management of cryptococcal osteomyelitis [110]. A course of therapy involving antifungals for cryptococcal meningitis according to the 2011 Infectious Diseases Society of America [111], intravenous amphotericin B and flucytosine should be the first line of treatment for diffuse cryptococcosis, with fluconazole to follow later. However, there is still some contention. First, prospective research on the best course of treatment for extrapulmonary cryptococcosis is lacking. Secondly, opinions regarding the duration of drug treatment are divided. Third, there is still debate regarding the best course of action, which involves surgery. Zhou et al. suggested against surgery for cryptococcal osteomyelitis, particularly in patients with compromised immune systems, as it may increase the risk of infection transmission [112]. At the same time, many cases have been successfully treated with non-surgical methods like the straightforward management of antibiotics [9,11,14,32,38,67,102]. Conversely, some researchers believe the best way to improve therapeutic outcomes is to combine antifungal medication with a thorough surgical debridement [79,93].

With the above problems, we conducted a second case report retrieval, excluding those literature reports that did not specify whether surgery, drugs, and treatment courses. In 1996 Wood et al. [96] published a review of all reported skeletal infections attributed to C. neoformans, which reviewed 44 reports on cryptococcal osteomyelitis from 1966 to 1995. A total of 45 cases were collected, including this report. The drugs used were Amphotericin B, Fluconazole, Ketoconazole, Flucytosine, and getamicin. The treatment course was 2 days–4 months. Among them, 20 cases (44.4 %) underwent surgical debridement. In 2016 Medaris et al. [41] reviewed 39 reports on cryptococcal osteomyelitis published between 1995 and 2015, including 5 cases from his study for a total of 44 cases. Based on the two studies mentioned above,a review of the literature was undertaken by searching PubMed and Web of Science for publications containing the terms “osteomyelitis” and “cryptococcus” or “cryptococcal” from 2015 to 2023. We identified an additional 37 cases with cryptococcal osteomyelitis and the general characteristics of these patients are listed in Table 2 (At the end of this article). Amphotericin B, fluconazole, voriconazole, and flucytosine were the principal medications utilized; a 12-week course of treatment to lifetime medication was administered; 26 cases (or 70.3 %) underwent surgery to remove lesions; the country with the highest number of cases was China with 13, followed by the United States with six. In our review of the literature, we noticed that: first, there was little variation in the therapeutic agents, with fluconazole being the most widely employed drug; second, the duration of treatment varied significantly, with patients receiving antifungal medication for a period ranging from 2 days to 2 years, or even lifelong, with an average duration of about 28 weeks. Third, the proportion of patients undergoing surgery is increasing, particularly for individuals with isolated lesions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example in which calcium sulfate bone powder coupled with an antifungal drug was used as a topical filler. Other substances, such as homograft bone [13] or bone cement [109], have previously been used for filling the defect area after osteomyelitis debridement. However, homograft bone cannot carry antimicrobials, and bone cement has the disadvantage of being non-absorbable and weakly porous, with diffusion to surrounding bone tissue limited to the cement's surface, and experiments have shown that only a small amount of antibiotic-loaded bone cement (less than 15 %) is released even after a long time [113]. In contrast, calcium sulfate bone powder loaded with antimicrobials is being utilized to treat chronic osteomyelitis. In contrast, calcium sulfate bone powder loading antimicrobials for the treatment of chronic osteomyelitis has emerged as a popular and effective therapy in recent years, with the effect of stabilizing the local structure, filling dead space, and continuously releasing a sufficient concentration of antibiotic bacteria [114].

In our opinion, there are the following advantages of filling the defect area with antifungal bone powder after bone debridement: 1) a high local concentration of antimicrobial drug level with minimal systemic adverse effects; 2) a sufficient and prolonged release of drugs compared to bone cement; 3) a degree of supportive properties that helps lower the incidence of pathological fractures; and 4) osteoconductivity, which speeds up the formation of new bone in the dead space. In this research, we combined calcium sulfate bone powder and voriconazole into a dough-like compact to cover the defect space left by bone debridement. The calcium sulfate-loading antifungal medication might keep its antibacterial activity going for 3 months at least. We refer to the compatibility ratio of antibiotics (such as vancomycin) loaded with calcium sulfate, which is generally known as 1 g of vancomycin mixed with 5 cc of calcium sulfate [115,116]. Although the probability of side effects caused antibiotics and calcium sulfate is low, we chose to 0.5g of voriconazole mixed with 5 cc calcium sulfate for safety. Voriconazole was chosen due to its water-soluble and powdered characteristics. So far, 15 months following surgery, this patient's cryptococcal osteomyelitis has healed successfully, with outstanding new bone growth in the bone defect location and complete elimination of clinical signs and symptoms. In terms of surgery, we suggest that if the lesion is isolated, patients with conditions should attempt surgical lesion removal, and the postoperative calcium sulfate-loading antifungal medication can be filled to reduce the recurrence rate and increase the doctor's confidence, thereby reducing the drug's use.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, this study does not carry on a molecular identification of Cryptococcus taxonomy; Second, no fungal susceptibility testing has been conducted, and the selection of antibiotics is based rarely on references and experience. Third, the follow-up time is not long enough, and there is a lack of more detailed information on the last follow-up, such as 3D CT and MRI. We hope to continue tracking and following up with this patient to collect more information. Besides, deepens our knowledge about fungal classification and drug sensitivity, collect more cases for further research.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of isolated cryptococcal osteomyelitis of the radius in a non-HIV middle-aged female patient. The treatment consisted of removing the osteomyelitis lesion and then filling it with antifungal drugs combined with calcium sulfate bone powder, which is considered a safe and effective method for eliminating dead space, long-term sustained release of antifungal drugs, and increasing bone stability.
