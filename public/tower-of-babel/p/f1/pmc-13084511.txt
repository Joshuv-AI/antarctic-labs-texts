Visible-Light-Mediated Metal-Free Desulfonylative Silylation for Alkenyl and Alkynylsilanes with Silacarboxylic Acids
Authors: Zhao-Zhao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herein, we report a brand-new example of visible-light-mediated desulfonylative silylation between silacarboxylic acids and α,β-unsaturated aryl sulfones. Valuable alkenyl and alkynylsilane skeletons had been efficiently synthesized, and abundant substrate sources made the reaction more alternative and widely applicable, simultaneously avoiding limitations from conventional methodologies. Meanwhile, the gram-scale reaction with a hydroxyl-containing substrate and successful involvement of dehydrocholic acid derivatives further demonstrated the practicality of this desulfonylative silylation process. Mechanically, a cascade process with silyl radical addition and PhSO2 – elimination was proposed.

Introduction

Organosilicon compounds represented emerging structures in pharmaceutical chemistry due to the low toxicity and biocompatible carbon isosteres role of the silicon atom. As a special type of structure, alkenylsilanes were useful synthetic intermediates that participate in a number of important transformations such as Hiyama cross-coupling and Tamao-Fleming oxidation. A conventional approach to alkenylsilanes was nucleophilic substitution of halosilanes with alkenyl metal reagents, while high substrate activity and low functional group tolerance limited its application (Scheme a). Correspondingly, transition-metal-catalyzed coupling with olefins acted as an alternative protocol, such as cross-coupling with organosilicon metal reagents (Scheme b, A) or cross-electrophile coupling with halosilanes (Scheme b, B) for vinyl electrophiles, as well as silyl-Heck reaction with silyl electrophiles (Scheme b, C) or dehydrogenative silylation with hydrosilanes (Scheme b, D) for vinyl substrates. Meanwhile, the conversion of alkynes concurrently occupied the major role in the synthesis of alkenylsilanes, and a preferable route would be the transition-metal-catalyzed hydrosilylation reaction (Scheme c, A). Stoichiometric or catalytic silylmetalation-protonation process with alkynes also achieved the synthesis of alkenylsilanes, especially for silylzincation and silaboration transformation (Scheme c, B–C). However, these widely developed strategies still endured resistance from complicated catalytic system optimization, regio- and stereoselectivity control, or even difficult preparation of silyl electrophiles and organometallic reagents. Additional, interaction between (2-PyMe2Si)2CHLi and aldehydes or ketones also gave the corresponding alkenylsilanes in extremely high yields with complete E-stereoselectivities through Peterson olefination, which had been proven to be a special route (Scheme c, D). Furthermore, silyl radical-promoted alkenylsilylation has gradually become a hot research field for green synthesis. Catalytic oxidation or hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) of hydrosilanes for silyl radicals was developed to proceed radical addition with alkenes, acrylic acids, or alkynes, and novel radical precursors as well as catalytic systems had also been well applied in silyl-alkyne addition (Scheme d).

Developed Approaches (a–d) to Alkenylsilanes

Besides the developed protocols for alkenylsilanes synthesis, substitution between metal acetylides and silyl electrophiles also produced alkynylsilanes, though disadvantages from strong base and harsh conditions limited the substrate scope (Scheme a). Meanwhile, transition-metal-catalyzed cross-coupling and cross-electrophile coupling of silyl electrophiles with alkynyl coupling partners have been proven to be an effective and reliable pathway despite complexly designed metal catalysts and difficultly prepared silyl coupling partners such as silazanes or iodosilanes (Scheme b, A,B). Moreover, catalytic dehydrogenation coupling reactions of inexpensive and convenient hydrosilanes with terminal alkynes further provided an atom-economical approach, and alkynylsilanes could be effectively synthesized with homogeneous or heterogeneous catalysis (Scheme b, C). However, competitive hydrosilylation of alkynes, sacrificial hydrogen acceptors, as well as additional exogenous bases or oxidants further complicated the reaction design. It was worth noting that strong-base promoted dehydrogenation coupling (Scheme b, D) and catalytic decarboxylation of silyl alkynoates (Scheme c) also promoted modest yields and selectivity, but elevated temperatures and narrow specific substrates weakened the effectiveness. More recently, silyl radical addition with alkynyl acceptors provided the complementary method to alkynylsilanes through hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) or a new reaction mode, but it still suffered limitations from specific HAT reagents and unavoidable competitive reactions from hydrosilanes (Scheme d). Therefore, a concise and straightforward system with high chemoselectivity is of high urgency. As a type of bench-stable radical precursors, silacarboxylic acids exhibited significant advantages over silylboranes, oligosilanes, silylphosphanes, and trialkylsilanes on mild activation conditions and green synthesis. To close this gap, herein, we described a distinctive strategy: a visible-light-mediated desulfonylative silylation between silacarboxylic acids and α,β-unsaturated phenyl sulfones (Scheme e). This strategy represented a new and green synthesis to give structurally important alkenyl and alkynylsilanes.

Results and Discussion

Initially, 4-acetyloxystyrylsulfone 1a was selected as a model substrate to confirm the reactivity with 2 equivalent of methyldiphenyl silacarboxylic acid 2a. Encouragingly, as shown in Table , desulfonylative silylation for desired product alkenylsilane 3a proceeded smoothly, and photosensitizer 4CzIPN as well as base NaHCO3 enabled 27% yield in toluene at room temperature under argon after 24 h (Table , entry 1). Screening of solvents, such as MeCN, THF, or DCE, resulted in substantially decreased yield (Table , entries 2–4). Considering the nonpolar and water-insoluble characters of toluene, which were unfavorable for the conversion of silacarboxylic acid in the homogeneous reaction, a series of mixed solvents with H2O had been screened. The results indicated that toluene/H2O (2:1) exhibited the highest efficiency on the reaction in 44% isolated yield (Table , entries 5–6). Importantly, the photocatalyst was critical to be a beneficial factor, and 3CzClIPN was carefully optimized to give the desired product 3a in 55% yield, besides eosin-Y, rhodamine B, acridine salt, or iridium photocatalyst (Table , entries 7–8, see electronic Supporting Information, ESI). Meanwhile, exploration of other light sources (390 nm) and bases such as DIPEA, K2CO3, or Et3N both failed to improve the yield (Table , entries 9–11). Surprisingly, decreased 0.05 equivalent loading of NaHCO3 and higher 3 equivalent loading of 2a both increased the reaction efficiency (Table , entries 12–13). It was worth noting that 72% yield of product was subsequently obtained after 36 h due to the adequate conversion of 1a, while a higher reaction temperature of 50 °C could also promote reaction well in 81% yield within 24 h. More importantly, excellent E/Z product ratios (>20:1) were obtained, accompanied by the increased temperature (Table , entries 14–15). Control experiments demonstrated that photocatalyst and light were essential for the desulfonylative silylation (Table , entries 16–17), and the optimized reaction conditions were confirmed as 1a (0.2 mmol), 2a (0.6 mmol), 3CzClIPN (5 mol %), and NaHCO3 (0.05 equiv) with 2.0 mL of toluene/H2O (2:1) mixed solvent under 440 nm Kessil LEDs at 50 °C under argon after 24 h.

Having established the optimal conditions (Table , entry 15), we sought to examine the substrate compatibility and scope of the desulfonylative silylation with alkenyl sulfones for alkenylsilanes. Generally, as shown in Scheme , for para-substituted alkenyl sulfones, electron-deficient groups including halogens exhibited good reactivity and stereoselectivity, which is probably due to the enhanced electrophilic phenyl benefit for the stability of the generated carbon radical after silyl radical addition to alkenyl (3a–e). Poor E/Z product ratios were obtained when the para-position was functionalized with chlorine (3d). For styrylsulfones containing alkyl and tert-butyl, the electron-rich character of substrates was well-tolerated, though slightly decreased yields were obtained (3f–g). Furthermore, ortho- and meta-substituted substrates were also applicable, and the effect of steric hindrance was proved to be limited in synthetically useful yield (3h–k). Interestingly, the compatibility of the reaction system with the hydroxyl group was unexpectedly observed, especially with 70% yield and excellent E/Z product ratios (3i). It should be noted that for a more sterically hindered substrate, the desired alkenylsilane product 3l was also smoothly synthesized in 81% yield. Moreover, for aliphatic vinyl sulfones, an unexpected desulfonylative phenyl-substituted product 3m was obtained in 34% yield with high E/Z selectivity, which probably due to the phenyl radical released from the transformation of silanol radicals initiated by silyl radicals. Finally, cyano- and nitro-styrylsulfones could not give the corresponding products smoothly, and the mixture products with 2-(2-(phenylsulfonyl)­vinyl)­pyridine indicated complex addition routes between vinyl sulfone and heteroaryl substrate.

Given the wide application of alkynylsilanes, desulfonylative silylation with alkynyl sulfones was continuously investigated. Encouragingly, the developed methodology was also suitable, and room temperature and air conditions finally made the reaction system more efficient with less silanol byproduct 2aa. Under reoptimized conditions, the effect of the electronic properties of alkynyl sulfones was contrary to that of alkenyl sulfones. As shown in Scheme , for para-substituted precursors, electron-donating groups on phenyl exhibited good reactivity (5a–e) and large-volume tert-butyl did not show a specific rule compared to methyl or propyl although with stronger electron-donating ability (5b–d). Moreover, alkynyl sulfones with electron-deficient groups, such as chlorine or fluorine, were also proved to be competent reactants in the construction of alkynylsilanes, though with relatively decreased yields (5f–g). The reactivity of electron-deficient groups like cyano- and nitro- was poor. Meanwhile, the negative effect of large steric hindrance from ortho- and meta-substitution was limited, and substrates with phenyl groups bearing methyl, fluorine, chlorine, or methoxy were all successfully transformed to the target products (5h–k).

To gain insight into the mechanism of this desulfonylative silylation, a radical capture experiment was conducted with TEMPO. As shown in Scheme , complete suppression of the product 3a revealed the involvement of radical species during the reaction process.

Radical Capture Experiments

Based on the obtained results and previous reports on the silacarboxylic acid, − a hypothetical mechanism of desulfonylative silylation with silyl radical for alkenyl/alkynylsilanes was proposed. As shown in Scheme , silacarboxylic acid anion A was first generated from the neutralization of 2a with NaHCO3. With irradiation by 440 nm blue light, the excited-state 3CzClIPN* induced a single-electron transfer (SET) process with silacarboxylic acid anion A through a reductive quenching cycle, leading to the formation of silacarboxyl radical B and the reduced-state 3CzClIPN•‑ species. Then, silyl radical C, which was generated from a smooth decarboxylation process, proceeded with a radical addition to alkenyl/alkynyl sulfones to give alkyl or alkenyl radical intermediate D immediately. Finally, intermediate E, produced from the single-electron reduction of D by reduced-state 3CzClIPN•‑ species, suffered a PhSO2 – elimination to afford the target product 3. However, preemptive PhSO2 • elimination before single-electron reduction by 3CzClIPN•‑ to PhSO2 – also could not be ruled out to complete the photoredox cycle.

Conclusions

In summary, we have established a brand-new example of visible-light-mediated desulfonylative silylation between silacarboxylic acid and phenyl sulfone to synthesize structurally important alkenyl and alkynylsilanes. The mild conditions and abundant substrate sources made the reaction widely applicable in the absence of an expensive noble metal catalyst. The gram-scale reaction with a hydroxyl-containing substrate and the generation of rarely reported dehydrocholic acid-derived triphenyl alkenylsilanes demonstrated the superiority of the methodology. Mechanically, a cascade process with addition reaction of silyl radical (generated from single-electron oxidation decarboxylation of silacarboxylic acid) and subsequent PhSO2 – elimination was proposed.

Experimental Section

General Information

For product purification by flash column chromatography, silica gel (200–300 mesh) and n-hexane were used. Solvents were freshly dried according to the purification handbook before use. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on 400 MHz in CDCl3, 13C NMR spectra were recorded on 100 MHz in CDCl3, and 19F NMR spectra were recorded on 376 MHz in CDCl3 using TMS as internal standard. All products were further characterized by HRMS (high-resolution mass spectra). Copies of their 1H NMR, 13C NMR, and 19F NMR spectra were provided. The starting materials were purchased from Energy Chemical, J&K Chemicals, Acros, Admas and used without further purification. A Kessil brand 440 nm LED was used in a reaction box equipped with a cooling fan to keep the reaction temperature at 25 °C.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of Alkenylsilanes 3a-3l (GP1)

In a 10.0 mL snap vial with a Teflon cover and a magnetic stirring bar, the alkenyl sulfones 1 (0.2 mmol), silacarboxylic acid 2a (0.6 mmol, 3.0 equiv), 3CzClIPN (0.01 mmol, 5 mol %), and NaHCO3 (0.01 mmol, 0.05 equiv) were filled. After degassing with argon by syringe needle for 5 min and dissolving with 2 mL of toluene/H2O (2:1), the reaction mixture was stirred for 3 min. Then, the vial was irradiated in a reactor with a temperature of 50 °C using a Kessil brand 440 (±15) nm LED (50 W). The reaction progress was monitored by TLC and GC-MS analysis. After full conversion (generally 24 h), the reaction mixture was transferred into a separating funnel, and 10 mL of distilled water and 2 mL of brine were added. Then the resulting mixture was extracted with EtOAc (10 mL × 2 mL), and the final combined organic layer was dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuum. Purification of the crude product was achieved by flash column chromatography using n-hexane as eluents on silica gel.

General Procedure for the Synthesis of Alkynylsilanes 5a-5k (GP2)

In a 10.0 mL snap vial with a Teflon cover and a magnetic stirring bar, the alkynyl sulfones 4 (0.2 mmol), silacarboxylic acid 2a (0.6 mmol, 3.0 equiv), 3CzClIPN (0.01 mmol, 5 mol %), and NaHCO3 (0.01 mmol, 0.05 equiv) were filled. After that, 2 mL of toluene/H2O (2:1) was added by using a syringe, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 3 min. Then, the vial was irradiated in a reactor with a cooling device at 25 °C using a Kessil brand 440 (±15) nm LED (50 W). The reaction progress was monitored by TLC and GC-MS analysis. After full conversion (generally 24 h), the reaction mixture was transferred into a separating funnel, and 10 mL of distilled water and 2 mL of brine were added. Then, the resulting mixture was extracted with EtOAc (10 mL × 2 mL), and the final combined organic layer was dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuum. Purification of the crude product was achieved by flash column chromatography using n-hexane as eluents on silica gel.
