Precision medicine’s new frontier: integrating palliative care at the right time
Authors: A Sampetrean, M Aldea, C Mateus
Published: 2023

In recent decades, the field of oncology has evolved in parallel with advancements in palliative care. The multifaceted demands of cancer treatment—including symptom management, psychosocial support, and other related care components—require escalating amounts of time and expertise for oncologists. Palliative care specialists seek to collaborate with oncologists to facilitate the comprehensive management of patients with advanced-stage cancer.1

Studies have consistently demonstrated the advantages of early integration of palliative care for patients diagnosed with advanced cancer, including enhanced quality of life through superior symptom management and comprehensive psychosocial support, improved survival rates, and less aggressive medical interventions during the end-of-life phase.2, 3, 4 Nevertheless, in clinical practice, we still face with a substantial proportion of referrals occurring during the final weeks of the patients’ lives. Part of this is due to the ongoing misperception of palliative care being solely for end-of-life management,5 further exacerbated by a lack of reliable prediction models to estimate disease evolution of an individual patient.

Currently, there are no definitive recommendations regarding the optimal timing for palliative care referral in oncological patients. While the American Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines suggest referral within 8 weeks following a diagnosis of advanced cancer,6 the applicability of such generalized recommendations across diverse cancer entities may not be the most appropriate approach. Notably, the progression and prognosis of various cancer types vary significantly, and studies assessing the influence of early palliative care were conducted before the widespread adoption of immunotherapies and targeted therapies. The advent of these novel treatment modalities has significantly altered the disease trajectory and prognosis of patients, affording patients with metastatic cancer the opportunity for prolonged survival. However, this enhanced survival is accompanied by increasingly intricate clinical scenarios, as heavily treated patients may often experience a higher burden of symptoms due to complications and sometimes side-effects arising from the treatments. Also, not every patient benefits from these novel therapeutics, which complicates even more the prediction of cancer evolution and patients’ survival.7

Within the context of modern personalized cancer therapy, the imperative for an individualized approach to palliative care becomes progressively evident. For instance, the identification of an ALK fusion in a patient with advanced non-small-cell lung cancer would call for every effort to urgently administer an anti-ALK-targeted therapy, regardless of the patient’s deteriorated clinical state or other factors that might predict a dismal prognosis. In such cases, the expectedly prolonged patient survival and the availability of numerous effective treatment options8 would diminish the utility of early palliative care integration. On the contrary, irrespective of a favorable initial clinical condition, the occurrence of aggressive cancers such as SMARCA4-deficient ovarian or lung carcinoma would necessitate the prompt incorporation of palliative care due to the unfavorable prognosis associated with these diseases.9,10

We advocate for the personalized adoption of palliative care, which involves the incorporation of prognostic and predictive biomarkers. Moreover, precision medicine should integrate diverse sources of information to accurately predict both early mortality and long-term survival outcomes. For instance, we could consider the integration of palliative care after the failure of available targeted therapies in patients with oncogene-addicted tumors, or an early integration with more frequent visits in case of primary progression under targeted therapy. Among patients with advanced lung cancer eligible for chemo-immunotherapy, approximately half are at risk of progression in the first 6 months, while some long responders continue to exhibit positive treatment outcomes for years.11 The prediction of primary resistance to immunotherapy remains a significant challenge, where substantial translational efforts are ongoing to understand the crucial elements involved, encompassing the disease itself, the tumor microenvironment, and the host.7 Artificial intelligence models are being developed to combine patient, biological and radiological characteristics into a single device, and these innovative algorithms are expected to enhance prediction of disease evolution in the near future.12 This approach aims not only to optimize the timing of palliative care integration but also to tailor the frequency of medical visits. To ensure its validity, it requires validation through prospective studies, utilizing outcome indicators such as overall survival, complication frequency, emergency room visits, hospitalization rates, and assessments of quality of life.

In conclusion, identifying the precise juncture for palliative care integration should be approached individually for each cancer type, considering the unique prognostic factors associated with each patient’s individual resources and with each specific cancer and its subtypes. The endeavor to discern distinct disease trajectories, guided by the principles of precision medicine, can facilitate the development of algorithms for customizing treatment based on patients’ unique characteristics. This individualized approach emphasizes the importance of timing and personalization in integrating palliative care, aligning with the broader goals of precision medicine in oncology.
