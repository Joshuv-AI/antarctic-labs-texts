Antiviral activity of non-neutralizing antibodies during steady-state HIV-1 infection in humanized mice
Authors: Manoj S. Nair, Sue Chong, Michael Liu, Jian Yu, Maple Wang, Yaoxing Huang, David D. Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFc-mediated effector functions of monoclonal antibodies can contribute to antiviral immunity, yet their quantitative impact for non-neutralizing antibodies (nnAbs) in HIV-1 infection remains poorly defined. Here, we directly measured the in vivo antiviral activity of two HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein-specific nnAbs, 7B2 (gp41-directed) and A32 (gp120-directed), during steady-state infection in humanized mice to compare the antiviral effects of wild-type nnAbs and their Fc-Null variants. Both nnAbs significantly increased the rate of plasma viral load decay, across multiple HIV-1 strains. Fc-dependent activity was evident when nnAbs were administered alongside a neutralizing anchor antibody and, to a lesser extent, when given by itself. These findings provide direct quantitative evidence that nnAbs can accelerate viral clearance in vivo through Fc-dependent mechanisms. Our results highlight the potential clinical relevance of nnAbs in antibody-based therapies and inform vaccine studies that aim to elicit protective antibody responses against HIV-1.

Introduction

A significant body of evidence underscores the critical role of Fc-mediated effector functions in the overall in vivo activity of antibodies. While the Fab region of an antibody primarily mediates virus neutralization, the Fc portion plays an important role by interacting with Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs), facilitating a broad spectrum of immune responses. These include phagocytic clearance by Kupffer cells, vaccinal effects by adaptive immune cells, and elimination of virus-infected cells by antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) via FcγR engagement.1,2,3 ADCC is predominantly mediated by natural killer (NK) cells,4 although macrophages and neutrophils also contribute significantly to this process.5 Macrophages and neutrophils also play key roles in ADCP.6,7

The importance of Fc-mediated antiviral activity has been firmly established in numerous animal models and clinical studies. For example, passively administered anti-neuraminidase monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with low virus-neutralizing activity required Fc engagement with FcγRs to enhance protection against influenza infection in mice.8 Similarly, investigations of mAbs directed to the Ebola virus glycoprotein9 or the N-terminal domain of SARS-CoV-2 spike protein7 demonstrated that Fc-mediated effector functions contributed significantly to in vivo protection, even when in vitro virus-neutralization activity was weak.6 These findings suggest that weakly neutralizing and non-neutralizing antibodies (nnAbs) can potentially confer a degree of protection through Fc-mediated mechanisms.

The protective and therapeutic potential of HIV-1 antibodies has long focused on their neutralizing capabilities. The critical role of Fc-mediated effector functions was first demonstrated by Hessell and colleagues,10 who showed that the passive administration of a neutralizing mAb b12 conferred protection to macaques challenged with simian HIV (SHIV). However, when Fc-mediated effector functions were abrogated by Fc-Null mutations (L234F, L235E, and P331S), this protection was significantly reduced.11 Notably, ablating complement-binding activity did not diminish the antibody’s protective effect in their study, indicating that other Fc effector functions were responsible for the observed immunity.10,12 Nonetheless, more recent research in humanized mice on non-neutralizing (nnAb) and weakly neutralizing antibodies (obtained from the Thai RV144 clinical trial) has shown their capacity to decrease virus burden by binding to both free virions and infected cells expressing the envelope glycoprotein, thereby mediating antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis and complement-mediated C1q-directed killing,13 among many antiviral effects. Notably, correlates analyses from RV144 demonstrated that ADCC-capable nnAbs were associated with reduced risk of HIV-1 acquisition,14,15 despite structural studies suggesting limited accessibility of relevant epitopes.16,17 This discrepancy highlights the need for direct in vivo measurements of antibody function.

A previous study using broadly neutralizing antibody (bNAb) PGT121 in macaques challenged with SHIV revealed no loss of protection when Fc-Null mutations were introduced,18 suggesting that the antibody’s potent neutralization activity overshadowed any contribution from Fc effector functions.19 While this may or may not be an outlier, this study does not negate the broader importance of Fc-mediated responses in immune defense. Preclinical studies using humanized mice underscore the importance of Fc effector functions, with antibodies exhibiting a stronger antiviral effect when FcγRs were engaged.20,21 For example, treatment with a combination of antiretroviral therapy (ART), bNAbs, and latency-reversing agents (LRAs) significantly delayed or reduced viral rebound. This effect was, however, diminished when Fc-deficient variants of the bNAbs were used, further demonstrating the importance of Fc interactions.22 Moreover, humanized mice treated with bNAb 3BNC117 suppressed HIV-1 more effectively compared to Fc-Null variants of the antibody.21 Clinical evidence also supports the protective role of Fc-mediated effector functions in human HIV-1 infection. Elevated levels of virus-specific ADCC activity in serum or plasma have been linked to slower disease progression.23 Additionally, ADCC has been implicated in controlling HIV-1 infection among elite controllers—patients who can naturally suppress the virus without antiretroviral therapy—and was suggested as an immune correlate of protection in the RV144 vaccine trial, which demonstrated modest efficacy in preventing HIV-1 infection.24

Direct quantification of Fc-mediated antiviral effects in vivo remains limited, however, perhaps due to the complexity of the components involved in its biology.25 Most previous studies used qualitative analyses to infer such contributions based on epitope mutations or reductions in founder virus populations.26,27 Our previous work with bispecific bNAbs in humanized mice and rhesus macaques quantified Fc contributions, estimating that 25%–45% of the total antiviral activity observed by several neutralizing antibodies was mediated via Fc-effector mechanisms.28 Studies examining Fc contributions of neutralizing antibodies in SHIV SF162P3-infected rhesus macaques led to similar findings where 21% of the antiviral activity was attributed to Fc-related effects.29

While bNAbs have demonstrated clear antiviral activity, the contribution of nnAbs remains less well defined. Previous studies have largely relied on either in vitro or ex vivo systems, such as ADCC assays, to infer potential antiviral roles.16,30 While these systems have provided valuable mechanistic insights, they capture the complexity of the in vivo immune microenvironment to a limited degree, with not much accommodation for multi-cellular interactions, tissue-specific dynamics, and temporal changes in viral protein expression. As highlighted in a recent work,31 current in vitro platforms are limited in their ability to fully model in vivo antibody-mediated effector functions. Therefore, direct in vivo quantitation of nnAb antiviral activity remains an important unmet need. We now attempt to delineate the impact of non-neutralizing monoclonal antibodies by evaluating the direct in vivo antiviral activity of nnAbs during steady-state HIV-1 infection in humanized mice. By comparing wild-type and Fc-Null (L234F, L235E, and P331S) variants, we provide a direct and quantitative assessment of Fc-dependent antiviral contributions of these nnAbs.

Results

Experimental design and characterization of antibodies

To address the role of the Fc-mediated effector functions, we utilize a selective loss-of-function approach within a cocktail of antibodies. We aim to quantify the exact contribution of nnAb activity within a combination therapy by comparing the efficacy of a neutralizing antibody (nAb) alone against a cocktail containing both the nAb and an nnAb. By substituting the wild-type nnAb with an Fc-effector null variant (L234F, L235E, P331S, and N297A mutations) while maintaining the nAb’s function, we can determine if the observed increase in clearance or protection is driven by independent effector recruitment rather than direct viral neutralization. A reduction in potency upon knocking out the nnAb’s effector function—despite the continued presence of the nAb—confirms that the nnAb provides a distinct, non-redundant mechanism of action that complements the nAb’s primary inhibitory effect.

With this rationale, we administered non-neutralizing antibodies in humanized NSG mice infected with HIV-1 along with a bispecific nAb (10E8.4/iMab) that was co-administered to serve the purpose of an “anchor” molecule. One arm of this molecule matched a clinical monoclonal antibody ibalizumab32,33 and binds the CD4 receptor preventing viral entry through steric hindrance while the other arm of this molecule matches an nAb, 10E8, capable of recognizing the membrane proximal external region of HIV envelope glycoprotein.17,34 Bispecific 10E8.2/iMab, the precursor to the 10E8.4/iMab molecule used in this study, is one of the most potent (IC50∼0.002 μg/mL) neutralizing antibodies against 118 HIV-1 isolates demonstrating >99% breadth in tests.35,36 Importantly, this molecule contains L234F, L235E, P331S (Fc-Null), and N297A37,38,39 Fc mutations that knock out binding to Fc receptors, rendering it Fc-Null and incapable of mediating Fc-dependent effector functions.28 Thus, the anchor antibody provides potent neutralization without contributing Fc-mediated activity, allowing isolation of nnAb-specific Fc effects. Additionally, its inclusion prevents the emergence of resistant variants during the short experimental window (≤5 days), as previously observed in monotherapy settings.35

The two non-neutralizing antibodies that we utilized in our study have distinct epitope specificities. The 7B2 mAb, targeting a gp41 epitope of HIV-1, was shown to have a 50% reduction in the number of unique viruses capable of inducing infection after a high-dose challenge of SHIV in rhesus macaques.26 The second antibody (A32), while also non-neutralizing, had a distinct epitope (the C1 loop of gp120)17,40 and, like 7B2, mediates early binding to HIV-infected cells. Antibodies like A32 that were directed to the C1 epitope were considered the primary correlate of the 31% protective efficacy seen in RV144 trial.14,41 We purified these monoclonal antibodies 7B2 and A32 showing high structural integrity (Figure S1A). Dosing in BALB/c mice revealed comparable serum half-lives for both WT and Fc-Null versions of the nnAbs, suggesting favorable pharmacokinetic properties for pursuing intended in vivo study (Figure S1B). Both 7B2 and A32 showed no neutralizing activity, alone or in combination with a neutralizing bispecific antibody 10E8.4/iMab against JRCSF and AD8 viruses (Figures S1C and S1D).

7B2 nnAb exhibits Fc-dependent anti-HIV-1 antiviral activity

Immunodeficient NOD/SCID/IL2rγnull (NSG) mice were reconstituted with human hematopoietic stem cells and then infected with HIV-1 JRCSF as performed in our earlier studies.28,35 As shown in Figure S2, mice exhibited stable human lymphoid cell engraftment. NK cells were present at lower frequencies (∼1%–10%) compared to humans but were detectable in all animals. Once infection reached steady-state levels (∼104–106 copies/mL) as measured by viral RNA levels in the plasma (Figure S3), the mice were divided into two comparable groups. One of the groups (n = 14) received 2.5 mg of 7B2 with an intact and functional Fc region, and the other group (n = 12) received an identical dose of 7B2 with Fc-Null. Both antibodies were administered intraperitoneally. An additional 2.5 mg of 10E8.4/iMab antibody, serving as an anchor molecule, was administered intraperitoneally to both groups at the same time, to prevent the emergence of resistant viruses for the duration of the study. Blood was collected from mice on day 0 of the study prior to treatment and for the subsequent 5 days to measure viral load changes. This period of the first 5 days would be the critical window when maximal information on the first round of rapid viral decay would be obtained as established by our previous viral dynamic studies,42,43,44 with minimal to no interference from decay of the virus due to subsequent rounds of virus production.45 In JRCSF-infected humanized mice, combination treatment of 7B2 WT with 10E8.4/iMab-Fc-Null showed a significantly steeper viral load decline compared to combination of 7B2-Fc-Null with 10E8.4/iMab-Fc-Null (Figure 1A). Beginning as early as the second day post-treatment, there was a significant difference in average viral load decline (slope) between the groups (Figure 1B). This indicates a measurable contribution of Fc effector function to viral clearance during early treatment. To validate these observations across strains, we repeated the experiment using a second cohort of HIV-1-AD8-infected mice at steady-state viremia. The first group (n = 7) received 2.5 mg of 7B2-WT and the other (n = 10) received an identical dose of 7B2-Fc-Null, both intraperitoneally, and both groups received additional 2.5 mg of anchor molecule 10E8.4/iMab-Fc-Null. Plasma collection from blood for viral RNA extraction was performed on an identical schedule as the earlier study to match conditions for assessing viral kinetics. AD8-infected mice treated with 7B2-WT displayed steeper declines in viral load than the Fc-Null group (Figure S4A). A32 nnAb also displays Fc-dependent anti-HIV-1 antiviral activity

To determine whether Fc-mediated effects extended beyond 7B2 to other nnAbs, we tested another antibody A32 in combination with the anchor molecule 10E8.4/iMab-Fc-Null against HIV-1 AD8 in a third cohort of humanized NSG mice with steady-state HIV-1 AD8 infection that was randomly assigned into two dosing groups. The first group (n = 7) received 2.5 mg of A32-WT nnAb, while the second group (n = 5) received 2.5 mg of A32-Fc-Null, in addition to 2.5 mg of anchor molecule provided to both groups at the same time. Viral load data collected from this cohort showed that mice receiving A32-WT had a slope of viral decay that was >1.8× steeper compared to those receiving A32-Fc-Null (Figures 1D and S4B).

nnAbs display an Fc-dependent antiviral activity when dosed without anchor antibody

To extend these studies further, we undertook the task of testing non-neutralizing antibodies in the absence of the anchor molecule for a shorter duration of 3 days. Shortening the duration mitigates the potential confounding effects due to the emergence of resistance to the monotherapy. We assembled a large cohort of humanized mice (n = 29) infected with HIV-1 AD8 virus at steady-state levels and divided them into two comparable groups. The first group (n = 16) received 5 mg of A32-WT intraperitoneally, and the second group (n = 13) received A32-Fc-Null at the same dose via the same route. Mice receiving A32-WT demonstrated a subtle but quantifiable decline in their viral load over the initial 3 days as indicated by the small negative average slope (−0.12/day) (Figure 2A), while the Fc-Null group showed no decline. These data clearly showed that there is a modest but distinct difference in the kinetics of viral load decay between the groups (Figure 2B). To measure if the modest decline in viremia can be verified across epitopes, we repeated the study with the second nnAb 7B2. Humanized mice (Figure S2A) were infected to reach steady-state viremia with HIV-1 JRCSF virus (Figure S5A) before dosing with 5 mg of either wild-type or Fc-Null-engineered 7B2 antibody. Mice receiving wild-type 7B2 (n = 5) once again showed the small, consistent measurable decline in their viral load over the initial 3 days as indicated by the small negative average slope (−0.10/day), notably missing in the mice that received 7B2 bearing Fc-Null mutations in their Fc (n = 4), as depicted in Figure S5B.

Discussion

We used our understanding of HIV-1 viral dynamics42,43,44 to quantify the effects of non-neutralizing antibodies on anti-HIV-1 activity in humanized mice through Fc-mediated effector functions. Two non-neutralizing antibodies, 7B2 and A32, targeting distinct envelope epitopes, accelerated virus clearance when their Fc regions were intact, an effect seen against both tier 2 and tier 3 HIV-1 strains.

Our findings aligned with previous work that suggested nnAbs may impact infectivity by reducing founder virus populations or forcing viral escape.26,27 However, these studies extrapolated epitope mutations as indirect endpoints to measure the role of such antibodies. The application of viral dynamics allowed us to make absolute and direct measurements in vivo to show the difference in the rate of viral decay between HIV-1-envelope-directed nnAbs that possess the Fc functionality to mediate this decay versus those that do not.

Importantly, the antiviral contribution of Fc function is not limited to a single antibody or a single virus strain. The replication of this phenotype in both 7B2, a gp41-directed nnAb, and A32, a gp120-directed nnAb, suggests that Fc-mediated activity is a generalizable feature of nnAbs. While the quantifiable activity was expectedly modest when nnAb was administered unchaperoned in contrast to co-administration with an anchor molecule bearing neutralizing activity, the presence of an antiviral signal and the requirement of a functional Fc to mediate the effector response were observed in all the studies.

Vaccines may elicit a broader array of antibodies, many of which are non-neutralizing. If designed to harness Fc-mediated mechanisms, such antibodies could still contribute significantly to viral control, especially at early stages of infection in mucosal sites. Our observations can be extended to rationalizing the modest clinical response observed when A32-like antibodies were generated in the RV144 clinical trial. Importantly, we do not propose that nnAbs alone are sufficient for therapeutic control of HIV-1 infection, nor do we suggest the need for new clinical trials based solely on nnAbs. Rather, our findings use a strategy unique from the reductionist approaches applied so far to studying nnAbs to provide quantitative evidence of their modest but measurable antiviral effects, particularly during early stages of infection, and we do so by extending our expertise on viral dynamics in vivo.42,43,44

In summary, our data provided direct, quantitative evidence that Fc-dependent effector functions of non-neutralizing antibodies enhance viral clearance during steady-state HIV-1 infection in vivo. These findings underscore the therapeutic and prophylactic potential of nnAbs, particularly in vaccine-elicited immune responses.

Limitations of the study

We did not dissect the Fc effector mechanisms (e.g., ADCC, ADCP, and CDC) responsible for the antiviral activity during the period of the study, as in vivo dissection of these pathways remains technically challenging. Additionally, in vitro studies to dissect the mechanism of Fc effector functions were not performed, as they were beyond the objectives of this study. Future studies with Fc receptor knockout models, Fc-enhanced mutations, or in vivo depletion of effector cells could help refine the exact contributions further. Our model of humanized NSG mice while on par with lymphoid cell immune reconstitution, have fewer NK cell abundance than more recent models. Although we have successfully established effector function in our model previously, likely quantitative differences from human immune systems should be considered when interpreting results. Finally, while humanized mice are a robust preclinical tool, reproducibility in clinical settings is essential.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cells lines and virus strains

Expi293 cells (Life Technologies) were used to transfect and produce all recombinant antibodies and clonal HIV-1 viruses used in this study. TZM-bl cells used for in vitro neutralization experiments performed in this study were obtained from BEI Reference laboratories (NIH). HIV-1 JRCSF and HIV-1 AD8 were obtained through BEI Resources. The proviral cDNA clones were transfected into Expi293 cells using FectoPRO transfection reagent (Sartorius; 101000007) to produce infectious virus that was titrated using TZM-bl cells to obtain a tissue culture infectious dose. Cell lines were purchased from authenticated vendors, and morphology was visually confirmed before use in assays. All cell lines were tested for mycoplasma using Mycoalert mycoplasma detection kit using luminescence (Lonza) prior to use in assays.

Animal models

All animal experiments were carried out in strict accordance with the Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the United States Public Health Service. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at The Columbia University (Animal Welfare Assurance no. D16-00003; Investigator protocol AABE9556). Mice were euthanized with CO2, with every effort made to minimize suffering. BALB/cJ mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratories for measuring in vivo pharmacokinetics (PK) of the antibodies used in this study. Humanized NSG mice prepared from CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells as described earlier28,35,48 by the Columbia Center for Translational Immunology Humanized mice core. NSG (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory for establishing breeding pairs. Newborns between day 1 and day 5 were irradiated with 100 rad and then provided intrahepatic injections of 0.2 × 106 human hematopoietic CD34+ stem cells 6 h later. Mice were weaned at day 21 post injections into groups of 5 mice of the same sex per cage. The sex of the humanized mice did not influence the outcomes measured in this study. Analyses of the human cell populations and experimental endpoints demonstrated no sex-dependent differences, and therefore data from male and female mice were combined for all reported results. Method details

In vitro characterization of antibodies

Two non-neutralizing monoclonal antibodies, 7B2 (gp41-epitope specific)26 and A32 (gp120-epitope specific),17,40,49 were constructed, expressed, purified and confirmed for integrity using size exclusion chromatography. Antibodies were engineered to either retain Fc effector function (wildtype IgG1) or lack it (Fc-Null) via L234F/L235E/P331S mutations. For each monoclonal antibody, variable genes were optimized for human cell expression and synthesized by GenScript. VH and VL were inserted separately into plasmids (gWiz) that encode the constant region for H chain and L chain. Monoclonal antibodies were expressed in Expi293 (Thermo Scientific) by co-transfection of H chain and L chain expressing plasmids using polyethylenimine and cultured at 37 °C with shaking at 125 rpm and 8% CO2. Supernatants were collected on day 5 for antibody purification using rProtein A Fast flow (Cytiva) affinity chromatography. Bispecific potent neutralization antibody 10E8.4/iMab also carries above Fc-Null mutations and additional N297A mutation thereby preventing binding (due to lack of glycan at position 297) and therefore any contribution to the Fc-mediated function possible in mice.37,39 Bispecific antibody utilizing the knob-into-hole and light chain crossover format was constructed as previously reported.35 Briefly, genes coding for the variable regions of the heavy and light chains of the monoclonal antibodies were inserted separately into gWiz plasmid encoding the constant region of the H chain or the L chain. Plasmids so generated were co-transfected in a ratio of 1:1 into the Expi293 cells using polyethyleneimine transfection reagent to express the antibodies. IgG was purified from the supernatant of cultured cells using rProteinA Fast flow. Size exclusion chromatography was used to assess physicochemical homogeneity of the test antibodies. Fifty micrograms of the antibodies were analyzed using an AKTA purifier FPLC (GE Healthcare) with column, flow rate, and mobile phase previously described.50 Neutralization activity was tested for all antibodies via TZM-bl reporter assays using HIV-1 clade B clonal strains JRCSF and AD8.

In vitro neutralization/lack of neutralization of antibodies

In vitro neutralizing activity of all antibodies used in the study was tested against HIV-1 JRCSF and HIV-1pNL(AD8), as previously described.51 Briefly, serial dilutions of antibodies were incubated with the viruses and added to TZM-bl cells carrying a luciferase reporter gene under HIV-LTR promoter control. Following a 48-h incubation, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using the Galacto-Star™ β-galactosidase reporter gene assay kit (Thermo-Scientific; T1014). Luciferase activity readout was used to generate a dose-response curve to determine percent neutralization of each virus by tested antibodies using GraphPad Prism 10.

In vivo dosing and longitudinal timeline of quantitation

PK studies in BALB/cJ mice were performed by injecting 100 μg of each purified monoclonal non-neutralizing antibody intraperitoneally into mice (n = 3 per antibody), followed by measuring total human monoclonal Ig levels from mouse plasma collected on days 2, 4, 7 and 10 post injection using ELISA. The level of human engraftment in was assessed 8 weeks after transplantation. These mice upon successful reconstitution were infected with either 75,000 TCID50 HIV-1 JRCSF or 37,500 TCID50 HIV-1 AD8 to establish steady-state infection. Mice were then treated intraperitoneally with monoclonal antibodies as described in each study. For studies where the bispecific neutralizing antibody was used, 2.5 mg of the 10E8.4/iMab antibody was combined with 2.5 mg of the studied non-neutralizing antibody in a single dose to the mice at the initiation of treatment. In experiments conducted using only the examined non-neutralizing antibody, 5 mg of A32 or 7B2 wild type or Fc-Null antibody was dosed per mouse in a single intraperitoneal injection. Plasma was collected from mice bled on the day of the antibody administration followed by days 1, 2, 3 and 5 post antibody dosing for viral load measurement.

Flow cytometric evaluation of reconstitution of human cells in engrafted NSG mice

Flow cytometry was used to assess human immune cell reconstitution in humanized NSG mice, including CD8+, CD4+, and NK cell populations using methods reported.28,35,48 Briefly, 100–200 μL of whole blood collected at indicated timepoints were subjected to density gradient centrifugation (Corning; 25072CI) to isolate PBMCs. Cells were stained for 1 h at 4°C using the following antibodies: anti-human CD45-PerCP-Cy5.5 (Biolegend), anti-mouse CD45-PECy7 (Biolegend), anti-human CD19-APC (BD), anti-human CD3-Pacific Blue (BD), anti-human CD4-PE (BD), anti-human CD8-FITC (BD), and anti-human CD335 (NKp46)-Alexa Fluor (Biolegend). Cells were washed and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde before FACS analysis on the BD LSR II flow cytometer. Further data analysis was performed using FlowJo (Waters Biosciences).

Quantification of HIV-1 viral RNA in mouse plasma

RNA was extracted from mouse plasma (50 μL) using QiAmp viral RNA extraction kit (Qiagen) in conjunction with on-column digestion of any associated DNA using RNAse-free DNAse (Qiagen). Purified plasma HIV-1 RNA (10 μL) was quantified using RT-qPCR targeting the HIV-1 pol gene using primers and probe published previously28,35 and indicated in the STAR Methods. Cycling conditions were 15 min at 48 °C, 17 min at 94 °C, followed by 50 cycles at 95 °C for 30 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Samples were run in duplicate in an Applied Biosystems 7,500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher), and the limit of detection was 1,000 copies per milliliter of plasma.

Quantitation and statistical analysis

Linear regression analysis to calculate viral decay post antibody treatment

Viral load decay kinetics were analyzed using linear regression in GraphPad Prism 10. Decay slope (log10 copies/day) and half-life of viral clearance were calculated and compared using Mann-Whitney U tests.

Statistical analysis for determination of significance of dataset

Statistical significance in change in VL between the two groups per day was measured using non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test and indicated as ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), ∗ (p < 0.05) and ns (p > 0.05).
