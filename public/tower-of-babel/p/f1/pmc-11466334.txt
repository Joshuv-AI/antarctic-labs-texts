Symptomatic Valvular and Subvalvular Stenosis: A Case Report
Authors: Jesus Romero, Sherif Elkattawy, Ana L. Romero, Murad Aldarayseh, Omar Elkattawy, Meherwan Joshi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aortic stenosis is a common valvular disease that is expected to become more prevalent with the aging population. It is often undiagnosed in socioeconomically deprived groups. Diagnosis relies on echocardiography, and additional imaging techniques such as cardiac computed tomography (CCT) and cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) are increasingly used. Surgical aortic valve replacement is the standard treatment, except for high-risk cases where TAVR is recommended. Subvalvular aortic stenosis (SAS) is the second most common cause of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. This case report highlights a 72-year-old Hispanic lady with both valvular and subvalvular aortic stenosis, emphasizing the need for specialized care and surgical intervention. Early identification, accurate assessment, and appropriate intervention are crucial for improving outcomes in aortic stenosis and subvalvular stenosis cases.

Introduction

Aortic stenosis is a commonly occurring valvular disease in developed countries, and its prevalence is expected to increase with the aging population. Severe aortic stenosis is estimated to affect between 4% and 7% of people over the age of 65. Despite the efforts of current healthcare systems, aortic stenosis is often undiagnosed in high-income countries, particularly in socioeconomically deprived groups. Echocardiography plays a significant role in diagnosing and managing aortic stenosis, while the use of cardiac computer tomography (CCT) and cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is also increasing.1

Although advancements have been made in diagnosing aortic stenosis, treatment options remain limited. Currently, there are no medical treatments to prevent or slow the progression of the disease, and identifying those at risk, accurately measuring the severity of stenosis, managing concurrent diseases, and timing and type of aortic valve replacement is crucial in improving patient outcomes. Surgical aortic valve replacement is the standard approach, except in cases where surgery carries a high mortality risk. Transcatheter aorticvalve replacement (TAVR) is recommended for patients with symptomatic aortic stenosis who have a high risk of surgery complications, medical conditions involving three other major organ systems, or severe impediment to surgery.2

While valvular aortic stenosis is well-understood, its subtype, subvalvular aortic stenosis (SAS), presents unique challenges. SAS is a type of aortic stenosis that is the second most common cause of left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, after valvular aortic stenosis. It mainly affects males and is associated with other heart defects in many cases. SAS is an acquired disease that often presents in the first decade of life with symptoms of progressive LVOT obstruction, left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), and aortic regurgitation (AR). While some patients with SAS may have no symptoms, others may experience chest pain, syncope, palpitations, or congestive heart failure.3

Early diagnosis is vital for improving patient outcomes, but it remains challenging in socioeconomically deprived groups. Diagnosis of SAS typically involves a systolic ejection murmur, as confirmed by echocardiography, which can also assess the extent of the obstruction. While echocardiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis, the use of cardiac MRI is increasing due to its higher sensitivity in certain cases. Cardiac catheterization may be used in certain cases. Unfortunately, there are currently no medical therapies available to treat SAS, and surgical intervention is typically necessary for patients with significant obstruction.3 Untreated aortic stenosis can lead to severe cardiac complications, such as heart failure.

This paper aims to discuss the complexities of diagnosing and treating aortic stenosis by presenting the case of a 72-year-old Hispanic lady with both valvular stenosis and subvalvular stenosis. The case highlights the diagnostic complexities and treatment challenges, especially when both valvular and subvalvular forms coexist, thereby making a unique learning point.

Case presentation

To illustrate the complexities in diagnosing and treating aortic stenosis, our patient presented to our Emergency Department (ED) complaining of left-sided chest pain, dizziness, and shortness of breath. Two weeks prior to the presentation, the patient began experiencing worsening shortness of breath. Two days before her ED visit, she started experiencing left-sided chest pain which was trigger by minimal exertion and relieved by rest. The patient described the pain as pressure-like, 7/10 in intensity, intermittent, and associated with nausea but no vomiting or diaphoresis. The patient also complained of repetitive episodes of dizziness, and she even reported the loss of consciousness once for approximately 5 min without tongue biting, jerking movements, and loss of urine or stool.

In the ED, the patient’s vital signs were as follows: blood pressure 115/72 mmHg, heart rate 63 beats per min, respiratory rate 18 breaths per min, and oxygen saturation 100% on room air. On physical examination, a late peaking 4/6 systolic murmur was appreciated in the aortic area with radiation to the carotid artery is characteristic of aortic stenosis. Pertinent labs include Cr 1.08 [0.4–1 MG/DL], BUN 21 [8–20 MG/DL], BNP 119 [<100 pg/ml], and D-dimer 229 [0–230 ng/ml], troponin 0.01 [<0.5 ng/ml].

EKG showed normal sinus rhythm, with T wave inversion in lead I. CXR showed mild pulmonary vascular congestion and diffuse interstitial prominence consistent with possible pulmonary edema. The elevation of BNP and the T wave inversion findings in the EKG suggested cardiac strain.

2D-Echocardiogram showed left ventricular ejection fraction >75%, hyperdynamic global left ventricular systolic function. Heavily calcified and restricted aortic valve. The aortic valve area by planimetry was 0.93 cm2, indicating severe aortic stenosis as per established guidelines (Fig. 1). The peak gradient across the aortic valve was 130 mmHg with a mean gradient of 77 mmHg (Fig. 2). There was a sub-valvular obstruction from the hyperdynamic left ventricle without systolic anterior motion of the anterior mitral leaflet. Mid-cavity level produced at least a 38 mm gradient on pulse Doppler interrogation (Fig. 3). Despite that, the aortic valve was still severely stenotic (Video 1 (https://scholarlycommons.gbmc.org/cgi/editor.cgi)).

Left heart catheterization was performed which revealed a mild disease of the left circumflex artery. Non-obstructive coronary artery disease. Severe aortic stenosis. A peak-to-peak gradient of 80 mmHg, and a mean gradient of 60 mmHg.

The findings were extensively discussed with the patient, and the decision was made to transfer the patient to a tertiary care center for aortic valve replacement since given the severe aortic stenosis and sub-valvular obstruction, as well as her symptoms and co-morbidities, surgical intervention was deemed necessary.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges in patients with concurrent valvular and subvalvular aortic stenosis and underscores the importance of comprehensive evaluation.

Discussion

Discrete subaortic stenosis (DSS) is a type of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction that usually appears in the first decade of life as a progressive condition, and its overall occurrence rate is between 6.5% and 20%. In up to 10% of cases, the subaortic stenosis forms a narrow, tunnel-like left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). The presence of other congenital anomalies, particularly membranous ventricular septal defect (VSD), is associated with subaortic stenosis, and it can occur in up to 65% of cases. Other congenital anomalies that can be present include the bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation of the aorta, and patent ductus arteriosus. The exact cause of subaortic stenosis is not fully understood. One theory suggests that there may be an underlying morphology that is more prone to increased turbulence at the LVOT, which can increase septal shear stress, stimulate local reactive cell proliferation, and lead to the development of the subaortic membrane.4

DSS can be classified into four anatomic variants, including a thin fibrous membrane, a thick funnel-shaped fibrotic ring, irregular fibromuscular tissue, and a long tunnel-like obstruction. This condition is considered to be acquired, as it is rarely present in newborns. Certain structural characteristics have been found to increase the risk of developing DSS, such as increased separation between the mitral and aortic annuli and a steeper angle between the aorta and the septum. These changes in geometry can cause turbulent flow in the LVOT, which is believed to trigger endothelial proliferation and the development of DSS.5

Hun Kang et al., describe a case of a 46-year-old gentleman who presented with similar complaints to our patient characterized by dizziness and who was found to have subaortic stenosis. However, in discrepancy with our findings, this patient had a subaortic membrane and no valvular stenosis was identified by echocardiography. The patient underwent surgical myomectomy along with subaortic membrane removal. Unlike the case presented by Hun Kang et al., our patient exhibited both valvular and subvalvular stenosis, highlighting the diagnostic complexity.6 Russell et al., describe the case of a 61-year-old lady who presented with exertional dyspnea and a systolic murmur and underwent transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiogram studies. The transthoracic echocardiography showed a normal appearance of aortic valve leaflets with normal excursion and without obvious calcifications, however, the transesophageal study revealed a significant flow acceleration in the LVOT and below the aortic valve evidenced by color doppler consistent with a crescentic fibromuscular membrane encircling the LVOT, in similarly with the findings described by Hun Kang et al.7

On the other hand, McGregor et al. reported three cases of concomitant aortic stenosis and subaortic membrane. Similarly, in our case, these patients were found to have aortic stenosis, however, the Subvalvular stenosis was determined by an anatomical component which was the presence of a subaortic membrane. Our patient rather than the evidence of an anatomical component, the subvalvular obstruction was driven by the hyperdynamic left ventricle without systolic anterior motion of the anterior mitral leaflet.5

Surgical intervention is the definitive treatment for SAS. The surgery can involve removing a simple membrane or performing a more extensive procedure like ring resection with or without myomectomy or a Konno procedure. The timing of the surgery depends on the patient’s characteristics, and there are no established medical therapies to reverse or halt SAS progression. Surgical intervention is preferred for patients with significant obstruction, particularly those with muscular or tunnel-like obstruction, and those with diffusely narrow LVOTs may require a Konno procedure or its modifications. However, surgical intervention can lead to postoperative complications, such as heart block, mitral valve injury, iatrogenic ventricular septal defect, incomplete relief or recurrence of obstruction, and infective endocarditis. Recently, enucleation of the fibrous ridge through blunt dissection with myomectomy has shown promising results for selected patients.3

Likewise, medical treatments are not yet available to prevent or slow down the progression of aortic stenosis. To improve patient outcomes, it is important to accurately identify those at risk, measure the severity of the obstruction, manage any other concurrent diseases, and decide on the appropriate timing and type of aortic valve replacement. The standard treatment approach for aortic stenosis is surgical aortic valve replacement, except when surgery is associated with a high risk of mortality. In such cases, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is recommended for patients who have symptoms of aortic stenosis and are at high risk of complications with surgery, have medical conditions that affect three or more major organ systems, or have a severe impediment to surgery. Given the absence of established medical therapies, surgical intervention is often the treatment of choice. However, the type of surgical procedure depends on the specific anatomical features, as seen in our case.2

Our case is particularly noteworthy because the sub-valvular obstruction was driven by a hyper-dynamic left ventricle, an uncommon presentation. The lack of medical treatments for DSS and aortic stenosis underscores the need for continued research into alternative therapies.
