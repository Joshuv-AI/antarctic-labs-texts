Two versus four weeks ureteral stenting prior to flexible ureteroscopy after failed primary ureteral access sheath insertion: a randomized trial
Authors: Baher Salman, Atef Badawy, Mohamed El-Shazly, Ammar Alorabi, Mohamed Elgharbawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDuring Flexible ureteroscopy; Failed primary placement of the ureteral access sheath (UAS) or backloading of flexible ureteroscopy occurred in up to 22% of patients who required ureteric stent insertion for some time. This study was conducted to compare the outcomes of two versus four weeks of preoperative ureteral stenting in patients with failed primary ureteral access undergoing flexible ureteroscopy (FURS).Patients and MethodsThis single-blind, randomized controlled trial included 60 patients with upper and/or renal stones < 20 mm who had failed primary access during FURS between April 2024 and October 2025. Patients were randomized into 2 groups to insert a ureteral double-J stent for either 2 weeks (n = 30) or 4 weeks (n = 30) before the definite FURS. The primary endpoint was successful insertion of a 10/12 Fr ureteral access sheath (UAS). Secondary outcomes included stone-free rate (SFR) (assessed by a postoperative 4-week CTUT), operative time, complications, and stent-related symptoms (assessed by the Ureteral Stent Symptom Questionnaire (USSQ)).ResultsBaseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups. Successful UAS insertion was achieved in 90% of the 2-week group and 93.3% of the 4-week group with no difference between groups (p = 0.64). No significant differences were found between groups regarding postoperative 4-week NCCT SFR (80.0% vs. 73.3%, p = 0.54), operative time (55 vs. 48.5 min, p = 0.44), or complication rates. Ureteric injuries were minor (6.7% in each group). Stent-related symptoms measured by USSQ did not significantly differ (p = 0.24).ConclusionIn this randomized trial, no statistically significant differences were observed between 2-week and 4-week preoperative stenting regarding UAS insertion success, stone-free rate, operative time, or complication rates. These findings suggest that prolonging stent dwell time beyond 2 weeks may not provide a substantial additional benefit in this patient population.Trial registrationThe trial was retrospectively registered in 12/2024 on clinicaltrials.gov under the number: NCT06761378.Keywords: Flexible ureteroscopy, Duration, Stone, Ureteral access sheath, Double j stent

Background

Retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) has significantly become recommended as the first or second-line treatment for renal and upper ureteric stones, including stones larger than 20 mm because of its high stone-free rate (SFR) and low complication rate [1]. The use of the ureteral access sheath (UAS) during RIRS decreases the intrarenal pressure, improves the SFR, decreases the operative time, and decreases the postoperative fever and urinary tract infection (UTI) [2]. However, primary access to the stones by either UAS or Direct ureteroscope may fail in 10–11% of patients particularly in patients with a narrow, kinked, or spastic ureter [3, 4]. Failed insertion of UAS can be overcome by coaxial or balloon dilatation, selecting a small-size UAS, or backloading the flexible ureteroscopy (FURS) over the guide wire [5].

The most suitable and safe decision for failed access is Double J stent insertion, which improves ureteral access after a time interval [6]. Stent-associated morbidity varies across the literature with more than 80% of patients may have a lower quality of life due to ureteric stents [7]. It is also associated with increased complications such as discomfort, hematuria and encrustations [8]. The duration of the Double j stent should be sufficient for better surgical outcomes. Unfortunately, the literature has insufficient data to determine this duration [9]. We hypothesized that extending preoperative ureteral stenting from two weeks to four weeks would improve UAS insertion success during secondary FURS after failed primary access.

Therefore, we aimed to compare two versus four weeks of ureteral stenting prior to secondary flexible ureteroscopy in patients with failed primary ureteral access, focusing on ureteral access sheath insertion success, stone-free rate, perioperative outcomes, complications, and stent-related symptoms.

Patients and methods

Study design, setting, and population

This single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted in a single center between April 2024 and October 2025 to evaluate the success rate of UAS insertion after stent duration of two weeks in comparison to four weeks.

The study included 60 patients with upper ureteric or/and renal stones less than 20 mm and negative urine culture planned for FURS with failed primary ureteral access. All patients who underwent ureteroscopy (URS) for non-calcular diagnosis or treatment were excluded.

Following the initial trial of undergoing FURS with or without UAS, patients who had failed primary access and required ureteric stenting were eligible for our study. Failure of primary access was declared after failure of all trials of rescue maneuvers including serial dilatation by Teflon dilators, ureteroscope advancement and FURS backloading. Those patients were then randomized into two equal groups; 2-week group, patients who had ureteric stent inserted for 2 weeks prior to FURS and 4-week group, patients who had ureteric stent inserted for 4 weeks prior to FURS.

The sample size was determined pragmatically based on the number of eligible patients presenting during the study period. No formal prior sample size calculation was performed.

Randomization procedure was done through a computer-generated random number sequence, and the allocation was concealed using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Surgeons performing second stage FURS and data collectors were blinded to patient allocation and duration of preoperative stenting.

Intervention and outcomes

Following the stent duration, either 2 weeks or 4 weeks according to the assigned group, under general anesthesia and after administration of single dose of preoperative antibiotic (3rd generation cephalosporin), diagnostic cystoscopy was done, followed by the initial double J removal. This was followed by diagnostic URS using semirigid URS (Storz 6.5/7 Fr URS) to evaluate ureteral accessibility.

This was followed by the UAS insertion. A flexible and navigable suction access sheath (FANS) (10/12 Fr) was used during the intervention (YigaoMed, Zhejiang YiGao Medical Technology Co., China), then the FURS (Scivita flexible URS 8.4 Fr) was used. Stones were disintegrated using laser lithotripsy (Sphinx Jr Holmium machine, 30 watts, Lisa Laser-Promed, USA), either using high energy settings (2 J, 10 Hz) or low energy settings (1 J, 15 Hz). Laser settings and operative strategy were left to surgeon discretion based on intraoperative stone characteristics.

The primary endpoint was successfully inserting UAS 10/12 Fr in the second intervention. Any other scenarios, such as backloading of FURS or the need for reinsertion of a new Double J stent, were considered failures. The secondary endpoints were perioperative complications (recorded and graded according to Clavien-Dindo classification), ureteric injuries (classified according to Traxer and Thomas [10]), the operative time of the second procedure (time from cystoscope insertion to double J insertion in minutes), stent related symptoms (Symptoms were assessed using the Ureteral Stent Symptom Questionnaire (USSQ) administered immediately before stent removal on undergoing second stage FURS, where 5 domains were included (urinary symptoms, pain symptoms, general health, work performance and sexual matter) and higher scores indicate greater symptom severity [11]) and the SFR (either clear, when no or < 4 mm fragments were present or residual, when ≥ 4 mm fragments were present, measured by a postoperative 4-week CTUT).

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the local university ethics committee (Institutional Review Board) under the number 5-2024UROL2 and the study was retrospectively registered in the http://clinicaltrials.gov/ database (registration number: NCT06761378). Trial registration was completed after enrollment had begun due to administrative delay, and no protocol modifications occurred after study initiation. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964). All participants were counseled and fully informed about the study, signed a written informed consent and were given schedule for follow up before enrollment. Confidentiality and data protection measures were implemented throughout the study.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square test and were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were compared with the Student’s t-test and presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normal data with the Mann-Whitney U test and presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial, no statistically significant differences were observed between two and four weeks of preoperative ureteral stenting regarding ureteral access sheath insertion success, stone-free rate, operative time, perioperative complications, or stent-related symptoms. Although the 4-week group demonstrated a numerically higher UAS insertion success rate, the absolute difference was small and did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that extending stent dwell time beyond two weeks may not provide substantial additional clinical benefit in patients undergoing secondary flexible ureteroscopy after failed primary access.

The placement of ureteric stent before FURS is associated with a higher success rate of UAS placement. This was evident in the study done by Yuk et al. (2020) who found that UAS placement in the stented group was more successful (93.8%) if compared to (85.3%) in the non-stented group (p = 0.023). Furthermore, the sheath size is larger in a stented group [12]. In our study, failed primary access was found in 22% of cases, which was lower than the failure rate found in the study done by Yavuzsan et al. (2022) who found a 40.9% primary failure rate [13].

In contrast to our study, Lavoie et al. (2021), found a much lower rate of failed primary access (8%). In their study, most patients with failed access had proximal ureteric stones (5 out of 8 patients (62.5%)) and Alkhamees et al. (2020) who reported primary failure rate of 10.7% [3, 14].

The timing of the second intervention by FURS after stent insertion is not well determined. In the study done by Hoare et al. (2021), the second FURS was not performed on any patient in their study (119 patients) before ten days. The second FURS was performed within 10 to 13 days in 19 patients (16%), after 14 to 21 days in 70 patients (58%) and after 21 days in 30 patients (25.2%). The success rate of secondary FURS was 99.2% (118/119 patients), with only one patient having failed secondary FURS after 14 days of stent insertion due to persistent ureteric spasm. This high success rate may be due to direct FURS insertion without UAS placement [15].

This was also demonstrated in the study by Sahin et al. (2024), who found that the longer the duration of the indwelling stent, the better the UAS insertion. The success rate of UAS insertion in their study was 67.9%, 75.8%, 91.2%, 95.2% and 97.5% after 14, 28, 45, 60, and more than 60 days respectively [9].

In our study, placement of UAS was successful in 27 patients (90%) in 2-week group and in 28 patients (93.3%) in 4-week group, which was statistically insignificant (p = 0.64).

This was similar to the study by Sunget al. (2020), who reported successful UAS insertion in 97.3% of patients after pre-stenting for 1–2 weeks [16].

Yuk et al. (2020), found that 93.8% of pre-stented patients underwent successful UAS insertion and adequate stone treatment at the time of definitive URS if compared to non-stented patients (93.8% vs. 87.5%; p = 0.038) [12].

With its known relaxing effect on the intramural ureteral part and possible easy passage of UAS, in our study, preoperative alpha-blocker use was not statistically significant in both groups. This was also comparable to the study by Erturhan et al. (2019), who found that despite the passage of UAS from the first attempt in their study group (15/23 patients (65.2%)) was easier than in the control group 11/25 patients (44%)), it did not reach the significance level (p = 0.141) [17].

In contrast to our study, Koo et al. (2018), who used a device to measure the force of UAS to reach the pelvic ureteric junction. They found that the study group who received preoperative alpha-blockers had much lower force than the control group [18].

The insertion of a ureteric stent may be associated with several stent-related complications such as UTI, hematuria and irritative lower urinary tract symptoms.

In our study, UTI was found in one patient (3.3%) in 2-week group and in two patients (6.7%) in 4-week group (p = 0.838). This was similar to the study by Hanna et al. (2020), who found UTI in (4.3%) (1 out of 23 patients who had the stent for less than one month), 26% (18 out of 68 patients who had the stent for a period of 1-2.5 months), and 26% (21 out of 81 patients who had the stent for more than 2.5 months) [19].

In the present study, the SFR was comparable in the two groups (24 patients (80%) in 2-week group and 22 patients (73.3%) in 4-week group). A study by Sahin et al. (2024) found no statistically significant difference between groups regarding the SFR. It was 77.4%, 76.6%, 71.6%, 74.6% and 70.9% after stent durations of 0–15, 16–30, 31–45, 46–60 and more than 60 days, respectively [9].

In the study done by Lee et al. (2019), there was no significant difference between the short- and long-term preoperative stent duration of less than and more than 7 days in terms of operative time and other perioperative complications [20].

In the current study, there was no statistically significant difference in the operative time between the two groups. However, the study by Sahin et al. (2024) found that pre-stented patients for more than two weeks showed statistically significant prolonged operative time. They attributed this unanticipated problem to pre-stenting increased renal edema from the stent’s presence as a foreign entity [9].

In our study, although the average USSQ score in 4-week group was higher than in 2-week group, there was no difference in the symptom score between both groups, which is comparable with a recent study by Polat et al. (2024), who reported that workday loss did not show a statistically significant correlation with stent indwelling time. However, in the same study, there was a statistically significant difference between the median work performance score done with a stent and one month following stent removal (p < 0.001) [21].

One of the serious complications that may result from UAS insertion is ureteric injury [22]. However, presenting is believed to lower the risk of this complication [23]. In our study, the rate of ureteric injuries was equal in both groups where both were 6.7% of cases, all injuries were minor (Traxer grade 1 or 2) and all were treated conservatively with stent insertion and resolved without sequalae. Consequently, we believe that preoperative stenting duration has no role in lowering the risk of ureteral injuries,

We believe that strengths of this study include its randomized controlled design, surgeon and data collector blinding during the second-stage procedure, inclusion of a homogeneous cohort with failed primary access, and using standardized postoperative assessment using non-contrast computed tomography to assess SFR.

Our study has limitations, such as the small number of patients in both groups as no formal prior sample size calculation was performed, and the sample size was based on feasibility. Although both groups demonstrated high success rates, the relatively small sample may have limited the statistical power to detect modest but clinically relevant differences between groups and may increase the risk of type II error. and the study being conducted in a single center and using a single UAS size (10/12 Fr) may limit external validity and generalizability. Further multicenter studies with large number of patients will support these findings.

Moreover, the trial population represented the difficult-access subset and findings may not generalize to all FURS patients. Another limitation was that, our study was designed as superiority study and although no statistically significant differences were observed between the two stenting durations, this study was not designed or powered as a non-inferiority trial. Therefore, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing non-inferiority or equivalence. Future trials with predefined non-inferiority margins and adequate power are warranted. Additionally, Alpha-blocker use was not standardized, which may be considered as a potential confounder. Also, the laser settings and operative strategy being left to surgeon discretion may represent a potential risk for variability.

Conclusion

Ureteric stenting prior to FURS facilitates subsequent UAS insertion after primary access failure. In this randomized trial, no statistically significant differences were observed between 2-week and 4-week preoperative stenting regarding UAS insertion success, stone-free rate, operative time, or complication rates. These findings suggest that prolonging stent dwell time beyond 2 weeks may not provide a substantial additional benefit in this patient population.
