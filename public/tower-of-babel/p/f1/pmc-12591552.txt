Vascular Niches Are the Primary Hotspots in Cardiac Aging
Authors: David Rodriguez Morales, Veronica Larcher, Mariano Ruz Jurado, Denada Arifaj, Lukas S. Tombor, Lukas Zanders, Andreas M. Zeiher, Christoph Kuppe, David John, Julian U.G. Wagner, Marcel H. Schulz, Stefanie Dimmeler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Aging is a major, yet unmodifiable, cardiovascular risk factor and is associated with vascular alterations, increased cardiac fibrosis, and inflammation, all of which contribute to impaired cardiac function. However, the microenvironment inciting age-related alterations within the multicellular architecture of the cardiac tissue is unknown.METHODS:We investigated local microenvironments in aged mice hearts by applying an integrative approach combining single-nucleus RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics of 3- and 18-month-old mice. We defined distinct cardiac niches and studied changes in their cellular composition and functional characteristics. We treated mice with broad-spectrum senolytics dasatinib and quercetin, and endothelial-specific senolytic fisetin and studied their effects on senescence and macrophage populations.RESULTS:Integration of spatial transcriptomics data across 3- and 18-month-old hearts allowed the identification of 11 cardiac niches, which were characterized by distinct cellular composition and functional signatures. Aging did not alter the overall proportions of cardiac niches but led to distinct regional changes, particularly in the left ventricle. While cardiomyocyte-enriched niches showed disrupted circadian clock gene expression, vascular niches showed major changes in proinflammatory and profibrotic signatures and altered cellular composition. We particularly identified larger vessel–associated cellular niches as key hotspots for activated fibroblasts and bone marrow–derived Lyve1− (lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1) and resident Lyve1+ macrophages in aged hearts, with interactions of both cell types through the C3:C3ar1 (complement C3 and Complement C3a receptor 1) axis. These niches were also enriched in senescent cells exhibiting high expression of immune evasion mechanisms that may impair senescent cell clearance. Removal of senescent cells by senolytics reduced the presence of Lyve1− macrophages.CONCLUSIONS:Our findings indicate that the perivascular microenvironment is particularly susceptible to age-related changes and serves as a primary site for inflammation-driven aging, so-called inflammaging. This study provides new insights into how aging reshapes cardiac cellular architecture, highlighting vessel-associated niches as potential therapeutic targets for age-related cardiac dysfunction.

We generated spatial transcriptomics from 3- and 18-month-old murine hearts and increased the resolution by integrating it with single-nucleus RNA sequencing. We mapped all the major cell types present in the heart and studied changes in their spatial distribution during aging. In addition, we identified cardiac niches with distinct distributions and signatures. We identified the large vessel–associated vascular niches as the primary hotspot of age-associated changes. The vascular niches were characterized by a proinflammatory and profibrotic environment with increased abundance of bone marrow–derived macrophages and activated fibroblasts. Senescence was also particularly enriched in these regions with a colocalization of immune inhibitory molecules promoting the immune cell evasion. Aging was also associated with an immune imbalance with a higher infiltration of bone marrow–derived macrophages in the perivascular region, which is reduced after the treatment with senolytics.

Aging is a natural physiological process associated with structural, functional, and senescence-related changes that can impair cardiovascular health and increase the risk of developing cardiovascular diseases, the leading cause of death worldwide. The aging heart is characterized by increased fibrosis, myocardial stiffening, and reduced cardiac compliance.1 Diastolic dysfunction becomes more pronounced, resulting in elevated filling pressures. In addition, both microvascular and macrovascular function deteriorate with age, contributing to endothelial dysfunction, reduced perfusion, and increased vascular stiffness. Aging also affects cardiac immune cells, particularly inflammatory macrophages, shifting them toward a more proinflammatory state.1 Particularly, Ccr2+ (C-C motif chemokine receptor 2) bone marrow–derived macrophages increase, driving chronic low-grade inflammation. This proinflammatory environment, known as inflammaging, plays a key role in cardiac senescence by promoting oxidative stress, DNA damage, and the activation of senescence-related pathways. These immune and structural changes accelerate cardiovascular disease progression and increase the risk of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in older individuals.2,3

In recent years, advancements in omics technologies, particularly single-cell RNA sequencing, have revolutionized the study of cardiac biology. They provide an unbiased framework for examining the cellular heterogeneity in the heart,4,5 enabling the precise characterization of gene expression profiles with single-cell resolution. However, the information generated lacks the spatial context. Several platforms have been developed to perform spatial transcriptomics, allowing to study changes in the spatial dynamics of the heart.6–8

To explore the spatial organization and molecular changes in the aging heart, we generated spatial transcriptomics data obtained through the 10× Genomics Visium Platform from 3- and 18-month-old murine hearts. Integration of this data with single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) enabled the characterization of cardiac niches and the assessment of changes in cell composition and interactions. Notably, vascular niches emerged as key regions of aging-associated alterations, showing increased expression of fibrosis-related genes and a shift in macrophage populations, fostering a profibrotic and proinflammatory environment. These niches also served as primary hotspots for cellular senescence in the aging heart. Treatment with senolytics reduced the infiltration of bone marrow–derived Lyve1− (lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1) macrophages and shifted the balance toward an enrichment of tissue-resident macrophages.

Results

Multiomics Integrative Analysis for the Characterization of the Heart

We applied an integrative approach to explore and characterize the cardiac changes upon aging using previously published murine snRNA-seq9,10 and newly generated spatial transcriptomics data obtained through the 10× Genomics Visium Platform (Figure 1A and 1B).

The snRNA-seq consisted of 13 samples from the hearts of 3- (n=4) and 18 to 20-month-old mice (n=9).9,10 After quality control, 64 845 nuclei were retained (Figure S1A). Nuclei were integrated and clustered, accounting for batch effects (Figure S1B through S1D). Cell type identification was performed using CellTypist,11 followed by manual cross-check with a curated list of gene markers4,12,13 (Figure 1C). For finer annotation, we performed subclustering of endothelial cells (ECs), myeloid cells, and fibroblasts. We identified 5 EC subpopulations, arterial, venous, lymphatic, endocardial, and capillary, each defined by distinct marker expression (Figure S1E). The myeloid population was subdivided into 2 subgroups: Ccr2-Lyve1+ tissue–resident macrophage Ccr2+Lyve1− bone marrow–derived macrophage14 (Figure S1F). In the fibroblast population, we annotated activated fibroblasts based on the expression of Postn and Meox1 (Figure S1G).

The spatial transcriptomic data set was obtained from the hearts of 3- and 18-month-old mice (n=5 per condition). To maximize the retention of spatial information, we performed a permissive quality control, resulting in a total of 15 838 spots (Figure S2A through S2C). To explore the spatial organization of the heart, we integrated and clustered the spots, accounting for both spatial information and batch effects (Figure S2D through S2F). We identified 11 clusters, subsequently called niches (Figure 1B; Figure S2F), representing shared domains across sections. Principal component analysis of pseudobulk counts showed that these niches were the second most significant source of variation (Figure S2G).

To improve the resolution of the spatial transcriptomics, we integrated the data with the snRNA-seq using cell2location,15 a deconvolution method that maps cell types and estimates their abundance in individual spots. We then calculated the proportion of each cell type per spot, observing expected distributions (Figure 1D), with an average of 5 cells per spot. Cardiomyocytes were the most abundant cell type, accounting for 65% of the total area (Figure S2H), followed by capillary EC (CapEC) and pericytes. The pericyte to CapEC ratio was 1:2.4, reflecting previous histological findings.16

Impact of Aging on the Cellular and Molecular Landscape of the Cardiac Niches

Aging leads to a progressive structural and functional deterioration of the heart. However, these changes may differ across anatomic regions. We explored the structural, molecular, and cellular age–dependent alterations across the cardiac niches. Their spatial distribution was examined by manually annotating 3 major anatomic regions: the right ventricle (RV), the septum, and the left ventricle (LV), which was subdivided into 3 sections: the subendocardial inner, middle, and subepicardial outer ventricle (Figure S3A). The LV displayed a higher diversity in niche composition compared with the RV (Figure 2A), reflecting the structural, physiological, and compositional differences of these regions.17 Niches 1 and 9 were enriched in the endocardial region (Figure 2A). Niches 0 and 8 were predominantly found in the RV. In addition, niche 0 showed an increased abundance toward the epicardial region of the LV, a pattern also observed for niche 5. Niche 10 abundance increased from the endocardial toward the epicardial region of the LV. The remaining niches were evenly distributed across all sections. With aging, the overall abundance of the cardiac niches remained comparable, except for niches 5 (increased) and 7 (decreased). However, these changes were not statistically significant, indicating no regional bias during sample preparation (Figure 2B; Figure S3B and S3C).

Aging Augments Fibrotic Gene Expression and Modulates Macrophage Populations in Vascular Niches

Next, we assessed the cellular composition of the niches and their functional differences using the estimated cell-type proportions and performing differential gene expression analysis (Figure 2C and 2D). Niche 7 was enriched in arterial, venous, lymphatic ECs, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and inflammatory cells. Consistent with these observations, processes related to vascular development and blood vessel morphogenesis were enriched (Figure 2D; Table S1), suggesting that niche 7 represents large vessel niches, comprising arteries, veins, and lymphatic vessels. In aging, an increase in collagen metabolism was observed (Figure S4A; Table S2), likely reflecting perivascular fibrosis associated with aging.18 In addition, a metabolic switch was noted, transitioning from lipid catabolism and glycogen biosynthesis to glycolysis and lipid formation (Table S2). Moreover, terms for synapse pruning, microglia activation and proliferation, and neuron apoptotic process were elevated in aging (Figure S4A), aligning with the recently described neurorepulsive environment of the aged vasculature.9

The cellular composition of niche 0 was comparable to niche 7 but contained predominantly epicardial cells, reflecting its spatial enrichment in the epicardial region (epicardial niche). This niche was characterized by immune-related processes, particularly antigen presentation, which were more pronounced in aging (Figure S4B). In contrast, processes related to EC remodeling and epithelial cell development were enriched at 3 months (Figure S4B).

Niche 10, enriched in the septum and LV, comprised immune cells and, to some extent, vascular ECs (Figure 2C). In line with this, it showed enrichment in processes related to myeloid immunity and fatty acid metabolism, with an overrepresentation of processes related to microglia cell activation and macrophages in aging (Figure S4C). Terms related to collagen metabolism were also observed.

Consistent with its spatial localization to the endocardial region, niche 1 was enriched in endocardial ECs and was identified as the endocardial niche. This niche was characterized by an enrichment of genes related to the regulation of heart rate and blood pressure. In the 3-month-old hearts, genes associated with junctions, focal adhesion, and endothelial barrier formation were prominent (Figure S4D), while in aging, processes related to microglia activation and macrophage polarization were predominant.

Niche 2 comprises primarily larger vessel ECs, with fewer SMC and higher enrichment of pericytes (Figure 2C), resembling regions that are a mixture of small arterioles and venules. Processes related to muscle cell development and adaptation, as well as vascular remodeling processes, were observed at 3 months, while genes associated with microglia activation were overrepresented in aging (Figure S4E).

All vessel-associated vascular niches (0, 1, 2, 7, and 10) in the 18-month-old hearts exhibit similar gene enrichment patterns, including unexpected microglia-related terms. While the heart lacks microglia, it harbors the resident and bone marrow–derived macrophages, with functional similarities to microglia. These niches were enriched in total macrophages and bone marrow–derived Ccr2+ macrophages, particularly in aging (Figure 2C and 2E). Microglia-related genes (Grn, Trem2, and Tyrobp) were expressed in both resident and bone marrow–derived macrophages (Figure S5A). In the male aged hearts, Ccr2+ macrophages showed upregulation of proinflammatory genes such as Fgr and Myo1f19,20 (Table S3). Tissue-resident macrophages showed enrichment for profibrotic genes (Cd209, Timp2, and Ccl8)21,22 (Table S3). These genes were also upregulated in the female spatial transcriptomics data (Figure S5B) and were enriched in vascular niches (Figure 2F). Thus, vascular niches form a complex microenvironment where macrophages drive both a proinflammatory and profibrotic environment.

Capillary and Cardiomyocyte Niches Undergo Remodeling and Alterations of the Circadian Clock Genes

Niche 3, composed of cardiomyocytes, CapEC, and pericytes, was localized to the septum and the LV middle region. Catabolism-related processes were observed, suggesting a higher metabolic activity. In the aged hearts, genes associated with hepatic stellate cell activation (Acta2, Cygb, and Rps6ka1) were expressed (Figure S5C; Table S2). Because activation of hepatic stellate cells occurs during liver fibrosis, these findings may suggest fibrotic pericyte activation and secretion of extracellular matrix proteins. Niche 6 had a similar composition but showed fewer cardiomyocytes and a higher presence of other cell types, such as fibroblasts. Upon aging, increased membrane trafficking activity and cardiac muscle repolarization terms (Figure S5D) were observed. Both capillary-enriched niches showed extracellular matrix remodeling–related processes at 3 months with alterations in the cell-cell junctions.

Niches 4, 5, 8, and 9 were cardiomyocyte-enriched, with niches 4 and 9 showing a higher presence of CapECs but not pericytes (Figure 2C). This suggests that they represent ECs in proximity to cardiomyocytes. Spatially, the niches mapped to distinct spatial locations: niche 4 to the epicardium and the septum, niche 9 to the endocardium (Figure 2A), niche 8 to the RV, and niche 5 to the epicardial region (Figure 2A). Niches 4, 8, and 9 showed an enrichment for metabolic processes (Figure 2D). The capillary cardiomyocyte–enriched niches showed an overrepresentation of cardiac muscle repolarization and circadian clock terms (Figure S5C through S5H; Table S2), with similar gene regulation in female spatial and male snRNA-seq data (Figure S5I). Processes related to remodeling dominated in 3-month-old hearts, including tight junction assembly, connective tissue replacement, and cell development (Figure S5C through S5H).

Cellular Interactions and Macrophage Activation in the Vascular Niche of the Aging Mouse Heart

Analysis of the cardiac niches revealed an enrichment of a profibrotic macrophage phenotype, particularly in the large vessel niche. To further understand the associations between cell types and potential interactions leading to macrophage activation, we used mistyR,23 a framework designed to assess cell type relationships reflecting colocalization or coexclusion of 2 cell types. We assessed interactions at 3 levels: (1) same-spot interactions, (2) hexamer space (direct neighbors) interactions, and (3) extended tissue space interactions (Figure 3A and 3B; Figure S6A and S6B).

As expected, the absence of cardiomyocytes correlated with an increased presence of other cell types, underscoring the organizational role of stromal cells (Figure 3B; Figure S6C). In line with the known anatomy of the vasculature, SMCs were associated with arterial ECs, while pericytes coincided with CapEC, even when extending the analysis to the hexamer space (Figure S6A and S6B). Inflammatory cell interactions differed in the aging heart: tissue-resident macrophages colocalized with fibroblasts (Figure 3C), while bone marrow–derived macrophages colocalized with B cells (Figure S6D). Inference of cell communication events using HoloNet24 predicted activation of the C3:C3ar1 (complement C3 and Complement C3a receptor 1) pathway in regions enriched in tissue-resident macrophages and fibroblasts (Figure 3D and 3E), with a significant upregulation in the aged heart (Figure 3F). The snRNA-seq further supported an interaction of both cell types, with fibroblasts expressing the ligand (C3), and macrophages expressing the receptor (C3ar1), both of which were upregulated in aging (Figure S6E). Activated fibroblasts also colocalized with SMCs (Figure 3B and 3G) though SMCs influence decreased with distance, whereas macrophages emerged as a stronger predictor for the presence of activated fibroblasts (Figure S6A and S6B).

Pathway Activity Dynamics Across the Cardiac Niches

Next, we investigated pathway activities using PROGENy,25 observing a stronger activation of the EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) pathway in the aged cardiac niches (Figure 3H), particularly within the epicardial region and the vasculature (niches 8, 0, and 7). The WNT (wingless-type MMTV integration site family) and TNFα (tumor necrosis factor a) pathways were also enriched in the aged heart. Hypoxia-related gene signatures were upregulated in niches enriched with CapECs (niches 2 and 3), as well as in niche 7. TGF-β (transforming growth factor beta) signaling showed higher activation in the aged heart, particularly in niche 7, aligning with the observed colocalization of SMCs and activated fibroblasts. Finally, the tumor suppressor gene p53, known for its role in maintaining cardiac homeostasis,26 was downregulated in most niches, except for cardiomyocyte-enriched niches (niche 4, 5, and 9).

Identification and Annotation of Large Vessels

We identified niche 7 as a vascular niche comprising all the major large vessels. However, vasculature regions were also associated with other niches, and the vessel subtypes could not be distinguished. In mice, arteries range from 150 to 160 µm, and veins can reach up to 250 µm in diameter,27,28 while the capture area of the spatial transcriptomic platform was 55 µm, and the spots were spaced 100 µm apart, leading to the partial capture of vessels. To overcome this, we extended the analysis to direct neighbors in the hexamer space (Figure 4A), aggregating the estimated cell abundance for the different cell types within a 100-µm radius, which represent direct neighboring spots. Next, we binarized the matrix, where a cell type is considered present if the aggregated abundance exceeds 1. The binarized matrix was then used to annotate spots containing the vessels. For each type of vessel, at least 1 EC subtype was detected within the hexamer space. In addition, for arteries, we imposed another constraint, requiring the presence of at least 1 SMC. Our strategy enabled the differentiation of arteries, veins, and lymphatic vessels, confirmed by hematoxylin and eosin staining (Figure 4B; Figure S7A). However, limited resolution caused mixed cell compositions and annotation for vessels in proximity (Figure 4C and 4D). The mixed regions contained arteries and veins (MixVasc), arteries and lymphatics (Art_Lymph), and veins and lymphatics (Vein_Lymph). These clusters were excluded from subsequent analysis. Differential gene expression analysis between each vessel and the nonvascular spots revealed an enrichment of marker genes characteristic of these regions (Figure 4C). Overall, our strategy provides an accurate supervised approach for identifying and annotating large anatomic structures, accounting for differential cell-type composition.

Age-Related Changes in Vascular Composition and Cell Dynamics

We next analyzed aging effects on vessels. Vessel quantification revealed a tendency for reduced vascularization, with a significant decrease observed for veins and lymphatics (Figure 4E). Overrepresented cell types in vascular compared with the nonvascular regions (Figure 4F) included tissue-resident and bone marrow–derived macrophage. Immunostaining confirmed CD68+ enrichment in the adventitial versus interstitial region (P=6.5×10−12; ANOVA test; Figure 4G and 4H). While interstitial macrophage composition was unchanged, aging shifted adventitial macrophages from CD68+Lyve1+ (resident) to CD68+Lyve1− (bone marrow–derived; Figure 4I and 4J). Aged lymphatic vessels exhibit fewer macrophages, suggesting decreased drainage.

Myocardial B cells usually remain intravascular with limited endothelial crossing.29 However, we observed B cells’ enrichment in veins (Figure 4F). T cells and adipocytes were present across the different regions, with a noticeable increase upon aging (Figure 4F), while fibroblasts were particularly enriched in the vascular region, with arteries showing more activated fibroblasts (Figure 4F).

Senescence Is Predominantly Induced in the Vascular Niches

Cellular senescence, a hallmark of aging, is essential for development and homeostasis but increases with age, driving structural and functional microenvironmental changes. To explore its spatial distribution, we identified hotspots of senescence (Figure 5A). Using established senescence gene sets30–34 and differentially expressed genes between 3- and 18-month-old samples, we compiled a list of 593 aging-sensitive genes (Table S4). We scored spots based on the expression of these genes (Figure 5B and 5C; Figure S7B), defining hotspots of senescence. The senescence score was highest in these hotspots and decreased with distance (Figure S7C). While 3-month hearts showed limited hotspots, aged hearts had higher and broader scores (Figure S7B and S7C). Senescence hotspots were particularly enriched in SMC, macrophages, and fibroblasts (Figure 5D), with aging adding cardiomyocytes and CapECs, indicating senescence spread (Figure S5D and S5E). The hotspots of senescence were primarily localized to the vascular niche (niche 7; Figure 5E), extending into the endocardial (niche 1) and epicardial niche (niche 0) with aging. Among vessels, arteries were most affected, followed by veins and lymphatics (Figure S7F). Senescence was also more abundant in immune cell-rich niches 0 and 1.

To determine the relevance in humans, we analyzed LV spatial transcriptomics data from human hearts,8 stratified by age with 65 years as a cutoff. We identified a similar vascular niche pattern, characterized by an enrichment of SMCs, immune cells, and activated fibroblasts in human niches h7 and h0 (Figure S8A and S8B), with fibroblast-macrophage colocalization illustrated in Figure S8C. As in mice, senescence was more prominent in individuals aged 65 to 70 years than 60 to 65 years, particularly in vascular-enriched niches h0 and h7 (Figure S8D through S8F). These findings suggest a comparable pattern of niche-specific senescence in the aging human heart.

To validate the transcriptomic data, we assessed senescence by performing SA-β-galactosidase activity and immunofluorescence staining in 3- and 18-month-old mice hearts (Figure 5F and 5G; Figure S9A). Consistent with the spatial transcriptomics, senescent cells increased in the adventitial region of large vessels in both sexes (Figure 5F and 5G; Figure S9A). Interestingly, we observed a correlation between the hotspots of senescence and genes associated with the recruitment of immune cells and immune inhibitory pathways, including Cd47, Sirpa (signal-regulatory protein alpha), Cd24a, and Clec4a (Figure 5H). Cells expressing genes associated with immune inhibitory pathways were increased in aged mice (Figure S9B). Upregulation of the immune inhibitory Sirpα in aging was additionally validated on the protein level by histology (Figure S9C). Upregulation of Sirpα was mainly detected in macrophages in the perivascular area surrounding larger vessels (Figure S9C), while the ligand Cd47 tended to be higher in both the adventitial and interstitial regions (Figure S9D). Enrichment of Sirpα and Cd47 was also observed in the human spatial transcriptomics data (Figure S9E).

Senolytic Treatment Re-Establishes the Immune Balance in Vascular Niches

To address to what extend cellular senescence contributes to the alterations of the vascular niches during aging, we assessed tissue sections from mice treated with the broad-spectrum senolytics dasatinib and quercetin9 (Figure 6A). As expected, dasatinib and quercetin treatment markedly reduced the number of senescent cells, particularly in perivascular regions (Figure 6B and 6C). A reduction was also observed in interstitial areas although the baseline prevalence of senescent cells was substantially lower in this region (Figure S10A). To gain further insights into the effect of selective interference with senescent vascular cells, we additionally used fisetin (Figure 6D), which was developed as an endothelial-selective senolytic35 but was recently shown to also affect SMC.36 Fisetin removed senescent cells around the larger vessels (Figure 6E and 6F; Figure S10B) and improved diastolic heart function and fibrosis to a similar extent as dasatinib and quercetin (Figure S10C through S10F). Both treatments mainly removed senescent SMC, which were the predominant senescent cell type in the perivascular regions (Figure S11A through S11D). However, clearance of senescent cells was more effective with dasatinib and quercetin treatment compared with fisetin.

Next, we assessed the effects of senolytic treatment on recruited immune cells. Both treatments reduced CD68+ macrophages in the adventitial region (Figure 6G and 6H). The reduction in macrophage was primarily driven by a decline in bone marrow–derived Lyve1− macrophages, while tissue-resident Lyve1+ macrophages reverted to levels seen in 3-month-old mice (Figure 6I and 6J). In the interstitial regions, total macrophage numbers were unchanged, but senolytics shifted them toward tissue-resident cells (Figure S12A through S12C). These findings suggest that cellular senescence contributes to the age-associated imbalance between bone marrow–derived and tissue-resident macrophage populations in the heart.

Discussion

Aging arises from accumulated molecular and cellular damage, driving structural and compositional changes in organs. Spatial transcriptomics of 3- and 18-month hearts revealed 11 cardiac niches with distinct composition and functions. While overall proportions remained stable, regional shifts emerged, with the LV showing greater diversity and exclusive enrichment for some niches like 6 and 9. This may reflect the different developmental origin37 and differences in pressure and wall stress conditions between the ventricles.

ECs, the most abundant nonmyocyte cell type in the heart,38 form a network essential for perfusion and nutrient support. A key finding of this study is that aging primarily affected vascular niches, especially large vessels, which showed profibrotic and senescence signatures, reflected by increased collagen metabolism, activated fibroblasts, and TGF-β signaling. With aging, resident macrophages decreased, while bone marrow–derived macrophages increased, colocalizing with fibroblasts and creating a complex inflammatory milieu that may drive vascular dysfunction.

Among the communication pathways enriched in aging, the complement C3:C3ar1 interaction stood out. Complement C3 and its receptor C3ar1 play complex roles in cardiac function, exhibiting both protective and potentially detrimental effects depending on the context and site of activation. While C3 provides protective effects in ischemia/reperfusion injury models,39 it contributes to RV dysfunction and fibrosis.40 Our study predicts that fibroblast-derived C3 activates C3ar1 in macrophages within the aging heart. Notably, C3ar1 promotes macrophage polarization toward an M2-like (anti-inflammatory) phenotype.41 While this may help suppress inflammation, M2-like macrophages are also known to drive fibrosis, a hallmark of aging.42 Recent studies suggest that cardiac fibroblasts exhibit functions in innate and adaptive immunity after injury.43 It is interesting to note that we detect a similar activation pattern in the aging heart, specifically in the vascular niches, suggesting distinct regional roles for fibroblasts.

Besides the profibrotic and proinflammatory environment, vascular niches were identified as hotspots of senescence. The enrichment of senescence in these regions is also supported by the metabolic shift toward glycolysis and lipid formation observed, which is consistent with senescence-associated metabolic alterations44,45 and suggests a vicious cycle that sustains a proinflammatory, senescent vascular environment.

Under physiological conditions, senescent cells are removed by macrophages and other immune cells through a process called immunosurveillance.46 Clearance of the senescent cells by macrophages can be disturbed by the expression of immune inhibitory molecules, which allow cells to evade macrophage phagocytosis and immune surveillance.47 We observed immune evasion genes, including Cd47, Sirpa, Cd24a, and Clec4a,48,49 enriched in the senescence hotspots. Histological analysis revealed that Sirpα was mainly found in macrophages in the aged perivascular area, which also showed the accumulation of senescent cells. High Sirpα levels are expected to result in a reduced clearance of cells and may contribute to the enrichment of the senescent cells in these niches. Eliminating senescent cells with senolytics restored immune balance in vascular niches, reducing macrophage accumulation and enriching for tissue-resident cells. This suggests a self-reinforcing interaction between senescent and macrophages in the perivascular space that may drive chronic inflammation and inflammaging.

We also observed age-associated changes in cardiomyocyte-enriched niches, showing a disruption of circadian clock genes. Circadian genes are critical for cardiovascular physiology, and aging has been shown to disrupt the circadian patterns.50 This dysregulation may underlie age-related electrical disturbances and arrhythmias, consistent with enriched muscle repolarization processes. Prior work links disrupted cardiomyocyte clock function to impaired conduction and arrhythmia risk.51 Besides the stronger activation of TGF-β in vascular niches, the analysis of other signaling pathways showed stronger activation of EGFR, WNT, and TNFα, while p53 showed less activation in some niches. The chronic activation of these pathways has been implicated in pathophysiological cardiac remodeling. For example, TNFα levels correlate with the severity of heart failure52 and can induce LV dysfunction and cardiomyopathy.53 Chronic activation of the WNT/β-catenin has a profibrotic and hypertrophic effect in the adult heart.54 On the other hand, downregulation of p53 has been linked to age-related cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure.26 Altogether, the dysregulation of these pathways highlights regions that are susceptible to pathophysiological cardiac remodeling.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of identified niches depended on clustering resolution; we selected a value balancing coarse versus overly fine, redundant clustering. Second, the spatial transcriptomics platform lacks true single-cell resolution, limiting precise attribution of molecular changes to specific cell types. Third, most findings are based on gene rather than protein expression, which may not always align, and we omit posttranscriptional regulation. While some findings were validated by histology, the causal roles of dysregulated genes remain untested. We also did not systematically compare sex differences. The limited human samples may restrict generalizability.

In summary, our findings reveal structural, cellular, and molecular remodeling across cardiac niches with aging, predominantly affecting vascular niches, emphasizing the vasculature’s central role in organ aging. The enrichment of macrophages, activated fibroblasts, and senescent cells highlights immune-fibroblast interactions as key drivers of cardiac aging, while circadian disruptions point to therapeutic targets. Future work should dissect the mechanisms of these niche-specific changes and explore targeted interventions to preserve cardiac function.
