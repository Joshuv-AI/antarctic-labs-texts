Photoactivatable Synthetic Exosomes for RNA‐Based Communication Between Artificial Cells and Living Cells
Authors: Alexander B Cook, Siwen Sun, Yudong Li, Jari Scheerstra, Jan CM van Hest
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial cells are self‐assembled microstructures engineered to replicate the functions of natural cells, such as the capacity to interact and communicate. Until now, communication between artificial and living cells has mainly been based on the exchange of small molecules. An important communication pathway in living systems, however, involves the exchange of bioactive molecules such as neurotransmitters and nucleic acids via their protected transport with vesicles or exosomes. Here, we describe a modular strategy to construct artificial cells able to regulate mammalian cell protein expression via vesicle‐based RNA communication. These artificial cells are assembled from amylose‐based complex coacervates stabilized by a terpolymer membrane, with polymersomes containing RNA sequestered inside. The block copolymer vesicles have photocleavable surface groups, allowing charge switching behavior and expulsion from the overall positively charged coacervate lumen. The ability of our system to transmit RNA signals to natural cells was demonstrated with in vitro artificial cell‐natural cell cocultures. Considering the crucial role of extracellular vesicles in genetic information transfer in nature, this artificial exosome design is an important step toward replicating nucleic acid communication pathways, further improving life‐like behavior in artificial cell systems.Keywords: Artificial cells, Biomaterials, Polymersomes, RNA communication, Supramolecular systems

Introduction

All multicellular systems composed of diverse cell types need to coordinate behaviors between individual cells.[ 1 , 2 ] In multicellular eukaryotic organisms this is achieved via different signaling pathways locally (paracrine) or over larger distances (endocrine).[ 3 ] Also in prokaryotes, consortia or quorums play a signaling role to control their behavior based on the total population density.[ 4 ] Cells communicate, exchange information, and sense their environment through different signaling strategies. While some signals are electrical or mechanical, most rely on molecules that diffuse through the surrounding environment, which can be released by signaling cells and are received by target cells. When errors in signaling interactions and cell information processing occur, diseases including cancer, autoimmunity, and diabetes can start to develop. Although the exchange of proteins, cytokines, ions, and metabolites is well established, RNA exchange has only recently been recognized as an important regulator of communication between various cell types or organs.[ 5 ] RNA communication is mediated by extracellular vesicles (or exosomes) to enable RNA protection from degradation in serum/extracellular fluid and uptake into cells via endocytosis or membrane fusion.[ 6 ] Indeed, along with eukaryotic cells and bacteria, it was recently shown that archaea also exchange RNA using vesicles, showing that the process is universal to life.[ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ]

In the field of artificial cell research, establishing life‐like functionality is a grand challenge that spans multiple fields, including synthetic biology, macromolecular and supramolecular chemistry, and bioengineering.[ 11 , 12 ] Artificial cells can help researchers understand the fundamental and dynamic processes of life, such as communication pathways, growth and division, metabolism, and motility.[ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ] Recently, more attention has been paid to the mimicry of communication aspects of living cells.[ 17 , 18 , 19 ] For example, enzyme cascade reactions involving diffusion of small molecule substrates or products between artificial cell populations,[ 20 , 21 , 22 ] or between artificial cells and natural cells[ 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] are becoming more widespread. The move toward genetic information signaling is an exciting development that has been first studied with DNA signaling—which has become increasingly complex with signaling circuits that can now program feedback loops,[ 30 , 31 ] amplification and logic tasks,[ 30 ] and differentiation.[ 21 ] Most systems rely on cell‐free protein expression, DNA‐strand displacement circuits, and the diffusion of DNA strands between artificial cell populations.[ 30 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 ]

While incorporation of transcription/translation machinery into artificial cells allows protein outputs, incorporation of RNA signaling will open a new paradigm in artificial cells. Expression of RNA origami in synthetic cells was recently achieved by the group of Gopfrich,[ 39 ] and diffusion of RNA strands between protocells in idealized buffer solutions by de Greef and colleagues;[ 40 ] however, the transport of RNA signals through complex media remains challenging, which becomes more relevant if communication pathways between artificial and living cells need to be established. In addition, the transition to use of RNA requires more temporal control over signal transmission and additional compartmentalization in protective vesicle transporters due to instability compared to DNA, particularly in serum or cell culture media. Various stimuli response mechanisms can be used for this temporal control, such as pH, redox, and enzymatic triggers;[ 41 , 42 ] however, in this article we utilize light as it can be externally applied in a controlled fashion. Overall, when RNA‐based signaling is interfaced with living cells and organisms, this will enable mimicry of natural signaling pathways with transient genetic feedback loops, allowing precise control over expression (or knockdown) of proteins.

In this article, we developed photoactivatable synthetic exosomes able to encapsulate RNA and allow paracrine signaling between artificial and living cells. Our synthetic exosome was successfully integrated into complex coacervate cell mimics. Photocleavable nitrobenzyl groups were used to cage primary amines at the surface of poly(ethylene glycol)‐block‐poly(caprolactone‐co‐trimethylene carbonate), PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMC) polymer vesicles, allowing charge switching behavior and expulsion from the overall positively charged coacervate lumen. Furthermore, its ability to transmit RNA signals to natural cells was demonstrated by the uptake of fluorescently labeled RNA into cells, and the functionality of the RNA was confirmed by knockdown assays. Due to the RNA compartmentalization inside artificial cells and the stimuli‐responsive nitrobenzyl cleavage, spatiotemporal control over synthetic exosome activation was also proven by selective irradiation of artificial cell‐natural cell cocultures. As extracellular vesicles are essential in genetic information transfer and signaling, this artificial exosome design is an important tool, enabling artificial cell platforms with improved life‐like signaling behavior.

Results and Discussion

Design of Artificial Exosome

To create an artificial cell platform with the ability to perform RNA‐based exosome signaling, we decided to build upon our recently developed amylose‐based coacervate platform.[ 11 , 43 ] Complex coacervate formation is commenced by the mixing and subsequent phase separation of an excess of positively charged quaternized amylose (Q‐Am) with negatively charged carboxymethylated amylose (CM‐Am), which is then stabilized by the addition of a terpolymer that forms a semipermeable membrane around the droplets (Figures S1–S3). We have employed this platform to mimic the crowdedness of the cytoplasm,[ 44 ] with additional compartmentalization,[ 45 , 46 ] and the barrier function of the cell membrane.[ 47 ] To introduce vesicles, we opted to include PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMC) polymersomes, utilizing caged primary amines and nonresponsive methoxy end groups. The block copolymer endgroups were designed to allow light‐responsive exposure of primary amines on the polymersome surface, thereby increasing the surface charge, leading to electrostatically driven diffusion out of the coacervate. Our specific block copolymers and the overall artificial cell strategy are shown in Figure 1.

Conceptualization of photoresponsive polymersomes for exosome‐inspired communication in artificial cell systems. Nucleic acid‐containing polymersomes with UV‐cleavable protected amine surface functionality allow charge‐switching behavior (i). The polymersomes are assembled inside amylose‐based complex coacervates via electrostatic attractive interactions, (ii) and (iii), and a semipermeable terpolymer membrane is assembled around the droplets to form artificial cell mimics (iv). Light irradiation is used to trigger exosome expulsion from the artificial cells and guide the RNA‐based paracrine signaling function of artificial cells to natural cells.

Earlier work showed that similar polymers with imidazole or poly(ethylene imine) functionality can self‐assemble into polymersomes that encapsulate RNA better than standard TMC‐containing block copolymers and disassemble at low pH.[ 48 , 49 ] We therefore opted to use the same block copolymer for our exosomes. First, cationic ring‐opening copolymerization was performed of the monomers trimethylene carbonate pentafluorophenyl ester (TMCpfp) and ɛ‐caprolactone (ɛCL) using either a monomethyl PEG or hydrochloride salt amine PEG macroinitiator (Figures 2a and S4–S12). The imidazole functionality was introduced by reacting the primary amine of 1‐(3‐aminopropyl)imidazole with the pentafluorophenyl ester incorporated in the backbone of the polymer, which was confirmed with 19F NMR spectroscopy (Figures S13 and S14). Finally, the amine end group was modified with the nitrobenzyl photocleavable functionality in a two‐step process (Figure 2b). Confirmation of the imidazole functional polymer structures was provided with NMR spectroscopy (Figures 2c and S8–S12) and SEC (Figures S15–S22). Successful cleavage of the nitrobenzyl‐capped amine end group, after 10 min of UV irradiation, was determined with 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S23 and S24).

Synthesis of photoresponsive polymersomes and their charge‐switching behavior. a) Synthetic schemes for the ring‐opening polymerization of CL and TMCpfp, from PEG macroinitiators and subsequent imidazole functionalization. b) Photocleavable nitrobenzyl functionalization of polymer amine end groups. c) 1H NMR characterization of polymer nBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) and intermediate products. d) Cryo‐TEM image of polymersome vesicles assembled from nBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid), (50:50 ratio with methoxy end group polymer) containing siRNA. e) Zetapotential and f) intensity distribution size from DLS of nBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) polymersomes containing siRNA (50:50 ratio with methoxy end group polymer) before and after 365 nm UV irradiation. g) Zetapotential of nBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) polymersomes containing siRNA before and after 365 nm UV irradiation (with different ratios with methoxy end group polymer).

The formation of functional exosome mimics was achieved by encapsulating siRNA inside PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) polymersomes via the direct hydration and re‐assembly method, where PBS was added quickly to a solution of block copolymer in low molecular weight PEG to form polymersomes. The particles were disassembled at pH 6.0, followed by the addition of Cy5‐siRNA and subsequently, reassembly was induced by adjusting the pH to 7.4. Excess RNA was removed by spin filtration and washing, after which an encapsulation efficiency (E.E.) of 34% (±5.7) was achieved (Figure S25 and Table S1). These functional nanostructures were determined to be approximately 150 nm in diameter, using cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryoTEM) (Figure 2d). Charge switching behavior of polymersomes composed of 50% nitrobenzylamine and 50% methoxy end‐functionalized block copolymers was assessed with zeta potential measurements (Figure 2e) and dynamic light scattering (DLS), which showed no change in polymersome size (∼100–130 nm, PDI ∼ 0.24) (Figure 2f). Different molar ratios of the methoxy and nitrobenzyl functional polymers were assembled to confirm the most optimal ratio for highest charge switching difference while maintaining uniform size characteristics (Figure 2g). With low amounts of nitrobenzyl polymer incorporation, charge switching was insufficient, while at high ratios particles were unstable after cleavage, therefore 50:50 was chosen as the best option, which also maintained good polymersome dispersion (Figure S26).

To confirm enhanced RNA encapsulation inside polymersomes with the imidazole functionality, PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMC) and nitrobenzylamine‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMC) without imidazole units were also synthesized (Figures S4–S6, S16, S17, S22 and S23) and tested for RNA encapsulation and light‐responsive charge switching. In general, these polymersomes behaved similarly with regard to their charge‐switching behavior dependent on molar ratio of nitrobenzyl amine end‐functional polymer incorporation (Figures S27–S29), but RNA encapsulation efficiencies of around 5% were significantly lower (Table S1).

Assembly of Synthetic Exosomes in Artificial Cells and Release

Next, the formation of subcompartmentalized exosomes containing artificial cells was performed using the established negatively charged polymersomes (containing RNA), which were sequestered by the coacervates during amylose mixing, followed by addition of membranizing terpolymer once the freely coalescing coacervate microdroplets were cell‐sized (approximately 25 µm). Confocal microscopy images showed that the majority of polymersomes were evenly distributed throughout the coacervate by the even fluorescent signal (150 nm polymersomes are beyond the diffraction limit of laser scanning confocal microscopes), with a minor amount of punctuated structures, indicating a small degree of vesicle clustering (Figure 3a). We quantitatively determined the sequestration efficiency (S.E.%) of polymersomes into coacervates using absorbance spectroscopy. Polymersomes were taken up into the coacervates with an S.E. of around 20% (cy5‐siRNA polymersomes in coacervates, S.E. = 23.1% ± 3.5%, and LAMP1‐siRNA polymersomes in coacervates, S.E. = 18.3% ± 2.3%). Previous research has shown that charged 100 nm liposomes can evenly distribute in aqueous two‐phase systems (ATPSs),[ 50 ] while within complex coacervates, they can form transient clusters of vesicles.[ 50 , 51 , 52 ] This vesicle dispersion behavior has been observed in complex coacervates with different compositions, such as DEAE‐dextran/polyacrylic acid (PAA), poly(diallyldimethylammonium chloride) (PDDA)/adenosine 5′‐triphosphate (ATP), PDDA/PAA, and poly(ethylene imine) (PEI)/PAA. Overall, there are a multitude of molecular interactions in coacervate droplets, and the electrostatic interaction strength is a determining factor for different dispersion states of recruited vesicles.[ 50 ] Our nBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) polymersomes (50:50, mPEG:nBenz) have a reasonably low negative charge (∼−10 mV) and low electrostatic interaction strength due to the charge stemming solely from the electronegativity of oxygens in the polymersome PEG chains, which helps explain their overall favorable internal coacervate dispersion. The dispersion appears to be a mixture of mostly evenly dispersed vesicles with some aggregates.

Assembly of synthetic exosomes in artificial cells and release. a) Confocal microscopy image of nBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) polymersomes (50:50, mPEG:nBenz), containing Cy5‐siRNA, assembled inside complex coacervate artificial cells. b) Schematic diagram showing strategy for UV (365 nm) triggered polymersome expulsion from artificial cells. c) Quantification of fluorescent signal decrease over time following 10 min UV irradiation of polymersome‐loaded coacervates, including naked siRNA‐loaded coacervate control to evidence minimal photobleaching, and nonresponsive polymersome control. d) DLS‐derived count rate quantification of supernatant‐located nanoparticles released from coacervates after 10 min UV irradiation. e) Confocal images showing UV‐triggered polymersome release over time (white outlines to show remaining coacervate locations). Scale bars, 25 µm.

To assess the ability of our artificial cell system to initiate RNA‐based signaling, NBenz‐PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid) polymersomes (50:50, mPEG:nBenz), containing Cy5‐siRNA, assembled inside complex coacervate artificial cells were irradiated with 365 nm light for 10 min and followed with confocal microscopy (Figure 3b,c,e). A decrease in Cy5 fluorescence could be observed over time due to electrostatically driven displacement of the polymersomes from the coacervate lumen. The fluorescent signal reached below 30% after 3 h for the charge‐switching polymersome sample, whereas the naked siRNA‐loaded coacervate control showed minimal photobleaching, and nonresponsive polymersome control still retained approximately 80% Cy5 signal after 3 h, indicative of low polymersome leakage. Additionally, the supernatant of the exosome‐containing coacervate samples was analyzed with DLS‐derived count rate, which increased over time due to increased scattering from released polymersomes in the solution (Figure 3d). Released polymersome Z‐average sizes were slightly larger than before encapsulation, but DLS measurements indicate intact vesicles were released (Figure S30). Importantly, the coacervate artificial cells remained intact after both polymersome loading and charge‐switching release, imitating a natural extracellular vesicle release pathway, as the electrostatic interactions allowed the liquid‐like coacervate to exist in its thermodynamically favorable state. Stable coacervates were even observed days after polymersome release, due to stabilizing features of the semipermeable terpolymer membrane. Our system therefore mimics natural extracellular vesicle release via multivesicular bodies (containing exosomes) by expulsion of small vesicles through the cell membrane, rather than the alternative natural pathway of cell membrane budding.

Vesicle‐Mediated Transfer of RNA from Artificial Cells to Natural Cells

Next, we explored the functionality of our photo‐activated artificial exosomes in engaging with HeLa cells (Figure 4a–c). Using CLSM, we observed that after UV irradiation and an additional 1 or 2 h incubation in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C, the Cy5 signal was enhanced inside the cells and reduced inside the artificial cells (additional images, Figure S31). We attribute this to the endocytosis of the positively charged polymersomes, as when we used nonresponsive methoxy end group polymersomes, a very low internal Cy5 signal was observed (Figure 4c). We did not observe uptake of coacervates or any artificial cells establishing direct contact with live cells, likely due to the hydrophilic PEG‐stabilizing block of the terpolymer membrane. Quantitative analysis indicated that polymersome uptake after 1 h was 2.1‐fold higher for photoresponsive polymersomes and 2.3‐fold higher after 2 h (Figure 4b). This positive first indication of RNA uptake via this artificial exosome pathway motivated us to continue to demonstrate biological functionality.

Biocompatibility is a critical design feature for complex coacervates for in vitro and in vivo use and is closely related to the choice of polymer materials making up the overall system. A previous study from our group showed that uncomplexed polyelectrolytes in the aqueous phase can cause toxicity, while the coacervate phase is typically much less toxic due to neutralized charges.[ 53 ] During loading of polymersomes into coacervates the purification step to remove free polymersomes also removed any free cationic polymer or terpolymer. Accordingly, we observed high cell viability when incubating artificial cells with HeLa cells and also when incubating the responsive and nonresponsive polymersomes (Figures 4d,e and S32).

RNA‐Based Signal Transduction in Natural Cells

In biology, light‐responsive cells are able to detect light and initiate cell–cell signaling pathways, which is important for processes such as phototropism.[ 54 ] After having demonstrated the use of light to trigger exosome release and uptake into cells, artificial cells were constructed to send RNA signals with a biofunctional output. We integrated lysosomal membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) siRNA into the vesicle assembly, which provides a functional output when the complementary anti‐LAMP1 antibody is added to quantify cell protein expression (Figure 5). Artificial cells were co‐cultured with HeLa cells in microscopy slides, and after irradiation of the co‐cultures for 1, 5, or 10 min, they were incubated for an additional 16 h. After artificial cell coculture, HeLa cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with anti‐LAMP1‐647 (red), WGA‐448 for the cell membrane (green), and Hoechst for cell nucleus visualization (cyan). Subsequent quantification of protein expression levels in the various groups (Figure 5a,b) showed successful knockdown of LAMP1 by our synthetic exosomes in a UV irradiation time‐dependent manner. Treatment with polymersomes containing LAMP1‐siRNA alone induced a mean 65% reduction in LAMP1 expression, while artificial cells containing polymersomes with no UV irradiation showed no significant reduction in protein expression. With increasing UV light exposure, the artificial cell signaling system reached 19%, 50%, and 56% LAMP1 knockdown for 1, 5, and 10 min of exposure, respectively. To our knowledge, this is the first example of RNA‐based artificial cell communication with natural cells. The use of such artificial exosomes would be amenable to different forms of RNA, including mRNA or CRISPR‐Cas9 systems, opening routes to inducing protein expression systems able to enact further downstream signaling. When carefully engineered, such artificial cells would also be able to mediate communication between cell populations that do not naturally communicate with each other.

The use of a facile light trigger allowed us to localize artificial cell‐natural cell signaling to specific regions. To demonstrate this, we co‐cultured artificial cells and HeLa cells in an imaging microwell slide and used a photomask to irradiate half of the culture (Figure 5c) to mimic tissue‐specific paracrine signaling pathways able to be achieved with exosomes in living systems. After overnight incubation and cell staining, we imaged the cells with CLSM to determine relative protein expression levels. Using anti‐LAMP1‐647, we visualized the distribution of LAMP1 expression in the cell culture (Figure 5d,e). Without UV irradiation, there was an even distribution of LAMP1 expression across the 3000 µm observation window, while in the case of the selectively irradiated sample, there was an observable decrease in protein expression in only the irradiated region of the cell culture. The boundary between UV‐exposed and masked regions has a small gradient zone due to diffusion and variable uptake of polymersomes. It has been reported that UV light causes no change in LAMP1 expression,[ 55 ] while in other cases, it has been reported that it can be upregulated with UV exposure,[ 56 ] which could cause an underestimate of the knockdown based on the polymersome transfection. Collectively, these results illustrate the effective development of an artificial cell system with light‐responsive synthetic exosomes that can facilitate RNA‐based communication and inhibit the expression of proteins in a spatially precise manner. This is particularly exciting for future applications within a complex multicellular context containing serum and other RNA‐degrading species.

Conclusions

Biological systems employ extracellular vesicle‐based RNA communication channels to collectively adapt to their environment, send signals to distant tissues, and genetically evolve. Here, we provide a conceptual framework for the construction of synthetic exosomes containing artificial cells, designed for the precise modulation of mammalian cell behaviors. Photoactivatable synthetic exosomes able to encapsulate RNA were designed with nitrobenzyl caged amine moieties at the surface of polymer vesicles, allowing charge switching behavior and transfer to natural cells. Encapsulation of the vesicles inside complex coacervate artificial cells, guided by electrostatic interactions, permitted nanometer‐scale compartmentalization within the droplets and enabled the nucleic acid signals to be expelled with a 365 nm UV light trigger. The vesicles’ ability to transmit RNA signals to natural cells was demonstrated by the uptake of fluorescently labeled RNA into cells. Demonstration of biological functionality was proven via siRNA‐induced LAMP1 expression inhibition in HeLa cells. Spatiotemporal activation of synthetic exosomes in artificial cells was also proven by selective irradiation of artificial cell‐natural cell cocultures.

This artificial cell platform with modular vesicle‐RNA compartmentalization allows the plug and play of alternative or more complex RNA designs to yield emerging functional communication pathways between artificial cells and natural cells. Living cells utilize a high level of temporal control and a wide range of nonlinear regulatory strategies, such as activation and inactivation of soluble factors, to tune intercellular communication dynamics. The integration of this system in an autonomous feedback‐dependent manner is a future research goal. The pH switch induced RNA encapsulation in PEG‐b‐P(CL‐co‐TMCimid.) polymersomes should allow use of various nucleic acid cargoes such as mRNA and CRISPR‐Cas9 gene editing systems. Compared to other communication pathways investigated by the synthetic cell field, including DNA circuits,[ 30 ] or synthetic biology approaches such as synNotch,[ 57 ] our strategy allows integration of typically unstable RNA signals, permitting integration into complex natural cell systems. We envision the ability to further fine‐tune artificial cell signaling pathways by varying the RNA cargo and by localizing protein inhibition/expression either in the extracellular space or to the membrane. The streamlined and broadly applicable design of our synthetic exosome system could yield simple multicellular model systems with the potential to decipher generalizable concepts in cell signaling.[ 58 ] From a technological point of view and beyond artificial cells, the ability to control vesicle‐based signals from RNA may lead to new exciting biophysical tools to manipulate cells and therapeutic tools to address disease.
