Enhanced Thompson sampling by roulette wheel selection for screening ultralarge combinatorial libraries
Authors: Hongtao Zhao, Eva Nittinger, Melissa A Yu, Symon Gathiaka, W Patrick Walters, Christian Tyrchan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical space exploration has gained significant interest with the increasing availability of building blocks, enabling the creation of ultralarge virtual libraries containing billions or trillions of compounds. However, challenges remain in selecting the most suitable compounds for synthesis, especially in hit expansion. Thompson sampling, a probabilistic search method, has recently been proposed to improve efficiency by operating in reagent space rather than product space. Here, we address some of its limitations by introducing a roulette wheel selection method combined with a thermal cycling approach to balance greedy search and diversity-driven exploration. The effectiveness of this method is demonstrated through 109 queries against twenty distinct 1-million-compound libraries using ROCS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13321-025-01105-1.Keywords: Thompson sampling, Combinatorial libraries, Virtual screening, Chemical space, Ultralarge libraries

Introduction

Over the past decade, synthesis-on-demand libraries have become a crucial tool for identifying hits in drug discovery [1, 2]. These libraries, typically based on readily available reagents and well-established chemical reactions, can provide access to billions or even trillions of compounds. They are usually accessed through databases of enumerated reaction products offered by chemical vendors. Drug discovery teams employ various virtual screening methods, including substructure and similarity searches, protein–ligand docking, and 3D similarity searches, to select compounds for synthesis and purchase [3]. Vendors generally deliver at least 80% of the requested compounds within four to eight weeks, at a cost of $100 to $300 per compound.

While synthesis-on-demand methods have been impactful, the enormous size of virtual libraries poses a challenge to traditional virtual screening workflows [4]. For instance, imagine docking 1 billion small molecules into a protein binding site. If docking a single molecule on one CPU takes 10 s, then docking 1 billion molecules in a day would require 116,000 CPUs. The total cloud cost for this process is approximately $10,000. Although this might be manageable within a typical pharmaceutical R&D budget, screening 10 billion or even 50 billion molecules would become extremely costly.

One of the simplest methods is to cluster or divide an ultralarge library into groups, sample molecules from each group, and then focus on sampling more from the most promising groups [5, 6]. In a related approach called active learning [7], computationally intensive tasks, such as docking, are performed on a small subset (usually 1–2%) of a large dataset [8, 9]. A machine learning model is then trained to predict docking scores using descriptors derived from the relevant chemical structures. These predicted scores help select the next set of molecules for docking, and this process can be repeated multiple times. Ultimately, molecules with the best docking scores are selected for synthesis or purchase. Although this approach is more efficient than a full exhaustive search, it still has its limitations. To make predictions, the entire library must be enumerated and stored on disk, which can require a large amount of space for the structures and descriptors of billions of molecules. Additionally, the time needed for a machine learning model to perform inference on such a large dataset is significant. For example, if a typical model processes 10,000 molecules per second, inference on a billion records would take over a day. While parallel processing can speed up this process, it also requires considerable infrastructure investment.

A second approach uses a “greedy” strategy during the search process, assessing libraries from a building block or “synthon” perspective [10–12]. In this method, the chemical building blocks that make up the library are individually processed and converted into synthons, which are then evaluated separately. For example, consider a two-component coupling reaction involving a carboxylic acid and an amine to produce an amide. If we have 1000 carboxylic acids and 1000 amines, we can create a library of 1 million amides. By evaluating the library at the building block level, we only need to perform 2000 evaluations instead of 1 million at the product level. At the beginning of this process, the reactive part of each chemical building block is removed to generate a synthon. In the case of the amide reaction, the carboxylic acid could be transformed into a synthon by converting it into a methyl ketone. Similarly, the primary amine could be changed into a secondary amine synthon by replacing one of the hydrogens attached to nitrogen with a methyl group. These synthons can then be docked and scored. The synthons with the highest scores can be further elaborated by performing a virtual reaction and attaching the carboxylic acid synthon to produce the fully formed amide. These product molecules are then scored and prioritized for synthesis. A similar strategy can be applied to other virtual screening methods such as 3D similarity searches [13]. One potential downside of this approach is the assumption that evaluating synthons in isolation will accurately reflect their performance within the final product. This is not always true. If a ligand from a protein–ligand crystal structure is divided into pieces and those pieces are docked, they rarely dock in the same orientation as in the full molecule crystal structure. The context of the full molecule is often necessary to reproduce the observed intermolecular interactions.

Thompson Sampling (TS), introduced in 2024 by Klarich [14, 15], aims to balance between synthon-based methods and full-molecule evaluations. TS uses a probabilistic approach inspired by the gambling “one-armed bandit” problem, where the goal is to find the best sequence to play multiple slot machines to maximize wins. In TS, each chemical building block is associated with a probability distribution. These distributions help sample promising building blocks more often and guide the search toward productive areas of chemical space. For example, if we have 100 of each of three building blocks A, B, and C, they can be combined to generate a library of 1,000,000 products (100 × 100 × 100). The process begins by sampling a random score from the probability distributions of each building block. The building blocks with the highest sampled scores are selected and combined to produce a virtual reaction product, which is then evaluated. For a 3D similarity search, this evaluation involves generating multiple conformers for the reaction product and comparing their shape and pharmacophoric features with a query molecule. These conformers are superimposed onto the 3D structure of the query molecule, and the highest overlap score is used to adjust the probability distributions of the related building blocks. This adjustment increases or decreases the likelihood of selecting a building block in future rounds. This sampling and evaluation process repeats thousands of times to prioritize and select a final set of molecules for experimental testing. In their 2024 paper, Klarich and colleagues demonstrated that TS, by evaluating only between 0.1 and 1% of the molecules in a library, could typically identify at least 90 of the top 100 molecules in libraries containing over 100 million compounds. It is important to note that, although probabilistic selections in TS are made at the building block level, all evaluations are performed on fully elaborated reaction products.

In this paper, we introduce roulette wheel selection (RWS), an alternative sampling method that employs a different approach to update the probability distributions associated with chemical building blocks [16]. Through extensive benchmarking involving 2.18 billion evaluations, we compare the performance of TS and RWS across 20 reactions applied to 109 shape-based virtual screens. We find that TS and RWS perform equally on two-component libraries, but RWS performs significantly better with three-component libraries. Additionally, we show that simple parallelization of RWS can yield performance that scales linearly with the number of CPUs.

Continue the search cycle until sufficient new molecules are collected to optimize parallelization efficiency, with a default target of 50 molecules per CPU core. Make and score the new molecules, then update the posterior distributions of their reagents. The search is aborted if one of two stop criteria is met: (1) reaching the target library percentage or (2) failing to sample a new molecule for a given number of consecutive searches. We introduce a thermal cycling approach to balance greedy search with diversity-driven exploration (Fig. 1).

The concept behind thermal cycling adaptation involves two main steps. Initially, sampling begins with a greedy-like selection process that prioritizes reagents with the highest scores, increasing the likelihood of identifying optimal candidates. When this approach stops yielding new molecules, the system adjusts the temperature to encourage a more diverse selection of reagents, particularly in one reaction component at the higher temperature, α. This treatment facilitates quick identification of intrinsically high-scoring reagents, which might be underscored during the warmup phase, by pairing them with high-scoring reagents from the other components at the lower temperature, β. Such diverse selection necessitates an update to the posterior mean using a Boltzmann-weighted average, rather than an arithmetic mean, to prevent the rapid decay of the posterior mean for high-scoring reagents due to their frequent pairing with low-scoring reagents.

To determine the initial temperature γ, we prioritize the reagent with the higher score when comparing two reagents whose sampled scores differ by σ. Here, σ serves as a threshold for defining a meaningful difference in the scores. By setting γ = 0.1, the probability of selecting the reagent with the higher score is about 22,000 times greater than that of selecting the lower-scoring one, as calculated by exp(∆y/σ/γ). This represents a strongly greedy-like selection mechanism. In contrast, if the two best reagents have sampled scores differing by less than 0.01 × σ, their selection probabilities become nearly identical—unlike the classic greedy selection scheme, which always selects the higher-scoring candidate. This approach to initializing γ ensures diversity among top-scoring reagents. Although increasing the temperature very slowly would allow for a smoother transition from greedy to diverse selection, it is not computationally efficient. Therefore, the rates at which temperature is adjusted were empirically optimized to balance performance and speed.

In the following section, we will compare the roulette wheel selection approach, termed RWS, with the greedy selection method, referred to as TS, which has been updated to randomize the order of reaction components in each search cycle since its initial publication.

Experimental section

To compare the performance of TS and RWS, we conducted 2180 separate shape-based virtual screens using ROCS from OpenEye Scientific Software. ROCS provides a quick method for superimposing molecules and assessing their shape and pharmacophoric feature overlap [18]. The ROCS overlap score, known as the TanimotoCombo, is composed of two parts: a shape score, which measures the volume overlap, and a color score, which evaluates the pharmacophoric feature overlap. Each score ranges from 0.0 to 1.0, with a maximum total score of 2.0 indicating perfect superposition.

To create a ground-truth reference dataset, we performed 109 exhaustive ROCS searches on each of 20 combinatorial libraries, each containing 1 million molecules. These libraries were derived from 10 common two-component and 10 three-component chemistries. The two-component reactions were based on reaction definitions from the combinatorial library “cookbook” created by eMolecules [19]. The three-component chemistries were manually encoded as Reaction SMARTS, based on the review by Graziano [20]. Since we aimed to find lead-like molecules, we chose not to include four-component chemistries such as the Ugi reaction, which tend to produce higher molecular weight reaction products. Table S1 provides details on the specific reactions. For the 2-component libraries, we used 1000 of each of two building blocks; for the 3-component libraries, we used 100 of each of three building blocks. The SMILES for the relevant reagents were selected from the ZINC database [21], and reaction products were generated using version 2024.09.6 of the RDKit Python toolkit for cheminformatics. These exhaustive searches involved 109 queries across 20 libraries, each containing 1,000,000 molecules, totaling 2.18 billion comparisons and requiring over 30,000 CPU hours. All reactions, reagents, products, and the results of the exhaustive searches are available in the GitHub repository associated with this paper.

A set of 109 lead molecules was selected from the supplementary material related to the publication titled “Fragment-to-Lead Medicinal Chemistry Publications in 2022” by A.J. Woodhead et al. [22]. The structures of these lead molecules were obtained from the supporting spreadsheet as SMILES strings and used as input for conformer generation with Omega version 5.0.1.3 [23]. The default ROCS conformer generation settings produced up to 50 conformers, with the lowest energy conformer used as the query molecule for ROCS searches. Conformers for the libraries were generated using the same Omega settings, creating up to 50 conformers per library molecule. Searches were performed with ROCS version 3.6.2.3 using default settings. The ROCS search results were saved as Apache Parquet [24] files and later used for TS and RWS evaluations. The RWS parameters were selected based on prior empirical optimization during project applications at AstraZeneca. The parameter settings were adjusted to ensure that TS and RWS provide comparable evaluations (Table 1).

Results and discussion

Exhaustive searches

To simulate a typical virtual screening campaign aimed at finding analogs of lead molecules, we selected 109 lead compounds from Woodhead's 2022 Fragment-to-Lead review [22]. This review is part of an annual series that compiles success stories from fragment-to-lead efforts published in the medicinal chemistry literature. We believe these lead compounds reflect common queries used in virtual screening. Their molecular weights range from 190 to 450, with an average of 358 g/mol.

To establish a baseline, we first performed exhaustive ROCS searches for each of the 109 query molecules. In each case, we used the lowest-energy conformer of each query to run a ROCS search across 20 libraries for molecules with similar shapes and pharmacophoric features. This yielded a total of 2,180 distinct sets of search results. For each search, we recorded the top 100 molecules with the highest-scoring TanimotoCombo and evaluated how effectively TS and RWS could recover the same top 100 molecules.

ROCS search performance of TS and RWS

The ground-truth data obtained from exhaustive searches were used to evaluate the abilities of TS and RWS in identifying the top 100 molecules from each of the 20 libraries. Because both TS and RWS have a stochastic component, 10 replicate searches were performed using each method for every query molecule. For each library, the results were compared to the top 100 compounds found via exhaustive search, and the average over 10 replicates was recorded for each query with both TS and RWS. All of the top 100 recoveries reported below are the mean values over 10 replicates.

Figure 2 shows the number of top 100 compounds recovered for the same datasets using TS and RWS. We observe a strong correlation between the methods, with an R2 of 0.61 for two-component reactions and 0.62 for three-component reactions. Importantly, most of the outliers in the plot are above the diagonal, indicating cases where RWS outperformed TS.

Finally, we aimed to examine how diversity among the top 100 molecules influences the performance of TS and RWS. In this analysis, we measured diversity by counting the total number of chemical building blocks in the top 100 product molecules. For example, if the top 100 from a 3-component reaction has 22 R1, 9 R2, and 15 R3, the total number of building blocks would be 22 + 9 + 15 = 46. We would consider this top 100 to be less diverse than a set with 27 + 13 + 12 = 70 building blocks. Since fewer building blocks need to be selected and evaluated, it should be easier for TS and RWS to identify the top 100 within a less diverse library. The influence of library diversity is shown in Fig. 5. In each plot, the x-axis represents the total number of building blocks in the top 100 compounds, with diversity increasing from left to right. The y-axis in each plot shows the average top 100 recovery over 10 replicates. Each library is color-coded differently in the plots. We observe that performance in selecting the top 100 decreases as the diversity of the top hits increases. However, the decline is more consistent and steady with RWS, whereas the performance of TS is much more scattered. The TS plots on the left have many more points below the diagonal than the RWS plots on the right, indicating less consistent performance.

Parallelization efficiency of RWS

While Thompson sampling provides high search efficiency, parallelization remains essential for screening extremely large combinatorial libraries. Although our benchmarking focused on the top 100 compounds, in real-world applications it is important to examine a broader selection beyond just the highest-ranked hits, since top-scoring compounds can sometimes be artifacts [25]. Therefore, virtual screening often relies on diversity-based compound selection to reduce this risk. To evaluate multiprocessing performance, we created an additional 100-million compound library from the 3-component Niementowski quinazoline synthesis and sampled 0.2% using different numbers of CPU cores. For ROCS screening, the time decreased from 63.4 h with a single core to 1.0 h with 64 cores, reaching nearly 100% parallelization efficiency. For FRED docking, the time reduced from 84.4 h with one core to 1.8 h with 64 cores, achieving 73% parallelization efficiency (Fig. 6).

Conclusions

In this work, we showed that the recently published Thompson sampling method can be improved with roulette wheel selection and thermal cycling. Encouragingly, our results show that both methods performed well, achieving a mean recovery rate greater than 80% after screening only 2% of the libraries. While RWS and TS perform equally in 2-component reactions, RWS offers a clear advantage with 3-component reactions. RWS enhances recovery by balancing greedy search and diversity-driven exploration. This balance is crucial for navigating the large chemical space of ultralarge combinatorial libraries, enabling more effective identification of viable compounds without losing diversity—a common challenge in greedy approaches. Additionally, the parallelization capability provided by roulette wheel selection increases sampling efficiency and allows for the simultaneous exploration of multiple molecules in each search cycle. Various scoring components, from 1 to 3D descriptors, can be integrated into Thompson sampling. Therefore, it acts as a versatile tool for examining ultralarge combinatorial libraries at the reagent level, potentially speeding up drug discovery by quickly finding hits and broadening chemical space exploration.
