Exploring trends of cost-effectiveness thresholds in South Korea: systematic review of pharmacoeconomic evaluations since introduction of the positive list system
Authors: Hyun-ji Kim, Kyung-ae Nam, Suk-Chan Jang, Kyung Ah Ban, Eui-Kyung Lee, Sun-Kyeong Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPharmacoeconomic evaluations are critical for healthcare decision-making, especially as South Korea has emphasized cost-effectiveness since the 2006 positive list system. However, no official cost-effectiveness threshold (CET) has been established. This study aimed to systematically review cost-utility analyses (CUAs) conducted in South Korea since 2007 and to describe how CET values have been reported and applied in published pharmacoeconomic studies.MethodsWe conducted this systematic review following the PRISMA guidelines. We searched five databases, including PubMed and KoreaMed, for CUAs published after 2007. We included CUAs of medications conducted in South Korea that reported CET. Data on baseline year, outcome measures, CET values, drug type (anticancer drugs, vaccines, and other treatments), and funding sources were extracted. We assessed the association between CETs and categorical variables using the chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.ResultsA total of 8,510 studies were initially identified, of which 53 met the inclusion criteria after screening. Median CETs showed an increasing trend over time. GDP per capita–based references were more frequently used than WTP-based approaches (60.4% vs. 37.7%). Anticancer drugs consistently demonstrated higher CET values than other treatment categories, and drug type was significantly associated with CET levels (p < 0.05). In contrast, other study characteristics, including funding source, were not significantly associated in categorical analyses.ConclusionThis study provides an overview of CET trends in South Korea, highlighting differences by drug type. As the number of high-cost drugs and healthcare demands increase, CETs are expected to play a key role in balancing financial sustainability and patient access. These findings offer insights into how CET values have been applied in the literature and may inform ongoing discussions on the use of CETs in health economic evaluations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00787-0.

Background

Globally rising healthcare costs, along with the emergence of innovative and expensive medications, have increased the need for economic analyses in coverage decision-making [1]. Various health technology assessment (HTA) agencies, including the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the UK and the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) in South Korea, recommend the use of quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) as a key outcome measure in economic evaluations [2, 3]. The incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR), which expresses the additional cost per QALY gained, is a widely used metric to evaluate the value of new healthcare interventions. To determine whether an intervention is cost-effective, ICURs are compared against a cost-effectiveness threshold (CET), making the definition and application of CET critical aspects of healthcare decision-making [4, 5].

There are no globally accepted CETs, and countries adopt varying approaches based on their economic conditions and healthcare priorities [5]. The UK explicitly defines a CET, with the NICE typically using a range of £20,000–£30,000 per QALY to guide reimbursement decisions [2, 6]. Australia does not have an explicit CET, but an implicit benchmark of around AUD 50,000 per QALY is commonly assumed in practice [7, 8]. In Canada, while no official national CET exists, a commonly cited range is CAD 20,000–50,000 per QALY, with flexibility for severe or life-threatening conditions [9, 10]. Japan has recently integrated economic evaluations into its HTA processes. Although a single uniform CET has not been formally established, the system applies reference values for price adjustment, typically centered around 5 million JPY per QALY, with higher ranges (e.g., 7.5–10 million JPY per QALY) considered depending on disease severity [11, 12]. Similarly, China does not have a formal definition for CET; however, empirical studies have suggested a range of approximately 1.5–2 times the gross domestic product (GDP) per capita based on willingness-to-pay (WTP) estimates and the value of statistical life approach [13, 14].

Since the introduction of the Positive List System in 2006, pharmacoeconomic evaluations have steadily increased in South Korea. In contrast, an explicit CET has not been established, and research on the selection and application of CETs remains limited. The appropriate selection of CETs in each study is of critical importance, as it directly influences the interpretation of ICURs, and consequently, healthcare decision-making. In addition, there is growing policy interest in refining CET frameworks in South Korea. Therefore, further studies are needed to inform the appropriate use of CETs in healthcare decision-making [15]. At the national level, research initiatives commissioned by governmental agencies, including HIRA, are currently underway to explore more appropriate approaches for threshold setting, including the incorporation of socioeconomic factors and disease severity considerations [16]. However, these efforts are still in progress, and no formal CET has been established to date. Therefore, a systematic review of published pharmacoeconomic evaluations may provide insights into how CET values have been selected and reported in pharmacoeconomic studies conducted in South Korea. In this context, we review the CET values reported by domestic pharmacoeconomic studies, offering insights into patterns in CET reporting and associated study characteristics, thereby providing useful context for interpreting CET values and informing future discussions.

Methods

Search strategy

We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and searched for published pharmacoeconomic evaluations conducted in South Korea. We focused our search on articles published after December 2006, when the positive list system was introduced in South Korea. We searched PubMed/Medline, the Cochrane Library, and Embase, as well as the Korean databases RISS and KoreaMed. For the Korean databases, search terms were also applied in Korean to ensure the inclusion of relevant domestic publications. The search strategy was designed to identify pharmacoeconomic evaluations conducted in South Korea. Accordingly, a combination of keywords such as “cost-effectiveness,” “cost-utility,” “cost-benefit,” “economic evaluation,” and “pharmacoeconomic” was used in conjunction with “Korea” or “South Korea.” The detailed search procedures are described in Supplementary Material Table 1.

Selection process

We included studies with a baseline year after 2007, when the positive list system was implemented. Eligible studies were economic evaluations of pharmaceuticals conducted in South Korea that reported a CET and evaluated outcomes based on QALYs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) not an economic evaluation (e.g., clinical trials, budget impact analyses, cost-benefit analyses, and cost minimization analyses); (2) not related to pharmaceuticals (e.g., studies focused on surgery, screening, or other non-pharmaceutical interventions); (3) not conducted in South Korea or not focusing on the South Korean healthcare context; and (4) abstracts, conference articles, reviews, letters, editorials, erratum, or interviews. The selection process began with screening of titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review of the remaining studies.

Data extraction

First, we extracted the following information from each included study: (1) baseline year; (2) outcome measures (e.g., QALY); (3) threshold values applied to the economic outcome; (4) drug type (e.g., anticancer agents, vaccines, and other treatments); (5) funding source (e.g., profit, non-profit, not funded, and not reported); and (6) CET reference type (e.g., WTP, GDP per capita, and others). For methodological consistency, studies reporting cost per QALY outcomes were classified as CUAs, and the corresponding ratios were classified as ICUR, even when the original articles referred to them as cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) or incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). Medications were classified as anticancer drugs only if they were indicated for the treatment of cancer. Drugs used for cancer-related complications, but not classified as anticancer agents, were included in the “other treatments” group. When extracting the baseline year, the value was determined based on the cost analysis timeframe or annual cost reported in each study, as this reflects the time at which economic inputs and cost estimates were generated and applied in the analysis. This approach was intended to align the baseline year with the analytic context of the economic evaluation, particularly because a time lag may exist between the conduct of the study and its eventual publication. When this information was not explicitly stated, the year of pharmaceutical pricing was used as a proxy. For studies in which CETs were expressed solely in terms of GDP per capita, the GDP per capita values were obtained from the World Bank. Two independent reviewers (Nam and Kim) extracted the data, and discrepancies were addressed by a third investigator (Park). When necessary, cost values were converted to US dollars using the exchange rate corresponding to the baseline year of each study.

Statistical analysis and quality assessment

Descriptive analyses were performed to summarize the characteristics of the included studies and to classify CET values into two groups: less than $35,000 and $35,000 or higher, using $35,000 as a descriptive benchmark based on recent GDP per capita values in South Korea to explore the distribution of reported CET values. To examine the associations between CETs and various factors, we conducted chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. We additionally performed cross-tabulation analysis to explore associations between funding sources and study characteristics. For this analysis, studies were categorized as profit-funded versus non-profit or not-funded, while studies with unreported funding sources were excluded. Because two studies reported both profit and non-profit funding sources, they were counted in both categories for descriptive purposes. Therefore, the funding-source analysis was observation-based rather than strictly study-based and should be interpreted as exploratory. This analysis was exploratory and intended to assess potential variation in CET reporting practices across funding contexts. The variables examined included baseline year, CET reference type (WTP or GDP per capita), drug type, orphan drug designation, and insurance status. To assess trends in CET over time, we employed the non-parametric Mann–Kendall test to detect monotonic trends and used Sen’s slope estimator to quantify the rate of change. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value <0.05. All data were analysed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and SPSS 28.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) statistical software.

The Quality of Health Economic Studies (QHES) instrument was used to evaluate the quality of all included studies [17]. Each item was rated as “yes” (1 point), “partial” (0.5 points), or “no/not clear” (0 points), and weighted according to the original QHES scoring system. QHES scores were additionally examined in an exploratory manner to assess whether methodological quality was associated with the selection and reporting of CET values.

Results

Literature search and quality assessment

A total of 8,510 records were initially identified through the systematic search. After removing 1,522 duplicates, 6,988 unique studies were subjected to further screening. The titles and abstracts of these studies were reviewed, and 6,875 articles that did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria were excluded. We reviewed the full text of the remaining 113 articles and excluded 60 articles. Finally, we included 53 studies that focused on pharmaceuticals based on Korean data. Detailed information on the study selection process using the PRISMA diagram is presented in Fig. 1. The full list of included studies and extracted variables is provided in Supplementary Material Table 2 [18–70].

The QHES instrument was used to assess the quality of pharmacoeconomic studies. Out of the 53 studies, 27 (50.9%) scored 90 or higher, including 3 studies (5.7%) that achieved a perfect score of 100, indicating strong adherence to methodological rigor. An additional 23 studies (43.4%) scored between 80 and 89, reflecting a high level of methodological quality. Only three studies (5.7%) scored below 80, highlighting areas where study design and reporting could be enhanced. The quality of the pharmacoeconomic studies was consistent regardless of the funding source (Supplementary Material Table 3).

Overview of all included studies

The distribution of key variables related to CET evaluations in South Korea is summarized in Table 1, providing an overview of patterns across baseline years, CET references, drug types, funding sources, and other relevant factors. The most frequently reported CETs were between $25,000 and $34,999 (n = 24, 45.3%), whereas CETs above $45,000 were reported by only three (5.7%) studies. CET references were most frequently based on GDP per capita (n = 32, 60.4%), followed by WTP (n = 20, 37.7%). Only one study (1.9%) did not report the CET reference type. Regarding drug types, vaccines (n = 11, 20.8%) and anticancer drugs (n = 5, 9.4%) accounted for a smaller proportion. Funding sources were predominantly profit funding (n = 28, 50.9%), followed by non-profit funding (n = 18, 32.7%). High methodological quality was evidenced by QHES scores, where 27 (50.9%) studies scored 90 or above. Additionally, 4 (7.5%) studies addressed orphan drugs. Most of the evaluations targeted insurance-covered drugs (n = 46, 86.8%).

Trends in CET values

Figure 2 illustrates the trends in the median CET from 2007 to 2022 and shows a general upward trend in CETs over time. This trend was further supported by the Mann–Kendall test (p < 0.01), which confirmed a significant increasing trend in CETs across the study period.

To explore CET trends in relation to pharmaceutical characteristics, we conducted separate analyses by drug type. Anticancer drugs were compared with other treatments, while vaccines were analysed separately due to their distinct characteristics and relatively small number of observations. An overall upward trend in CETs was observed across drug categories as the baseline year increased. Notably, anticancer drugs consistently demonstrated higher CETs than both vaccines and other treatments across the study period (Fig. 3).

Comparison of study characteristics by CET levels and funding sources

Figure 4 presents the distribution of the studies by funding source and drug type, along with their corresponding CET values. We classified funding sources into four main categories: profit, non-profit, not funded, and not reported. Excluding studies in which the funding source was not reported (n = 7, 12.7%), the most common funding source was profit (n = 28, 50.9%), followed by non-profit (n = 18, 32.7%) and not funded (n = 2, 3.6%). Two studies by Kim et al. (2024) [68, 69] reported both profit and non-profit funding sources and were therefore counted in both categories, resulting in a total of N = 55 observations in the funding source analysis. The median CET was highest in non-profit-funded studies ($29,754), followed by profit-funded studies, while the lowest median CET was observed in not-funded studies ($20,979).

Table 2 compares study characteristics by CET level, categorized as <$35,000 and ≥$35,000. Among the variables, only the drug type showed a significant difference (p = 0.036). Anticancer drugs were more frequent in the higher CET group (30.0% vs. 4.4%). In contrast, vaccines were evenly distributed across both CET groups, and other treatments were more common in studies with CETs below $35,000. Other variables, including baseline years, CET reference type (GDP per capita vs. WTP), QHES score, orphan drug designation, and insurance status, did not differ significantly between groups. As shown in Supplementary Material Table 4, no statistically significant differences were found between profit-funded and other-funded studies in CET levels or other variables (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically reviewed pharmacoeconomic studies conducted in South Korea since 2007, highlighting several important findings related to the use of CETs. Over time, the median CET exhibited a gradual upward trend. This pattern may be partly explained by the widespread use of GDP per capita–based references, which accounted for the majority of cases in our review (60.4%), compared with WTP-based approaches (37.7%). Because GDP-based thresholds are inherently linked to economic growth, their predominant use may have contributed to the observed increase in reported CET values. In the early period following the introduction of the positive list system (2007–2013), CET values were generally aligned with approximately one GDP per capita [15, 71].

Subsequently, policy developments—including the expansion of coverage for severe diseases and the introduction of risk-sharing agreements (RSA) in 2014 to facilitate patient access to high-cost medicines under the positive list system—may be associated with greater flexibility in the acceptance of higher ICER values, particularly for anticancer and rare disease treatments [72, 73]. Treatments for severe or life-threatening conditions are often associated with a higher societal willingness to pay, reflecting their clinical and ethical importance. In established HTA frameworks, disease severity and unmet medical need are frequently considered when interpreting cost-effectiveness evidence. For example, NICE in the United Kingdom has adopted severity-based modifiers that assign greater weight to health gains in severe conditions, allowing greater flexibility in the acceptance of higher ICER values in such contexts [74–76]. Although these approaches do not directly determine the CET values reported in individual studies, they provide useful context for understanding why relatively higher CET values were observed in studies evaluating severe conditions.

We further explored CET reporting by funding source as part of our assessment of study-level characteristics. In this exploratory analysis, no meaningful differences in CET values were observed across groups, suggesting that CET selection in published studies may not be systematically driven by funding-related factors. Differences observed across therapeutic areas should therefore be interpreted separately from the overall temporal trend.

Recent international discussions on cost-effectiveness thresholds have expanded beyond traditional GDP- and WTP-based approaches to include opportunity cost–based thresholds and severity-based modifiers [6, 7, 77]. Although these approaches cannot be directly applied to South Korea because of differences in healthcare systems and decision-making frameworks, they provide useful context for ongoing discussions regarding CET selection and interpretation in the Korean setting. Although South Korea does not have a clearly defined CET, research in this area has been steadily advancing, reflecting ongoing efforts to enhance the framework for rational healthcare decision-making. Previous studies, such as those by Bae et al. (2018) [15, 78] and Ahn et al. (2013) [71], have explored CETs through theoretical frameworks, focusing on approaches such as WTP- and GDP-based thresholds. While these studies highlighted the importance of societal values and flexibility in evaluating severe diseases, they provided limited empirical evidence regarding how CETs are applied in practice.

Furthermore, although HIRA publicly discloses the ICER values for new drug submissions that undergo pharmacoeconomic evaluation, these figures should not be interpreted as explicit CETs. The published ICERs represent accepted values in individual cases; however, the threshold used in decision-making remains undefined [79, 80]. Moreover, the CET values reported in published pharmacoeconomic studies reflect analytic assumptions adopted by researchers rather than the decision criteria used in reimbursement appraisals. Therefore, the findings of this review should be interpreted as reflecting reporting practices in the published literature, not as implicit threshold used in reimbursement decision-making.

A potential selection bias exists in this study regarding drugs under RSA, particularly anticancer drugs [73, 81]. Pharmaceutical companies may be less inclined to publish the results of economic evaluations of drugs covered under RSA because the data may not align with their strategic or market interests [82]. This could lead to a limited representation of the economic evaluation of such medications, resulting in an incomplete understanding of their cost-effectiveness. Given that anticancer drugs are often prioritized for RSA owing to their high costs and significant clinical benefits, the underrepresentation of these drugs in this study may have influenced the representativeness of the findings [83]. Therefore, this limitation should be carefully considered in policymaking and further research to ensure that decisions regarding anticancer drugs are informed by comprehensive and unbiased economic evidence.

The baseline year was defined based on the cost analysis period of each included study, as it reflects the time at which economic inputs and cost estimates were generated. Using the cost analysis year as the baseline may introduce potential bias in interpreting temporal trends, as it may not fully capture changes in policy or decision-making contexts. In addition to this limitation, several other limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, this review was limited to published pharmacoeconomic studies. Consequently, unpublished studies, confidential reimbursement submissions, and other non-publicly available economic evaluations could not be included. Therefore, the reported CET values may not fully reflect the range of CET assumptions adopted across all economic evaluations conducted in South Korea, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Second, outcome reporting bias is another factor, whereby studies may selectively report favorable results, further distorting the understanding of drug effectiveness and cost efficiency. Third, the conversion of CET values into USD using exchange rates in baseline years may have introduced bias due to exchange-rate fluctuations, potentially affecting the interpretation of temporal trends. Therefore, the observed temporal trends should be interpreted as exploratory patterns in reported CET values and may partly reflect exchange-rate movements, GDP growth, and the predominance of GDP per capita–based references. Finally, the $35,000 cutoff used for categorical analyses was intended solely as a descriptive benchmark and should not be interpreted as an explicit or implicit cost-effectiveness threshold used in reimbursement decision-making. Given temporal variations in GDP per capita and exchange rates, the findings from these categorical analyses should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusion

We conducted a systematic review of CETs reported in South Korean pharmacoeconomic evaluations, with a focus on temporal trends and key study characteristics, such as funding type and drug category. As the number of high-cost pharmaceuticals continues to rise and the aging population places increasing pressure on the national healthcare insurance budget, CETs are expected to play a key role in shaping policies that balance financial sustainability and patient access. Therefore, ensuring that CETs remain at appropriate levels—neither excessively high, which could overburden healthcare budgets, nor too low, which may restrict access to essential treatments—remains an ongoing challenge. This study provides a descriptive overview of CET reporting practices in South Korea and highlights variations in CET values across different study contexts. These findings provide useful insight into how CET values are selected in published economic evaluations and may serve as a reference for interpreting cost-effectiveness thresholds in both academic research and policy discussions.
