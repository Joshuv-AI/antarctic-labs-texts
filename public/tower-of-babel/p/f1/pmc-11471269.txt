Induction cladding of alloys and metal-matrix composite coatings: A review
Authors: Jing Yu, Shuai Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInduction cladding is a promising surface technology that combines the advantages of surface coatings and induction heating. It is an energy-efficient, environment-friendly, and cost-effective method that facilitates the fabrication of coatings with controllable thicknesses and ensures metallurgical bonding between the coating and the substrate. Owing to the high power-conversion efficiency of helical coil, induction cladding is particularly adaptable for the application of coatings on long shafts and rod parts, which find widespread use in mining and energy machinery. This paper provides a comprehensive overview of the state-of-the-art methods in induction cladding. Herein we focus on its mechanisms, cladding process and parameters, commonly used materials, simulations, innovative induction cladding technologies, industrial applications, problems, and future developments in this field.Keywords: Induction cladding, Alloy coating, Metal-matrix composite coating, Process parameters, Simulation

Introduction

Most failures in metallic engineering components originate from their surfaces or sub-surfaces. Common failure modes include wear, abrasion, fatigue, fretting, corrosion, oxidation, and stress corrosion cracking (SCC) [1], which significantly impede operating reliability and service life. Therefore, enhancing the quality of component surfaces is of utmost importance. Certain commonly applied surface coating techniques in industry include electroplating, electroless plating, thermal spraying, welding, laser cladding, and induction cladding. Fig. 1 illustrates the coating thicknesses achieved by applying the various surface coating techniques. Coatings produced via electroplating, electroless plating, and thermal spraying bond with substrates mechanically. This affords lower bonding strength and limited coating thickness, and the coating can be easily detached under heavy load conditions. In contrast, coatings fabricated using welding, laser cladding, and induction cladding are bonded to the substrates metallurgically and have dense microstructures.

Surface coating techniques in engineering application.

Induction cladding, which combines the advantages of induction heating and surface coating technology, facilitates the preparation of metallic and metal-matrix composite layers on substrates. This method, characterised by its energy efficiency, environment-friendliness, and cost-effectiveness [2], facilitates easy fabrication of coatings with controllable thicknesses and metallurgical bonding. Using “induction cladding” or “induction heating” and “coating” as keywords, we searched for previous publications, including journal articles and dissertations, from the Web of Science and CNKI database (China National Knowledge Infrastructure). The statistical results of these publications (Fig. 2) show a gradual increase in the number of publications on induction-clad coatings over the past two decades.

Number of publications on induction-clad coating in recent two decades.

Despite numerous studies on induction cladding and several industrial applications on websites, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, systematic reviews on this topic are scarce. Thus, this review paper summarises the state-of-the-art methods in induction cladding, focussing on the mechanisms, cladding process and parameters, commonly used clad materials, applications with other technologies, simulations, industrial applications, problems, and future developments in this field.

Principles of induction cladding

The fundamental theories related to electromagnetic induction were developed approximately 200 years. They laid the foundation for the electromagnetic applications observed today, such as the heating, welding, and surface hardening of metallic structures [3,4]. The principal foundations for induction cladding encompass Maxwell's equations, Faraday's law of induction, the Joule heating effect, the skin effect, and other electromagnetic phenomena. Induction cladding utilises two heating sources: one arises from the induced electromagnetic field within the metal work piece, whereas the other is the hysteresis heat produced when the temperature is below the Curie temperature (for ferromagnetic materials, such as iron, nickel, and cobalt, as well as their alloys). However, once the heating temperature exceeds the Curie point, the material transforms from metallic state to a non-ferromagnetism state. This results in the disappearance of hysteresis heat. Therefore, the primary heating source during induction cladding is the induced eddy current. Owing to the disparate electromagnetic properties of the coating and the substrate, the induced current changes abruptly at the coating/substrate interface. Consequently, the melted coating material and substrate surface are subjected to metallurgical bonding.

Induction heating of coating layer and metallic substrate

Self-fluxing alloy powders, which are commonly used as cladding materials, have an approximate melting point of 1000 °C, and relatively low Curie points (e.g., the Curie point of nickel-based alloy powder is below 200 °C). At the initiation of induction heating, the coating materials rapidly transform from magnetic to non-ferromagnetic [5]. Moreover, the eddy current within the coating layer shows minimal change. In contrast, the Curie point of bulk metallic substrates is relatively high (e.g., the Curie point of 45 steel is 768 °C), which results in gradual variation in their ferromagnetic properties as the induction heating proceeds. For ferromagnetic materials, the magnetic permeability and the electrical conductivity significantly change under cold (below the Curie point) and heating (above the Curie point) conditions, which results in a variation in the penetration depth of eddy current.

At the beginning of induction heating, the eddy current near the substrate surface exhibits a distribution under the cold condition (Curve 1 in Fig. 4). The closer to the substrate surface, the larger value the eddy current density, consequently, the more rapid the rise in the temperature. When the temperature near the substrate surface is above the Curie point, the thin layer loses the magnetic property, whereas the adjacent substrate still maintains ferromagnetism. Owing to the decrease in magnetic permeability and electrical conductivity within the demagnetised layer, the intensity of eddy current decreases in this area. This results in the occurrence of the highest eddy current density in the magnetised/demagnetised layer interface (Curves 2 and 3, respectively, in Fig. 4). The varied intensity of the eddy current facilitates a more rapid temperature rise at the interface than that on the substrate surface. The demagnetised layer moves into the interior of substrate, which results in the whole substrate being continuously heated layer by layer until the heating penetration depth is close to the skin effect depth. This heating mechanism is referred to as the penetrative heating mode, characterised by induction heating in ferromagnetic materials.

Distribution of eddy current during induction heating [6].

Induction heating at coating/substrate interface

The differences in the electromagnetic properties of the coating and substrate result in a sudden change in the eddy current density at the coating/substrate interface, which is a prerequisite for metallurgical bonding. The distribution of eddy current density within the coating and substrate is shown in Fig. 5. Self-fluxing alloy powders are transformed into non-ferromagnetic materials when the heating temperature exceeds 200 °C. Meanwhile, the bulk metallic substrates still maintain their ferromagnetic properties. At the initiation of induction heating, a significant portion of the eddy current is concentrated within the coating and on the substrate surface, leading to rapid temperature increase (Curve 1). With the progress of induction cladding, the substrate gradually heats from the surface to the interior, causing the ferromagnetic substrate to be converted into a non-ferromagnetic substrate layer by layer. Therefore, two significant changes occur in the current density during this cladding stage (Curve 2). With further progress of induction cladding, the substrate is completely converted to a non-ferromagnetic material. Meanwhile, the eddy current density at the coating/substrate interface decreases (Curve 3).

If the heating power density is excessively low, even if δheating<δeddy, the actual heating mode shifts to heat conduction. Conversely, when the heating power density is sufficiently high, the temperature within δheating can rapidly reach the design value, and the heat effect on the substrate is minimal. However, if the heating duration is excessively short, the cladding coating may be partially melted and/or the coating/substrate interface may be defective. Therefore, ensuring deposition quality necessitates the careful design of proper parameters.

Induction cladding process

The induction cladding process includes the pretreatment of substrate, prefabrication of deposited coatings, and induction heating, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Each step influences the quality and property of coating.

Schematic of induction cladding process.

Substrate pretreatment and coating prefabrication

A clean and roughened surface is vital for enhancing the bonding strength between the substrate and the coating. To achieve this, the substrate surface must be subjected to sandblasting and ultrasonic cleaning for degreasing and activation before proceeding with the subsequent processes. Coating prefabrication involves the application of cladding materials onto a pretreated substrate surface. This can be realised through commonly used methods such as thermal/plasma/cold spraying and cold precoating.

The combination of various spraying technologies and induction heating offers the benefits of dense coating and metallurgical bonding. Zou et al. [8] deposited a WC-Ni60 composite layer on 17-4 PH steel using high-velocity oxygen fuel (HVOF). Subsequently, they performed induction cladding on the precoated layer. The bonding between the coating and substrate transformed from mechanical and partial metallurgical bonding to complete metallurgical bonding following induction cladding. OH et al. [9] performed induction heating on a thermally sprayed Co-based alloy coating and observed a decrease in porosity from 8.73 % to 0.14 %. Although prefabricated coatings using thermal spraying are simple and convenient, their cost is relatively high. Moreover the cladding materials are susceptible to severe oxidation. Wang et al. [10]. found that both thermally sprayed and cold-precoated layers exhibited metallurgical bonding with substrates; however, the cold-precoated coatings exhibited a denser microstructure, slightly greater hardness, and better corrosion resistance than induction-remelted coatings. Liu et al. prepared an FeCoCrAlCu HEA coating on 45# steel via cold-spray-assisted induction remelting [11]. The HEA coating comprised an amplitude-modulated structure BCC phase, a nanoscale BCC phase, and a nano-twinned FCC phase. Koshuro et al. [12] fabricated titanium oxide coatings via plasma spraying followed by induction heating, which increased the microhardness and adhesive strength. Wu et al. [13] compared the induction heating to furnace heating as the post-process of cold sprayed Inconel 625 coating. Due to the enhancement of atomic diffusion by eddy current, the induction heated sample possesses larger grain size and larger δ precipitates compared to the furnace heated one. Consequently, the induction heated sample shows slightly lower hardness, better ductility and adhesion strength. They also investigated the influence of heating temperature [14]. With the increase of heating temperature, the porosity and the inter-particle bonding was enhanced. Due to the eddy current crownding and detouring, preferential Joule heating concentrated around the pores, resulting in closure of the pores, as shown in Fig. 8. The mechanism of the formation of dense coating and metallic bonds is similar to induction sinstering [15,16].

Schematic diagram of cold-spray-assisted induction remelting [11] (a), optical microscope of as-sprayed sample and induction heating at various temperature (b–f), porosity depending on the heating temperature (g) and, the mechansim of closure of the pores (h) [15,16].

Cold precoating, the most studied method, involves mixing the cladding powder with a binder to form a paste, which is then precoated onto the substrate surface. The coated workpiece is subsequently preheated at 150–200 °C, close to the Curie point of the cladding powder, for several hours. Commonly used binders include rosin, sodium silicate, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) [17], and carboxymethyl cellulose. The type and blending ratio of the binder significantly impact the coating quality. A high proportion of binders can result in blistering and swelling of the precoated layer, resulting in pores, inclusions, and even breakage. Conversely, a low proportion of binders may result in defects and gas pores in the coating. However, limited studies have focused on the influence and mechanisms of the binders. Xu et al. [18] found that both a rosin–turpentine mixture and sodium silicate exerted no significant influence on the microstructure of induction-clad coatings; however, the coating containing a rosin–turpentine mixture exhibited higher hardness. Li et al. [19] employed sodium silicate as a binder to prepare a Ni60/Invar 36 composite coating. To prevent oxidation and breakage of the coating, they created a protective layer containing flour and clay (1:5). Dong et al. [20] sprayed nickel and titanium mixed powder on a YG8 substrate to synthesise NiTi alloy coating via high-frequency induction heating under an argon protective atmosphere, as shown in Fig. 9. Liu et al. prepared a nickel-based coating employing one-step spin coating with nickel powder, epoxy resin, silica, and butyl acetate [21]. Hu et al. [22] synthesised a Mo-coating on 40Cr steel via pack-cementation and inductioin heating, which provides a new method of procating.

Schematic of the preparation of NiTi alloy coating [20].

Researchers have proposed innovative methods for replacing cladding materials, as shown in Fig. 10. Hu et al. placed an alloy powder between a cylindrical specimen and a graphite crucible to coat the exterior of Q235 steel followed by ultrasonic-frequency inductive cladding (Fig. 10(a)) [23]. Chang et al. utilised a steel crucible containing alloy powder to manufacture a Co-based alloy/AISI 4140 bimetal via vacuum induction cladding (Fig. 10(b)) [24]. Sosnovakii et al. [25] devised an induction deposition method for the interior surface of a cylindrical hollow workpiece and theoretically calculated the cladding parameters (Fig. 10(c)).

Influence of process parameters

Based on Eqs. (5), (6), (7), it is evident that the primary process parameters during induction cladding include current density, frequency, heating time, and the gap between the inductor and workpiece. The current density directly affects the electromagnetic energy input to the coating. Higher current density facilitates increased Joule heating, resulting in lower porosity and fewer defects in the coating. However, excessively high current density can be detrimental to the formation of metallurgical bonds and may cause overheating and breakage of the deposited coating [26]. For a specific device, the current frequency can only vary within a certain range. As most of heating should concentrate on the coating and a thin layer of the substrate, the appropriate frequencies for induction cladding are ultrasonic (20–30 kHz) and high frequencies (200–300 kHz). Higher frequencies result in a smaller heating depth and higher heating rate. The heating duration can be adjusted based on the heating power and the moving speed of the coil/workpiece. Chen et al. [27] reported that a decrease in the scanning speed of the coil results in higher cladding temperature and longer heating time, thereby resulting in a higher content of γ-FeNi substitutional solid-solution and more even microhardness in the coating. Although a prolonged heating time yields a denser coating, excessive heating input may generate an overburnt coating and thus exert a significant heat effect on the substrate [28,29]. Thus, we propose a multistep induction cladding method to gradually input thermal energy, which facilitates a dense coating and good metallurgical bonding [30], as shown in Fig. 11.

Multistep induction cladding and an example of Ni-based coating [30].

Induction cladding coatings and performances

At present, there exists no specialised raw material for induction cladding. Most studies have used coating materials intended for laser cladding or thermal spraying. The commonly used materials for induction cladding include self-fluxing alloy powders, comprising Ni-based, Fe-based, and Co-based alloys with the addition of elements that promote the formation of eutectics at low melting points. In recent years, many researchers have fabricated metal-matrix composite coatings via the incorporation of hard ceramic particles, self-lubricating materials, and rare-earth elements. Table 1 summarises recent studies on induction-clad materials. The fundamental requirements for cladding materials include flowability at molten state and appropriate thermo-physical properties. In addition, the electromagnetic properties of cladding materials, such as the Curie temperature, electrical resistivity, and magnetic permeability, are crucial considerations for processing parameters and coating quality.

Summary of induction cladding materials and performances.

Ni-based alloy coatings: Ni-based alloy powders, particularly Ni-based self-fluxing alloys, are the most widely used coating materials in surface engineering, with a predominant focus on induction cladding studies. Based on the elemental composition of the alloys, Ni-based alloy powders can be categorised into NiBSi and NiCrBSi types. Researchers commonly observe a distinct bright white diffusion-transition band between the coating and substrate, which indicates the formation of metallurgical bonding. The resulting Ni-based coatings exhibit a dense microstructure, with carbides and borides uniformly dispersed in the γ-Ni phase, thereby imparting high hardness and excellent wear properties [31,32]. Chang et al. [33] investigated the influence of temperature on the phase composition fraction (Ni3Si + Ni3B, γ-Ni + Ni3Si, chromium carbides, and borides) and hardness of various phase structures during vacuum induction. Muntean et al. [34] fabricated NiCrBSi coatings with various chemical constitutes via flame spraying and induction heating. The results indicated that the induction-remelted coatings exhibited lower porosity and better wear resistance than the as-sprayed ones. Liu et al. conducted induction cladding of Ni60 coatings on the interior and exterior walls of a steel pipe [35]. They observed that the corrosion rate of the coating was 1/11 times lower than that of 1045 steel in salt fog. Static and dynamic corrosion rates of the coating were 1/8 and 1/100 lower, respectively, under a high-pressure CO2 atmosphere. In another study, we applied Ni60 layers on grey cast iron using induction cladding and identified abrasive and mild oxidation wear as the primary wear mechanisms [30,36]. In addition, the corrosion resistance in various corrosive medium was investigated. In simulated seawater and dilute H2SO4 solutions, the passive film on the coating surface significantly impeded electron transfer [37,38]. In the immersion test conducted in concentrated H2SO4 solution at 150 °C, a dense oxide firmly adhered to the coating surface, resulting in a corrosion rate of the coating that was 1/10 of that of the substrate [28]. Li et al. [39] employed a mixture of Invar36 and Ni60 powders as an induction cladding material. This exhibited enhanced thermal compatibility with the substrate. Yang et al. [40,41] prepared Ni45 alloy coatings with the directional structure via plasma spraying followed by induction heating and water cooling. Compared with the as-sprayed and simply induction-remelted coatings, the coating with directional structure, produced by forced cooling, exhibited a relatively lower hardness, and demonstrated superior wear resistance, as shown in Fig. 12. Zhao et al. [42] conducted vacuum induction heating on an electroplated Ni coating on TC4 alloy, which promoted the diffusion process and the formation of intermetallic compounds at the coating/substrate interface.

Optical micrographs and cross-section of Ni45 alloy coatings fabricated by plasma spraying (a, d), induction remelting (b, e), and forced cooling (c, f) [40].

Fe-based alloy coatings: Fe-based self-fluxing alloy powders are primarily composed of Fe with specific amounts of B, Si, and Cr. These powders find extensive use in surface engineering applications owing to their low cost. However, during the heating process, iron powders are prone to oxidation, leading to a higher melting temperature and the occurrence of pores and slag inclusions in the coating. The commonly employed Fe-based alloy powders can be categorised as austenitic stainless steel or high-chromium cast iron. Considering the similar chemical compositions of the coating materials and carbon-steel substrate, elemental diffusion readily occurs at the coating/substrate interface. The phase structures of induction-clad Fe-based coatings primarily comprise α-Fe, γ-Fe, eutectic structures, carbides, and borides. Hu et al. investigated the wear properties of induction-clad Fe-based coatings at both room and elevated temperature [43,44]. These coatings exhibited superior wear resistance at high temperatures, with mild abrasion being the primary wear mechanism. They also investigated the corrosion properties of induction-clad Fe-based coatings in 3.5 wt% NaCl solution, demonstrating a one-order-of-magnitude lower corrosion rate than that of Q235 steel [23]. In another study, we incorporated a welding flux agent into the cladding powder to prevent oxidation of the Fe-based alloy powder and to promote complete melting [45], as shown in Fig. 13. Bing et al. prepared a 316 L coating on a 304 substrate using high-frequency induction cladding with wire feeding [46]. The microstructure of the cladding layer at the cross-section varied along the temperature gradient, similar to that of the laser-clad layer.

Schematic and micrograph of the forming mechanism of Fe-based coating without and with welding flux agent [45].

Co-based alloy coatings: Co-based alloy powders primarily comprise Co, Cr, and Fe. The phase structures of Co-based alloy coatings are characterised by the dispersion of Cr7C3 and CrB in a stable Co/Cr solid-solution phase, enhancing their hot hardness, wear resistance, corrosion resistance, and oxidation resistance. In contrast to iron (769 °C) and nickel (358 °C), cobalt (1150 °C) exhibits the highest Curie temperature, thereby offering a wide temperature range of ferromagnetic transition. However, owing to their scarce reserves and high cost, Co-based alloys are primarily utilised in high-value components such as aerospace and medical instruments. Certain studies have focused on the induction cladding of Co-based alloys. Chang et al. reported a vacuum induction-clad Co-based coating comprising a Co-rich phase, Cr-rich phase, and W3CoB3. They investigated its corrosion resistance in various acid solutions at both low and high temperatures [24,47], as shown in Fig. 14. Ok et al. precoated a Co-based alloy layer onto a steel substrate using gas flame spraying, followed by an induction melting treatment to enhance the bonding interface [9]. In another study, we conducted a comparative analysis of induction-clad Ni-, Fe-, and Co-based coatings regarding their properties, energy consumption, and cost [48], revealing that the wear resistance under dry sliding ranked as Ni-based > Co-based > Fe-based.

Microstructure of Co-based alloy coating [47].

Metal-matrix composite coatings and HEA coatings: As mechanical equipment operating conditions become increasingly severe, single-metallic alloy coatings are no longer adequate. Therefore, numerous studies have focused on metal-matrix composite coatings via the addition of reinforced materials or in-situ synthesis. Commonly used reinforced materials include ceramic particles with high hardness and melting points, such as WC, SiC, TiN, and TiC. Kılıçay et al. [49] studied the microstructure and wear properties of induction-clad NiCrBSi coatings with various WC contents. They reported that certain WC particles dissolved into the dendritic and eutectic structures, while others remained un-melted and embedded in the NiCrBSi matrix. The increase in WC addition resulted in higher microhardness and improved wear resistance. Compared with plasma spraying and laser cladding, induction cladding exhibits lower input energy density, resulting in larger amount of un-melted WC powder and pore defects in the deposited coating [50]. Ji et al. [51] found that short graphite fibres were primarily distributed at the grain boundaries, with the coating hardness slightly increasing with increasing graphite content. Sun et al. [52] fabricated Ni60/20 % SiC composite coatings with a thickness of 400 μm and a microhardness of 750 HV using intermittent induction heating modes in an argon atmosphere. Pan et al. [53] fabricated a Ni-based coating with Ni-coated TiC-reinforced particles on an H13 steel plate using high-frequency induction cladding. First, they coated a Ni-P layer on TiC particles via electroless plating and then blended it with Ni-based alloy powder using a ball mill to reduce the electrical resistivity of the prefabricated coating. The induction-clad Ni-coated TiC-particle-reinforced Ni 60 coatings exhibited better corrosion resistance and thermal fatigue in molten aluminium alloy compared to the Ni-based coating [54], as shown in Fig. 15. Huang et al. [55,56]. investigated the influence of different heat treatments on the microstructure, hardness, and anti-wear properties of high-frequency induction cladding Ni-based TiC coatings. They also produced an induction-clad Ni-based self-lubricating composite coating with Y2O3 on 35CrMo steel, exhibiting lower friction coefficient and wear loss, along with better corrosion resistance compared to coatings without Y2O3 and the substrate [57]. Furthermore, they found that the addition of 10 % TiN to Co-based alloy coatings resulted in excellent hardness and wear resistance [58]. Si et al. [59] prepared a composite Ni-based coating with diamond on 65Mn steel via induction heating. The element diffusion and metallurgical bonding were observed at the diamond/filler alloy interface, which aids in increasing the wear resistance of the composite coatings, as shown in Fig. 16.

SEM morphologies of Ni-coated TiC-reinforced coating and Ni-based coating [54].

In-situ-synthesised composite coatings were prepared using chemical reactions among the raw materials during induction cladding. The in-situ reinforced phases exhibited uniform distribution in the metal-matrix, controllable size, and a prefect interface. Dense and pore-free in-situ TiC/TiB2 reinforced Ni-based composite coatings with metallurgical bonding to the substrate were fabricated from a mixture of Ni-based alloy powder and B4C + Ti using induction cladding [63]. Induction-clad TiC-reinforced Ni-based composite coatings were produced from a mixture of Ni60A, titanium, and carbon powder, exhibiting high hardness of 1000 HV and low wear loss [64]. Yu et al. reported that during induction cladding process B and Ti were completely reacted to in-situ synthesised TiB reinforcements, which were uniformly distributed in dual-phase Ti matrix [65]. They investigated the mechanical properties of in-situ TiC/Ti composite coatings using grid indentation combined with a three-peak Gaussian fitting method and isolated indentation. Consequently, they revealed the comprehensive mechanical properties of the coatings [66]. The in-situ formation of TiC reinforcements followed the dissolution-precipitation mechanism, with the morphology, dimensions, and distribution of the reinforcements primarily governed by the raw powder constituents and cladding parameters [29,67]. Meng et al. produced high-entropy alloy (HEA) coatings CoCrFeNiCux using induction cladding and investigated the influence of various Cu contents [68,69], as shown in Fig. 17. They reported that with an increase in Cu content, the metastable single-phase structure transformed into a stable dual-phase structure, and the grain size was refined. However, a higher Cu content decreased the hardness and wear resistance, coupled with degraded antioxidant ability and corrosion resistance.

Simulation of induction cladding

Induction cladding constitutes a complex physical and metallurgical process, involving the interaction of electromagnetic, temperature, and stress fields. On the other hand, eddy current and temperature-dependent material properties facilitate a redistribution of the multiple-physics field within the cladding layer and substrate with the progress of induction heating. Consequently, owing to varying mass transfer conditions, the coating exhibits a gradient microstructure and a non-equilibrium solidification microstructure. Therefore, an accurate numerical model of induction cladding is invaluable for deeper comprehension of the cladding mechanism and optimisation of process parameters. Tong et al. [71] proposed an electromagnetic-thermal-phase transformation-mechanical coupled numerical model for the induction heat treatment of shaft parts. Their model calculated the temperature field, microstructure, and stress evolution during induction hardening and subsequent tempering. Mühl et al. [72] elaborated on the influence of phase-dependent material properties of low-alloyed steel on residual stress and hardening depth during induction hardening using a multi-physical finite element method. Khalifa et al. [73] conducted numerical investigations on the edge effect during induction heating and optimised the hardness profile of a specimen using a coil with flux concentrators based on COMSOL software. In the course of numerical simulation of induction heating, there two critical problems: the self-consistent boundary value problem of electrodynamics and thermal conductivity for the “inductor-conductor” system [74]. Wang et al. [75] explored the melting process by considering swelling deformation and cladding breakage during vertical tin cladding using the solidification/melting model in ANSYS FLUENT. Despite numerous studies on the simulation of induction heating, numerical investigations on induction cladding are scarce. Unlike induction heating, induction cladding involves two different types of materials: a deposited layer and a substrate, which renders the coating/substrate interface and bonding behaviour intricate. Notably, there is a lack of property parameters for cladding powders, particularly temperature-dependent parameters. At present, most researchers substitute the parameters of cladding powders with those of corresponding bulk alloys, which possess similar elemental compositions. Specifically, the electromagnetic properties of the cold precoatings significantly vary with the types and content of binders. Liu et al. conducted a numerical study on the temperature field during high-frequency induction cladding of Ni-based alloy coatings. The deviation between simulation and experiment was within ±25 °C, demonstrating the high accuracy of the proposed 3D model [76]. Mao et al. compared the accuracy of calculations between two-dimensional and three-dimensional models and delineated the contexts suitable for each model [77]. Cen et al. established a numerical model for fabricating Ni-based coatings on boiler tubes. They observed that the speed of electromagnetic heating was correlated with the power frequency and input current, with the optimal operating conditions being a frequency of 250 kHz and a current of 1180 A [78]. Dey et al. [79] developed a numerical models of induction-cladded Zn layer on mild steel and, determined the optimal heating parameters. In our previous studies, we numerically investigated electromagnetic and temperature fields during induction cladding using COMSOL Multiphysics software [80]. In addition, we integrated circulated cooling water within the hollow inductor coil and shielding gas in the numerical model, thereby improving simulation accuracy [81], as shown in Fig. 18. Further, we studied the uneven distribution of the electromagnetic field using a numerical model. Consequently we revealed a relatively concentrated magnetic field line in the middle of the induction coil, whereas it was sparse at the upper and lower ends [82]. Liu et al. [83] and Sun et al. [84] constructed a three-dimensional model of induction cladding on a metal plate. In addition, Sun developed a numerical model of UHF induction deposition with wire feeding and investigated the flow behaviour and mass transport in the molten pool [85]. Li et al. [86] investigated the influences of four processing parameters on the induction-cladding quality using experimental-numerical approach and quantitative sensitivity analysis methodology.

Simulation results of induction cladding a Co-based coating on 45# steel [81].

Innovative induction cladding and industrial applications

Xu et al. treated Ni-40 % WC composite coatings using a self-developed electrical contact strengthening (ECS) device (Fig. 19) [87]. Following the ECS treatment, the induction-cladded coating exhibited decreased porosity and grain size, increased adhesion strength onto the substrate, and improved wear resistance. Cui [88] applied ultrasound of 66 W on induction-clad coating during the solidification stage. The results showed that ultrasound did not exert any significant influence on the microstructure; however, it damaged the bonding quality of the coating/substrate interface. Chen et al. [89] investigated the influence of tempering temperature on the microstructure and properties of an induction cladding Ni-40WC coating. Following heat treatment, the hard phases were homogeneously dispersed in the coating, and the grain size decreased, resulting in higher microhardness. Song et al. [90] found that after ultrasonic impact treatment, the microstructure of an induction-clad CoCrFeNiCu HEA coating transformed from a dual-phase FCC to a single-phase FCC. Further, the dislocation density and subgrain boundary increased, leading to improved mechanical properties, wear, and corrosion resistance. Du et al. [91] applied mechanical vibrations under a substrate during induction cladding and observed a decrease in gas pores and a more uniform microstructure. In recent years, certain researchers have designed movable inductors that can be applied in induction cladding on irregular or local surfaces. Liu et al. proposed continual local induction cladding and fabricated a WC-reinforced Ni60A composite coating on 45 steel using a self-developed device (Fig. 20) [83,92]. Their studies explored the feasibility of employing induction cladding for local strengthening and repair of large components with complex geometries.

(a) Geometric mode, (b) photograph of an innovative inductor with magnetiser, and (c) schematic of continual local induction cladding [83].

Hascoёt et al. [80] first proposed a novel technique to melt metal wire and substrate using an inductive power source in additive manufacturing (Fig. 21). Based on Hascoёt's method, Sun et al. devised laser-assisted ultra-high-frequency (UHF) induction deposition with wire feeding (Fig. 22) [94] During deposition, UHF induction heating was used to melt the metal wire. Meanwhile, low laser power was applied to heat the local surface of the substrate, thereby benefiting the formation of metallurgical bonds between the deposited material and the substrate.

The cylindrical spiral coil exhibited the highest electromagnetic conversion efficiency, rendering induction cladding particularly suitable for long shafts and rollers such as pump plungers, sucker-rod joints, and hydraulic props. Fig. 23 shows a high-performance coating applied to the outer surface of sucker-rod joints, achieved through flame spraying combined with induction cladding, a method extensively utilised in the petroleum industry [95]. Yang et al. [96] employed high-frequency induction cladding to fabricate a WC-reinforced Fe-based composite coating, approximately 2 mm thick, on a cutting-pick surface. The resulting coating, metallurgically bonded to the 20CrMnTi substrate, exhibited superior hardness and wear resistance compared to the substrate. Cheng et al. [97] utilised high-frequency induction cladding to produce a NiCrBSi coating on a sucker rod. They explored the impact of La2O3 content on microstructure and properties. Their findings indicated that the distribution of microstructure and phase structure became uniform with the increase in La2O3 content, and the hardness and erosion resistance at 6 % La2O3 content. Liu et al. [98] engineered an induction cladding machine featuring direct powder feeding and utilised it to prepare Ni-based alloy coatings on long shaft parts.

Problems and future development

(1)Induction cladding harnesses induced electromagnetic energy to apply an alloy or metal-matrix composite layer onto a metal substrate, offering numerous advantages such as high energy conversion efficiency, production efficiency, environmental friendliness, and low device and operating costs, thus highlighting its promising application prospects. However, several pivotal challenges impede the widespread adoption of induction cladding in industry.

Induction cladding harnesses induced electromagnetic energy to apply an alloy or metal-matrix composite layer onto a metal substrate, offering numerous advantages such as high energy conversion efficiency, production efficiency, environmental friendliness, and low device and operating costs, thus highlighting its promising application prospects. However, several pivotal challenges impede the widespread adoption of induction cladding in industry.

(2)Specialised requirements for induction cladding materials. Currently, induction cladding is reliant on self-fluxing alloy powders primarily used in thermal spraying and laser cladding. However, there exists a dearth of tailored materials specifically designed for induction cladding. In addition to factors such as fluidity, wettability, particle size, and thermo-physical properties, induction cladding requires the use of raw powders with specific electromagnetic properties, including low electric resistivity and high magnetic permeability. Moreover, the absence of temperature-dependent material parameters for cladding powders, particularly those incorporating various binders, poses a challenge for numerical modelling and process parameter optimisation.

(3)Challenges in the induction cladding process. During induction cladding in atmospheric conditions, the coating surface is susceptible to oxidation. Meanwhile, the uneven distribution of eddy currents may result in localised melting and flow of the molten alloy, resulting in coating breakage. Certain researchers have proposed vacuum induction cladding or the fabrication of protective layers outside the deposited coating to mitigate these issues. In the case of induction-clad metal-matrix composite coatings, the addition of reinforcement particles introduces discontinuities in coating properties, thereby resulting in uneven heating. For in-situ-synthesised composite coatings, the required energy for generating the reinforced phase must be factored into the cladding parameters. In addition, owing to the narrow parameter window, the application of design of experiments and optimisation methodologies becomes crucial for bridging multiple cladding parameters and coating properties.

(4)Simulations and cladding mechanisms. The fundamental principles of induction cladding revolve around the Joule heating effect and skin effect of the induced electromagnetic field. The temperature dependence and material property disparities result in a shifting coating/substrate interface during induction cladding. However, several intricate phenomena in induction cladding remain poorly understood. For instance, few numerical models consider the fluid behaviour and mass transfer in the molten zone, as well as the influences of the Lorentz force on microstructure, particularly the distribution of reinforced phases. These aspects have not been thoroughly explored in analytical and numerical investigations. Moreover, although cylindrical solenoid inductors are commonly used for long shafts and rods, other inductor shapes have lower electromagnetic energy conversion efficiency. This renders it challenging to perform induction cladding on flat surfaces and irregular parts. Thus, advancements in automation and integration with wire or powder feeding systems will broaden the applications of induction cladding to surface coatings with coatings with complex geometries and additive manufacturing.
