Professional Nurses Engagement in Non‐Nursing Work? An Examination of Reasons and Outcomes
Authors: Joyce J. Fitzpatrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo critically examine the effect on professional nurses' engagement in non‐nursing tasks on nurse, patient, and organizational outcomes.BackgroundResearch undertaken in several countries indicates that a significant amount of nurses' time is spent in non‐direct nursing care, often in non‐nursing tasks. Negative outcomes of professional nurses' time spent on non‐nursing tasks have been identified, including missed nursing care and burnout and turnover among nurses. Challenges for change in care delivery are addressed.ConclusionsThere are multiple opportunities for workforce redesign, including extending the applications of technology in care delivery. Nurse leaders and professional nurses must seize opportunities to engage in solutions to current challenges of nursing work, particularly within hospitals, to focus professional nursing work directly on patient care that leads to optimal health outcomes.Clinical RelevanceReducing the non‐nursing work for clinical nurses will reduce the overall workload and potentially lead to positive nurse and patient outcomes.

Recently, the Chief Nurse of a major hospital shared the story of her interaction with a professional nurse on a medical surgical unit. The nurse was using her own tools, brought from home, to put together a patient's walker. Why? Simply stated, the patient needed the walker for ambulation to progress toward discharge, and the nurse was meeting the patient's needs. When questioned about assuming this non‐nursing task (NNT), the nurse indicated that the hospital department responsible for putting the walker together was not responding in a timely manner. So, the nurse took it into her own hands to meet the patient's needs.

Simply stated, NNTs are those carried out by professional nurses that are not within the scope of professional nursing practice; these tasks do not require clinical knowledge or judgment by professional nurses. NNTs fall into several categories, including administrative tasks, medical tasks, auxiliary tasks, and those expected by other health professionals. And, any nurse who has worked in a hospital will recognize the frequency with which nurses engage in NNTs that are peripheral to professional nursing practice. Yet, these tasks often keep the workflow more efficient, and professional nurses are central to maintaining the workflow. In fact, professional nurses are often described as the “glue” in hospital functioning, the “backbone” of healthcare, present 24 h a day, 365 days a year.

Why do nurses assume NNTs? What are the structural and philosophical challenges that lead nurses to do NNTs? And what do we know about the related outcomes such as missed care? And, importantly, if nurses were relieved of NNTs through technological applications and additional auxiliary and technically trained personnel, would there be a nursing shortage?

Nurses are committed to providing the highest quality of nursing care for their patients. And, in the moment of providing care, nurses will do whatever is necessary for their patients, often without assessing who else might be available to assist. So, if the patient needs to be transported and no one else is around to do so, the nurse will transport the patient. If the patients' medication has not been retrieved from the pharmacy in a timely manner, the nurse will intervene and secure the medications. The nurse will also “hunt and gather” other materials that are needed, bed linen, an extra blanket, a glass of milk in the middle of the night. And, in the case of the story above, the nurse did the work of a technician, an individual who was assigned to put the walker together, but was not available when needed. While some of these NNTs may provide opportunities for direct patient interaction and observation of the patient's status, many of the activities fall outside of opportunities for patient interaction.

Several researchers have documented the percent of time professional nurses spend on NNTs and the resultant outcomes. The results across countries are consistent, including the link between NNT and patient quality in Oman (Al Amri et al. 2019); a time‐motion study in the United States which documented that approximately 10% of nurses' time spent on NNTs could be used more effectively for patient care (Yen et al. 2018); qualitative analyses in Jordan in which researchers identified the negative impact of performance of NNTs on nurses' effectiveness, productivity, and job satisfaction (Al‐Akash et al. 2024; Saifan et al. 2025); a cross‐sectional study in an Italian province in which approximately one‐third of nurses' shift time was devoted to NNTs, mainly administrative and auxiliary tasks (Grosso et al. 2021); and in an Egyptian study, missed nursing care was linked to NNTs (Hammad et al. 2021). A promising approach to further delineate conceptual understandings and empirical results related to nurses' engagement in NNTs should result in the proposed scoping review protocol to be implemented by Magro and colleagues (Magro et al. 2025).

Of additional concern is the effect of nurses' engagement in NNTs on the nurse workforce shortage. We are all aware of the current and projected shortages of professional nurses globally. In the recent World Health Organization (WHO) report on the State of the World's Nursing 2025, a current shortage of 5.8 million nurses was noted. Also of note was the inequitable distribution across regions (WHO 2025). Structural changes could be implemented to address both the shortages of professional nurses and the NNTs that nurses implement.

New models of care are a hot topic in healthcare today, models that address a wide range of challenges. Workforce redesign, particularly in hospital settings, is a continuing topic of deliberations. Often these deliberations are related to staff turnover and the resultant financial implications and the overall application of new technology in care delivery. While deliberations and experimentation are important, the most critical outcome of any healthcare design is the effect on patient care quality. Thus, patient outcomes and quality of care must be paramount in any healthcare redesign. Current models of care must be examined for their lack of attention to the “top of license” practices among professional nurses. Below license activities such as NNTs not only create inefficiencies but also negatively affect professional nurses' understanding of their own value and expertise.

Increasing the number and training of supportive personnel, including technicians for specific healthcare related tasks (e.g., phlebotomists, psychiatric mental healthcare technicians), and assistive personnel such as nursing assistants would ease the time and care burden of professional nurses. Undoubtedly, challenges exist in providing these support personnel throughout the 24 h that nurses work in hospitals. Additional challenges may exist in recruiting individuals to these narrowly defined jobs without opportunities for career advancement. The positive nature of healthcare work might be used to recruit individuals as healthcare delivery provides opportunities for meaningful interactions with individuals in need of care.

There also are technological supports that can be implemented immediately to ease the burden of professional nursing work and reduce nurses' engagement in NNTs. Robots have been introduced into healthcare settings to provide support and companionship for elderly patients and monitor individuals that need 1:1 surveillance, provide medication delivery, and transport supplies and equipment. The introduction of electronic health systems for charting, while expected to reduce the burden on professional nurses' work, has in fact taken professional nurses away from providing direct care. For nurses, the documentation burden is of particular concern, taking as much as 23% of nurses' time away from direct patient care during a 12‐h shift (Alobayli et al. 2023). Further, the documentation burden is often directly linked to nurse burnout, a major concern that often leads to nurse turnover and exacerbates the global shortage of nurses (Alobayli et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2021). Yet, enhancing the work environment through new models of care and new modalities can lead to changes in time that professional nurses spend in direct nursing care. Also, new uses of artificial intelligence, such as ambient listening, have the potential for reducing the documentation burden. Importantly, nurse‐centric models of technology applications should be part of the current and future planning of any technology changes in healthcare delivery (Johnson et al. 2025).

Also, in most hospitals and healthcare institutions nurses are employees, either working for the organization directly, or working on government or other contracts. Thus, the work requirements are often dictated by the employing agency, not necessarily consistent with professional nursing standards. In a recent opinion paper, I argued for a model in which nurses, through professional practice corporations or organizations, would contract professional nursing services to hospitals and healthcare organizations (Fitzpatrick 2026). While this model has potential application in the United States due to the predominant model of health care delivery, in countries where healthcare is government administered there might be another adaptation. Professional nurses could serve as consultants, similar to medical doctors, contracting with hospitals and healthcare organizations for services provided.

Any solution to our current and future healthcare challenges requires leadership, among nurse leaders, healthcare professional leaders, healthcare executives and government officials. Healthcare is complex and the work is demanding. There is not one solution to a multidimensional challenging situation. Yet, changes in technology and new understandings of system design and delivery portent dramatic changes in our world, including the world of health and healthcare delivery. In a recent McKinsey & Company report focused on nurses in the United States, the consultants estimated that if half of the time freed from NNTs and technology applications could be captured, 15% of nursing time could potentially be saved (Berlin et al. 2026). These changes could equate to an additional 4.5 million nurses among a workforce of 30 million nurses. The financial benefits to hospitals in the United States would be substantial as the cost of nurse turnover per nurse is estimated at $85,497 (Razmpour et al. 2026). In 2025, it was estimated that total annual losses per hospital averaged at least $5.19 million (Gamble 2026). While these financial estimates do not apply to hospitals and healthcare organizations globally, the benefits of reducing nurse turnover, i.e., maintaining high quality patient care and valuing the work of professional nurses, are similar.

In this same McKinsey & Company survey, nurses indicated that they wanted to spend more time with their patients, beyond the 54% of their time that was currently spent in direct patient care. These nurses also indicated that coaching and training of new nurses and professional development activities such as continuing education and participation in unit and organizational level governance activities were important aspects of their professional role. Less than 10% of nurses' time is devoted to these additional professional activities (Berlin et al. 2026).

As global nurse leaders we need to steer the debate, design the research to evaluate the change, and think more broadly about the challenges. The International Council of Nurses (ICN) has developed a policy guide for involving nursing leaders strategically in health workforce planning globally, noting that often policies about nursing workforce planning are made without the involvement of nurses (ICN 2025). Key recommendations include improving data accuracy within and across countries, optimizing the roles and contributions of the entire healthcare team members, including ensuring that nurses can work at their full scope of preparation for practice, and improving the delivery and organization of care (ICN 2025). Nurse leaders and professional nurses must seize the opportunity to design the future of healthcare at the local community levels as well as at the WHO policy levels. Policy content is often not currently embedded in nursing curricula and often nurses stand on the sidelines in policy making activities. Engagement and active participation is critical for change, and nurse participation through community and organizational activism can lead to global health policies that move the agenda on healthcare.
