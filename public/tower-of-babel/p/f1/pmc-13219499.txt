Effect of ethanol vapor on anti-browning and antioxidant properties of fresh-cut stem lettuce via regulation of reactive oxygen species metabolism
Authors: Minke Shi, Jiarui Wang, Qihan Guo, Jiayu Wang, Sarengaowa, Yaru Ji, Ying Xiao, Ke Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stem lettuce is a nutritious vegetable rich in dietary fiber, vitamins, and bioactive compounds, but fresh-cut processing accelerates browning, oxidative stress, and quality deterioration during storage. This study investigated the effects of ethanol vapor on browning inhibition, antioxidant capacity, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism in fresh-cut stem lettuce. Samples were treated with ethanol vapor at 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L and stored for 12 days. Among the treatments, 0.5 mL/L ethanol vapor most effectively delayed browning by suppressing the activities of peroxidase, polyphenol oxidase, and phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, while reducing the accumulation of superoxide anion radicals and hydrogen peroxide. This treatment also enhanced antioxidant defense systems by increasing the activities of superoxide dismutase, catalase, ascorbate peroxidase, and glutathione reductase, as well as non-enzymatic antioxidant capacity, as indicated by elevated FRAP, DPPH, and ABTS scavenging activities. By the end of storage, FRAP and DPPH values in the 0.5 mL/L group were approximately 1.8 and 2-fold higher than those of the control. Overall, ethanol vapor at 0.5 mL/L effectively alleviated oxidative stress, maintained ROS homeostasis, and delayed enzymatic browning, demonstrating its potential as a practical preservation strategy to improve postharvest quality and extend the shelf life of fresh-cut stem lettuce during cold storage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46703-7.

Introduction

Stem lettuce (Lactuca sativa L. var. angustana), commonly known as “Chinese lettuce” or “asparagus lettuce,” is a widely consumed vegetable in many countries. It is highly valued for its rich nutritional profile, including abundant minerals, plant polysaccharides, and various bioactive compounds1,2. China is the largest producer and consumer of stem lettuce, accounting for approximately half of the world’s total lettuce production and cultivated land area3. Data from the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People’s Republic of China (2021) indicated that the annual production of stem lettuce exceeds 3 million tons, cultivated over 150,000 hectares, underscoring its significant role in the domestic vegetable industry3,4. In recent years, stem lettuce has attracted increased attention as a fresh-cut product, offering the advantages of healthfulness, convenience, and better preservation of nutrients5. Fresh-cut stem lettuce is widely consumed around the world, with regional preferences and culinary applications varying by culture. However, the surface of stem lettuce undergoes oxidative browning, transitioning from pale green or white to a reddish-brown hue during storage after cutting. This discoloration accelerates quality deterioration, reduces shelf life, and poses challenges for commercial distribution6. A variety of physical and chemical preservation technologies have been explored to maintain the quality of fresh-cut stem lettuce. The application of radio frequency heating, UV-C irradiation, chlorine dioxide, and natural compounds like allicin has been shown to be effective in reducing browning and microbial load4–7. However, their practical applications remain limited due to concerns such as high implementation costs, safety risks, and operational complexity. Therefore, the development of safe, simple, and efficient technologies for browning inhibition remains a critical priority in the fresh-cut produce industry, especially for extending the shelf life and maintaining the quality of fresh-cut stem lettuce8.

Ethanol (C2H5OH), a secondary metabolite naturally produced in plants and classified as Generally Recognized as Safe, has been widely used as a food-grade agent for postharvest preservation of horticultural crops9. Ethanol treatment can maintain cell membrane integrity10, inhibit respiration and ethylene synthesis11,12, delay browning13, inhibit microbial proliferation14, maintain high quality of fresh-cut fruits and vegetables, and extend the storage period15. Previous studies had demonstrated that ethanol application, either through immersion in ethanol solutions or via ethanol vapor, effectively reduces nutrient loss and microbial growth while delaying browning and senescence in vegetable products16. A few studies have investigated the effects of ethanol vapor treatment or ethanol soaking on various vegetables and fruits. Ji et al.11, reported that ethanol vapor treatment significantly delayed the softening and senescence of blueberries and improved their flavor attributes. In fresh-cut lotus root, ethanol inhibited browning by downregulating the enzyme activities and gene expression, thereby reducing the accumulation of total phenolics and soluble quinones17. Similarly, ethanol vapor decreased respiration rate and ethylene production in persimmons, contributing to delayed ripening and extended postharvest life18. In kiwifruit, ethanol effectively preserved fruit firmness, delayed vitamin C and chlorophyll degradation, and suppressed pericarp browning12. Ethanol has been reported to inhibit browning or improve storage quality in fresh-cut products, such as yam19, eggplant16, sugarcane20, and banana21, as well as in postharvest whole produce, including potato22, broccoli23, tomato24, and cherry tomatoes (Liu et al.25,. Taken together, ethanol vapor can potentially be applied as a safe and effective storage treatment in a variety of fruits and vegetables.

Recent studies have shown that ethanol vapor can delay enzymatic browning and enhance antioxidant defense by regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism. Moderate ethanol concentrations help maintain redox balance, alleviate oxidative stress, and preserve tissue color during storage. These dual anti-browning and antioxidant effects highlight the potential of ethanol vapor as an efficient preservation strategy for fresh-cut stem lettuce. However, research on the use of ethanol vapor to reduce browning, inhibit reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and enhance antioxidant defense in fresh-cut stem lettuce remains limited. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the effects of ethanol vapor treatment on the browning, ROS accumulation, and antioxidant activity of fresh-cut stem lettuce during storage and explore the underlying physiological and biochemical mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Preparation of stem lettuce samples

Fresh stem lettuce was purchased from a local market in Macau, China. The samples were produced in Guangdong Province, China, and transported to the laboratory within two hours under refrigerated conditions (4 °C). Samples with uniform size, color, and maturity, and free from mechanical damage, were selected for subsequent experiments. Fresh stem lettuce was thoroughly washed with tap water, the outermost leaves were removed, and the stems were disinfected with a 200 µL/L sodium hypochlorite solution for 5 min. Subsequently, the stems were peeled with a stainless-steel knife. The peeled stems were then rinsed with pre-chilled tap water, disinfected with a 50 µL/L sodium hypochlorite solution for 3 min, drained, and sliced into 5 mm thick pieces using a stainless-steel knife3. The slices were gently drained to eliminate surface water before ethanol vapor treatment.

Ethanol vapor treatment

Ethanol (≥ 99.7% purity), which is commonly used in the food industry due to its antimicrobial properties and GRAS status, was purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and used for ethanol vapor treatment. The experimental procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1. The vaporization device consisted of a plastic box (11.5 × 9.5 × 6.2 cm, Hengshui Changhui Trading Co., Ltd., Hengshui, China), with a sterile filter paper containing the ethanol affixed to the top inside the box. Fresh-cut stem lettuce slices (4 slices per box) were randomly selected and evenly placed in polypropylene boxes26. The slices were exposed to ethanol vapor at different concentrations (0, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L) for 22 h at 4 °C27. The ethanol concentrations were selected based on previous studies and preliminary experiments. The exposure duration (22 h) allowed sufficient volatilization and distribution of ethanol vapor within the sealed containers. The ethanol concentration (mL/L) was calculated as the ratio of the added ethanol volume to the internal volume of the sealed container. The samples in each treatment group were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, compressed into a single mixed pellet, and stored in an ultra-low-temperature freezer at −80 °C (Thermoscientific Co., Ltd., USA) until biochemical analysis. At five designated storage time points (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 days), samples were collected to assess appearance, browning-related enzymes activities, ROS content, antioxidant enzymes activities, and antioxidant capacities. Each treatment contained three repeats.

Overall visual quality evaluation and colour parameters

Fresh-cut stem lettuce slices (4 slices per box) were treated using the same ethanol vapor method as described for the comprehensive analysis of the browning index. Samples were randomly collected during storage times at 4 °C. Three boxes (serving as biological replicates) were collected for visual and subjective evaluation of the colour parameters. The surface of fresh-cut stem lettuces was measured using a colorimeter (CR-400, Minolta Co., Osaka, Japan) equipped with a CIE standard illuminant C and an 8 mm diameter measurement aperture, three samples from each group were chosen to measure the surface color, taking an average of three surface measurements per sample28. The device was calibrated using a standard white plate (L* = 0.00, a* = 0.00, b* = 0.00). The L* value reflects the lightness of the samples, with higher values indicating lighter colors.

Browning-related enzymes activity assay

The activities of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and peroxidase (POD) were determined using commercial plant assay kits (Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China)28. For each assay, 0.5 g of stem lettuce slices was homogenized in 1.0 mL of extraction buffer provided in the kits, using a pre-chilled mortar and pestle. All enzyme extraction procedures were performed on ice to maintain enzyme stability. The homogenate was centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. Reagents were added to microcentrifuge tubes according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction mixtures were incubated at the specified temperature and duration prior to absorbance measurement. POD activity was measured by recording absorbance at 470 nm after incubating the mixture at 25 °C for 1 min. PPO activity was determined at 525 nm following a 10 min reaction at 25 °C. PAL activity was measured at 290 nm after incubation at 30 °C for 30 min using a spectrophotometer (UV-2600i, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Enzyme activities were expressed as U/g, where one unit of PAL activity (U) was defined as 0.01 × ΔA290 nm per minute, one unit of PPO activity (U) was defined as 0.01 × ΔA525 nm per minute, and one unit of POD activity (U) was defined as 0.01 × ΔA470 nm per minute.

O₂⁻· and H₂O₂ content assay

Superoxide anion radicals (O₂⁻·) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) content were measured using the following assay kits (Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China)28. Frozen stem lettuce samples (0.5 g) in triplicate were put with 1 mL extract liquid and ground in the mortar among cooling and then centrifuged at 10,000 × g at 4 °C for 10 min and the supernatant were collected. Absorbance was measured at 530 nm and 415 nm. The O₂⁻· content was expressed as a percentage (%), while the H₂O₂ content was expressed in µmol per gram (µmol/g).

Antioxidant enzymes activity assay

The ascorbate peroxidase (APX), glutathione reductase (GR), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) enzyme extractions were performed according to the instructions on the plant kits (Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). Frozen stem lettuce samples (0.5 g), in triplicate, were homogenized with 1.0 mL of extraction solution in a chilled mortar. The homogenates were centrifuged according to the specific requirements for each enzyme: at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C for SOD and CAT, 13,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C for APX, and 8000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C for GR. The resulting supernatants were collected for analysis. Absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer according to the instructions provided in each kit, and the enzyme activities were calculated based on the corresponding standard operating procedures to ensure reproducibility. The activity of APX, GR, SOD, and CAT were determined by measuring the absorbance at 290, 340, 560, and 240 nm, respectively. Antioxidant enzyme activities were expressed as U/g, where UAPX = 0.01 × ΔA290 nm per min, UGR = 0.01 × ΔA340 nm per min, USOD = 0.01 × ΔA560 nm per min, and UCAT = 0.01 × ΔA240 nm per min.

Antioxidant capacity assay

The DPPH radical scavenging capacity, ABTS radical scavenging capacity, and iron-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) were determined according to the instructions provided with the plant assay kits (Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China). A 0.5 g samples of fresh-cut stem lettuce samples were mixed with 1.0 mL of the extraction reagent supplied in the kit. The samples were ground thoroughly in a mortar placed on ice to preserve antioxidant activity during homogenization. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 8,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for analysis. The order and volume of reagent additions strictly adhered to the standard operating procedures provided by the manufacturer to ensure consistency and reproducibility. After incubation for the specified time and at the designated temperature, the absorbance of the reaction mixtures was measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-2600i, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Results were expressed as µmol Trolox equivalents per gram (µmol Trolox/g).

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation of fresh-cut stem lettuce was conducted during storage at 9 days under different ethanol vapor treatments (0, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L). A panel of 20 volunteers (10 females and 10 males, aged 22–45 years) from Macau University of Science and Technology (Macao, China) participated in the evaluation. Samples were presented to panelists in a randomized order and evaluated at room temperature (approximately 25 °C). Evaluators scored color, appearance, odor, texture, and overall acceptability using a 9-point hedonic scale, ranging from 1 (dislike extremely) to 9 (like extremely). The detailed scale was defined as follows: 9 = like extremely; 8 = like very much; 7 = like moderately; 6 = like slightly; 5 = neither like nor dislike; 4 = dislike slightly; 3 = dislike moderately; 2 = dislike very much; and 1 = dislike extremely29,30. The mean score for each attribute was calculated, and samples with scores below 5 were considered unacceptable31,32. Although the storage period lasted 12 days, sensory evaluation was conducted only up to day 9, as samples showed evident quality deterioration thereafter.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software to evaluate the effects of ethanol concentration, storage time, and their interaction. When significant differences were detected, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied to compare mean values among treatments. In addition, regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the trends of selected parameters in response to ethanol concentration and storage time, and the corresponding confidence intervals were calculated. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Appearance and color value

The L* value, representing surface brightness, is a key indicator of visual quality and consumer acceptance in fresh-cut produce33. As shown in Fig. 2A, the L* value exhibited distinct trends among treatments during storage. The control group showed a continuous decline in brightness, whereas ethanol-treated groups maintained higher L* values throughout storage. In particular, the 0.5 mL/L treatment displayed a gradual increase up to day 9, while the 1.0 mL/L and 2.0 mL/L groups showed a slight rise followed by a moderate decline at the end of storage. In the control group, the L* value sharply dropped from 36.5 at day 0 to 20.1 at day 3 and remained at a low level (approximately 20.3) throughout storage. On the contrary, ethanol vapor treatments significantly delayed the reduction in L* value (p < 0.05). Specifically, the 1.0 mL/L ethanol vapor treatment consistently maintained the highest L* value, ranging from 28.5 to 30.2 during days 3–12, which was approximately 1.5 times higher compared with the control at corresponding time points; however, it showed a noticeable decline on day 12. In comparison, the 0.5 mL/L treatment exhibited a gradual increase in L* value during storage, reaching the highest level (approximately 30.0) on day 12. The qualitative appearance shown in Fig. 2B. On day 0, all treatments displayed a uniform fresh green color with high brightness. By day 12, the control group exhibited severe browning and surface darkening; in contrast, the 0.5 mL/L treatment retained a bright, fresh green appearance with minimal browning. Additionally, the 1.0 mL/L group showed better color retention compared with the control, although localized discoloration did appear, whereas the 2.0 mL/L group exhibited visible signs of surface deterioration, characterized by the presence of white mucilaginous substances, which were presumed to be mold growth. Therefore, ethanol vapor at 0.5 mL/L was the most effective treatment in maintaining the surface color of fresh-cut stem lettuce and reducing the occurrence of browning throughout the storage period.

Browning-related enzyme activity

Peroxidase (POD) is a typical enzyme associated with browning34. As shown in Fig. 3A, POD activity was consistently higher in the control group than in the ethanol-treated groups (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L) after the day 0. On day 3, POD activity in the control group increased to 990 ± 30 U/g, which was significantly higher than that in the ethanol-treated groups (p < 0.05). However, POD activity in the control group continued to rise and reached the highest level by day 9. In the later stages of storage (9–12 days), POD activity showed a general declining trend in both the control and treatment groups. By the end of the storage period, POD activity in the control group (776 ± 30 U/g) was 1.43 times that of the 2.0 mL/L ethanol-treated group (542.6 ± 25 U/g). The results indicated that ethanol treatments significantly reduced POD activity, effectively inhibiting browning in fresh-cut stem lettuce.

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO), an enzyme containing copper as a cofactor, is responsible for catalyzing the oxidation of monophenols and diphenols to quinones, thereby contributing to enzymatic browning35. As shown in Fig. 3B, PPO activity in the treatment groups showed an overall decreasing trend, followed by an increase during storage. At the early stage of storage (day 0), all ethanol-treated groups showed higher PPO activity compared with the control, with the 1.0 mL/L group exhibiting the highest level (39.2 U/g). On day 3, the PPO activity in the control group reached 19.7 U/g, which was significantly higher (p < 0.05) compared with the 0.5 mL/L (14.7 U/g), 1.0 mL/L (9.2 U/g), and 2.0 mL/L (16.0 U/g) ethanol-treated groups. From days 6–12, PPO activity in the 1.0 mL/L treatment group remained the highest among all the treatments, although the trend was declining, and that of the 0.5 mL/L and 2.0 mL/L treatment groups was rising. This suggested that ethanol vapor treatments at different concentrations were effective in suppressing PPO activity at an early stage, potentially reducing enzymatic browning in fresh-cut stem lettuce.

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), a crucial enzyme involved in the phenylpropanoid metabolism, followed a downward trend at the early stage of storage, followed by an upward trend at later stages36. In the early stage (0–3 days), PAL activity was significantly suppressed by ethanol vapor treatments at all concentrations (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L), with values significantly lower compared with the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). Ethanol vapor effectively inhibited PAL activity, which in turn possibly reduced the synthesis of phenolic compounds, thereby helping in delaying the browning of fresh-cut stem lettuce. However, the inhibitory effect was mainly observed during the early storage period, since PAL activity began to rise after day 6. Notably, on day 6, the 1.0 mL/L ethanol vapor treatment exhibited the highest PAL activity while the 2.0 mL/L group maintained the lowest, indicating a concentration-dependent regulatory effect of ethanol at this stage. On day 9, PAL activity reached its peak across all groups, with the 0.5 mL/L treatment showing significantly higher activity than other treatments. In the later stages of storage (9–12 days), the 0.5 mL/L treatment group showed enhanced PAL activity, which might have contributed to the improvement of antioxidant capacity.

Reactive oxygen species content

O2⁻· is a major reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced during oxidative stress, and its scavenging is a key indicator of the antioxidant status in biological systems37. As shown in Fig. 4A, the O2⁻· scavenging content in fresh-cut stem lettuce exhibited periodic fluctuations during storage. On day 3, all ethanol-treated groups showed significantly lower O2⁻· scavenging content compared with the control group (p < 0.05). However, by day 6, the O2⁻· scavenging content of the 0.5 mL/L ethanol-treated group (52.25%) was significantly higher compared with the control group (47.58%) (p < 0.05), representing approximately a 1.1-fold increase over the control group. On day 9, the O2⁻· scavenging content of the 2.0 mL/L ethanol-treated group was the lowest (27.42%), suggesting a greater accumulation of ROS due to impaired antioxidant defense. The results indicated that appropriate ethanol concentrations, such as 0.5 mL/L, could enhance O2⁻· scavenging content and delay oxidative damage. Although the O₂⁻· scavenging content in the 2.0 mL/L ethanol group increased at the later stage of storage (day 12), this may represent a delayed antioxidant response to prior oxidative stress.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is a compound containing oxygen and hydrogen, and belongs to the ROS group38. While it functions as an important signaling molecule and reaction intermediate in biological systems, it is a potential oxidizing agent capable of inducing oxidative stress37. As shown in Fig. 4B, the H2O2 content in fresh-cut stem lettuce varied throughout the storage period across all treatment groups. Ethanol vapor treatment altered the dynamic changes in H2O2 levels, with different concentrations displaying distinct temporal patterns. On day 3, all ethanol-treated groups exhibited significantly higher H2O2 content compared with the control group (0.18 µmol/g), suggesting that ethanol might initially induce oxidative stress by triggering ROS production as part of the plant’s defense response. By day 6, the H2O2 level in the 2.0 mL/L treatment group was significantly lower compared with the control and other ethanol-treated groups, indicating a possible enhancement of the antioxidant defense system or activation of ROS-scavenging enzymes under high ethanol exposure. Beginning on day 6, the 1.0 mL/L ethanol treatment showed a remarkable increase in H2O2 levels, resulting in a distinct divergence from other groups, which stabilized by day 9. This suggested a concentration-dependent modulation of oxidative stress and antioxidant response. A clear concentration-dependent trend was no longer evident among the ethanol-treated groups by day 12, indicating that ROS metabolism may be regulated in a complex and dynamic manner over time.

Antioxidant enzyme activity

In this study, we evaluated the activities of antioxidant-related enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, (SOD), catalase (CAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and glutathione reductase (GR). SOD, a key antioxidant enzyme, exhibited significant changes during storage, with activity increasing in the control group and varying depending on ethanol concentration (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L). As shown in Fig. 5A, the SOD activity in the control group increased significantly from an initial level of 20 ± 2 U/g to 60 ± 5 U/g on day 12, which was approximately three times higher than the values observed in the ethanol-treated groups. In contrast, the 0.5 mL/L ethanol-treated group consistently maintained low SOD activity (ranging from 20 ± 3 U/g to 40 ± 4 U/g), characterized by minimal fluctuations and a gradual upward trend over time. On day 3, SOD activity exhibited a clear concentration-dependent decline, with higher ethanol concentrations resulting in lower activity levels. By day 6, and particularly on day 9, the high-concentration ethanol treatments (1.0 and 2.0 mL/L) led to more pronounced reductions in SOD activity. Among them, the 2.0 mL/L treatment group showed a significant decrease on day 9, with activity remarkably lower compared with the other treatment groups and the control. By the last day of storage, the activity in the control group increased sharply while that in the treatment groups (especially 1.0 mL/L and 2.0 mL/L) showed significant inhibition of the increase, suggesting a more prominent potential to retard oxidative reactions.

CAT is a key antioxidant enzyme that decomposes hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, protecting cells from oxidative damage39. In this study, CAT activity showed a general trend of initial decline followed by an increase during the storage period, with fluctuations observed across all groups (Fig. 5B). The control group exhibited consistently lower CAT activity compared with the ethanol-treated groups (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L). In the 0.5 mL/L treatment group, CAT activity was the highest on day 0 (1.8 ± 0.1 U/g), followed by a decline accompanied by moderate fluctuations during storage. By day 12, activity in the 0.5 mL/L treatment group had decreased significantly to 0.8 ± 0.1 U/g. Among the treated groups, the 2.0 mL/L group maintained the most stable and the lowest CAT activity throughout the storage period. On day 12, the CAT activity in the 2.0 mL/L group was 0.6 ± 0.1 U/g, significantly lower compared with the control and other treated groups.

APX and GR are two key enzymes in the ascorbate-glutathione (AsA-GSH) cycle, which plays a critical role in scavenging ROS and maintaining redox homeostasis in plant cells40. In this study, APX activity increased during the early storage period (0–3 days), gradually decreasing thereafter until day 12 (Fig. 5C). The initial increase suggested an active ROS-scavenging response triggered by the mechanical wounding stress arising from fresh cutting. At the beginning of storage (day 0), the control group exhibited lower APX activity than all ethanol-treated groups. However, the latter groups (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L), especially the 2.0 mL/L group, exhibited lower APX activity compared with the control group from day 3 to day 12. On day 3, the APX activity in the 0.5 mL/L ethanol treatment group was 550 ± 15 U/g, significantly lower than that in the control group (757 ± 18 U/g), corresponding to approximately 72% of the control level. This suppression of APX activity could be attributed to ethanol vapor inducing modulation of metabolism, which potentially reduced ROS accumulation and lowered the enzymatic demand.

In contrast, GR activity, which is responsible for regenerating reduced glutathione (GSH) from its oxidized form (GSSG), exhibited a different response pattern under ethanol treatment (Fig. 5D)41. On day 0, GR activity levels across all groups were approximately 60 ± 5 U/g, indicating that ethanol had no immediate effect on baseline GR activity. By day 3, GR activity had declined to approximately 20 ± 8 U/g in the ethanol-treated groups, although the reduction was not statistically significant compared with the control, suggesting a transient downregulation of GR activity. Interestingly, during the mid to late storage stages (days 6 and 9), GR activity showed a slight rebound in all ethanol-treated groups, with the 2.0 mL/L treatment exhibiting relatively higher activity. This increase could reflect an adaptive or compensatory activation of the AsA-GSH cycle in response to prolonged exposure to mild oxidative stress or ethanol-induced metabolic signals. Overall, the effect of ethanol on GR activity was mild, and the differences across the various concentration groups were small, with no significant deviation from the control group.

Antioxidant capacity

The effect of different ethanol concentrations on the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) values of fresh-cut stem lettuce is shown in Fig. 6A. The FRAP values increased during the early storage period and then declined, showing a general trend of initial rise followed by a decrease, with some fluctuations during storage. Ethanol vapor treatment enhanced the antioxidant activity of stem lettuce. At the initial stage of storage (day 0), the FRAP values of the 2.0 mL/L ethanol-treated groups were significantly higher than those of the control group. On day 3, the FRAP value of the control group increased significantly to 0.150 µmol Trolox/g, approximately three times its initial value. Over days 6–12, the FRAP values of the 0.5 mL/L and 1.0 mL/L treatment groups remained consistently higher than those of the control group, with the 0.5 mL/L group reaching a peak value of 0.251 µmol Trolox/g on day 12, approximately 1.8 times that of the control group (0.139 µmol Trolox/g, p < 0.05). In contrast, the 2.0 mL/L treatment group did not exhibit a noticeable enhancement in FRAP values, suggesting potential adverse effects or limitations associated with higher ethanol concentrations.

During the storage period, the DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacity assays showed fluctuating trends (Fig. 6B and C). As shown in Fig. 6B, the DPPH radical scavenging capacity generally increased during the early to mid-storage period and declined in the later stages. Over days 3–6, the control group exhibited higher DPPH scavenging activity than all the ethanol-treated groups, suggesting a temporary suppression of antioxidant activity by ethanol treatment. However, the 0.5 mL/L treatment group achieved the highest DPPH value (0.33 µmol Trolox/g, p < 0.05) on day 9, surpassing the control and other treatments. By day 12, the DPPH scavenging capacity in 0.5 mL/L treatment group was approximately twice that of the control, indicating that ethanol vapor effectively preserved the antioxidant potential during storage.

The ABTS radical scavenging capacity generally exhibited an overall trend of initial increase and then decrease during the storage period (Fig. 6C). Notably, the 1.0 mL/L treatment group showed a significantly lower ABTS radical scavenging activity compared with the control group on day 0, with a value of approximately 0.32 µmol Trolox/g (p < 0.05). However, by day 6, the 1.0 mL/L group exhibited significantly higher ABTS scavenging capacity compared with both the control and other treatment groups. At the end of storage (day 12), the 1.0 mL/L treatment maintained an ABTS capacity approximately 1.5 times greater compared with the control group, demonstrating that ethanol vapor exposure contributed to the enhancement and stabilization of antioxidant defense in fresh-cut stem lettuce over time.

Sensory analysis

Fresh-cut stem lettuce was subjected to sensory evaluation after 9 days of storage (Fig. 7). The control group exhibited the lowest sensory scores across all evaluated attributes, with overall acceptability decreasing to approximately 3, indicating severe quality deterioration. In contrast, ethanol vapor treatments significantly improved sensory quality. Among the treatments, the 0.5 mL/L group showed the highest sensory scores, with values reaching approximately 6–7 for colour, appearance, and overall acceptability, corresponding to ratings between “like slightly” and “like moderately”. The 1.0 mL/L treatment maintained moderate scores (approximately 4–6), which were higher than those of the control but slightly lower than those of the 0.5 mL/L group. However, the 2.0 mL/L treatment showed a decline in some sensory attributes, particularly in odour and overall acceptability (approximately 3–4), suggesting possible negative effects of excessive ethanol exposure. For individual attributes, ethanol-treated samples generally maintained better colour (approximately 5–7) and texture (approximately 4–6) compared with the control (approximately 2–3). The control group showed the lowest scores in odour and overall acceptability, reflecting accelerated senescence and quality loss. Overall, moderate ethanol concentrations (especially 0.5 mL/L) were most effective in preserving sensory quality, whereas excessive ethanol (2.0 mL/L) partially reduced consumer acceptability.

Discussion

Effect of ethanol vapor on the appearance and browning-related enzyme activity

Fresh-cut processing of stem lettuce typically induces mechanical damage and oxidative stress, accelerating enzymatic browning and quality deterioration in stem lettuce42. In this study, changes in color parameters (L*) showed that ethanol vapor treatment, especially at 0.5 mL/L, effectively preserved the surface brightness (L*) during storage. Moreover, ethanol vapor treatment significantly improved the visual quality and delayed enzymatic browning by regulating the activities of key browning-related enzymes in fresh-cut stem lettuce. Previous research had reported the positive effects of ethanol on color maintenance and delayed browning of various fruits and vegetables43. For example, de França et al., found that ethanol treatment significantly reduced fruit decay in bananas; 3 days after storage, ethanol-treated bananas maintained a lower decay index compared with the control group until day 12, when treated fruits showed a decay index of 26 compared with 43 in the control. The study suggested that ethanol inhibited pathogen activity, thereby reducing browning and delaying senescence of the banana peel. Similarly, Romanazzi et al., reported that ethanol treatment inhibited rot incidence in peaches and table grapes44. Our study offered novel insights by applying ethanol vapor treatment to fresh-cut stem lettuce that has received very little attention in this regard till date. Additionally, while earlier studies focused on relatively short storage durations, such as 5–8 days for bananas and table grapes43,44, 7 days for fresh-cut strawberries26, and 8 days for fresh-cut eggplant16, our study assessed a longer shelf-life period of approximately 12 days, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of browning control under commercial storage conditions. These differences in commodity type and storage duration suggested that the browning-inhibitory mechanisms of ethanol vapor may vary across commodities and storage contexts, potentially involving variations in tissue structure, physiological responses, and browning-related enzymatic activities45. Since enzymatic browning is closely associated with oxidative stress, the observed color preservation may also be attributed to the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism and antioxidant enzyme activities induced by ethanol vapor. Therefore, to understand the mechanisms better, we further evaluated the effects of ethanol vapor treatments on the activities of key enzymes associated with enzymatic browning.

As shown in Fig. 7, the occurrence of enzymatic browning is a kind of protective and repair response to wounding stress, caused by the enzymatic reaction in which phenolic substances are oxidized to colored quinone in the presence of oxygen and PPO (Liu et al.46,. POD can affect the enzymatic browning processes of phenolic compounds using H2O2 as a receptor47. PAL indirectly influences the enzymatic browning process by regulating phenolic substance production in the phenylalanine pathway48. The activities of POD, PPO, and PAL were all downregulated in the 0.5 mL/L treatment group on day 3. This coincided with the critical period of browning initiation observed in the control group. POD activity exhibited an increasing trend during the early storage period in the control, followed by a decline, whereas the 0.5 mL/L treatment group effectively suppressed the elevation. This indicated that ethanol vapor possibly delayed browning by modulating the oxidative enzyme activity at early stages. Similarly, PPO activity in the treatment group was significantly lower compared with the control on day 3, aligning with the initial onset of visible browning. Further, PAL activity showed a biphasic pattern. It was significantly inhibited in the early stage under 0.5 mL/L treatment, which might have reduced the synthesis of browning precursors. During the later stage of storage, PAL activity increased gradually, possibly reflecting a delayed stress response that enhanced the antioxidant capacity of the tissue.

The results suggested that ethanol vapor treatment at 0.5 mL/L could delay enzymatic browning by modulating the activities of POD, PPO, and PAL. Similar inhibitory effects of ethanol treatment on POD activity have been reported in other fresh-cut vegetables and fruits as well. For instance, ethanol treatment was found to reduce POD activity in sugarcane and lotus root, resulting in delayed browning17,20, while PPO activity was suppressed in fresh-cut apples and mushrooms following ethanol exposure, alleviating oxidative discoloration during early storage49. Collectively, the consistency of these findings across diverse horticultural products reinforced the practical value of ethanol vapor treatment in maintaining the shelf life and visual quality of fresh-cut stem lettuce.

Effect of ethanol vapor on reactive oxygen species metabolism

O2⁻· is a common ROS in plants, and its elevated levels indicate oxidative stress, which negatively impacts postharvest quality. In this study, ethanol treatment enhanced O2⁻· scavenging in the early stage of storage, helping delay oxidative damage. Interestingly, transient increases in O2⁻· under moderate ethanol concentrations could reflect a dynamic ROS response that activates antioxidant defenses. In contrast, the 2.0 mL/L ethanol treatment showed reduced scavenging content at later stages, suggesting weakened antioxidant defense and redox imbalance. Moderate concentrations (0.5–1.0.5.0 mL/L) maintained ROS scavenging optimally, contributing to quality preservation. Similar concentration-dependent effects have previously been reported in fresh-cut broccoli, apples, and strawberries, where moderate ethanol reduced ROS and delayed senescence while excessive ethanol caused oxidative imbalance and tissue deterioration26,50,51.

H2O2 functions both as an oxidative stress indicator and a signaling molecule. Elevated H2O2levels in early storage could activate antioxidant enzymes, initiating adaptive responses to oxidative stress. The transient increase in H₂O₂ observed during the early stage of storage may also be associated with the combined effects of mechanical wounding and ethanol-induced metabolic responses. Fresh-cut processing disrupts cellular compartmentalization, which can temporarily enhance ROS generation as part of the wound-response signaling network. In addition, moderate ethanol exposure may stimulate cellular metabolism and trigger stress-related signaling pathways, resulting in a short-term accumulation of H₂O₂. This transient ROS burst is often considered a signaling event that activates antioxidant defense mechanisms, including the upregulation of ROS-scavenging enzymes, thereby helping plant tissues restore redox homeostasis during subsequent storage. For instance, Li et al.26, reported that ethanol vapor treatment in fresh-cut strawberries caused a temporary rise in H2O2 that stimulated antioxidant enzyme activities, contributing to delayed senescence. Conversely, higher ethanol concentrations effectively suppressed H2O2 accumulation during the later stages of storage, reducing oxidative damage and prolonging shelf life, as observed in studies on fresh-cut apples52 and spinach53. The findings collectively suggested that the effect of ethanol on H2O2 and ROS metabolism is dose-dependent, balancing between stress signaling and oxidative damage mitigation. Nonetheless, since ROS signaling also participates in stress adaptation, excessive suppression of ROS by high ethanol levels might impair normal defense signaling and wound-healing responses in plant tissues, potentially leading to metabolic imbalance during prolonged storage. Moderate ethanol concentrations, particularly 0.5 mL/L, appeared to provide an optimal balance between stress signaling and antioxidant activation, thereby enhancing ROS scavenging and delaying oxidative damage. In contrast, excessive ethanol exposure (e.g., 2.0 mL/L) may disrupt cellular metabolism and membrane integrity, leading to redox imbalance and weakened antioxidant defenses. High ethanol levels can also cause physiological stress or slight phytotoxicity in plant tissues, which may counteract the protective effects observed at moderate concentrations. Therefore, an appropriate ethanol concentration is critical to achieve the desired preservation effect without inducing additional metabolic stress.

Effect of ethanol vapor on antioxidant enzyme activity

We found that different ethanol concentrations influenced the activities of key antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT, APX, and GR, significantly during storage. The findings highlighted the role of ethanol in modulating oxidative stress responses in fresh-cut stem lettuce and suggested its potential for maintaining postharvest quality.

In this study, the ethanol-treated group exhibited a moderate increase in SOD activity and a stable CAT activity profile. This suggested that ethanol reduced oxidative stress, lowering the need for antioxidant enzyme upregulation. Similar findings have been reported in fresh-cut cucumber and strawberry as well, where ethanol treatment maintained higher CAT and SOD stability under stress conditions26,28. Since SOD and CAT serve as key enzymes in the detoxification of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, respectively, their stabilization indicated that ethanol could possibly alleviate postharvest oxidative stress by limiting ROS production and preserving cellular homeostasis.

Ethanol vapor treatment seemed to alleviate oxidative stress and delay quality deterioration in fresh-cut stem lettuce by modulating the activities of key enzymes in the AsA-GSH cycle, particularly APX and GR. The early increase in APX activity likely reflected a typical stress response to mechanical injury from fresh-cut processing. Notably, the reduction in APX activity observed in the ethanol-treated group, particularly at 0.5 mL/L, indicated a lower level of oxidative stress, which in turn reduced the need for H2O2 scavenging by APX. This observation was consistent with previous findings in fresh-cut spinach, where ethanol treatment was shown to suppress APX activity and delay senescence by mitigating ROS accumulation53. The ability of ethanol to modulate APX activity could, therefore, be a key mechanism contributing to its antioxidative and quality-preserving effects during storage. GR, another critical enzyme in the AsA-GSH cycle, is responsible for regenerating reduced GSH from its oxidized form GSSG, thereby maintaining a sufficient GSH pool to support antioxidant defense. Although ethanol treatment did not significantly influence GR activity in the beginning of storage, a gradual decline was observed in all groups as storage progressed. This possibly suggested that the antioxidant effect of ethanol, particularly regarding the AsA-GSH cycle, was more pronounced in the early stages of storage and could diminish over time. Similar findings were reported by Xu et al.54,, who observed limited changes in GR activity in ethanol-treated fresh-cut Pakchoi, indicating that GR could be less responsive to ethanol vapor than other enzymes such as APX or CAT54. It should be noted that the enzymatic antioxidant responses to ethanol vapor can differ among species and storage environments. For example, variations in temperature, humidity, or packaging permeability may influence ethanol diffusion and thereby alter the activation thresholds of antioxidant enzymes. This differential response could be due to the relatively stable cellular demand for glutathione recycling under mild oxidative conditions, where ethanol effectively suppressed ROS production and reduced the necessity for GR activation.

Effect of ethanol vapor on antioxidant capacity

This study found that ethanol treatment significantly enhanced the antioxidant capacity of stem lettuce. The effect might result from ethanol reducing oxidative stress or enhancing the accumulation of antioxidant compounds, thereby improving the tissue antioxidant capacity. During storage, the FRAP value increased significantly over time, and treatment with 0.5 mL/L ethanol resulted in a greater increase compared with other concentrations. This suggested that the moderate ethanol concentration of 0.5 mL/L could activate antioxidant mechanisms in the plant, such as enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity or promoting the synthesis of relevant secondary metabolites. Additionally, although the DPPH and ABTS free radical scavenging activities fluctuated, the ethanol-treated groups consistently exhibited significantly higher scavenging activity compared with the control group, further confirming the effectiveness of ethanol treatment in boosting antioxidant activity. Similar findings have been observed in other studies as well. For example, Zhang et al., (2020) reported that ethanol treatment significantly improved the antioxidant capacity of broccoli, increasing the free radical scavenging activity by approximately 25% and total antioxidant capacity by 18%55. Liu et al., (2021) found that ethanol treatment enhanced antioxidant phenolic compound content by 22% and free radical scavenging activity by 30% in raspberries. The results collectively highlighted the broad applicability and potential value of ethanol treatment in enhancing antioxidant activity. In conclusion, ethanol treatment combined with low-temperature storage (4 °C) would be an effective strategy to enhance the antioxidant capacity of stem lettuce.

Cluster analysis of antioxidant and enzymatic activities

Changes in enzyme activity and antioxidant capacity are the key indicators of quality deterioration and storage performance in fresh-cut stem lettuce3. These biochemical parameters collectively reflect the degree of tissue oxidation, membrane integrity, and defense capability against postharvest stress. Pearson correlation analysis revealed significant interactions among antioxidant capacities, ROS, and enzymatic activities in fresh-cut stem lettuce under ethanol vapor treatments. Notably, FRAP, DPPH, and ABTS exhibited strong positive correlations (p < 0.05), indicating consistency across antioxidant assays. PAL activity also showed significant positive correlations with the antioxidant indices, suggesting its involvement in enhancing antioxidant potential, likely through phenolic compound biosynthesis. Conversely, PPO and POD activities were positively correlated with H2O2 (p < 0.05), highlighting their potential role in ROS accumulation and enzymatic browning. Strong positive correlations were observed between H2O2 and O2⁻·, further confirming the interplay between ROS species. Antioxidant enzymes, such as SOD, CAT, APX, and GR, were significantly correlated with one another, indicating a coordinated defense mechanism against oxidative stress. Interestingly, FRAP was negatively correlated with CAT and GR activities, possibly reflecting a compensatory relationship between non-enzymatic and enzymatic antioxidant pathways. The findings suggested that ethanol vapor treatments modulated both enzymatic browning and antioxidant defense systems, with PAL, PPO, and ROS-scavenging enzymes playing pivotal roles in preserving the quality of fresh-cut stem lettuce during storage. While this study provides valuable insights into the effects of ethanol vapor on enzymatic browning and antioxidant defenses in fresh-cut stem lettuce, further research is needed to explore a wider range of conditions, biochemical markers, and sensory evaluations to fully establish practical applications.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of different concentrations of ethanol vapor treatments (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mL/L) on the storage quality and browning of fresh-cut stem lettuce. Results demonstrated that the 0.5 mL/L treatment group inhibited phenolic oxidase activity effectively, modulated antioxidant enzyme responses, delayed browning, and enhanced antioxidant capacity during storage. Furthermore, the study confirmed that ethanol vapor can significantly slow down enzymatic browning in fresh-cut stem lettuce and improve postharvest storage quality. Future research can build on these results by applying integrated multi-omics techniques, such as metabolomics and transcriptomics, to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effects of ethanol on browning Fig. 8 and 9.
