Assessment of national reimbursement anticancer medications availability in China: based on a medical institution perspective
Authors: Wenxin Meng, Shudi Yang, Zhangke Wang, Jinping Xie, Rong Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh clinical value national reimbursement anticancer medications (NRAMs) are pivotal treatments for patients with cancer. However, the availability of NRAMs in medical institutions is unknown. This study aimed to assess the availability of NRAMs in national and provincial medical institutions.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized national health insurance data to access the availability of NRAMs in national and provincial medical institutions. Further statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted in terms of medical institution level and daily cost. Using the Spearman’s rank correlation test (α = 0.05), we calculated the correlation between the availability rates of NRAMs and their negotiation access time, daily cost, per capita disposable income, provincial gross product, and number of policy releases.ResultsOverall, 81 NRAMs, with an average availability rate of approximately 1.01% nationwide, were included. There were significant differences between provinces for each drug, and the availability of NRAMs gradually decreased in tertiary (13.41%), secondary (1.58%), and primary medical institutions (< 0.05%). Differences were also observed in the availability rate of NRAMs in various daily drug cost ranges. Among the factors examined, negotiation access time (r1 = 0.425), daily cost (r2 = − 0.326), per capita disposable income (r3 = 0.645), provincial gross product (r4 = 0.433), and number of policy releases (r5 = 0.461) were all correlated with the availability of NRAMs.ConclusionsThe low availability of NRAMs in national and provincial medical institutions indicates that their willingness to equip NRAMs needs to be improved. All factors examined in this study affected the availability of NRAMs. Our findings can guide policymakers in improving relevant policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19786-y.Keywords: National reimbursement drugs, Anticancer medications, Availability, Medical institutions

Background

In recent years, a steady increase has been observed in the global prevalence and mortality rates of cancer [1]. Since 2010, cancer has consistently been a leading cause of death in China [2], as the country ranked first globally in terms of cancer-related mortality in 2020 [3]. According to the latest estimates, there will be about 4,824,700 new cancer cases and 2,574,200 new cancer deaths in China in 2022. Lung, colorectal, thyroid, liver and stomach cancers were the top five cancer types, accounting for 57.42% of new cancer cases [4]. Although there is increasing interest in the research and development of anticancer medications, a notable challenge is the high cost of these drugs. In 2020, the global expenditure on anticancer medications was approximately $164 billion, and it is projected to increase to $269 billion by 2025 [5]. This imposes a huge financial burden on patients in terms of drug costs and also raises concerns related to drug availability.

To alleviate the financial burden on patients and ensure the availability of medications, China started the government reimbursement negotiation in 2016, and the drugs that were successfully negotiated were included in the National Reimbursement Drug List (NRDL). The key procedures of the negotiation mainly include comprehensive review, price measurement and on-site negotiation. Based on the comprehensive assessment of the effectiveness, safety, economy, innovation, and fairness of the drug, pharmacoeconomics experts and fund calculation experts will respectively carry out the calculation of the economic value of the drug, and ultimately form the “envelope price” held by the experts at the negotiation stage [6]. When assessing the safety, effectiveness and economy of drugs, China has introduced Health Technology Assessment (HTA) methods and evidence in recent years, which can help the government to select more cost-effective drugs [7]. In the on-site negotiation session, the experts representing the National Healthcare Security Administration mainly based on the “envelope price” to negotiate with the enterprise offer, and finally reached a consensus. From 2016 to 2022, there has been an average reduction of over 50% in drug prices [8]. Notably, from 2019 to 2022, anticancer medications were the most negotiated drugs within the NRDL, with the quantity of drugs increasing annually [9]. In 2022, anticancer medications accounted for approximately 21% of newly negotiated drugs [10], covering 55 indications such as non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, etc., of which 24 anticancer medications can treat multiple cancers, which can cover a wide range of cancers in a larger scope (Supplementary Table 1).

Medical institutions also play a crucial role in the success of patients’ access to negotiated drugs, but the government has not made it mandatory for medical institutions to be equipped with national reimbursement anticancer medications (NRAMs). In other words, medical institutions retain autonomy regarding whether to equip with NRAMs. However, under China’s management system, medical institutions not only have to consider controlling the expenditure of the medical insurance fund, but also subject to the performance appraisal of patients’ medication costs [11]. At the same time, the medical insurance access of innovative drugs has achieved remarkable results, and insufficient clinical experience has also become one of the factors preventing medical institutions from equipping NRAMs [12]. Hindered by various factors, medical institutions cannot only consider patients’ treatment needs when developing their anticancer medication supply catalogs. Therefore, the uneven and insufficient deployment of NRAMs among medical institutions have been persistent issues [13–15]. In such circumstances, the availability of NRAMs for patients is inevitably affected.

Considerable amounts of research have been conducted on medical insurance for anticancer medications; however, there is limited research at the national level regarding the availability of these medications in medical institutions [16]. Additionally, several studies have been conducted in specific provinces [17], regions [18, 19], or medical institutions [20], with limited research addressing the nationwide equipment of NRAMs. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the current availability status of NRAMs in medical institutions across China during the 2022 agreement period. It also seeks to explore the influencing factors, providing reference insights for the government to formulate targeted management measures.

Methods

Data source

We used data mainly from the National Health Insurance Service Platform. This database contains large amounts of data related to national reimbursement drugs (NRDs), including 31 provincial-level administrative regions in China (excluding Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan) where NRDs are provided. It also contains data on the classification and code of drugs, as well as the number, type, and level of medical institutions at the national and provincial levels, among others. Other data used in this study, such as the year when NRAMs were first introduced into medical insurance and their prices, the per capita disposable income and provincial gross product, and the issued policy in each province (city), were obtained from yaozhi Network, the National Bureau of Statistics, and the official websites of the Healthcare Security Administration of each province (city), respectively.

Outcome measures

We used the availability of NRAMs as an indicator to assess whether Chinese patients with cancer could use the NRAMs in medical institutions. The formula for measuring each of the indicators is shown below:

Where the drug availability rate specifically refers to the availability of a specific NRAMs in medical institutions. This formula is extended to calculate the availability rate of NRAMs under different dimensions based on the level of medical institutions, provinces, daily treatment cost, etc. In the calculation process, if a NRAMs is not available in a particular level of medical institution or province, it is recorded as 0. The final drug availability rate is taken as the average of the availability rates of NRAMs in the corresponding dimension. Furthermore, according to the WHO evaluation criteria for drug availability, we defined an availability rate of NRAMs < 30% as very low, 30% ≤ drug availability rate < 50% as low, 50% ≤ drug availability rate ≤ 80% as high, and drug availability rates > 80% as very high [21].

Additionally, we queried the prices and specifications of NRAMs to measure the daily cost of drug therapy for cancer patients. In this study, we defined the drug price as its minimum unit package price, which was calculated by dividing the sales price of the drug by the package conversion rate. In cases where different prices were obtained for the same drug, we chose the lowest price of NRAMs for the analysis. The duration of treatment using NRAMs varies, and some drugs can be discontinued during treatment. Therefore, to ensure the accuracy of the measurements, we calculated the average daily cost of the patient’s medication for a treatment course based on the drug manufacturers’ instructions. According to the exchange rate in 2023 (US $1 = 7.12 CNY), treatment costs were converted into and reported in USD.

Statistical analysis

Since the National Health Insurance Service Platform only provides queries and all data cannot be directly downloaded, we obtained the corresponding data according to the research needs without a data cleaning link. According to the research needs, we screened all the anticancer medications in the NRDL in 2023. Based on these drugs, we extracted the name of corresponding equipped medical institutions, and queried the type and level of each of the medical institutions to form the data base of this study.

We first sorted out the year in which NRAMs were first entered into the NRDL to calculate how long a drug has been on the list, and measured the average daily cost for each drug in a treatment cycle. Second, we systematically analyzed the availability rates of the included NRAMs at the national and provincial levels, which were presented in the form of circular bar charts and heatmaps, respectively. And then, the number of primary, secondary, and tertiary medical institutions for each NRAM was summarized to further analyze the availability rate of the drug from the perspective of medical institution level. In addition, for the average daily cost of treatment, we analyzed the availability rate of NRAMs from different cost intervals, the top 10 and bottom 10 NRAMs in terms of national availability rate.

To examine factors that influence medical institutions equipped with NRAMs, we used two-factor correlation analysis. Spearman’s rank correlation test was used to assess the extent to which the availability rate of NRAMs in medical institutions across the country was associated with the year of access to government reimbursement negotiation, the daily cost of drug therapy over a course of treatment, as well as the extent to which a province’s availability rate of NRAMs was associated with its per capita disposable income, provincial gross product, and number of policy releases (test level α = 0.05). Furthermore, the correlation results were visualized and presented in scatter plots. We performed all statistical analyses using Excel 2021 (Microsoft Corp., Washington, USA), SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA), R 4.2.3, and R Studio 2022.07.2 + 576 (R Studio, PBC, Massachusetts, USA). The requirements were waived by the appropriate institutional board as no patient-related data were used in the study.

Results

Information on national reimbursement anticancer medications

As of May 20, 2023, a total of 81 NRAMs had been screened through the database, of which 32 were tablets, 28 were injections, 19 were capsules, 1 was powder and 1 was a bulk dose with an extended-release implant (Supplementary Table 1). Since the first government reimbursement negotiation in 2017, NRAMs with high clinical value have gradually entered the NRDL, with some of NRAMs undergoing renewal negotiations. Among the 81 NRAMs included in this study, 15 drugs whose initial access was in 2022, while the rest were renewal negotiation NRAMs. Among them, 32 NRAMs were initial access before 2020. And there were 16 and 18 NRAMs initially negotiated in 2020 and 2021, respectively (Supplementary Table 2).

Through calculation and analysis, it was found that 69.14% of NRAMs had a daily cost of less than $60. Among them, the daily cost of 45 NRAMs ranged from $20 to $60, while 11 NRAMs had a cost of less than $10. Among these, shenyi capsules, a traditional Chinese medicine, had the lowest daily average drug consumption within one course of treatment, at only $3.47. The number of drugs with a daily cost exceeding $60 was relatively low. Specifically, only 5 NRAMs had a daily cost between $80 and $100, and among the 10 drugs with a daily cost of treatment exceeding $100, Mitoxantrone Hydrochloride Injection for Tracing had the highest cost at $502.99 (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, a series of policy documents have been issued by national and provincial government departments to promote the orderly provision and use of NRAMs (Supplementary Table 4).

Overall national availability

Overall, the availability rate of NRAMs was very low: the average availability of the 81 drugs was approximately 1.01% in medical institutions nationwide (Fig. 1). Only injectable camrelizumab had an availability rate of more than 5%. The availability rates of 29 drugs, including anlotinib hydrochloride capsules, apatinib, and crizotinib capsules, were between 1% and 5%, with an average of approximately 2.07%. The remaining 51 NRAMs had relatively low availability rates of less than 1%. Of these, 29 drugs, including injectable cetuximab, had availability rates between 0.3% and 1%, 22 drugs, including injectable obinutuzumab, had availability rates of less than 0.3%. And lorlatinib tablets were the least equipped nationwide, with only 0.01% of medical institutions equipped with the drug.

Availability of drugs at different provinces

To facilitate the presentation of the results, we selected the top 10 NRAMs in terms of national availability rate to analyze their distribution in each province (Fig. 2, availability rates of all NRAMs in 31 provinces are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1). It can be found that the overall availability rate of NRAMs in medical institutions varied widely among provinces. The average availability rate of 10 NRAMs was the highest in Beijing and Zhejiang, both exceeding 10%; in 26 provinces, including Heilongjiang and Tianjin, the average availability rate was 3.8%. While availability was lowest in the provinces of Tibet, Qinghai and Chongqing, where on average less than 1% of medical institutions in each province were equipped with the above medications. Possibly due to differences in the regional economic development, the availability rate of NRAMs was generally higher in eastern provinces of China, such as Zhejiang, Guangdong, Jiangsu, and Shanghai, than in western provinces of China, such as Qinghai, Gansu, Ningxia, and Tibet.

Availability of drugs at different levels of medical institutions

In China, a total of 7,845 medical institutions have access to 81 different NRAMs, with a low availability rate of approximately 9.80%. To promote a rational division of labor among medical institutions and to improve the efficiency of medical resource utilization, the Chinese Government classified medical institutions into tertiary, secondary, and primary medical institutions based on their level of medical technology, medical equipment, and other factors. The availability rate of NRAMs at different levels of medical institutions is shown in Fig. 3, where drug IDs are ranked in descending order according to the availability rate. Overall, tertiary medical institutions are the mainstay of the provision and use of NRAMs, exhibited the highest availability rates, approximately 13.41%; secondary medical institutions ranked second, at approximately 1.58%; while primary medical institutions reported the lowest rate, falling below 0.05%. Among tertiary medical institutions, only injectable carilizumab and amlotinib hydrochloride capsules had high availability, with their rates exceeding 50%; 8 NRAMs, including crizotinib capsules and apatinib mesylate tablets, rated low in terms of availability, with an average of about 36.14%. The remaining 71 NRAMs were equipped in less than 10% of tertiary medical institutions on average, with very low availability ratings.

Availability rate of NRAMs at different levels of medical institutions across China

Availability of drugs by daily cost ranges

There were differences in the availability rates of 81 NRAMs under different average daily therapeutic cost ranges. As shown in Fig. 4, where the ellipses visualize trends, dispersion, and correlation in the dataset. The 11 NRAMs below $20 had the largest average availability rate of 1.78% compared to other average daily treatment cost ranges, followed by 26 drugs in the $20-$40 range at about 1.10%. In contrast, the 10 NRAMs with average daily costs of treatment above $100 had the lowest average availability rate of 0.51%. In terms of species distribution, among the top 10 NRAMs in terms of national availability rate, 70% of them had a lower average daily consumption of less than $40 during the course of treatment, with the exception of injectable mecapegfilgrastim, which had an average daily cost of more than $100. In contrast, 40% of the NRAMs in the bottom 10 ranked national availability rates had an average daily cost of more than $ 100, and 50% were concentrated in the $40-$80 range.

Distribution of availability of NRAMs in different daily cost ranges

Correlation analysis

The results of the correlation analysis indicated that the earlier an NRAM entered the NRDL, the greater its availability in medical institutions nationwide (r1 = 0.425, p1 < 0.01). Conversely, as the daily cost increased, the availability of NRAMs decreased (r2 = − 0.326, p2 < 0.01). Examining the availability of NRAMs in medical institutions across provinces revealed that the economic development level was one of the influencing factors. With an increase in per capita disposable income and provincial gross product, the availability of NRAMs across provinces improved (r3 = 0.645, p3 < 0.01; r4 = 0.433, p4 < 0.05). Disparities in economic development levels among provinces led to variations in the availability of NRAMs. Additionally, in order to promote the availability of NRAMs, provincial governments issue policies, such as updating the reimbursement drug list and setting payment rates. The results of our analysis revealed that as the number of policies related to NRDs issued by a given province increased, the availability of NRAMs in that province increased (r5 = 0.461, p5 < 0.01) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the current availability rates of NRAMs in medical institutions across China. Overall, NRAMs have low availability rates in medical institutions across China; furthermore, the availability rates varied based on the province, medical institution level, and daily cost. We observed that the access time for government reimbursement negotiation of anticancer medications, daily costs, provincial gross product, per capita disposable income, and the number of policy releases can influence the availability of NRAMs.

NRAMs usually have high clinical value and are urgently needed by patients; however, due to their high price, harsh storage conditions, and high management costs for clinical application, medical institutions are equipped with a relatively small number of these medications. In contrast, China’s vast geographical area and large base of medical institutions make NRAMs less available nationwide. In addition, some differences may exist in the availability rates of NRAMs due to the different patient bases for the indications of the drugs, and the scale, capital investment, and marketing ability of the manufacturing enterprises to which the drugs belong. We observed that such differences are also reflected in the equipment of provinces, and that the availability rate of NRAMs is higher in eastern China than in western China, possibly due to differences in geography, economic development, daily life and work, and the spectrum of diseases in different provinces.

The level of a medical institution is a combination of its size, medical equipment, available technology, and management level. We observed that the availability rates of NRAMs were higher in tertiary medical institutions than in secondary and primary medical institutions. This finding is consistent with the management requirements and objectives of the tiered diagnosis and treatment system implemented by the government [22]. The policy specifies that tertiary medical institutions are mainly responsible for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and critical illnesses, as well as difficult and complex diseases; secondary medical institutions are responsible for patients in the stabilized and recovered stages of serious illnesses; and primary medical institutions are mainly responsible for the provision of medical services to patients with well-defined diagnoses of chronic illnesses and geriatrics, particularly those in stable conditions. Medical institutions are required to transfer patients with different degrees of disease severity according to their own diagnostic and treatment capabilities. Additionally, based on the specificity and severity of neoplastic diseases, previous studies have confirmed that cancer patients are more willing to seek medical treatment in high-grade medical institutions [23]. Therefore, due to several factors such as national policy requirements for the role of high-grade medical institutions, transfers of critically ill patients from other low-grade medical institutions, and the demand for treatment of cancer patients in high-grade medical institutions, the higher the number of NRAMs equipped in the higher-grade medical institutions, the higher the availability of the drugs. In addition, the type of medical institution may have an impact on the availability of NRAMs. For example, oncology specialty hospitals tend to outperform other types of medical institutions in terms of the number of NRAMs available due to the specialized treatment areas.

Among the factors that influence the availability of NRAMs, the timing of government reimbursement negotiations emerged as a facilitator, providing additional data support when compared with the findings of previous studies [16]. Since the negotiation, in order to guarantee the availability of medications, China has introduced various policies related to NRDs. It emphasizes that medical institutions are the first to be responsible for rational clinical provision and the use of NRDs. It also requires medical institutions to be equipped with NRDs and to use them, and it also links their rational equipping and use of NRDs to annual performance appraisals [24–26]. Furthermore, for some NRDs with long use periods and high treatment costs, reasonable reimbursement methods were formulated, which can effectively guarantee the availability and affordability of these medications to patients. In the actual process of use in medical institutions, clinicians will have a cognitive process for most innovative drugs in NRDL [27]. Clinicians’ familiarity with NRAMs will affect their willingness to use them and the willingness of medical institutions to equip them. Therefore, the earlier the anticancer medications enter the NRDL, the more influenced by the implementation of the policy, the longer the time of clinical use, and the higher the willingness of medical institutions to equip and use, which promotes the availability of NRAMs in medical institutions.

Our findings indicated that a high average daily cost was a factor that limited the availability of NRDs, which is consistent with the findings of a study by Moye-Holz et al. [28, 29]. Other previous studies focused only on the relationship between the price of anticancer medications and their availability. However, as the cycle of use and dosage differences between anticancer medications may bias the study results, we added further evidence by using the average daily cost of NRAMs over the course of treatment to study the impact of drug cost on availability rates. We also considered the fact that, although government reimbursement negotiations have significantly reduced the prices of anticancer medications [30], the willingness of institutions to stock drugs with high daily treatment costs is low, which ultimately affects drug availability. This may be attributed to the fact that the current evaluation mechanism for the use of medications in medical institutions in China is still imperfect. Currently, in order to observe the increase or decrease of drug costs in medical institutions, indicators such as the increase of outpatient and inpatient per diem drug costs have been set up in the annual performance appraisal and evaluation system of public tertiary medical institutions, which are required to be gradually reduced. The use of NRAMs with high average daily costs will significantly increase the values of the corresponding indicators, resulting in poor evaluation of the annual performance appraisal of medical institutions, which in turn reduces the willingness to stock these drugs. At the same time, excessively high drug prices will increase the pressure on medical insurance fund expenditures of medical institutions, which is not conducive to the availability of NRAMs in medical institutions.

We found differences in the availability rate of NRAMs among the 31 provinces with varying levels of economic development. Their gross product, disposable income per capita, and number of policy releases were all factors that contributed to drug availability. Previous studies have confirmed that cancer treatment and health services are concentrated in richer regions or those with higher economic levels [31–33]. This is also true for the availability of NRAMs, as our results revealed that most of the top ten provinces with the highest drug availability rates were located in economically developed regions in the central and eastern parts of China, such as Beijing, Zhejiang, and Guangdong. Provinces with high gross product and high per capita disposable income have more medical resources and better medical security systems, and drug production and distribution enterprises are concentrated in these provinces, resulting in a more abundant supply of drugs on the market. Meanwhile, eastern provinces with higher levels of economic development have less pressure on and higher affordability of their medical insurance funds [34], which may provide more disposable space for medical institutions to equip and use NRAMs. The availability of NRAMs will thus be improved.

The number of policies issued can reflect the importance that the government attaches to a particular area [35]. Since most of the NRDs have a high clinical value and long use cycles, the government attaches great importance to their provision and use. Consequently, provinces have closely introduced a variety of differentiated management policies. We collected the relevant policies issued by 31 provinces and found that the number of policies released varied among the provinces. Our results showed that provinces with a higher number of releases were mainly concentrated in the eastern region than in the western region. The number of releases may be influenced by factors, such as resource distribution and financial support; moreover, there are differences in policy inclination and emphasis among provinces [36]. In addition to the number of policy releases, the corresponding refined management measures also affect the availability of NRAMs. Possibly due to the high emphasis and strong policy implementation of local governments, some provinces, such as Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Yunnan, have a higher rate of NRAMs availability. These provinces have clearly stipulated typical measures, such as convening a pharmacy meeting of medical institutions within a certain time frame after the release of NRDL. The selection of medical institutions to equip NRAMs will go through a series of procedures such as clinician assessment, conducting pharmacy meetings, and selecting distribution suppliers, which extends the time for equipping. Setting a time limit for the conduct of pharmacy meetings is conducive to shortening the length of drug selection in medical institutions. To a certain extent, it avoids affecting the equipping of NRAMs due to delays in conducting pharmacy meetings. At the same time, the inclusion of NRDL equipment and use in the assessment system of medical institutions is to promote the rational equipment and use of NRDs, and by actively equipping and rationally using NRDs, medical institutions can obtain good performance scores. These measures are conducive to improving the availability of NRAMs.

This study has some limitations. First, numerous factors affect the availability of NRAMs in medical institutions; hence, only a limited number of factors were included in this study. Second, in China, except for provinces, there are large differences in economic level and medical development between cities and regions. However, based on the available data, we could only explore the availability of medical institutions at the national and provincial levels, and could not conduct further analysis. Third, since this database dynamically updates information on the availability of medical institutions, we could not track real-time availability rates. Therefore, our results may differ from the actual state of availability rates in medical institution.

Future studies could further collect information on the availability of NRAMs in medical institutions between cities and regions based on the National Health Insurance Service Platform, as well as conduct qualitative interviews to incorporate other possible influencing factors. It may make the analysis of the availability of NRAMs in China more comprehensive.

Conclusions

Current NRAMs have low availability rates in medical institutions across provinces in China. Moreover, our study showed that the time of access to government reimbursement negotiations for anticancer medications, provincial gross product, per capita disposable income, and the number of policy releases have a positive impact on the availability of NRAMs, whereas the daily cost has a negative impact on availability rates. This suggests that the government should formulate and improve the annual evaluation and incentives to ensure that medical institutions are equipped with NRDs according to the differences between the provinces in order to improve their willingness to stock current NRAMs and enhance their availability.
