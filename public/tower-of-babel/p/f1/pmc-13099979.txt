Parametric assessment of rainfall-related slope stability through SRM modeling and orthogonal experimental design: insights from the Zhuquedong slope, China
Authors: Feng Liu, Zhigui Dong, Majid Khayatnezhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rainfall-induced slope instability is a major geotechnical hazard in mountainous regions, where slope behavior is governed by the combined effects of geometry, soil mechanical properties, and hydrological conditions. Quantifying the relative influence of these factors remains challenging due to the high dimensionality and computational cost of numerical models. This study presents a parametric numerical–statistical investigation of rainfall-related slope stability by combining the strength reduction method (SRM) with an orthogonal experimental design and statistical analysis. Twenty-eight slope scenarios were generated using an L28 orthogonal array, enabling simultaneous and structured variation of slope geometry, soil strength parameters, and hydrological conditions while limiting computational demand. For each scenario, the factor of safety (Fs) was obtained through SRM-based finite element analysis and subsequently examined using correlation analysis, one-way ANOVA, multiple linear regression, and range analysis to evaluate parameter sensitivity and relative influence. The results indicate that slope geometry, particularly slope height and gradient, exerts the strongest control on stability, while rainfall-related hydrological conditions significantly reduce Fs by amplifying pore-water effects. Soil shear strength parameters provide secondary stabilizing influences, whereas unit weight shows a comparatively minor effect within the investigated range. Rather than proposing a new numerical formulation, this study demonstrates how orthogonal experimental design and statistical post-processing can efficiently support parametric screening of rainfall-related slope stability in engineering applications.

Introduction

As one of the most damaging natural hazards globally, landslides threaten lives, ecosystems, and infrastructure. Over 55,000 mortalities were ascribed to non-seismic landslides on the globe between 2004 and 2016 only, and Asia accounted for most of these phenomena1. Equal destruction occurs by economic losses, both directly and indirectly, and billions of dollars are expended every year on calamity retrieval, slope stabilization, and land-use rehabilitation (Haque et al., 2019). Simultaneously, pushing fast urban area expansion into mountainous territory and increasing the strength and occurrence of rainfall events due to climate change have even caused a more crucial challenge of apprehending and alleviating slope disasters2,3. According to these classifications, shallow and deep-seated landslides differ not only in failure depth but also in controlling mechanisms, triggering factors, and temporal evolution. Shallow landslides are often highly sensitive to hydrological forcing and near-surface strength degradation, whereas deeper failures may involve more complex interactions between geological structure, groundwater conditions, and external triggers.

Landslides represent a broad class of gravity-driven mass movements involving soil, rock, or debris, exhibiting diverse movement mechanisms, material compositions, and failure depths. A widely adopted conceptual approach for describing landslide processes is provided by the classification system originally proposed by Varnes4, which categorizes landslides based on the type of movement and the nature of the displaced material. This approach was subsequently refined and updated by Hungr et al.5, to better reflect advances in landslide mechanics and to improve consistency in landslide terminology across engineering and geomorphological applications.

The balance between driving and resisting forces basically governs slope stability. Slope height and gradient are among the geometric factors that escalate the driving stresses, whereas the resisting capacity is defined by geotechnical parameters, namely soil cohesion, internal friction angle, and unit weight6. Hydrological conditions, in particular rainfall infiltration and groundwater fluctuations, can lead to a rapid reduction in soil strength by raising pore-water pressure, which thus destabilizes slopes that could otherwise stay unchanged under dry conditions7. Rainfall-driven alterations in effective stress primarily trigger shallow landslides, particularly in residual soils and weathered rock, as confirmed in many case studies over various geologic environments8,9.

Rainfall-induced landslides are primarily governed by hydrological processes that modify the stress state and shear strength of slope materials. As rainfall infiltrates the soil, a wetting front propagates downward, increasing the degree of saturation and reducing matric suction in unsaturated soils. This process leads to a decrease in effective stress and shear strength, while prolonged or intense rainfall may result in groundwater table rise and pore-water pressure accumulation.

Physically based hydrological–geotechnical models have demonstrated that the interaction between infiltration dynamics, soil hydraulic properties, and slope geometry plays a critical role in controlling the timing and magnitude of rainfall-induced failures, particularly for deep-seated landslides10. Although the present study does not explicitly simulate transient infiltration processes, these mechanisms provide the physical basis for the simplified hydrological representations adopted herein.

The last decades have witnessed the development of diverse analytical approaches to assess slope stability. Traditional limit equilibrium techniques, e.g., Bishop’s and Janbu’s simplified approaches, still have wide applications because they are simple, but there is a need to predefine slip surfaces, and they are unable of capturing progressive failure mechanisms11. Finite element (FEM) and finite difference (FDM) are among numerical methods that present a more all-inclusive methodology as they simulate stress–strain behavior and deformation patterns. Among the mentioned methods, the strength reduction method (SRM) within finite element approachs has arisen as a potent tool due to its avoidance of former assumptions about the slip surface, enabling directly evaluating safety factors via incremental strength reduction6,7. Recently reported advancements also integrate combined hydro-mechanical modeling, which enables assessing rainfall infiltration and transient pore-pressure impacts on slope stability12.

Beyond deterministic methods, probabilistic approaches have received eminence in recent years, which recognize the inherent variability of soil properties and are not certain spatially and temporally. Reliability-based methods and random field simulations have gained applications in quantifying the likelihood of failure; thus, they more realistically quantify slope safety12,13. Simultaneously, machine learning and data-driven techniques have unveiled novel opportunities for landslide vulnerability mapping and early warning. Substantial developments in predictive performance in comparison with traditional deterministic models have been established using ensemble models and hybrid approachs14,15. Although these probabilistic and AI-based tools show promise, they are still limited in practical application by data requisites, interpretability challenges, and integration with engineering design standards. Despite extensive applications of the strength reduction method (SRM) in slope stability analysis, many existing studies rely on one-factor-at-a-time sensitivity analyses, in which individual parameters are varied independently while others are held constant. Such approaches do not adequately capture the coupled influence of slope geometry, soil mechanical properties, and hydrological conditions, and may therefore provide an incomplete picture of parameter dominance under rainfall-related scenarios. Although fully coupled hydro-mechanical and probabilistic approaches can address these interactions more rigorously, their practical application is often limited by data availability and computational cost, particularly in preliminary or data-scarce engineering assessments. As a result, there remains a need for structured yet computationally efficient parametric approaches that allow simultaneous evaluation of multiple controlling factors while maintaining transparency and interpretability. To respond these gaps, numerical modeling, orthogonal experimental design, and statistical analysis are integrated in our research for the systematical examination of the relative impact of slope height, gradient, cohesion, internal friction angle, and unit weight on the safety factor (Fs) under natural and saturated situations. The investigation adopts an orthogonal design to diminish computational efforts but to capture multi-parameter interplays, providing a methodologically advantageous approach relative to one-factor-at-a-time methodologies. Simulation results are validated by statistical analyses, namely correlation, ANOVA, and regression, to present a better understanding of parameter sensitivity. Not only is the primary function of slope geometry and hydrology reinforced by the results, but also practicable recommendations are delivered to stabilize the slope and alleviate the risk. This is a combined method contributing to slope stability research advancement and providing engineers, planners, and policymakers with actionable knowledge to develop safer infrastructure in landslide-exposed settings. In addition to hydrological triggers, seismic loading is another important factor capable of initiating or accelerating landslide processes. Earthquake-induced landslides are commonly associated with transient strength reduction, dynamic stress redistribution, and progressive damage accumulation within slope materials. Numerous studies have demonstrated that seismic shaking can significantly alter slope stability patterns and landslide susceptibility at regional scales16,17.

While earthquake-induced mechanisms are not explicitly considered in the numerical simulations of this study, their recognition is essential for a comprehensive understanding of landslide initiation processes. Accordingly, the present work focuses on rainfall-related stability conditions, while acknowledging seismic loading as an additional triggering factor beyond the scope of the current approach. Previous studies have employed a range of analytical and numerical approaches for slope stability assessment, including limit equilibrium methods, finite element methods, and finite difference techniques. While these approaches have significantly advanced the understanding of landslide mechanisms, many studies rely on case-specific analyses or isolated parameter variations, limiting systematic comparison of multiple interacting factors. This study addresses this gap by integrating numerical slope stability modeling with orthogonal experimental design and statistical analysis, enabling an efficient and structured evaluation of parameter sensitivity under rainfall-related conditions.

Although the strength reduction method (SRM) has been widely applied in slope stability analysis, most existing SRM-based sensitivity studies rely on one-factor-at-a-time parameter variation, which neglects the coupled effects between slope geometry, soil properties, and hydrological conditions.

The methodological contribution of this study does not lie in proposing a new numerical formulation, but in restructuring how SRM simulations are designed and interpreted. Instead of isolated parameter perturbations, we employ a design-of-experiments–based SRM approach, in which geometric, mechanical, and rainfall-related factors are varied simultaneously and systematically.

Furthermore, SRM-derived factors of safety are treated as response variables within an integrated statistical inference workflow, allowing the relative dominance and interaction of controlling factors to be quantified under realistic multi-parameter conditions. This approach transforms SRM from a descriptive stability assessment tool into a decision-oriented approach for prioritizing stabilization measures in rainfall-induced slope failures.

Materials and methods

Study area and landslide background

The Zhuquedong landslide is located in Hunan Province, China (Fig. 1), within a region characterized by complex terrain and frequent rainfall-triggered slope instabilities. The landslide mainly involves shallow soil slopes developed on weathered materials, which are representative of many rainfall-sensitive slopes in subtropical mountainous environments of southern China.

Previous field investigations and technical reports indicate that shallow slope failures have occurred repeatedly in this area, particularly during periods of intense or prolonged rainfall. These failures are typically controlled by slope geometry, soil strength degradation, and variations in groundwater conditions. The Zhuquedong site was therefore selected as a representative case to investigate rainfall-related slope stability mechanisms through numerical and statistical analyses, rather than to reproduce a specific failure event in detail.

Data collection and site investigation

High-resolution topographic and geotechnical data were collected to characterize the geometry and material properties of the Zhuquedong landslide.

LiDAR survey

The slope geometry was derived from airborne LiDAR data with a spatial resolution of approximately 0.5–1.0 m, acquired by the local surveying authority in [year]. The raw point cloud data were filtered to remove vegetation and non-ground returns, and a digital elevation model (DEM) was generated using standard interpolation techniques. Key geometric parameters, including slope height, slope gradient, and surface morphology, were extracted from the DEM and subsequently used as input variables for numerical modeling and orthogonal experimental design.

Geotechnical investigation

The subsurface conditions were characterized through a combination of field sampling and laboratory testing. Representative soil samples were collected from different depth layers along the slope profile using boreholes and test pits. Laboratory tests were conducted following standard ASTM procedures. Soil unit weight was determined according to ASTM D7263, while shear strength parameters (cohesion and internal friction angle) were obtained from laboratory shear tests conducted in accordance with ASTM D3080 and ASTM D4767. These parameters were statistically summarized and discretized into representative levels for use in the numerical simulations and statistical analyses.

The integration of LiDAR-derived terrain data and laboratory-tested soil parameters ensured that the numerical model was grounded in site-specific conditions while maintaining consistency across the experimental scenarios.

Hydrological representation and modeling assumptions

In this study, hydrological effects associated with rainfall are incorporated in a simplified and parametric manner. Detailed hydrological or meteorological analyses, such as rainfall intensity–duration relationships, transient infiltration processes, or unsaturated soil mechanics, are beyond the scope of this work.

Instead, rainfall-related influences are represented through predefined groundwater table depths and saturation ratios, which are treated as independent controlling factors in the numerical simulations. These parameters are intended to capture representative moisture conditions corresponding to dry, partially saturated, and fully saturated states commonly associated with rainfall-triggered shallow landslides.

This approach allows for a systematic comparison of slope stability responses under different hydrological conditions while maintaining consistency with the orthogonal experimental design and statistical analysis adopted in this study. Accordingly, the role of hydrological conditions herein is not to reproduce rainfall processes explicitly, but to enable a comparative assessment of their relative impact on slope stability.

Scope and limitations of data usage

The dataset employed in this research supports a parametric and statistical investigation of slope stability rather than a fully coupled hydro-mechanical or meteorological analysis. While the adopted assumptions simplify the physical processes involved, they allow for efficient exploration of factor sensitivity and interaction effects under rainfall-related conditions.

The limitations associated with these assumptions are acknowledged and discussed in the Discussion section, where future extensions incorporating transient infiltration modeling, probabilistic hydrological inputs, and unsaturated soil behavior are suggested (Fig 2).

Integrated numerical–statistical workflow

To clearly distinguish the proposed approach from conventional SRM-based sensitivity analyses, this study adopts an integrated numerical–statistical workflow. Rather than performing isolated parameter perturbations, the approach systematically links field data, experimental design, numerical modeling, and statistical inference into a unified analysis chain.

The database used in this study was generated through a controlled numerical–statistical procedure rather than collected from independent field events. A total of 28 slope configurations were constructed based on an L28 orthogonal experimental design, which allows for the efficient and balanced combination of multiple influencing factors at predefined levels. Each configuration represents a physically meaningful slope condition and was analyzed numerically to obtain the corresponding factor of safety.

The term “experimental combination” in this study refers to a simulated slope scenario rather than a real landslide occurrence. All combinations are numerically simulated but are grounded in realistic ranges of geometry, soil properties, and hydrological conditions derived from field investigations and engineering practice.

Numerical modeling

Slope stability analyses were carried out using the Strength Reduction Method (SRM) implemented in OptumG2/PLAXIS. The soil behavior was described by the Mohr–Coulomb elastic–perfectly plastic constitutive model, with cohesion (c) and internal friction angle (φ) as the governing strength parameters, due to its robustness and suitability for parametric sensitivity analysis involving multiple simulations.

In the SRM procedure, slope failure was induced by progressively reducing c and tanφ until numerical non-convergence occurred, and the corresponding reduction factor was taken as the factor of safety (Fs).

The numerical domain was discretized using an unstructured finite element mesh with local refinement near the slope face and potential failure zones. A mesh sensitivity check confirmed that further refinement led to negligible changes in Fs, indicating mesh-independent results. The base of the model was fixed in both horizontal and vertical directions, while lateral boundaries were constrained horizontally and free vertically; the slope surface was left free.

Hydrological effects were represented through predefined saturation states rather than transient infiltration modeling, allowing systematic comparison of stability under different moisture conditions within a quasi-static approach. All 28 orthogonal experimental cases were analyzed following the same numerical procedure to ensure consistency.

Experimental design

The impact of slope geometry, soil mechanical properties, and hydro-environmental conditions was investigated using an L28 orthogonal array as a structured sampling strategy for numerical parametric analysis. The primary objective of adopting the orthogonal array was to efficiently explore the relative influence of multiple controlling parameters on slope stability while limiting the total number of strength reduction method (SRM) simulations. Compared with a full factorial design, which would require several hundred numerical runs, the L28 array provides a balanced and computationally efficient approach for parameter screening.

In this study, the orthogonal design is not intended to represent a physical or laboratory-based experimental campaign. Instead, it is employed as a systematic approach to organize numerical simulations and to ensure that variations in geometric, geotechnical, and hydrological parameters are explored concurrently rather than through one-factor-at-a-time sensitivity analyses. This strategy improves interpretability of relative parameter influence while acknowledging the simplified and deterministic nature of the numerical modeling.

Five primary factors, including slope height (H), slope gradient (θ), cohesion (c), internal friction angle (φ), and unit weight (γ), together with secondary descriptors such as depth layer, temporal condition, and hydrological state, were incorporated into the orthogonal array. For continuous parameters, three representative levels (low, intermediate, and high) were defined to span plausible ranges derived from site investigation data, laboratory testing, and values commonly reported in the literature. These discretized levels were used exclusively for range analysis and factor ranking, whereas the SRM simulations themselves employed physically realistic parameter values.

The L28 orthogonal array was selected to accommodate the number of investigated factors while maintaining numerical balance and reducing confounding among main effects. Given the limited number of scenarios (n = 28), the orthogonal design supports qualitative sensitivity assessment and parameter ranking rather than statistical inference of interaction effects or predictive modeling.

Statistical analyses

The compiled database of 28 test cases, which integrates geometric, geotechnical, and hydro-meteorological variables and their related safety factor (FS), was subjected to all statistical analyses described in the Results. These comprise descriptive statistics, seasonal/zone/depth summaries, Pearson correlations, one-way ANOVA, orthogonal (range) analysis with factor level aggregation, multiple linear regression, pairwise relations, distributional plots by season and depth, and a bivariate response surface for Height–Saturation effects. In this study, numerical modeling and statistical analyses are combined in a complementary manner, where SRM provides physically based stability responses and statistical tools enable structured interpretation and sensitivity assessment.

Sensitivity analysis and qualitative model consistency check

A sensitivity analysis was performed to qualitatively examine the response of the factor of safety (Fs) to variations in key input parameters. Each primary parameter was independently perturbed by ± 10% around its baseline value while other parameters were held constant, and the corresponding changes in Fs were recorded. This analysis is intended as a qualitative consistency check rather than a formal uncertainty assessment. The results indicate that Fs is most responsive to changes in slope geometry parameters, particularly slope height, followed by hydrological indicators such as saturation ratio and water table depth. Shear strength parameters, including cohesion and internal friction angle, exhibit moderate sensitivity, whereas unit weight shows a comparatively minor influence within the investigated parameter range. These trends are consistent with the outcomes of the correlation analysis and orthogonal range analysis. A qualitative comparison was also conducted between simulated Fs values and observed slope conditions at the study site to assess the plausibility of the numerical results. Slope sections exhibiting signs of instability were generally associated with lower Fs values, whereas areas characterized as relatively stable corresponded to Fs values greater than unity. This comparison serves as a contextual consistency check rather than a formal model validation, given the absence of independent monitoring data.

Software and analytical tools

OptumG2/Plaxis was used for numerical modeling, and standard statistical and scientific software (e.g., SPSS V.27, R, MATLAB, and Python) were employed for statistical analyses (descriptive statistics, correlation, ANOVA, regression, and orthogonal analysis) and data visualization (plots and 3D surfaces).

Results

The slope stability results obtained from the SRM analyses are expressed in terms of the factor of safety (Fs). Across the 28 numerical scenarios, Fs values ranged from approximately [Fs_min] to [Fs_max], reflecting stable, marginally stable, and potentially unstable conditions under different combinations of geometric, geotechnical, and hydrological factors.

For the reference condition representing the current slope geometry and average soil properties under unsaturated conditions, the calculated Fs was approximately [Fs_ref], indicating a [stable/marginally stable] slope state. Under saturated conditions, Fs values consistently decreased, in some cases approaching or falling below unity, highlighting the susceptibility of the slope to rainfall-induced instability.

The results observed in the experiments, simulations, and statistical assessments of this research are presented and analyzed in this section. After collecting geometric, geotechnical, and hydro-meteorological data and implementing numerical modeling, the slope Fs was tested as the key index of stability. The results are prearranged into descriptive and inferential analyses, namely basic statistics, correlation tests, group comparisons by the slope zone, season, and soil depth, as well as multivariate regression modeling and one-way ANOVA. Moreover, the interactive impacts of main parameters (e.g., slope height, gradient, saturation ratio, and shear strength properties) are studied on slope stability. The observations present an all-inclusive approach to understand slope behavior under variable conditions and underpin the succeeding discussion.

Slope gradient. FS decreases as slope steepness increases, with K̄L1 = 2.097, K̄L2 = 2.025, and K̄L3 = 1.825, corresponding to R = 0.272. This suggests that slope gradient plays a secondary but still relevant role in slope instability.

Saturation ratio. Increased saturation is associated with a reduction in FS, as reflected by a decrease in K̄ from 2.091 at L1 (lower saturation) to 1.861 at L3 (higher saturation), with R = 0.230. This behavior is consistent with fundamental geotechnical principles, whereby increased pore-water pressure reduces effective stress and shear resistance.

Cohesion. FS shows an increasing trend with higher cohesion values, with K̄L1 = 1.907, K̄L2 = 2.038, and K̄L3 = 2.137, yielding R = 0.229. While cohesion contributes positively to slope stability, its relative influence in the present dataset is smaller than that of geometric and hydrological factors.

Water table depth. A deeper groundwater table corresponds to higher FS values, with K̄ increasing from 1.966 at L1 to 2.095 at L3 (R = 0.129). This result suggests that groundwater lowering through drainage can enhance slope stability, albeit with a moderate effect compared to primary controlling factors.

Friction angle. The influence of the internal friction angle is positive but relatively limited, with K̄ values of 1.993, 1.983, and 2.078 for L1–L3, respectively, and R = 0.095. This indicates that increases in φ improve stability, though the sensitivity within the investigated range is comparatively small.

Unit weight. Soil unit weight exhibits the smallest range value (R = 0.093), with K̄L1 = 2.044, K̄L2 = 1.952, and K̄L3 = 2.045. Variations in unit weight therefore have only a minor influence on FS within the examined parameter space.

Discussion

Overview of results

The contribution of this study lies not in reiterating that slope height or rainfall-induced saturation reduces stability observations that are well documented in previous studies but in demonstrating how the relative importance of controlling factors evolves under coupled conditions. By integrating SRM with a DoE-based statistical inference approach, this study quantifies parameter dominance and interaction patterns that cannot be revealed through isolated sensitivity analyses.

This perspective provides a more consistent basis for engineering decision-making, particularly in determining whether stabilization efforts should prioritize geometric modification, drainage control, or material reinforcement under rainfall conditions. Although the destabilizing effects of increased slope height and rainfall-induced saturation are widely recognized, the findings of this study extend beyond confirming these general trends. By integrating SRM with a design-of-experiments–based statistical approach, the results demonstrate that the relative importance of controlling factors is highly dependent on their coupled conditions.

The analysis reveals that parameter dominance varies systematically across different geometric and hydrological configurations, highlighting that slope stability cannot be reliably interpreted based on isolated parameter effects alone.

In our investigation, slope geometry (height and gradient) appeared as the primary factor influencing slope stability assessed by the Fs. A significant role was also observed for material strength parameters (cohesion, friction angle), and unit weight showed a lower but non-insignificant impact. Fs was intensely reduced by saturation and rainfall infiltration, highlighting hydrological control. The recorded modes of failure (toe slides, base slides, and face slides) changed with geometry and soil type variations.

Comparison with previous studies

Geometry effects: height and angle

The dramatic impact of slope height and gradient on stability is corroborated in numerous recent reports. Kumar et al.19, found a nonlinear increase in Fs by dropping slope height, and an almost linear rise in Fs with diminishing slope angle. Likewise, reducing height and angle improved safety, although the efficiency was dependent on soil type (20. Zhang et al.14, claimed the maximum sensitivity coefficient of slope angle, followed by weak layer dip, and then height in multi-weak-layered rock slopes.

Material strength & hydrological effects

Reductions in both cohesion and friction angle with rising water content (via rainfall), which degrades stability, have been documented in studies on residual soils21,22. According to Lan et al.9,, shear strength shows a non-linear decline with moisture content and saturation duration; cohesion’s tendency is to follow a quadratic reduction, and the friction angle is more logarithmic. Wang et al.23, emphasize frequent Fs overestimation in slope stability analyses when they ignore the deterioration of strength parameters under water content variations.

Weak layers, strata dip, and composite effects

In a study on coal measure strata by Li et al. (2023), weak interlayers and dip angles modulated the impact of height and slope angle: they observed a specific dip (~ 30°), in which Fs tended toward a minimum. A substantial impact exists when structural discontinuities (weak layers) are present, as Fs tends to decline for greater height slopes in cases with weak layers.

Volume & scale effects

In the examination of sizable landslide inventories, a reverse correlation was observed between landslide volume and friction angle: greater mass failures tended to present less friction angle and cohesion (24. This corresponds to our finding that soil strength (particularly friction angle) has a consistent function, principally in greater and more crucial slope scenarios.

In addition to the factors already discussed, a number of prior studies have highlighted the significant role of geological discontinuities such as weak layers, bedding planes, and strata dip in governing slope stability. Field and numerical analyses demonstrate that weak stratigraphic layers with unfavorable orientations can act as preferred failure surfaces, markedly reducing the resisting strength of slopes independent of external triggers such as rainfall or earthquakes. For example, Soralump et al., (2018) showed in back-analysis of a rock slope that a weak foliation plane within phyllitic sandstone, oriented with a distinct dip, significantly controlled the failure mechanism and required flattening and drainage interventions for stabilization30. Similarly, numerical and monitoring studies on high cut-slopes indicate that the interaction of weak bedding planes, structural faults, and extreme rainfall can accelerate and complicate landslide processes; the presence of weak planes and faulting not only reduces strength locally but also facilitates the development of connected slip surfaces under hydro-mechanical loading. These findings are consistent with broader research on stratigraphic and structural controls on slope stability, where unfavorable strata dip relative to slope face orientation can enhance the likelihood of planar or wedge failures, particularly in layered or foliated materials31,32. This body of literature complements the present numerical–statistical results by providing mechanistic evidence that intrinsic geological features can modulate how rainfall-induced pore pressure changes translate into reduced factors of safety.

Overall, the statistical analyses demonstrate that slope stability is controlled by the combined and interacting effects of geometric, geotechnical, and hydrological factors. While individual parameters exhibit moderate correlation strengths and F-values, their effects are statistically significant and consistent across multiple analytical methods. The integration of correlation analysis, ANOVA, and orthogonal experimental design allows internally consistent identification of dominant trends while avoiding overinterpretation of individual metrics.

Conclusion

This study presents a parametric numerical–statistical investigation of rainfall-related slope stability by combining strength reduction method (SRM) simulations with an orthogonal experimental design and classical statistical analyses. Rather than introducing a new numerical formulation, the work demonstrates how a structured sampling strategy can be used to efficiently explore the relative sensitivity of slope stability to geometric, geotechnical, and hydrological parameters within a limited number of numerical simulations. The results consistently indicate that slope geometry, particularly slope height, exerts the strongest control on the factor of safety, reflecting its primary role in governing driving stresses. Hydrological conditions associated with rainfall, such as increased saturation and shallow groundwater, show destabilizing trends, while soil shear strength parameters provide secondary stabilizing effects within the investigated parameter ranges. Unit weight exhibits a comparatively minor influence. These trends are mutually supported by correlation analysis, orthogonal range analysis, regression interpretation, and qualitative sensitivity assessment. The proposed approach is intended for parametric screening and interpretive assessment rather than predictive modeling or formal design application. Its applicability is constrained by simplified hydrological representation, deterministic material properties, and a limited number of scenarios. Future work should focus on incorporating transient infiltration processes, probabilistic parameter variability, and independent monitoring data to further enhance the robustness and transferability of numerical–statistical slope stability assessments.
