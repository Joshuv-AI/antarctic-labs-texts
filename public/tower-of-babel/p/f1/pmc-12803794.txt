Influence of IgG+ serum on Zika virus infection response in a model of neural precursor cells
Authors: Ismael Plentz, Douglas Pazzin, Thales Previato, Fernanda Wagner, Marina Boff, Larissa Fernandes, João Ismael B Gonçalvez, Daniel Marinowic, Jaderson Costa da Costa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe high frequency of Zika virus (ZIKV) infection prompted the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare it an emerging threat in 2016. Presently, countries in South America continue to report a significant number of Zika virus cases. Zika virus infection has been associated with neurological diseases. This article explores the impact of ZIKV infection on IgG + Zika-reactive conditions using a neurosphere model. For this investigation, neurospheres derived from Wistar rats were exposed to the Zika virus and to IgG + conditions reactive to Zika. The relative expression levels of the NOTCH-1, HES-1, and HEY-1 genes were assessed through quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR). Additionally, the concentrations of GCSF and IL-10 proteins were quantified using a Luminex immunoassay. Morphological evaluation of the neurospheres was conducted, with parameters such as area and irregularity rate being analyzed using Image Pro Plus 7 software.The irregularity rate of ZIKV neurospheres decreased at its lowest concentration; however, our morphology analysis was insufficient to fully elucidate the impact of ZIKV on neurospheres. Interestingly, IgG + serum exhibited neuroprotective effects against subsequent Zika virus exposure, restoring NOTCH-1 and HES-1 expression levels to normal. HEY-1 expression remained unaffected by Zika virus exposure but decreased with IgG + serum. Surprisingly, levels of the anti-inflammatory markers GCSF and IL-10 showed no significant changes. These findings highlight the complex interplay between ZIKV infection, immune response, and neurodevelopmental processes. Further research is necessary to elucidate the precise mechanisms underlying these observations and explore potential therapeutic interventions.Keywords: Zika virus, Neurospheres, IgG+ serum, Neural precursor cells

Introduction

The Zika virus, a flavivirus transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, poses a significant risk of global transmission due to the widespread distribution of these arthropods across all continents [1]. ZIKV infections are often asymptomatic or mild discomfort, with symptoms such as fever, conjunctivitis, joint pain, and rashes, while severe cases are rare. However, the virus's association with an increased incidence of neurological disorders—particularly Guillain-Barré syndrome and congenital abnormalities like microcephaly—has garnered significant scientific attention. This concern led the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare a global health emergency in 2016 [2,3]. According to the Brazilian Ministry of Health, 14,453 Zika cases were reported in the first half of 2023, with 1294 cases involving pregnant women, 787 of which occurred in the early stages of pregnancy—periods most closely linked to severe fetal outcomes [4,5]. Notable fetal outcomes include microcephaly, cerebral calcifications, ventriculomegaly, hydranencephaly, intrauterine growth restriction, and neurodevelopmental issues such as ocular defects, hydrops fetalis, and hearing impairments [6].

Since then, numerous studies have been conducted to assess the impacts of ZIKV infection, which may provide opportunities to investigate ways to treat these infections in the future. A study of brain tissue from Wistar rat pups infected with ZIKV revealed morphological damage, alterations in the blood-brain barrier, and evidence of neuroinflammation, even in individuals without microcephaly [7]. Furthermore, neurosphere culture indicates that ZIKV infection disrupts the circadian rhythm in the Central Nervous System (CNS), resulting in an imbalance in brain homeostasis [8].

Mammalian embryonic neurodevelopment depends on the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of neuroepithelial cells, which comprise multipotent neural and progenitor cells. Neurospheres, clusters of neural stem cells (NSCs) and progenitor cells, serve as an in vitro model of embryonic neurogenesis [9].

Based on these findings, the present study generated neurosphere models from Wistar rat brain tissue to evaluate the effects of prior ZIKV infection versus re-exposure to the virus during in vitro neurodevelopment, considering the broad impact of ZIKV on the CNS.

Materials and methods

Ethical aspects

Neural progenitor cells were obtained from four neonatal Wistar rats, aged between 1 and 2 days. To isolate ZIKV-specific IgG+ antibodies, serum samples from pregnant women with confirmed ZIKV infection were used. These samples originated from studies funded under the public call MCTI-CNPq/MEC-CAPES/MS-Decit No. 14/2016. Four patients were selected based on serological profiles: positive for ZIKV IgG and negative for IgM, and negative for both IgG and IgM for dengue virus (DENV). The viral strain used in the experiments corresponded to a Brazilian ZIKV isolate, provided by the Virology Laboratory at the Federal University of Rio Grande do Sul (UFRGS). The study was duly registered with the National System for the Management of Genetic Heritage and Associated Traditional Knowledge (SiSGen), under registration number AE30E11. Ethical approval was granted by the Animal Use Ethics Committee of PUCRS (approval no. 002/2019) and by the Human Research Ethics Committee (CAAE: 74959817.9.3001.5336; approval no. 2.638.760). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, in accordance with Resolution No. 466/12 of the Brazilian National Health Council.

Neurosphere acquisition

Neurospheres were derived from cultures of subventricular zone progenitor cells (SZPC). Wistar rats were euthanized via decapitation, and their heads were treated aseptically with 70 % ethanol. The brain was exposed through an incision and carefully extracted. Following this, the brain was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the subventricular zone (SZ) was subsequently isolated. The SZs were macerated and incubated with 1x trypsin at 36 °C for 5 min. Following this, an inactivation medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 20 % fetal bovine serum) was added. The macerated tissue was then centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The cells were subsequently resuspended in preparation medium (a 1:2 ratio of E8/DMEM F12), which was further supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin, 2 ng/mL epidermal growth factor (EGF), and 1 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor (FGF). Subsequently, 2 mL of the cell suspension was added to each well of a non-adherent 24-well plate and incubated at 36 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere. Each well was supplemented with 50 μL of supplementation medium (comprising 8 μL of EGF and 4 μL of FGF per 1 mL of DMEM/F12, along with 80 ng of EGF and 40 ng of FGF). After 7–10 days, the neurospheres became visibly identifiable.

IgG + exposure

Neurospheres with a diameter of ≥100 μm were selected and transferred to an adherent 24-well plate, where they were exposed to each specific experimental group. For each treatment, the neurospheres were exposed to IgG + serum for 2 h at 36 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator. ZIKV concentrations were adjusted to 5 × 107 PFU/mL for 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 MOI, and maintained for 24 h at 36 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator. Following exposure to viral particles, the medium was replaced with a preparation medium. This procedure was followed for each ZIKV exposure treatment group.

Morphology evaluation

The neurospheres were assessed for morphology immediately upon acquisition (day 10) and after exposure to treatment at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. The integrity of their cell membrane was evaluated by measuring initial roughness and post-treatment changes. Additionally, shape alterations were analyzed by comparing the distances between the innermost and outermost points of the neurosphere's perimeter. All analyses were performed using the Image Pro Plus 7 image analysis software.

Gene expression

Gene expression was analyzed using the qRT-PCR technique and quantified through relative expression. Total RNA was extracted using the SV Total RNA Isolation System (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) and subsequently converted into cDNA using the GoScript™ Reverse Transcription Mix with Oligo(dT) primers (Promega). Real-time PCR was performed with the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The amplification was initiated with 20 ng of cDNA per sample. Assays were conducted on separate plates for each target gene, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous control. The target genes analyzed were NOTCH-1, HES-1, and HEY-1. All procedures followed the protocols provided by the respective manufacturers. The primer sets used are listed in Table 1.

Protein quantification

Protein quantification was performed using a Luminex® immunoassay to measure GCSF and IL-10 levels in the supernatant of cultures from each treatment after 2 h of exposure to ZIKV. Magnetic beads specific to each marker were added to a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 50 μL of each sample and the corresponding dilution points of the standard curve. The plate was then placed on a shaker at 500 rpm for 2 h. Subsequently, the plate was washed with wash buffer, and the detection antibody was added. The plate was shaken at 500 rpm for 30 min, after which 50 μL of streptavidin was added. Following another wash with the wash buffer, the plate was analyzed using a Luminex MAGPIX system.

Statistical analysis

Morphological assessments, gene expression analysis, and protein quantification were performed using GraphPad Prism 6. Morphological data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post-test to determine statistical significance among groups. For gene expression, relative quantification was conducted using the 2∧–ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous control. Statistical analysis of gene expression data was performed using one-way ANOVA (SPSS Software, Inc.), followed by Bonferroni's post-hoc test to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Results

Morphology evaluation

No significant changes in total neurosphere area or spherical shape were observed following ZIKV exposure at MOIs of 0.1, 0.01, or 0.001. However, pre-exposure to IgG + serum followed by ZIKV at MOI 0.1 led to a significant reduction in neurosphere area at 24 h post-exposure (p = 0.0246), which was not sustained at later time points. Neurospheres exposed to the highest viral titer (ZIKV MOI 0.1 and IgG+/ZIKV MOI 0.1) exhibited increased morphological irregularity. In contrast, the IgG+/ZIKV group at the lowest viral concentration (MOI 0.001) demonstrated a significant decrease in irregularity at 0 and 24 h post-exposure (p = 0.012 and p = 0.003, respectively; Fig. 1).

Gene expression

The ZIKV at MOI 0.1 led to an increase in NOTCH expression in both analyzed periods. In the time 0 h, NOTCH expression increased 6.36-fold, showing statistically significant differences between the control and IgG+/ZIKV groups (p = 0.037 and p = 0.017, respectively; Fig. 2A). After 72 h of culture, the significant increase in NOTCH-1 expression continued, reaching 11.63-fold (p = 0.0007 vs. Control Group and p = 0.0014 vs. IgG+/ZIKV Group; Fig. 2A).

Pre-exposure to IgG + serum attenuated the early increase in NOTCH-1 expression upon ZIKV exposure, with a 1.95-fold rise compared to control and a 4.68-fold reduction relative to the virus-only group at time zero. At 72 h, NOTCH-1 expression increased markedly in the ZIKV group (11.63-fold), while the IgG+/ZIKV group showed reduced expression (5.44-fold lower) compared to the virus-only group. The IgG + only group maintained stable NOTCH-1 levels at baseline but exhibited a 4.01-fold increase at 72 h relative to control. At MOI 0.01, neurospheres showed minimal changes in NOTCH-1 expression at 24 h post-exposure.

At 24 h post-exposure, HES-1 expression was reduced 2.08-fold in the ZIKV group. Neurospheres pre-exposed to IgG + serum showed a 3.15-fold reduction, while those exposed only to serum exhibited a 5.36-fold decrease (p = 0.0393). At 72 h, the virus-only group maintained a significant 6.11-fold reduction (p = 0.0216 vs. control). Conversely, groups exposed to ZIKV in the presence of IgG + demonstrated upregulation of HES-1, with increases up to 5.69-fold relative to baseline (p = 0.004). Similar expression patterns were observed at MOI 0.01, with significant reductions in the IgG+/ZIKV and IgG + groups compared to control (p = 0.026 and p = 0.015, respectively). After 72 h, HES-1 levels in the virus-only group returned to baseline, whereas IgG + co-exposed groups showed significant upregulation. At MOI 0.001, marked downregulation of HES-1 was noted in both ZIKV and IgG + groups (p < 0.0001), with no significant differences at 72 h. Temporal analysis confirmed significant changes in IgG+/ZIKV and IgG + groups (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0087, respectively) (Fig. 2B).

At MOI 0.1, immediate HEY-1 expression remained unchanged in the virus-only group. However, exposure to IgG + serum caused significant downregulation, with an 8.54-fold reduction in the IgG + only group (p = 0.025 vs. control) and a 4.79-fold reduction in the IgG+/ZIKV group (p = 0.0011 vs. control). After 72 h, HEY-1 expression increased 2.7-fold in the virus-only group (p = 0.016 vs. IgG+), and 3.59-fold in the IgG+/ZIKV group compared to the IgG + only group (p = 0.0074). Temporal analysis showed an 8.38-fold increase in HEY-1 expression in the IgG+/ZIKV group from baseline to 72 h (p = 0.0026), while the IgG + only group maintained suppressed expression levels (3.54-fold lower than control). At MOI 0.01, minor changes in HEY-1 expression were observed in the virus and IgG+/ZIKV groups, whereas the IgG + only group exhibited a pronounced 8.54-fold reduction at baseline (p = 0.0012 vs. control). After 72 h, HEY-1 expression increased in the virus and IgG+/ZIKV groups, while remaining reduced in the IgG + group (p = 0.0089 and p = 0.024, respectively).

At MOI 0.001, both IgG+/ZIKV and IgG + groups exhibited significant downregulation of HEY-1 expression at baseline, with reductions of 7.06-fold and 8.54-fold, respectively, compared to control (p = 0.0012 and p = 0.0004) and virus-only groups (p = 0.0004 and p = 0.0001). At 72 h, gene expression increased 3.49-fold in the virus-only group and 2.84-fold in the IgG+/ZIKV group. The IgG + group maintained suppressed HEY-1 expression, 3.54-fold lower than control (p = 0.0406 vs. control, p = 0.0030 vs. virus, and p = 0.0056 vs. IgG+/ZIKV). Temporal analysis revealed a 10.55-fold increase in HEY-1 expression for the IgG+/ZIKV group (p = 0.0003) and a 5-fold increase for the IgG + only group (p = 0.0243). (Fig. 2C).

GCSF and IL-10 expression

At MOI 0.1, GCSF levels in culture supernatants increased immediately following exposure to IgG + serum (IgG+ and IgG+/ZIKV groups). After 72 h, GCSF levels remained largely stable in the ZIKV and IgG+/ZIKV groups, while a reduction was observed in the IgG + only group. At MOI 0.01, no significant change in GCSF levels was detected immediately post-exposure, though a slight increase was noted in the serum-only group. After 72 h, GCSF levels decreased in the serum-only group. At MOI 0.001, minimal variation in GCSF was observed at baseline across groups, with reductions evident in all groups after 72 h (Fig. 3). A slight reduction in IL-10 levels was observed in groups exposed to IgG + serum at MOI 0.1. Similarly, at MOI 0.01, IL-10 expression decreased modestly, especially in neurospheres exposed to IgG + serum. At the highest dilution, the virus-only group also exhibited reduced IL-10 levels, approaching the minimal expression observed in the IgG+/ZIKV and IgG + groups at other concentrations. (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the risk of damage from the anti-ZIKV antibody complex in neurosphere cultures obtained from the SVZ of neonatal rats. To simulate a re-exposure to ZIKV, neurospheres were pre-exposed to IgG serum reactive to ZIKV and then incubated with the virus for 24 h. Some studies suggest that ZIKV has the potential to alter the morphology of neurospheres and brain organoids, leading to changes in phenotype and a size reduction, indicating increased precursor cell death [10,11].

Irregularities and size differences between neurospheres are common physiological variables observed after the application of different isolation protocols. Confocal microscopy studies revealed variations in cell size, viability, cytoplasmic content, and presence and distribution of active mitochondria in neurospheres derived from human embryonic stem cells. Furthermore, comparison of neurospheres derived from the same brain demonstrated significant heterogeneity regarding shape, structural regularity, and presence of hair cells on their surface [12]. Our results of neurosphere obtaining showed morphological variability in the total area and irregularity even before exposure to IgG + serum or ZIKV. However, IgG + serum for ZIKV appeared to exhibit a protective potential on the cells, resulting in a significant decrease in neurosphere irregularity when exposed to lower virus titration (MOI 0.001). ZIKV infection of neurospheres induces a phenotype characterized by abnormalities such as irregular sphere formation and cell detachment, as well as additional alterations including cell death and growth impairment. These findings demonstrate that ZIKV is capable of disrupting neurosphere formation and reducing the growth of brain organoids, indicating that ZIKV infection may lead to severe damage to embryonic neurodevelopment [10]. In this context, prior exposure to IgG-positive serum may exert a protective role or mitigate the damage caused by ZIKV infection in the developing brain.

The expression of the NOTCH protein is essential for a variety of fundamental biological processes within an organism. Insufficient or absent NOTCH expression can contribute to the development of several diseases, including neuronal disorders. Genes such as NOTCH-1, along with NOTCH-3, play critical roles in neuronal cell differentiation and the formation of structures like the dentate gyrus [13]. Additionally, maintaining consistent expression of the NOTCH-1 gene is vital for promoting nerve cell growth, as this protein is integral to the development of the central nervous system [14].

In our findings, we observed that ZIKV infection leads to an increase in NOTCH-1 expression as early as 24 h post-infection, with this elevated expression persisting through to the final time point analyzed (72 h). This effect may be attributed to the intrinsic properties of these cells, which depend on tightly regulated developmental pathways for their growth. ZIKV infection appears to disrupt the maintenance and proliferation of stem cells, often altering genes involved in development, particularly NOTCH [14]. Abnormal NOTCH-1 expression has been linked to several pathologies, including bicuspid aortic valve, astrocytic hypertrophy, and Alzheimer's disease [[15], [16], [17]]. In our study, we observed a significant alteration in NOTCH-1 expression, with levels notably higher than those in the control group. It is possible that, under the influence of ZIKV, these cells initiated a premature differentiation process, resulting in increased NOTCH-1 expression. This phenomenon could contribute to the depletion of progenitor cells [18]. Our findings are consistent with recent studies indicating that ZIKV preferentially infects neural progenitor cells (NPCs), leading to enhanced NOTCH-1 expression and impairing the viability and growth of these cells [19]. Furthermore, recent research has shown that ZIKV promotes the degradation of the Numb protein, which acts as an inhibitor of Notch signaling [20]. This mechanism may contribute to the observed increase in NOTCH-1 expression induced by ZIKV infection.

HES-1 functions as a crucial transcriptional repressor in cell proliferation and differentiation during embryogenesis [21]. Studies highlight the significance of HES-1 expression in young cells, as reduced expression of this gene during gestation or shortly after birth can lead to severe neurological defects in rodents, potentially resulting in death [22]. Additionally, research has shown that low levels of HES-1 expression in mice cause hypoplasia in certain organs due to the depletion of precursor cells [23], emphasizing the need to maintain regular HES-1 levels in precursor cells. In our study, exposure of neurospheres to ZIKV resulted in a persistent decrease in HES-1 expression at higher virus titers (MOI 0.1 and 0.01). Thus, ZIKV appears to directly decrease the expression of this gene. Conversely, IgG + serum reactive to ZIKV was found to modulate expression levels, with HES-1 expression returning to normal levels over time following ZIKV exposure. Given that HES-1 is involved in the proper regulation of embryonic neurogenesis and neural tube formation, IgG + reactive to ZIKV may play a neuroprotective role by inhibiting the downregulation of this gene upon re-exposure to the virus. Subsequently, when we assessed the expression levels of the HEY-1 gene, which is also involved in embryonic development and neurogenesis, we found that it was not significantly affected by exposure to ZIKV at any of the viral titers. Surprisingly, only the neurosphere group exposed to IgG + serum for ZIKV exhibited a persistent decrease in HEY-1 expression levels. Acutely, a reduction in gene expression was also observed in the group initially exposed to IgG + serum and then incubated with ZIKV, but this decrease was only noted at the 0-h time point, with expression levels returning to baseline by 72 h after virus exposure.

IL-10 plays a critical role in maintaining tissue homeostasis by limiting excessive inflammatory responses, positively regulating innate immunity, and promoting tissue repair mechanisms. As an anti-inflammatory cytokine, IL-10 may serve a protective function in neurodevelopment by modulating immune responses during ZIKV infection. Elevated IL-10 levels could potentially reduce the inflammatory damage induced by ZIKV, although specific studies on the direct effects of IL-10 in this context remain limited [24,25].

Our study revealed a trend toward reduced IL-10 levels in neurospheres exposed to IgG-positive serum, either alone or in combination with ZIKV exposure. IL-10 regulates neurodevelopment by maintaining neural progenitors in an undifferentiated state, upregulating pro-neural gene markers, and downregulating neuronal gene expression. This mechanism ultimately reduces neuronal differentiation and impairs endogenous neurogenesis in the subventricular zone of the adult brain [26].

Conclusions

In conclusion, while ZIKV IgG+ serum exhibited a modest protective effectdemonstrated by a reduction in morphological irregularities in neurospheres at its lowest concentration. Morphological analysis alone was insufficient to fully clarify the impact of ZIKV on neurosphere integrity. Notably, exposure to the highest concentration of ZIKV resulted in a significant increase in NOTCH-1 expression. In contrast, neurospheres pretreated with ZIKV IgG+ serum displayed notable neuroprotection upon subsequent viral exposure, with NOTCH-1 levels remaining comparable to those of the control group.Moreover, HES-1 expression was sensitive to microenvironmental changes, with reduced levels observed in all groups at 0 h post-exposure. However, in the IgG+ serum group, HES-1 expression returned to baseline levels after 72 h of ZIKV exposure, suggesting a potential modulatory effect. Additionally, HEY-1 expression showed a progressive decline over time in the IgG+ serum group. Interestingly, no significant changes were observed in GCSF or IL-10 levels in neurospheres previously exposed to IgG+ serum and subsequently to ZIKV, nor in those exposed solely to the virus.
