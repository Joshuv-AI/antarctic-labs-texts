CNN-based compensation of faulty planar phased-array radiation patterns
Authors: Bendref Mansour Djassem, Mouloud Challal, Robert Staraj, Hamid R. Abdolmohammadi, Nima Khosravi, Adel Oubelaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phased-array antennas experience severe radiation pattern degradation when elements fail. Traditional compensation methods, such as genetic algorithms and particle swarm optimization are computationally expensive, requiring 120–300 s of iterative optimization per failure event. This paper proposes a novel convolutional neural network (CNN) framework for rapid, pattern-level compensation of faulty planar phased arrays. The CNN processes a 64 × 64 orthographic 2D radiation pattern image (azimuth–elevation) from a 4 × 4 array with one failed element and one fixed reference element. It directly predicts recovery phase shifts for the 14 remaining elements to restore the original beam pattern. Trained on 8,000 CST-generated patterns with randomized phases, the CNN achieves sub-degree accuracy (Mean Absolute Error (MAE) = 6.1 × 10− 3 (rad) and high fidelity (R² = 0.97). Quantitative and visual results demonstrate effective recovery of main-beam characteristics and sidelobe suppression, closely matching intact-array performance. Crucially, inference takes about 200 ms on a standard GPU—orders of magnitude faster than iterative optimizers, enabling real-time self-healing. In addition, the model reduces average pattern error by 32.1% (± 3.8%) across 120 test cases, with performance ranging from 28.28% to 35.91% improvement in RMSE, demonstrating consistent fault compensation capability, indicating substantial recovery of the degraded radiation pattern. This proof-of-concept demonstrates that CNNs can effectively learn complex pattern-to-phase inverse mappings for faulty arrays, offering a computationally efficient alternative to traditional optimization methods for scenarios where radiation pattern measurements are available. While the current study is limited to single-element failures in simulation, it establishes feasibility for future practical implementations incorporating measurement systems and multi-fault scenarios.

Introduction

Phased-array antennas are critical in modern communication and radar systems due to their agile beamforming capabilities1. However, even a single element failure can severely distort the radiation pattern, degrading system performance. On platforms where physical repair is infeasible (e.g., satellites, remote radar installations), software-based compensation using remaining functional elements becomes necessary2. This motivates the development of rapid compensation algorithms.

In this study, a 4 × 4 planar array with one faulty element and one fixed reference element is considered, leaving fourteen functioning radiators. Upon fault detection, the distorted 2D radiation pattern (elevation vs. azimuth) is acquired from the faulty array, and new excitation phases for the fourteen remaining elements are determined to restore the beam to its original form.

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is proposed to address this problem at the pattern level. The CNN takes as input an orthographic 2D projection (azimuth–elevation map) of the array’s radiation intensity and outputs the fourteen phase shifts for the operable elements. By processing the full two-dimensional pattern, the CNN can learn spatial features of the distortion introduced by the missing element. In contrast to traditional optimizers (which must search iteratively), this learning-based approach yields excitation updates almost instantaneously (approximately 200 ms on standard GPUs). While this speed is promising for future real-time implementations, this pattern-level compensation operates rapidly once trained and eliminates the need for repeated optimization runs. The computational efficiency makes this approach promising for applications where pattern data can be acquired. However, practical deployment on platforms like satellites requires additional considerations regarding pattern measurement infrastructure, which we address in Section V. Ultimately, our contribution focuses on establishing the technical feasibility of CNN-based pattern-to-phase inversion rather than immediate field readiness.

Importantly, unlike Tsai’s earlier study, which dealt with a one-dimensional (linear) array and utilized a fully connected deep neural network (DNN)3, our method handles a planar array and employs a CNN to exploit the two-dimensional pattern structure. This enables a more complete restoration of the original beam in both azimuth and elevation. This approach uses power patterns (real-valued intensity) rather than complex electric-field patterns. This choice simplifies the CNN input (single-channel images vs. two-channel complex data) and aligns with typical measurement systems that directly measure power. While this precludes phase information at the pattern level, the CNN successfully learns the phase-to-power mapping inverse for compensation purposes.

A novel CNN-based method is introduced to learn the inversion of the 2D radiation-pattern distortion caused by a failed element. Given an observed faulty pattern image, the network directly predicts the phase shifts for the remaining fourteen antennas to regenerate a beam close to that of the original intact array.

Classical pattern synthesis for phased arrays relies on either analytical methods or numerical optimization4. Function-based methods use standard analytical weightings (e.g. Fourier-series, Dolph–Chebyshev, Woodward–Lawson, etc.) to create specific beam shapes4,5. These methods are straightforward and efficient when the desired pattern matches the function family, but they are limited: the array must be uniform, and only certain patterns are possible6. For arbitrary or fault-affected patterns, numerical optimization is typically used. Optimization methods formulate pattern deviation as a cost function and apply algorithms (gradient methods, convex solvers, etc.) to minimize error. Though very general, this approach is computationally intensive7. As Tsai et al. note, optimization-based synthesis can handle virtually any pattern, but “the major disadvantage of this method is that the optimization process is time-consuming and requires powerful computing capability”3. In practice, each compensation would require many simulation or solver iterations, which may be unacceptable for rapid fault recovery8–15.

Many researchers have applied evolutionary and swarm algorithms specifically for faulty-array recovery. For example, Saleem et al. used a genetic algorithm (GA) to compensate a 4 × 2 planar array with failed center elements. They computed new phase-only (and amplitude-phase) weights for the remaining elements to restore the main-beam gain and sidelobe level. In their GA-based scheme, the undamaged elements’ weights are iteratively adjusted until the distorted pattern (due to the malfunction) is brought back to match the intended pattern16. Similarly, Munsif and Ullah present a non-dominated sorting multi-objective particle-swarm-optimization (NS-MOPSO) self-healing scheme that recalculates the independent phases of the remaining (working) elements to recover a conformal array’s distorted radiation pattern caused by malfunctioning phase shifters. Applied to a 1 × 7 wedge-shaped conformal array at 2.45 GHz (and tested for surface bends 0°–45°), the NS-MOPSO jointly minimizes a peak-gain error and an MSE-based pattern difference to restore peak gain, first-sidelobe level (FSLL), and HPBW for both single and adjacent centre-element malfunctions; MATLAB/CST simulations and anechoic-chamber measurements show close agreement with the original pattern after correction. The authors report practical settings (300 particles) that balance convergence and complexity, and they note limitations for some double-fault scenarios when the defect phase differences grow large, which can produce suboptimal recovery17. Other works have explored related numerical methods (e.g., quantum PSO, bacterial foraging, or hybrid algorithms), but all share the same drawback: after a fault is detected, a new optimization must run from scratch, taking many iterations (or seconds/minutes of compute) to converge. Such iterative schemes can yield high-quality restoration given time, but they are poorly suited to on-the-fly compensation in delay-sensitive or resource-constrained systems.

In recent years, machine learning has offered a promising alternative for array design and fault recovery. By training on large datasets of patterns and weights, neural networks can learn the inverse mapping from a desired beam to element excitations. This turns a slow iterative problem into a fast inference problem. Reviews of ML in antenna design note that learned models can speed up the design process by orders of magnitude18. For example, Tsai et al. train a deep neural network specifically for faulty-array compensation: when given a “desired” (intact) pattern, their network outputs the needed excitations for the remaining elements. They demonstrate that the network can produce P excitation phases in about 0.1 s once trained, enabling near-instant recovery after a failure. Likewise, King et al. (2024) presented a physics-informed CNN that takes a full 2D beam-pattern image and predicts the complex weights of a rectangular array. Their CNN learns spatial pattern features to map images into amplitude and phase excitations, with a tailored loss to ensure physically consistent radiation19. In general, these supervised learning approaches, using either DNNs or CNNs, have shown that an antenna can learn to adjust its weights directly from pattern data, thus bypassing repeated optimization runs.

Reinforcement learning (RL) and related approaches have also been explored for antenna control. For instance, Zhang et al. (2022) applied a Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) algorithm to control a conformal phased array over a very wide angle. In their work, the RL agent learns a policy that instantaneously selects element weights for beam steering, without an explicit cost-function optimization in each step20. More broadly, researchers have proposed actor-critic and RL methods for high-dimensional beamforming problems20. These methods can adapt to changing objectives or environments, but they generally require careful reward design and long training, and have not been widely applied to static fault-correction problems. In summary, ML-based techniques (supervised DNN/CNN and some RL variants) offer dramatically faster inference once trained compared to classical optimization, at the cost of requiring an offline training phase. Tsai et al. emphasize that their DNN method achieves rapid response and minimal computational overhead” relative to traditional side-lobe reduction techniques3, reflecting the key trade-off.

Prior fault-compensation work with deep learning has typically focused on 1D (linear) arrays3. While recent CNN applications to phased arrays exist, including digital predistortion for multi-beam transmitters21, frequency-invariant beamforming22, and compensation for continuous array imperfections23. These address different problems than discrete element failure recovery. Our work is distinguished by learning direct 2D pattern-to-phase inversion specifically for compensating complete element failures in planar arrays, a capability not previously demonstrated in literature. Our approach is novel in combining a 2D radiation-pattern input with a CNN to output phase corrections for a planar array. In doing so, it leverages spatial convolution to capture distortion features (as in King’s beam-prediction network19, but applies them to the faulty-array context. This stands in contrast to earlier supervised methods that only generated weights for ideal beams or to RL methods that learned dynamic steering policies. By training on examples of faulty patterns, our CNN implicitly learns the inverse transformation needed to “heal” the array. In effect, it automates what classical iterations do, but with a single forward pass. As a result, our method can compute all fourteen phase shifts together in one shot, restoring a 2D pattern rapidly – a capability that, to our knowledge, has not appeared in prior literature.

In summary, the literature shows that classical synthesis (Fourier/Chebyshev) is fast but inflexible, and iterative optimization (GA/PSO) is general but slow17. Deep-learning methods can be extremely fast at runtime18, but previous work has not yet applied CNN-based 2D pattern inversion to faulty-array compensation. Our proposed CNN model fills this gap by learning a direct, low-latency mapping from the distorted 2D pattern to the excitation phases needed for recovery, combining the speed of ML with full-spatial pattern fidelity.

The term “compensation” refers to the system-level objective of restoring pattern performance, not the phase semantics. The CNN predicts absolute excitation phases (not differential corrections) that synthesize a pattern approximating the intact-array performance using only the remaining functional elements. During training, the phases are arbitrary random values; during inference for compensation, they become the optimized excitations to achieve a desired pattern.

We emphasize that the predicted phases are synthesized excitations rather than “compensation phases” in the differential sense (i.e., they are not corrections applied to some baseline). The CNN learns to directly output the phase configuration that will produce a pattern closest to the target.

Unlike traditional optimization methods that require iterative calculations and significant computational resources, our CNN-based approach provides rapid phase compensation predictions directly from the distorted radiation patterns.

The CNN is trained to learn the inverse mapping between 2D far-field gain patterns and the absolute excitation phases that produced them. During training, phases are randomly assigned to the 14 active elements (not optimized for any objective), and the resulting faulty-array pattern is simulated. During inference for compensation, we input a desired target pattern (the original intact-array pattern), and the CNN predicts the absolute phase excitations for the 14 elements that will best approximate this target.

CNN architecture design

Our CNN architecture is specifically designed to exploit the spatial correlations inherent in 2D radiation patterns, as shown in Fig. 3. The network follows a hierarchical feature extraction approach through four convolutional blocks, followed by fully connected layers for phase regression. This design choice is motivated by the CNN’s superior ability to capture local spatial features compared to fully connected Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), as the 2D radiation pattern naturally resembles image data with strong spatial dependencies.

Convolutional feature extraction

The network architecture consists of four sequential convolutional blocks with progressively increasing filter depths:

First Convolutional Block: The input radiation pattern (64 × 64 × 1) passes through a 7 × 7 convolutional layer with 64 filters, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation. A subsequent 3 × 3 convolutional layer with 64 filters further refines the features. This block concludes with 2 × 2 max-pooling and dropout (rate = 0.10) for regularization.

Second Convolutional Block: Features are processed through a 5 × 5 convolutional layer (128 filters), batch normalization, and another 3 × 3 convolutional layer (128 filters). Max-pooling (2 × 2) and dropout (0.15) are applied to reduce spatial dimensions and prevent overfitting.

Third Convolutional Block: Two consecutive 3 × 3 convolutional layers with 256 filters each, separated by batch normalization, extract higher-level features. The block includes 2 × 2 max-pooling and dropout (0.20).

Fourth Convolutional Block: A 3 × 3 convolutional layer with 512 filters captures the most abstract features. Instead of traditional max-pooling, global average pooling (GAP) is applied to reduce the feature maps to a 512-dimensional vector, substantially decreasing the number of parameters while preserving spatial information. Dropout (0.30) is applied for regularization.

Phase Prediction through: Dense Layers. The extracted features from the convolutional blocks are processed through three fully connected layers:

Analysis

CNN-based compensation achieves substantial performance recovery across critical antenna metrics, with recovery rates ranging from 25% to 81.3% depending on the electromagnetic characteristic.

Beam Pointing (81.3% reduction) shows the highest recovery: element failure causes 3.2° beam squint, which the CNN reduces to 0.6°. This spatial correction is critical for tracking and precision targeting, demonstrating that phase redistribution effectively corrects main beam displacement.

Beamwidth Control (76.9% recovery) is similarly effective: HPBW broadens from 14.2° to 16.8° with element failure; CNN compensation narrows this to 14.8°, restoring 95.8% of spatial resolution. The success in both pointing and beamwidth reflects the CNN’s strength in managing spatial characteristics governed primarily by phase relationships.

Sidelobe Suppression (48.5% improvement) shows moderate recovery: faulty elements degrade sidelobe level from − 14.5 dB to − 11.2 dB; CNN compensation restores this to − 12.8 dB, achieving 88.3% of intact suppression capability. While not a complete restoration, the − 12.8 dB level remains adequate for many operational scenarios.

Peak Gain (25% recovery, 96.3% retention) shows limited recovery of the 0.8 dB fault-induced loss, though the 15.6 dBi recovered gain maintains 96.3% of the intact 16.2 dBi performance. This reflects the fundamental constraint that phase-only control cannot fully compensate for reduced physical aperture.

The variation in recovery rates (25–81.3%) is not a CNN limitation but an expected consequence of phase-only compensation physics: metrics dominated by phase relationships (pointing, beamwidth) recover effectively, while amplitude-sensitive characteristics (gain, deep nulls) face inherent constraints. Nevertheless, the compensation achieves operationally significant restoration, transforming a severely degraded faulty array into a system with performance approaching intact operation.

Conclusion

The convolutional architecture provides decisive advantages for this pattern-to-phase inversion task. The 2D radiation pattern exhibits strong spatial correlations that CNNs naturally exploit through localized receptive fields and hierarchical feature extraction. Fully connected networks, by treating the input as a flat vector, discard this structural information and require far more parameters to achieve inferior performance. This comparison validates our architectural choice and demonstrates that CNNs are not merely a preference but a substantively better approach for radiation pattern processing tasks.

Conclusion of CNN approach

This paper introduced a convolutional neural network (CNN) framework that learns the inverse mapping from 2D orthographic radiation patterns to excitation phases for faulty phased arrays. Applied to a 4 × 4 planar array with one failed element, the CNN predicts compensating phase shifts for the remaining 14 elements with sub-degree accuracy (MAE = 6.1 × 10− 3 rad) and strong correlation (R² = 0.98), recovering 28–36% of the fault-induced pattern degradation across 120 simulated test cases.

The computational efficiency of this approach—200 ms inference on a standard GPU—represents a 2–3 order of magnitude speedup over traditional iterative optimization methods (genetic algorithms: 180–300 s, particle swarm optimization: 120–240 s). Furthermore, the CNN architecture requires 18.7× fewer parameters than an equivalent fully connected neural network while achieving 27% better prediction accuracy, demonstrating that convolutional structures are fundamentally better suited for exploiting the spatial correlations inherent in radiation patterns.

This work establishes proof-of-concept feasibility for CNN-based pattern-to-phase inversion in the context of antenna fault compensation. The demonstrated capabilities are achieved under controlled simulation conditions with well-defined constraints: single-element failures at a fixed location, noiseless CST-generated patterns, and a specific 4 × 4 array geometry at 28 GHz. We position this contribution as demonstrating that CNNs can effectively learn complex electromagnetic inverse problems, offering a computationally efficient alternative to traditional optimization when radiation pattern data is available, rather than claiming immediate field deployability.

The primary practical challenge is the requirement for full 2D radiation pattern measurements, which conflicts with the “resource-constrained” contexts often cited for this technology. While our 200 ms inference is rapid, acquiring the necessary measurement data could take days or weeks on operational platforms like satellites. We identified several potential pathways to address this:

Contribution Summary: This work advances the state of knowledge by demonstrating that CNNs can accurately and rapidly perform 2D pattern-to-phase inversion for faulty planar arrays—a capability not previously shown in literature. The comparison to fully connected architectures validates the importance of exploiting spatial structure, and the antenna-level performance metrics (25% gain recovery, 81% beam squint reduction) confirm that pattern-level compensation translates to meaningful electromagnetic performance restoration. While practical deployment requires additional system engineering to address measurement challenges, the core technical feasibility is established, opening pathways for future implementations in satellite, radar, and wireless communication systems where fast, automated fault compensation would enhance reliability and reduce operational costs.
