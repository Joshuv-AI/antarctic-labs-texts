Protofilament-specific nanopatterns of tubulin post-translational modifications regulate the mechanics of ciliary beating
Authors: Gonzalo Alvarez Viar, Nikolai Klena, Fabrizio Martino, Adrian Pascal Nievergelt, Davide Bolognini, Paola Capasso, Gaia Pigino
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryControlling ciliary beating is essential for motility and signaling in eukaryotes. This process relies on the regulation of various axonemal proteins that assemble in stereotyped patterns onto individual microtubules of the ciliary structure. Additionally, each axonemal protein interacts exclusively with determined tubulin protofilaments of the neighboring microtubule to carry out its function. While it is known that tubulin post-translational modifications (PTMs) are important for proper ciliary motility, the mode and extent to which they contribute to these interactions remain poorly understood. Currently, the prevailing understanding is that PTMs can confer functional specialization at the level of individual microtubules. However, this paradigm falls short of explaining how the tubulin code can manage the complexity of the axonemal structure where functional interactions happen in defined patterns at the sub-microtubular scale. Here, we combine immuno-cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), expansion microscopy, and mutant analysis to show that, in motile cilia, tubulin glycylation and polyglutamylation form mutually exclusive protofilament-specific nanopatterns at a sub-microtubular scale. These nanopatterns are consistent with the distributions of axonemal dyneins and nexin-dynein regulatory complexes, respectively, and are indispensable for their regulation during ciliary beating. Our findings offer a new paradigm for understanding how different tubulin PTMs, such as glycylation, glutamylation, acetylation, tyrosination, and detyrosination, can coexist within the ciliary structure and specialize individual protofilaments for the regulation of diverse protein complexes. The identification of a ciliary tubulin nanocode by cryo-ET suggests the need for high-resolution studies to better understand the molecular role of PTMs in other cellular compartments beyond the cilium.Keywords: tubulin posttranslational modification, motile cilia, tubulin code, cryo-ET, U-ExM, tubulin glycylation, tubulin polyglutamylation, Chlamydomonas, cilia motility, microtubule protofilaments, molecular nanopatterns

Introduction

Cilia are thin, elongated, microtubule-based organelles with key conserved roles in organismal development and homeostasis.1,2,3,4 Their correct function relies on the precise organization of different macromolecular components along a microtubular scaffold called the axoneme.5,6,7,8,9,10 Axonemal protein complexes, such as dynein arms and the nexin-dynein regulatory complex (N-DRC), stably bind on the A-tubule of the microtubule doublet and transiently interact with the B-tubule of the adjacent microtubule doublet to perform their functions. Both stable and transient interactions form repetitive patterns along the axoneme. For instance, outer dynein arms (ODAs) bind to the A-tubule on protofilaments A07 and A08 every 24 nm and interact with protofilaments B05–08 of the adjacent B-tubule with their microtubule binding domains (MTBDs), while the N-DRC binds to A01–03 every 96 nm and interacts with protofilament B09 of the neighboring B-tubule11,12,13,14 (Figure 1A). Historically, microtubules were considered mere structural components of the axoneme with no regulatory function in ciliary beating. However, cilia are highly enriched in post-translational modifications (PTMs), many of which are located on the C-terminal tail of tubulin lying on the exterior of microtubules. Tubulin PTMs can functionally differentiate subpopulations of microtubules or individual microtubules in the cell.15 Given the ubiquitous interaction of different axonemal proteins with microtubules, PTMs could be expected to contribute to the assembly and/or function of axonemal and ciliary components. Although PTMs have been shown to be required for proper ciliary function,16,17,18,19,20,21,22 the molecular mechanisms remain elusive.

Work from our lab has shown that tubulin glycylation is required for proper ciliary beating in mouse sperm, as it contributes to the regulation of axonemal dynein activity.23 However, it remains unclear whether this is a consequence of a direct physical interaction between the MTBDs of axonemal dyneins and glycylated protofilaments. We hypothesize that other PTMs might be involved in the specific regulation of other axonemal components. To achieve such a spatially constrained regulatory function, PTMs would have to be distributed over the microtubule surface forming a pattern at the sub-microtubular scale that is consistent with the distribution of functional interactions between axonemal components and individual protofilaments.

Previous studies that have investigated the distribution of PTMs in the axoneme of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii showed differences in modifications between the central pair and microtubule doublets, and even between the A-tubule and the B-tubule of each doublet.24,25,26,27 Based on immunogold labeling of microtubule doublets and negative staining electron microscopy (EM), it was predicted that polyglutamylation and detyrosination are found on distinct regions of the B-tubule.25 However, these studies were limited in resolution and/or labeling efficiency. Thus, the regulatory role of tubulin PTMs in relation to the function of different axonemal protein complexes remains largely unknown. In this study, we address this issue by resolving the distribution of two tubulin PTMs at the nanoscale, and we test our hypothesis that the presence of a “tubulin code” nanopattern would reflect the axonemal architecture. Our results demonstrate that a conserved nanopattern of polyglutamylation and glycylation in the axoneme is required for the proper function of the N-DRC and axonemal dyneins, respectively, thus shedding light on the role of tubulin PTMs in ciliary mechanics.

Results

Tubulin glycylation and polyglutamylation form distinct protofilament-specific patterns over the B-tubule of the microtubule doublet in Chlamydomonas

To test our hypothesis that axonemal tubulin PTMs’ distribution reflects the organization of the axoneme, we developed a protocol to visualize their localization at the nanoscale. We incubated axonemes with antibodies against different tubulin PTMs, plunge-froze these samples, and performed cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) and sub-tomogram averaging (Figure S1A, see STAR Methods for details). This approach resolved the binding site of antibodies against different PTMs in electron density models of the axonemal 96-nm repeat, revealing the location of specific tubulin PTMs in flagella/cilia of Chlamydomonas cells. Wild-type control axonemes (without antibodies) did not show any decoration over the exposed surface of the microtubule doublet (Figure 1B). Upon addition of Glypep1 antibodies, against glycylated tubulin, we observed obvious extra densities over most of the exposed microtubule surface, comprising protofilaments A08–10, B01–08, and B10 (Figure 1C). The antibody decoration clearly followed the tubulin dimer repeat (Figure 1C1), reflecting the expected antigenicity of the antibody against glycylated β-tubulin, which has been shown to be present mostly on the C-terminal tails of β-tubulin.28 Although the MTBDs of axonemal dyneins would compete with the antibodies for their binding site on glycylated protofilaments, the presence of antibody labeling in our averages demonstrates that antibodies show a stronger affinity than MTBDs in these conditions.

As a negative control to validate the specificity of the antibody, we analyzed axonemes from the glycylation knockout (KO) strain ttll3- (Chlamydomonas Library Project [CLiP] library, LMJ.RY0402.124984, Cre03.g145447, chromosome 329) (Figure S1B). The structure of the 96-nm repeat, with the typical microtubule-associated axonemal complexes, appeared identical between the ttll3− strain and wild type (Figures 1D and S2A). Consistently, ttll3::BSD axonemes lacking tubulin glycylation showed a normal axoneme and complete microtubule decoration, including small filamentous microtubule internal proteins (MIPs) (Figures S2B and S2C). As expected, upon addition of Glypep1 antibodies to ttll3− axonemes, no decoration was found (Figure 1E). These data show that glycylation occupies protofilaments A08–10 and most of the B-tubule surface, with the exception of protofilament B09 (Figures 1C2 and 1J). Interestingly, the MTBDs of inner dynein arms (IDAs) and ODAs interact with protofilaments of the B-tubule (B10 and B05–08, respectively).11,12,13,14 Thus, the MTBDs of axonemal dyneins interact directly with glycylated protofilaments. Consistent with this, the absence of glycylation in mouse sperm axonemes causes defective ODA and IDA activity and an impaired motility phenotype.23

Tubulin glycylation and polyglutamylation are thought to compete for the same initiation sites,30,31,32 and tubulin polyglutamylation is predicted to be located preferentially on the B-tubule.24,25,26,27 To investigate the localization of polyglutamylation, we incubated isolated axonemes with polyE antibodies raised against polyglutamate chains and observed a clear enrichment along protofilament B09 (Figures 1D–1G and S3). The density generated by the antibodies followed a clear 8-nm repeat, consistent with the observation that polyglutamylation mostly occurs on the C-terminal tails of α-tubulin in the Chlamydomonas axoneme.28 Despite the size of the antibodies’ densities and the unknown workspace of the modified C-terminal tails of axonemal tubulin, polyE antibodies are clearly located at and connected to protofilament B09 (Figure S3). As a negative control for the localization of the polyE antibody, we used the strain tpg1, whose levels of axonemal tubulin polyglutamylation are significantly decreased as a result of knocking out the polyglutamylase TTLL9, which is an elongase of polyglutamylation chains in Chlamydomonas.20 Analogous to the glycylation KO mutant (ttll3−), the axonemal 96-nm repeat structure of tpg1 appeared normal (Figures 1H and S2A). Also the structure of the polyglutamylation KO mutant ttll9::NAT, phenotypically tested by western blot (Figure S1C), appeared normal, including the presence of small filamentous MIPs (Figure S2B). Upon addition of polyE antibodies, tpg1 showed a much-reduced signal on B09 protofilament (Figure 1I), consistent with the phenotype of this strain. Protofilament B09 is near to the IDA MTBDs and stalks, which are often visible in the averages of wild-type axonemes. Because of the positioning of the polyglutamylation-associated signal over protofilament B09, we wanted to exclude the potential contribution of these binding domains and stalks. Therefore, we used the strain ida5, which lacks four out of eight IDAs,33 and immunolabeled its axonemes with polyE and Glypep1 antibodies separately. Imaging reinforced the results obtained with wild-type axonemes (Figure S4), where tubulin polyglutamylation is enriched over protofilament B09 and glycylation occupies the rest of the exposed microtubular surface.

Iterative expansion microscopy experiments were carried out, verifying the presence of polyE all along microtubule doublets as a continuous signal, which indicates a repeat smaller than 25 nm (the approximate resolution of our iterative expansion microscopy approach) (Figure S5).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that it is possible to precisely locate tubulin PTMs at nanometric level on native microtubular assemblies and reveal that tubulin glycylation and polyglutamylation form mutually exclusive protofilament-specific patterns in the Chlamydomonas axoneme (Figures 1A, 1J, and 1K).

The tubulin glycylation and polyglutamylation nanopattern is conserved in motile cilia from algae to mammals

To test the conservation of this pattern in motile cilia of other eukaryotes, we isolated mouse trachea respiratory cilia and performed the same immuno-cryo-ET procedure. Analysis of control axonemes (without antibodies) showed the typical structure of the 96-nm repeat of mouse respiratory cilia34 (Figures 2A and S6). Upon addition of Glypep1 antibodies, most of the exposed microtubular surface was decorated by extra densities (protofilaments A08–10, B01–08, and B10) (Figures 2B and 2C) with protofilament and repeat patterns that recapitulated what we observed in Chlamydomonas (Figures 1B1 and 1E). Upon addition of polyE antibodies, a clear decoration was observed specifically over protofilament B09 (Figures 2E and 2F). The labeling in mouse trachea axonemes is slightly less dense than for Chlamydomonas, potentially due to differences in the sample preparations. Mouse samples contain more contaminants, such as cell debris, that are likely to contribute to the noise in the images and to reduce the visibility of the antibody signal. However, the complementarity of the glycylation and polyglutamylation nanopatterns is conserved between distantly related species like algae and mice, suggesting it is a characteristic of all motile cilia. Such a conserved pattern calls for a fundamental role of these two PTMs in the correct function of motile cilia across species.

How are the tubulin glycylation and polyglutamylation nanopatterns generated?

The enzymes that generate tubulin PTMs are thought to be spatially and temporally regulated to modulate the interaction of proteins with microtubules in different parts of the cell and at different moments of the cell cycle.15 Our results show that modifying enzymes are able to generate precise nanopatterns of glycylation and polyglutamylation at the level of a single protofilament. We then set out to investigate how this tubulin PTMs pattern is formed during ciliary assembly, using ultrastructural expansion microscopy of regrowing cilia of Chlamydomonas. We simultaneously labeled acetylated tubulin, tubulin, and either polyglutamylated or glycylated tubulin at different stages of ciliary regeneration (Figure 3). Ciliary tips, where tubulin dimers are added to the plus ends of A-microtubules, showed an intense signal for tubulin antibodies, while none of the modifications we studied were present in this area (Figures 3A, 3C, 3E1, 3E2, S7, and S9). These results were consistent in both growing and steady-state ciliary tips (Figures 3A, 3C, S7, and S9). During ciliary growth, the tubulin acetylation signal was located just proximally with respect to the unmodified tip area. Glycylation and polyglutamylation are instead located proximally with respect to acetylation leading edge, indicating that acetylation is established before glycylation and polyglutamylation (Figures 3A and 3C). The intensity of the fluorescence signal associated with tubulin acetylation along the cilium was constant during ciliary assembly and in full-length cilia (Figure 3F). Similarly, axonemal polyglutamylation levels remain constant regardless of the ciliary growth stage (Figures 3B and S7). On the other hand, tubulin glycylation slowly builds up along the axoneme during ciliary growth, showing a clear difference in relative intensity between short (0–5 μm) and long (>5 μm) cilia (Figures 3D and S8). Based on these results, we propose that polyglutamylation enzymes carry out their activity mostly at the tip of newly assembled B-tubules and are able to quickly modify all polyglutamylation targets, while glycylation enzymes take a significantly longer time to fully decorate the remaining sites on the B-tubule and A-tubule lattices that we showed by immuno-cryo-ET.

Lack of glycylation affects phototaxis and swimming of Chlamydomonas

We previously showed that lack of tubulin glycylation impairs axonemal dynein regulation and ciliary motility in mouse sperm cells.23 Our immuno-cryo-ET experiments reveal a direct interaction of the MTBD of axonemal dyneins with glycylated protofilaments (Figure 4A). Thus, we checked if the lack of tubulin glycylation affects the swimming of Chlamydomonas cells. The ttll3− mutant from the CLiP library was an inappropriate background strain for swimming experiments, as the background strain itself shows significantly reduced swimming velocities, compared with wild-type (CC-124). Therefore, we generated a suitable CRISPR-Cas mutant ttll3::BSD from CC-124, which lacked tubulin glycylation in its axoneme (Figure S9A). The lack of additional insertions due to CRISPR-Cas treatment was demonstrated by whole-genome sequencing of the mutant (see STAR Methods for details). Additionally, the lack of expression of the TTLL3 gene in this mutant was tested by qPCR (Figures S9C and S9D). Ttll3::BSD cells showed an altered phototactic response that formed a different pattern, compared with wild type (Figures 4B and S10A), and did not manage to accumulate cells at one side of the plate even after long times of exposure to light (Figure S10B).

We then recorded high-speed movies of Chlamydomonas cells in liquid culture, which allowed us to accurately measure cell position, beating frequency, and swimming speed. Surprisingly, ttll3::BSD cells swam faster than wild type (Figures 4C and S9B). Although the beat frequency was lower for ttll3::BSD cells when compared with wild type (Figure 4D), ttll3::BSD cells showed higher forward displacement per beat cycle (Figure 4E), explaining why this mutant swims faster than wild type. These swimming parameters were restored to wild-type levels in the rescue strain (Figures 4C–4E and S9B). These results now show that the phenotype in Chlamydomonas and the previously observed phenotype of ttll3,8−/− mice23 is mediated by the loss of a direct physical interaction between glycylated protofilaments and the axonemal dynein MTBD.

N-DRC and the polyglutamylated protofilament function as a sliding joint that ensures axonemal integrity during the ciliary beat cycle

Another axonemal component that directly interacts with the B-tubule is the N-DRC, which is known to passively bridge neighboring microtubule doublets and to contribute to the regulation of axonemal dyneins.35,36 The N-DRC specifically interacts with protofilament B09 (Figure 5A), which our results show to be highly enriched in tubulin polyglutamylation. This is consistent with studies that link the interaction between the N-DRC and polyglutamylation to axonemal integrity during ciliary beating.35,36 While our analysis showed a continuous decoration (once every tubulin dimer) of the B09 protofilament by polyglutamylation, consistent with the observation that polyglutamylation mostly occurs on the C-terminal tails of α-tubulin in the Chlamydomonas axoneme,28 a previous study proposed that there is a single site for tubulin polyglutamylation per 96-nm repeat.27 This polyglutamylated site would provide a punctiform distribution of negative charges that would function as an electrostatic anchor for the interaction with the positively charged lobular region of the N-DRC.37 In this scenario, and because doublets have been shown to slide during ciliary beating,38 it was proposed that the N-DRC would stretch and/or tilt along the axoneme’s long axis39 to preserve the interaction of its lobular region with the neighboring microtubule doublets and thus the stability of the axonemal structure. In order to test these hypotheses, we performed cryo-ET on isolated axonemes whose motility is induced by the addition of 150 μM ATP (reactivated axonemes) to simultaneously and precisely quantify inter-microtubule sliding (Figure 5B). This allowed us to visualize the speculated conformational change of the N-DRC structure during microtubule sliding (Figure 5C). Cumulative sliding displacements between neighboring doublets reached values of around 80 nm measured over 1.5 μm of ciliary length upon bending at physiologically relevant curvatures (Figure 5B). Thus, we estimated that in distal sections of the axoneme, a single 96-nm repeat interacts with at least three contiguous axonemal repeats from neighboring doublets throughout a breaststroke beating cycle. We then analyzed the structure of N-DRC complexes subjected to positive, negative, and no sliding by sub-tomogram averaging. At the resolution determined, the N-DRC did not tilt or stretch along the microtubule axis upon inter-microtubule doublet sliding (Figure 5C). We cannot exclude subtle small changes in the conformation or posture of the N-DRC at higher resolution, which would be anyway negligible compared with the measured sliding of the microtubule doublets. We conclude that to preserve the interaction between the N-DRC and the neighboring doublet during the ciliary beat cycle, the positively charged lobular region of the N-DRC slides over the continuous distribution of negative charges generated by tubulin polyglutamylation all along protofilament B09 (Figure 5A, right). These results change the way the interaction between the N-DRC and microtubules is understood, providing a new framework for physical modeling of ciliary beating and its regulation.

Discussion

With this work, we show that a combination of immunolabeling and cryo-ET can reveal the nanometric location of tubulin PTMs within higher-order microtubule structures in their native context. Using this method, we show that the tubulin code forms a protofilament-specific nanopattern in cilia that reflects the molecular organization of axonemal structures and contributes to their regulation.

The mutually exclusive nanopatterning of axonemal tubulin polyglutamylation and glycylation is striking. The distribution of glycylated protofilaments allows them to directly interact with the MTBDs of dynein arms. Dynein arms are known to be the main driver of the beating activity of cilia, contributing to its amplitude, frequency, and waveform.6,40 We and others have shown that the lack of tubulin glycylation can affect the motile function of cilia in zebrafish and mice.18,23,41 Consistent with this, our experiments in Chlamydomonas show an effect on ciliary-based motility upon depletion of tubulin glycylation: mutant cells swam faster than wild-type cells, although their beat frequency was slightly reduced. The fact that these cells advance more with every beat cycle suggests that they have a stronger powerstroke, which might be caused by an altered interaction between the MTBD of dyneins and the microtubular surface. Although faster, ttll3::BSD cells appear to have an impaired fine control over their movement as their phototactic response is affected by the lack of glycylation. These results indicate that the described distribution of glycylation protofilaments is required for the regulation of the activity of axonemal dyneins through a direct physical interaction with their MTBDs. Recently, a Chlamydomonas mutant lacking the C-terminal tail of β-tubulin has been shown to also lack tubulin glycylation and to have paralyzed flagella.28 The mutant is also characterized by the loss of the central apparatus,28 which explains their paralysis. This can be explained by the role of the C-terminal tail in regulating microtubule severing enzyme activity42 and the importance of this activity for central pair formation.43 However, this mutant is not useful to dissect the role of glycylation alone in the control of axonemal beating.

Our data in motile cilia show that depletion of tubulin polyglutamylation or glycylation does not disrupt axonemal assembly in Chlamydomonas, indicating that these modifications do not play a major role in the incorporation of axonemal components onto the algae ciliary microtubules. Nevertheless, tubulin polyglutamylation is important for axonemal integrity and proper ciliary motility through its interaction with the N-DRC.35,36,44 The electrostatic interaction between positively charged residues in the DRC4 protein N terminus37 and negatively charged glutamates from polyglutamylated tubulin C termini has been shown to contribute to the physical integrity of the axoneme.27,45

It has been proposed that polyglutamylation occurs at a specific location of the 96-nm repeat facing the N-DRC of the neighboring microtubule doublet.27 This would mean that only one tubulin is polyglutamylated per 96-nm repeat. If we were to consider a 96-nm-long section of the entire axoneme, including 9 microtubule doublets and the central pair microtubules, there would be approximately a total of 5,592 tubulins. Having 9 tubulins polyglutamylated would represent 0.16% of the total tubulin, while the decoration over the whole protofilament that we propose would imply having 108 polyglutamylated tubulins. This would represent 1.9% of the total tubulin, which is very close to an estimation from biochemical data of a 1.5% of polyglutamylated tubulin present in the cilium.27

According to the polyglutamylation distribution previously proposed,27 the N-DRC would have to act as a linear spring element, rigidly anchored at both ends,39 that tilts or stretches during ciliary beating to accommodate microtubule sliding. However, our results indicate that the N-DRC does not act as a simple linear elastic element. Instead, the positively charged globular region of the N-DRC glides over the negatively charged B09 protofilament from the neighboring microtubule. We propose that the interaction between the N-DRC and the polyglutamylated protofilament (B09) acts as a sliding joint that ensures a proper lateral interaction between microtubules throughout a whole ciliary beating cycle. This sliding joint energetically favors microtubule motions parallel to the ciliary axis, while it prevents large relative displacement perpendicular to the ciliary axis.

The model described here could explain the lower speed and beat frequency observed in tpg1 cells.20 In this model, tubulin polyglutamylation is required for proper ciliary beating because it couples the dynamics of microtubule sliding and microtubule bending through its interaction with the N-DRC, which in turn participates in dynein regulation. Changes in intraciliary calcium concentration, which are known to influence beating frequency and waveform,46 could lead to a modulation of the electrostatic interaction of the N-DRC with the neighboring microtubule doublet. These results suggest the existence of a flagellar beat control mechanism by which inter-microtubule sliding force generation can be locally modulated by the electromechanical interaction between the N-DRC and the B-tubule, providing a new and alternative answer to the problem of ciliary beat regulation. The evolutionarily conserved nature of the N-DRC and of the polyglutamylation pattern can explain the motility phenotypes found in Chlamydomonas47 and mice.19

This study demonstrates that a specific polyglutamylation/glycylation pattern is correlated with the position and regulation of axonemal components, namely dynein arms and the N-DRC. The question that naturally arises is how the modifying enzymes are able to generate such fine patterns. The enzymes involved in tubulin polyglutamylation and glycylation are assumed to compete for their initiation sites on glutamate residues present in the tubulin C terminus.30,31,32 Our results strongly suggest that the modifying enzymes for tubulin polyglutamylation and glycylation specifically recognize their targets and shed some light on the sequence of events giving rise to the precise nanopatterning of PTMs. We found that the distal tip of both steady-state and growing flagella is enriched in unmodified tubulin. The very distal part of the axoneme is known to undergo a continuous tubulin turnover also in steady-state cilia.48 Thus, the absence of tubulin PTMs in this area might allow for a faster exchange of tubulin dimers at the plus end. Polyglutamylated tubulin signals remain at constant levels along the cilium during assembly, suggesting that most of the polyglutamylases rapidly carry out their activity in the tip area, apparently treadmilling with the growing B-tubule tip. On the other hand, tubulin glycylation accumulates over time along the cilium, indicating a less localized activity of diffusive modifying enzymes. Our data suggest that acetylation is applied to the assembling microtubules before glutamylation. The full decoration of the axoneme by the glycylation enzyme is the last among these three modifications to be completed. Thus, we could infer that protofilament B09 is made unavailable for the TTLL3 enzyme (glycylation) by the early activity of the TTLL9 enzyme (polyglutamylation). Experiments using fluorescently labeled versions of the enzymes involved will be useful to clarify their mode of action during ciliary assembly.

It is possible that TTLL9 recognizes its specific target by site-specific cues that are already present in the assembled microtubule doublets, presumably patterns of MIPs or, alternatively, differential protofilament binding affinities defined by tubulin lattice curvature. The presence of FAP52 and/or FAP90 proteins along protofilament B099 could explain the protofilament-specific activity of modifying enzymes during ciliary assembly. In particular, FAP90 occupies areas in the extraluminal side of the microtubule around protofilament B09, potentially serving as a cue for specific activity of polyglutamylation enzymes. It will be interesting to study whether the lack of these MIPs interferes with the modification of axonemal microtubules. Experiments on the step-by-step assembly and maturation of the axoneme during ciliogenesis might be required to ultimately clarify the sequence of events giving rise to this precise biochemical nanopatterning.

With this study, we reveal that the tubulin code of motile cilia consists of a protofilament-specific nanopattern of tubulin PTMs that is consistent with the organization of the axoneme and the distribution of specific functional interactions between axonemal components and the microtubule. This provides a framework to mechanistically understand the specific role of different tubulin PTMs for the regulation of ciliary components that are required for proper ciliary beating. Here, we focused on the mutually exclusive nanopattern of glycylation and polyglutamylation, but the same approach can be used to investigate other axonemal tubulin PTMs and their potential interactions with other ciliary components, such as the intraflagellar transport machinery (IFT). Similarly, this new understanding of the tubulin code at the nanoscale will revolutionize the way we understand the function of tubulin PTMs in other stable microtubular structures, such as centrioles and cortical microtubules.

Method details

Axonemal isolation from Chlamydomonas reinhardtii

300ml of 4 days old Chlamydomonas culture in liquid TAP were used for each isolation experiment. After centrifugation at 1200g (JLA16.25) for 10 minutes at room temperature (slow break), cells were resuspended in 10mL of chilled 10mM HEPES. Clean cell suspensions were centrifuged at 1500g (rotor JA25.50) for 10 minutes at 4°C (no brake). In order to deciliate the cells, they were resuspended in 10mL of 5mM Dibucaine in chilled HDMS and incubated for 2 minutes on ice. Immediately, 10mL of HDMS containing 1mM DTT, 1μM Aprotinin and 0.5mM EGTA were added. Cell bodies were removed by centrifugation at 1500g (rotor JA25.50) for 20 minutes at 4°C (no brake). The supernatant was centrifuged at 30000g (rotor JA25.50) for 15 minutes at 4°C (max. brake). The resulting flagellar pellet was resuspended in 2ml of HMDEK buffer with 1mM DTT, 1uM Aprotinin and 0.1mM PMSF. The remaining cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 800g for 3 minutes at 4°C (Ependorf, 5415R tabletop centrifuge). Clean flagellar suspensions were demembranated by addition of 1% Igepal and incubation on ice for 20 minutes.

Axonemal isolation from mouse trachea

Fresh 1ml stock 2mg/ml Digitonin solution was previously prepared by heating up to 95°C until the detergent dissolves. Dissected tracheas were obtained in cold PBS at 4°C. Tracheas were immersed into 2ml of deciliation buffer (10mM Tris, 50mM NaCl, 10mM CaCl2, 1mM EDTA, pH adjusted to 7.5 using HCl at 4°C, containing 20μM Digitonin, 7mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1% Protease inhibitor cocktail Sigma Aldrich, P1860). The buffer was gently flushed 20 times through each trachea to boost deciliation for about 5 minutes. Clear supernatant was then collected and centrifuged at 500g for 1 minute at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 5000g for 5 minutes at 4°C to obtain a ciliary pellet. The pellet was resuspended in 20μl of HMDEK buffer containing 1mM DTT, 1uM Aprotinin, 0.1mM PMSF and 1%Igepal.

Reactivation of Chlamydomonas axonemes

Following axonemal isolation, 150μM ATP was added to the axonemal suspension. After inspection under the light microscope of a correct beating behavior of the axonemes in the mentioned condition, they were prepared for plunge freezing as described below. ATP was added just prior to plunging.

Western blotting

Wild-type (CC125), ttll3-(CLiP 124984) and ttll3::BSD strains were cultured for 4 days in liquid TAP as described above. Flagellar suspensions obtained through isolation following the described protocol were loaded in ready-to-use 4-12% electrophoresis gels (NuPAGE Bis-Tris Mini, Invitrogen), together with a molecular weight ladder (PAGERuler Plus or Chameleon Duo). Electrophoresis was carried out in MOPS buffer by applying a constant current of 50mA for 1h (Electrophoresis kit, Invitrogen). Tubulin concentrations were assessed either by using Coomassie staining or acetylated tubulin content. A second gel was loaded with matching sample concentrations. The resulting bands were transferred onto a membrane using iBlot2 dry transfer (iBlot 2 NC or PVDF Mini Stacks) with a 3 steps program of 20V each (7min total run time). After washing it with deionised water, the blotted membrane was immunolabeled using an iBind with the iBind Flex FD solution kit with a primary rabbit antibody (1:10000 dilution) against glycylated tubulin (Glypep1) and (in the case of ttll3::BSD) a primary mouse antibody against acetylated tubulin. Secondary antibodies consisted of infrared-fluorophore-conjugated antibody (1:3000 dilution) against rabbit (LiCor IRDye 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit) and, in the case of ttll3::BSD, an additional secondary (1:4000) antibody against mouse (LiCor IRDye 680LT Goat anti-Mouse). Using a LI-COR fluorescence scanner and Image Studio software, fluorescence images of the tubulin glycylation and acetylation associated channels were acquired and analyzed.

Immunolabeling of axonemes for cryo-ET

Chlamydomonas axonemal suspensions were diluted to a very low concentration to expose isolated axonemes to saturating antibody concentrations. Optimal dilution was achieved when, after quickly loading 4μL of dilution on a glass slide and covering it with a coverslip, approximately 15 axonemes appeared adhered to the glass surface per field of view (∼6650μm2, observation with a 60X oil immersion objective using phase contrast). 1 μL of antibodies against a particular tubulin post-translational modification were added to 100μL of diluted axonemal suspension and incubated for at least 1.5h inside 0.5mL tubes lying over an ice surface prior to plunge-freezing. In the case of mouse trachea axonemes, antibodies were used 1:100 directly on isolated axonemes. Antibodies used:

polyE: glutamate chains (>4E), AG-25B-0030 Adpipogen

Glypep1: glycylated β-tubulin, Gadadhar et al.15

Plunge freezing

A Leica Automatic Plunge Freezer EM GP machine was used to perform plunge freezing as it comes with an integrated humidity control chamber. Cu supported 3.5/1 Holey Carbon grids (Quantifoil) were glow discharged on both sides for six seconds each using atmospheric air in order to render them more hydrophilic. In the meantime, the plunge freezer chamber temperature was set to 32°C in order to obtain and maintain high humidity (above 80%). The plunge freezer was set to detect the wetting of the filter paper (Whatman) upon blotting (around 2 seconds blotting time, no automatic plunging), which was mounted on one side only with the purpose of blotting from the grid back side. The sample was loaded on both sides (3.5μL total) and incubated for 30 seconds before the addition of 1.5μL of sonicated gold fiducials suspended in PBS, followed by immediate blotting. In many cases automatic blotting was not optimal, requiring swift manual blotting before plunging. Grids were stored in custom made grid-boxes (MPI-CBG Workshop) which were themselves stored in cryogenic tanks.

Cryo-electron tomography

Acquisition of cryo-EM images were done with a 300 kV Thermo Fisher Titan Halo TEM with a field emission gun (FEG) electron source and a Gatan K2 Summit direct electron detector with energy filter using a slit width of 20 eV. To facilitate the location of regions of interest containing well preserved cilia, SerialEM51 was used to generate a full grid overview by automatically acquiring and stitching low magnification (210x) images. Tilt series were acquired with SerialEM on areas of interest at 30,000x nominal image magnification, resulting in a calibrated pixel size of 4.72 Å (counted mode). Tilt series were recorded with 2° increments with a bidirectional tilt scheme from –24° to 42° and from -26° to –42°. The tilt step was varied up to 6° whenever dose limitations applied. The defocus target varied from –1.5 to -5.5μm and the cumulative dose was from 95 to 120 e- per Å 2 per tomogram. Images were acquired in the dose fractionation mode with 0.25 s frame time to a total of around 8 frames per tilt image. Drift was kept below 1nm/s. The frames were aligned using K2Align software, which is based on the MotionCorr algorithm.52 Tilt series alignment (coarse alignment based on cross-correlation of images followed by gold fiducial model based local alignment) and tomographic reconstruction (weighted backprojection) was performed using Etomo from IMOD v.4.10.11-α.53 Contrast transfer function curves were estimated with CTFPLOTTER and corrected by phase-flipping with the software CTFPHASEFLIP, both implemented in IMOD.61 Dose weighted filtering was also performed using the mtffilter command from the IMOD package. To enhance the contrast of macromolecular structures and facilitate visualization/interpretation of tomograms and sub-tomogram picking, individual images were treated as to synthetically remove gold fiducials and linearly binned by two prior to tomographic reconstruction. In some cases, a nonlinear anisotropic diffusion filter by IMOD53 was applied on low-contrast tomograms acquired closer to focus.

Sub-tomogram averaging

IMOD v.4.10.11-α and PEET v1.11.0 were used for sub-tomogram averaging.54 Using 3dmod, included in the IMOD package, tomograms were inspected looking for signs of adequate structural preservation, namely, roundness and straightness of the axonemes and presence of unaltered axonemal components such as radial spokes and dynein arms. To model the location of each 96-nm repeat within tomograms, the coordinates of points of the central axis of A-tubules at the base of every RS2 were determined manually and saved in.mod format (supported by IMOD software). Approximately, at least a thousand of these points were determined per experimental condition. Using PEET (v 1.11.0), each sub-tomogram containing a 96-nm repeat was aligned to a common reference so that all microtubule axes remained parallel to each other with the right polarity. The particles from each microtubule doublet were averaged independently to determine the angles of rotation that should be applied to each microtubule doublet to pre-align it with the common reference, providing a good starting point for automated fine alignment. Missing wedge compensation was used, one single particle was commonly used as an initial reference per experiment. Soft masks outlining the 96-nm repeat structure were used for alignment optimization. The maximum search ranges for translations and rotations of each particle during alignment to iteratively refined references were estimated based on the precision achieved during manual modeling and pre-alignment, normally resulting in values of +-12° around the Y axis, +-6° for the remaining axes and 8 pixels for translations in each direction. This strategy, laborious compared to fully automated sub-tomogram averaging routines, provides human- proofread averages without missing-wedge, chirality or polarity artifacts. Routinely, the final average was generated discarding the worst 10% of particles based on their cross- correlation coefficient score against the final reference (consisting of the average of the 66% best aligned particles). More than 600 particles were used for each average. Visualization of averaged electron density maps was performed in 3dmod from IMOD. 3D rendering of isosurfaces and structure fitting was performed using UCSF Chimera (v 1.14).62 Thresholds for signal detection in electron density maps were calculated using False Discovery Rate Control software.55

Ultrastructural expansion microscopy of regrowing flagella

Chlamydomonas cells from the strain CC125 were inoculated from a TAP agar plate to a liquid culture containing 400ml TAP, in a 500ml bottle, equipped with a bubbling device connected to compressed air. After growing in a light-dark cycle for 3 days, 200ml of culture was spun down at 500g for 2 minutes and the cell pellet resuspended in 40ml of 20mM HEPES, pH 7.2. The pH was lowered to 4.5 with acetic acid and quickly raised to 7.2 with NaOH. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500g for 2 minutes and resuspended in TAP. The time zero of the time course during flagellar regrowth is when cells are fully resuspended. Cells were placed in a 50ml glass beaker and mixed with a magnetic stir bar at low speed. Cells were harvested every 5 minutes until time point 30 minutes, after which cells were harvested every 10 minutes until time point 60 minutes. One additional time point was collected at 90 minutes. At each time point 200ul cells were applied to a 12mm coverslip previously treated with poly-D-lysine. After 5 minutes the coverslip was lightly blotted on the side with a paper towel and placed in absolute methanol at -20°C. After 15 minutes the coverslip was equilibrated in 1x PBS for 15 minutes.

The coverslips were then treated for expansion microscopy (Ultrastructure expansion microscopy63). The coverslips were submerged into cross linking prevention solution (2% acrylamide (Sigma, A4058) and 1.4% formaldehyde (Sigma, F8775) ) for 3 hours at 37°C. Gel solution was prepared for each coverslip as follows: Ice cold monomer solution (sodium acrylate (Sigma, 408220) 23%,acrylamide 10%, N,N-methylenebisacrylamide (Sigma, M1533) 0.1% in 1x PBS) was mixed with TEMED and APS, vortexed, and 35ul of the final gel solution were placed on a slice of parafilm, ice cold, and the coverslip layered on top with the cells facing the gel drop. This was repeated for all the coverslips of all the time points. Coverslips were placed in a sealed humid chamber and incubated 15 minutes on ice and 1 hour at 37°C. Cover slips were then submerged in a denaturing solution (200mM SDS, 200mM NaCl, 50mM Tris-BASE) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Gels were detached from the coverslips and placed in microfuge tubes and heated at 95°C for 1 hour and 30 minutes. After denaturing, the excess of denaturing solution was removed, and gels were expanded by incubating them 2 times in 150ml of distilled water for 30 minutes each and 1 time overnight. The following day a quarter of a gel of the time point of interest was stained as follows. The antibodies used will be listed below. The gels of interest were shrunk by submerging them in 1x PBS for 20 minutes, 3 times. Primary antibodies were mixed at 1:300 dilution in 2% BSA solubilized in 1x PBS and incubated for 3 hours at 37°C on a rocking platform. Primary antibody incubation was followed by 3 washes with 1x PBS added with Tween20 at 0.1% final concentration. Secondary antibodies were incubated for 3 hours at 37°C and then washed 3 times with 1x PBS added with Tween20 at 0.1% final concentration. Gels were expanded by placing them in distilled water for 20 minutes, for 2 times, and overnight for the last step. Stained gels were imaged with a Zeiss LSM980 in Airyscan mode, with a 63x Zeiss oil immersion lens with 1.46NA, with a 0.4% laser power and with Z stacks with 0.150um sampling rate.

Polyglutamylation coverage on axonemal microtubules by iU-ExM

Iterative-Ultrastructural expansion microscopy (iU-ExM) was performed as previously described.64 In brief, Chlamydomonas cells from the CC125 stain were grown in the same manner as in classical U-ExM. After depositing the cells on Poly-D-lysine coated, 12 mm coverslips, cells were anchored in formaldehyde (1.4%) and acrylamide (2.0%) for 3 hours at 37°C. 30 minutes prior to anchoring completion, monomer solution aliquots consisting of sodium acrylate (19%), acrylamide (10%), and N,N′-(1,2-Dihydroxyethylene)bisacrylamide (0.1%), were placed on ice, as well as aliquots of APS and TEMED. Glass gelation chambers were constructed, placed in a humid chamber, and placed at -20°C. Upon completion of anchoring, the anchored cells on coverslips were gently blotted, placed inside the gelation chambers on ice, and 100 mL of activated monomer solution (by the addition of APS (0.25%) and TEMED (0.25%)) was gently pipettted inside. Gelation proceeded for 15 minutes on ice, followed by 45 minutes at 37°C. Following gelation, the gels were removed from the coverslips by gently rocking the coverslip / gel apparatus for 15 minutes in denaturation buffer containing SDS (200 mm), Tris-HCl (50 mM), NaCl (200 mM), and at pH 6.8. The slightly expanded gels were collected, placed in a 1.5 mL eppendorf tube filled with denaturation buffer, and placed on a heat block at 85 degrees for 1.5 hours. The gels were then washed 2X with water for 30 minutes in 10 cm petri dishes, and placed in water a third time for overnight expansion

The following morning, the gels were stained in the same manner as classical U-ExM, with the exception being the concentration of primary and secondary antibodies, which were reduced to 1:200 dilutions in PBS / 2% BSA.

The next day, the expanded gels were cut into 1 cm2 pieces, and placed in a 6-well plate on ice. The cut pieces were then submerged to activated neutral gel containing acrylamide (10%), N,N′-(1,2-Dihydroxyethylene)bisacrylamide (0.05%), APS (0.1%) and TEMED (0.1%) in ddH2O, while still on ice. The gels were incubated 3X for 10 minutes, on ice at 4°C, while shaking. After incubation, the gel pieces were placed on a glass slide and gently blotted with a Kimwipe to remove excess neutral gel solution, gently covered with a 22 mm2 coverslip, and placed in a humid chamber at 37°C for one hour. The gels were then placed in an anchoring solution containing formaldehyde (1.4%) and acrylamide (2.0%) for 3 hrs at 37°C while shaking. After the second anchoring, the gels were washed 1X for 45 minutes in PBS.

Immediately following the PBS wash, the gels underwent another round of gel solution embedding by adding the second monomer solution, containing sodium acrylate (19%), acrylamide (10%), N,N′-Methylenebis(acrylamide) (0.1%), APS (0.1%) and TEMED (0.1%). The gels were incubated 3X for minutes, on ice at 4°C, while shaking. After incubation, the gel pieces were placed on a glass slide and gently blotted with a Kimwipe to remove excess monomer solution, gently covered with a 22 mm2 coverslip, and placed in a humid chamber at 37°C for one hour.

After the final gel polymerization, gel pieces were placed in NaOH (200 mM) for one hr, and given successive washes in PBS lasting for approximately 20 minutes until the pH reached 7.0. The gels were then expanded by continuous washes of 20 minutes in ddH2O until gel expansion plateaued.

Samples were imaged on a Zeiss LSM980 Airyscan2 with a 63X, 1.4 NA oil immersion objective, in Airyscan mode with “maximum resolution” parameters selected.

The expansion factor was calculated by utilizing the basal body as an internal ruler. The true width of the proximal basal body was measured in dispersed areas of the gel piece, divided by the known 225 nm width of the basal body, and averaged to generate the expansion factor of each gel piece.

Generation of ttll3::BSD and ttll9::NAT mutants by CRISPR

Both mutants were generated based on our previous protocols.65 Both strains were created using Chlamydomonas CC-124(-) 32M which was received as a kind gift from Susan Dutcher. The mating strains used for autolysin preparation CC620 and CC621 were obtained by the Chlamydomonas Resource Center. Cultures were maintained on 1.5% TAP agar plates. at 26°C under 12h light/12h dark cycle in an Environmental Chamber (FitoClima 12.000 PHL by Aralab). CRISPR Cas9 gRNAs for the Chlamydomonas reinhardtii Tubulin Tyrosine Ligase Like 3 (TTLL3, Cre03.g145447_4532) and Tubulin Tyrosine Ligase Like 9 (TTLL9, Cre17.g699500_4532) were designed using Genious Prime (GraphPad Software, LLC). Guides were picked based on high Doench 2016 activity score and no predicted off-target activity against the v6 reference genome, targeting exon 2 and exon 3 for TTLL3 and TTLL9 respectively. Interstional cassettes conferring Blasticidin S (BSD) or Nourseothricin (NAT) resistances were assembled from pAPN_crBSDr and pAB262-NAR-R respectively (availabe from the Chlamydomonas Resource Center) by NEBuilder HiFi assembly using synthetic linkers containing 50bp homology up- and downstream of the Cas9 cut site according to the manufacturer's instructions. Constructs were cloned in Dh5α or TOP10, mini-prepped (ZymoPure Plasmid MiniPrep Kit) and sequenced. Final donor DNA was prepared using ZymoPure Plasmid MidiPrep kit with EndoZero endotoxins removal, digested by BspQI repurified using Zymo Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (all Zymo Research).

CrRNA and tracrRNA (IDT AltR) were resuspended in Duplex buffer (30 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM potassium acetate, IDT) at 100 μM, mixed in a 1:1 ratio and annealed for 2 min at 95 °C followed by cooling on the bench to produce gRNA. RNPs were assembled by mixing gRNA and Cas9 (Alt-R™ S.p. Cas9 Nuclease V3, IDT) in at 5 μM each and incubated for 10 min at RT. Chlamydomonas cells used for transformation were grown on 1.5% TAP agar plate for 3 days in light/dark cycle at 25 °C. The day of the experiment the cells were scraped off and resuspended in TAP medium at high concentration (108 cells/ml) and aliquoted to electroporate 1.5x106 cells. Before electroporation cells were treated with 3 rounds of autolysin for a total ∼3 hours at RT and then heat-shocked at 40°C for 30 min, followed by washing 3 times with TAP with 40 mM sucrose (TAPS) and finally resuspended in TAPS and mixed with 5 μL of the RNP solution and 1500ng of donor DNA per 50 μL of cell suspension. Transformation was performed using a Neon electroporation system (Invitrogen) at 2300V in 3 pulses of 12ms. Reactions were recovered overnight in the light and consequently selected on 1.5% TAP Agar plates with added Nourseotricin (7.5 μg/ml) or Blasticidin S (50 μg/ml). Plates were incubated in constant lighting until colonies appeared. The colonies were picked and transferred to 96 well plates containing 200 ul TAP medium and grown up in constant light.

To isolate genomic DNA, 10 ul of culture from 96 well plates were mixed with 10 ul of Quick Extract Solution (by Epicentre) and incubated 6 min at 65°C and 2 min at 95°C. The insertion regions were screened by PCR and verified by Sanger sequencing. 2-3 clones were retained from each knock-out experiment.

Whole genome sequencing and testing of ttll3::BSD mutants

DNA was extracted using a CTAB/phenol based protocol (as described in Chlamydomonas DNA extraction, Sequencing Technology Group, JGI, Page 1 of 1). The product was then sequenced by the Genomics Facility of Human Technopole using Nanopore technology. 1.2 μg of genomic DNA in 60 μl were fragmented in an Eppendorf® MiniSpin plus using Covaris g-TUBE (cat. No. 520079) at 5,500 rpm for 60 seconds. The fragment size was checked on a 4200 TapeStation system (Agilent) using a Genomic DNA ScreenTape. Libraries for sequencing on a PromethION sequencer were prepared with the Ligation Sequencing Kit (SQK-LSK114, Oxford Nanopore Technologies) according to the manufacturer protocol, and loaded on a FLO-PRO114M flow cell. The final coverage was around 57X. We then analyzed the sequencing data to look for additional insertions in the genome.

Sequencing data analysis

Raw reads were aligned to the reference genome of the strain 7P (CC-5816) from Susan Dutchers lab (GCA_026108075.1_ASM2610807v1),66 since this is the closest published assembly to our background 32M strain, which was kindly donated to us by Dr. Dutcher as the opposite mating type strain to 7P. Read alignment was performed using minimap2 (v.2.26, using parameters “-x map-ont”)67 and structural variants called with sniffles2 (v2.2, using default parameters).68 While sniffles2 assumes diploidy when genotyping, we filtered identified variants downstream with bcftools (v1.18, using parameters “-f PASS -i 'GT="1/1") retaining only high-quality, homozygous alterations, which best fit with our experimental setting. We identified 284 insertions passing our first round of filters. We further processed all the insertions reported by the sniffles2 by computing, for each, a levensthein distance-based similarity score with respect to our CRISPR insert. Only the expected insertion in TTL3 had >90% similarity with the original insert, with the second most-similar insertion reaching only ∼50% similarity. For the 61 insertions in the callset being 30% similar or more to the original insert, we fetched the reads from the initial alignment and re-compute a consensus sequence through spoa69 (v4.1.0 using default parameters). We then manually inspected the alignment of these consensus sequences to the reference genome. All checked possible insertions were confirmed to be repeat elements of the Chlamydomonas genome and blast hits would typically result in multiple possible locations across different chromosomes. Since Chlamydomonas does not cause any significant amount of SNPs during integration, we assume that these differences to the 7P assembly are remainders of the genetic heterogeneity between CC-124 and CC-125 which are the paternal strains of 7P and 32M and not due to the mutagenesis process.

Rescue of tubulin glycilation in ttll3::BSD cells

The TTLL3 gene was cloned from the Fosmid 39496 which spans the entire sequence of the gene (obtained from the Chlamydomonas Resource Center). A 9185bp fragment including the entire gene and 1176bp upstream of the 5’ UTR was amplified by PCR using the primers TTLL3_GA_F (5’-CGCTATACATAACCACTCAGCTACCACATCATCAGGTGTATCCT-3’) and TTLL3_GA_R (5’-AGCTTGATGGGATCTTAAGCCACAGCACTTCGTTAACGA-3’) and subsequently assembled with pAPN_AphVIII linearized with NheI (available from the Chlamydomonas Resource Center) using NEBuilder HiFi Assembly Master mix according to the manufacturer's protocol to add AphVIII (Paromomycin resistance).

ttll3::BSD cells were grown in bubbling liquid culture, concentrated and treated twice with autolysin for 30min to remove cell walls, then washed three times with TAPS. 200uL of cells at ∼108cells/mL were mixed with 400ng of the TTLL3-AphVIII plasmid linearized with SspI and vortexed with zirconia beads for 30s. The reaction was recovered for ∼18h in the light, then plated on 1.5% TAP agar with 10mg/mL Paromomycin.

Resulting colonies were screened by immunofluorescence: Cells were adhered briefly to poly-L-lysine treated wells on a 12-well immunofluorescence slide (Thermo Scientific ER-202W-CE24), fixed by exchanging the liquid for 2.4% formaldehyde in PBS for 5 minutes followed by blocking in IF buffer (1x PBS, 1% BSA, 0.2% NaN3, 0.1% Tween-20) for 5 minutes. The wells were then exchanged with IF buffer supplemented with 1:1000 Glypep1 antibody for 2 hours, then washed twice with IF buffer, exchanged with IF buffer with 1:1000 goat anti-rabbit Alexa488 secondary (Invitrogen A-11008) for one hour. Finally, wells were aspirated and sealed with Prolong Diamond mounting medium and imaged on a widefield fluorescence microscope (Zeiss Axio Imager Z2). Clones with fluorescent cilia were retained and analyzed by western blot.

Swimming experiments

Flow chambers were made by mounting a thin coverslip (0.17mm) over a glass slide using double sided sticky tape (Tesa 05338). Liquid culture cells grown as explained above were pipetted inside the flow chamber right before imaging. An inverted Nikon Ti2 microscope was used in diascopic imaging mode using a white light transilluminator lamp (Nikon Intensilight C-HGFI). Movies were acquired at 387.4 fps and 0.564 um/pixel using a 20x objective (Plan Apo lambda, NA=0.75, WD=1000um) and a PRIM 95B 25MM camera (Teledyne Photometrics). All movies were acquired focusing in the middle of the flow chamber. The acquired movies were analyzed using the TrackMate plugin in Fiji50 which provided accurate sub-pixel localizations for each cell at each point in time. A custom Python script (Data S1) was used to compute swimming speeds, beat frequencies and forward and backward displacements per beat cycle.

Phototaxis experiments

Cells in light phase were resuspended in a phototaxis assay buffer (5 mM Hepes pH 7.4, 0.2 mM EGTA, 1 mM KCl, and 0.3 mM CaCl2, ∼107 cells/mL) and placed under red light for more than an hour.70 7ml of liquid culture were placed on a 55mm diameter plastic petri dish. The petri dish was illuminated from the side with a green LED light (60mW), inducing a negative phototactic response in the cells. The set-up was covered with a black box and imaged at different timepoints. A picture from the petri dish was taken with a smartphone.

Microtubule sliding quantification

In order to measure the deformations of the ciliary structure during active and passive beating, isolated Chlamydomonas axonemes were resuspended in HMDEK buffer containing 150μM ATP, and 1mM Casein. Both axonemal preparations were plunge-frozen as described in the plunge freezing section for immunolabeled axonemes. Cryo-ET tilt series of axonemal sections presenting different curvatures were acquired at 24.000X magnification from -60 to 60 degrees. Tomographic reconstructions were carried out (see cryo-electron tomography), enabling manual annotation of each 96-nm repeat position in the axoneme and the discretization of the ciliary axis. A custom Python script was used to compute the orthogonal projection of each 96-nm repeat over the discretized ciliary axis, by finding the smallest distance between the localization of each repeat and the discretized ciliary axis. Then cumulative sliding displacements between each pair of neighboring microtubule doublets were calculated based on these projections. The values shown in Figure 3 have been filtered with a 5-element moving average to reduce noise and present overall displacement trends for each pair of microtubule doublets. The measured sliding distributions between different doublets were in agreement with the bending plane observed in tomograms. 96-nm repeat sub-tomograms associated with regions of negative (n=211), positive (n=201) and no sliding (n=900) were independently analyzed by sub-tomogram averaging (see sub-tomogram averaging).

Cryo-electron tomography and subtomogram averaging for the structural characterization of ttll3::BSD and ttll9::NAT axonemes

ttll3::BSD and ttll9::NAT axonemes were isolated and prepared for cryo-electron tomography as described in previous sections. Respectively, 84 and 90 tomograms were acquired using a 300 keV Titan Krios with energy filter (20 eV slit width) and a Falcon 4i camera. To facilitate the identification of regions of interest, intermediate magnification maps were acquired using SerialEM at promising grid squares. Tilt series were acquired using SerialEM at 42.000x magnification (3.02Å pixel size, counting mode). A dose-symmetric tilt scheme from -60 to 60 degrees with 3 degrees increment was followed. A defocus range from -1.5 to -4.5 microns in 5 steps was used and the cumulative dose per tomogram was kept below 120 e-/Å2. Movies were recorded using counting mode and stored in.eer format. Drift was kept below 1 nm/s. Tomoman software56 was used to quickly batch-reconstruct all the tomograms in a high performance cluster with minimal user intervention, following a scheme similar to the one described in previous sections, including pre-treatment of the movies using motion correction, ctf-correction and dose-weighted filtering. The tomograms were reconstructed using weighted backprojection. Denoising using a deconvolution filter (WARP71) was performed to facilitate tomogram inspection and particle picking. The outline of microtubule doublets, C1 and C2 microtubules were traced in imod with points sparsely distributed along their length. Using custom scripts in matlab, initial.mod files generated with imod containing models representing the microtubules were simultaneously converted to .star files in STOPGAP,57 interpolated and oversampled at 4nm. Each particle was defined to have a y-axis aligned with the microtubule axis. Additionally, random rotation angles around this defined y-axis were applied to each particle in order to reduce missing wedge based bias in further alignments. The models were then combined in a single one and then, using scripts from the following software packages, translated to DYNAMO58 format, and from there translated to RELION59 to make them WARP-compatible. Particles were then extracted from the tomograms using WARP and averaged using RELION. Using 3D classification, the different repeats of microtubule associated protein decorations were separated. The data was then unbinned, realigned and refined using M.60 After M-refinement another round of alignment with RELION was performed. Finally, the averages were post processed using RELION. More than 10.000 particles were used for each average.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For all Chlamydomonas swimming characterization experiments n equals number of cells. P-values were calculated in Python using a T-test (scipy.stats.ttest_ind). Statistical significance was associated with p-values smaller than 0.005.
