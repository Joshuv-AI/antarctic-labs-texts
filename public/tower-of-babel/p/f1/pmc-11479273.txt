Employing deep learning model to evaluate speech information in acoustic simulations of Cochlear implants
Authors: Rahul Sinha, Mahan Azadpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Acoustic vocoders play a key role in simulating the speech information available to cochlear implant (CI) users. Traditionally, the intelligibility of vocoder CI simulations is assessed through speech recognition experiments with normally-hearing subjects, a process that can be time-consuming, costly, and subject to individual variability. As an alternative approach, we utilized an advanced deep learning speech recognition model to investigate the intelligibility of CI simulations. We evaluated model’s performance on vocoder-processed words and sentences with varying vocoder parameters. The number of vocoder bands, frequency range, and envelope dynamic range were adjusted to simulate sound processing settings in CI devices. Additionally, we manipulated the low-cutoff frequency and intensity quantization of vocoder envelopes to simulate psychophysical temporal and intensity resolutions in CI patients. The results were evaluated within the context of the audio analysis performed in the model. Interestingly, the deep learning model, despite not being originally designed to mimic human speech processing, exhibited a human-like response to alterations in vocoder parameters, resembling existing human subject results. This approach offers significant time and cost savings compared to testing human subjects, and eliminates learning and fatigue effects during testing. Our findings demonstrate the potential of speech recognition models in facilitating auditory research.

Introduction

Owing to advances in machine learning and deep learning techniques, automatic speech recognition systems have approached human-like performance and impressive robustness to various forms of noise and distortion1. While the initial goal in designing speech recognition systems was to achieve human-level performance, there is now growing interest in leveraging these systems as computational models of human speech processing2,3. This potential offers exceptional value to the research in speech and hearing science fields. For instance, deep learning speech recognition systems may provide a computational modelling tool to elucidate the speech cues that contribute to intelligibility across various acoustic environments, contributing to the field of acoustic phonetics. Moreover, speech recognition models may provide a tool to emulate individuals with impaired auditory function, aiding in the development of novel treatment or rehabilitation options for speech and hearing disorders.

This study aimed to investigate the potential of utilizing deep learning speech recognition models in cochlear implant (CI) research. CIs are clinically used neural stimulation technologies designed to restore hearing in individuals with profound deafness. They directly stimulate auditory neural pathways via electrodes that are surgically placed in the cochlea. Deaf patients who use CIs generally benefit from their device for hearing sounds and speech communication. However, most CI patients struggle participating in oral conversations particularly in the presence of environmental noise. A primary goal in CI research is to enhance patient outcomes by further improving and optimizing CI signal processing and sound coding strategies, processes that convert acoustic signals picked up by a microphone to electric stimulation of the auditory nerve4.

As a first step to investigate the potential of automatic speech recognition systems in CI research, we focused on evaluating vocoder CI simulations. Acoustic vocoders are designed with a signal processing that closely resembles speech degradations introduced by the sound processing in CI devices5. They extract amplitude envelopes in limited number of spectral channels, discarding temporal fine structure. Vocoders have been extensively used to simulate various CI signal analysis elements as well as physiological or psychophysical factors in individual CI patients. The commonly used vocoder parameters include the number of spectral channels5–7, envelope processing8,9, analysis-to-synthesis mapping10,11, synthesis filter overlap12,13 and loudness mapping14. The latter three are examples of vocoder parameters that pertain to the cochlear presentation of auditory information or post-cochlear processes and can emulate physiological or psychophysical factors in individual CI users: analysis-to-synthesis mapping simulates CI electrode insertion depth 10,11; synthesis filter overlap simulates interaction been CI stimulation channels 12,13; and loudness mapping simulates loudness perception in CI users 14. Some of these individual-specific factors would be challenging or impossible to manipulate or study in actual CI patients. Vocoder simulations offer a means to isolate different factors that influence CI outcomes through independent and selective manipulation of vocoder parameters, and have significantly contributed to understanding of the factors that influence CI outcomes. As an example, consider the number of spectral channels. Vocoder studies have indicated that speech information is largely robust to spectral degradations caused by reducing number of channels 15. These studies show that vocoder-processed speech is more intelligible for normally-hearing listeners than CI signal is for CI patients, even when using the same number of spectral channels. This finding leads to the conclusion that poor outcomes in CI patients cannot be solely due to a limited number of spectral channels, implying that there must be other factors contributing to suboptimal CI outcomes.

Traditionally, speech information in acoustic vocoder simulations is evaluated by testing normally-hearing human subjects. Subjects are typically asked to articulate or identify sentences, words or phonemes processed through vocoders. While human vocoder studies have provided invaluable contributions to the CI field, they are sometimes difficult or impossible to perform. Research involving human subjects is generally time-consuming and costly in terms of both subject recruitment and testing. Furthermore, individual cognitive factors such as learning, attention or fatigue during a testing session can impact the results. Perception of vocoder-processed speech by normally-hearing listeners varies greatly among individuals and can be significantly influenced by brief periods of familiarization or exposure 16–18. These limitations prevent researchers from being able to evaluate several different vocoder parameters with the desired precision.

Rather than testing normally-hearing human subjects to evaluate the intelligibility of vocoder CI simulations, we employed Whisper, an advanced deep learning speech recognition model developed by OpenAI 1. The intelligibility of vocoder-processed words and sentences was assessed by executing the Whisper deep learning model with vocoder-processed stimuli as input. Several vocoder parameters were manipulated to simulate CI signal processing settings as well as psychophysical deficits experienced by CI patients. The results of the Whisper model were evaluated within the context of the audio analysis implemented in the model, and were compared to the human vocoder simulation results reported in the literature.

Interestingly, despite not being originally designed to emulate the human speech processing system, Whisper exhibited human-like patterns as a function of vocoder parameters, closely resembling the published human vocoder studies. These findings support the potential of employing advanced deep learning speech recognition models as an alternative to human participants in conducting CI simulation studies. The potential to utilize these models as substitutes for human participants is significant, especially when human subject studies are not feasible. In fact, it would be almost impossible to conduct a human subject experiment with the scale of the stimuli tested in this study. We evaluated Whisper’s speech recognition on roughly 270,000 sentences and 900,000 words in about 450 vocoder manipulations in quiet and noise. Testing human subjects with this number of stimuli would require more than 2,000 h of testing per participant, assuming 500 sentences or words could be tested in an hour. Instead of thousands of hours of testing time spread over several months and years, the Whisper model could provide results within hours running on the currently available high performance computing platforms. This study demonstrates the potential of employing speech recognition models to investigate and optimize large-scale sets of speech processing parameters without testing human subjects.

Results

This section presents Whisper’s sentence and word recognition scores in relation to various vocoder parameter and noise level conditions. For each vocoder and noise condition, we evaluated Whisper’s performance on 200 CNC (consonant-nucleus-consonant) words and 60 AzBio sentences19. The symbols in results graphs show the average score from five repetitions of the same sentence or word lists. The error bars are the standard deviation of the five scores, indicating the variability in Whisper performance with the same input. We compare Whisper results for each vocoder condition with similar human subject studies from the literature, considering the audio analysis stage of the Whisper model. We also discuss the implications of these CI simulation results for the clinical CI population.

Number of channels

Figure 1 summarizes AzBio sentence and CNC word recognition scores of the Whisper model as a function of number of vocoder channels. The results are shown in quiet condition as well as in the presence of speech-shaped noise at 5dB SNR (signal-to-noise ratio). As expected, the performance of the Whisper model increased monotonically as the number of channels was increased from 4 to 22. Scores in quiet increased sharply from 4 to 6 channels, approaching a plateau at around 8–10 channels. The peak performance in quiet approached ceiling, approximately 99% for vocoded sentences and 94% for vocoded words. In noisy conditions, sentence scores plateaued at around 90% with 12 channels, while word scores continued to increase up to 22 channels without reaching a clear plateau. The lower word scores in noise suggests that vocoder processing significantly diminishes the speech information necessary for intelligibility in such conditions. Sentence scores were better than word scores, which is explained by Whisper’s remarkable human-like ability to use contextual and linguistic information within sentences. The error bars were small for the majority of the tested conditions, suggesting little test–retest variability in Whisper results.Fig. 1Effect of number of channels on Whisper performance in quiet and noise. Comparison to the published human results

The patterns of Whisper’s performance shown in Fig. 1 correspond with the results obtained with normally-hearing human subjects 6,7,15,20. Consistent with Whisper results, the published human vocoder studies have also demonstrated that as few as 8–10 spectral channels can be sufficient to achieve high levels of speech understanding in quiet. In noisy conditions, human studies have revealed significant deterioration in the perception of vocoder-processed speech, even with large number of vocoder channels 15. This finding aligns with Whisper ‘s results. Whisper’s word recognition score in noise was below 60% even with 22 vocoder channels.

While it is possible to compare the overall pattern of results for different vocoder studies, we found it challenging to compare absolute speech recognition scores between studies. Firstly, there was little test-to-test variability in Whisper results to conduct statistical comparisons with human subject results. The error bars shown in the results graphs are zero or very small for most of the tested vocoder and stimulus conditions. Secondly, there are notable methodological differences among the different vocoder studies. The human vocoder studies significantly vary in vocoder processing implementation, speech testing materials, noise type, experimental procedures, practice trials, etc. The majority of human vocoder studies provide training or practice trials to help subjects adapt to the vocoder stimuli, sometimes hours of practice trials during testing sessions. Small practice trials or even mere exposure to vocoder-processed speech stimuli can significantly enhance recognition scores through perceptual adaptation16–18. Unlike human subjects, the Whisper model lacks the capability to adapt to vocoder-processed stimuli during testing. This pre-trained speech recognition model remains entirely naïve and unfamiliar to the vocoder-processed stimuli throughout the testing sessions.

To be able to assess Whisper’s performance more fairly and accurately, we generated a new set of results replicating two human vocoder studies that provided explicit details about their vocoder processing and testing procedure 20,21. These new results were generated using the specific speech materials, vocoder processing parameters, and the noise type used in Goupell et al. 21 and Grange et al. 20 studies. Both these studies tested normally-hearing subjects with IEEE sentence materials. Goupell et al. used two-talker babble noise to evaluate the effect of SNR on perception of stimuli processed with 8-channel vocoder. We used the adult subject data of Goupell et al. to compare with Whisper. Grange et al. evaluated the effects of number of vocoder channels on sentence recognition in speech-shaped white noise at 7dB SNR. We used their results replicating Oxenham and Kreft 22 vocoder implementation with the least amount of simulated channel interaction. Grange et al.'s subjects underwent brief practice trials with vocoded speech, and we used initial testing session of Goupell et al. to compare with Whisper. This supposedly minimizes training or practice effects in the data we used from these two studies.

Whisper results and the results of Grange et al. and Goupell et al. are shown in Fig. 2a (left panel) and Fig. 2b (right panel), respectively. The patterns of Whisper’s performance were very similar to the patterns of human subjects listening to vocoded speech. The scores for Whisper and normally-hearing listeners were very comparable in quiet and at higher number of channels. However, Whisper showed slightly lower scores at low number of channels (Fig. 2a, left panel) and low SNRs (Fig. 2b, right panel). The human subjects outperformed Whisper in the difficult listening conditions. This observation may suggest that the Whisper speech recognition model is still far from a true model for human speech recognition system. More technological deep learning advancements and larger naturalistic training sets may be required to develop deep learning speech recognition models that mimic human performance. Whisper may also suffer from suboptimal audio analysis, which could discard important speech cues by removing temporal and spectral details from the signal (see methods section "Audio Feature Analysis of Whisper", Fig. 9). Another explanation for better human subject results in Fig. 2 is concerned with attentional, cognitive and learning capabilities in humans that were not implemented in the Whisper deep learning model used in this study. Human subjects participating in vocoded speech experiments are consciously aware of the difficult listening task, potentially aiding them to focus attention for listening to the degraded speech signal. Furthermore, as discussed earlier, human subject’s perception can rapidly adapt to vocoded stimuli during a testing session16–18, particularly when feedback is provided throughout the testing session21. The superior results of the human studies in noisy backgrounds may also be contributed by the onset delay between the target sentence and the background noise. A time delay is commonly introduced between target and noise in human studies to help the subjects focus attention to the target speech. These human-specific attentional and learning mechanisms were not incorporated in the Whisper speech recognition model we used in this study.Fig. 2Comparison between Whisper and average human subject scores of Grange et al. Despite the slightly poorer Whisper performance compared to the human results, the data shown in Fig. 1 provide a comprehensive summary of vocoded speech intelligibility as a function of number of channels, stimulus type and noise level. Implications for CI patients

The number of spectral channels in vocoder CI simulations corresponds to active electrodes in the clinical CI devices, typically ranging from 12 to 22. The Whisper data in Fig. 1 and the vocoder simulation studies with human subjects15 indicate that speech information in quiet is largely robust to reducing the number of spectral channels to 8–10. These data predict that the speech information provided by the typical clinical CI devices should be sufficient for high speech recognition performance, particularly in quiet. However, CI patients often underperform these predictions, with average word recognition scores not exceeding 60% in quiet settings and significant variability among individuals 23. This discrepancy between vocoder simulation results and the actual CI patient outcomes implies that poor CI outcomes are not solely due to limited number of electrodes in clinical CI devices. Other factors, such as channel interaction, neural health, suboptimal electrode insertion, etc. may significantly restrict CI users’ access to speech information. Identifying the factors contributing to poor CI outcomes is a key research area.

Another inference from the Whisper results of Fig. 1 pertains to the poor CI outcomes in the presence of background noise. Many CI patients find it challenging to understand speech in noise. Consistent with this observation, the right panel of Fig. 1 reveals that Whisper’s word recognition score in noise remained under 60% with 22 vocoder channels, whereas the score in quiet approached ceiling. This finding suggests that vocoder processing greatly affects speech information in noisy settings. The results in Fig. 1 represent optimal CI scores without taking into account the anatomical, physiological or psychophysical limitations. The vocoder processing used to obtain these results incorporated CI processing only, by preserving channel envelopes and removing temporal fine structure details. The finding that the intelligibility of vocoder-processed words remained low in the presence of background noise, even with 22 vocoder channels, implies that CI processing can significantly contribute to CI patients’ struggle in noise, presumably due to eliminating fine structure details.

Channel frequencies

The second vocoder parameter we manipulated was channel frequency boundaries. Channel frequency boundaries were manipulated by changing the low and high cutoff frequencies of vocoder processing while keeping the bandwidth of all the intermediate channels equal in octave. Figures 3 and 4 show the effects of changing the lower and higher frequency boundaries (start and end frequencies) across varying number of spectral channels, sentence and word materials, both in quiet and in speech-shaped noise at a 5dB SNR. The ending frequency or upper boundary didn’t have a systematic effect on Whisper’s performance as shown in Fig. 4. However, there was consistent drop in performance for both CNC word and AZBio sentences as the starting frequency (lower cutoff) was increased (Fig. 3). The detrimental effect of increasing starting frequency on speech recognition is intuitive, as high starting frequencies discard important energy peaks (formants) in the low frequency region of the speech signal. Figure 3 shows that Whisper’s performance dropped gradually as the starting frequency was increased up to a knee point, above which there was a steeper performance drop. The knee point is around 800Hz for most conditions, and is lower in noise and for smaller number of channels. The performance drop is steeper for CNC words than AzBio sentences. These results suggest that removing low frequency information up to around 800Hz may not significantly affect intelligibility of vocoder CI simulations, at least in quiet and for higher number of spectral channels.Fig. 3Effect of the low cutoff frequency of the signal on Whisper model performance in quiet and noise. Also, Fig. 4 shows a peak in performance at 7000 Hz ending frequency. We further investigated these effects by re-testing Whisper with additional sets of equal-octave band frequency boundaries for starting frequencies 150, 200 and 300 Hz and ending frequencies 7000 and 7900 Hz. In addition to the equal-octave bands, we also tested the standard frequency boundaries that are used in the commercial CI devices manufactured by Cochlear (Cochlear Ltd., Macquarie Park, AU). The results, shown in Fig. 5, confirm that the condition with frequency range between 300 and 7000 Hz resulted in the best model performance for all channels. Certain effects of channel frequency boundaries observed may be attributed to the frequency analysis conducted in the Whisper model. Whisper obtains a mel-spectrogram at the initial audio analysis stage, which is subsequently utilized by the deep-learning stages (see Methods section "Audio Feature Analysis of Whisper", Fig. 9). The mel-scale frequency boundaries used for spectrogram analysis interact with the channel frequencies of the vocoder processing. This suggests that the performance patterns of Whisper, as a function of vocoder channel frequencies, are likely influenced by the choice of mel-scale frequency boundaries. However, despite the potential impact of the spectrogram’s mel-frequencies, Whisper results have important implications. The results shown in Fig. 5 imply that even minor alterations in frequency boundaries could considerably affect the intelligibility of vocoded speech, especially with smaller number of channels.

Comparison to the published human results

To the best of our knowledge, there is no systematic report on the effects of frequency range and channel boundaries on the perception of vocoded speech by normally-hearing listeners. In a vocoder study that aimed to simulate frequency-to-place mismatch in CI patients, Faulkner et al. 24 showed that increasing starting frequency to 600 Hz had negligible effect on speech perception scores whereas starting frequency of 1600 Hz dramatically reduced scores. These results are consistent with the Whisper results shown in Fig. 3. While the patterns of Whisper results as a function of start frequency are consistent with the results of Faulkner et al., the scores were higher in the human subject study. This is likely due to differences in stimuli and procedures between the two studies. Particularly, Faulkner et al. provided subjects with extensive hours of training with feedback on the correct response. As mentioned earlier, the Whisper model employed in this study does not incorporate human-like attention and learning mechanisms during testing.

Implications for CI patients

Vocoder channel frequencies simulate the frequency-to-electrode allocation in CI devices. Altering the start frequency or the low-cutoff of the CI frequency allocation table may have two opposing effects on speech perception outcomes. It has been proposed that raising the start frequency may enhance speech perception by reducing potential mismatch between frequency and cochlear place of excitation within an implanted ear 11,25 or across ears in bimodal or bilateral CI listeners 26,27. Conversely, a higher starting frequency could discard crucial low frequency speech cues. The results shown in Fig. 3 suggest that the latter may not be a concern. Whisper’s performance remained relatively stable even when the starting frequency was increased up to 600–800 Hz, suggesting that speech intelligibility is largely unaffected by the removal of low frequency information.

The Whisper results with varying vocoder channel frequencies could have significant implications for designing frequency allocation tables for CI devices. Manipulating the CI frequency allocation tables alters the distribution of speech information across spectral channels, and Whisper results in Fig. 5 suggest that even minor changes could have significant effect on speech perception. This emphasizes the importance of the distribution of speech information across spectral channels. Note that these Whisper results could only be explained by the distribution of speech cues across channels. These vocoder simulations did not consider the factors influencing frequency mismatch in CI patients, including electrode location in the cochlea, neural survival, and current spread.

Envelope low-cutoff frequency

Figure 6 shows the patterns of Whisper results as a function of the envelope low-cutoff frequency. Whisper performance dramatically improved as the envelope low-cutoff was increased from 5 to 20 Hz. However, there was no improvement above 20 Hz for most of the vocoder and stimulus conditions. The patterns of results were similar for sentences and words in quiet and noise. These findings indicate that envelope frequencies exceeding 20 Hz may not offer substantial speech information, at least when there are more than four spectral bands. Interestingly, a gradual decline in performance can be observed for some of the tested conditions when the low pass envelope exceeded 60 Hz. This is particularly noticeable for words in noise and for smaller number of channels. The reason behind this observation may be related to spectral splatter due to the vocoder processing implemented in this study. Even though noise bands were band-pass filtered after they were multiplied by the envelope (see section "Vocoder Processing" of Methods, Fig. 10), broad envelope frequencies could result in significant energies beyond the band-pass range, leading to interference with the neighboring channels.Fig. 6Effect of the low pass cutoff frequency of the envelope on Whisper model performance at different number of channels in quiet and noise. The results show mean and standard deviation of Whisper scores for 5 repetitions of 60 AzBio sentences (left) and 200 CNC30 words (left).

Effect of the low pass cutoff frequency of the envelope on Whisper model performance at different number of channels in quiet and noise. The results show mean and standard deviation of Whisper scores for 5 repetitions of 60 AzBio sentences (left) and 200 CNC30 words (left).

The results presented in Fig. 6 should be evaluated in the context of the Whisper’s mel-spectrogram analysis (see section "Audio Feature Analysis of Whisper" of Methods). The audio frame length used to compute spectrograms represents a tradeoff between temporal and spectral resolutions. In Whisper, spectrogram is calculated by applying FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) to overlapping 25-ms audio frames (Fig. 9). This audio frame length corresponds to 40 Hz spectral resolution, leading to a low-pass filtering of the temporal trajectory in each spectrogram bin with a 40 Hz cutoff frequency. While the spectrogram’s low-pass filtering effect could result in a plateau in Whisper’s response as a function of envelope cutoff frequency, it cannot explain the pattern of results depicted in Fig. 6. The plateau in Whisper results occurred at 20 Hz envelope cutoff, which is significantly lower than the spectrogram’s 40 Hz low-pass cutoff. This reaffirms the earlier conclusions that envelope variations greater than 20 Hz have limited or no speech value.

Comparison to the published human results

The results of Whisper as a function of envelope cutoff frequency are in agreement with findings of the published human vocoder studies. Souza and Rosen 9 reported that increasing envelope cutoff from 30 to 300 Hz didn’t have an effect on speech perception in normally hearing listeners. Xu et al.8 reported phoneme perception scores in normally-hearing listeners as a function of the number of vocoder channels and envelope cutoff frequency. Phoneme scores increased when the envelope cutoff frequency was doubled from 8 to 16 Hz. However, no additional increase in score was observed with further doubling of envelope cutoff frequency, particularly with more than four vocoder channels. These results are consistent with Whisper’s results, which showed a plateau at 20 Hz with 4 to 22 channels. It should be noted that despite the similar patterns of Whisper and human subject results, a direct comparison of the actual speech scores between these studies isn’t feasible. The human subject studies used different speech materials than those used with Whisper. Furthermore, they provided practice trials with feedback to familiarize the subjects with the vocoder-processed stimuli.

Implications for CI patients

CI sound coding strategies typically modulate the amplitude of electric pulses delivered by CI electrodes using the temporal envelope in the corresponding channels. It has been proposed that the psychophysical ability of CI users to detect and discriminate rapid amplitude modulations is related to speech perception outcomes 28,29. This may imply that CI users who can perceive rapid amplitude modulations would have access to additional speech cues provided by these modulations. However, both the Whisper results in Fig. 6 and the results from human subjects reported in Xu et al. 8 indicate that envelope variations above 20 Hz do not significantly contribute to speech cues, at least with more than four channels. This finding questions the role of rapid amplitude modulations in speech intelligibility, leading to alternative explanations regarding the reported relation between amplitude modulation detection and speech perception outcomes in CI users. One possible explanation is that while high-frequency amplitude modulations may not provide substantial speech cues, the ability to detect and discriminate these modulations may correlate with fundamental psychophysical capabilities that form the basis of speech perception. The results in Fig. 6 suggest that CI processing strategies may not need to preserve envelope modulations higher than 20 Hz for encoding speech. However, while rapid envelope variations may not contribute to speech intelligibility, they may provide cues for other important tasks not investigated in this study, such as pitch perception.

Envelope dynamic range

The results in Fig. 7 show the effect of envelope dynamic range (DR) manipulation on speech intelligibility. This manipulation was performed by setting the envelope values below a certain threshold to zero. The threshold was determined by the DR represented in dB, in the way that the dB difference between the maximum envelope intensity and the threshold equals to the DR. Whisper results show that intelligibility improved as envelope DR was increased as much as 60 dB, particularly for words and at smaller number of channels. The patterns of changes as a function of envelope DR look similar for quiet and noisy conditions. These results demonstrate that envelope intensities as low as 60 dB below the maximum can significantly contribute to the intelligibility of vocoder-processed speech, at least for small number of vocoder channels and noisy conditions.Fig. 7Effect of the envelope dynamic range on Whisper performance in quiet and noise. Comparison to the published human results

To the best of our knowledge, the effect of envelope dynamic range on the intelligibility of vocoder simulations has not been evaluated directly with human subjects. The closest published study we are aware of is Loizou et al.14, which showed that compressing vocoder envelopes had a large effect on speech perception. In that study, the extracted vocoder envelopes were compressed without removing any envelope information. This manipulation is different from our approach that reduced the range of envelope intensities. The Whisper results suggest that very low envelope intensities can contribute to the intelligibility of vocoder-processed speech. However, human subjects may not be able to utilize the cues provided by low intensities. First, these cues would only be accessible if they fall within the audible range. Second, rapid temporal variations are less perceivable at lower intensities 30. However, the latter may not necessarily restrict access to low-intensity speech cues in human subjects. These cues may primarily rely on slow variations or just presence of low-intensity energy in the signal envelope and could be useable. One way to investigate whether rapid envelope modulations at low intensities contributed to Whisper performance would be by eliminating envelope modulations at these intensities.

Implications for CI patients

One of the CI processing parameters associated with the envelope DR is the input audio DR. However, although these two parameters may be related, they are not the same. Envelope DR is the range of envelope intensities in each spectral band of the signal, whereas input DR represents the range of values in the audio signal captured by the microphones and used in the CI sound coding strategy. Zeng et al. examined the impact of input DR on speech perception of CI patients and demonstrated that a 50 dB DR yielded maximum consonant and vowel scores 31. These findings align with Whisper results, emphasizing the significance of low signal intensities in conveying important speech cues. In CI devices, envelope values are typically mapped to electric current amplitudes. To the best of our knowledge, the impact of envelope DR on speech perception has not been evaluated in CI patients. The Whisper results suggest that low envelope intensities can offer significant speech cues. CI users may benefit from low-intensity speech cues if these cues are audible and involve slower modulations. Perception of more rapid envelope modulations deteriorates at lower intensities by CI users 32,33.

Envelope quantization

Figure 8 shows Whisper performance as a function of the number of quantized envelope intensities. Envelope quantization was implemented by dividing the envelope DR into a number of equal steps in dB, with values between two steps set to the higher value. Whisper performance dramatically improved as the number of quantized levels was increased to 20, at which point a plateau was reached across all vocoder conditions. The plateau was reached earlier, between 5 to 10 quantized levels, with larger number of vocoder channels and in quiet. Comparison to the published human results

The results in Fig. 8 are consistent with the reports on the effects of envelope quantization on speech perception in normally hearing listeners. Loizou et al. 34 demonstrated that normally-hearing human subjects also required less than 20 quantized envelope intensities to reach asymptotic performance when listening to vocoder-processed sentences in quiet. Also, similar to Whisper, normally-hearing listeners required less than 5 quantized levels to reach asymptotic performance with 16 channels. A larger number of quantized levels was required to reach asymptotic performance with smaller number of channels. It should be noted that the implementation of envelope quantization in Loizou et al. 34 was slightly different from that of the current study. The quantized levels in the current study were obtained by dividing envelope dynamic range into equal dB steps. Loizou et al. divided envelope intensities into equal linear steps. We used logarithmic steps to be consistent with the logarithmic acoustic mapping performed in the Whisper model. The Whisper’s audio analysis stage applies logarithmic amplitude mapping to the output of the mel-spectrogram, emulating the logarithmic loudness mapping in human listeners. Despite the difference in envelope quantization implementation, the patterns of results from Whisper remarkably mirror those of normally-hearing human subjects in Loizou et al. 34. As previously discussed, comparison of actual speech scores between normally-hearing subjects and Whisper isn’t straightforward due to the different speech materials used in the two studies and the fact that Loizou et al. provided practice trials involving vocoder-processed speech. Whisper does not have the capability for perceptual and procedural learning during testing sessions.

Implications for CI patients

The aim of envelope quantization was to simulate the psychophysical ability of CI patients to discriminate envelope intensities, or intensity resolution. A larger number of quantized levels simulates those CI subjects who have more discriminable envelope intensities or better intensity resolution. The number of discriminable envelope intensities in CI users is primarily determined by internal noise or uncertainty in loudness perception, and can be influenced by stimulation parameters such as pulse rate 35. It has been shown that the number of discriminable envelope intensities can vary significantly among CI patients, ranging from less than 10 to over 60 36. Both Whisper results in Fig. 8 and the normally-hearing subject results from Loizou et al. 34 indicate that as small as 20 discriminable envelope intensities is sufficient for optimal speech perception performance. This finding suggests that the intensity resolution ability of most CI patients should be sufficient for speech comprehension tasks, implying that envelope discrimination might not be the primary factor contributing to the variability in speech perception outcomes among CI patients. On the other hand, these results also suggest that poor intensity resolution, specifically with fewer than 10 discriminable envelope intensities, could significantly impair speech perception performance in CI patients.

Discussion

This study supports the potential of advanced high-performing speech recognition models for evaluating intelligibility of acoustic CI simulations. Whisper results showed remarkable robustness to vocoder processing, resembling the results of the published human vocoder studies obtained with normally-hearing subjects. With only four channels in the vocoder implemented in this study, Whisper correctly recognized 25% of words in AZBio sentences and 10% of CNC words in quiet with 4 channels. The model’s speech recognition score dramatically increased with six channels and reached more than 90% for sentences and 60% for words in quiet. In noise, word recognition scores remained below 60% even with 22 vocoder channels. This suggests that vocoder processing, which discards temporal fine details, can significantly affect speech cues in the presence of background noise. As expected, the model scored higher for sentences than for words, thanks to its human-like capability to utilize context cues, a feature enabled by the transformer network structure.

It is important to note that because of the methodological differences across vocoder studies, it is not straightforward to accurately compare Whisper results to the results of the published human vocoder studies. Numerous factors must be taken into consideration when comparing speech recognition findings from different vocoder studies. These factors include vocoder processing parameters (e.g. channel frequency boundaries, envelope cutoff frequency, etc.), speech testing materials (phoneme, word or sentence corpus), and the level and type of background noise or other environment distortions. To provide a more valid comparison between Whisper and the published human subject studies, we performed additional Whisper simulations using similar implementation and methodological details as two of the published human vocoder studies. While the patterns of Whisper results were very similar to the results of human subject studies, Whisper’s scores were slightly lower for small number of vocoder channels and in the presence of large amount of noise (Fig. 2). One reason for Whisper underperforming human subjects in these difficult listening conditions may be suboptimal deep learning and neural network architecture and training paradigm. More technological advancements and larger naturalistic training sets may be required to develop deep learning speech recognition models that achieve human performance. Another explanation for human subjects outperforming Whisper in severely degraded stimulus conditions is concerned with attentional, cognitive and learning abilities during testing sessions. The normally-hearing subjects participating in vocoder CI simulation studies are cognitively prepared for the task of listening to distorted speech sounds and may benefit from the ability to focus attention to extract phonetic and linguistic information from the degraded stimuli. Furthermore, human vocoder studies often involve various amount of training or practice trials during testing to familiarize subjects with the stimuli and the task. It has been shown that even small amount of practice or simply passive exposure to vocoded stimuli can significantly improve perception of these sounds 17,21. These human cognitive and attentional privileges were not implemented in the Whisper speech recognition model employed in this study. The speech perception scores in human vocoder studies would likely be lower if, like Whisper, human subjects remained naïve to the stimuli throughout the experiment and did not have the ability to benefit from experience gained in previous trials.

The vocoder parameters manipulated in this study simulate CI signal processing factors or perceptual aspects that are relevant to speech perception with CIs. One set of parameters varied the frequency range and frequency boundaries of vocoder channels, emulating frequency-to-electrode assignment in CI devices. The model results showed that the intelligibility of vocoder CI simulations did not significantly diminish with an increase in the low cutoff frequency up to 600–800 Hz, suggesting that speech cues remain relatively robust to removing low frequency information. However, a significant drop was observed as cutoff frequency was increased above 1000 Hz. Whisper results also interestingly showed that small changes in channel frequency boundaries could affect the intelligibility of vocoder simulation, particularly for smaller number of channels. These results suggest that the frequency-to-electrode assignment in CI devices could affect speech perception outcomes by altering the distribution of speech frequencies across CI processing channels. The cutoff frequency of vocoder envelopes was manipulated to simulate the psychophysical ability of CI patients to perceive rapid envelope cues. Lowering envelope cutoff frequency corresponds to removing access to rapid envelope variations. Consistent with the published human subject results 8, Whisper results showed that access to envelope variations above 20 Hz does not significantly enhance speech scores. These results suggest that CI users may not require the ability to perceive rapid envelope variations for speech perception. However, it should be noted that while envelope variations above 20 Hz may not contribute to speech intelligibility, they may provide cues for other important tasks not investigated in this study, such as pitch perception. The results of manipulating envelope dynamic range were rather unexpected, showing that very low envelope intensities (> 50dB below maximum) can significantly contribute to speech intelligibility. Finally, envelope quantization results suggest that speech intelligibility is largely preserved with as low as 20 quantized envelope levels, even in noise and for low number of vocoder channels. These results resemble the results of human studies 34. Envelope quantization was performed to simulate the psychophysical ability of CI patients to access intensity cues. Whisper results showed that fewer than 10 quantization levels were sufficient with more than 8 vocoder channels. These results predict poor speech perception for CI users who have very poor intensity resolution. In general, the trend of the Whisper model results as a function of the vocoder parameters tested in this study closely agree with the published human subject results.

The results of the Whisper model support the potential of employing speech recognition models in auditory research. Although deep learning speech recognition models were not originally designed to mimic the way humans process speech, this study supports the potential of these models as a reliable tool to assess the intelligibility of distorted or degraded speech signals. This study focused on vocoder CI simulations as a first attempt to validate the potential of deep learning speech recognition models in CI research. However, although vocoder simulations have significantly contributed to the CI field, they still cannot simulate all aspects of sound processing and perception in CI patients. For instance, the properties of the primary auditory neurons and their responsiveness to electrical stimulation cannot be easily simulated with vocoder-processed acoustic signals presented to the normal hearing system. This limitation may be overcome by leveraging speech recognition models to incorporate computational models of auditory nerve response to CI stimulation 37–39. Speech recognition models typically consist of an audio analysis stage that emulates peripheral auditory processing in a normal ear. The auditory analysis stage can be substituted with computational models of CI stimulation and peripheral neural responses to CI stimulation 37. This modification could provide a computational platform to directly simulate electrode-neural interface properties in individual CI patients, and evaluate the effects of peripheral factors on speech recognition. This approach has the potential to considerably accelerate research and clinical practice in the CI field. For instance, computational models of speech recognition through CI stimulated auditory system could provide a tool to optimize large-scale signal processing and stimulation parameters for simulated individual implant patients.

Methods

The Whisper Speech Recognition Model

This study employed the OpenAI Whisper, an open-source publicly available multi-lingual deep-leaning speech recognition model (https://github.com/openai/whisper). We used the large model of Whisper version 1.1.10. Whisper was selected for this study due to its exceptional performance in various types of noisy and distorted speech, which is comparable to normally hearing humans 1. Whisper has been trained on a very large corpus of around 680,000 h of multilingual speech. The training set consisted of speech of various qualities recorded in different naturally realistic environments including different types and levels of background noise and other natural distortions. The Whisper model boasts a complex, advanced architecture with over 1.5 billion parameters associated with a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and a sophisticated transformer network. Transformers leverage the contextual dependencies across words and sentences40 and are widely employed in the modern natural language processing systems. With default settings, the Whisper model assigns a probability to the possible candidates for each word in a sentence and chooses among the highest probability candidates. This multi-candidate word decoding process can improve sentence recognition performance 1. Due to the probabilistic decoding, model’s performance might vary between different runs of the same speech token, somewhat analogues to perceptual variability and uncertainty in human speech perception.

The initial stage in almost all speech recognition models, preceding the deep learning language processing stages, is audio feature analysis. The audio analysis stage typically computes a mel-frequency and log-amplitude spectrogram, replicating the frequency analysis in the healthy human cochlea. This spectrogram is utilized by the deep learning stages of the model for phonemic and linguistic processing. Despite efforts to replicate the analysis in healthy human cochlea, the audio analysis in automatic speech recognition models does not perfectly match or fully represent the nuances of human auditory processing. The following section provides an overview of the technical characteristics of the audio feature analysis used in the Whisper speech recognition model. This information is crucial for interpreting Whisper results and for comparing these results with human CI simulation studies.

Audio Feature Analysis of Whisper

The initial audio analysis stage of Whisper involves computing a mel-spectrogram by applying a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) to overlapping frames of the input audio. FFT frames are constructed by sliding a Hanning window over the audio signal, with the amplitude of audio samples scaled by the Hanning amplitudes. The length of audio frames is 400 samples, which equals to 25 ms given the input audio sampling rate is 16,000 samples/second. The hop-length of the sliding windows (i.e., the non-overlapping samples between adjacent frames) is 160 samples, equal to 10ms. The amplitude FFTs of the consecutive audio frames are concatenated to construct a linear-frequency spectrogram. This spectrogram has 200 frequency bins, which is half of the FFT frame’s sample size, and spans frequencies up to 8,000 Hz, i.e. half the sampling rate. Therefore, the frequency resolution of the linear spectrogram defined as frequency range divided by the number of frequency bins is 40 Hz. The next step in audio analysis involves transforming the linear spectrogram to a logarithmic scale, both in frequency and amplitude. The logarithmic-frequency spectrogram is obtained by combining the linear-spectrogram values across the frequency domain using the Mel scale, which is derived from the frequency resolution in the human cochlea. Whisper’s mel-frequency spectrogram consists of 80 frequency bins. Finally, the amplitude of the mel-frequency spectrogram is log-transformed to construct a log-amplitude mel-frequency spectrogram. This final spectrogram is the input to the deep learning language processing stages of the model.

Vocoder Processing

Vocoder stimuli were generated through the signal processing stages shown in Fig. 10. The first stage is dividing the input signal into N frequency channels using non-overlapping band-pass filters. The output of band-passed filters is half-wave rectified and low-pass filtered to obtain channel envelopes. The envelope from each band-pass channel modulates a narrow-band random noise obtained using the band-pass filter corresponding to the frequency channel. In some of the vocoder simulations of this study, the extracted envelopes were subjected to additional degradations before multiplying by narrow-band noise, including dynamic range reduction and quantization. Finally, the modulated noise bands for each channel are band-pass filtered using the channel’s band-pass range, and are summed to produce the final vocoder output. The final band-pass filter ensures that the modulated noise bands remain within the channel bounds. Low-pass and band-pass filtering was implemented using forth and sixth order Butterworth filters, respectively.

The vocoder processing parameters that were manipulated in this study and the rage of parameter values are shown in Table 1. These parameters intended to simulate CI signal processing factors as well as psychophysical limitations in individual CI patients. Number of channels: This parameter simulates the number of active electrodes in CI devices. Number of channels was varied between 4 and 22, which spans the range of active electrodes in the majority of CI patients.

Channel frequencies: The frequency boundaries for vocoder channels were determined in two ways: 1) manipulating the lowest and highest frequency boundaries of the vocoder processing with equal-octave bandwidth across channels, 2) mimicking the standard channel boundaries of commercial Cochlear devices (Cochlear Ltd., Macquarie Park, AU). The bandwidth for equal-octave channel boundaries was determined by converting the overall frequency range of the vocoder processing (difference between the highest and lowest frequency boundaries) to octaves and then dividing the octave range by the number of vocoder channels. The obtained octave bandwidth was used to calculate frequency boundaries for individual channels starting from the lowest frequency boundary.

Envelope low cut-off frequency: The low cutoff of channel envelopes was manipulated to simulate the range of envelope frequencies that is accessible to CI users. Envelope cutoff frequency was varied between 5 and 400 Hz. The results have significant implications for determining envelope frequencies that provide significant speech information and should be accessible to CI users.

Envelope dynamic range (DR): The dynamic range (DR) of channel envelopes was manipulated to evaluate the range of envelope amplitudes that contribute to speech intelligibility. Manipulating envelope DR involved setting a binary threshold for envelope intensities, with any values below this threshold reduced to zero. The DR was defined as the dB range between this threshold and the maximum envelope amplitude across all channels for a given stimulus. Envelope dynamic range values tested in this study ranged between 10 and 100 dB.

Envelope quantization: We manipulated the number of quantized levels in vocoder envelopes to simulate the number of discriminable envelope levels in CI users. Smaller number of discriminable envelope levels indicates poorer sensitivity to envelope intensity increments or intensity resolution. Our goal was to assess how an impaired sensitivity to envelope level increment could affect CI users’ speech perception. The number of discriminable envelope levels, determined by psychophysical loudness discrimination ability 35, can vary from a few to several tens in CI users 41. We implemented envelope quantization by dividing the envelope dynamic range (DR) in dB into a number of quantized levels and rounding envelope intensities between adjacent quantized levels to the higher level. The step size between adjacent quantized levels was logarithmic and was determined by dividing the envelope DR in dB by the number of quantized levels. We used logarithmic quantization steps, aligning with the logarithmic amplitude transformation used in Whisper (Fig. 9) and the discriminable acoustic level steps in normally hearing human listeners. The number of quantization steps tested in this study was between 1 and 100. Speech testing

Stimuli

Whisper model was tested on a set of 60 AzBio Sentences 19 and 200 CNC words. IEEE sentences were used for comparison with human vocoder studies as discussed in section "Comparison to the published human results". The same set of words and sentences was used for the different vocoder and noise conditions. The sentences were presented as 60 separate audio files. The 200 CNC words were divided in six lists of 30 words each and one list of 20 words. The words in each list were included in an audio file. CNC words were presented as lists of words, rather than single words, for efficiency and accuracy. The Whisper model, by default, processes the audio input as 30 s segments. Shorter files are padded with zeros to create 30 s segments. The CNC word test would run much quicker when audio files consist of 30-s list of words as opposed to a single word. We also found that the model output was more accurate with audio files consisting of a word list than a single word.

The vocoder-processed sentence and word stimuli were evaluated both in quiet and in the presence of speech-shaped noise at 5dB SNR (signal to noise ratio) or two-talker babble noise (as discussed in section "Comparison to the published human results"). Speech shaped noise was generated using the pyAcoustic python package (https://github.com/timmahrt/pyAcoustics/blob/main/pyacoustics). Two-talker babble noise as generated with the same method as Goupell et al. 21. Since the probabilistic selection factor was used at the decoding stage of the Whisper model, the model output could vary each time it was run. We ran the Whisper model 5 times for each set of 60 sentences and 200 words at each vocoder condition, and used these repetitions to estimate true mean and standard error of the mean.

Scoring

We implemented automated scoring algorithms to grade the results of sentence and word recognition by Whisper. It was not feasible to grade hundreds of thousands of words and sentences by hand. The scoring was done by comparing the text output of the Whisper model, which we refer to as model result, to the transcription of the input audio, which we refer to as audio transcript. The first step in the automatic scoring was to “clean” the model results and audio transcripts by removing all punctuations and lower-casing all letters. We implemented separate grading algorithms for sentence and word stimuli.

Sentence Scoring Algorithm: In this algorithm, each single sentence was graded by counting the number of common words between the model result and the audio transcript of that sentence. The total percent correct score for sentences was obtained by summing the individual sentence grades and dividing by the total number of words in the set of sentences. The algorithm for grading sentences did not take the order of words into account. For example, the two sentences “Abbey skipped rocks” and "Skipped Abbey rocks" were graded as 3 equal words. This approach is consistent with how speech perception tests with human subjects are commonly graded.

Words scoring Algorithm: The words scoring algorithm compared the model result for each 30-word list to the transcribed input of the list on a word-by-word basis. To obtain the final score, the number of words correctly recognized by the model was divided by the total number of words. One major challenge in scoring words was that the model result and the audio transcript lists didn’t always have the same number of words. The model could miss words or interpret a single word as multiple words. We addressed this challenge by using an iterative approach to finding the optimal alignment that resulted in the highest word similarity score between the audio transcript and the model result. The optimal word-by-word alignment between model result and audio transcript was found by iteratively sampling single words from the model result or from the audio transcript and calculating the word-by-word similarity score for the remaining set of words. Each sampled word was then replaced and the scoring was repeated until all the words were sampled one at a time. The sampled word associated with the highest similarity score was permanently removed and the process of sampling single words and calculating similarity score was repeated. The algorithm stopped when the number of remaining words in the model result and the audio transcript were equal. The highest score that was obtained with different alignments was used as the true correct word score.

Another issue in scoring words was to address homophones, which are words that are pronounced the same but spelled differently. An example is “whine” and “wine”. Homophones are phonetically identical and should be scored as correct when comparing the results of speech recognition model to the audio transcript. Scoring of homophones was addressed by implementing the grapheme-to-phoneme (g2p) python package (https://pypi.org/project/g2p-en/) to analyze all words that had different spellings and compared them by phonetic content rather than just spelling.
