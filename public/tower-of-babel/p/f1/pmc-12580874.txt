DNA Methylation in Ovarian and Endometrial Cancers: Predictive and Mechanistic Roles in PARP Inhibitor and ICI Response
Authors: Shuhei Kitamura, Ayumi Taguchi, Kana Tamai, Yoko Yamamoto, Anh Quynh Duong, Daisuke Yoshimoto, Ayako Mori, Aya Ishizaka, Saki Tsuchimochi, Kenbun Sone, Masahito Kawazu, Katsutoshi Oda, Yasushi Hirota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer treatment is shifting from an organ‐based approach to one driven by biological phenotypes, emphasizing the need to understand molecular mechanisms. DNA methylation plays a pivotal role in tumor biology, not only through gene silencing but also by inducing distinct behaviors beyond genetic mutations. In gynecologic cancers, molecular diagnostics, such as homologous recombination deficiency status guiding poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitor therapy in ovarian cancer and deficient mismatch repair/microsatellite instability‐high status informing immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy in endometrial cancer have already been used in clinical practice. However, tumors with epigenetically driven functional deficiencies, such as BRCA1 promoter methylation in homologous recombination‐deficient ovarian cancers or MLH1 promoter methylation in deficient mismatch repair/microsatellite instability‐high endometrial cancers, often exhibit poorer prognoses and reduced therapeutic responses compared to their genetically mutated counterparts. Given the unique impact of DNA methylation, precise detection is crucial. Integrating methylation analysis into molecular classification could refine diagnostics—both by identifying mechanistic contributors to treatment response and by serving as predictive biomarkers for therapy selection—thereby optimizing patient management. This review explores the role of DNA methylation in modulating responses to PARP inhibitors and ICIs, highlights its promise as a biomarker in precision oncology, and outlines current developments and clinical challenges in BRCA1 and MLH1 methylation assays.

Introduction

Cancer treatment is shifting from an organ‐specific approach to one based on biological phenotypes, emphasizing the importance of understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms. Among these, DNA methylation has emerged as a key area of research not only because of its strong association with tumor phenotypes through gene silencing, but also due to its ability to induce distinct biological behaviors compared to gene mutations, which represent another form of gene inactivation.

In gynecologic cancers, companion diagnostics based on molecular phenotypes—such as homologous recombination (HR) status guiding the use of poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors in ovarian cancer, and mismatch repair (MMR) status or microsatellite instability (MSI) status guiding immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) use in endometrial cancer—have already been implemented in clinical practice.

However, tumors with the same functional deficiencies caused by epigenetic mechanisms, such as Breast Cancer Susceptibility Gene 1 (BRCA1) promoter methylation in HR‐deficient ovarian cancers or MutL homolog 1 (MLH1) promoter methylation in MMR‐deficient (dMMR) or MSI‐high (MSI‐H) endometrial cancers, tend to have poorer overall survival (OS) compared to those with underlying gene mutations, with BRCA1 promoter methylation leading to earlier resistance to platinum‐based chemotherapy and MLH1 promoter methylation (MM) associated with reduced responses to ICIs [1, 2]. Notably, recent findings from our own cohort indicate that BRCA1 promoter methylation may be more frequent in Japanese patients with high‐grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC) than previously reported [3]. This highlights potential ethnic variability in epigenetic drivers and the importance of region‐specific data in refining molecular classification strategies.

As DNA methylation‐related dysfunction differs from genetic mutations, accurate and timely detection is becoming more important. Integrating DNA methylation analysis into the current molecular classification system could improve diagnostic accuracy (Figure 1).

In this review, we explore the influence of DNA methylation on the efficacy of PARP inhibitors and ICIs, highlighting its dual significance as a predictive biomarker and a mechanistic contributor to treatment response.

Gene Silencing by DNA Methylation

DNA methylation is an epigenetic regulatory process in which a methyl group (CH3) is added to the 5th carbon of cytosine residues. Although 60%–90% of cytosine‐phosphate‐guanine (CpG) nucleotides in the genome are methylated, CpG islands (CpG‐rich regions) within the gene promoter regions are typically unmethylated, allowing normal gene transcription [4]. DNA methylation is mediated by the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) family. DNMT1 maintains existing methylation patterns during DNA replication [5], whereas DNMT3A and DNMT3B introduce de novo methylation to unmethylated regions [6]. When methylation occurs in promoter regions, the binding of transcription factors to DNA is inhibited, leading to gene silencing.

Methylated DNA is specifically recognized and bound by the methylated CpG‐binding domain (MBD) protein group, which recruits histone‐modifying enzymes [7]. This leads to changes in chromatin structure, resulting in chromatin condensation and further transcriptional suppression. Thus, DNA methylation, in conjunction with histone modifications, regulates gene expression, particularly in tumor suppressor genes. This mechanism is believed to contribute to cancer development and progression.

When promoter methylation occurs in tumor suppressor genes, it can lead to a phenotype comparable to that caused by genetic mutations. However, methylation‐induced alterations and those induced by mutations may differ in their stability and drug sensitivity. Therefore, we focused on the clinical significance of DNA methylation in two key phenotypes: HRD in ovarian cancer and dMMR in endometrial cancer.

The Role of DNA Methylation in HR‐Deficient Ovarian Cancer: Implications for the Sensitivity of PARP Inhibitors

HRD and PARP Inhibitors in Ovarian Cancer

Defects in HR cause genomic instability (GI) and promote tumorigenesis [8]. HRD is present in 50%–60% of HGSOC [1, 9, 10]. In HR‐deficient tumors, including ovarian cancer and other HRD‐related malignancies, cells evade cell death by utilizing alternative repair pathways such as nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ), which repairs double‐strand breaks (DSBs) in an error‐prone manner [11]. However, when PARP inhibitors prevent the repair of single‐strand breaks, these lesions accumulate and eventually convert into DSBs [11]. Since NHEJ alone is insufficient to manage this excessive DSB burden, the accumulation of unrepaired DSBs ultimately leads to apoptosis. This simultaneous inhibition of the two complementary pathways leading to cell death is known as synthetic lethality and allows for cancer‐specific lethality without affecting normal cells [12]. Given that PARP efficacy depends on HR status, HRD testing (MyChoice CDx test) is used as a companion diagnosis of PARP inhibitors, mainly in newly diagnosed, advanced ovarian carcinomas. Currently, the MyChoice CDx test is widely used to determine HR status and is based on two components: the presence of BRCA1/2 mutations and the GI score derived from metrics such as loss of heterozygosity, telomeric allelic imbalance, and large‐scale state transitions [13]. However, a critical limitation of this approach is that the GI score reflects cumulative, irreversible genomic damage (i.e., genomic instability) and does not capture dynamic changes in HR capacity over time. Therefore, in cases where BRCA1 promoter methylation is lost and HR function is restored, the GI score would remain elevated in recurrent tumors and may falsely indicate HRD. This disconnect poses a significant challenge for real‐time assessment of HR status and treatment decision‐making at recurrence in ovarian cancer.

Characteristics of DNA Methylation in HR‐Deficient Ovarian Cancer

The most pivotal cause of HRD is BRCA1/2 mutation—both germline (linked to hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (HBOC)) and somatic—found in 21%–28% of HGSOC cases [1, 9, 11]. Additionally, 10%–15% of HGSOC show HRD due to BRCA1 promoter hypermethylation, which silences BRCA1 gene expression (Figure 2A) [1, 14]. Key trials about newly diagnosed advanced ovarian carcinomas support this: SOLO‐1 showed that olaparib significantly improved progression‐free survival (PFS) in germline BRCA‐mutated ovarian cancer [15]; PAOLA‐1 demonstrated the added benefit of olaparib with bevacizumab, especially in HRD cases identified by the MyChoice CDx test [16]; and the PRIMA trial confirmed the efficacy of niraparib as a maintenance therapy, particularly in patients with HRD [17].

However, tumors with BRCA1 promoter hypermethylation, despite being classified as HRD, often have a poor prognosis similar to HR‐proficient tumors [18, 19]. One explanation is that BRCA1 promoter hypermethylation can be reversed through demethylation after chemotherapy or treatment with a PARP inhibitor, thereby restoring BRCA1 function and potentially leading to PARP inhibitor resistance [20]. Supporting this, the ARIEL‐2 trial demonstrated that loss or reduction of BRCA1 methylation at recurrence in patients initially positive for BRCA1 methylation was associated with decreased platinum sensitivity and a lower response to rucaparib [21]. Even in cases with pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations, reversion mutations can restore BRCA1 function by recovering the open reading frame [22]. Since demethylation is more reversible than mutations, it highlights the importance of timely BRCA1 methylation status assessment in ovarian cancer treatment (Figure 2B).

RAD51C also plays a key role in HR; pathogenic mutations and aberrant promoter hypermethylation of RAD51C have been reported in approximately 1% and 1%–3% of HGSOC cases, respectively [1, 14]. Similar to patients with pathogenic BRCA1 mutations, those with germline RAD51C mutations have a better prognosis than those without pathogenic variants in HR‐related genes [23]. Bernards et al. demonstrated that, unlike mutations, RAD51C and BRCA1 methylation were not associated with improved survival or greater sensitivity to platinum chemotherapy [24]. Furthermore, Nesic et al. reported that under PARP inhibitor treatment, loss of RAD51C promoter methylation led to the acquisition of PARP resistance in a patient‐derived xenograft model [25].

As the methylation of BRCA1 and RAD51C can be reversible, timely assessment of these methylation states is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies.

Next, we turn to a parallel mechanism—mismatch repair deficiency—and examine how MM impacts both MMR status and the response to ICI in endometrial cancer.

DNA Methylation and Its Impact on the Efficacy of ICI in Endometrial Cancer

MMR Status and ICI

In addition to HR, MMR represents a key DNA repair mechanism, and its deficiency can be a strong marker for ICIs in pan‐cancers. dMMR can also be driven by either genetic mutations in MMR genes or epigenetic promoter hypermethylation of MLH1 [26]. A comparative understanding of these genetically versus epigenetically mediated phenotypes offers a novel framework for elucidating resistance mechanisms and improving therapeutic strategies across tumor types. Genetic mutations in MMR genes can occur either in the germline, as seen in Lynch syndrome (LS), or somatically, giving rise to so‐called Lynch‐like (LL) tumors. In contrast, epigenetic silencing of MLH1 via promoter hypermethylation is typically observed in sporadic cases [26]. These distinct mechanisms of dMMR generation differ not only in etiology but also in their clinical and immunologic characteristics, with implications observed across multiple cancer types, including those associated with LS.

dMMR cancer cells elicit antitumor immunity via the production of neoantigens, which are recognized by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). However, cancer cells evade CTL attack via immune checkpoint pathways. Tumors with high neoantigen loads, such as MSI‐H or tumor mutational burden (TMB) ‐high tumors, respond well to ICIs [27, 28]. MSI‐H results from dMMR‐induced genomic instability, which also leads to neoantigen generation [29]. Though MSI‐H/dMMR tumors activate immune responses, they often escape through checkpoint mechanisms, making them responsive to ICIs [30].

Clinical trials have confirmed the efficacy of ICIs in dMMR tumors. In the KEYNOTE‐158 trial, pembrolizumab showed durable responses across MSI‐H/dMMR cancers [31]. In endometrial cancer, the KEYNOTE‐868 and RUBY trials demonstrated that adding ICIs (pembrolizumab or dostarlimab) to chemotherapy significantly improved PFS, especially in patients with dMMR tumors [32, 33].

Characteristics of MLH1 Promoter Methylation in dMMR Endometrial Cancer

It has been reported that approximately 20% of endometrial cancers are dMMR tumors [34, 35]. The causes of dMMR in endometrial cancer can be broadly categorized as either genetic (germline or somatic mutations in MMR genes, i.e., LS or LL) or epigenetic (MLH1 promoter methylation, MM) [36]. Germline and somatic MMR mutations account for approximately 10% and 15% of dMMR endometrial cancers, respectively [37, 38]. In contrast, most dMMR/MSI‐H endometrial cancers arise due to somatic alterations, with MM leading to MLH1 silencing as the primary factor, accounting for 72%–76% of MSI‐H endometrial cancers (Figure 3A) [37, 39].

MSI‐H/dMMR tumors caused by MM present a treatment challenge, as they do not respond as effectively as those linked to LS/LL [2, 30, 40, 41, 42]. For instance, in a study of 1673 endometrial cancers with MLH1 aberrations (1492 hypermethylated and 181 mutated), overall survival (OS) was significantly worse in MM compared to LS/LL (hazard ratio = 1.5; 41.4 vs. 55.9 months; p = 0.005) [2]. Furthermore, although based on a small cohort, Bellone et al. reported that dMMR endometrial cancers treated with pembrolizumab showed a worse prognosis in cases with MM than in LS/LL‐associated cases [40]. One possible reason for poor response to ICIs is the immune microenvironment in MM tumors, which tend to have lower PD‐L1 positivity [41] and reduced TMB and immune cell infiltration (Figure 3B,C) [42].

Understanding the impact of BRCA1 and MLH1 promoter methylation is essential, as they share certain biological features but differ in clinical implications. In both cases, promoter demethylation can restore the underlying repair pathway—HR‐deficient to HR‐proficient for BRCA1, and dMMR to MMR‐proficient (pMMR) for MLH1. However, key genomic consequences persist: BRCA1 demethylation leaves irreversible GI, whereas MLH1 demethylation leaves an irreversible, historical mutational and neoantigen burden accumulated during prior dMMR. This distinction may affect drug responsiveness. For PARP inhibitors, real‐time HR capacity largely determines sensitivity, making BRCA1 methylation status highly relevant at recurrence. However, for ICIs, the mutational and neoantigen burden accumulated in the past remains a major determinant of response, even after MMR restoration. Consequently, serial MM monitoring may not have a clear predictive value for ICI efficacy, although baseline MM remains important for classification (epigenetic vs. genetic), prognosis, and contextualizing the immune microenvironment.

Impact of DNA Methylation on Antigen Presentation Capability and Its Association With ICI Sensitivity

As discussed earlier, MM–driven dMMR tumors often respond less favorably to ICIs than those with MMR gene mutations, despite having similar MSI‐H/TMB‐H features. This raises the question of whether additional immune‐evasion mechanisms contribute to ICI resistance in this subgroup. One candidate is impaired antigen presentation via the human leukocyte antigen class I (HLA‐I) pathway, which plays a crucial role in immune recognition by presenting intracellular antigens to CTLs.

HLA‐I is expressed on the surface of nearly all nucleated cells, not just antigen‐presenting cells, thereby facilitating recognition and elimination of tumor cells by CTLs. However, its functional impairments due to decreased HLA‐I expression and genomic alterations, including loss of heterozygosity (LOH), have been reported in various types of cancers, including gynecologic cancers [43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. This downregulation is a critical mechanism by which tumor cells evade recognition by CTLs and is associated with disease progression, decreased OS, and ICI resistance across various cancer types [45, 46, 47, 48, 49].

Methylation of HLA‐I‐related genes, specifically the HLA‐A and Proteasome subunit beta type‐8 (PSMB8) regions, has been associated with decreased HLA‐I expression. In estrogen receptor–negative and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–negative breast cancer, low HLA‐A expression is associated with poor prognosis, and its downregulation has been linked to DNA methylation of the HLA‐A gene [50]. In MSI‐H colorectal cancer, hypermethylation of the PSMB8 region, an HLA‐I‐related gene, was linked to reduced HLA‐I expression and suppressed CD8‐positive T‐cell infiltration [51]. Consistent with these observations, our recent study in cervical cancer demonstrated that HLA‐A promoter methylation was significantly associated with reduced HLA‐I expression and diminished CD8‐positive T‐cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment [52].

These findings suggest that epigenetic silencing of antigen presentation machinery could contribute to ICI resistance in MLH1‐methylated endometrial cancers. Moving forward, it will be crucial to further explore the relationship between methylation of HLA‐A and PSMB8 regions, the tumor immune microenvironment, and efficacy of ICIs in gynecologic cancers, especially in endometrial cancers.

Collectively, the examples of BRCA1‐ and MLH1‐driven phenotypes illustrate how promoter methylation can both parallel and diverge from genetic alterations in shaping treatment response, highlighting the need for precise, context‐aware diagnostics. Next, we evaluate current and emerging methods for detecting DNA methylation in clinical settings.

Methods for Detecting DNA Methylation

As discussed in Chapters 2 and 3, the clinical significance of detecting DNA methylation is increasingly being recognized. In parallel, various analytical methods for methylation detection have been developed.

BRCA1 Methylation Analysis

Various methods have been developed for analyzing BRCA1 promoter methylation, each with unique strengths and limitations that influence their suitability for research versus clinical use. Representative techniques include methylation‐specific PCR (MSP), bisulfitesequencing, pyrosequencing, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), Methylation‐Specific Multiplex Ligation‐dependent Probe Amplification (MS‐MLPA), and Fragment Analysis of Methylation (FAM) (Table 1).

MSP is a PCR‐based method using methylation‐specific primers or fluorescent probes on bisulfite‐converted DNA. A study that performed MSP on formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues from 124 patients with HGSOC found hypermethylation of the BRCA1 promoter in 73.7% (56/76) of primary ovarian tumors and 20.8% (10/48) of recurrent tumors; these findings highlight the significant difference in methylation status between disease stages (p < 0.0001) [20]. MSP is a simple and cost‐effective method that serves as the basis for various bisulfite‐based methylation analyses; however, it has a limitation in that it cannot quantify the level of methylation on its own.

As a method capable of quantifying methylation levels, bisulfite sequencing and pyrosequencing enable single‐base resolution quantification. Bisulfite sequencing is a highly accurate method used to verify the accuracy of other methylation assays [3]. A study pyrosequenced 90 FFPE samples from patients with epithelial ovarian cancer without BRCA1/2 germline mutations reported BRCA1 and BRCA2 methylation in 17 of 88 cases (19.3%) and 4 of 86 cases (4.6%), respectively [56]. Furthermore, ddPCR enables highly accurate analysis and absolute quantification of methylated DNA copies, even from small amounts of DNA, and it has been reported that BRCA1 methylation‐homozygous tumors show greater sensitivity to PARP inhibitors compared to heterozygous ones [59].

Alternatively, methods that do not require bisulfite treatment have also been developed, including methylation‐sensitive restriction enzyme‐based MS‐MLPA and FAM [3, 62]. MS‐MLPA uses probe hybridization, ligation, and fragment analysis to semi‐quantitatively assess methylation status and simultaneously quantify copy number variations. In a study applying this method to FFPE samples from ovarian cancer patients, BRCA1 methylation was observed in 13 samples derived from 17% (9/53) of sporadic cases; moreover, all these samples exhibited BRCA1 LOH, suggesting that LOH of the BRCA1 locus is not rare in the HR‐deficient (i.e., genomically instable and LOH‐prone) ovarian cancers [62]. Additionally, we developed the FAM method, which does not require bisulfite treatment, hybridization, or ligation. This method is simple, rapid, and cost‐effective, and demonstrated a 97.1% concordance with target bisulfite sequencing—higher than the concordance between MS‐MLPA and target bisulfite sequencing (89.9%)—indicating high accuracy [3]. Although currently limited to frozen specimens, its compatibility with short base pair lengths suggests enough potential for adaptation to FFPE samples. Given its cost‐effectiveness and procedural simplicity, broader clinical application may be possible in the future.

Despite this diversity of analytical methods, none has been fully implemented in clinical practice. For clinical application of BRCA1 methylation analysis—particularly real‐time monitoring of methylation status in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) samples—several challenges remain owing to the nature of the specimens. Plasma cell‐free DNA (cfDNA) is present in significantly lower quantities than DNA obtained from tumor tissues. In addition, cfDNA is predominantly derived from normal tissues, and the fraction of ctDNA within cfDNA is often very low. This ctDNA fraction can be extremely small, sometimes approaching the limits of detection. Furthermore, cfDNA is highly fragmented, typically around 165 base pairs in length, further complicating analysis [64].

This is particularly relevant for BRCA1 methylation, as loss of methylation can restore HR and drive PARP inhibitor resistance, requiring precise, timely detection in recurrent ovarian cancer. Therefore, it is essential to establish methylation detection methods with both high sensitivity and applicability for fragmented DNA.

Recently, a study used MS‐qPCR to detect BRCA1 methylation in a total of 135 plasma cfDNA samples collected from ovarian cancer patients before and during chemotherapy [53]. All cfDNA samples that tested positive by MS‐qPCR (n = 62) were confirmed to have high methylation at multiple CpG sites by Sanger sequencing, with no false positives in healthy individuals, indicating very high specificity. However, the correlation with tumor tissue remains unclear, and the sensitivity of methylation detection in ctDNA has not been clearly defined.

MLH1 Methylation Analysis

Methods for analyzing MLH1 promoter methylation (MM) have been primarily developed in the context of colorectal cancer (Table 1). In sporadic cases of MSI‐high colorectal cancer, MM along with BRAF mutations is known to be a characteristic genetic feature [65]. It has been reported that MM serves as a highly sensitive marker for distinguishing sporadic from hereditary MSI‐high colorectal cancers (i.e., LS) [54, 57], highlighting its significance as a biomarker. From this perspective, MM testing has been recommended for cases with MLH1 deficiency in the context of universal screening for LS [58]. Bisulfite‐based methods such as MSP and pyrosequencing have been applied to analyze MM in FFPE samples [54, 57]. The MethyLight method, which incorporates fluorescent probes into qMSP to improve sensitivity and specificity, also enables absolute quantification and is one of the recommended techniques for LS screening [58].

Other approaches using FFPE samples include ddPCR, which has been shown to allow accurate analysis even in samples with low DNA content that are difficult to evaluate using qMSP [60]. Additionally, MS‐MLPA, which does not require bisulfite treatment, has been reported for use in FFPE samples, demonstrating sensitivity and specificity comparable to the MethyLight method and is also utilized for LS screening [58].

Universal screening was initially a concept limited to colorectal cancer; however, in 2014, Anne et al. reported that at least 41% of LS‐associated endometrial cancers did not meet the criteria of the traditional Bethesda guidelines [66]. Following this, international guidelines currently recommend universal screening for all endometrial cancer cases [67, 68]. Since then, methylation analyses of FFPE samples from endometrial cancer patients have been reported, primarily using the MethyLight method and MS‐MLPA [42, 43]. Additionally, although still unpublished, our group is currently adapting the FAM method for MM analysis [63], as we have successfully done for BRCA1 [3]. Array‐based methylation profiling has also been applied to FFPE specimens of endometrial cancers, revealing an association between MM and both low TMB and reduced tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) scores [44]. While array‐based analysis offers high sensitivity, quantitative accuracy, and genome‐wide coverage, its high cost renders it unsuitable for evaluating methylation at a small number of specific loci.

Currently, MM analysis has become more widespread and has demonstrated clinical utility, particularly in excluding sporadic cancers during LS screening. However, in endometrial cancer, the clinical significance of MM remains insufficiently understood. To further elucidate its role, additional data are needed regarding differences in prognosis, sensitivity to ICIs, and the tumor immune microenvironment according to MM status.

As discussed in Chapter 4, the clinical relevance of real‐time monitoring of MM is limited. However, MM assays using ctDNA have been successfully reported. In a study employing ddPCR on ctDNA from colorectal cancer patients, methylation analysis achieved a sensitivity of 78% and a specificity of 100%, demonstrating the feasibility of this approach [61].

Collectively, MM analysis is becoming established in certain clinical contexts, particularly in LS screening for colorectal cancer. Its potential applications also include assisting in the differential diagnosis of dMMR and clarifying its clinical implications. With the recent expansion of applications into endometrial cancer and ongoing technological advances, further efforts will be essential to fully establish its clinical utility and support broader implementation.

Conclusions and Hopes

In the treatment of gynecologic cancers, tumor characteristics, such as HRD and MSI‐H/dMMR are key biomarkers of response to PARP inhibitors and ICIs. DNA methylation is a major determinant of these phenotypes, influencing clinical outcomes beyond genetic mutations.

Despite its significance, methylation assessment remains to be integrated into clinical practice due to challenges with accuracy and cost. In particular, BRCA1 promoter methylation is a dynamic and reversible process; therefore, drug sensitivity can fluctuate during treatment. This underscores the urgent need for real‐time, repeatable monitoring approaches—such as those utilizing ctDNA—to track methylation dynamics over the course of therapy.

Importantly, the clinical relevance of methylation‐based classification extends beyond gynecologic oncology. In gliomas, the methylation status of the MGMT promoter is a well‐established predictor of response to alkylating agents and an essential component of molecular subtyping (e.g., via the Heidelberg classifier) [69]. These parallels across cancer types suggest that simplified and scalable methylation assays could be used as a pan‐cancer biomarker in precision oncology, enabling real‐time molecular classification, therapeutic stratification, and resistance monitoring in multiple tumor contexts. This represents a significant step toward integrating epigenetic biomarkers into longitudinal treatment planning across multiple cancer types.
