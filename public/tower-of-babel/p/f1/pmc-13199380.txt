Context-dependent indirect effects mediate ecological transitions between parasitism and mutualism
Authors: Yu-Hsuan Lee, Wei-Jiun Lin, Mu-Tzu Tsai, Brendan Lan, Yi-Ling Chu, Jiue-In Yang, Syuan-Jyun Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSymbiotic interactions frequently shift along a mutualism-parasitism continuum, altering host fitness, population dynamics, and microbial community structure with ecological context. Here, we integrate field surveys, reciprocal breeding experiments, feeding assays, and microbiome profiling to dissect interactions between phoretic mite (Poecilochirus carabi), nematode (Rhomborhabditis regina), and their burying beetle host (Nicrophorus nepalensis) during reproduction. We show that high nematode densities reduce beetle offspring survival during metamorphosis, but co-occurring mites mitigate these costs by preying on nematodes and suppressing vertical transmission, thereby enhancing beetle fitness. Both mites and nematodes are equally effective at suppressing bacterial loads on carrion, but mites drive greater shifts in carcass microbiome composition. Carcasses exposed to both symbionts display the most pronounced shifts in bacterial communities, suggesting that inter-symbiont interactions have cascading effects on host-associated microbiota. Intriguingly, in the absence of nematodes, mites impose fitness costs on their beetle hosts. These results demonstrate that the net effects of mites on beetle fitness emerge largely through indirect, density-dependent interactions with nematodes and microbial competitors. By integrating macro-symbiont and microbiome perspectives, our study highlights how phoresy extends beyond passive dispersal to actively restructure host–symbiont networks, highlighting the overlooked potential of indirect effects in driving context-dependent mutualism within multisymbiotic systems.Subject terms: Behavioural ecology, Community ecology

Introduction

Symbiotic interactions, defined as intimate associations between species, are fundamental to evolutionary processes and biodiversity maintenance1–3. These relationships span a spectrum from parasitism, where one species benefits at the host’s expense, to mutualism, where both parties gain. Commensalism occupies the intermediate position; yet, rather than a fixed category of strict neutrality2, it is increasingly recognized as a dynamic ‘grey area’. It often involves subtle cost-benefit tradeoffs that can tip the interaction along the mutualism-parasitism continuum depending on ecological conditions or species interactions4–7. These shifts can feed back into host population dynamics, amplifying or stabilising symbiotic effects over time8. Understanding the drivers of this variability is critical for unravelling the ecological significance of symbioses and their cascading effects on communities. Multisymbiotic systems, where hosts simultaneously harbour multiple symbionts, represent a key yet underexplored driver of such variation9,10.

In multisymbiotic systems, interactions among co-occurring symbionts can amplify or mitigate their effects on hosts. In particular, infection by one parasite may facilitate or suppress another’s success, as species compete for host resources or space, altering host conditions beyond single-infection scenarios9,11,12. Such interactions are classically understood as indirect ecological effects, where one species alters the fitness of another species not directly, but by modifying the abundance or effect of another species13,14. Early evidence for such dynamics comes from bark beetle-fungus associations, where the southern pine beetle harbours both antagonistic and mutualistic fungi whose competitive interactions shift outcomes for the host15,16. These studies demonstrated that context-dependent shifts between mutualism and antagonism can shape host fitness, a pattern later documented across diverse host–symbiont systems2,17. Such interactions can yield unexpected outcomes: a parasite might benefit the host by outcompeting a more virulent rival18, potentially boosting host population growth, or multiple parasites may synergistically exacerbate harm, reducing it. For example, prior infection of Daphnia magna by a less virulent strain of the bacterium Pasteuria ramosa has been shown to reduce the fitness of a subsequently invading, more virulent P. ramosa strain19. A pivotal determinant of these dynamics is symbiont density. High parasite loads often intensify costs, such as resource depletion or immune activation. A classic case involves freshwater branchiobdellids17, which transition from mutualistic gill cleaners to parasites that consume host tissue at elevated densities. Moreover, the density of one symbiont can modulate its interactions with others—via predation, competition, or facilitation20—reshaping its collective influence on host fitness and population stability21,22. These density-dependent effects highlight the sophisticated balance within host-symbiont systems and the need to integrate them into ecological and evolutionary frameworks.

Phoresy, where symbionts hitchhike on hosts for dispersal without affecting the fitness of the host during the transportation phase, exemplifies this complexity23,24. Phoretic relationships are widely found across taxa, particularly among small-bodied organisms such as mites and nematodes. In invertebrates, particularly those relying on ephemeral resources like carrion or dung, phoresy has long been interpreted as a commensal interaction where the phoront benefits from dispersal without impacting the host9,24 (Fig. 1). Yet, an increasing amount of evidence challenges this assumption4,18,25,26. Phoretic mites on dung beetles prey on pathogenic nematodes in sheep faeces, reducing parasite loads27, while mites on burying beetles reduced nematode densities by six-fold, mitigating density-dependent fitness costs for adults13. However, mites could harm the beetles by preying upon the eggs or larvae, particularly at high mite densities25,28, and in the absence of common enemies18. These findings suggest that phoronts can act as precursors to mutualism or parasitism, contingent on ecological context. However, while mite-mediated nematode suppression could potentially benefit adult beetles, whether these advantages extend to beetle offspring—crucial for long-term population dynamics—remains untested. Similarly, the role of nematode density in tipping phoretic mites along the mutualism-parasitism continuum lacks empirical grounding, despite its theoretical plausibility within multisymbiotic systems.

Burying beetles Nicrophorus spp. provide an ideal system to address these density-dependent symbiotic dynamics, as they co-host phoretic mites and nematodes throughout their reproductive cycle26,29 (Fig. 1). These symbionts are horizontally transmitted among adult beetles and vertically passed from parents to offspring during breeding on carcasses (video S1). Prior to breeding, beetles transport the mites as deutonymphs13 and nematodes as dauers30 (Fig. S1), both of which are resistant to environmental stress (e.g., desiccation, heat stress). While mites are carried externally and nematodes internally—reducing opportunities for direct interaction—both reproduce on the carcass prepared by the beetles29 (Fig. 1). Post-breeding, symbionts disperse with adult beetles to new carcasses, or remain with developing larvae. Crucially, the carrion resource serves as an intense arena for microbial competition, where rapid bacterial decomposition poses a major threat to beetle reproductive success31. As both mites and nematodes are widely known bacterivores, they may actively regulate the carcass microbiome32,33, potentially suppressing pathogenic or putrefactive taxa (e.g., Staphylococcus and Proteus) and altering the sanitary environment for developing beetle offspring. Although the dual presence of mites and nematodes is well-documented, the mechanisms by which these symbionts interact—via predation, competition, or microbial modulation—and their cascading effects on host reproduction and host offspring fitness remain poorly understood. This multisymbiotic system thus offers a valuable opportunity to test how symbiont interactions and density tip the balance between mutualism and parasitism in a complex host-symbiont network23.

Here, we integrated field surveys and laboratory experiments to investigate the multisymbiotic dynamics and their interaction outcomes during breeding cycles of the burying beetle Nicrophorus nepalensis. We began by quantifying natural densities and co-occurrence of nematodes and mites on wild beetles, followed by morphological and genetic identification of nematode species. We then manipulated nematode densities (0, 10, 100 individuals per beetle) and mite presence/absence during breeding to evaluate fitness effects on beetles, nematodes, and mites, and to track vertical transmission to beetle offspring. To determine whether mites suppress nematodes through direct antagonism, we conducted feeding trials that confirmed mite predation on nematodes. In parallel, we isolated bacterial colonies from beetle-prepared carcasses and tested whether mites and/or nematodes influence microbial load, providing functional evidence for symbiont-driven microbial regulation. Finally, we carried out 16S rRNA gene sequencing to evaluate how these interactions (i.e., beetle-mite-nematode) shape the composition of carcass-associated bacterial communities. This integrated approach allowed us to disentangle nematode-driven costs from potential mite-mediated benefits, revealing that mite predation on nematodes and symbiont-driven shifts in carcass microbiota can fundamentally alter the balance between parasitism and mutualism in this multisymbiotic system.

Results

Mites and nematodes co-occur but are differentially distributed upon their host burying beetles

We sampled a total of 124 field burying beetles Nicrophorus nepalensis to assess natural symbiont loads. Of these, 77 (62.1%) carried the mite Poecilochirus carabi, with a mean abundance of 8.9 ± 20.1 per infested beetle (median: 4; range: 1–148); 100 (80.6%) of beetles hosted the nematode Rhomborhabditis regina, with a mean abundance of 14.5 ± 20.4 per infested beetle (median: 7, range: 1–103). Co-occurrence was common, with 62 beetles (50%) hosting both symbionts. The mites attached externally to various beetle body surfaces (e.g., pronotum, thorax)9, whereas the nematodes occupied the beetles both externally and internally. In laboratory-reared beetles, the nematode abundance was significantly higher (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W(153) = 541, p < 0.001), with a mean abundance of 50.5 ± 48.8 per infested beetle (median: 32; range: 3–192). Nematode distribution across body parts varied significantly (χ² = 582.46, d.f. = 2, p < 0.001; Fig. S2a), with the highest counts in genitals (video S2), followed by intestines, and the fewest counts were on the beetle’s external surface. Proportional distributions mirrored this pattern (χ² = 891.22, d.f. = 2, p < 0.001; Fig. S2b).

Mites increase beetle fitness at low nematode densities and reduce nematode transmission

We manipulated initial nematode densities (0, 10, 100 individuals per beetle) and mite presence/absence during beetle breeding to test their effects on reproduction. Neither mites (χ² = 3.09, d.f. = 1, p = 0.079) nor nematodes (χ² = 0.36, d.f. = 2, p = 0.835) affected the clutch size (Fig. S3). Brood size remained unaffected by either symbiont (Table S1). However, mite presence increased total brood mass, independent of nematode density (Table S1). Average larval mass also rose with mites, but this effect interacted with nematode density (Table S1). Specifically, mites boosted larval mass by 8.0% at 0 nematodes (t = −3.30, p = 0.001) and 19.8% at 10 nematodes (t = −3.55, p < 0.001), yet had no effect at 100 nematodes (t = −0.28, p = 0.778). Larval mass also scaled inversely with brood density (brood size divided by carcass size) but was unrelated to female size (Table S1). Nematodes dispersing with females were comparable at 10 and 100 densities, but mite presence reduced this by 69.3% (Fig. 3a; Table S2). Mite offspring dispersal peaked at 0 nematodes and declined in the presence of nematodes (Fig. 3b; Table S2).

To further understand nematode transmission to the beetle offspring, we tested whether nematodes persist through metamorphosis by selectively removing them externally during the larval and/or pupal stages of beetle (see “Methods”). When sterilisation was applied only during the larval stage, 67% of emerging adults still carried nematodes, compared to 93% in the untreated control (Fig. S4). Larval sterilisation reduced the probability of nematode presence (posterior estimate = −4.39 ± 2.59, 95% CI: −10.89 to −0.70), while pupal sterilisation had an even stronger effect (estimate = −16.81 ± 7.87, 95% CI: −37.59 to –7.19). There was no evidence of residual nematode presence when pupae were sterilised, regardless of larval sterilisation. These findings suggest that nematodes associate with beetle larvae both externally and internally, but ultimately relocate to the external surface during pupation, before re-entering hosts upon adult emergence (Fig. 1).

Mites reverse nematode-induced survival cost to beetle offspring at high nematode densities

Nematode densities and their interaction with mites shaped the survival of beetle offspring to eclosion (interaction initial nematode density x mite treatment, χ² = 19.13, d.f. = 2, p < 0.001; Fig. 2d). Without mites, survival dropped from 96.0% at 0 nematodes to 78.4% at 100 nematodes (z = 3.62, p < 0.001). With mites, the pattern reversed: survival fell to 77.9% at 0 nematodes but rose to 88.6% at 100 nematodes (z = -2.32, p = 0.053), resulting in a higher survival compared to those without mites at 100 nematodes (z = −2.15, p = 0.032). This indicates mite-mediated rescue at high densities of nematodes but also costs of mite association in the absence of nematodes as evidenced by a lower beetle offspring survival rate (z = 3.78, p = 0.002). Among eclosed beetle offspring, nematode loads varied depending on the nematode density and mite treatment (interaction initial nematode density x mite treatment, χ² = 150.74, d.f. = 1, p < 0.001; Fig. 3c): at 10 nematodes, mite presence increased nematode counts on beetle offspring (1.20 ± 1.27) compared to those in the absence of mites (0.61 ± 0.65), but at 100 nematodes, mites reduced them to 1.19 ± 0.83 from 3.93 ± 2.84 in the absence of mites, matching levels at 10 nematodes without mites. The number of mites attaching to the eclosed beetle offspring was similar irrespective of the nematode density treatment (χ² = 3.80, d.f. = 2, p = 0.150; Fig. 3d).

Mites reduce bacterial loads more than nematodes do, while mites also prey on nematodes

To test the effects of mite-nematode interactions on microbial communities, we introduced carcass-derived bacteria onto sterile substrates and manipulated the presence/absence of the mite and the nematode (see Methods). Bacterial colony-forming units (CFUs) were affected by presence of nematodes and mites (treatment: χ² = 44.88, d.f. = 3, p < 0.001; Fig. 4a), with mites and nematodes singly reducing CFUs by 34.6% and 26.8% comparing with the control group without symbionts, respectively. Presence of both nematodes and mites resulted in the greatest significant reduction in CFUs by 61.4% (z = −6.76, p < 0.001). In a separate experiment, we assessed whether mites also prey on nematodes by introducing a fixed nematode density with a single mite, with or without pork mince as a carrion mimic (see “Methods”). After 24 h, mites reduced nematode counts by 35.3% without mince and 79.4% with mince (treatment, χ² = 85.65, d.f. = 3, p < 0.001; Fig. 4b), demonstrating predation and greater efficiency in a carrion-like environment.

Mites and nematodes alter the bacterial community composition on carrion

Building on our results that both nematodes and mites significantly reduced bacteria loads on carcasses, it remained unclear whether such reductions in bacterial load also reflect the underlying shifts in the composition and diversity of the carcass microbiome. To address this, we conducted a follow-up breeding experiment, in which we specifically sampled carcass microbiome with five treatments: (1) untreated carcasses, (2) beetles only, (3) beetles with nematodes, (4) beetles with mites, and (5) beetles with both nematodes and mites (see Methods).

Alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences among treatments in Shannon’s diversity index (Kruskal-Wallis test: χ² = 6.74, d.f. = 4, p = 0.15; Fig. S8a), indicating limited variation of the diversity within a single sample. However, beta diversity analysis revealed strong and significant differences in bacterial community composition among treatments (permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA); F5,25 = 5.50, p < 0.001; Fig. 5a; see Fig. S5 for complete beta diversity analyses using multiple ordination and distance metrics). Untreated carcasses formed a distinct cluster, clearly separated from all beetle-associated treatments. Pairwise comparisons indicated that bacterial communities on carcasses exposed to beetles with both nematodes and mites were particularly distinct from those with beetles alone (BH-adjusted p = 0.013) or beetles with nematodes only (BH-adjusted p = 0.014), whereas beetles with either symbiont alone (nematodes or mites) did not differ significantly from beetles alone. UPGMA hierarchical clustering corroborated these findings, showing that beetles with both nematodes and mites drive the greatest divergence in carcass-associated microbiota (Fig. S8b).

Taxonomic analyses revealed that beetle presence, regardless of breeding with nematodes or mites, had resulted in substantial changes in phylum-level composition in bacterial communities, especially the dominant Firmicutes, Actinobacteriota, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota (Figs. S6a, S7a). Specifically, the carcass-only group was characterized by high relative abundance of Staphylococcus (Staphylococcaceae), Enterococcus and Vagococcus (Enterococcaceae), Proteus (Morganellaceae), and Kurthia (Planococcaceae). In contrast, whereas Acinetobacter (Moraxellaceae), Morganella (Morganellaceae), Vitreoscilla (Neisseriaceae), and Myroides (Flavobacteriaceae) decreased (Fig. 5b, c, Fig. S7b). In beetle’s presence, the relative abundances of Peptoniphilus (Peptostreptococcales-Tissierellales), Acinetobacter (Moraxellaceae), Vitreoscilla (Neisseriaceae), Morganella (Morganellaceae), and Myroides (Flavobacteriaceae) increased, whereas those of Proteus (Morganellaceae) and Staphylococcus (Staphylococcaceae) slightly decreased compared to the carcass only group (Figs. 5b, c, S7b). In treatments involving addition of mites, nematodes, or both, a consistent reduction in the relative abundance of Enterococcus (Enterococcaceae) was observed, while Koukoulia (Wohlfahrtiimonadaceae) showed increased abundance only in the presence of mites, irrespective of nematode presence (Figs. 5b,c, S7b).

Discussion

Our experiments demonstrate that the phoretic mites Poecilochirus carabi shift along the mutualism-parasitism continuum with the burying beetles Nicrophorus nepalensis, and are strongly contingent on the density of the nematode Rhomborhabditis regina. Equally important, our study highlights strong indirect ecological effects: mites altered beetle fitness in ways consistent with both suppressing nematodes and shifting carcass-associated bacterial community composition. High nematode densities reduced beetle offspring survival without mites, indicating a density-dependent fitness cost. Mites mitigated these costs, reducing nematode transmission to host offspring under high densities and boosting host larval mass at low-to-moderate densities. These results support our hypotheses that mites alleviate the costs by suppressing nematode abundance and transmission. Both mites and nematodes reduced culturable bacterial loads in our assays, but mites additionally resulted in significant alteration in bacteria composition. Although our data do not resolve the relative contributions of direct consumption versus other mechanisms (e.g., physical disturbance or changes mediated by beetle activity), feeding experiments confirmed mite predation on nematodes. This predation was notably enhanced by the presence of carrion, likely due to spatial aggregation where both symbionts concentrate on the resource, increasing encounter rates. By suppressing nematodes early in the carcass preparation phase, mites may exert a functional early-stage filtering effect, sanitising the resource before vulnerable beetle larvae hatch. Yet, without nematodes, mites reduced beetle offspring survival, revealing a context-dependent cost. This finding parallels interactions in other multisymbiotic systems, such as reef-cleaning mutualisms34, where cleaners can shift from beneficial to harmful depending on the ecological context and resource availability. These findings indicate that phoretic associations extend beyond simple commensalism, exerting dynamic, context-dependent effects on host fitness within multisymbiotic systems.

Without mites, nematodes impaired beetle offspring survival but not larval mass, suggesting nematodes primarily affect viability rather than growth. Unlike prior studies reporting reduced brood size and larval mass at similar densities at 10 and 10026, we found no evidence that nematodes hinder reproduction, even at high loads. We also did not find evidence that mites or nematodes influenced the female’s reproductive investment based on our estimation of clutch size (Fig. S3). These contrasting findings might be attributed to difference in the ecological specialisation and adaptation of N. vespilloides and N. nepalensis with the nematodes, which might consist of multiple cryptic species of R. regina (just like the mite P. carabi35). These similar hidden diversity in R. regina or differences in host-associated microbial context could contribute to variation in fitness effects. Distinguishing among these alternatives would require comparative sampling across host populations and symbiont lineages. A mutually non-exclusive explanation is that differences in host- and symbiont-associated microbiomes modulate nematode effects, either directly (e.g., via pathogen abundance) or indirectly by altering carcass preservation and nutritional quality for larvae (Fig. 5). Our microbiome data are consistent with such pathways, but do not establish causality. Notably, nematodes reduced survival during beetle metamorphosis, potentially through direct physiological harm or through costs associated with re-colonisation. Indeed, the absence of mites resulted in higher beetle offspring nematode loads. Our sterilisation experiments reveal that nematodes must exit the host during the pupal stage to re-colonise the emerging adult. This obligate external phase likely exposes nematodes to predation by co-occurring mites. These findings suggest that nematodes function as conditional parasites rather than benign passengers, particularly during adult emergence. The protective role of mites prevents density-dependent mortality driven by nematodes, underscoring the necessity of integrating multisymbiotic interactions into estimates of host fitness.

Mites transition from mildly costly to mutualistic as nematode density rises, aligning with context-dependent symbiosis. At low nematode densities, mites increased larval mass but reduced survival to eclosion (Fig. 2c, d), suggesting a fitness trade-off between offspring growth and viability. This survival cost is consistent with the hypothesis that mites may occasionally harm beetle offspring (e.g., by interacting negatively with vulnerable developmental stages), but we did not directly quantify the proximate cause of mortality. Alternative explanations include mite–larva competition for carrion resources or mite-mediated changes in the carrion microbial environment that disproportionately affect survival. By contrast, at high nematode densities, mites no longer increased larval mass but did enhance survival and reduced nematode loads on beetle offspring (Fig. 2). This suggests that, under heavy nematode challenge, mite-associated benefits are expressed primarily through improved viability rather than increased growth. One possibility is that high nematode loads or associated microbial changes constrain larval growth such that the growth benefit of mites is diminished, but this requires direct tests. These opposing outcomes demonstrate how the net effect of mites on beetle fitness emerges indirectly, through density-dependent interactions with nematodes and microbial competitors, in addition to any direct mite–beetle interactions. Although mites appeared to prey on nematodes under these conditions, their reduced reproductive success compared to nematode-free environments (where they feed exclusively on carrion fluids and microbes) is consistent with a fitness cost associated with nematophagy (Fig. 3b). This trade-off could result from the nutritional limitations of nematode consumption relative to other resources or energetic costs of predation. Similar patterns have been reported in the wolf spider Schizocosa, where feeding on suboptimal prey leads to reduced growth and survival despite temporary initial benefits36.

Our findings are consistent with previous findings of mite-mediated nematode suppression in dung beetles27 and suggest that such effects can extend to host offspring performance in N. nepalensis. Notably, our bacterial culturing assays showed that both mites and nematodes reduced culturable bacterial loads, with the strongest reduction when both were present (Fig. 4a). However, we acknowledge that the mechanism driving this reduction remains to be determined. While mites are known to consume nematodes, it is unclear if they actively graze on bacteria or reduce bacterial loads indirectly through antimicrobial secretions or physical disturbance. Our results show that mite presence is associated with distinct shifts in carcass bacterial composition that may limit the proliferation of taxa linked to resource degradation or larval harm31,37 (e.g., Staphylococcus and Proteus (Fig. S7)). Our study contrasts with Duarte et al. (2017), who increased bacterial diversity and reduced antibacterial activity in N. vespilloides exudates without reproductive benefits33; differences in design and potential beetle-mediated responses may contribute to these divergent outcomes. Nevertheless, the causal direction between microbiome shifts and beetle performance remains unresolved. Microbial changes could reflect direct effects of mites and nematodes, indirect effects mediated by beetle behaviour or secretions, or both; manipulative inoculations would be required to disentangle these pathways.

By testing mites and nematodes under simplified, sterile conditions, we isolate their direct and interactive effects on bacterial load. Combined with the breeding experiment, these results suggest that symbiont–symbiont interactions can indirectly shape the ecological environment of the carcass and host fitness outcomes. We found taxon-specific shifts in bacterial relative abundance associated with mite and/or nematode presence within the carcass microbiome. Enterococcus, often considered a core member of carrion microbial communities transmitted by the beetles38, was consistently reduced across treatments involving mites, nematodes, or both. These results suggest that phoretic arthropods can influence carcass-associated microbiota rather than acting solely as passive dispersal agents. Beetle introduction alone also restructured bacterial composition, with notable decreases in putrefactive taxa such as Proteus and increases in genera like Morganella39 and Peptoniphilus. The proliferation of Peptoniphilus, an obligate anaerobe, implies that beetle burrowing creates localized oxygen-depleted microsites, shifting the microbial metabolism from aerobic putrefaction to anaerobic fermentation. This shift likely reduces toxic metabolic intermediates while preserving decomposition efficiency. The elevated abundance of Koukoulia (Wohlfahrtiimonadaceae) in the presence of mites is consistent with specialized arthropod-bacteria associations within carrion systems. As Wohlfahrtiimonadaceae are documented to possess fermentative, degradative, and nitrate reduction capabilities40–42, their enrichment suggests that mites may facilitate specific decomposition pathways, though the functional consequences for larval nutrition require targeted metabolomic verification.

These findings emphasise that the outcome of this complex symbiosis is highly contingent on the broader biotic context, specifically the modification of the shared resource by multiple phoretic partners. These patterns suggest that different phoretic species associates may differ in how they correlate with carcass microbiome composition, an idea that should be evaluated with comparative, multi-species datasets. Rather than a simple bipartite interaction, our results reveal a community-level process: beetles construct the broad-scale microbiome architecture, while phoretic mites fine-tune bacterial populations through targeted suppression. This does not necessarily imply a tight co-evolutionary relationship, but rather that the distinct ecological conditions shaped by these co-occurring symbionts likely feed back into host fitness. Future studies could further investigate the mechanistic basis of the mite’s dietary shift between microbes and nematodes through analysis of nutrients or metabolomics. Additional microbiome analyses, such as functional prediction, co-occurrence network analysis to pinpoint potential microbial interaction hubs, and differential abundance testing across treatments, could help determine whether specific ‘core’ bacterial taxa mediate these arthropod–microbe interactions. Mites’ dual roles in nematode antagonism and associations with bacterial community shifts suggest that they can generate disproportionately large indirect effects on carrion-based reproduction. The net benefit at high nematode loads suggests a mechanism by which beetle–mite associations are maintained in populations experiencing variable parasite pressure. By reducing nematode transmission to offspring under high nematode densities, mites may reduce nematode carryover across generations. This may be particularly relevant because nematodes were more prevalent than mites in our field samples and can increase rapidly after breeding (Fig. 3). While our experimental results suggest that co-occurrence can be beneficial, the observation that many field beetles host only a single symbiont likely reflects the stochastic dynamics of dispersal to ephemeral resources. Since the fitness benefits of co-occurrence are density-dependent, beetles with few nematodes may survive equally well without mites, maintaining single infections in the population—particularly given the costs of mite association at low nematode densities. Given that phoresy is common across insect systems, density-dependent shifts in net outcomes may occur more broadly, suggesting that phoronts are underappreciated key modulators of symbiotic networks. Comparative analyses across phoretic systems could help assess how general such cost–benefit switching is and identify the ecological conditions under which it occurs. More broadly, our findings demonstrate that indirect effects – arising from density-dependent interactions between mites and nematodes, together with the associated shifts in carcass microbial communities—can be major determinants of host fitness outcomes in multisymbiotic systems. Recognising these dual cascades across macro- and microbial symbionts highlights that the phoretic partners can be ecologically consequential beyond transport, and motivates field tests of whether such interaction networks have measurable implications for host population processes.

Methods

Nicrophorus nepalensis collection and symbiont quantification

To quantify natural nematode and mite densities, we collected field beetles during their breeding season (from Nov 2023 to Feb 2024) from 29 carcass-baited hanging traps in Taipei and New Taipei City, Taiwan. Traps were baited with 4-day-old mouse carcasses and left in the field for 4 days to attract beetles. We used commercially available frozen-thawed feeder mice (Mice Plus, Changhua, Taiwan) as standard carrion resources; these mice were humanely euthanized by the vendor using controlled CO₂ asphyxiation prior to −20 °C freezing. Upon capture, all beetles were transported to the laboratory, sexed, and frozen at −20 °C for 2 h to immobilise mites. Mites (Poecilochirus carabi) were then brushed off and counted. To extract nematodes (Rhomborhabditis regina), each beetle was submerged in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube with 1 mL tap water, vortexed for 1 min to dislodge surface nematodes, and dissected using pairs of tweezers and forceps to remove the abdomen. The abdomen was stored in water at room temperature (26–28 °C) for 48 hours, allowing internal nematodes to disperse into the solution for counting via a binocular stereomicroscope (Leica M205A).

To further understand the distribution of nematodes within a beetle, we examined newly eclosed adults from laboratory colonies (see below). Beetles were frozen at −20 °C for 5 min to immobilise them for dissection without affecting nematode survival, submerged in 1 mL tap water, vortexed for 1 min, and dissected to separate genitalia and intestines. Then each part was stored in tubes with 1 mL tap water for 48 h, and nematode counts per body part were recorded. This approach minimised prior breeding effects seen in field beetles.

Nematode identification

We verified nematode identity using 17 samples from nine different burying beetle families of lab colonies derived from the field study sites (see below). Nematodes were mounted on slides, photographed, and measured (body length, width) under a transmitted-light microscope (Leica DM 2500), with traits compared to the literature43. The same individuals were subsequently transferred into microcentrifuge tubes for lysis and subjected to PCR amplification using the 28S rDNA primers (D2A: D3B (5’-GTACCGTGAGGGAAAGTTG–3’/5’-TCGGAAGGAACCAGCTACTA-3’))44 to obtain the target fragment. Sequencing (Genomics BioSci & Tech Co.) and subsequent BLAST analysis against the NCBI nucleotide database confirmed that all specimens most likely belonged to the same species, R. regina (Percent identity: 85.8-86.9%; E value: 6.00 × 10−176–8.00 × 10−165).

Establishment and maintenance of laboratory colonies

Laboratory colonies of N. nepalensis, P. carabi, and R. regina were derived from six wild populations in northern Taiwan, mixed in 2022. Each year, field beetles were interbred with lab colonies to prevent inbreeding. In Sep 2023, we established a nematode-free population of burying beetles, using a surface-sterilisation protocol26. In brief, eggs were collected 12–24 h post-laying and surface-sterilised with an antimicrobial solution containing hen egg white lysozyme (1 mg/mL), streptomycin (500 μg/mL), and ampicillin (100 μg/mL). The eggs were then transferred to boxes filled with autoclaved moist soil and placed onto a freshly thawed mouse carcass, which was dissected in the abdomen to facilitate beetle larval colonisation. The first generation of larvae was reared without parental care to block potential nematode transmission, and subsequent generations were maintained with normal care on autoclaved soil. For each brood, carcasses were inspected during and at the end of breeding to confirm the absence of nematode infection. Experimental beetles were from the third generation post-sterilisation.

Nematodes were isolated from wild N. nepalensis, morphologically identified as R. regina (see above), and cultured with autoclaved soil alongside N. nepalensis. For subsequent breeding experiments, nematodes were sourced from laboratory colonies of adult beetles collected post-breeding (when nematode dauers typically disperse) on mouse carcasses (20–30 g; n = 10) since these beetles had not undergone sterilisation to remove R. regina. This approach allowed us to establish a stable source of laboratory-maintained nematode population. To maintain colonies of mites, we established ten boxes, each initially stocked with mites sourced from field-collected beetles. Once a month, 3–5 mites were randomly selected from each box, pooled to a total of 20 mites, and introduced onto a thawed mouse carcass (20–30 g) with a single beetle for breeding. Both nematode and mite colonies were maintained under standard lab conditions (mean 17.8 °C, range 16–20 °C, 10:14 light:dark cycle).

Breeding experiment

We tested mite and nematode effects on beetle reproduction using a 3 × 2 factorial design (nematode densities: 0, 10, 100; mite presence/absence). Virgin males and females, both two weeks post-eclosion, were paired for mating 24 h prior to the experiment. Body size was measured as pronotum width at its widest point (a standard measure of burying beetle body size45) to the nearest 0.01 mm using digital calipers. Females were then placed in cylindrical containers (14.2 × 6.3 cm) containing 2 cm of autoclaved soil and a thawed mouse carcass weighing 22–30 g (26.11 ± 2.04 g).

Nematodes used were sourced from other beetles that had bred successfully with a mouse carcass in a similar setup beforehand (see above). Post-breeding, beetle adults were submerged in 1 mL of tap water in 1.5 mL tubes. Tubes were vortexed for 1 min to dislodge nematodes from the beetle surface. The resulting washes from 10 beetles were pooled to create a stock solution. Aliquots were then sampled and counted under a dissecting microscope to estimate nematode density (block 1: 10.5 ± 2.3 nematodes/μL; block 2: 10.8 ± 3.8 nematodes/μL). Based on these concentrations, working solutions were diluted to reach final doses of 10 or 100 nematodes per 100 μL, which were pipetted under the beetle elytra for experimental inoculations. Ten mites per trial, within the natural range observed (see Results), were introduced directly onto the carcass, resulting in a total of 163 breeding events. After 11 days, we counted third-instar larvae (brood size) and weighed broods to 0.0001 g (brood mass), and determined average larval mass of the brood by dividing brood mass by brood size. Female beetles were frozen at −20 °C for 5 min to facilitate mite immobilisation and counting, and nematodes were quantified by vortexing beetles in 1 mL water, dissecting abdomens, and averaging the number from three 10 μL subsamples after 48 hours.

From each family, we randomly selected up to six larvae and placed them individually in eclosion boxes (2 × 2 × 2 cm) to monitor survival to adulthood. Approximately a month later, beetle offspring fitness was assessed using these individuals (830 adults from 144 families). Survival to eclosion was recorded, with mite loads counted directly on successfully eclosed beetles (n = 711), and nematode loads quantified using the same sampling method as for their female parent (see above). The experiments were conducted in two blocks, using third- and fourth-generation beetles to remove any confounding effects of the absence of parental care during the colony establishment.

Stage-specific colonisation of beetle hosts by the nematodes

To determine the developmental stage at which Rhomborhabditis regina enter Nicrophorus nepalensis, we conducted an experiment to test whether nematodes colonise beetles during larval development or only after adult eclosion. We used five beetle families that had been reared under standard breeding conditions and were naturally infected with nematodes. From each family, larvae were randomly assigned to one of the four treatments (n = 15 per treatment): (1) no sterilisation (control), (2) surface sterilisation at the larval stage, (3) surface sterilisation at the pupal stage, and (4) sterilisation at both larval and pupal stages. In all cases, beetles were reared on autoclaved soil following sterilisation. Surface sterilisation followed the same protocol previously used for generating nematode-free colonies: individuals were immersed for 1 min in an antimicrobial solution containing hen egg white lysozyme (1 mg/mL), streptomycin (500 μg/mL), and ampicillin (100 μg/mL), then rinsed with sterile water before transfer to clean substrate. Upon adult eclosion, beetles were frozen briefly at −20 °C and dissected under a stereomicroscope to examine their intestines and reproductive organs for nematode presence, following the same quantification procedures as described above. This design tested the hypothesis that nematodes may transiently colonise the larval gut but leave the host during pupation, re-entering upon eclosion. If true, sterilising pupae and rearing them in sterile conditions should result in nematode-free adults.

Feeding experiment

To test whether mites prey on nematodes, we assessed mite-induced reductions in nematode density under controlled conditions. We introduced 15 nematodes onto a moist tissue paper disc (1 cm²) in a 5-cm-diameter Petri dish. Trials were assigned to four treatments in a 2 × 2 factorial design: (1) nematodes alone (control), (2) nematodes with one mite, (3) nematodes with pork mince (0.5 g), and (4) nematodes with one mite and pork mince (n = 15 per treatment). The mince, a carrion proxy, tested whether a food source alters mite predation, as the absence of carrion might otherwise compel mites to feed on nematodes. To prevent desiccation, each Petri dish was sealed within a cylindrical container (14.2 × 6.3 cm) with a moist base. Experiments were conducted in an incubator at 17.8 °C (range 16–20 °C, 10:14 light:dark cycle) for 24 h. Post-incubation, nematodes were recovered by rinsing the tissue and mince with sterile water, then counted under a stereomicroscope (Leica M205A).

Bacterial culturing assay

To quantify the effects of nematodes and mites on carcass-associated bacterial communities, we conducted a controlled culturing experiment. Bacterial suspensions were collected from mouse carcasses (20–30 g) prepared by breeding adult Nicrophorus nepalensis beetles. Carcasses were harvested 3 days post-preparation, before egg hatching46, and rinsed with sterile water to dislodge surface bacteria. The resulting suspension was stored at 4 °C until use within 24 h.

For experimental treatments, a dry paper towel was placed in a sterile petri dish, onto which 1000 μL of the 1000 times diluted bacterial suspension was applied at the centre. Four different treatment conditions were tested: (1) 100 μL sterile water, (2) 10 nematodes in 100 μL sterile water (derived from a 1000 nematodes/mL stock), (3) one mite in 100 μL sterile water, and (4) both 10 nematodes and one mite in 100 μL sterile water. Each treatment was replicated 15 times. The prepared plates were incubated in a controlled environment simulating burying beetle breeding conditions (mean: 17.8 °C, with daily variations between 16 and 20 °C) with a 10:14 light-to-dark cycle for 24 h.

Post-incubation, the paper towel was soaked in 1 mL sterile ddH₂O to recover bacteria, and mites and nematodes were removed from the bacterial solution before storage at 4 °C for up to two days using a fine brush (mites) or filtration (nematodes). The suspension was diluted by 1000 times, and 100 μL out of 1 mL was spread onto nutrient agar (Neogen) plates using the spread-plate technique. Plates were incubated at 28 °C (a standard laboratory culture temperature that supports the growth of a broad range of bacterial taxa47) for 16–20 h, and colony-forming units (CFUs) were counted to assess bacterial growth across treatments.

Microbiome composition under nematode and mite treatments

To investigate this, we conducted a 16S rRNA-based microbiome profiling experiment. Nicrophorus nepalensis were reared on autoclaved soil, and assigned to one of the four treatment groups (n = 6 per group): (1) beetle: no symbionts added, (2) mite: 10 mite deutonymphs added, (3) nematode: 10 J2 nematodes added, and (4) nematode-mite: 10 J2s and 10 mite deutonymphs added. All symbionts were precisely quantified and introduced immediately as we introduced the beetles to breed. In addition, we included two control groups: (a) carcass: breeding boxes containing carcasses but no beetles (n = 6), and (b) soil: box with autoclaved soil only (n = 1) to serve as environmental and sterile controls, respectively. After 72 h of breeding, we collected microbial samples from the opening of the mouse carcasses using sterile cotton swabs, which were then soaked in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at −20 °C. Samples were submitted to Genomics BioSci & Tech Co. for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and microbial community analysis. This design allowed us to test whether interactions between nematodes and mites alter not only microbial abundance but also the taxonomic composition of carcass-associated microbiota under ecologically relevant breeding conditions.

Microbiome composition analysis

Briefly, DNA was extracted from each swab sample (see Supplementary information), and the V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using specific primers

(341F-805R48; forward: 5’-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGNCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’ and reverse: 5’-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGNGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3’), each with an overhang linker sequence for Illumina library preparation.

Each 25 μL PCR reaction contained 12.5 μL of KAPA® HiFi HotStart Ready Mix (KAPA BIOSYSTEMS), 5 μM of forward and reverse primers, and ~20 ng of DNA template. PCR was performed with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel.

The 1st stage amplicons were purified by AMPure XP system (Beckman) and followed by Nextera XT Index Kit v2 Set (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), and the quality of the libraries was assessed on a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and Qsep400 with N1 High Sensitivity Cartridge Kit (Bioptic Inc., Taiwan). The qualified libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform with the 300 bp paired-end reads generated by Genomics, BioSci & TechCo., New Taipei City, Taiwan.

Raw reads generated by Illumina sequencing were first subjected to adapter and primer trimming using Trimmomatic (v0.39) and Cutadapt (v3.5). Quality filtering was then performed to remove low-quality reads and bases. Read quality was evaluated with FastQC, and summary reports were aggregated using MultiQC. Denoising, chimera removal, and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference were conducted using the DADA2 algorithm implemented in QIIME2. DADA2 uses an error-correction model to distinguish sequencing errors from biological variation, and returns high-resolution ASVs with single-nucleotide accuracy. Forward and reverse reads were denoised independently, and merged reads were filtered to remove chimeras and singletons. Taxonomic classification was performed using a Naive Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA reference database (version 138, 99% similarity). Only the V3–V4 region, amplified by the specific primer set used during library preparation, was extracted from the reference database to train the classifier using QIIME2’s feature-classifier plugin.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted in R (v. 4.1.2)49 using generalised linear mixed models (GLMMs, package lme450) or generalised linear models (GLMs) where appropriate. Initial models included interactions between initial nematode density (0, 10, 100) and mite treatment (presence/absence), which were retained if significant (P < 0.05) or dropped otherwise. Significant interactions were explored with post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the package emmeans51 (Tukey-adjusted P-values). Model fit was evaluated using residual diagnostics, with overdispersion tested via the testDispersion function in the package DHARMa52.

To determine nematode distribution on beetle body parts, the number of nematodes across body parts (surface, intestines, genitals) were analysed with a negative binomial GLMM to account for overdispersion. We considered the explanatory variables the body parts, and beetle sex. Beetle identity was included as a random factor.

To investigate the effects of nematodes and mites on beetle reproductive success, we analysed the clutch size and brood size with negative binomial GLMMs to account for overdispersion, and the brood mass and average larval mass with Gaussian distribution. In these models, we included the explanatory variables initial nematode density and mite presence/absence, and their interactions. In these analyses, body size of females and carcass mass were also included as covariates since these have been shown to affect reproduction4,53. When analysing the average larval mass, we additionally included brood size as a covariate since it is known to determine average larval mass. In these models, the female family nested within the block was included as a random factor to account for relatedness and block variation.

To determine the reproductive success of nematodes, we analysed the number of nematode offspring on adult beetles using a Gaussian GLMM. The number of nematodes was log (x + 1) transformed prior to analysis to address non-normal residuals. For this analysis, we only considered treatments with 10 and 100 nematodes. To determine the reproductive success of mites, we analysed the number of mite offspring on adult beetles using a negative binomial GLMM. Similarly, in these models, we included female body size and carcass mass as covariates, whereas the female family nested within the block was included as a random factor.

To assess how sterilisation during different developmental stages affected nematode presence in newly eclosed adult beetles, we fitted a Bayesian GLMM using the brms package. Nematode presence (1 = present, 0 = absent) was modelled as a Bernoulli-distributed response with a logit link function. Fixed effects included larval sterilisation and pupal sterilisation, while beetle family was included as a random factor to account for non-independence among siblings. Due to complete separation in the pupal sterilisation group (i.e., all individuals had zero nematodes), a Bayesian approach was used to ensure proper convergence and credible interval estimation. The model was run with four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, each with 2000 iterations (1000 warm-up). Posterior estimates and 95% credible intervals were used to interpret the strength and direction of effects.

To investigate the effects of nematodes and mites on beetle offspring, we analysed the survival (0: dead, 1: alive) of beetle offspring using a binomial GLMM. We analysed the number of nematodes and mites associated with each beetle offspring using a Gaussian and a negative binomial GLMM, respectively. In the analyses of beetles and nematodes, we considered explanatory variables nematode density treatments, mite presence, and their interaction, while in the analysis of mite number, only treatments with mite presence were analysed. Female family nested within the block was included as a random factor since multiple individuals were sampled from each family.

To test the effect of larval and pupal surface sterilisation on nematode presence in newly emerged N. nepalensis, we used a Bayesian GLMM with a binomial error distribution and logit link, implemented within the package brms (cite Burkner 2017). We specifically chose a Bayesian approach because our experimental design resulted in cases of complete separation (i.e., pupal sterilisation treatments produced only uninfested individuals), which can cause convergence and estimation issues in traditional logistic regression. We included the binary response variable as nematode infection status at adult emergence (1 = present, 0 = absent), and fixed effects as larval sterilisation (yes/no), pupal sterilisation (yes/no), and their interaction. Beetle family was included as a random effect to account for repeated usage of some families. Models were run with four Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, each with 2000 iterations. Model convergence was confirmed by checking Rhat values (~1.00) and adequate effective sample sizes. We compared full and reduced models (with and without the interaction term) using leave-one-out cross-validation (loo), finding that the reduced model was preferred since elpd differences were small and standard errors overlapped zero. We reported posterior means and 95% credible intervals (CIs) for all parameters. Effects were considered statistically credible if the 95% CI excluded zero.

To investigate the effects of mites on nematodes in the feeding experiments, we analysed the number of nematodes with a Poisson GLM and considered the explanatory variable experimental treatment (i.e., nematodes alone, nematodes with one mite, nematodes with pork mince, and nematodes with one mite and pork mince). To investigate the effects of nematodes and mites on the bacteria culturing, we analysed the effect of experimental treatment (i.e., without nematodes – without mites, with nematodes – without mites, without nematodes – with mites, and with nematodes – with mites) on CFU of carcass-associated bacteria, with a negative binomial GLM to account for the issue of overdispersion.

To investigate microbial alpha diversity, we calculated the Shannon index and tested for differences among treatments using the Kruskal-Wallis test. To examine beta diversity, Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was computed, and community composition differences were assessed with PERMANOVA (adonis) and ANOSIM. Ordination and clustering methods including non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) and UPGMA clustering, as well as heatmaps, were generated in R for visualisation.
