Child health in war crisis: barriers to healthcare utilization among internally displaced Sudanese caregivers 2024–2025
Authors: Alaa T. Omer, Alaa A.Y Ahmed, Anmar B. Birier, Baha Aldeen Alshareif, Isra Isameldeen Mohmmed Mohmmedalageel, Logien Abuelgasim Ahmad Ibrahim, Maaza Osman, Mohamed I. Idris, Mohammed Osman, Mudathir Elsiddig, Salah Aldin K. Wedaatalla, Suodad Elhassan, Yagoub Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSudan’s already under-resourced health system has been strained by war, with over 10 million people displaced and 67% of hospitals rendered non-operational. The disruption has led to limited access to essential services. Children face both physical and psychological consequences. This study explores caregivers’ use of health services, observed behavioral changes in children, reports of sexual harassment, and alignment between caregiver experiences and facility data.MethodThis descriptive cross-sectional community-based study was conducted among internally displaced caregivers of children in four relatively safe states—Red Sea, Northern, Nahr Elnil, and Kassala—using questionnaires for both caregivers and health facility directors. A total of 437 caregivers and 12 facilities participated. A multistage sampling method was followed by convenience sampling at the community level. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25 with descriptive and inferential statistics.ResultsThe study included 437 caregivers, 86% of whom were women, with a mean age of 35.4 years (± 9.2 SD). The average age of the children involved in the study was 4.4 ± 4.2 years. Despite 91.8% of children under five having access to basic immunizations, 10% missed essential vaccines, with measles accounting for 50% of missed doses. Pneumonia and gastroenteritis were the most frequently reported illnesses. Psychosocial impacts were prevalent, with 26.8% of children exhibiting fearfulness, anxiety, or irritability.Health facilities findings revealed that 66.7% of facilities experienced disruptions in working hours. Only 25% of facilities had pediatric intensive care units. Key emergency resources, including oxygen therapy (81.8%) and insulin (90.9%), were available in most facilities. T availability of key support staff—such as psychologists and social workers—was notably limited (16.7%).ConclusionOverall, the study’s findings are in partial agreement with other conflict-region research, notably on disease burden, psychosocial impact, and health system degradation. However, Sudan’s context reveals some unique features. Despite extreme displacement, immunization outreach and breastfeeding practices showed higher-than-expected retention. Nevertheless, the weak critical care infrastructure, referral breakdowns, and mental health service gaps highlight urgent needs that require national and international collaboration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24627-7.

Introduction

Sudan’s three-tiered health system—comprising over 5,000 rural health centres and 1,000 peripheral units at the primary level, 140 district hospitals at the secondary level, and eight tertiary teaching hospitals in major towns—has provided healthcare services, including child health services [1]. The Ministry of Health spends less than 4% of GDP, with doctor-to-population ratios of 0.5 per 10,000 in remote areas and 3.5 per 10,000 in Khartoum [1].

The war resulted in more than 10.4 million people having been uprooted, including 8.3 million internally displaced persons; children constitute half of this cohort [2, 3]. Direct effects of war include death and injury from violence, environmental hazards, torture, and the forced recruitment of minors [4]. Indirectly, war disrupts essential health facilities; an estimated 67% of hospitals are non-operational, mirroring patterns observed in other conflict zones such as Ukraine [5]. War further complicates access to regular preventive healthcare [6]. War impacts food security, water accessibility, and impedes the management of chronic diseases [7].

In 2023, in the Eastern Mediterranean Region of WHO, nine countries, namely Afghanistan, Iraq, the Islamic Republic of Iran, Lebanon, Pakistan, Somalia, Sudan, Syria, and Yemen have reported acute watery diarrhea (AWD)/suspected cholera cases [8]. In Sudan, immunization coverage, particularly for diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus (DPT-3), has plummeted, with the Darfur region reporting only 30% coverage which decline raises concerns about the increased risk of outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) [2]. Based on a systematic review, the number of armed conflicts is inversely related to vaccination rates [4]. This is a result of a decrease of outreach programs and delays in the distribution of new vaccines and immunization campaigns [4].

Armed conflicts are associated with a heightened prevalence of anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress, and depression among children. These outcomes stem from their exposure to traumatic events, disruptions to family structures, and social disintegration [9]. Displaced children or those living in conflict zones face heightened risks of exploitation, early marriage, harassment, and sexual assault [9]. A systematic review reported that 21% of evacuated women and children experienced sexual abuse; however, this figure is underestimated due to social stigma, inadequate law enforcement, and limited support services [9].

Limited research exists on the consequences of Sudan’s war on child health, despite the evident devastation and loss of life. This study investigates the war crisis impact on child health service utilization among displaced caregivers in Sudan. It explores the extent to which caregivers of children under five are using health services, the behavioral changes observed in children since displacement, the reported prevalence of sexual harassment incidents, and how caregivers’ experiences compare with available health facility data on service availability and delivery.

Study design and setting

This is a descriptive cross-sectional community-based study conducted during war crises among the internally displaced Sudanese caregivers of children, using two structured questionnaires from caregivers and from the healthcare directors.

Study area

Sudan is divided into 18 states, with Khartoum State serving as the capital [9]. Since April 15, 2023, Khartoum has been embroiled in a war crisis. During our research period, 11 states were officially designated by the government as areas of war, grappling with widespread conflict and insecurity. In contrast, the remaining 7 states were classified as safe zones and considered as sample frames.

Study period

We carried out our study between January 10, 2024 and December 10, 2024. This included several stages, such as proposal writing, obtaining ethical approval, data collection and management, and manuscript preparation. The data was collected from January 20 to March 31, 2024.

Study population

Sudan’s population is more than 51 million as of 2024 with a median age of 18.4 years [10]. Sudan has the world’s worst internal displacement issue, with around 9 million people affected as of 2024 [11].

Participants, sample size and sampling technique

We focused on a sample frame comprising seven relatively safe states where individuals had been displaced from other regions, excluding eleven states due to prevailing insecurity and challenges in data collection. These relatively safe states are the Red Sea, Kassala, Sinnar, Northern, Nahr Elnil, White Nile, and Algadarif states. We used towns within the states as sample units. The states and towns involved in the study were chosen using a multistage sampling approach. Stage one involves selecting four states using simple random sampling, followed by stage two selecting two towns in each state using simple random selection too. So, the study conducted on four: the Red Sea State (Portsudan and Swakin), the Northern State (Marawi and Algolid), the NahrElnil State (Shendi and Eldamar), and the Kassala State (Kassala and New-Halfa).

The sample frame is considered as known. The actual number of displaced persons and children can be obtained from the UNICEF reports [2]. We used a sample size calculator program to calculate the sample size from the sample frame shown. The sample size was 400 people as calculated by openepi [12]. We chose an equal number of individuals from each town (almost 50 in total). Ultimately, at least we obtained 55 from each town.

Caregivers were eligible to participate if they had been displaced due to the war crisis in Khartoum or other officially designated conflict-affected states, were currently residing in one of the selected safe states (Red Sea, Northern, Nahr Elnil, or Kassala), and were responsible for at least one child under the age of 18. Participants had to be 18 years or older and willing to provide informed consent. Caregivers were excluded if they had not been displaced by the conflict, or were unable to complete the questionnaire due to illness, disability, or language barriers.

To ensure timely and feasible data collection in a challenging field setting, a convenience sampling technique was employed to select caregivers. Participants were recruited from community centers, or during outreach visits. This approach was chosen due to logistical constraints, such as limited access to a complete sampling frame and security or displacement-related challenges that made random sampling impractical. The study achieved a high response rate, with 437 out of 400 targeted caregivers participating, resulting in a response rate of approximately 109%, which indicates strong participant engagement and data completeness. We conducted 12 health facility questionnaires from each facility that provided health services for participants in the study.

Data collection method and instruments

We collected data via two questionnaires from child caregivers and from the facilities directors. As a technique, a Google Form was used. The two questionnaires, which the authors designed based on a literature review, are adequate for covering the study’s objectives. They were structured questionnaires written in the English language. The study authors then translated the caregiver questionnaire into Arabic and had a language specialist translate it back into English; the two copies were then evaluated for reliability.

The two questionnaires were validated by four experts: a statistician, a research expert, and two pediatricians. The two questionnaires consist of five segments and each segment focuses on a specific goal and leads to the next. Eight medical experts—one assigned to each town—were responsible for data collection. To minimize potential bias and ensure consistency in data gathering, all experts participated in a structured training workshop prior to fieldwork.

Data management

Firstly, the authors entered and cleaned data using Microsoft excel sheet, then transferred and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0, descriptive statistics used and categorical data presented in form of frequencies (n) and percentages (%), while numeric data presented in form of means and SD. For inferential statistics, Chi-square test was used, P value of < 0 0.05 was considered significant.1.

Results

Sociodemographic of the children’s caregivers and breadwinners

The study surveyed 437 caregivers of children, of whom 376 (86%) were female, with mothers accounting for 353 (80.8%) of participants. Among the caregivers, 173 (39.7%) held a university degree, and their mean age was 35.4 years (± 9.2 SD). Fathers served as the primary breadwinners for 354 (81.2%) of children, with 220 (50.5%) of breadwinners holding university degrees. In terms of occupation, free worker individuals constituted the largest group 216 (49.5%), followed by governmental employees 102 (23.4%). The mean age of breadwinners was 41.4 years (± 9.9 SD) (Table 1).

Utilization of child-health services, child personality changes, and incidents of sexual harassment of children

The average age of all children of caregivers who participated in the study was 4.4 ± 4.2 years. Among the sick children, the mean age was higher, at 6.8 ± 3.5 years. Since April 2023, 530 children (66.3%) had contracted an illness, with pneumonia being the most common illness, affecting 190 children (23.8%) (Table 3). Of the sick children, 145 (18.1%) required hospital admission; however, only 129 of them were actually admitted. Most children (89.9%, N = 337) received treatment, with 62 children (10%) needing emergency interventions (Table 3).

Among the 92 children (11.5%) who had chronic illnesses, 63 (64.9%) had access to treatment, while 37 (34.9%) were able to access follow-up care. Complications related to chronic illness were reported in 37 children (27.6%), and 69.6% (N = 39) received treatment (Table 3).

Personality changes were observed in 108 children (26.8%), with fearfulness (31.6%), anxiety (18.4%), and irritability (19.5%) being the most common ones. Additionally, 58 children (10%) were reported to have responsibilities beyond their age, and 35 families (4.4%) faced financial difficulties, prompting their children to work. Finally, 10 children (1.3%) showed signs of sexual abuse or harassment, as shown in Table 3.

Baseline information of the health facilities involved in the study

Data were collected from 12 health facilities that were visited by caregivers seeking health advice and treatment for their children. The majority of the facilities were classified as hospitals (n = 8), while the remaining included primary health care centers, private clinics, and government clinics. Medical officers (58.3%) and nurses (50%) were the most available health personnel at the time of visit to the hospital (Fig. 1).

At the time of data collection, significant disparities in the availability of health personnel were observed across the surveyed facilities. In Sawakin, there were no specialists, registrars, pharmacists, or laboratory technicians present. Al-Goled and New Halfa had only a pediatric specialist for each. The three facilities in El-Damar lacked both pharmacists and lab technicians, with only one facility having a specialist and a registrar. Kassala and Portsudan were the only towns where all essential health staff categories were available, and Marawi was the only location with a specialist present per each facility (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The efficacy of humanitarian public health initiatives relies on precise and prompt identification and execution of the most suitable interventions, a comprehensive understanding of priority needs, and contextual awareness [13]. This study offers critical insights into the state of child healthcare utilization and healthcare delivery in Sudan amid the ongoing war crisis. The findings reflect both the resilience of some health system functions—such as immunization outreach—and the significant disruptions experienced by displaced communities, particularly in accessing comprehensive care, mental health support, and critical care infrastructure.

The overwhelming majority of caregivers in this study were women (86%), consistent with findings from prior Sudanese research, where women are often the primary caregivers for children in both stable and crisis settings. A similar demographic profile was reported in a 2020 study on displaced communities in Sudan, which found most of surveyed caregivers were mothers or female guardians [14]. This gender distribution underscores the compounded burden on women during conflict, as they often navigate caregiving under resource scarcity and personal displacement.

Over half of both breadwinners and carers had university-level education. Still, because of systematic collapse, financial restrictions, and resource limitations, even educated carers said they had trouble finding assistance. These results coincide with statistics from nations experiencing conflict, such as Syria and Yemen, where health system disintegration [15, 16] rendered socioeconomic level insufficient guarantee of healthcare utilisation.

Among caregivers with infants born after the war began, the majority (80.9%) reported delivering in health facilities, which is encouraging and suggests that facility-based delivery remained partially intact in selected areas. This finding is consistent with pre-war facility delivery rates in urban Sudan, which ranged from 70 to 85% according to the 2014 Sudan Household Health Survey [17]. However, only 59.7% reported receiving breastfeeding counselling, which is markedly lower than the national targets. While exclusive breastfeeding rates (83.9%) appear relatively high, they may reflect maternal effort rather than health system support. In contrast, studies from conflict zones have documented a sharp decline in exclusive breastfeeding during displacement, due to the lack of counselling and poor nutrition support [18].

Despite an immunisation coverage of 91.8% among children under five, 10% have missed at least one critical vaccine, predominantly the measles vaccine. This disparity elicits significant apprehensions, given Sudan was formerly poised to eradicate measles prior to the conflict. Sudan’s vaccine deficiency arises not only from insecurity but also from interrupted cold chains, financial constraints, and the cessation of humanitarian assistance owing to conflict-related hazards, despite analogous patterns in South Sudan [19]. Moreover, community interviews indicated a lack of trust in temporary outreach teams, a phenomenon often observed in conflict zones when communication failures between humanitarian organisations and displaced individuals impede engagement [20].

Although 75% of the assessed health facilities had immunisation units and 66.7% of them provided free drugs, carers claimed regular referral failures. This captures the larger institutional neglect to guarantee continuation of treatment. Referral delays—sometimes reaching two hours—have been related, as discovered in Afghanistan, with poor results and care desertion [21]. These findings underline the urgent need of decentralising significant services and financing mobile units and local stabilising centres reducing dependency on ineffective referral systems. The availability of immunization services in Swakin and Port Sudan was notably better than in more rural or unstable areas, which aligns Studies from Ethiopia have similarly reported urban-rural disparities in vaccine continuity, often due to logistical limitations and security threats in rural regions [19].

The incidence of prevalent childhood diseases, including pneumonia and gastroenteritis (66.3%) due to overcrowded living conditions, poor sanitation, and disrupted immunization services, indicates limited access to services. Although analogous increases are recorded in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Yemen [22], the disease burden in Sudan necessitates contextualisation with pre-war baselines. The Sudan Household Health Survey (2014) indicated that acute respiratory infections impacted fewer than 25% of children under five; the present threefold rate implies a significant decline associated with war-induced relocation, hunger, and inadequate shelter [23]. These findings validate the health repercussions of violence and indicate a humanitarian crisis that necessitates prompt response. While over 89% of sick children received some form of treatment, the availability of essential diagnostics and treatment tools was inadequate—only 50% of facilities had X-ray services, and oxygen was available in less than 10%. This gap between disease burden and resource availability mirrors findings from Syria, where life-saving interventions were frequently missing in war-damaged health facilities [24].

Of children with chronic conditions, one-third lacked consistent care and only 64.9% had gotten any kind of follow-up. Looting and internal migration have aggravated Sudan’s structural health problems including drug shortages and personnel shortages. Although it is sometimes neglected in conflict areas, chronic care is vital and its absence causes permanent damage particularly in young populations [20, 21, 24, 25]. Future projects have to include management of chronic diseases into emergency response systems.

Though around 26.8% of children showed behavioural or personality changes suggestive of psychological distress. Though these results are similar to those of Gaza and Afghanistan [26–28], the lack of standardised assessment methods in our study makes an accurate diagnosis challenging. Still, the stated symptoms fit post-traumatic stress symptoms. These findings also resonate with earlier Sudanese research in west Kordofan and east Darfur, which found increased rates of school dropout, child labor, and emotional withdrawal among displaced children [29]. The limited availability of mental health staff—psychologists and social workers were present in only 16.7% of facilities—means children likely receive no formal psychosocial support, putting them at long-term risk for anxiety, PTSD, and behavioral disorders. Furthermore, proof of children assuming adult responsibilities or engaging in sexual violence exposes extreme psychological damage. These results necessitate quick scale-up of child-centered mental health interventions including trauma care integration into clinics, shelters, and schools [30].

Facility-level data revealed marked inequities in the availability of health personnel and resources. Several towns—such as Swakin and El-Damar—lacked pharmacists, lab technicians, or specialists altogether, significantly limiting service capacity. Kassala and Port Sudan fared better, with full staffing reported in select hospitals. These disparities mirror findings from pre-war assessments, which documented a long-standing concentration of skilled personnel in urban centers like Khartoum, leaving peripheral regions under-resourced [31]. Although some health facilities were functioning 7 days a week, one-third of facilities reported work disruptions since April 2023, primarily due to financial constraints and supply chain failures. Similar patterns have been documented in Yemen, where sustained conflict forced health facilities to limit operations, despite high patient loads [32]. The absence of neonatal intensive care (NICU) in all but one facility further reflects systemic neglect of advanced pediatric care, which becomes critical during epidemics or mass displacements.

Overall, the study’s findings are in partial agreement with other conflict-region research, notably on disease burden, psychosocial impact, and health system degradation. However, Sudan’s context reveals some unique features. Despite extreme displacement, immunization outreach and breastfeeding practices showed higher-than-expected retention—possibly due to historical investment in primary care networks before the conflict. Nevertheless, the weak critical care infrastructure, referral breakdowns, and mental health service gaps highlight urgent needs that require national and international collaboration.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the expanding evidence concerning the devastating effects of armed war on child health in Sudan. The combination of systemic healthcare problems, immunisation failures, chronic care neglect, and mental health emergencies emphasises the necessity for conflict-sensitive and child-centered humanitarian health interventions. This study explores Sudan-specific features in war-affected communities, including disparity between education and utilization, the breakdown of referral networks, and the oversight of chronic children care. These findings stress the necessity of designing practical, contextually relevant interventions that prioritize emergency stabilisation, continuity of care, and psychosocial resilience.

Recommendations

To address the pressing health needs of children in conflict-affected areas, In the immediate term, Sudan’s Ministry of Health, in coordination with international agencies such as UNICEF and WHO, must prioritize restoration and protection of emergency and primary health services, including the deployment of mobile clinics in high-need regions. The government must re-establish and expand immunization campaigns, using mobile outreach strategies and strengthening cold chain systems to prevent outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles. These efforts should be guided by recent trends indicating increased susceptibility to vaccine gaps during war periods.

Addressing the shortage of essential medicines is also a national priority. The procurement and distribution of both chronic and acute medications—including for conditions such as diabetes, malaria, and pneumonia—must be coordinated centrally with support from global donors. Mental health and psychosocial support services should be integrated into primary care settings and educational schools, ensuring early detection and response to trauma-related symptoms which has proven effective in conflict zones such as Gaza and Afghanistan [33]. Additionally, schools should serve as access points for child health services, integrating routine medical screenings, deworming campaigns, and school feeding programs. These programs must be designed in collaboration with local education officials and NGOs to ensure broad reach and sustainability.

Also short-term interventions must begin with the rehabilitation of disrupted clinics, especially in displacement camps and underserved rural communities. This includes restoring electricity and water supplies, replacing damaged equipment, and ensuring cold chain functionality—possibly through solar-powered alternatives. Community health workers should be mobilized for home-based outreach, especially to support maternal care, deliver immunizations, and educate caregivers on nutrition and hygiene. Involving respected community leaders and local NGOs in these activities will enhance cultural appropriateness and promote trust in healthcare services.

Limitations

The conflict setting of this study presented major constraints. Active violence hindered access to several highly needed areas, therefore reducing geographical representativeness. The cross-sectional design of this study presents inherent limitations, as it captures data at a single point in time, which may not adequately reflect changes in health service utilization over the course of the ongoing conflict. Additionally, the issue of electricity insecurity can compromise data collection efforts, affecting both the reliability of information gathered and the operational capacity of health facilities. Furthermore, employing multiple data collectors introduces variability in data quality and potential biases, as differences in training, interpretation of questions, and interactions with participants may influence the consistency and accuracy of the findings.In addition, the lack of uniform approaches for mental health reduced the scope of psychosocial research possible. These challenges should be taken into account while assessing the results since they most surely contributed to underreporting in several areas.
