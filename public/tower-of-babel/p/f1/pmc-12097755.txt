3D Melt Blowing of Elastollan Thermoplastic Polyurethane for Tissue Engineering Applications: A Pilot Study
Authors: Advay Pawar, Bruce Anderson, Behnam Pourdeyhimi, Amy L McNulty, Matthew Fisher, Rohan Shirwaiker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractScaffolds, in addition to being biocompatible, should possess structural and mechanical properties similar to the natural tissues they intend to replace. Many tissue engineering applications require porous 3D scaffolds characterized by unique microfibrous organization and mechanical anisotropy. Manufacturing process principles and process parameter-biomaterial interactions ultimately govern the properties that can be achieved in the scaffold. In this study, we investigate a recently developed nonwoven scaffold fabrication process, 3D melt blowing (3DMB), for processing Elastollan®, a thermoplastic polyurethane with basic mechanical properties suitable for musculoskeletal tissue engineering. The range of feasible processing parameters was screened and the effects of two sets of critical process parameters (fiber deposition offset and surface velocity of the collector) that produced contrasting scaffold morphologies were assessed. Results showed that scaffolds of Group B that were fabricated at the higher fiber deposition offset (90%) and higher surface velocity of the collector (6×105 mm/min) possessed significantly smaller fiber diameter and higher porosity and degree of fiber alignment along the principal direction of collector rotation during 3DMB (all p<0.05) compared to Group A scaffolds (fabricated at 50% offset and 1×105 mm/min surface velocity). Although both groups possessed similar tensile stiffness, the elongation at failure was significantly different (p<0.0001). The higher elongation at failure of Group B correlated with the higher degree of fiber alignment in these scaffolds. In contrast, the more isotropic fibrous organization of Group A contributed to their higher compressive stiffness (p=0.004). The introduction of NaOH treatment to improve hydrophilicity of the scaffolds resulted in a significant reduction of tensile stiffness of Group A (p<0.05) but not Group B. This treatment did not significantly affect the elongation at failure or compressive stiffness of both groups. With NaOH-treatment, both groups demonstrated good biocompatibility when seeded with fibroblast cells over 14 days. This study confirms the ability to fabricate via 3DMB, biocompatible, micro-fibrous, Elastollan scaffolds relevant for musculoskeletal tissue engineering.Keywords: tissue engineering, nonwoven, meltblowing, musculoskeletal, scaffolds, thermoplastic polyurethane, Elastollan

Introduction

Musculoskeletal tissues, including the menisci, ligaments, tendons, and cartilage of the knee play a pivotal role in maintaining the stability and mobility of the joint. They are consistently subjected to mechanical forces during routine activities including walking and running, and as such, are prone to injuries and degenerative joint diseases. However, their healing capacity is minimal due to limited blood and nutrient supplies in the joint environment [1–2] Due to the suboptimal long-term outcomes associated with current clinical treatments, tissue engineering technologies are being actively explored as alternatives [3–7]. These technologies leverage biofabrication processes to create biomaterial scaffolds designed to recapitulate the properties of native tissues. Appropriately engineered scaffolds facilitate cell attachment and proliferation and promote subsequent production of extracellular matrix (ECM), resulting in biofunctional tissue constructs [8].

There is a strong relationship between the structure and function of tissues, which must be mimicked in the scaffold design and enabled by biofabrication [8–9]. Biomimetic scaffolds for tissues like ligaments, tendons, and bone must recapitulate the tissue-specific microarchitectural and/or mechanical anisotropy. For example, 3D biofabricated ligament and tendon scaffolds should possess a highly aligned microfibrous architecture to resist the tensile loading that these tissues primarily experience, while trabecular bone scaffolds must mimic the quasi-random interconnected porous network to dissipate the complex dynamic mechanical loads [10–12]

Processes such as electrospinning and extrusion printing have been widely employed for the fabrication of musculoskeletal scaffolds in literature. However, these techniques have limitations associated with multi-scale biomimicry and processing throughput [13–16]. For example, extrusion printing processes can successfully reproduce 3D macro-geometries of target tissues in the scaffolds, but the scaffold fiber and pore sizes are typically at least an order of magnitude larger than extracellular matrix fibers comprising joint tissues. Conversely, electrospinning can produce biomimetically-sized fibers and porosity, but the process throughput and its ability to create scaffolds of physiologically-relevant thicknesses and macro-geometries is limited. To overcome these challenges, we recently developed a new nonwovens-based process – 3D melt blowing (3DMB) (Fig.1)[13], [17]. Herein, a polymer melt extruded simultaneously through multiple nozzles is drawn into fibers at a high rate of speed via high speed-high temperature annular air jets. The fibers are collected on a rotating cylindrical collector underneath the nozzles. 3DMB can create scaffolds of physiologically-relevant thicknesses with fibrous micro-architectures to mimic natural tissues at throughputs of at least an order of magnitude higher than electrospinning [13], [15], [18–19].

Schematic of (a) 3DMB system, and (b) three key process parameters – DCD, FDO, and SVC

In previous studies, we investigated the 3DMB of polyesters such as polycaprolactone (PCL) and polylactic acid (PLA), which are widely used in tissue engineering and biofabrication literature [15], [19]. We demonstrated that the interactions between the polymer melt and the blowing and collection parameters influence the morphological and mechanical attributes of scaffolds. Although PCL and PLA are being used in a variety of biomedical applications due to their biocompatibility, their mechanical properties make them less suited for musculoskeletal soft tissue applications. In particular, these materials possess relatively high stiffness and tend to undergo permanent deformation under the dynamic loads that musculoskeletal tissues typically experience. Thermoplastic polyurethanes (TPU), which are typically characterized by high wear and abrasion resistance, high elongation at failure, and moderate tensile and compressive moduli can serve as possible alternatives [20]. Being thermoplastics, they possess high processability and thus can be excellent candidates for a variety of melt-based biofabrication processes [21]. To date, TPUs have been used in implantable medical devices such as catheters, antimicrobial filters, and drug carriers, but only to a limited extent for musculoskeletal soft tissue applications [22–25].

In this study, we investigated for the first time, the 3DMB of Elastollan® B 85 A, a TPU elastomer for musculoskeletal soft tissue engineering. Elastollan is a commercially available TPU having excellent mechanical properties, wear resistance, and chemical and thermal stability. These characteristics make this material an ideal candidate for potential tissue engineering applications [22–25]. The objective of this study was to screen key 3DMB process parameters and assess their effects on the fibrous morphology, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility of Elastollan scaffolds.

Materials and Methods

3DMB Setup and Experimental Design

Our 3DMB system utilizes a die (Biax FiberFilm, WI) with annular nozzles arranged in a 3×3 array. During processing, polymer melt is ejected through each central nozzle (⌀0.3 mm) and drawn by high temperature, high velocity air jet from the coaxial annular opening (⌀1.4 mm). The resulting fibers are collected onto a rotating cylindrical collector (⌀208 mm) mounted on a computerized numerical control 6-axis robotic arm (Denso, Japan).

To establish the range of feasible 3DMB processing parameters, a screening study was conducted in which six parameters – 1) extrusion temperature, 2) air throughput, 3) polymer throughput, 4) distance between the die face and the collector surface (DCD), 5) offset between the axis of rotation and the fiber collection location expressed as a percentage of collector diameter (FDO), and 6) surface velocity of the collector (SVC) – were varied. The parameters that resulted in continuous fiber formation and successful deposition onto the collector to form 3D fibrous scaffold mats with negligible visually identifiable structural defects (e.g., significant beading, bulk instead of porous fibrous architectures, smaller broken segments than long fibers) were identified. The parameters at the extremities of this feasible process design space were investigated in further detail for their effects on the morphology, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility of the scaffolds.

Elastollan® B 85 A (BASF, NJ) pellets were pre-dried in a heater at 60°C overnight. They were then melted in an extruder at 235°C, ejected through the 3×3 nozzles (polymer throughput per nozzle = 1×103 mg/min), and attenuated by annular air jets at 260°C (air throughput = 1.7×105 ml/min). The DCD was maintained constant at 150 mm. Two groups of fibrous scaffold mats were fabricated at different FDO-SVC combinations – Group A at 50% - 1×105 mm/min and Group B at 90% - 6×105 mm/min. Each fibrous mat was collected for 30 minutes. Scaffold samples of specific shapes required for characterization and mechanical testing were then punched out from these mats.

To improve the hydrophilicity of the scaffolds to facilitate media and cell infiltration that is critical during biocompatibility testing, based on a preliminary study, a subset of the scaffolds were treated with NaOH. For NaOH treatment, the scaffolds were submerged in 3M NaOH at 60°C for 1 hour in 50 ml tubes. Scaffolds were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline and allowed to air-dry for 48 hours.

Morphology Characterization

Three scaffolds (ø8 × 3 mm) were punched out at random locations from the melt blown mat for each group for imaging analysis. The samples were sputter-coated with gold and palladium (60:40 ratio) and visualized via scanning electron microscope (Phenom Pro, ThermoFisher Scientific, MA) at 5 kV accelerating voltage (n = 3 images/disc at 200X and 450X each). Average fiber diameter was determined via 50 random measurements from the 450X images (N = 50×3×3 = 450 measurements/group) in ImageJ [26]. The OrientationJ plugin of ImageJ was used to determine the average fiber alignment, expressed as the degree of angular coherency (%), for each group using the 200X images (N = 3×3 = 9 measurements/group). Note that higher angular coherency indicates a greater proportion of fibers aligned along a principal direction. Average 2D porosity of each group was determined from the 200X images as the percentage of the total area covered by fibers (N = 3×3 = 9 images analyzed/group).

Mechanical Testing

For tensile testing, ASTM D-1708 compliant microtensile bars (gauge: 5×22×3 mm; n = 3/group) were punched from each mat along the direction of collector rotation and tested on an Instron Universal Testing Machine (Norwood, MA) with a 2 kN load cell. The samples were pre-loaded to 0.01 N and tested to failure at a constant displacement rate of 50 mm/min, and the Young’s modulus and elongation at failure were recorded. For compression testing, punched discs (ø8×3 mm; n = 3/group) were compressed between parallel platens in the Instron at a constant displacement rate of 1 mm/min to 50% strain, and the compressive modulus was recorded. To assess the effect of NaOH treatment on the tensile and compressive properties, treated scaffolds (n = 3/group) were tested using the same protocols and compared with non-treated scaffolds.

Biocompatibility Testing

NaOH-treated scaffolds (ø8x3 mm) were sterilized in 70% isopropyl alcohol for 30 min, rinsed thoroughly with sterile PBS, and air dried for at least 24 hours prior to testing. NIH 3T3 cells (2.5 × 105) (ATCC, VA) were seeded onto one side of each scaffold (n = 3/group) and cultured in DMEM-High Glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, MO) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich) in 24-well plates for 24 hours. Then, the scaffolds were inverted, seeded with 2.5 × 105 cells on the new top-facing side, and cultured in the growth medium for another 24 hours, followed by 14 days of culture. The culture media was changed every 48 hours. Cell viability at Day 1 was assessed using calcein AM staining (ThermoFisher Scientific). Furthermore, cell metabolic activity in the scaffolds (n = 3/group) was assessed on Days 1, 7 and 14 using the alamarBlue (aB) assay (ThermoFisher Scientific). Acellular scaffolds (n = 1/group) were used as controls.

Statistical Analysis

For all testing analyses, the groups were compared using ANOVA and Tukey HSD post hoc tests (α=0.05) in JMP (SAS, NC).

Results and Discussion

Fiber Morphology

Fig. 2 shows representative images of the fibrous microarchitectures of the two groups and the corresponding fiber diameters, alignment, and porosity achieved using different 3DMB parameter combinations. It is evident that contrasting microarchitectures that would be relevant for different tissue types (e.g., Group A for bone [27] and Group B for tendons and ligaments [28]) can be achieved by tuning the key collection parameters, FDO and SVC.

Representative SEM images of the two contrasting 3DMB scaffold groups and their fibrous characteristics expressed as mean ± standard deviation

The fiber diameter of Group B was significantly smaller (p=0.0028) and the angular coherency significantly higher (p<0.0001) than that of Group A. This is due to the fact that the fibers experience an enhanced drawing force when deposited at 90% FDO and higher SVC, which stretches the fibers. This reduces the fiber diameter and aligns them along the direction of collector rotation.

Conversely, at 50% FDO, the fibers are being deposited on a significantly larger surface area, where the temperature of the collector is also higher as compared to 90% FDO [29]. Here, the collector drawing force is unable to overcome the normal force and whipping instability of the fibers so the fibers are deposited in a random orientation and not stretched. The higher temperature causes greater adhesion of the fibers to the collector, reducing the drawing force further. This results in minimal stretching of the fibers, leading to fibers of larger diameters being deposited in relatively random orientations. These phenomena are similar to those seen in electrospinning and other nonwoven processes [30–33]. Group A scaffolds were also characterized by a significantly lower 2D porosity compared to Group B (p = 0.0061). The higher residual heat around the collector rotating at lower speeds contributes to a higher temperature and slower rate of solidification of collected fibers, which leads to more inter-fiber and inter-layer bonding, and ultimately, lower scaffold porosity.

Mechanical Characterization

The difference between the average tensile stiffness of Groups A and B before NaOH treatment (2 MPa and 1.99 MPa, respectively) was not significant (Fig. 3a). However, the average elongation at failure of Group B (708%) was significantly higher than that of Group A (506%) (p<0.0001; Fig. 3b).

Comparison of (a) tensile stiffness, (b) elongation at failure and (c) compressive stiffness of non-treated and NaOH treated scaffolds expressed as mean ± standard deviation; *p<0.05

The scaffold morphology plays a role in how each group resists tensile deformation. In Group A, the tensile load acts upon scaffolds possessing more randomly oriented fibers and a higher degree of fiber entanglement and inter-fiber bonding. Conversely, in Group B scaffolds, the tensile forces are resisted synchronously by fibers that are primarily aligned along the direction of loading. Similar relationships between fibrous microarchitectures and resulting mechanical properties of polypropylene and PCL scaffolds have been reported in other experimental and computational studies [15],[34–36]. The results highlight that the difference in scaffold morphology, especially the degree of fiber alignment, directly influences the elongation of the fibers.

Of note, Elastollan scaffolds, irrespective of their morphology, demonstrated much higher elongation at failure compared to nonwoven PCL scaffolds. Elongation in the range of 2~200% has been reported for electrospun PCL scaffolds [33], [37–40]. Elongation at failure indicates a material’s ability to absorb stress, deform elastically and regain its original shape. Higher elongation at failure suggests an ability to withstand cyclic loading without failure [41]. The high elongation at failure of Elastollan scaffolds highlights their relevance to musculoskeletal tissue engineering applications, especially for tissues such as ligaments and tendons that primarily experience tensile loading.

The difference between the average compressive stiffness of Groups A and B before NaOH treatment (0.92 MPa and 0.11 MPa, respectively) was significant (p = 0.004; Fig. 3c). This corroborates the morphology analysis – the denser and more randomly oriented microarchitecture of Group A scaffolds contributes to their higher stiffness in compression compared to the more porous and anisotropically organized Group B.

In analyzing the effect of NaOH surface treatment on the tensile stiffness, two-way ANOVA with group and treatment as the variables indicated that the treatment had a significant effect (p = 0.0024). Tukey HSD post hoc tests showed that the treatment resulted in a significant decrease in the tensile stiffness of Group A (p = 0.018) but not Group B. This can be attributed, in part, to the higher surface area within the Group A scaffolds that is subjected to erosion by NaOH, due to the larger fiber diameters and randomly oriented morphology compared to Group B. The NaOH treatment did not, however, significantly affect the elongation at failure under tensile loading or compressive stiffness of the scaffolds.

Overall, the mechanical properties of the scaffolds of both groups, even after NaOH treatment, were within the relevant range for musculoskeletal tissues such as articular cartilage (tensile modulus ≈ 1 MPa, compressive modulus ≈ 0.2-2 MPa) and meniscus (tensile modulus ≈ 1-20 MPa, compressive modulus ≈ 0.1-0.6 MPa) [9],[42]. Depending on the specific application, the scaffolds with the appropriate morphology and mechanical properties can be selected for further investigation.

Biocompatibility Testing

The biocompatibility of melt blown Elastollan scaffolds was assessed by analyzing their ability to support attachment and proliferation of NIH 3T3 fibroblast cells. As seen in Fig. 4, the cells seeded on both groups of scaffolds demonstrated excellent viability (>95%) (indicated by green fluorescence) on Day 1. Both scaffold groups also showed favorable metabolic activity over the 14 days of culture; two-way ANOVA results showed that neither the scaffold group nor time point had a significant effect on the %aB reduction. These results indicate that Elastollan scaffolds, irrespective of their morphology and mechanical properties in the context of this study design, are conducive to cell attachment and proliferation over 14 days.

Conclusion

This is the first study investigating the 3DMB of thermoplastic elastomer scaffolds as candidates for applications in musculoskeletal tissue engineering. The results demonstrate the versatility of this nonwovens process in terms of fibrous morphologies and mechanical properties that can be achieved by controlling appropriate process parameters and confirms the biocompatibility of Elastollan scaffolds. The fibrous morphology and mechanical properties reported herein, including the tensile stiffness of Group A that was affected by the NaOH treatment required for facilitating the scaffolds’ biological function, are within physiologically relevant ranges for musculoskeletal tissues. The notably high elongation at failure highlights the flexibility of the scaffolds and is an indication of their potential ability to sustain cyclic loading.

The high process throughput while creating scaffolds of physiologically-relevant thicknesses with biomimetic fibrous microarchitectures reflect the potential of 3DMB for scale-up and clinical translation. While this pilot study established the feasible 3DMB process design space for Elastollan and highlighted selected structure-function relationships at the extremes of that design space, future studies will focus on comprehensive process-structure-function characterization across the full design space. These studies will consider additional 3DMB parameters including the DCD and air and polymer flow rates, which can synchronously influence the fiber morphology as well as the fatigue properties of resulting scaffolds. Given the complex and dynamic interplay of high throughput high temperature air, polymer melt, and the rotating collector involved in the 3DMB process, complementary computational modeling efforts can help to better understand the process behaviors. Validated computational models, in conjunction with experimental studies, can accelerate process optimization to achieve desired scaffold morphologies for specific tissue applications. In-depth in vitro and in vivo biological characterization of tissue-specific (e.g., meniscus and tendons) scaffolds with relevant multi-scale architectures and cell types are also within the scope of our future work.
