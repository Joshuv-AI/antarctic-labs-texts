Twenty-year oncology sperm banking experience at a Canadian academic fertility centre: a retrospective study examining the usage and reproductive outcomes from oncology patients
Authors: Tianhui Chen, Scot Hamilton, Kimberly E Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundMany cancer treatments pose a threat to fertility for patients. Semen cryopreservation before cancer treatment is an effective method to preserve fertility. There are sparse long-term data on the usage of samples from Canadian oncology sperm banks.MethodsA retrospective chart review of all oncology sperm banking samples at a Canadian academic fertility centre from 2001 to 2020 was conducted.ResultsFrom 2001 to 2020, 4521 samples were banked by 2504 patients. The most frequent diagnoses among these patients were testicular cancer (29.5%) and lymphoma (26.9%). Of these patients, only 81 (3.2%) patients returned to use their samples with intrauterine insemination (IUI) or in vitro fertilisation (IVF) treatment and 62 (2.5%) patients transferred their samples to another clinic. The time between banking and return for usage of the sperm ranged from 1 to 131 months with a median of 18 months after banking. A total of 66 IVF cycles (104 embryo transfers) and 101 IUI cycles from 67 patients were reviewed. Of the 67 couples who used their samples, 53.7% achieved a clinical pregnancy. The clinical pregnancy rate was 6.6% per cycle for IUI and 30.8% per embryo transfer for IVF. Higher sperm concentration or total motile count was not associated with a higher chance of pregnancy. Patients who conceived had on average 1.9±0.8 (p=0.02) more usable embryos per cycle than those who did not conceive.ConclusionsSperm cryopreservation provides a valuable option for patients with cancer to achieve parenthood after potentially gonadotoxic cancer treatment. However, the overall usage of banked oncology sperm samples is very low.Keywords: REPRODUCTIVE MEDICINE, ONCOLOGY, Health economics, Male infertility

Introduction

It was estimated that there were 233 900 new cancer cases in Canada in 2022.1 Of all cancer cases in Canada, approximately 8.7% of male patients with cancer are under the age of 50.1 Cancer treatment or the cancer itself can cause problems with fertility for these young patients, and permanent infertility is a risk with many treatment regimens.2,4 Despite ongoing research in cancer treatment therapies and the evolution of treatment regimens to be less gonadotoxic, it is still difficult to predict whether a patient is at risk of permanent sterility or how long it will take for their sperm production to resume.2 Therefore, sperm cryopreservation is an important fertility preservation option to discuss with patients.5

Fertility clinics around the world have reported sperm cryopreservation with subsequent use of assisted reproduction techniques (ARTs) to be a safe and effective option for male oncology patients to achieve parenthood.6,9 A systematic review which looked at the effectiveness of oncology sperm banks from 19 reports calculated that the aggregated percentage of patients who used their banked sperm and subsequently achieved parenthood was 49%, with a 95% CI between 44% and 53%.10 This demonstrated a high rate of success for oncology patients who used their cryopreserved sperm to achieve parenthood.

However, it is well documented that the utilisation of banked sperm from oncology patients is low, with most clinics having less than 10% of patients returning to the clinic to use their sperm.10 The aggregated rate of usage from 30 studies was shown to be 8%, with a 95% CI of 8% to 9%.10 If we consider that about 49% of patients successfully achieved parenthood using banked sperm, we can estimate that only a very small portion of all patients (less than 4%) actually benefit from oncology sperm banking programmes. In addition, the cost of sperm banking, the accessibility of patient resources and the banking process itself impose stress and barriers for patients.11 12 Clinics also face the issue of maintaining storage for many years with many abandoned samples when patients become lost to follow-up.

Currently, there are sparse long-term data on the usage of banked sperm samples for Canadian oncology sperm banks. In order to determine the utilisation, disposition and pregnancy rate of a Canadian oncology sperm bank, we performed a retrospective chart review of all oncology sperm banking samples from 2001 to 2020 at Mount Sinai Fertility (MSF), one of the largest academic fertility clinics in Canada.

Methods

Patient population and data management

Except for paediatric patients who required surgical retrieval of semen/testicular tissues, all male oncology patients referred to MSF between 2001 and 2020 were included in the study. Data on patients’ diagnoses, sperm analysis results and disposition of the sperm samples were entered into an electronic database and maintained by the department’s andrology lab for the past 20 years. Patient’s complete medical charts were stored in paper charts in the clinic’s secure document storage system until 2019. Date of birth (DOB) for patients was obtained by linking an administrative database with the sperm banking database; however, we were not able to successfully link these two databases for some patients. The missing DOBs were distributed across the time period of our study but were higher in earlier years. For patients who returned to use their sperm sample(s), their partners’ charts were retrieved from the clinic’s secure document storage system and data on the partners’ ART cycles were reviewed and analysed. Data on the usage of sperm and ART cycles were collected until August 2022. Some patients’ partners’ charts were requested by the patient to be transferred to other clinics or destroyed and were not available for review. However, all available charts from the patients’ partners were reviewed with no bias or exclusion.

Semen analysis and ARTs

Semen analyses were done by the andrology lab. Semen and sperm parameters were reviewed and analysed. These included volume, pH, concentration, per cent motility and morphology prior to freezing. Concentration and motility were also assessed in a test thaw sample postfreeze in order to recommend appropriate future treatment options. This was rarely not possible (<5% of cases) when initial sample volume, concentration and/or motility were limiting. Semen analyses were performed according to current WHO recommendations. Although reference values for motility and morphology changed from 4th (1999)13 to 5th (2010)14 editions, the method of assessment was consistent for cases reviewed in this study. Raw semen was frozen by slow freeze with glycerol as the cryopreservative (Behrman et al15; Di Santo et al16).

ART cycles including intrauterine insemination (IUI) cycles and in vitro fertilisation (IVF) cycles were reviewed and analysed. IUI cycles were either performed based on patients’ natural cycles or patients were stimulated using folicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), clomiphene citrate or letrozole. Patients’ age and pregnancy outcomes were reviewed and analysed. For IVF cycles, GnRH agonist or antagonist protocols, or minimal stimulation protocols were used. For our oncology sperm banking patients, IVF inseminations were performed almost exclusively with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). For each IVF cycle, the number of eggs retrieved, the number of injectable eggs and the number of usable embryos, the patient’s age on egg retrieval date and their pregnancy outcomes following embryo transfer (ET) either fresh or frozen were reviewed and analysed. Pregnancy outcomes include those indicated by serum ß-hCG with clinical intrauterine (IU) pregnancy being confirmed on ultrasound.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the distribution of patients’ demographic parameters as well as semen parameters. χ2 statistics were used to compare the distribution of cancer diagnosis among those who returned to use their sperm sample versus all patients in the study. Paired t-tests were used to compare how patients’ semen parameters change in fresh ejaculates vs in post-thaw (PT) analysis. PT analysis was not performed for every patient, especially for patients who had limited number of semen produced; however, all available data were analysed. Unpaired t-tests were used to compare semen parameters between patients with different cancer diagnoses; as well as between pregnancy positive and pregnancy negative groups.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Patient population and demographics by cancer type and age

From 2001 to 2020, 2732 oncology patients were referred. Of these, 228 (8%) patients were unable to bank any samples either due to inability to produce any sample or because no useable sperm was identified in the fresh semen samples produced. The remaining 2504 (92%) patients were able to successfully bank at least one sample at MSF. The number of oncology patients referred per year has steadily increased, from 60 patients in 2001 to 205 patients in 2020 (figure 1A). The most common diagnoses among them were testicular cancer (29.5%) and lymphoma (26.9%), including both Hodgkin’s and non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (figure 1B). The patient’s age at the time of cryopreservation ranged from 11 to 70 with a mean age of 29.8 and a median age of 29 (figure 1C), although age information was missing on 38.5% of patients (see the ‘Methods’ section).

Usage of banked sperm samples

Of 2504 patients who banked, 81 (3.2%) patients used their samples for treatment at our clinic, 452 (18.1%) patients discarded their samples without using them, 62 (2.5%) patients transferred their samples to other fertility clinics, 45 (1.8%) patients donated their samples to research and the remaining 1864 (74.4%) patients still have their samples stored and have not yet used them. The distribution of used banked samples was examined in 5-year increments and no specific trend was seen among the four 5-year brackets (figure 2A).

For the 81 patients who returned to use their sample, the time between banking and return for usage of the sperm ranged from 1 to 131 months with a median of 18 months after banking (figure 2B). Most patients (80%) returned within 3 years to use their sample.

The mean age for all our oncology patients was 29.8 at the time of banking and the mean age for those who returned to use their samples was 36.5 at the time of banking. This suggests that age at the time of banking could be a factor in predicting whether the patients return to use their sample, with older age correlating with a higher likelihood of returning to use samples.

Semen analysis

The freezing process significantly decreased the percentage of progressive motile sperm (grade ‘a’ and ‘b’ sperm based on WHO fourth edition manual for semen analysis) in the sample. Percentage of progressive motile sperm (grade a+b) decreased 58.4% for PT verses prethaw values. Concentration appeared to decrease by 49.1% but this is explained by the addition of sperm freeze media at a volume approximately equal to the semen volume (table 1A).

The sperm parameters also differed significantly between patients with different cancer diagnoses. Patients with testicular cancer had 49.9% lower sperm concentration than patients with cancer diagnosis other than testicular cancer or lymphoma; and though the mean per cent progressive motile (grade a+b) sperm from patients with testicular cancer was slightly higher than patients with other cancer diagnosis, the absolute progressive motile grade a+b sperm count in testicular cancer patients was 40.7% lower than other patients (table 1B, p<0.0001). Lymphoma patients also experienced lower sperm concentration and lower progressive motile sperm counts, however, to a lesser extent than testicular cancer patients, with a 16.0% reduction in concentration and a 17.0% reduction in progressive motile grade a+b count as compared with patients with other diagnosis (table 1B, p<0.0001 and p<0.001).

Despite the fact that the semen quality was much poorer in patients with testicular cancer and lymphoma than in other patients with cancer, the type of cancer diagnosis did not predict whether a patient was more or less likely to come back to the clinic to use their banked sperm—there was no significant difference between the proportions of testicular cancer and lymphoma among patients who returned to use their samples to everyone who banked (figure 2C).

Patients who used their banked sperm for IUI and/or IVF

81 out of the 2504 patients who banked in the 20-year period, returned to the clinic to use their sample. We were able to review pregnancy outcomes for 67 of the 81 patients. We were unable to review the charts for 14 patients to determine their treatment type or outcome. A total of 66 IVF cycles with ICSI resulted in 104 ET and a total of 101 IUI cycles were available for review. 42 of the 67 female patients achieved at least one positive ß-hCG, with 36 patients (53.7%) having at least one clinical IU pregnancy confirmed by ultrasound. There were five patients with biochemical pregnancies or spontaneous abortion and one patient with an ectopic pregnancy. 25 of the 67 female patients (37.3%) did not conceive using banked sperm samples.

Of the 101 IUI cycles (from 44 patients) reviewed, pregnancy outcome data were available for 91 cycles. Seven of the 91 cycles resulted in a positive ß-hCG, with 6 clinical IU pregnancies and 1 biochemical pregnancy. The overall clinical pregnancy rate per IUI cycle was 6.6% for clinical pregnancy, and overall 6/44 (13.6%) patients achieved a clinical pregnancy with IUI. Female patient age on the day of IUI ranged from 25 to 44.

We also reviewed IVF/ICSI outcomes for 52 patients (age ranged from 27 to 42). Overall, 31/52 (59.6%) patients achieved a clinical pregnancy with IVF. Data from 66 IVF/ICSI cycles from 50 patients were available. Three of the 66 cycles had no usable embryos. Of the remaining 63 cycles, a total of 104 ETs were performed. Of these, 49 resulted in a positive ß-hCG, with 32 continuing to clinical IU pregnancy. Subsequent failure included 15 with biochemical pregnancy or spontaneous abortion, 1 with anembryonic pregnancy and 1 ectopic pregnancy. Pregnancy rate per ET was 47.1% as indicated by a positive ß-hCG with 65.3% of those progressing to clinical pregnancy, making the overall clinical pregnancy rate per ET to be 30.8%.

Sperm quality was then compared for samples resulting in pregnancy versus those that did not. For IUI cycles, sperm quality did not appear to differ between the two groups (table 2A). According to PT sperm parameters, sperm quality for IVF cycles was actually worse for patients who successfully achieved pregnancy when compared with those who did not achieve pregnancy. The mean PT sperm concentration and PT progressive motile (grade a+b) count for patients who achieved a pregnancy were, respectively, 52.2% and 65.1% lower than those who did not (table 2B, p<0.01).

Discussion

Many studies have addressed barriers faced by patients regarding access to fertility preservation in Canada, including inadequate information regarding fertility preservation options for patients and their care providers; inadequate information regarding the use of fertility preservation and later success rates for the use of the preserved material and financial costs associated with preservation, storage and subsequent ARTs.17,19 These challenges are being addressed on an ongoing basis with improved physician education and experience,20 and in 2015, Ontario launched the Ontario Fertility Programme to provide funding to patients seeking fertility care which included the cost of fertility preservation. Here, we show that in the 20-year time period, there has been a continuous increase in the number of patients being referred to our clinic for oncology sperm cryopreservation. This increase might have been correlated with the increasing incidence of cancer diagnosis as well as increased access to our services. However, despite this increase, the number of patients who have returned to use their samples has remained consistently low throughout each 5-year bracket over the 20-year period. Overall, the usage was low with only 81 (3.2%) patients who returned to use their samples and 62 (2.5%) patients who transferred their samples to other clinics during the 20-year period. Data on return for usage were collected until August 2022 and we showed that more than 80% of patients returned within 3 years of banking to use their sperm for subsequent ARTs. Despite the availability of the funding programme established in 2015, we did not see any significant increase in the number of patients returning to the clinic to use their sperm suggesting that cost associated with ARTs may not be the most important factor in influencing patients’ decision to return. In addition, given that newer cancer treatment regimens have been proven to be less gonadotoxic,2 21 22 recent year patients might be able to conceive naturally without needing the banked sperm. Further studies should consider the incidence of resumption of natural spermatogenesis following cancer treatment and on current patients’ perceptions and attitudes towards using banked sperm. Information from these studies would improve our understanding of evolving trends in the usage of banked sperm.

In this study, we have shown that IVF with ICSI was superior to IUI in helping oncology patients achieve clinical pregnancy. The use of IUI yielded only 6.6% clinical pregnancy per cycle for our oncology patients, as compared with 30.8% per ET from IVF with ICSI. Most importantly, we showed that IVF with ICSI was able to help patients achieve clinical pregnancy despite poor sperm quality based on analysis of sperm concentration and motility. Considering that most of our oncology patients had a diagnosis of either testicular cancer or lymphoma which was associated with poor sperm concentration and motility, and considering that oncology patients have a limited source of stored sperm, IVF with ICSI is the most effective option. However, it is worth noting that our current methods of evaluating sperm quality using sperm motility and morphology parameters alone might be limited in the ability to predict outcomes of ARTs. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies have found no association between sperm quality, based on concentration and motility, and clinical pregnancy rates from ARTs.23 New studies have been investigating potential biomarkers such as sperm DNA fragmentation and sperm-borne microRNAs to aid in our ability to better assess sperm quality and predict outcomes of ARTs.24 In our study, a patient’s cancer diagnosis did not seem to predict whether the patient would be more or less likely to return to use their sperm despite some of them having poorer semen concentration and sperm motility at the time of banking. Though a Swedish study showed that certain cancer diagnoses were associated with an increased likelihood of using ARTs,25 data on cancer diagnosis/cancer treatment status and ART outcomes from the sperm cryopreservation before cancer treatment cohort has been historically limited due to the lack of patient return to use their stored sperm. Similarly, our study was limited to the small sample size of those who returned.

Though our oncology sperm banking patients were facing potentially significant male factor infertility, there can also be significant female factor contributions to the outcome of treatment. Our patient’s female partner age ranged from 25 to 44 for IUI cycles (with a median of 36 per cycle on the day of IUI) and 27–42 for IVF cycles (with a median of 36 per IVF cycle on the day of OPU). As demonstrated by the Canadian Fertility and Andrology Society’s 2016 report for treatment cycles from 2001 to 2015, the success rates for live birth with IVF was 41% for women under the age of 35 but dropped significantly for subsequent age groups (34% for oocyte-provider age 35–37 to 6% for those 43 years of age and above).26 In our study, we showed that patients who did not achieve a positive pregnancy had less usable eggs per IVF cycle. This implies a potential female factor contribution to their infertility. We would ideally correct for or perform regression analysis on factors such as female age or other fertility diagnosis on the cycle outcomes, however, we were limited by the small number of patients who returned in the last 20 years. This further demonstrated the complexity of fertility care provided to our oncology patients.

Our study is the largest that examined oncology sperm banking at a Canadian fertility centre. The utilisation of sperm samples banked by oncology patients from 2001 to 2020 at our centre was comparable to rates of usage at other centres worldwide.6,927 28 A systematic review published in 2016 looked at 30 studies around the world and reported the aggregated usage to be 8%; and we reported that over the 20-year period, 3.2% of patients returned to use their samples in our clinic and 2.5% transferred their samples to other clinics. A smaller scale study from our clinic published in 2009 reported the success rate for a clinical pregnancy per cycle to be 21% for IUI and 50% for IVF/ICSI from 31 patients who banked between 1985 and 2005 and who returned between 2003 and 2005.9 However, with the inclusion of more patients in more recent years, success rates in our study were 6.6% for IUI and 30.8% per ET for IVF/ICSI. These numbers are comparable to those from other larger scale studies—12.8% for IUI in Bizet et al’s 2012 study29; and 35% per ET in Stigliani et al’s study.6 It is interesting to note that the average age of childbirth continues to rise for both mothers and fathers, and that the total fertility rate across Canada has been steadily decreasing from 2009 to 2019.30 These changes might further influence the rate of success of ART for our oncology patient cohort over time.

There are many factors that play into a patient’s decision to use their banked sperm. Our study is limited in that it is looking at the usage retrospectively. We demonstrated that the use rate for the oncology sperm bank at MSF in the 20-year time period was very low. In our study, 18.1% of patients discarded their stored sperm samples without ever using them and there are 74.4% of patients who still have samples stored but have never used them. We were unsure about the reasons for why the patients discard or have not returned to use their sample; whether it was because of a return of natural spermatogenesis or that they no longer desire parenthood. Given that most patients returned within 3 years to use their banked sperm, it is expected that most of these remaining patients will not return to use their sperm. Cryopreservation and storage are costly for the patient and also put a significant burden on clinics. Therefore, the low usage as well as maintaining the storage for patients who will most likely not return raises the question of the cost-effectiveness of oncology sperm banking services. Though Shankara-Narayana et al31 demonstrated that high disposal rates (62.6%) are possible with regular follow-up by experienced cryostorage nurses, most clinics worldwide have reported low disposal rates29 32 33 with an aggregated rate from five studies concluded to be 16%.31 Future research should involve a more detailed exploration into reasons for a patient’s return or lack of return to use their banked sperm. The findings from such studies could help us provide a more tailored and cost-effective service for our oncology patients.
