Co-assembly of Azospirillum–Pseudomonas biofilms in the rhizosphere enhances lettuce root colonization, growth, and heat-stress resilience
Authors: Pablo R Diaz, Eduardo De Gerónimo, Maria P Borrajo, M Mercedes Labarthe, María V Martino, Cecilia M Creus, Guillermo A Maroniche
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding plant growth-promoting bacteria interaction is essential for developing of effective multi-strain inoculants. Here, we investigated how Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 interact when establishing biofilms under rhizospheric conditions and its impact on root colonization and plant growth. Mixed biofilms assembled in vitro on root exudates revealed competition between both strains, with Sp245 outcompeting A506. On lettuce roots, they formed spatially segregated biofilms according to their individual niche preferences: Sp245 exhibited dense biofilms on and along the main root, while A506 grew preferentially associated to root hairs. Both strains co-localized only in certain hotspots on the root surface and hairs bases. Yet when colonizing roots in substrate, their colonization was mutually enhanced, suggesting that cooperation prevails under these conditions. Co-inoculation of Sp245 and A506 promoted lettuce growth synergistically, increasing leaf area, fresh and dry biomass, and root dry weight. Moreover, co-inoculated plants showed enhanced survival and growth after heat stress. Our findings unveil a complex yet complementary interaction between Sp245 and A506 in the rhizosphere, where their spatial segregation does not preclude cooperation and synergistic plant-beneficial effects. Likewise, the results highlight the potential of simplified two-strain synthetic communities for enhancing crop productivity and resilience under climatic stress.

Introduction

One of the most pressing challenges of the 21st century is climate change, whose consequences extend across multiple sectors, including agriculture. Although plants have evolved strategies to cope with environmental stress, the increasingly extreme conditions exceed their capacity to respond, leading to significant reductions in productivity (Cushman et al. 2022). The use of synthetic consortia of plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) as bioinputs for crops is an eco-friendly powerful approach for improving agricultural performance under unfavorable conditions. However, for a consortium to be successful in promoting plant growth, its members must cooperate to first establish an association with the host plant through chemical signaling, attachment, and biofilm formation (Martins et al. 2023).

The rhizosphere is the primary site of interaction between plants, soil, and microorganisms. In this dynamic environment, the assembly of microbial communities is strongly influenced by root exudates (Ali and Glick 2024). Many of these compounds are chemoattractants, stimulating bacterial motility via pili and flagella, which in turn facilitates adhesion, anchorage, and the development of biofilms on plant tissues. This process occurs sequentially in a manner similar to biofilm development under controlled in vitro conditions: reversible adhesion, irreversible adhesion, microcolony formation, and maturation (Bogino et al. 2013). Disrupting any of these steps could potentially inhibit the colonization of plant tissues. Once the plant-bacteria association is established, PGPB can alter root architecture, facilitating nutrient acquisition and stress tolerance, and influence plant growth and immune responses positively (Vacheron et al. 2013).

Azospirillum and fluorescent Pseudomonas are among the best-studied PGPB, displaying multiple beneficial properties that improve plant yield and health, which led to their combination to formulate mixed inoculants (Valverde et al. 2015). Several studies have demonstrated the success of these co-inoculants in increasing the yields of extensive crops such as wheat and maize (Valverde et al. 2015, Fernández et al. 2024). Regarding horticultural crops, we have previously shown that Azospirillum and Pseudomonas co-inoculation in lettuce can produce synergistic or antagonistic effects on root architecture and aerial growth, depending on strain compatibility (Díaz et al. 2023).

Previously, we demonstrated that Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 can engage in dual-species biofilms in vitro, in which Sp245 takes advantage of the matrix scaffold and microaerophilic conditions to enhance its growth (Diaz et al. 2022). Here, we studied the interaction between Sp245 and A506 in the rhizosphere as mixed biofilms, and the impact of this interaction on root colonization and plant growth promotion capacity.

Material and methods

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

Strains of A. baldaniorum Sp245 and P. fluorescens A506 were routinely cultured on Congo Red (RC) medium (Rodrıguez Cáceres 1982) and Nutrient Agar (NA, nutrient broth supplemented with 1.5% bacteriological agar) and incubated at 28°C. For bacterial re-isolation after plant tissue inoculation, the rifampicin-resistant spontaneous mutant A. baldaniorum Sp245-rif was obtained as described by Borrajo et al. (2022) and cultured on 50 µg ml−1 of rifampicin. To study bacterial interactions and visualize colonization in plants, fluorescent variant strains Sp245CH (Sp245 expressing mCherry) and A506G (A506 expressing EGFP) were grown and manipulated as described by Diaz et al. (2022).

Biofilm formation on exudates

Suspensions of A. baldaniorum Sp245CH and P. fluorescens A506G were prepared as detailed by Diaz et al. (2022). Briefly, bacterial cultures grown in nutrient broth (NB) until stationary phase were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min and resuspended in sterile saline solution (SS, 0.85% NaCl) to an OD600 of 2 (Sp245CH) or 0.2 (A506G). Suspensions were then mixed in equal parts to obtain a 1:1 mixed inoculum (or with SS to obtain individual inocula) with ~10⁸ CFU ml−1 of both strains.

Lettuce (Lactuca sativa cv. Elisa) seeds were superficially disinfected (Borrajo et al. 2022), placed on 2% agar in Petri dishes, and allowed to germinate in a growth chamber at 22 ± 2°C with a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod. After 4 days of growth, seedlings with ~2 cm-long roots were removed from the plates, and 10 µl of bacterial suspensions of Sp245CH, A506G, or a mixed suspension at a 1:1 ratio (Sp245CH: A506G), were applied drop-wise adjacent to where the roots were located. In the case of supplementing the exudates with nitrate, 1 ml of 2% w/v KNO3 was spread on the plates with a Drigalski spatula before seeding the biofilms. After the plates were incubated for 3 days at 28°C, biofilms were imaged and quantified as described previously (Diaz et al. 2022).

Alternatively, lettuce root exudates were collected in water by a hydroponic system. To this end, lettuce seeds were surface-sterilized, placed on 0.8% agar plates (10 seeds per plate) and incubated in the growth chamber. After 4 days, seedlings of similar size were transferred to conical centrifuge tubes containing 50 ml of sterile distilled water (SDW). The seedlings were placed on top of the tube caps previously perforated to allow root passage. The tubes were incubated for 10 more days in the growth chamber, plants were removed and root exudates were recovered. Pooled exudates were concentrated 25-fold with a freeze-dryer (BK-FD10S, Biobase, China), mixed with 2% agar to a final exudate concentration of 2.5% to obtain an “exudate medium,” and used to analyze individual and mixed biofilm formation as described above. When indicated, the extracted exudates were supplemented with 19.8 mM of KNO3 (Exu+NO3), 18.7 mM of NH4Cl (Exu+NH4), 5 mM of glucose (Exu+Glu), 5 mM of fructose (Exu+Fru) or 1 mM of L-glutamate (Exu+Glt). To do this, nutrients were prepared as concentrated stocks, filter-sterilized and added before plating.

Biofilm formation under rhizospheric conditions

To visualize biofilm formation on lettuce roots and rhizosphere in vitro, seeds were disinfected and germinated on 2% agar in the growth chamber. After 4 days of incubation, seedlings of similar size were selected and transferred to 12 cm-square Petri dishes with 2% agar (3 plants per plate). Single or mixed biofilms were then seeded on top of the roots by applying 5 µl-drops of bacterial suspension prepared as mentioned above. The plates were placed vertically and incubated for 3 more days in the growth chamber. After this period, biofilms were visualized by epifluorescence microscopy (EFM) (TE-300, Nikon, Japan) or confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (C1, Nikon, Japan), as detailed elsewhere (Diaz et al. 2022). Uninoculated roots were examined as a control of autofluorescence (Fig. S3D). When required, the Fiji software (Schindelin et al. 2012) was used to deconvolute EFM images with the DeconvolutionLab2 plugin (Sage et al. 2017), and to analyze the colocalization of Sp245CH and A506G signals in CLSM images with the coloc2 plugin from Fiji, which provided Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) values.

Analysis of lettuce root colonization

Seeds were disinfected and inoculated with A506 and Sp245-rif, either individually or mixed, by applying 0.5 µl of bacterial suspensions per seed (final dose: ∼5 × 104 CFU seed−1) and incubating at 28°C for 1 h. After incubation, residual suspensions were removed and the seeds were sown in multi-well trays (7 cc) containing substrate (TS1, Klasmann, Germany; properties: extra fine texture, pH 6, 1 g l−1 fertilizer + trace elements). Trays were placed in the growth chamber with a completely randomized design, with four replicates (trays) per treatment, and irrigated by capillarity with distilled water as needed. After 7 days post-inoculation (dpi), roots from a pool of three plants per tray were collected, gently washed with water, and homogenized with 10 vol (ml: g) of SDW. Homogenates were serially diluted and used for CFU count of Sp245-rif on RC medium with 25 µg ml−1 rifampicin and incubated at 37°C for 72 h, or A506 on NA with 50 µg ml−1 rifampicin and incubated at 28°C for 24 h. The trial was repeated 3 times independently.

The same procedure was followed for the microscopic visualization of lettuce root colonization after seed-inoculation of the fluorescent strains Sp245CH and A506G. At 7 dpi, the roots were carefully washed, mounted on coverslips, and examined by CLSM as described above.

Analysis of lettuce root architecture

To analyzed the architecture of lettuce roots, plants were raised under the same hydroponic growth system used for exudate collection. Lettuce seeds were surface-sterilized and inoculated as described before with individual or mixed (1:1) bacterial suspensions containing a final concentration of 10⁸ CFU ml−1 of each strain. After growing in the hydroponic system for 10 days (14 dpi), plants were retrieved, roots were spread on plastic trays with water, scanned (Epson Perfection V700 Photo, Epson, Japan) and analyzed with RhizoVision Explorer (Seethepalli et al. 2021).

Plant inoculation assay under greenhouse conditions

To evaluate the growth-promoting effects of bacteria on lettuce under greenhouse conditions, seeds were inoculated with individual or mixed formulations at 1:1 (Sp245: A506) and sown in multi-well trays as described before. At 10 dpi, a set of seedlings was transplanted into 120 ml pots with TS1 substrate (Klasmann, Germany), and transferred to a greenhouse with a North-South orientation and natural lighting, arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with 3 blocks (replicates) of 5 plants (sub-samples) per treatment. Irrigation was carried out by capillarity, and fertilization was applied according to crop needs using a nutrient solution containing N, P, K, and Ca in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. At 30 dpi, growth parameters were evaluated: number of leaves per plant; chlorophyll index (SPAD 502 Plus, Minolta, Japan), leaf area measured with a planimeter (LI-3100, LI-Cor Inc., USA), and fresh and dry weight of the aerial part.

A second set of seedlings of the multi-cell trays were transplanted at 10 dpi into 2 l pots, and grown in the greenhouse until harvest time. The pots were arranged in a RCBD with 5 blocks of 2 plants per treatment. At 80 dpi, plants were evaluated for the number of leaves per plant, fresh and dry weight of the aerial part, and rosette coverage area. The latter was assessed by taking aerial photographs and analyzing the green pixels with ImageJ.

Data analysis

All plots represent the mean values and the error bars indicate the standard deviation. The data were statistically analyzed as indicated in the figure legends using Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., USA). In all cases, differences were considered significant at P ≤ 0.05. Unless stated otherwise, data were analyzed by ANOVA plus Tukey’s test, as depicted in figure legends.

Results

Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 compete when forming mixed biofilms on root exudates

Experiments were conducted to determine whether the capacity of A. baldaniorum Sp245 to exploit P. fluorescens A506 in dual-species macrocolony biofilms (Diaz et al. 2022) persists under rhizospheric conditions. We analyzed the growth of individual and mixed (1:1 ratio) macrocolonies formed on root exudates. To this end, lettuce plants were grown on agar plates for 4 days and then removed, obtaining plates with agarized exudates that were then used to seed the biofilms (which then grew without any contact with the roots) (Fig. 1A). Under white light illumination, Sp245CH biofilms exhibited greater biomass than those formed by A506G, which grew very poorly (Fig. 1B). Sp245CH macrocolonies displayed slightly higher fluorescence levels when grown individually than in mixed macrocolonies, although the difference was not statistically significant. Meanwhile, A506G biofilm development was significantly inhibited when co-cultivated with Sp245CH (Fig. 1C). Similar results were observed when macrocolonies were formed on agar supplemented with root exudates that were previously collected from plants growing in water (Fig. S1).

We then investigated whether the poor growth of P. fluorescens A506 biofilms on exudates was due to insufficient nutrient availability. The analysis of the exudates by UHPLC-MS revealed an overall low concentration of sugars (glucose, fructose, and sucrose), which are C sources preferred by P. fluorescens A506, and other nutrients (amino acids, organic acids, and N-compounds), all of which were in the order of a few µM to nM. No nitrate could be detected (Table S1). Accordingly, the supplementation of the exudates with glucose, fructose, glutamate, or nitrate improved A506 biofilm growth, while no change was observed with ammonium. On the other hand, only the addition of ammonium and glutamate improved the growth of Sp245 biofilms (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, and as previously observed in vitro (Diaz et al. 2022), the addition of nitrate to the medium altered the interaction of A506 and Sp245 in mixed biofilms, leading to a growth enhancement of Sp245 while A506 was still negatively affected by Azospirillum (Fig. 1D). This effect was not observed when the other nutrients were added to the exudates (results not shown).

Notably, planktonic growth of both Sp245 and A506 in liquid exudates was very limited, showing only a slight non-significant increase of CFU after 48 h of incubation (Fig. S2A). Since nutrient supplementation led only to a limited improvement in bacterial growth (Fig. S2B), we evaluated the presence of inhibitory compounds in the exudates. The growth of both strains in minimal medium with or without exudates was comparable under liquid culture with agitation (Fig. S2C). However, the presence of exudates in the growth medium had a positive and negative impact on biofilm formation by Azospirillum and Pseudomonas, respectively (Fig. S2D).

Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 coexist on lettuce roots but with distinct niche preferences

Individual and mixed biofilm formation by A. baldaniorum Sp245CH and P. fluorescens A506G was evaluated on lettuce roots grown axenically on agar. This methodology involved seeding bacterial suspensions drop-wise on an emerging root, and later examining it for bacterial dispersion and biofilm formation. Individually, both strains formed an extended biofilm on the exudates-containing agar within the inoculation zone (Fig. 2A). Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245CH biofilms were rough but continuous, while P. fluorescens A506G formed patches of growth (Fig. 2B) that eventually merged into a biofilm of fissured appearance (Fig. 2C). Away from the inoculation site (maturation zone), Sp245CH formed a dense biofilm along the root-agar contact zone (Fig. 2D) and microcolonies across the primary root surface (Fig. 2E), with additional colonization hotspots at lateral root primordia (Fig. 2F) and the root cap (Fig. 2G). A506G also colonized the root surface (Fig. 2H) and grew along the primary root-agar interface, but with a more complex pattern: the biofilm growth was reduced or completely inhibited when proximal to the root surface, while showing a denser accumulation in the immediate outward region (Fig. 2I and J). Notably, A506 was particularly prone to colonizing trichoblasts, showing a very strong signal at the base of the hairs (Fig. 2K and L) and forming biofilms, often in a striped pattern, around those in contact with the agar (Fig. 2J and M). The root tip was also colonized by A506, where it formed a continuous biofilm on the surrounding agar, likely influenced by rhizodepositions (Fig. 2N).

When co-inoculated, Sp245CH and A506G established a dense mixed biofilm on the seeding area, where both strains were heterogeneously distributed and Pseudomonas was mostly confined to regions more distant from the root (Fig. 3A and B). Correlation coefficients ranged from a strong spatial correlation (Fig. 3A) to a weak or negligible one (Fig. 3B). In the rest of the root, both strains exhibited an overall increased fluorescence signal in comparison to individual treatments, although each one with its distinct spatial distribution pattern. Sp245CH formed a robust biofilm that covered the primary root and extended to the nearby agar, while A506G grew further away, surrounding or embedded in Azospirillum biofilm, along the entire length of root hairs (Fig. 3C, D and Fig. S3). In some cases, Azospirillum was able to form small patches of biofilm in association with root hairs, but always enclosed by Pseudomonas growth (Fig. S3). In general, colocalization analysis of these agar-supported biofilms yielded low ρ values, confirming independent spatial distributions for both strains with minimal localization overlap. However, zones of high co-colonization (ρ ≈ 0.7) were detected on the surface of the primary root and at the base of some root hairs (Fig. 3C and E). Finally, the root tip was strongly colonized by A506G, with a limited presence of Sp245CH, leading to ρ values around 0.2 (Fig. 3G).

Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 co-inoculation in lettuce results in a mutual enhancement of root colonization

Root colonization was examined in seed-inoculated lettuce plants, which were grown in substrate for 7 days in the growth chamber. Viable cultivable cell counts in root extracts revealed that Azospirillum recovery was overall lower than that of Pseudomonas. When co-inoculated with Pseudomonas, Sp245 was recovered in significantly higher numbers than when applied individually. Likewise, A506 colonization was strongly enhanced when co-inoculated with Sp245 (Fig. 4A and Fig. S2B). The spatial distribution of both strains in the roots of seed-inoculated plants was also investigated. Microscopy analysis revealed that, when inoculated alone, A. baldaniorum Sp245CH formed isolated microcolonies all along the root (Fig. 4B). In contrast, P. fluorescens A506G was much harder to detect, usually showing a diffuse signal on the root surface and specifically localizing in intercellular spaces within the vascular zone (Fig. 4C). Upon mixed inoculation, Sp245CH maintained its colonization habit as microcolonies along and within the root, while A506G increased its signal appreciably and modified its spatial distribution to produce microcolonies on the root surface, similar to those of Sp245CH (Fig. 4D and Fig. S2B). However, no spatial overlap (ρ ≈ 0.1 to 0.2) was observed between the two strains (results not shown). Notably, in specific regions of the co-inoculated root, Sp245CH and A506G exhibited an internal longitudinal distribution in the vascular zone, in which the colocalization index was higher (Fig. 4E and Fig. S3C). No fluorescence signal was detected in uninoculated roots (Fig. S3D).

Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 co-inoculation in lettuce results in a synergistic plant growth promotion

To investigate how the interaction of A. baldaniorum Sp245 and P. fluorescens A506 during colonization influences plant growth, plants root architecture was first examined. In this case, to avoid damaging the fragile roots during cleaning, seed-inoculated plants were raised in a water-based hydroponic system that allowed the scanning of intact roots (Fig. 5A). Seedlings inoculated with A. baldaniorum Sp245 evidenced an increase in total root length (Fig. 5B) and surface area (Fig. 5C) when compared to the uninoculated plants, showing in turn a lower root diameter that was not significantly different (p = 0.235) from that of the control uninoculated plants (Fig. 5D). Conversely, inoculation with P. fluorescens A506 had no impact on root growth (Fig. 5). When Sp245 and A506 were combined, co-inoculated seedlings developed root systems of intermediate growth that were not significantly different from the rest of the treatments in any parameter (Fig. 5).

(B, C, and D) Total root length (B), surface area (C) and average diameter (D) values, as determined from scanned images by RhizoVision Explorer software. Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test. Different letters indicate significantly different groups.

The effect of bacterial inoculation on lettuce growth was further assessed under greenhouse condition at 30 and 80 dpi. At 30 dpi, Azospirillum inoculation had no effect on plant growth (Fig. 6). Instead, treating with A506 alone led to a significant increase in the number of leaves per plant (Fig. 6A) and root dry weight (Fig. 6C), and a reduction in the chlorophyll index when inoculated either alone or in combination with Sp245 (Fig. 6B). Notably, plants co-inoculated with A506+Sp245 exhibited a significant increase in total leaf area, fresh and dry aerial biomass, and root dry weight in comparison to uninoculated plants or those inoculated with Sp245 alone (Fig. 6A and C). Even though the primary root length was not significantly altered by any of the treatments (Fig. 6C), the mean values followed a similar trend to that observed in root architecture analysis (Fig. 5B and C). At harvest time (80 dpi), all bacterial treatments induced a significant increase in the number of leaves, as well as in the aerial fresh and dry weight (Fig. 6D to F). A significant effect of Sp245+A506 on the rosette area (Fig. 6G) could be observed only when compared pairwise with the untreated control group (P = 0.016).

Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 co-inoculation protects lettuce from heat-induced stress

An additional greenhouse trial was conducted during the springtime of 2023 to evaluate the effect of Sp245+A506 inoculation on lettuce growth. At that time, the temperatures before the transplant (8 dpi) were unusually high, peaking at 32°C outside (Fig. 7A) and reaching 45°C inside the greenhouse. These conditions imposed a severe thermal stress on the seedlings, and only 35.4% of the uninoculated plants survived. Under these conditions, seedlings co-inoculated with A506 and Sp245 exhibited an outstanding stress resilience, resulting in a significantly higher survival rate (95.5%). Although both individual treatments also improved the survival rates (i.e. 59.3% and 79.2% for Sp245- and A506-treated plants, respectively), only P. fluorescens A506 did so significantly (Fig. 6B).

After the heat shock, at 10 dpi, a set of surviving plants (Fig. 6C) were transplanted and raised for 20 more days in the greenhouse before growth analysis. Only lettuce plants treated with Sp245+A506 combination exhibited an improved growth when compared to the uninoculated ones, as revealed by a significant increase in leaves area (Fig. 6E) and chlorophyll index (Fig. 6F). This was not the case for Sp245 or A506 single-inoculation treatments, which did not alter lettuce growth significantly (Fig. 6D to G), even when survival rates had been higher (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

Despite the strong scientific foundation supporting PGPB-mediated plant growth promotion, the conditions that allow their successful combination in synthetic consortia to benefit plants synergistically are not fully understood. Detecting incompatibilities between strains through in vitro and, if possible, in planta studies is increasingly recognized as a necessary step for a successful consortium assembly, given that multispecies inoculants do not always result in superior effects when compared to single species, or even non-inoculated conditions (Niu et al. 2020). Previously, we found that A. baldaniorum Sp245 and P. fluorescens A506 can engage in a particular exploitative relationship in dual-species biofilms in vitro (Diaz et al. 2022). In this work, we went further to investigate how this interaction plays out under in vivo conditions, by analyzing mixed biofilms formed on lettuce exudates and roots, the impact of Sp245 and A506 association on root colonization, and their later influence on plant development.

Formation of macrocolonies on lettuce root exudates as the sole nutrient source, either in situ or ex situ, demonstrated the superior capacity of A. baldaniorum Sp245 for biofilm formation on exudates when compared to P. fluorescens A506. The relatively poor performance of A506 is not surprising given the epiphytic nature of the strain, which is adapted to colonize aerial plant surfaces, where it rapidly consumes photoassimilates such as sucrose, fructose, and glucose (Mercier and Lindow 2000). These and other C-compounds were present in the exudates, but their overall low concentration was a limiting factor for the growth of A506 biofilms, which could then be improved by the supplementation of additional C-rich nutrients. Thus, our data indicate that the differential growth of Sp245 and A506 on exudates answers to a different strategy to cope with nutrient starvation. In this sense, the well-known capacity of Azospirillum to form cysts and store polyhydroxybutyrate to overcome prolonged starvation periods (Kadouri et al. 2003), as well as a quick induction of biofilm mode (since the exudates do not seem to sustain significant planktonic growth), might be key traits that explain the superior capacity of Sp245 to grow on exudates. These differential adaptative characteristics can also explain why Sp245 outcompeted A506 in mixed macrocolonies grown on exudates. Unlike the interaction of both strains on agarized synthetic media, Sp245 growth was not increased nor decreased significantly in the biofilm. This may be attributed to the poor growth of A506, which is required by azospirilla to increase its population (Diaz et al. 2022). Notably, Azospirillum growth stimulation in mixed biofilms was only detected on nitrate-supplemented exudates, suggesting that this molecule modulates Pseudomonas physiology in a way that lowers the secretion of Azospirillum-inhibitory compounds (i.e. siderophores or antibiotics). This evidence resembles what happens in vitro on RC medium, where nitrate similarly acts as an inducer of the exploitative relationship between Sp245 and A506 (Diaz et al. 2022). Thus, the RC medium may be an appropriate choice for predicting multi-species biofilm formation under a rhizospheric nutritional condition.

It has been established that, after being attracted by exudates, rhizobacteria first anchor stably to the root surface and then settle by forming a biofilm. This colonization process is important for plant-growth stimulation by beneficial bacteria (Bogino et al. 2013). We demonstrated that both Sp245 and A506 can feed on exudates to establish biofilms on and around the main root but, in agreement with the above-mentioned results, Sp245 growth is more robust and continuous than the weaker-patchy biofilms of A506. Interestingly, when co-inoculated, Sp245 and A506 competed for niche occupation, forming spatially segregated biofilms with minimal physical overlap. Once again, Azospirillum primed over Pseudomonas around the primary root. However, in spite of being displaced by Sp245 to the outermost region of the biofilm, A506 showed an increased fluorescence, suggesting the existence of some kind of metabolic complementation or facilitation that favors Pseudomonas growth. Likewise, colocalization hotspots observed on the root surface and the base of root hairs may represent microhabitats where atmospheric conditions, nutrient availability and/or chemical signaling allows their coexistence or cooperation through metabolic engagement. The positive side of the interaction between Sp245 and A506 also emerged during root colonization of plants grown on substrate, endowing A506 with improved adherence to the root surface in the form of microcolonies and enhancing Sp245 endophytic colonization of the vessels. Taken together, our results reveal a complex dynamical tripartite interaction between Sp245, A506 and lettuce roots, where the three major scenarios of interaction proposed by Jacoby and Kopriva (2019), i.e. competitive exclusion, niche differentiation and niche creation, are at play simultaneously.

Away from the primary root, P. fluorescens A506 had a notable predilection for growing on root hairs, either alone or in the presence of A. baldaniorum Sp245, which was instead restricted to the base of the cells or to small patches within Pseudomonas biofilm. We were particularly intrigued by the sharply-delimited and intense localization of A506 in the base of root hairs. This disc-shaped cellular domain is a zone of high cell wall loosening and expansion activity during root hair initiation (Grierson et al. 2014). We speculate that the unique cell wall signature of the root hair base may facilitate A506 attachment, to then reach the apoplast and spread throughout the endosphere. This belief is supported by the microscopic observation of A506, which was hardly detected on the surface of roots but invaded the vascular tissues frequently, and by its capacity to translocate systematically to the rest of the plant (Diaz et al. unpublished results).

The sharp delimitation of A. baldaniorum Sp245 and P. fluorescens A506 growth in the biofilms according to their niche preference is most probably driven by differences in exudates composition. Root exudates are known to modulate microbiome establishment through the diversity and quantity of the secreted compounds, and vary significantly depending on plant developmental stage and root zone (Chen and Liu 2024). Specifically, in Arabidopsis, root hairs are thought to fine tune the regulation of endophyte colonization through the production of certain compounds, such as coumarins (Stringlis et al. 2018, Verbon et al. 2023). These iron-mobilizing compounds of selective antimicrobial activity are differentially exudated by distinct cells types: scopoletin is produced by root hairs whereas other coumarins (i.e. fraxetin and esculetin) are secreted through all epidermis cells (Robe et al. 2020). Since lettuce, as part of the Asteraceae family, also produces coumarins (Viacava et al. 2018), a similar exudation pattern may be influencing the distribution of Sp245 and A506. In particular, the high sensitivity of Azospirillum to siderophores (Maroniche et al. 2018) can possibly explain its low presence on and around root hairs where coumarins are expected to be at high concentrations.

Despite their spatial segregation in root-associated biofilms, when co-inoculated in seeds, both strains mutually enhanced their capacity to colonize roots in substrate-grown plants. Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 was particularly benefited from the interaction, improving its root colonization capacity by almost a 10-fold increase. This enhancement was evident under the microscope, where numerous microcolonies of A506 could be observed on the surface of roots grown in substrate, but only when co-inoculated with Sp245. Conversely, Sp245 colonization of root vascular tissues was facilitated by A506 interaction. These results suggest that under natural conditions, where other factors such as competing microorganisms or environmental stress are present, the cooperative behavior of Sp245 and A506 prevails, resulting in a synergistic attachment and colonization of the roots. In line with this notion, A506 did not cooperate but interfered with Sp245 root growth stimulation under axenic hydroponic conditions. Several mechanisms may explain Sp245 and A506 cooperation under natural conditions: metabolic complementation, altered root exudation, attachment facilitation, etc. Discerning which one is at play in this case demands further investigation.

The cooperation of A. baldaniorum Sp245 and P. fluorescens A506 when colonizing roots under natural conditions had a positive impact on the growth of inoculated plants at 30 dpi, which exhibited a synergistic increase in the size and weight of the aerial part when co-inoculated. This result aligns well with our previous findings about the synergistic potential of A506 with Azospirillum (Díaz et al. 2023). However, as lettuce development progressed to harvest time (80 dpi), the synergistic effect of Sp245+A506 on shoot biomass was no longer observed. Instead, all the bacterial treatments improved the growth of mature plants was improved similarly. Therefore, our results suggest that, at advanced stages of lettuce growth, strain-specific beneficial traits of PGPB lose influence, and biostimulation is primarily driven by general mechanisms, such as induced systemic resistance. Notably, the synergy between Sp245 and A506 was neither observed in 10-day-old seedlings raised hydroponically under axenic conditions. Only Sp245 individually stimulated root growth, a capacity that is extensively documented in Azospirillum (Steenhoudt and Vanderleyden 2000). In fact, the presence of A506 appeared to attenuate the capacity of Sp245 to increase root growth, suggesting that in this hydroponic system, as in the other axenic systems used in this work, both strains compete for root colonization. We have previously reported that in a low-oxygen environment, where Azospirillum thrives due to its microaerophilic nature, Sp245 and A506 engage in a competitive interaction, ultimately reducing their growth mutually (Diaz et al. 2022). Thus, the lack of water aeration in our hydroponic system may have intensified their competition, thereby hampering Azospirillum stimulatory activity.

The synergy of A. baldaniorum Sp245 and P. fluorescens A506 for promoting lettuce growth was observed even under extreme thermal stress conditions. The co-inoculation with Sp245 and A506 significantly enhanced lettuce seedling survival after a temperature peak (45°C inside the greenhouse) that occurred just before the transplant, far surpassing the untreated and Sp245-treated groups. The superior effects of Sp245+A506 treatment were visible even 20 days after transplanting the surviving plants, producing a significant improvement of growth parameters such as chlorophyll index and leaves size, plus higher mean values of leaf number and aboveground fresh weight. These results unveil complementary effects of Sp245 and A506 that go beyond mere survival, contributing to the general vigor of the plant during and after stress exposure. Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245, for its part, is already known to confer stress-tolerance to plants through different mechanisms, including phytohormones secretion and the induction of the plant’s oxidative and stress response (Creus et al. 2010, Fasciglione et al. 2012, Cohen et al. 2015, Pagnussat et al. 2020). Pseudomonas fluorescens A506, on the other hand, has not been characterized in this regard yet. Interestingly, A506 genome codes for a rhl-type quorum sensing system, which is signalized by N-butanoyl-L-homoserine lactone (Pesci et al. 1997). This molecule is a strong candidate to mediate the establishment of a cooperative interaction of A506 with the host plant, as it is known to induce defense mechanisms and promote plant growth (von Rad et al. 2008). Nevertheless, given that the survival of Sp245+A506 co-inoculated seedlings was higher, but not significantly different to A506-treated plants, it is evident that Pseudomonas is a key actor conferring thermal stress resilience to the plants. This is also true for overall lettuce biostimulation, since the individual inoculation of A506 improved several growth parameters in 30-day-old lettuce plants in both experiments. Thus, it is reasonable to hypothesize that Sp245+A506 synergy can be attributed, in part, to the enhanced plant colonization of P. fluorescens A506 when co-inoculated with Sp245. Still, the specific mechanisms through which A506 promotes lettuce growth remains to be investigated.

Conclusions

This study provides novel insights into the ecological interaction between Azospirillum baldaniorum Sp245 and Pseudomonas fluorescens A506 during lettuce root colonization, revealing that despite their competitive behavior in vitro, both strains can establish a complementary and spatially segregated colonization pattern in planta. Co-inoculation enhanced the colonization efficiency of both strains and led to synergistic effects on plant growth and resilience under abiotic stress. These findings highlight the potential of combining functionally complementary PGPB strains to enhance crop productivity under unfavorable environmental conditions.
