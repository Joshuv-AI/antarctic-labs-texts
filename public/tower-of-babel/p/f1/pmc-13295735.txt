Upstream passage windows for anadromous fish at a large barrier in the Rhine-Meuse estuary, 1996–2018
Authors: Melanie P Meijer zu Schlochtern, Leopold AJ Nagelkerke, Anthonie D Buijse, F Tim Vriese, André W Breukelaar, Jochem Hop, Hendrik V Winter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPassage behaviour at barriers of migratory fish is well studied in rivers, whereas little is known about fish movement at estuarine barriers. In response to climate change, more estuarine barriers are likely to be erected in the future, further compromising connectivity for diadromous fish. As these fish species are already severely endangered, there is an urgent need to better understand passage behaviour at barriers to facilitate connectivity. These fish face challenges at estuarine sluice gates, where suitable passage windows occur only infrequently. To enhance fish migration, sluice management at the Haringvliet barrier, located at the mouth of the Rhine and Meuse rivers, was recently adjusted to allow controlled inflow from the sea (“Kier management”). We assessed upstream passage windows for large anadromous fish prior to this adjustment, using long-term (1996–2018) telemetry (NEDAP Trail System®) and environmental data. Of 1,751 tagged Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar), sea trout (Salmo trutta), and sea lamprey (Petromyzon marinus), 516 (29%) entered the rivers and for 68 fish we could determine the exact conditions during sluice passage. Our results show the barrier effect of the sluices and indicate the importance of river discharge to attract anadromous fish. Sluices discharged only during low tide, with flow velocities depending largely on water level differences between Haringvliet and the sea. Fish passage occurred primarily (72%) at the end of discharge periods, when the incoming tide reduced water level differences and flow velocities were lowest. Our findings also underline species-specific differences in passage conditions. Sluice passage for salmon and sea trout was limited to daylight hours, whereas sea lamprey showed no diel preference. Sea trout took, on average, more time between release and successful passage and showed a wider range in conditions, while sea lamprey had a limited seasonal range, and salmon appeared most selective in their passage conditions. The frequency of windows in time with potential passage conditions was strongly limited for all species under these unnatural conditions. These findings highlight the need for species-specific management to enhance connectivity at estuarine barriers. Introduction

Passage behaviour of migrating fish is often studied at river barriers [1, 2] that are regularly mitigated by fish passage facilities [3]. In contrast, little is known about fish movement and passage behaviour at barriers in estuaries. These barriers reduce connectivity for migrating diadromous fish and alter natural estuarine features, such as tidal dynamics and river discharge [4]. Various large estuarine barriers have been constructed to regulate freshwater flow and prevent saltwater intrusion using discharge sluices [4]. However, upstream fish passage through these sluices is often only possible by surmounting strong currents or bypassing them during short migration windows [5, 6]. For instance, the study by Griffioen et al. [5] conducted at a large estuarine barrier in the Netherlands demonstrated poor upstream passage success for sea lamprey, Petromyzon marinus.

Globally, many estuaries have already been severely altered or obstructed, hindering and delaying successful fish migration [7]. Connectivity in estuaries is fundamental, given their essential role of connecting sea and river ecosystems [8]. At the same time, estuarine barriers help mitigate climate change impacts by reducing flood risk and securing freshwater supply to meet increasing demands [4]. Nonetheless, due to climate change, more estuarine barriers are likely to be constructed in the near future [9]. Therefore, connectivity in estuaries and deltas is expected to decline, highlighting the urgent need for maintaining or improving conditions for fish migration.

Many diadromous fish populations in the North Atlantic basin have already experienced severe declines or local extinctions, emphasizing the need for their conservation and restoration [10–12]. Many species from the families Salmonidae (including salmons, trouts, and whitefishes), Clupeidae (shads), Petromyzontidae (lampreys), and Acipenseridae (sturgeons) are now classified as threatened or endangered [10] and are listed under the EU Habitats Directive Annex II, IV or V. Further declines risk the loss of species and the associated ecosystem services provided by diadromous fish [1, 10, 13, 14]. An important contributor to these declines is migration hinderance, limiting the number of fish reaching upstream spawning grounds or feeding areas [13, 15].

Several studies have shown that river barriers hamper migration and contribute to declines in diadromous fish populations and reproductive success [2, 7, 15]. Barriers can cause migration delays due to unsuccessful passage attempts or misleading cues, leading to additional energy losses while attempting to pass [13]. These delays impact reproductive success as fish may arrive late for spawning and egg production or deplete all their energy reserves during migration [15, 16]. Furthermore, delays at barriers strongly increase the risk of mortality, as fish become more vulnerable to fisheries and predation, particularly because higher concentrations of fish near barriers attract more predators [17, 18]. Therefore, to stimulate recovery of diadromous fish species, it is essential to restore connectivity and saline gradients between freshwater and coastal marine ecosystems around estuaries [10, 13, 19].

In addition to barriers obstructing fish migration, passage through estuarine sluices is controlled by discharge management, whereas natural estuarine migration is driven by tides, river discharge, temperature, and salinity [5, 14]. Such discharge management complicates upstream fish passage in several ways. First, successful passage depends on the ability of fish to locate sluice entrances at the right time, e.g. for salmonids visual cues play an important role in these challenging barrier environments [20]. Second, in the Netherlands, most sluice gates operate only at low tide, further restricting passage opportunities [5, 6, 21]. Moreover, fish passage is also limited by water flow through the sluices: low velocities occur only briefly at the start and end of discharge periods when sea-freshwater level differences are minimal. Both strong swimmers, such as salmonids, and weaker swimmers, like lampreys, have been observed passing these short low-velocity windows [5, 21]. However, knowledge of successful fish passage windows at estuarine sluices remains limited.

One transboundary river where populations of diadromous fishes could be restored is the Rhine river. Long-term reintroduction programs and management efforts have been undertaken to restore diadromous populations, for example for Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) and North Sea houting (Coregonus oxyrinchus) [19, 22]. Nevertheless, Atlantic salmon has not re-established a viable population in this river yet and a high percentage of adults disappears in the Dutch (74%) and German Rhine (78%) [12]. Historically, the Rhine discharged into the Haringvliet estuary; however, an estuarine barrier with sluices was installed in 1970 [8]. This barrier significantly impacted diadromous fish migration and upstream spawning populations. To improve migration in the Dutch lower Rhine, sluice management of the Haringvliet barrier has been debated in studies and policy since the 1990s [8]. Full recovery of estuarine dynamics in the Haringvliet is politically unfeasible due to freshwater needs for agriculture and drinking water. Recently, adjusted sluice management ("Kier management") has been implemented to allow limited saltwater inflow and improve fish passage [6, 23].

The aim of this study was to identify when and under which circumstances upstream passage of large migratory fish through estuarine sluices occurred prior to adjusted sluice management. We used long-term telemetry monitoring (NEDAP Trail System®) and environmental data from 1996 to 2018 to examine upstream passage conditions of anadromous fish through the Haringvliet sluices. We evaluated the role of river discharge for upstream migration and river entry choices after fish release at the Haringvliet barrier, to assess its effect on anadromous fish and the potential loss of passage windows.

Methods

Study area

The estuarine barrier in the Haringvliet is located in the delta of the Rhine and Meuse rivers (Fig. 1). The Haringvliet sluice complex, approximately 1 km long, has seventeen sluice gates (seaside sluice doors are each 57.5–58.5 m wide), which protect the Dutch lowlands against flooding and storm surges [6, 24]. Since barrier construction in 1970, the Haringvliet also serves as a large freshwater reservoir, and its water level is managed to prevent saltwater intrusion. The sluices severely changed water exchange, as freshwater discharge only occurred during low tide, with no saltwater inflow permitted at high tide [6]. In addition to the sluice gates, the barrier includes six fish locks, five salt funnels (unsuitable for fish passage) and a ship lock (Fig. 2) [6].

The operation of the Haringvliet sluices depends on the discharge of the river Rhine and the tidal cycle, and regulates water movement in the Rhine-Meuse delta [6, 24, 25]. The sluices are closed when river Rhine discharge is < 1100 m³·s⁻¹, ~ 5% of river water flows through them when discharge is up to 1750 m³·s⁻¹, increasing to 60% at very high discharges (Fig. 4A). Below the threshold of 1100 m³·s⁻¹, the Haringvliet sluices are continuously closed and the river discharges entirely via the Port of Rotterdam (Nieuwe Waterweg), that has a permanently open free-flowing connection to the sea for fish [6, 25]. Above the river discharge threshold, the sluice gates are raised to discharge river water into the sea during the entire low tide part of the tidal cycle, when the Haringvliet water level is at least 12 cm higher than sea level, thus preventing saltwater intrusion [25]. Flow velocities through the sluice gates depend largely on the water level difference between Haringvliet and the sea. They are lowest just after the sluice gates open and just before they close, and highest in the middle of discharge periods at low tide (Fig. 8A). Sluices lift upwards from the middle of the seaside water column at an elevation of -5.5 m N.A.P. (Normaal Amsterdams Peil, which corresponds to mean sea level), resulting in a gate height that varies between 0.43 and 6.05 m depending on river discharge [6, 25].

Six fish locks are integrated into the pillars of the Haringvliet sluices (Fig. 2). Their purpose is to facilitate fish migration, and they operate during all low tide periods independent of discharge management [24]. Fish lock openings measure 2 m in height and 1.5 m in width, have a length of 60 m, and are positioned at an elevation of -5.5 m N.A.P [6, 24]. The fish locks function similarly to ship locks, alternately opening sea and river doors while partially opening (15–25 cm) the opposite side to create attraction flow. Each complete cycle lasts approximately 25 min, with each door (sea and river side) fully open for about 7 min; the remaining time is used for operating the doors (pers. com. S. de Goederen, Rijkswaterstaat). To increase opportunities for glass eel to enter the Haringvliet on tidal currents, the fish locks operate continuously during their migration period from 1 March to 1 June [25]. Fish can also enter the Haringvliet through the ship lock in the harbour of Stellendam [6], although no substantial freshwater discharge occurs here.

Telemetry data

We used telemetry data collected with the NEDAP Trail System® during 1996–2018 [12, 21, 26] to analyse fish migration opportunities at the Haringvliet sluices. This system is a low-frequency inductive telemetry network which detected tagged fish when passing a detection station [21, 26]. Fish were tagged with transponders (Nedap B.V., Groenlo, The Netherlands), consisting of biocompatible, inert glass or HDPE capsules, containing a ferrite rod, a custom integrated circuit, and two compact batteries [23, 26]. Each detection station consisted of an antenna loop (three parallel cables), a receiver, and a transmitter that activated the transponders every 4 s. When activated the tag transmitted its unique ID to the receiver [26, 27]. Breukelaar et al. [26] reported that the system has reliable detection over antenna lengths up to at least 550 m and water depths up to at least 15 m, but it cannot determine the direction of fish movements [21, 28].

Since 1996, a growing network of permanent detection stations across the Rhine and Meuse rivers has been tracking fish with NEDAP transponders [21, 23, 26]. Our current study focused on stations in the Rhine-Meuse delta (Fig. 1C), considering that stations were sometimes inactive, replaced, or removed [29]. Detection stations at the Haringvliet sluices were relocated over time: initially inside Haringvliet (2000–2001), then to two stations within the sluices (December 2001), and later to a single station ~ 50 m behind the sluices (December 2014) [21, 29, 30]. One station was continuously placed in the ship lock on the river side, and stations at Nieuwe Waterweg and Hartelkanaal became operational in 2010 to monitor northern entry via the Port of Rotterdam (Fig. 1). Detection efficiency of stations varied by species and location, but was generally high for downstream migrating fish, with an average of about 90% at five stations in the Rhine-Meuse delta for silver eels in 2018 and around 85% at all stations for smolts in 2006–2016 [23, 31, 32].

Between 1996 and 2018, wild adult anadromous fish were primarily captured in salmon fykes set within 1 km of the southern seaside of the Haringvliet sluices and tagged: sea trout (Salmo trutta) and Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) since 1996 [26], and sea lamprey (Petromyzon marinus) since 2010 (Table 1) [33]. Salmon and sea trout were tagged with large glass transponders (15 × 85 mm; 38 g in air, 3–5 year battery life) in 1996–2008, and with medium glass transponders (15 × 66 mm; 26 g in air, ≥ 2 year battery life) in 2009–2018 [21, 26, 29, 34, 35]. Sea lamprey were tagged with small HDPE-encased transponders (12 × 38 mm; 11.5 g in air, ≥ 3 months battery life) [33]. The mean tag-to-body weight ratio (tag burden) ranged from 1.14 to 2.53% for the three species (Table 1), remaining within the 5% threshold reported by Verhelst et al. [27]. In early tagging experiments (1996–2000), when only larger tags were available, 1.48% of all fish (mostly sea trout and one salmon), exceeded the 5% tag burden (maximum 6.75%). Studies indicate that fish can tolerate higher tag weights [36, 37]. After capture, fish were held for up to one day in a 1,500 L seawater holding tank with circulation [21], then anesthetized in a 40 mg·L− 1 benzocaine solution, measured and weighed, and transponders were surgically implanted in the abdominal body cavity [38]. After recovery, fish were released either at the seaside of the Haringvliet sluices or, in 2010–2015, also in the seaside harbour (Fig. 2; approximately 25% of all fish), depending on local constraints such as boat availability or rough seas.

Environmental and sluice management data

We compared upstream fish entry at the Haringvliet sluices with environmental and sluice management data (Table 2; Fig. 1). River discharge data (1996–2018) for the river Rhine at Lobith near the German border were obtained from Rijkswaterstaat, which also modelled discharge distribution between Port of Rotterdam and the Haringvliet sluices as a function of Rhine discharge. Water temperature, water level at the nearest monitoring station, and sluice management data were also retrieved from Rijkswaterstaat. Wind data (2002–2018) were downloaded from the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI). All data were plotted over time and examined for potential errors.

Several sluice management variables were derived from sluice gate height data recorded every 10 min. First, we calculated the cumulative gate height of all open sluices and the number of open gates. Total gate opening was then computed as the product of gate height and sluice width. Second, we calculated the cumulative time sluices were open within each discharge period, expressed on a 0–1.04 scale (relative time): 0 when closed, 0.02 when just opened (i.e. within the first 10-minute interval after opening), 1.00 for the last 10-minute interval before closure, and 1.02 or 1.04 at closure (Supplementary materials, Text S1). Individual discharge periods varied in duration (typically 7–10 h) depending on tidal conditions and water levels in Haringvliet. Water level differences were calculated every 10 minutes as the water level at Hellevoetsluis (town on Lake Haringvliet; Fig. 1C) minus the sea water level. Water temperature data from the highly correlated ‘Brouwershavensche Gat’ station (Pearson’s r(606711) = 0.988, p < 0.0001; Supplementary materials, Fig. S1) were used due to large data gaps at ‘Haringvliet 10’ (nearest measuring station). Missing water temperature values (max. ~45 days) were estimated using linear interpolation. Wind direction was transformed into cosine and sine components to represent north–south and east–west axes [40]. Daylength and daylight hours at latitude 51.831015 were calculated using the suncalc R package [41].

Initial selection of fish data

The NEDAP databases contained all tagged fish, detections, and station data for 1996–2018. Fish tagging data was pre-processed by standardizing variable coding and removing all duplicate entries originating from multiple data sources. We then retained only the most reliable records for duplicate entries of the target species, following these steps: First, 49 fish had duplicated records with slight differences in their registered characteristics (e.g., weight or length), we retained data from the consistently accurate source ‘visadvies’, the organization responsible for most of the tagging) and discarded the other entries. Next, 269 fish were removed because their transponder numbers were reused; we retained only those fish whose release dates differed by more than two years, matching the average transponder battery life. Finally, for these retained fish, unique transponder IDs (ID + a, b, or c) were assigned and merged with the detection data, along with all non-duplicated fish.

After merging fish with detection data, we selected the anadromous fish species: Atlantic salmon, hereafter “salmon”, sea trout and sea lamprey. We included only adult fish (total length ≥ 39 cm for all species) that were caught and released on the seaward side of the Haringvliet sluices. We then checked whether these individuals were detected by NEDAP stations at the river entries and within the Rhine-Meuse delta (Fig. 1C). Six fish were excluded because, for their detections, the database contained duplicate entries of the same event with conflicting station names.

Data analysis

Attraction of river discharge for upstream migration (step 1 in Fig. 3)

The extent to which river discharge explained the probability of river entry was assessed by plotting the frequency of released and detected fish for each species across categorical discharge classes of 250 m³·s⁻¹ during 1996–2018. For each species, a logistic regression model was fitted with river discharge during fish release as a continuous explanatory variable and probability of river entry as response variable (0 = no river detection and 1 = positive river detection). Models were fitted using the glm function in R [42] with a binomial family. Model validity was evaluated using marginal model plots [46] and randomized quantile residuals [47]. We assumed that river discharge during release closely approximated discharge during capture, as tagging generally occurred within one day after capture in the fykes. Daily discharge changes on consecutive days were modest (median = 54 m³·s⁻¹, which is smaller than the 250 m³·s⁻¹ discharge classes).

River entry routes for upstream migration (step 2 in Fig. 3)

Based on first detections, upstream movement, and flow directions, we defined three river entry categories for the Rhine-Meuse delta (Fig. 1): (1) via Haringvliet (first detected at Haringvliet sluices, ship lock, or Hellevoetsluis); (2) via the Port of Rotterdam (first detected at Nieuwe Waterweg, Hartelkanaal, Oude Maas, De Noord, or Lek); or (3) entry unknown (detected inland but missed at river entry stations) [12]. Fish classified as unknown may have used either route due to missed detections or non-operational stations (e.g., Nieuwe Waterweg and Hartelkanaal, often offline and only installed in 2010). Fish first detected at Spui or Dordtsche Kil were also classified as unknown, as they can migrate via both routes depending on tide, discharge, or flow direction.

Data preparation steps (step 3–5 in Fig. 3)

To determine fish passage timing and conditions at the Haringvliet barrier, we selected all upstream migrating fish whose first detections were at the ship lock or at the Haringvliet sluices in the period 2002–2018 (Fig. 2). Each entry detection (in minutes) was matched to the nearest 10-minute interval of environmental and sluice management data. Additional steps were taken to determine fish passage timing and sluice closure events (Supplementary materials, Text S1). We defined three entry categories into Haringvliet: (1) passage during discharge of the Haringvliet sluices either through the sluice gates or fish locks, (2) passage via the fish locks when no discharge occurred, or (3) passage via the nearby ship lock (Fig. 2). Fish passage during discharge of the sluices could be via the discharge sluices or via the fish locks, as the NEDAP station spanned the entire sluice complex and no separate detection cables were installed at the fish locks (Supplementary materials, Text S2). Fish entering via the ship lock were excluded from further analysis, as this fell outside the study scope. We used each fish’s first detection at the Haringvliet sluices station to determine conditions during passage, assuming this detection best reflected the actual time of passage (Supplementary materials, Text S3).

The detection stations placed in 2002–2014 within the Haringvliet sluices, were occasionally able to detect transponders from fish that had not yet passed through the sluices (Supplementary materials, Text S4). Therefore, we categorized fish detections in three uncertainty levels based on upstream detections and the number of detections at the Haringvliet sluices (Fig. 3): (1) fish entry exact timing (detected upstream and all Haringvliet sluices detections within 10 min); (2) fish entry approximate timing (detected upstream with multiple Haringvliet sluices detections during > 10 min); (3) fish entry uncertain (no upstream detections and multiple Haringvliet sluices detections: these were not used for further analysis). The detection station installed in 2014, located 50 m behind the Haringvliet sluices, showed no inconsistencies, and all fish passage times were classified as ‘(1) fish entry exact timing’ (including fish with no upstream detections).

Seasonality in Haringvliet sluices passage (step 3 in Fig. 3)

To assess seasonality, fish detections with exact and approximate timing were plotted to compare monthly passage timing alongside water temperature.

Passage entry paths and conditions (step 4 in Fig. 3)

Only fish with exact timing were used for this analysis due to rapidly changing discharge conditions. We compared time from release to successful sluice passage, identified passage timing by hour of the day and daylight period (dawn, daylight, dusk, night), assessed wind speed and direction effects on passage, and identified passage between the two entry paths (discharging vs. fish locks during no discharging).

Passage conditions during discharge sluices (step 5 in Fig. 3)

To analyse passage conditions during sluice discharge, we used only fish with exact entry times. Passage timing was compared to water level differences, flow velocity, relative time open, total gate opening, number of gates open, and gate height.

To assess differences in passage conditions among species (steps 3–5 in Fig. 3), we performed Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm method. All analyses were conducted in R with the rstatix package [48].

Potential passage windows (step 6 in Fig. 3)

To estimate how many potential passage windows were available to fish we identified the range of environmental and sluice management conditions under which successful passage occurred. For each species, we identified the ranges of these conditions during successful passage by selecting the minimum and maximum values. Because fish passage took place at the start and the end of discharge periods (relative time open) we used minimum and maximum values for both time slots. We then assessed how often the combined set of conditions occurred across 2002–2018 for each species. We calculated the loss of potential migration windows (expressed as % of time) by stepwise application of the variable filters, starting with the seasonal variables and ending with the sluice management variables.

Under natural estuarine conditions, we assumed 100% of the migration windows could be used as long as species-specific seasonal circumstances (specific month and temperature) were met. We then compared these natural migration windows to the current situation at the sluices and estimated the stepwise reduction of potential passage windows per species, distinguishing between passage during discharge via sluices or fish locks and during no discharge via fish locks [42, 43].

Results

During 1996–2018, of the 1,751 anadromous adult fish we selected from the dataset, 516 (29%) fish successfully entered the Rhine-Meuse delta or further north. The proportion of fish entering the rivers varied slightly among species: sea trout (31%), sea lamprey (29%), and salmon (24%).

Attraction of river discharge for upstream migration (step 1 in Fig. 3)

Despite frequently occurring lower discharges, most fish were caught and released during discharge classes > 1750 m³·s⁻¹, i.e. 214 salmon (92%), 1222 sea trout (92%), and few (8%) during lower discharges, although the latter occurred frequently (Fig. 4). 138 (72%) sea lamprey were released during discharge classes over 1750 m³·s⁻¹ and 54 (28%) below. Detection rates in the rivers varied by discharge class: salmon (9–57%), sea trout (20–58%), and sea lamprey (19–75%) were detected mostly when released at higher discharges (1750–4500 m³·s).

Only for sea lamprey a clear positive relationship between river discharge at release and the probability of river entry was found (χ²(1) = 22.37, p < 0.001) (Supplementary materials, Fig. S2). Higher discharge slightly increased detection odds (odds ratio = 1.001, 95% CI [1.0006, 1.0015]), resulting in more fish detected when released during higher river discharge. For sea trout, a marginal negative effect was found (χ²(1) = 6.45, p < 0.05), with higher discharge slightly decreasing detection odds (odds ratio = 0.9998, 95% CI [0.9997, 0.9999]). No significant effect of discharge was detected for salmon.

River entry routes for upstream migration (step 2 in Fig. 3)

Entry routes could be determined for 392 (76%) of the 516 fish with river detections. 32 (58%) salmon and 36 (65%) sea lamprey entered the Rhine-Meuse delta via Haringvliet by passing the sluices complex or the ship lock (Fig. 5), while 11 (20%) salmon and 10 (18%) sea lamprey entered via the Port of Rotterdam. More sea trout entered via Rotterdam (40%, N = 161) than via Haringvliet (34%, N = 138); three sea trout (1%) did not enter the Rhine-Meuse delta and were detected at entry points further north (via the IJssel River branch or North Sea Canal). Fish mostly entered the Haringvliet via the Haringvliet sluices (N = 166) and fewer via the ship lock (N = 27). We detected several individuals over two consecutive years within the Rhine–Meuse delta, but only for sea trout.

Ten (83%) salmon, 44 (87%) sea trout and 34 (100%) sea lamprey passed the Haringvliet sluices during spring (Mar–May) and early summer (Jun–Aug) (Fig. 6A). An additional six sea trout and two salmon passed in autumn (Sep–Nov) or winter (Dec–Feb). Passage entry paths and conditions (step 4 in Fig. 3)

Most fish (82%, N = 56) passed the Haringvliet sluices within 30 days of release, although six sea trout and one salmon took over 100 days (Fig. 6B). Salmon showed the shortest time between release and sluice passage (median = 0.19 days), with 63% (N = 5) passing within seven hours. Sea trout showed greater variation and longer delays, ranging from 1 to 489 days (median = 17.4 days). Most sea lamprey (82%, N = 27) passed within 10 days, and all within 30 days (median = 1.08 days). A Kruskal–Wallis test showed a significant difference in time between release and Haringvliet sluices passage among species (χ²(2) = 26.4, p < 0.0001). Dunn’s post hoc tests showed sea trout took longer than salmon (p < 0.001) and sea lamprey (p < 0.0001), while salmon and sea lamprey did not differ significantly (p = 0.51).

All eight salmon and 26 of the 27 sea trout passed the Haringvliet sluices during day hours, mostly in the afternoon (Fig. 7A, Supplementary materials, Fig. S3A). Sea trout showed slightly more variation, with a single individual passing at night or early morning. Sea lamprey showed no clear preference for daylight or night passage (Fig. 7A). Salmon (100%) and sea trout (96%) only passed the Haringvliet sluices during daylight, except for one sea trout. In contrast, salmon and sea trout moving via the Port of Rotterdam showed no preference for daylight river entry, with 67% and 42% respectively first detected at night or twilight (Supplementary materials, Fig. 3B).

Sea trout and sea lamprey showed no relationship with wind direction during Haringvliet sluices passage (Supplementary materials, Fig. S5A). In contrast, all salmon passed during winds with a western component. A significant difference between salmon and the other two species was found in correlation with the east-west component of wind direction (sine) (Kruskal–Wallis χ²(2) = 6.77, p = 0.034; Dunn’s post hoc test: p < 0.05) (Supplementary materials, Fig. S5B). No relationship was found with the north–south wind component (cosine), or wind speed (Supplementary materials, Fig. S5C).

All three species mainly passed the Haringvliet during sluice discharge, although they were also able to pass during no discharge via the fish locks (Fig. 7B). Salmon (88%, N = 7) and sea trout (96%, N = 26) predominantly passed during discharge, with one individual per species passing during no discharge via the fish locks. Sea lamprey also mainly passed during discharge (73%, N = 24), but a larger proportion (27%, N = 9) used the fish locks during no discharge, typically at high tide with flow directed into Haringvliet.

Passage during discharging sluices (step 5 in Fig. 3)

Fish predominantly passed the sluices at the end of discharge periods (72%, N = 41; relative time open ≥ 0.8) and fewer at the start (21%, N = 12; ≤ 0.2) (Fig. 8 fish passage). Salmon (86%, N = 6) mainly passed at the end. Sea trout showed more variation: 20 (77%) passed at the end, three (12%) at the start, and three during peak flow. Sea lamprey passed mostly at the end (63%, N = 15) or at the start (33%, N = 8). Flow velocities through the sluices were lowest at the start and end of discharge periods, and highest in the middle (Fig. 8 flow velocity).

Most fish (84%, N = 48) passed at sluice flow velocities below 2.5 m·s⁻¹ or under inland directed ‘negative’ velocities (i.e., flow into Haringvliet), which is corresponding to the start and end of discharge periods and to small water level differences (< 50 cm) between sea and Haringvliet (Fig. 8; Supplementary materials, Fig. S6). Mean passage velocities did not differ significantly among species (Kruskal–Wallis χ²(2) = 5.31, p = 0.07). However, seven sea trout and two sea lamprey passed at higher velocities (> 2.5 m·s− 1), reaching up to 4.6 and 4.2 m·s− 1, respectively (Supplementary materials, Fig. S6).

Potential passage windows (step 6 in Fig. 3)

Potential passage windows (% of time that fish potentially could use for passage), calculated per 10-minute interval, were substantially reduced for all species compared to the assumed natural migration windows due to constraints at the Haringvliet sluices (Table 3). During discharge, fish could pass either via the sluices or the fish locks: sea trout and sea lamprey could potentially use 18% and 16% of their seasonal windows, respectively, while salmon could potentially use only 4%. When no discharge occurred, passage was only possible via the fish locks. In this case, sea lamprey had relatively more potential passage windows (36%), mainly because the fish locks remained open throughout high tide in March–May (for glass eel migration) and salmon and sea trout could potentially use 6% and 10%, respectively.

Discussion

This study aimed (1) to describe variation in upstream passage conditions at the Haringvliet estuarine sluices among three anadromous fish species before adjusted management, and (2) to identify factors affecting passage and reducing potential passage windows. We showed that estuarine sluices act as a migration barrier for anadromous fish. Passage conditions varied between species, but for all, passage during discharge via sluices or fish locks mainly occurred at the end of discharge periods when flow velocities were lower. Overall, the potential passage windows were reduced compared to the assumed natural estuarine situation. Below we first discuss our findings on the importance of river discharge, followed by species-specific passage conditions. We then conclude with recommendations for adjusted management.

Effect of river discharge on anadromous fish migrating upstream

The importance of river discharge for upstream migration for salmon and sea trout is indicated by low numbers caught (8%) and released during periods when discharge is low (< 1750 m³·s− 1). Under low-discharge conditions, salmon and sea trout may fail to locate the river entrance, lack the migratory cue to enter, or are attracted by the larger proportion of discharge via the Port of Rotterdam (Fig. 4A). This might be the reason we did not find an increase in the river entry probability with discharge volume at higher discharges (Supplementary materials, Fig. S2). Direct selection of the Port of Rotterdam as river entry route could not be evaluated in this study, as no fish were tagged there. The study from Bij de Vaate et al. [21] also indicated the importance of river discharge for sea trout for their choice of inland entry route. In contrast, sea lamprey were caught and released more often during lower river discharge (28%) than the two salmonid species. Pheromone attraction excreted by their larvae is an additional cue for inland migration and the selection of suitable spawning habitat, as this species spawns in suitable rivers rather than returning to natal sites [49–51]. In addition, sea lamprey has a more confined spring migration period [52], which we expect comes with a higher motivation to migrate also when discharges are low. Yet, their river entry probability increased with river discharge, probably due to a combination of higher attraction and better conditions to pass the sluices.

Estuarine sluices are a migration barrier

The proportion of tagged fish detected in the rivers was low for all species (on average 29%). This low detection rate could be explained by several causes. First, most fish were probably not able to enter through the Haringvliet sluices and turned around to the North Sea to look for other inland entry points. The discontinuity of flow attraction due to the interrupted discharging scheme might demotivate fish to stay in the area, which is also described by Griffioen et al. [5]. Second, not all fish caught at the seaside of the Haringvliet sluices were necessarily motivated to enter the Rhine-Meuse delta. Some individuals may have originated from other nearby river systems and therefore had little or less motivation to migrate upstream into the Rhine-Meuse, or others displayed opportunistic behaviour or foraging in the area. For instance, sea trout often shows foraging behaviour in coastal areas and river mouths [21, 53], indicating another dispersal motivation than an upstream migration to their spawning grounds. This is in line with our results that showed a wider range in sluice passage throughout the year, longer times between release and passage and a larger proportion (40%) of sea trout entering inland waters via the Port of Rotterdam. In contrast, a higher proportion of salmon (58%) and sea lamprey (65%) entered via the Haringvliet sluices after release. As salmon and sea lamprey are known to predominantly move upstream into river basins for reproductive migration [50, 54], their motivation for inland migration can be assumed to be higher than that of sea trout.

Third, fish could be predated during attempted sluice passage or high predator presence caused a more rapid disappearance of fish from the area [5]. Predation pressure can be high at barriers, and large predators, like harbour seals (Phoca vitulina) and grey seals (Halichoerus grypus), have shown a strong population increase in recent decades in the Netherlands [55]. In addition, both seal species are often recorded at the seaside of the Haringvliet sluices and are also found to prey on adult salmonids [56]. Fourth, some individuals might have died during attempted sluice passage or died due to the effects of tagging [57]. Last, turbulence on the seaside of the sluices could affect fish swimming and reduce their chances to pass the barrier, e.g. causing problems in maintaining orientation [21, 58] or higher chances being predated.

Our results show that a substantial proportion of fish (salmon 20%, sea trout 40% and sea lamprey 18%) took an extended route via the Port of Rotterdam, although caught and released in front of the Haringvliet sluices, indicating both their motivation to migrate inland and a barrier effect of these sluices. The stepwise reduction in potential passage windows also indicates a barrier effect of the Haringvliet sluices (Table 3). During discharge of the sluices, high flow velocities limit opportunities for fish passage. Although fish also have opportunities to pass via the fish locks when the sluices are not discharging, such passages were rarely observed (Fig. 7B), suggesting that the reduction in potential passage windows is even larger than indicated in Table 3. We are not sure about the exact decrease of potential passage windows for each species, as we cannot reconstruct the situation before construction of the Haringvliet barrier, it is clear that they are significantly reduced for all species. The potential passage windows were calculated per 10-minute interval and this influences the interpretation of the calculated reduction. The importance of this reduction in potential passage windows depends on the actual delay that fish experience. Waiting for feasible passage conditions could potentially be uncomplicated for certain species, although delays of days or weeks could impact fitness and repeated failed passage attempts could lead to exhaustion [13]. However, there is not much knowledge how delayed migration impacts individuals and population viability [3]. Fish could be more vulnerable to predation when waiting at the sluices or searching for an entrance [3, 12].

Passage opportunities within discharge periods

All three anadromous species passed the estuarine sluices during discharge more frequently (salmon 88%, sea trout 96% and sea lamprey 73%) than during no discharge when fish locks were open. Fish passing the sluices during discharge occurred primarily at the end of discharge periods (72%; relative time open ≥ 0.8) when water velocities diminished due to smaller water level differences with the approaching high tide. However, low velocities also occurred at the start of discharge periods, yet these were used less frequently (21%; ≤ 0.2). A potential explanation for this is the limited freshwater attraction at the beginning of discharge periods. As freshwater attraction flow increases during discharge periods, water velocities rise, reducing suitability for fish passage; velocities then decrease toward the end of the discharge period, improving passage conditions. The importance of freshwater attraction to migrate into rivers [59, 60] and to attract these fish to find passage entrances and overcome barriers is known for salmonids [61].

Apparently, most fish passed at lower flow velocities through the sluice gates (84% of all species at velocities < 2.5 m·s− 1) when the differences in water levels between sea and Haringvliet were low (< 50 cm). Notably, these low velocities occur less than higher velocities within a discharge period (Fig. 8, flow velocity presented by dash-dot axis lines). A study by Griffioen et al. [5] observed a similar passage pattern at an estuarine barrier, where sea lamprey were passing only during low velocities (< 2 m·s− 1), which also occurred at the beginning and end of discharge periods.

Species-specific passage conditions

Although flow velocity is a common passage limitation for anadromous fishes, our results also emphasise the variation in passage conditions among species. Despite the relatively low number of individuals, salmon appeared most selective in their passage conditions. Compared to the other species, more salmon passed at the end of discharge periods and with a smaller range of flow velocities. Salmon are exceptionally good swimmers, and field studies have documented burst swimming speeds up to 8 m·s− 1 [62]. However, sprinting is an unsustainable, physiologically exhausting swimming mode that quickly leads to fatigue and more severe exhaustion, and salmon are demonstrated to be maximizing their overall swimming efficiency on route to their spawning grounds [62]. This might further explain why salmon after being attracted to the sluice openings during discharging may be hesitant to directly attempt surpassing when confronted with high flow conditions. In addition, it is remarkable that salmon only passed the sluices during westerly wind conditions, but is unclear to us what the cause might be.

Unlike salmonids, sea lamprey passed the sluices at calculated flow velocities through the sluices that would exceed their expected swimming capacity, with a maximum of 4.2 m·s⁻¹. Keefer et al. [63] reported a sprint capacity of only 2.7 m·s− 1 for pacific lamprey (Entosphenus tridentatus). Passage through the fish locks during discharge is also feasible, as flow velocities there are strongly reduced, ranging between 0.3 and 0.5 m·s− 1 depending on the height of the partially opened lock (15–25 cm) (pers. com. R. Kroes, ATKB; S. de Goederen Rijkswaterstaat). Therefore, sea lamprey could be expected to use the fish locks as well as the sluice gates during discharge. However, the clustering of successful passages at the start and especially at the end of discharge periods, corresponding to the lowest velocities available, suggests that most passed through the sluice gates rather than the fish locks. Unlike sea lamprey, sea trout exhibit strong swimming capacity [64], and individuals passing during peak discharge, when flow velocities were high, may have used the fish locks rather than the sluice gates.

The confined passage period (April–May) for sea lamprey makes the species more susceptible for delays, which likely increases its motivation to pass the Haringvliet sluices, leading to increased passage via the fish locks during no discharge. This confined migration period coincides with the period of glass eel migration. To facilitate glass eels the fish locks are functioning 24/7 in March–May providing more opportunities to pass via the fish locks. In contrast, when the sluices were not discharging only one salmon and one sea trout passed the sluices via the fish locks. The minor freshwater flow via the fish locks may be insufficient to attract salmonids, and these dark, confined spaces can discourage passage, as hardly any salmonid was observed to pass during nighttime. We found that salmon and sea trout predominantly passed the sluices during daylight, whereas no daylight preference was observed at the open connection of the Port of Rotterdam. Keefer et al. [20] indicated more daylight activity of salmonids when confronted with challenging conditions, suggesting salmonids prefer also using eyesight when attempting to overcome these. In contrast, sea lamprey showed no diel preference for passage, despite several studies describing the nocturnal activity of lampreys during migration and at challenging conditions [20, 52].

Although we cannot distinguish whether fish passed during discharge via a sluice gate or fish lock, the uneven distribution of passage timing towards the start and especially the end of discharge periods suggests passage through the sluice gates when flow velocities are lower, rather than through the fish locks, where flow velocities remain favourable throughout the entire discharge period. Moreover, given their preference for daylight passage, sea trout and salmon are unlikely to use the always dark fish locks. For sea lamprey, the occurrence of passage at flow velocities seemingly too high for them suggests that some individuals may have used the fish locks, given that they are willing to pass under dark conditions. However, if all had done so, a more evenly distribution of passages over discharge periods would be expected. We therefore conclude that passage primarily took place via the sluice gates during discharge, where sparse additional passage through the fish locks may have occurred for sea lamprey.

We also observed differences between sea trout, salmon, and sea lamprey in the time between release and sluice passage. Sea trout showed significantly more time between release and passage (median = 17.4 days) and a broader seasonal range during 9 months of the year (Feb-Aug and Nov-Dec), whereas salmon and sea lamprey passed within hours or days after release (median = 0.19 and 1.08 days, respectively). This difference may be due to sea trout not explicitly arriving at the sluices for upstream spawning migration but also for foraging. Sea trout are known to remain nearshore for extended periods, although larger individuals may venture into offshore waters [53].

Adjusted sluice management can assist upstream passage

Our long-term study indicates limited upstream passage opportunities for anadromous fish at the Haringvliet estuarine barrier. Fish passage is restricted by the high flow velocities during the greater part of the time during sluice discharge. Haringvliet’s dual function, providing flood protection and preventing saltwater intrusion as it is a freshwater reservoir, prevents the permanent or complete opening of the estuarine sluices. In addition to passage success, also delay and energy spending influences successful spawning migration, as Burnett et al. [65] showed that altering flow management at a dam had beneficial effects on sockeye salmon post passage recovery and mortality. Management adjustments that prolong feasible passage conditions can provide more potential passage windows for anadromous species, especially during discharge of the sluices, which all species preferred for passage. Prolonged sluice opening during low flow velocities (< 2.5 m·s⁻¹) and small water level differences (< 50 cm) in either flow direction will facilitate fish passage better. Salmon and sea trout are expected to benefit most from extended openings directly after discharge periods, particularly during daylight in spring and summer. This could potentially double the duration of potential passage windows, although it remains unclear to what extent fish utilize these windows when flow is directed into Haringvliet. In addition to that, we also expect specifically sea lamprey to benefit from early sluice opening before the start of discharge periods, specifically during their migration period in spring at day and night. The potential to adjust this, largely depends on the acceptable amount of saltwater intrusion during inflow into Haringvliet, which is lower when water level differences are small. Passage during no discharge via the fish locks, which sea lamprey were observed to do, should be further investigated during discharge periods to identify their potential role for salmonids and other diadromous species.

Our study showed the importance of long-term data collection prior to adjusted management of these estuarine sluices. These results will be useful to determine the success of adjusted management in the future and to improve upstream migration at estuarine barriers. Continued and progressed research on fish passage attempts and passage behaviour are required to understand if these management adjustments are impactful to contribute to rebuilding their populations.

Conclusions

This study represents the first long-term assessment of upstream passage at an estuarine barrier with varying passage conditions and infrequent windows for successful entrance of adult anadromous salmon, sea trout, and sea lamprey. Our results show that the Haringvliet sluices are a substantial migration barrier for these species. Only a small proportion (29%) of fish released seawards of the sluices entered into the rivers, and 18–40% entered via an alternative route (Port of Rotterdam). This study also indicates the importance of river discharge to attract anadromous fish to migrate upstream, and fish predominantly passed the sluices at the end of discharge periods (72%). Our findings underline species-specific differences in passage conditions and migration motivation, emphasising the need for tailored considerations. Salmon and sea trout passed the sluices limited to daylight hours, contrary to sea lamprey which passed both day and night and via the fish locks when there was no discharge. Sea trout took, on average, more time between release and successful passage and showed a wider range of conditions, while sea lamprey had a limited seasonal range, and salmon appeared to be more selective in their passage conditions. However, windows with low flow velocities through the sluice gates were essential for passage. By linking conditions for successful fish passage with their long-term occurrence, this study provides valuable insights into the species-specific consequences of estuarine barriers. Understanding fish passage possibilities prior to management changes is crucial for improving connectivity at (estuarine) barriers with sluices.
