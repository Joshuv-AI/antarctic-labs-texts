Clinical effects of different types of flaps selected according to local conditions in the treatment of diabetic foot defects
Authors: Yan Yang, Zhe-ming Cao, Nian-zhe Sun, Li-ming Qing, Pan-feng Wu, Ju-yu Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe repair of diabetic foot defects (DFD) is a major challenge in clinical practice. The purpose of this study was to investigate the difference in clinical efficacy of different flap techniques in repairing DFD wounds, and to compare the difference in clinical efficacy of anterolateral thigh perforator flap (ALTP) in repairing DFD and non-DFD.MethodsThis study is a retrospective clinical study of different types of flap reconstruction in patients with DFD admitted to our hospital from January 2010 to December 2021. A total of 40 patients with DFD and 43 patients with non-DFD were included in this study. Detailed preoperative basic information, intraoperative details, postoperative complications and long-term follow-up results were collected.ResultThe comorbidities, wound infection and wound duration of DFD group were more serious than those of non-DFD group. In addition, the incidence of complications was higher in DFD group, the wound healing time was longer, the aesthetic evaluation, the functional recovery rate of ankle joint and the sensory recovery effect of flaps were worse.ConclusionIn this study, it is concluded that different flap techniques can obtain better clinical efficacy in repairing DFD wounds. Compared with non-DFD wounds, the postoperative risk of DFD wounds using free ALTP flaps is higher, but the risk can be reduced by reasonable preoperative vascular examination. Free flap can deal with various irregular wounds and provide more options for clinical repair.Level of evidenceIII, Case–control study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-024-05122-y.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer is a severe complication of diabetes, and it is estimated that by 2045, there will be 629 million diabetes patients worldwide, with 25% developing diabetic foot ulcers [1]. Clinically, diabetic foot ulcers are considered a complex wound primarily caused by diabetic distal lower extremity neuropathy and/or vascular disease, resulting in extensive skin loss, aggressive infections, and impaired tissue viability [2]. They typically occur in weight-bearing areas of the foot, with large size, significant depth, and prolonged duration, posing significant challenges for clinical treatment and repair. The difficulty in healing diabetic foot ulcers is attributed to varying degrees of peripheral neuropathy and vascular compromise in the lower limbs, exacerbated by sustained hyperglycemia.

Currently, a multidisciplinary approach is commonly employed in clinical management [3], utilizing various treatment modalities including conservative methods such as hyperbaric oxygen therapy [4], electrical stimulation [5], advanced dressings [6], and biotherapy [7]. However, when diabetic foot ulcers involve critical structures (e.g., bones, tendons, blood vessels, and nerves) with Wagner grade ≥ 2 [8], conservative treatments often fail to promote successful wound healing. Requirements for modern management of diabetic foot include early diagnosis and timely combination of anti-infective drugs and surgery, and it is believed that the combination of antibiotics and surgery appears to be more effective than either treatment alone [9, 10]. Surgical intervention is important for long-term ulcer relief, mechanical stability and coordination of the foot [11]. In recent years, with advancements in microsurgical techniques and precise patient selection, flap reconstruction has shown higher success rates in repairing DFD [12], reducing the amputation and mortality rates in diabetic foot ulcer [13, 14]. Due to the vascular disease of lower extremity in diabetic foot ulcer, pedicled flap has become the first choice in primary medical institutions. However, pedicled flaps cannot be repaired for wounds with large defect area and distal extremity, and free flaps are also the appropriate choice for limb preservation, but the prerequisite is to perform corresponding hemodynamic evaluation. At present, there are many flap techniques for repairing diabetic foot ulcer, but the clinical studies reported are limited to small sample sizes and lack comprehensiveness. Which treatment method has the best clinical effect is still a clinical problem to be solved.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore the clinical efficacy of selecting suitable flap types for wound repair of diabetic foot ulcer according to the characteristics of defect site and vascular conditions, including anterolateral thigh perforator (ALTP) flap, medial sural neurotrophic (MSN) flap, posterior tibial artery perforator propeller (PTAP) flap (PTAP), medial plantar (MP) flap, and keystone flap (Table 1). Patients and methods

This study is a retrospective clinical research on different types of flap reconstruction in patients with diabetic foot admitted to our hospital from January 2010 to December 2021. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria for DFD patients and non-DFD patients are shown in Fig. 1. A total of 40 DFD and 43 non-DFD were included in this study. All surgeries were performed by the same surgeon and team. This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University.

Surgical technique

During debridement, thorough removal of necrotic tissue within the wound and opening of any abscesses are performed, ensuring adequate drainage. The debridement extends to the healthy soft tissues and bone at the edges of the wound. After the wound infection and blood glucose control were satisfied, the appropriate flap technique was selected for the corresponding repair.

The flaps used in this study included anterolateral thigh perforator (ALTP) flap (Fig. 2, Supplement materials Figs. 1, 2 and 3), posterior tibial artery perforator propeller (PTAP) flap (Fig. 3), medial sural neurotrophic (MSN) flap (Supplement materials Fig. 4), medial plantar (MP) flap (Supplement materials Fig. 5), and keystone flap (Supplement materials Fig. 6).

Anterolateral thigh perforator flap

The thickness and sectionable width of the flap were measured by “lifting pinch test” before operation. Hand held Doppler (HHD) or color Doppler ultrasound (CDU) were used to locate and mark the first perforating branch of D-LCFA (Descending branch of lateral circumflex artery) near the midpoint of the iliac patellar line and the second perforating branch located near the distal(near)side to penetrate the body surface of fascia lata. The flap was harvested by orderly retrograde four-side dissection. For flaps that need sensory reconstruction in the weight-bearing area, the nerve in the flap was anastomosed with the Medial plantar nerve (MPN) or Sural nerve (SN) or Medial cutaneous nerve dorsalis pedis (MCNDP) in the recipient area. The wound at the donor site can be directly closed after complete hemostasis and reliable drainage. The traditional perforator flap has great limitations, and many special and complex wounds cannot be repaired by traditional perforator flaps. In addition to the traditional form of perforator flap, in this study we used two special forms of anterolateral thigh perforator flap to repair wounds, including polyfoliate perforator flap and chimeric perforator flap. The surgical methods have been described in detail in our team’s previous studies [15].

Medial sural neurotrophic flap

Design the flap based on the size and shape of the wound, with the axis of the flap located at the midpoint between the popliteal fossa and the midpoint of a line connecting the Achilles tendon to the lateral malleolus. The rotation point is the perforator detected by preoperative Doppler ultrasound, which is the lowest muscle interstice perforator of the peroneal artery 5–7 cm above the lateral malleolus and provides blood supply for the flap. The size of the donor area is designed according to the size of the recipient area, with the donor area being approximately 20% larger than the recipient area. The skin, subcutaneous tissue, and deep fascia are sequentially incised, and the sural nerve and small saphenous vein are severed and included in the flap. The pedicle is greater than 3 cm, and the rotated flap covers the wound, with the donor area directly sutured or grafted.

Posterior tibial artery perforator propeller flap

The rotation point is generally selected to be the thick perforating branch close to the wound edge, and the length of the perforating branch should meet the requirements of flap transposion. The axis is from the midpoint of the connection between the Achilles tendon and the medial malleolar posterior margin to the line of the medial condyle of the tibia. It is divided into big paddle and small paddle, the big paddle is used to cover the wound and the part of the original small paddle, and the small paddle is used to cover the donor area. The flap was cut using the “combination method” and separated under the deep fascia. At the same time, attention should be paid to observe whether the perforating branch is stuck after the flap rotation. If the entrapment is found, the length of the separated perforating branch should be increased as much as possible [16]. A more detailed introduction to surgical methods was described in our team’s previous articles [17].

Medial plantar flap

Use the surface projection line of the medial plantar artery as the axis of the flap. The distal end of the flap should not extend beyond the metatarsal head in the weight-bearing area of the foot, while the medial border is the medial edge of the sole and the lateral border is the centerline of the sole. Within this range, design an appropriate flap based on the size of the defect. Preoperatively, detect the vessels with a Doppler ultrasound and incise the skin below malleolar canal along the surface projection line of the posterior tibial artery and medial plantar artery. Dissect the posterior tibial artery and vein, make a distal incision for the flap near the metatarsal head, and then cut the skin and plantar fascia. The medial plantar arterio-vein and nerve are found in the gap between abductor pollicis muscle and flexor digitorum brevis, and the free flap is lifted retrograde to the beginning of the medial plantar blood vessel to form a medial plantar island flap with medial plantar arterio-vein pedicle. Damage to the cutaneous artery should be avoided when the flap is drawn.

Keystone flap

Preoperatively locate the position of the perforating blood vessel and select a relaxed soft tissue area around the wound to design a keystone flap, with the apex of the flap forming a 90° angle with the wound edge, and the width of the flap being equal to that of the wound. Incise the skin along the design line and bluntly dissect the surrounding tissue to obtain the flap. For smaller wounds, directly push the flap over the wound. If the wound is larger, the lateral deep fascia should be divided and released to increase the distance the flap can be pushed. Suture the incision without tension, leave a drain in place, and apply sterile dressings to the surgical site. If the donor area cannot be closed, proceed with a skin grafting procedure.

Evaluation of outcomes

The intraoperative and postoperative conditions of the two groups (DFD and non-DFD group ) were observed, and the aesthetic appearance of the donor area and recipient area of the patients was evaluated subjectively and objectively during the follow-up. The AOFAS (American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society) scoring system was used to evaluate ankle joint function; Semmes-Weinstein monofilament was used to evaluate changes in sensory recovery in DFD and non-DFD group [18, 19]. To observe the postoperative complication rate, final functional outcome and sensory recovery of patients with diabetic foot treated with different types of flaps.

Statistic analysis

SPSS Version 21.0 and GraphPad Prism Version 5.0 were used for statistical analysis and plotting graphs, respectively. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using two independent sample T-tests. Qualitative data were expressed as numbers or percentages and compared using χ2 test and Fisher exact test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

DFD group and non-DFD group outcomes

In this study, the clinical efficacy of foot soft tissue repair with free ALTP flap was compared between DFD group and non-DFD group. A total of 74 patients met the inclusion requirements, including 31 patients with diabetic foot and 43 patients without diabetes. The comparison of basic data (age, gender, defect site etc.) between the two groups showed no statistical difference, indicating that the two groups were relatively homogenous and comparable. The comorbidities and wound infection of DFD group were significantly more serious than non-DFD group, and the duration of wound disease was significantly longer (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

The comparison of the intraoperative conditions between the two groups showed that there were no statistical differences in the area of flap incision, blood loss, the time of flap acquisition, and the operation time. The incidence of complications in donor and recipient areas was analyzed during short-term follow-up. In the DFD group, 2 cases had complete flap necrosis due to vascular crisis (wound healing after reoperation with ipsilateral ALTP flap); 4 cases of partial flap necrosis due to infection and hematoma (healed with antibiotic treatment and dressing change). In the non-diabetic group, there was only 1 case of partial necrosis due to vascular crisis (the vascular crisis was successfully explored and healed after dressing change). The results showed that the complication rate of flap in DFD group was significantly higher than that in non-DFD group with statistical difference (P = 0.019). Compared with non-DFD group, the wound healing time of DFD group was significantly longer and had statistical difference (P < 0.001). In the overall aesthetic evaluation, the subjective and objective evaluation showed that the appearance of the donor and recipient areas in the non-DFD group was significantly better than that in the DFD group (P < 0.05). AOFAS evaluation was performed 12 months after surgery in the two groups, and the results showed that the recovery rate of ankle joint function in non-DFD group was significantly better than that in DFD group, with statistical difference (P = 0.003) (Table 3). S-W monofilament evaluation results showed that the sensory recovery effect of the flap in the non-DFD foot group was better than that in the DFD group during follow-up, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05, Fig. 4A).

Results of repairing diabetic foot defect with different types of flap

In this study, a total of 40 diabetic foot salvage patients were included, including 34 ALTP flaps, 3 MSN flaps, 2 PTAP flaps, 2 MP flaps and 2 Keystone flaps. Due to vascular lesions in diabetic, the resection area of free flaps was significantly larger than that of other pedicled flaps. It inevitably increases the risk of complications. In the ALTP flap group, 2 patients were completely necrotic due to vascular crisis, and the wounds healed after reoperation; Some of the 4 patients with flaps caused by infection and hematoma healed after dressing change. No complications occurred in the other groups. Ankle joint function evaluation 12 months after surgery showed that 12 patients had excellent walking ability, 16 patients had good walking ability, 8 patients had fair walking ability, and 3 patients had poor walking ability. Among the 31 patients who underwent defect repair with free ALTP flap, 15 cases of nerve anastomosis due to repair of weight-bearing area. The results of S-W monofilament evaluation showed that the sensory recovery of free ALTP group was significantly worse than that of other groups. (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B).

Discussion

DFD are a common chronic complication of diabetes. Peripheral neuropathy, vascular disease, and local wound infection are the main factors contributing to the development of foot ulcers. Poor wound healing can also lead to chronic wounds, which in severe cases may result in amputation. In recent years, with the advancement and widespread use of microsurgical techniques, the success rate of flap repair has been constantly increasing. It is widely recognized in the process of diabetic foot ulcer repair for its strong anti-infection capability, good elasticity, and shortened wound healing time [20]. However, there is currently no unified standard for choosing flap repair methods. For diabetic foot ulcers, each flap repair method has its advantages and disadvantages. We should follow the principle of achieving the best limb function and appearance at the least cost, and customize the repair plan accordingly. In formulating preoperative plans, many factors should be taken into account, such as the size and depth of the wound, the vascular and neural conditions of the affected limb, and the requirements of sensory and motor functions for wound repair.

Patients with diabetic foot may have other comorbidities at the same time, and coupled with the aging factor, patients often have vascular diseases in the lower extremities. As a result, free flap has become a relative contraindication, and most research centers choose pedicled flap for repair. In this study, we believe that the free ALTP flap can be used as the first choice to determine the availability of recipient vessels in the lower extremities by preoperative color Doppler ultrasonography or angiography. Compared with non-diabetic patients, diabetic foot patients had higher postoperative complications (due to relatively poor vascular conditions), longer wound healing time (due to poor blood sugar control and infection factors), and poor appearance satisfaction (toe amputation due to distal toe infection and necrosis, and poor skin texture due to chronic lesions). Poor walking function (immobilization in patients due to long duration of the disease, and impaired movement in patients with toe amputation), poor sensory recovery in the flap (poor glycemic control affecting nerve regeneration rate). There have been many successful reports on the clinical application of anterolateral thigh perforator flap to repair foot and ankle injuries, and its vascular pedicle is relatively constant, the blood supply is abundant, the anti-infection ability is strong, and the influence on donor function is small [21]. However, for patients with diabetic foot ulceration, lower extremity vascular lesions are common, and the incidence of vascular crisis and flap necrosis is high when free skin flap is used to repair the wound. Preoperative Doppler ultrasound or CTA should be used to evaluate lower extremity vascular conditions and make surgical plans. If blood vessel occlusion is serious and vascular surgical treatment is ineffective, free skin flap transplantation should be abandoned. For diabetic foot ulcers with deep wounds, such as tendons and bones exposed, studies have shown that choosing anterolateral thigh perforator flap can not only cover the wounds, but also facilitate the treatment of osteomyelitis [22]. With the development of perforator flap techniques, special forms of procedures have emerged based on traditional methods to cater to different wound repair needs, becoming commonly used flaps in microsurgical wound reconstruction. For example, flow-through perforator flap not only covers the wound, but also increases blood flow to the distal limb [23]. For another example, chimeric perforator flap can cut tissue flap with different components according to the characteristics of the wound to repair the wide wound with dead cavity. The recovery of sensation in the weight-bearing area is an important concern in the surgical repair of diabetic foot ulcers. Several studies have shown [24–26] that the use of flaps with innervation in the weight-bearing area of the foot offers advantages such as improved appearance, functional recovery, and restoration of protective sensation. This enables patients to resume normal activities earlier. In this study, 15 cases included flaps carrying the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve or its branches, which were anastomosed with nerves near the wound. This reconstruction technique helped restore sensation in the weight-bearing area of the foot and reduced the occurrence of pressure ulcers and other complications.

In 1992, Masquelet conducted anatomical research on cutaneous neurotrophic vessels and surrounding skin of the lower leg, and successfully applied sural neurovascular flaps into clinical practice [27]. In 1991, Hyakusoku et al. proposed a “propeller flap”, based on a random subcutaneous pedicle, the length of the flap is much larger than its width, and the pedicle is the center, which is divided into two parts of the blade [28]. Pedicled perforator flap has the advantages of relatively simple anatomical structure, high survival rate, multiple blood supply sources, no damage to large vessels, and can cover large and medium-sized defects [29, 30]. However, due to the limited pedicle length, it is not suitable for repairing the distal foot wound, and the incision area of this flap is limited. At present, there are two main clinical applications: one is the retrograde repair of soft tissue defects of the leg, ankle and foot; The other is to use anterograde repair of soft tissue defects near the lower leg and around the knee joint [31, 32]. Gang Zhao et al. [33] reported 29 cases of anterograde repair, of which 26 cases survived and 3 cases of epidermal necrosis were healed after dressing change. Sensory flaps can be made in the load-bearing area of the foot if needed. PTAP flap can carry the saphenous nerve, and the sural nerve is carried by the MSN flap itself. The separation should avoid damaging the blood vessels around the nerve, so as to protect the skin sensation in the innervation area and ensure the blood supply of the flap as much as possible.

The skin of the plantar bearing area has characteristics like abrasion resistance, pressure resistance, and non-slip properties. When it becomes damaged, the requirements for repairing the skin flap are relatively high. In recent years, the medial plantar flap has been increasingly used for repairing soft tissue defects of the plantar [34, 35]. The medial plantar flap is relatively wear-resistant, with dense and elastic subcutaneous tissue. The deep plantar fascia of the flap can attach well to the heel bone, reducing the movement of the flap. It has good blood supply and nerve innervation, stable anatomical structure, a large pedicle, good flexibility, and a high survival rate after flap transplantation [36]. The donor site of the flap is located in the non-weight-bearing area of the sole, which has a relatively minimal impact on postoperative weight-bearing function of the foot. It can be used as a pedicled flap to transfer to the same-side defect or as a free flap to transfer to the opposite-side defect. However, the application of the medial plantar flap is also limited by the size of the defect and the severity of previous injuries [37]. When applying this flap, several points should be noted. Firstly, the flap should be harvested within the non-weight-bearing area of the sole, with a size of less than 6 cm. Secondly, full-thickness skin grafting should be used to close the donor site. Thirdly, consideration should be given to the postoperative appearance and function of the foot, minimizing damage to the donor site. The principles of flap repair should follow the biomechanical characteristics of the foot, utilizing plantar tissues to repair the weight-bearing area of the sole is the most ideal choice, beneficial for the restoration of weight-bearing function and sensation, and can reduce complications such as pressure ulcers.

In 2003, Behan first reported the Keystone Design Perforator Island Flap, which was named after its resemblance to the keystones of Roman arches [38]. The application of Keystone flap conforms to the principle of proximity in the repair of soft tissue defects and offers advantages such as simplicity in design and operation, reliable blood supply, similar texture to the recipient site’s skin, and preservation of sensory function of the flap. It is considered an effective method for soft tissue defect repair [39]. Pripotnev and White [40] applied keystone flap to repair 39 cases of lower leg wounds, and concluded that for lower limbs with higher tension, the flap width and defect width of 3:1 is conducive to wound repair. This flap utilizes the redistribution of lax soft tissues around the wound edges to cover the defect. Therefore, it is not suitable for use in cases where the defect size is too large, as it may result in excessive tension and subsequent flap necrosis.

The standard for repairing diabetic foot defects with flap transplantation should not only be based on flap survival and wound healing, but also on a comprehensive evaluation of the impact on the function and appearance of both the donor and recipient areas. The recognized basic principle of flap selection is to repair the main tissue with secondary tissue, starting with simple cases before complex ones, prioritizing proximity over distance, and focusing on the reconstruction of function and morphology in the recipient area while minimizing damage to the function and appearance of the donor area [41]. Surgeons should base their selection of flaps on anatomical structures, taking into account factors such as the location, size, depth, presence of infection, presence of dead spaces, recipient area function and appearance requirements, and the vascular condition of the affected limb. By incorporating principles of aesthetics, personalized flap designs can be created to achieve three-dimensional aesthetic repair of the wound.

Therefore, it is necessary to weigh the pros and cons and choose the best treatment plan for each patient. Our recommendations are as follows: ALTP flap is suitable for larger foot defects (> 10 cm) with good recipient artery or good hemodynamics after treatment. MSN flap is suitable for relatively large defects on the heel, lateral foot, and outer ankle skin (6–10 cm). PTAP flap is suitable for relatively large defects on the medial and posterior sides of the ankle joint, the medial side of the heel and the anterior medial side of the dorsalis(6–10 cm). If the defect on the heel or sole is smaller (< 5 cm), MP flap is the preferred flap for repair. The Keystone flap is suitable for repairing small to medium-sized foot defects and requires sufficient healthy and relaxed soft tissue around the wound. There are some limitations in this study, mainly due to the retrospective design and non-randomized selection of patients, which lead to some bias in the study results. In addition, the number of complete flap necrosis in this study was small, and the patients had complications, which made it difficult to identify the real cause of flap necrosis.

Conclusions

In summary, selecting the appropriate flap repair based on each patient’s specific condition is essential for effective treatment of DFD. Compared with non-DFD wounds, the postoperative risk of DFD wounds using free ALTP flaps is higher, but the risk can be reduced by reasonable preoperative vascular examination. Free flap can deal with various irregular wounds and provide more options for clinical repair.
