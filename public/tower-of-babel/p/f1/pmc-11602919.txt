Applying the Estimand Framework to Non‐Inferiority Trials
Authors: Helle Lynggaard, Oliver N Keene, Tobias Mütze, Sunita Rehal
Published: 2024

Introduction

Limited guidance is given in the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) E9(R1) addendum [1, 2] on how to construct estimands for trials with a non‐inferiority objective. Furthermore, since the release of the addendum, the literature on estimands in clinical trials has almost exclusively, either implicitly or explicitly, focused on estimands for superiority clinical trials.

Existing guidelines from ICH [3, 4], European Medicines Agency [EMA] [5, 6], U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA] [7] and National Medical Products Administration [NMPA] [8] provide details on how to plan, conduct, analyse and interpret results relating to a non‐inferiority objective. These guidelines all predate ICH E9(R1) and do not discuss the underlying treatment effect of interest. Therefore, it is unclear how sponsors should construct estimands for non‐inferiority objectives while meeting requirements in the existing guidance.

Licensing of a new drug includes evaluating if the benefits outweigh the risks. The gold standard for demonstrating efficacy of a test treatment is to conduct a superiority trial where the new test treatment is compared to a placebo or an active comparator (reference treatment), usually standard of care, which is expected to be less clinically effective compared to the test treatment. Efficacy of the test treatment is demonstrated if there is a statistically significant difference in favour of the test treatment versus the reference treatment and if the estimated treatment effect is clinically relevant. However, in many situations, a superiority trial cannot be conducted due to ethical or operational issues and instead non‐inferiority trials are conducted. Non‐inferiority trials pose challenges different from superiority trials.

Overall, there are two objectives related to non‐inferiority trials [6]: (1) to demonstrate efficacy of the test treatment by demonstrating indirect superiority of the test treatment versus placebo and (2) to demonstrate that there is no clinically relevant loss of efficacy when replacing the reference treatment with the test treatment. The second objective is often accompanied by an added benefit of the test treatment, for example, reduced costs, fewer adverse events and increased convenience. The most common reason for conducting a non‐inferiority trial is related to the second objective.

Within some therapeutic areas, non‐inferiority trials are frequently conducted. For example, non‐inferiority objectives are frequently seen in trials in Type 2 diabetes [9]. The purpose of most non‐inferiority trials within Type 2 diabetes is related to the second objective above.

It is commonly stated that the objective of a non‐inferiority trial is to demonstrate that a test treatment is ‘not inferior to’ or ‘at least as good as’ the reference treatment. In practice, it is only possible to demonstrate that the test treatment is not unacceptably worse than the reference treatment by more than a pre‐specified amount, Δ, called the non‐inferiority margin [6].

It is not sufficient to state that the objective of a trial is ‘to demonstrate non‐inferiority’, the precise non‐inferiority objective of the trial should be clearly specified in relation to the two objectives mentioned above. That is, it should be clear if the objective is to demonstrate indirect superiority of the test treatment versus placebo or if it is to demonstrate that the reference treatment can safely be replaced by the test treatment. For simplicity, in this paper, we shall use the terminology ‘to demonstrate non‐inferiority’ throughout the remaining paper.

The hypothesis to assess non‐inferiority differs from the hypothesis to assess superiority. For superiority trials, the aim is to reject the null hypothesis that the test treatment is equal to placebo. For non‐inferiority, the null hypothesis H0 is that the test treatment is worse than the reference treatment by more than Δ and the alternative hypothesis HA is that the test treatment is no worse than Δ. The assessment of non‐inferiority is determined by one bound of the confidence interval relative to the margin. For example, for a treatment where a beneficial effect corresponds to a smaller value, if the upper bound of the two‐sided 95% confidence interval (this is equivalent to a one‐sided 97.5% CI) is lower than the pre‐specified margin (Δ), then non‐inferiority has been demonstrated.

Details on different outcome scenarios and how they can be interpreted can be found in the paper by Piaggio et al. [10].

The EMA points‐to‐consider on switching between superiority and non‐inferiority [5] discusses the role of the two analysis sets, the full analysis set [FAS] based on the intent‐to‐treat [ITT] principle [3] and the per‐protocol analysis set [PPS]. Thus, the FAS would consist of all observed data from all randomised participants regardless of what events may occur after treatment initiation. Historically, such analysis was often described as ITT when it included all randomised participants, but excluded the data after an undesirable event happened, for example, initiation of rescue medication or treatment discontinuation [11]:

ITT analysis is often interpreted as referring only to including all randomised patients in the population analysed […].

In addition, the ICH E9(R1) training slides [2] mention this as an issue in the section ‘Concerns with current practice’:

ITT is mentioned in protocols, statistical analysis plans and trial reports, but only as a way of specifying, which patients are included in the analysis set, and not to reflect the principle that patients will be followed‐up regardless of ‘their compliance to the planned course of treatment’.

Note that including or excluding data after an undesirable event has occurred targets distinctly different estimands. In contrast, the PPS typically did not include all randomised participants but excluded participants depending on a set of criteria (e.g., protocol violation, prohibited medication and treatment switching).

The EMA points‐to‐consider [5] specify that the two analysis sets are equally important: ‘In a non‐inferiority trial, the full analysis set and the PP analysis set have equal importance and their use should lead to similar conclusions for a robust interpretation’.

The FDA position is less clear. The guidance [7] includes reflections on the challenges of following the ITT principle in non‐inferiority trials but concludes that the best advice to minimise the extent of these challenges is to emphasise trial quality at the planning stage and to continuously monitor the trial during conduct and analysis stages. We interpret this as implying that only an ITT/FAS analysis is required provided the trial is viewed as being of sufficient quality.

Consequently, because of the clear EMA recommendation, pre‐ICH E9(R1) [1], the approach taken in non‐inferiority trials was to conduct two analyses, one based on FAS and one based on PPS, with the perception of addressing the same clinical question of interest for a non‐inferiority objective. The reason for not only using FAS is stated in ICH E9: [3] ‘However, in an equivalence or non‐inferiority trial use of the full analysis set is generally not conservative and its role should be considered very carefully’. In the non‐inferiority context this means that use of the FAS can lead to falsely concluding non‐inferiority. Of note, a PPS analysis can be biased in either direction depending on who is excluded [12].

The ICH E9(R1) addendum [1] does not specify which estimands are appropriate in a non‐inferiority setting but notes ‘Estimands that are constructed with one or more intercurrent events accounted for using the treatment policy strategy present similar issues for non‐inferiority and equivalence trials as those related to analysis of the FAS under the ITT principle. […] Estimands could be constructed to directly address those intercurrent events which can lead to the attenuation of differences between treatment arms (e.g., discontinuations from treatment and use of additional medications)’.

Regarding the use of PPS, the ICH E9(R1) [1] states that ‘In respect of the framework presented in this addendum, it may not be possible to construct a relevant estimand to which analysis of the PPS is aligned. As noted above, analysis of the PPS does not achieve the goal of estimating the effect in any principal stratum […]’. There is also a note of warning when determining the non‐inferiority margin from estimated effect sizes from completed trials, since consistent definitions of estimands, particularly the handling of intercurrent events, will need consideration.

The ICH E9(R1) addendum [1] lists considerations for non‐inferiority trials rather than providing clear guidance. This has led to confusion regarding how to meet regulatory requirements as laid out in guidelines [3, 4, 5, 7, 8] predating ICH E9(R1) [1] and at the same time implementing the estimand framework.

The four co‐authors of this paper are all members of the sub‐team on estimands in non‐inferiority trials of the Estimand Implementation Working Group [13, 14]. Inspired by the discussions in the sub‐team, we have set out to summarise the impact of the estimand framework on non‐inferiority specific issues such as assay sensitivity and choice of non‐inferiority margin, Δ, and to provide recommendations for how to implement the framework in relation to a non‐inferiority objective, while emphasising that the relevance of the recommendations should be assessed on a case‐by‐case basis.

In this paper, we implement the estimand framework and thinking process into a non‐inferiority context by discussing the underlying clinical question of a non‐inferiority trial and the relation between the estimand framework and the topics of assay sensitivity, choice of non‐inferiority margin and switching between non‐inferiority and superiority. We end by summarising our recommendations for implementing estimands in non‐inferiority trials.

What Is the Clinical Question of Interest in Non‐Inferiority Trials?

The ICH E9(R1) training slides [2] suggest a process for constructing an estimand. The first step is to carefully consider the therapeutic setting and intent of treatment. Next, the intercurrent events should be identified. Intercurrent events do not depend on whether a trial is a non‐inferiority trial or a superiority trial.

The next step in deciding on the estimand of a clinical trial is to determine the intercurrent event handling strategies. There is no requirement that the handling strategy must be the same for each of the intercurrent events [2]. While having multiple intercurrent events and handling them with different strategies may complicate estimation and interpretation, we are of the opinion that defining each intercurrent event and considering how to handle each one helps to clarify what is of clinical interest to estimate regardless of the complexity.

When a treatment policy strategy is used to address intercurrent event(s) related to the treatment conditions, for example, use of rescue/additional medication/discontinuation of treatment, the intercurrent event will be an essential part of the treatment strategy. Thus, instead of assessing the non‐inferiority of the randomised treatments, non‐inferiority of treatment strategies, that is, test treatment with the effect of the intercurrent event(s) versus reference treatment with the effect of intercurrent event(s), is studied rather than non‐inferiority of the randomised treatments. For example, in a setting where the intercurrent event is ‘use rescue medication as needed’, the treatment effect for a two‐arm randomised parallel group trial would be a comparison of test treatment with rescue (as needed) versus reference treatment with rescue (as needed) and not a comparison of only the randomised test treatment versus the reference treatment. However, this type of strategy may need careful consideration, especially if the intercurrent event is more prevalent in one of the treatment conditions being compared. In the example of use of rescue medication as needed, higher usage of rescue medication in the test treatment arm could result in the test treatment plus rescue arm having a similar effect as the reference treatment plus rescue, due to the imbalance in the use of rescue medication rather than the test treatment being non‐inferior to the reference treatment, raising questions about the clinical relevance of the studied question of interest. Consequently, the estimated treatment effect should always be accompanied with an overview of the distribution of each type of intercurrent events by treatment group to make a valid interpretation of the results.

If the clinical question of interest is to estimate the treatment effect of test treatment versus reference treatment as if the intercurrent event had not occurred, a hypothetical strategy may be employed instead. However, the ICH E9 (R1) addendum [1] states that not all hypothetical strategies are clinically meaningful or of regulatory interest. A recent paper puts forward the view that if an intercurrent event cannot be intervened on or prevented in clinical practice, then a hypothetical strategy for the intercurrent event may not currently be of regulatory interest [15]. An example for a hypothetical strategy that would be difficult to justify is the effect ‘had participants not discontinued their randomised treatment due to an adverse drug reaction’ since it is not conceivable how this would be achieved in clinical practice.

Hypothetical strategies that might be of regulatory interest are often those where the intercurrent event is a result of the clinical trial design. For example, for ethical reasons, diabetes clinical trials have to offer the use of rescue medication. As such, the availability of rescue medication is a design feature and the hypothetical scenario ‘had rescue medication not been made available’ could in theory be achieved through a change in the trial design. The EMA guideline on clinical investigation of medicinal products in the treatment or prevention of diabetes mellitus [16] describes this as a treatment effect of interest. To conclude, employing a hypothetical strategy for an intercurrent event in a non‐inferiority trial requires careful consideration of the regulatory acceptability of the hypothetical scenario, as in a superiority trial.

Alternatively, the interest might be in showing non‐inferiority of the test treatment versus the reference treatment in a target population, which is defined by the occurrence or absence of intercurrent events. For example, interest might be in showing non‐inferiority of the test treatment versus the reference treatment in patients who would follow their initially assigned treatment on either arm. A key property of estimands applying the principal stratum strategy to one or more intercurrent events for parallel group trials, regardless of whether the trial aims to show non‐inferiority or superiority, is that patients' membership of a particular stratum is usually not known since patients are randomised to only one arm [17]. This raises the question how the prescribers can identify the patients who would benefit from the test treatment and whether the test treatment, or a treatment strategy involving the test treatment, is non‐inferior in a wider population beyond the principal stratum or even inferior or harmful. For a more general discussion on the role of principal stratum estimands, we refer to existing literature [18, 19, 20, 21, 22].

If a composite strategy is employed, the occurrence (and potentially the timing) of the intercurrent event is reflected in the variable attribute of the estimand. There is no unique way of implementing a composite strategy and defining the variable to incorporate the occurrence or timing of the intercurrent event. For example, in a diabetes clinical trial, a composite binary variable might be defined as ‘responder’ if a patient's HbA1c at week 26 is below 7% and the patient has not used rescue medication between baseline and Week 26. An alternative way of defining a ‘responder’ would be if a patient's HbA1c improved by 2%‐points between baseline and Week 26 and the patient has not used rescue medication within this timeframe. In these settings, interest would be in showing non‐inferiority of the test treatment versus the reference treatment for a responder endpoint where those who used rescue medication are considered as non‐responders, reflecting a poor clinical outcome. Through the composite strategy, multiple measures are combined into a single measure, which can make interpretation of the resulting effect difficult if each treatment affects the various components of the composite differently.

Finally, the ICH E9 (R1) addendum [1] outlines the while‐on‐treatment strategy for handling intercurrent events. Here non‐inferiority of the test treatment versus the reference treatment is of interest while patients are on their initially assigned treatment condition up to the time of the intercurrent event. For example, in a diabetes trial, interest might be in the change of HbA1c from baseline to Week 26, discontinuation of the randomised treatment or to the usage of rescue medication whichever occurs first. As in a superiority setting, an estimand with a while‐on‐treatment strategy might be difficult to interpret if the duration of treatment differs between the groups or if the duration of treatment is dependent on the value of the variable. Thus, it is important to investigate the distribution of durations across treatment groups when applying the while on treatment strategy.

Some authors [23, 24, 25, 26, 27] discuss the clinical question of interest based on adherence, though not discussing it from a non‐inferiority perspective. The idea of establishing non‐inferiority in adherers is a logical consequence from the perspective that the trial conduct should be of high quality with high compliance, few protocol deviations and few missing data. However, adherence is an ambiguous concept in a clinical trial and needs to be clearly defined [28]. The ambiguity arises as it is often not clear whether adherence refers to the randomised treatment, a treatment strategy or the protocol, and whether this includes a measure of compliance (e.g., what percentage of tablets were taken). If a clearly defined intercurrent event that reflects adherence has been defined, any of the strategies outlined in ICH E9(R1) [1] can be used to handle it. For example, the effect in ‘adherers’, could be the effect in the patients who would have completed treatment on either arm (which corresponds to using a principal stratification strategy) or it could be the effect of what a participant's outcome would be where discontinuation of the initially assigned treatment would not occur (which corresponds to a hypothetical strategy for the intercurrent event of treatment discontinuation). We therefore recommend using the estimands framework for all non‐inferiority trials to have a precise description of the treatment effect of interest and to refrain from using terms such as ‘adherence’ unless they are precisely defined in the trial protocol [28].

As outlined in the introduction, in non‐inferiority trials, pre‐ICH E9(R1), according to the EMA points to consider on switching between superiority and non‐inferiority [5], the FAS analysis based on the ITT principle and the PPS analysis were equally important, because, in general, the FAS analysis was considered to favour the experimental treatment in a non‐inferiority setting. That is, the FAS analysis was described as ‘not conservative’ or ‘biased.’ Unfortunately, this has led to the use of the terms ‘conservatism’ and ‘bias’ in relation to estimands. For example, in a superiority trial, statements are sometimes made that an estimand handling all intercurrent events with a treatment policy strategy is the more ‘conservative estimand’ than an estimand where all intercurrent events are handled using a hypothetical strategy. However, in our opinion, use of such terminology does not make sense when describing an estimand since conservatism/bias is about how a result (i.e., an estimate from an estimator) relates to the truth (i.e., the targeted treatment effect). Therefore, we recommend the terminology of conservatism/bias should be reserved for estimators and not estimands. First, we should be discussing the relevance of the estimand in the given setting. Subsequently, we may discuss if the estimator chosen to estimate the estimand is conservative or biased.

In this section, we discussed the possible clinical questions of interest in a non‐inferiority trial. The discussion emphasises that the decision regarding the intercurrent event handling strategies in a non‐inferiority trial must be made on a case‐by‐case basis. What might be of clinical interest in a non‐inferiority trial in one disease setting might not be relevant for another disease setting. For example, in a trial in Type 2 diabetes, the EMA guidance [16] specifies that it may be of interest to handle the intercurrent event ‘introduction of other medication that will influence HbA1c values (additional/rescue medication)’, with a hypothetical strategy. In contrast, for asthma trials, rescue medication is regarded as part of the treatment condition [29] and is therefore handled by the treatment policy strategy.

Despite the differences noted above between non‐inferiority and superiority trials, many of the considerations when deciding on an intercurrent event handling strategy are the same. In both types of trial, there is no single intercurrent event handling strategy that should universally be applied to all intercurrent events, and an overview of the frequency and timing of intercurrent events by treatment arm is important to facilitate the understanding and interpretation of the estimated treatment effect.

Assay Sensitivity

All regulatory guidelines stress the importance of ensuring assay sensitivity. Assay sensitivity according to ICH E10 [4] is ‘a property of a clinical trial defined as the ability to distinguish an effective treatment from a less effective or ineffective treatment’.

Showing non‐inferiority of the test treatment versus a reference treatment does not imply that the test treatment is effective unless certain aspects are fulfilled [7]:

Historical evidence of sensitivity to drug effects (reference treatment or treatments very similar to reference).

Historical evidence of sensitivity to drug effects (reference treatment or treatments very similar to reference).

Constancy of trial design and clinical practice (trial population, concomitant treatments, method of assessment, endpoint, dose of reference treatment, analytical approach, etc.).

Constancy of trial design and clinical practice (trial population, concomitant treatments, method of assessment, endpoint, dose of reference treatment, analytical approach, etc.).

High quality of trial conducts more important than for superiority trials (few protocol deviations (PDs), high compliance, little use of certain concomitant medication and few missing data).

High quality of trial conducts more important than for superiority trials (few protocol deviations (PDs), high compliance, little use of certain concomitant medication and few missing data).

Many of these aspects relate to the estimand attributes, but also cover estimation and trial conduct aspects. Applying the estimand framework when planning, conducting and analysing a non‐inferiority trial can provide additional precision when assessing assay sensitivity. Through the treatment attribute, the treatment conditions, including that of the reference treatment, are described precisely. From this it can be determined under which conditions the reference treatment was shown in previous trials to be superior to placebo. For instance, it can be envisaged that the reference treatment was superior to placebo (both without use of rescue medication) but for the treatment strategy that includes the use of rescue medication as needed, reference plus rescue treatment may not have been superior to placebo plus rescue treatment. In addition, the target population in which assay sensitivity should hold is determined by the population attribute, and the variable and population‐level summary precisely define what is measured on a patient level and how treatment conditions are compared.

Choice of Non‐Inferiority Margin

The choice of the non‐inferiority margin should be a combination of statistical reasoning and clinical judgement [6]. The non‐inferiority margin is the largest difference between the test treatment and the reference treatment that is clinically acceptable. Both EMA [6] and FDA [7] discuss in detail how the non‐inferiority margin should be determined.

In the approaches outlined in the FDA guidance [7], first, the effect of the reference treatment versus placebo must be estimated using a meta‐analysis of historical trials. This effect is named M1. Most often the non‐inferiority margin will be less than M1, since otherwise only assurance that the test treatment is superior to placebo is provided, not whether the effect size is clinically relevant. Therefore, the non‐inferiority margin should be determined based on the largest loss of effect of the test treatment versus the reference treatment that is clinically acceptable. This is referred to as M2 or degree of inferiority in the FDA guidance [7].

Two methods are described, the fixed margin method and the synthesis method. In the fixed margin method, M1 and M2 are explicitly defined, whereas for the synthesis method, only the ratio M2/M1 is explicitly defined (fraction of effect of reference to be preserved, indirect superiority to placebo).

The effect M1 of the reference treatment versus placebo generally depends on the intercurrent event strategy, that is, the magnitude and interpretation of the effect size would change with the intercurrent event strategy. In PIONEER 3 [9], for instance, if a treatment policy strategy is employed for the intercurrent events treatment discontinuation and use of rescue medication, then the margin M1 describes the effect of the strategy ‘sitagliptin 100 mg once‐daily added on to metformin with or without sulfonylurea regardless of treatment discontinuation and use of rescue medication as needed’ compared to ‘Placebo added on to metformin with or without sulfonylurea regardless of treatment discontinuation and use of rescue medication as needed’. On the other hand, if a hypothetical strategy is employed for the two intercurrent events, then the margin M1 describes the effect of ‘sitagliptin 100 mg once‐daily added on to metformin with or without sulfonylurea’ versus ‘Placebo once daily added on to metformin with or without sulfonylurea’ as if patients had not discontinued treatment and rescue medication had not been made available.

Another example, which highlights that the margin M1 depends on the intercurrent event strategy is provided through the composite variable strategy. In the composite variable strategy, the variable definition differs as compared to using another strategy, which affects the size of the effect between the reference treatment versus placebo and which effect sizes are clinically relevant.

The influence of the intercurrent event strategy on the non‐inferiority margin has practical implications on the selection of historical studies for determining the margin M1. This is because the treatment effect of interest might not be available in historical studies conducted prior to the implementation of the estimand framework since the occurrence of what is now termed ‘intercurrent events’ were not routinely reported. In this case, information on any exclusions from the FAS/PPS will need to be considered if they fulfil the definition of an intercurrent event and if so, the impact of those should be accounted for when determining M1. Where this information is not available, discussions within trial teams of expected rates of intercurrent events and how the intercurrent event affects the variable may be required to support the decision of M1. Furthermore, it is also likely that different estimands will be needed compared to historical ones. Therefore, we anticipate that defining M1 based on historical trials will remain a challenge to trial teams.

For a principal stratum estimand, it is not immediately clear how the margin would be determined. For example, in a non‐inferiority trial where the interest is in the population of patients who would have the intercurrent events neither under the test treatment nor the reference treatment, for the non‐inferiority margin to be appropriate, it would need to be determined based on the effect of the reference treatment versus placebo in the population of interest defined by the principal stratum for which generally no studies are available.

M2 will be based on clinical judgment and since changing the intercurrent event strategies changes the clinical question, a logical consequence will be that M2 will also be impacted. However, in situations where the impact is negligible, M2 may not change.

Multiple Questions of Interest

The pre‐ICH E9(R1) [1] approach to non‐inferiority trials of conducting two analyses, one based on the FAS and one based on the PPS, raises the question of whether two estimands need to be defined for a non‐inferiority trial. For example, this could be interpreted as needing to define an estimand that addresses all intercurrent events with a treatment policy strategy to reflect the idea of the ITT principle on which the FAS is based, and one estimand that addresses all intercurrent events with a hypothetical strategy (or principal stratum strategy) to reflect the idea of a PPS‐based analysis. The major concerns about this approach are that (1) PPS analyses, which generally remove randomised participants, do not target a (clinically) meaningful estimand unless strong assumptions are made [30] and (2) FAS analyses do not imply a particular estimand as pre‐ICH E9(R1) it most often only referred to all randomised participants being used in the analysis set without specifying how intercurrent events were handled. Therefore, equating a FAS‐based analysis and a PPS‐based analysis with an estimand that handles all intercurrent events with a particular strategy to mimic what was done pre‐ICH E9(R1) is in general not appropriate.

Careful consideration on the relevant clinical question(s) of interest within each specific therapeutic context is needed and might result in the conclusion that multiple questions are of interest. There are multiple ways in which addressing multiple clinical questions of interest within a single trial can be implemented, but defining multiple primary estimands may introduce multiplicity issues. Examples of how to handle this include: co‐primary estimands (win‐on‐all), multiple primary estimands (type 1 error rate control needed), or one primary estimand together with supplementary estimand(s). A detailed discussion of the regulatory considerations can, for example, be found in FDA's guidance document on Multiple Endpoints in Clinical Trials [31].

Defining co‐primary estimands for a clinical trial with a non‐inferiority objective suggests that multiple primary clinical questions, which, by definition, are different, are of equal importance. Co‐primary estimands should not be applied simply to reflect the pre‐ICH E9(R1) approach of two analyses sets nor to provide extra reassurance on the non‐inferiority objective without clear clinical questions of interest. These two rationales are not in the spirit of the estimand framework. Furthermore, each co‐primary estimand will require specification of a non‐inferiority margin that may differ between estimands.

We think that the concept of co‐primary estimands or multiple primary estimands should be reserved for therapeutic settings where the primary trial objective is to address multiple questions of interest and for non‐inferiority to be demonstrated on both. Moreover, superiority trials overwhelmingly have only a single primary estimand and it is unclear why having multiple primary questions of interest would be more common or the norm in non‐inferiority trials.

Consequently, in general, we recommend that clinical trials with a non‐inferiority objective have a single primary estimand, similar to clinical trials with a superiority objective. A similar approach is suggested by the EMA guideline on clinical investigation of medicinal products in the treatment or prevention of diabetes mellitus [16]. As outlined in ICH E9(R1) [1], a supplementary estimand could be defined to better understand the compound. As is common in clinical trials, the probability of erroneous conclusions is not controlled for the supplementary estimands.

Finally, if different regulatory authorities are interested in differing questions of interest, one possibility is to define multiple estimands and conduct the corresponding analysis each with the full significance level [32, 33].

Switching Between Non‐Inferiority and Superiority Objectives

It is common that clinical trials are designed to first assess the non‐inferiority of the test treatment versus the reference treatment and if non‐inferiority is confirmed, to then proceed to testing for superiority. The EMA document on points to consider when switching between non‐inferiority and superiority [5] discusses considerations to be made in such situations.

Applying a treatment policy strategy to handle intercurrent events is still the most common approach in clinical trials with a superiority objective although it does not always address a clinically relevant question of interest [34].

In clinical trials with both non‐inferiority and superiority objectives, a key aspect is whether the non‐inferiority and superiority claims are to be made for the same estimand, that is for the same treatment condition(s), the same population, the same variable and population‐level summary as well as the same intercurrent events handling strategies. The EMA Guideline on clinical investigation of medicinal products in the treatment or prevention of diabetes mellitus [16] states that the same attributes may be used: ‘For active controlled trials with a non‐inferiority (NI) hypothesis, the same primary estimand strategy as outlined above might be justified […] Furthermore, it is likely necessary that a supplemental estimand is specified to address the impact of important intercurrent events like protocol violations and deviations’. Therefore, careful consideration is needed to identify a clinical question of interest that can be regarded as relevant for both objectives.

Choosing two different estimands for the non‐inferiority and superiority objective could have implications on the analysis as different statistical estimation approaches are likely to be employed. Likewise, if the estimand is the same for the non‐inferiority and superiority objectives, it may raise discussions if the same estimator can be used to estimate it. An estimator that is conservative for assessing superiority may not be conservative for assessing non‐inferiority [16]. This will complicate the interpretation and communication of trial results that require precise and transparent description of results in the clinical study report, product labelling and scientific papers and presentations. Pre‐ICH E9(R1), the same estimator was used for assessing non‐inferiority and superiority and the underlying clinical question was implicitly assumed to be the same. Consequently, the estimated treatment effect and 95% confidence interval would apply to both the non‐inferiority hypothesis and the superiority hypothesis. With different estimands or different estimators (or both), it may happen that the results from the non‐inferiority analysis would support both non‐inferiority and superiority claims, but when addressing the superiority hypothesis with a different estimator (or different estimand and likely a different estimator), the superiority will no longer hold. This may give rise to misunderstandings and confusion and will require an elaborate explanation.

Discussion and Conclusions

In this paper, we have described specific considerations to be made when defining estimands in a non‐inferiority trial. The implementation of the estimand framework is mandatory for trials that are part of regulatory decision‐making, including non‐inferiority trials.

There are special considerations for non‐inferiority trials that do not apply to superiority trials, for example, regarding assay sensitivity and the choice of the non‐inferiority margin, which are impacted by the choice of the estimand. However, limited guidance is provided in ICH E9(R1) [1] on defining estimands in non‐inferiority trials and there is little experience shared in the literature on how to apply the estimand framework in a non‐inferiority setting.

Current regulatory guidance has not yet been updated to reflect the implementation of the estimand framework in non‐inferiority trials, but we find it reassuring that EMA has published a draft concept paper [37] with the intention to revise the guideline on choice of non‐inferiority margin [6] and the points to consider on switching between superiority and non‐inferiority [5] into one guideline and implement the estimand framework.

The current EMA guidance states that non‐inferiority should be established based on results from both a FAS analysis and a PPS analysis, since in general, the FAS analysis is not ‘conservative’ in the non‐inferiority setting [5]. This has led many to apply the same terminology to estimands, for example, statements like ‘one estimand is more conservative compared to another’. We think conservatism is not a term that is defined for estimands, because ‘conservative’ is relating the estimator to the targeted treatment effect. In other words, the discussion regarding ‘conservatism’ belongs to the estimator chosen to estimate the estimand. Instead, we should discuss whether an estimand is relevant in each specific setting.

The estimand framework has clarified the limitations of a PPS analysis and such analyses appear to have no continued role in evaluating trials. There is continued reference in the statistical literature to ‘per‐protocol effects’ or the ‘effect in adherers’ [23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. These terms have different interpretations, and a better approach is specific identification of key intercurrent events, particularly as different strategies can be appropriate for different intercurrent events.

Pre‐ICH E9(R1), analyses of the FAS, based on the ITT principle, and the PPS were perceived to be related to the same objective of demonstrating NI. The estimand thinking has revealed that the two analyses address different questions. Introduction of the estimand framework has led to a deeper understanding of the underlying clinical question. The approach pre‐addendum of generating two different sets of results has led to the idea that two different estimands should be defined, reflecting these two analyses. While this may be appropriate for a particular trial, in general it may be that one carefully defined estimand will be sufficient. We recommend defining one primary estimand and then considering if there is a need for defining a supplementary estimand.

Often the primary objective is to first establish non‐inferiority and the next objective is to investigate superiority (given non‐inferiority is confirmed). In that case, we strongly encourage to define the same estimand. However, this may warrant a shift of the current practice of commonly handling intercurrent events with a treatment policy strategy for regulatory decision making. As for superiority trials, it will always be important to supplement the treatment effect estimates and confidence intervals with an overview of the frequency and timing of the intercurrent events to fully interpret the results. This applies regardless of the strategies applied to the intercurrent events. It is particularly important to compare frequencies and timing across treatment arms in non‐inferiority trials, since severe imbalance may lead to bias towards demonstrating non‐inferiority, for example, in relation to use of rescue medication or treatment switching.

Determining the non‐inferiority margin is also affected by the estimand framework. When the non‐inferiority margin is determined using historical control information, it is necessary to have information on the underlying estimand [35]. Not only should the historical placebo trials be based on a similar estimand, but also the estimand in the non‐inferiority trial should be aligned to that historical estimand. This is likely to remain a problem, since many of the historical trials will not have estimands defined explicitly in the trial protocol and because estimands different from the historical ones may become relevant.

For each intercurrent event handling strategy, estimation considerations have been discussed in the literature: Mallinckrodt et al. [36] provide an overview of estimators aligned to different strategies, Wang et al. [38], Wolbers et al. [39], Drury et al. [40], and Bell, Drury and Mütze [41] for the treatment policy strategy; Parra, Daniel and Bartlett [42], Lasch and Guizzaro [43] for the hypothetical strategy, Bornkamp et al. [22], and Qu, Luo and Ruberg [44] for the principal stratum strategy; Keene [45], Darken et al. [46] for a composite strategy, and Keene et al. [47] for a while‐alive strategy.

It could be argued that the principal stratum strategy resonates with the requirement of assay sensitivity in the non‐inferiority setting in the sense that a principal stratum could be defined for those who would not experience the intercurrent event(s) of interest. Therefore, for future research, it will be interesting to evaluate estimand approaches that use the principal stratum strategy such as the Tripartite framework (Akacha, Bretz and Ruberg [23], Qu, Luo and Ruberg [44]) or the approach by Lou, Jones and Sun [48] This should include discussions on the relevance of using the same estimand for assessing non‐inferiority followed by assessment of superiority, and the feasibility in relation to determining the non‐inferiority margin as discussed above in ‘Choice of non‐inferiority margin’.

We have summarised our recommendations in Table 1, but more work in this field is needed and we welcome more discussion on the topic and encourage more publications on case studies.
