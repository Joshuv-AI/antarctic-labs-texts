Non-invasive profiling of exosomal miRNA and protein biomarkers from vaginal discharge for the early detection of preterm labor
Authors: Taewoon Kim, Jee Yoon Park, Hyo Jin Lee, Bo Young Choi, Hyeon Ji Kim, Luke P Lee, Jong Wook Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPreterm labor is a serious concern that can lead to preterm birth, posing substantial risks for both the mother and the neonate. Despite approximately 15 million preterm births worldwide each year, there is a lack of sufficient strategies for predicting and preventing preterm labor. Here, we present a non-invasive method for simultaneously detecting exosomal miRNA and protein biomarkers in vaginal discharge, enabling early diagnosis of life-threatening conditions in both the mother and the neonate. Our non-invasive vaginal discharge biopsy using a swab enables the isolation of enriched intact extracellular vesicles through our microfluidic platform called Biologically-intact Exosome Separation Technology (BEST). We observed differential expression of specific miRNAs, including up-regulated hsa-miR-206 and down-regulated hsa-miR-3674, hsa-miR-365a-5p, and hsa-miR-193b-3p, in mothers experiencing preterm labor. We also found significant differences in protein expression in mothers with preterm labor compared to full-term mothers, indicating the involvement of HGS, ATL3, APOH, and GUSB in preterm labor mechanisms. We envision a future in which non-invasive detection of unique miRNA and protein biomarkers in vaginal discharge transforms global healthcare by enabling early detection and effective treatment of preterm labor.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04277-6.Keywords: Preterm labor, Vaginal discharge, Extracellular vesicles, Exosomes, Proteomics, RNA-seq

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation [1, 2], is a profound global health challenge, with prevalence rates ranging from 5% in Europe to 18% in Africa [3]. The estimated worldwide occurrence of preterm birth was approximately 10% in 2020, solidifying its status as the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality [4]. Despite approximately 15 million preterm births worldwide each year, practical strategies for prediction and prevention remain insufficient [5, 6]. Infants born prematurely not only encounter heightened risks during birth and infancy, but also contend with enduring health issues throughout their lives, including conditions such as cerebral palsy, cognitive disability, epilepsy, blindness, or hearing loss [3, 7]. When stratifying by degrees of prematurity, the risks are highest for babies born significantly preterm before 28 weeks of gestation, followed by those born very preterm between 28 and less than 32 weeks, and moderate to late preterm between 32 and less than 37 weeks [8–10]. This underscores the pressing need for further research and comprehensive public health initiatives.

The causes of spontaneous preterm birth are preterm labor, preterm premature rupture of membranes, and acute cervical insufficiency [11, 12]. Among those, preterm labor is defined as the development of regular uterine contractions before the gestational period reaches 37 weeks, and cervical remodeling, such as dilatation or progression of effacement, usually accompanies this, resulting in preterm birth [13]. Two-thirds of all preterm births have been known to result from spontaneous preterm labor, and most pregnant women with preterm labor suffer various degrees of abdominal pain and/or back pain due to increased uterine contractility [14]. Moreover, uncontrolled preterm labor ultimately results in gross premature rupture of membranes. Tocolytic agents to relieve uterine contractility are used to manage preterm labor; however, global guidelines or meta-analyses have suggested they are not significantly effective in reducing the rate of preterm birth [15–17]. Therefore, tocolytics are recommended to be administered only for a short time for antenatal corticosteroids to accelerate fetal lung maturation or for transferring the patient to tertiary centers with neonatal intensive care units [18]. There are several pieces of evidence that intraamniotic microbial invasion with inflammation caused by ascending infection is the most critical pathophysiology of spontaneous preterm birth; antibiotics are not recommended to be used for preterm labor with intact membranes [19, 20].

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-bound particles released by cells that play crucial roles in intercellular communication and modulation of local microenvironments [21, 22]. Based on their biogenesis and size, EVs are broadly classified into ectosomes, which are shed directly from the plasma membrane, and small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), typically ranging from 30 to 200 nm in diameter [23, 24]. sEVs are commonly referred to as exosomes in the literature [25], as they are generally associated with an endosomal origin involving multivesicular bodies, although direct demonstration of this biogenesis pathway is often challenging in biofluid-based clinical studies [26]. sEVs are released from diverse cell types and bodily fluids, including blood [27], saliva [28], breast milk [29], urine [30, 31], tears [32], and vaginal discharge [33]. These small vesicles transport bioactive compounds like proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids [34–36]. In the specific context of pregnancy, current research highlights the significant impact of sEVs as potential mediators in maternal-fetal communication [37, 38]. During pregnancy, intricate communication between maternal and fetal cells is vital for the proper development and maintenance of the gestational environment. Moreover, sEVs derived from plasma have demonstrated promising diagnostic potential for various pregnancy outcomes, establishing a solid foundation for exploring their role in predicting complications related to preterm birth [39–41]. However, most sEV-based studies related to preterm birth and preterm labor have predominantly focused on systemic biofluids such as maternal blood [42, 43] or urine [44], whereas systematic molecular profiling of sEVs derived from vaginal discharge remains limited. This gap underscores the need to explore alternative, locally accessible biofluids that may more directly reflect pathological processes associated with preterm labor.

Vaginal discharge is a composite of mucus originating from the vagina. It is consistently generated by secretory cells in the endocervix and the anterior vaginal epithelium. This mucus protects the female reproductive tract, ensuring the moisture and lubrication of epithelial surfaces to safeguard against potential threats [45–47]. Despite regular exposure to pathogenic microorganisms, the female genital tract maintains homeostasis with a relatively low incidence of infection, underscoring the presence of numerous defense mechanisms [48, 49]. Critical components of this intricate defense system involve efficiently removing adherent bacteria through shedding epithelial cells [50]. Additionally, the crucial hydration of the cervical-vaginal mucosa is achieved through secretions from cervical and vaginal glands, as well as plasma transudate [51]. These collective mechanisms work synergistically to protect against potential threats and reinforce the tract’s resilience in the face of microbial challenges. During pregnancy, the physiology of the cervix changes, and thus, the characteristics of secreted fluid collected in the vagina are also modified. Because obtaining vaginal fluid is easy and non-invasive, challenges to the utility of vaginal discharge to predict obstetric complications have continued. Fetal fibronectin in vaginal fluid is the only marker that is used in clinical practice to test the possibility of impending preterm birth [52, 53]. It originated from the amniotic membrane; therefore, the presence of fetal fibronectin in the vagina implies a microscopic rupture of membranes. Although it has a high negative predictive value, the positive predictive value is only around 10%. Accumulated evidence has demonstrated that the microbiome in the vagina is closely related to pregnancy outcomes such as preterm birth [1]; therefore, exploration of markers from vaginal discharge seems to be the key to understanding the pathophysiology of preterm complications in pregnancy. In recent studies, a paper has been published indicating the significant impact of sEVs derived from female vaginal discharge on the function and fertility of human sperm [54]; however, there is a lack of research on the association between sEVs in the vagina and obstetric complications due to the technical limitations caused by the heterogeneous and mucoid nature of the vaginal discharge.

In this study, our goal is to understand the causes of preterm labor by analyzing miRNA and protein expression in sEVs isolated from vaginal discharge of both preterm labor patients and those with normal, full-term births. By examining the sEV cargo from vaginal discharge, we hope to identify potential differences that may be linked to the onset of preterm labor. Our ultimate aim is to discover specific molecular markers that could be used for early diagnosis of preterm labor and pave the way for future clinical interventions in high-risk preterm birth situations.

Results

Characterization of sEVs isolated from term birth (TB) and patients with preterm labor (PTL) samples

The clinical symptoms associated with PTL include regular contractions, abdominal pain, and changes in vaginal discharge (VD), which necessitate the analysis of VD for potential biomarkers (Fig. 1A). Our workflow, illustrated in Fig. 1B to D, involved the initial dilution and centrifugation of VD samples to reduce viscosity and eliminate cell debris (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Subsequently, sEVs were isolated from pretreated VD samples using a precipitation method (Supplementary Fig. 1B) and biologically intact exosome separation technology (BEST) chip, the advanced version of our tunable microfluidics (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1C) [55]. Our state-of-the-art BEST chip is specifically designed to isolate undamaged sEVs while maintaining their biological integrity. As a result, we can obtain the highest-quality sEVs, which yield the best results for exosomal proteins and miRNAs as biomarkers. Isolated sEVs were then used for downstream analysis, as shown in Fig. 1D.

We investigated the properties and concentrations of sEVs isolated from TB and PTL samples using BEST (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 2A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed distinct size distributions of three samples: VD Prep. (pretreated vaginal discharge), VD-exo Precipitation (sEVs isolated using the precipitation method), and VD-exo BEST (sEVs isolated using BEST). The VD-exo BEST peaks at 65 nm and 145 nm; VD-exo Precipitation shows peaks at 135 nm and 205 nm; and VD Prep. exhibited peaks at 155 nm and 225 nm (Fig. 1F). To specifically evaluate EVs within the exosome-size range, particle concentrations between 30 and 200 nm were quantified (Fig. 1G). The particle concentrations within the 30–200 nm range, obtained through precipitation and the BEST method, were comparable, and no statistically significant difference was observed between VD-exo Precipitation and VD-exo BEST. These findings suggest that both isolation techniques yielded similar quantities of particles within the exosome size spectrum.

Protein concentration analysis further indicated significantly higher levels in VD-exo BEST than in VD-exo Precipitation (Fig. 1H). However, despite the lower total protein concentration in VD-exo Precipitation, the particle-to-protein ratio was highest in VD-exo Precipitation (Supplementary Fig. 2B), reflecting method-dependent differences in total protein recovery rather than differences in particle yield or sEV enrichment. In addition, analysis of exosomal markers demonstrated enrichment of CD9, CD63, CD81, and TSG101 between TB and PTL samples isolated using precipitation and BEST, supporting the preservation of exosomal characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Together, these results demonstrate that BEST efficiently isolates sEVs with well-defined size distributions and preserved exosomal protein content, highlighting its suitability for downstream exosomal biomarker analysis in preterm labor.

RNA-seq to identify miRNA biomarkers for diagnosing PTL

In this study, we analyzed the miRNA expression profiles of sEVs isolated from TB and PTL samples using three samples: VD Prep., VD-exo Precipitation, and VD-exo BEST (Supplementary Table 1). Venn diagram analysis revealed overlapping and distinct miRNA profiles across these methods and conditions. Overall, 1,459 miRNAs were identified with 849, 793, 802, and 824 miRNAs from TB VD-exo BEST, TB VD-exo Precipitation, PTL VD-exo BEST, and PTL VD-exo Precipitation groups, respectively, based on the criterion of log2(Normalized data) 0. There were 360 common genes across all four groups (Fig. 2A). The principal component analysis (PCA) plot showed differentiation between PTL and TB samples. The two-dimensional (2D) plot categorized the samples into six groups (Fig. 2B). Each group appeared distinct, with the first two PCs explaining 26.9% of data variance (PC1 = 17.3%, PC2 = 9.6%). We further investigated the expression profiles of 20 miRNAs, which were differentially expressed in VD-derived sEVs, using criteria of Foldchange > 2.0 and P < 0.05 (Fig. 2C). The hierarchical clustering heatmap in sEV groups indicated differentiation between PTL and TB. To gain a deeper understanding of the impact of VD-derived sEV in distinguishing between PTL and TB, we performed a volcano plot analysis to examine miRNA expression differences. Applying criteria of Foldchange > 2.0 and P < 0.05, a comparison of VD Prep. between PTL and TB revealed 1 up-regulated and 7 down-regulated miRNAs, as shown in Fig. 2D. However, we identified 5 differentially expressed miRNAs between PTL and TB in VD-exo Precipitation. Among these, 1 miRNA was up-regulated, and 4 miRNAs were down-regulated (Fig. 2E). The VD-exo BEST showed a pattern like VD Prep. (Fig. 2F), with 1 up-regulated miRNA, hsa-miR-206, and 7 down-regulated miRNAs, including hsa-miR-3674, hsa-miR-365a-5p, and hsa-miR-193b-3p. However, to account for multiple testing inherent to high-dimensional RNA-seq data, P-values were further adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. After FDR correction, none of the miRNAs met the predefined adjusted q-value threshold. Accordingly, the differentially expressed miRNAs identified based on nominal P-values are interpreted as exploratory candidates rather than statistically validated biomarkers.

We performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment on the differentially expressed miRNAs between TB and PTL sEV, following the criteria of Foldchange > 2.0 and P < 0.05, to explore the potential biological functions of miRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). The GO analysis revealed the top 5 most significant GO terms for biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF), respectively. The results indicated that BP primarily involves the metabolic process of cellular nitrogen compound, biosynthetic process, gene expression, cellular protein modification process, and neurotrophin tyrosine kinase (TRK) receptor signaling pathway. Regarding CC enrichment analysis, miRNAs significantly participated in the organelle, nucleoplasm, cellular component, cytosol, and protein complex. Regarding MF analysis, miRNAs were mainly enriched in ion binding, nucleic acid binding transcription factor activity, and molecular function, followed by enzyme binding and protein binding transcription factor activity. The KEGG pathway analysis of target genes revealed 17 significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.05), including glycosphingolipid biosynthesis, proteoglycans in cancer, and glioma.

Differential expression and exploratory discriminative performance of miRNAs in TB and PTL

Notably, key differential genes in sEVs, such as hsa-miR-206, hsa-miR-3674, hsa-miR-365a-5p, and hsa-miR-193b-3p, showed significance exclusively in sEVs, suggesting their potential association with PTL. Analyzing pooled miRNA expression data, we identified the four most differentially expressed miRNAs, with their profiles displayed in Fig. 3A to D. Specifically, hsa-miR-206 exhibited up-regulation, while hsa-miR-3674, hsa-miR-365a-5p, and hsa-miR-193b-3p were down-regulated in PTL compared to TB.

Differential protein expression analysis of sEVs isolated from TB and PTL samples

Furthermore, we analyzed the protein expression profiles of sEVs isolated from TB and PTL samples using three samples: VD Prep., VD-exo Precipitation, and VD-exo BEST (Supplementary Table 2). As shown in Fig. 4A, the Venn diagram revealed distinct and overlapping sets of differentially expressed proteins across the different isolation methods and conditions, based on the criterion of log2(Average of abundances) ≠ 0. Specifically, a core set of 2,345 proteins was shared among all groups, indicating a common protein signature in sEVs regardless of the isolation method. PCA plot illustrated a clear separation between TB and PTL samples based on protein expression profiles (Fig. 4B). The first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, accounted for 19.1% and 15.3% of the total variance, respectively. VD-exo BEST showed distinct clustering of TB and PTL samples, indicating that this method effectively differentiates between the two conditions. Similarly, VD Prep. displayed a clustering pattern similar to VD-exo BEST. In contrast, VD-exo Precipitation showed significant overlap between TB and PTL samples, suggesting that this method is less effective in distinguishing between these conditions.

Quantitative comparison of protein abundance in sEVs and exploratory discriminative performance of candidate proteins

We performed a quantitative analysis to compare the abundance of specific proteins in three samples from TB and PTL. The analyzed proteins included HGS, ATL3, APOH, and GUSB. As shown in Fig. 5A, HGS was found to be significantly higher in PTL compared to TB when using the VD-exo BEST (P = 0.013). In contrast, no significant differences were observed with VD Prep. (P = 0.15) and VD-exo Precipitation (P = 0.31). Secondly, ATL3 showed a significant difference between TB and PTL only with the VD-exo BEST (P = 0.042), while other methods did not show significant differences (Fig. 5B). Additionally, APOH was significantly more abundant in TB than in PTL using both VD Prep. (P = 0.019) and VD-exo BEST (P = 0.015), but no significant difference was observed with the VD-exo Precipitation (P = 0.89) (Fig. 5C). Lastly, GUSB did not show statistically significant differences between TB and PTL in any of the samples (Fig. 5D).

To explore the potential discriminative performance of these candidate proteins (HGS, ATL3, APOH, and GUSB) within the same cohort, ROC curve analysis was performed (Fig. 5E to H). For HGS, the AUC values were 0.92 (95% CI, 0.738–1.102) with the VD-exo BEST, 0.72 (95% CI, 0.358–1.082) with the VD-exo Precipitation, and 0.64 (95% CI, 0.238–1.042) with VD Prep. (Fig. 5E). These findings indicate that the discriminative performance of candidate proteins varied depending on the sample preparation method and should be interpreted as exploratory results rather than definitive diagnostic validation.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of VD samples collected non-invasively from individuals experiencing PTL and those with TB, aiming to identify molecular signatures associated with the risk of PTL. Our findings highlight the potential utility of VD-derived sEVs as non-invasive biomarkers for distinguishing between PTL and TB cases. Through meticulous analysis, we observed a notable shift in particle size distribution following sEV isolation and a significant increase in sEV and protein concentration in PTL samples compared to TB. These observations underscore the effectiveness of our isolation method and the potential of sEV-based biomarkers in PTL diagnosis.

Our miRNA profiling provided valuable insights into the molecular landscape associated with PTL. Distinct miRNA profiles were delineated between PTL and TB groups, with PCA corroborating differentiation between the two cohorts. GO enrichment analysis revealed the involvement of miRNAs in critical biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions relevant to preterm birth pathogenesis. Furthermore, differential expression analysis uncovered a panel of miRNAs exhibiting significant dysregulation in PTL compared to TB, particularly within sEV samples. Notably, miRNAs such as hsa-miR-206, hsa-miR-3674, hsa-miR-365a-5p, and hsa-miR-193b-3p have emerged as potential candidates associated with PTL. We explored the reported functions of these miRNAs, and a few studies investigated miRNAs in pregnancy. For example, Deng et al. discovered that hsa-miR-206 plays a role in myometrium contraction using uterine tissue [56]. Akehurst et al. also found that has-miR-206 significantly increased in maternal plasma samples of pregnant women with preeclampsia [57]. Unlike those studies, our study involves research conducted solely using vaginal discharge, which is characterized as non-invasive samples that can be obtained quickly and repeatably as well. Since pregnancy research must encounter maternal and fetal stability, exploring biomarkers related to pregnancy outcomes from non-invasive methods is precious. We also found hsa-miR-193b-3p as a miRNA involved in cell differentiation and proliferation [58]. The association of hsa-miR-365a-5p with the oxytocin signaling pathway was identified through a miRNA target study conducted using miRWalk 3.0 [59]. Given oxytocin’s critical functions in uterine contractions during parturition and milk release during lactation, there is a potential linkage between hsa-miR-365a-5p and PTL. However, information regarding the roles of hsa-miR-3674 in PTL remains limited based on our investigation.

The dysregulation of the four miRNAs, which were overexpressed in sEVs but appeared insignificant in the vaginal discharge itself, can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, vaginal discharge comprises a heterogeneous mixture of various RNA species and other molecules, potentially diluting the relative abundance of miRNAs. Additionally, the expression of miRNAs in vaginal discharge may fluctuate over time, exhibiting subtle changes under specific conditions or time points. Moreover, factors influencing the stability and preservation of miRNAs within vaginal discharge could also contribute to these observations. Therefore, careful consideration of these factors is necessary, and further experiments and analyses may be required to fully understand why the miRNAs overexpressed in sEVs exhibited insignificance in the vaginal discharge itself.

Furthermore, among the proteins analyzed, HGS, ATL3, APOH, and GUSB exhibited significant differential expression between PTL and TB, suggesting their potential roles in the pathophysiology of preterm labor. HGS is known to be involved in regulating endosomal trafficking and the sorting of ubiquitinated proteins into lysosomal degradation pathways. It has also been implicated in signal transduction pathways that modulate cell growth, survival, and inflammation [60]. The upregulation of HGS in PTL suggests its potential involvement in the inflammatory processes often associated with preterm labor. Inflammation is a crucial factor in the pathogenesis of PTL, and HGS may contribute to the amplification of inflammatory signals within the reproductive tissues, leading to premature uterine contractions and cervical changes. ATL3 is crucial for maintaining endoplasmic reticulum (ER) morphology and function. It plays a significant role in ER network formation and membrane fusion [61]. The downregulation of ATL3 in PTL indicates that disruptions in ER function might contribute to the onset of preterm labor. ER stress and the unfolded protein response have been linked to adverse pregnancy outcomes, including PTL [62]. Therefore, the decreased expression of ATL3 may impair cellular stress responses, potentially leading to cellular dysfunction and contributing to the mechanisms driving preterm labor. APOH, or Beta-2-Glycoprotein I, is involved in lipid metabolism and the regulation of blood coagulation [63]. It also possesses immunomodulatory properties. The reduced expression of APOH in PTL could suggest a disruption in the balance of coagulation and immune responses, which are critical for maintaining a healthy pregnancy. Abnormal blood coagulation and immune dysregulation have been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including PTL [64]. Therefore, the downregulation of APOH may contribute to an increased risk of thrombosis and aberrant immune responses, leading to preterm labor. GUSB is an enzyme crucial for breaking down glycosaminoglycans, which are large sugar molecules within cells [65]. Specifically, GUSB works in lysosomes to help break down these complex molecules into simpler forms that the body can either reuse or eliminate. Although GUSB’s direct role in preterm labor has not been established [66], it is critically involved in other diseases, particularly lysosomal storage disorders, inflammatory arthritis, and influencing drug toxicity during cancer treatment [67–69].

Isolation of sEVs from vaginal discharge has not been explored widely since vaginal discharge is not homogenously formed due to the nature of vaginal space, and the fluid is relatively mucoid. However, finding markers from vaginal discharge to explain obstetric complications or to predict pregnancy outcomes is the essential critical step for developing precision medicine since each woman has her unique colonization and environment in the vagina, and obtaining the sample from the vagina is highly accessible even the patient can do it by herself at home, not only in medical clinics. Despite the promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the sample size in our study was relatively small, emphasizing the need for validation in larger cohorts to ensure the reliability and applicability of our findings. Moreover, the cross-sectional design of our study restricts our ability to determine causality between altered exosomal profiles and PTL. Longitudinal studies tracking changes in exosomal markers throughout pregnancy could offer valuable insights into the predictive value of these biomarkers for PTL.

In this study, we demonstrated the feasibility of non-invasive simultaneous detection of exosomal miRNAs and proteins from maternal vaginal discharge as an effective method for identifying preterm birth at an early stage. We used a simple swab to collect vaginal discharge, which is non-invasive and localized. We then used our BEST chip to obtain enriched intact sEVs and identify the molecular signatures associated with preterm labor. Our ultimate goal is to develop a personalized precision molecular diagnostic platform to assess and manage the risk of preterm birth and significantly improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. However, although candidate exosomal miRNA and protein markers were identified through omics-based profiling, validation of individual markers using conventional assays, such as qRT-PCR and immunoblotting, will be necessary to confirm their robustness and clinical utility. Further investigation in large populations with long-term follow-up is crucial to validate these results and establish the clinical significance of exosomal biomarkers in prenatal care and obstetric practice.

Materials and methods

Non-invasive and local collection of vaginal discharge

Vaginal discharge samples were obtained from five patients with preterm labor and five healthy controls with term gestational period (mean gestational age at sampling ± standard deviation, 29.8 ± 4.09 vs. 38.4 ± 0.84, respectively), following approval by the Seoul National University Bundang Hospital Institutional Review Board (B-2312-871-301). Preterm labor was defined as regular uterine contractions with cervical change or shortening of cervical length through transvaginal ultrasound but without evidence of ruptured membranes. All patients with preterm labor were hospitalized and received tocolytic agents. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Through speculum examination, vaginal discharge samples were collected using a specimen collection swab (NobleBio, Republic of Korea) by obstetricians. They were then transferred to a transport medium (NobleBio, Republic of Korea) suitable for downstream analysis. They were stored at -80℃ for subsequent analysis.

Isolation of sEVs

Vaginal discharge underwent a 10-fold dilution in Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS, SolBio, Republic of Korea) to decrease its viscosity (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The diluted sample was centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 30 min to eliminate whole cells and cell debris [70, 71]. The resulting supernatant was then used for isolating sEVs derived from vaginal discharge using the precipitation method and Biologically intact Exosome Separation Technology (BEST) [55], as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1B and C.

BEST is a microfluidics-based EV isolation platform that enables selective separation of exosome-enriched EVs under mild flow conditions without relying on externally applied physical forces such as ultracentrifugation or high-pressure filtration. Instead, BEST exploits finely tuned hydrodynamic principles, combined with precisely engineered microchannel geometries, to continuously separate sEVs based on their transport behavior in laminar flow [72]. This design allows sEVs to be isolated without exposure to harsh mechanical stress or chemical precipitants, thereby minimizing vesicle deformation, disruption of membrane integrity, and loss or alteration of surface-associated proteins.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

NTA measurements were conducted with the NanoSight LM10 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). Before each measurement, the sample chamber was thoroughly cleaned with ethanol to ensure optimal analysis conditions. Due to the requirement of the NTA system for samples to fall within a concentration range of 106 to 109 vesicles per mL, all samples were subjected to a 1000-fold dilution to meet this specified concentration range. The sample was injected into the chamber using a syringe until the liquid overflowed from the opposite side. A thermal sensor facilitated temperature monitoring during each measurement to account for potential variations. NanoSight NTA software (Ver. 3.4 build 3.4.4) was employed for accurate data recording and analysis, enabling precise measurements of vesicle concentration and size distribution. Each sample was measured three times, lasting 15 s, and the sample chamber was meticulously cleaned with clean deionized water between each experiment to prevent any potential cross-contamination.

Protein measurement

Samples were mixed with RIPA lysis buffer containing a 1x protease inhibitor cocktail and incubated for 10 min at 4℃. Subsequently, the lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4℃ to remove unlysed debris. Protein supernatants were collected and used for a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay to determine protein concentration. The assays were conducted following the manufacturer’s protocols.

RNA extraction

Following the manufacturer’s protocol, exosomal RNA was extracted using TRIzol LS reagent (Ambion, Life Technology, USA). The quality of the RNA was evaluated using the RNA 6000 Pico Chip on an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Netherlands), while quantification was conducted utilizing the NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Library preparation and sequencing

Library construction was conducted using the NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep kit (New England BioLabs, Inc., USA), following the manufacturer’s guidelines for both control and test RNAs. The process involved ligating adaptors to total RNA samples and synthesizing cDNA with adaptor-specific primers and reverse transcriptase. PCR was utilized for library amplification, followed by cleanup using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Inc., Germany) and polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gel. The yield and size distribution of the small RNA libraries were evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument with the High-sensitivity DNA Assay (Agilent Technologies, Inc., USA). High-throughput sequencing was performed using the NextSeq550 system with single-end 75 sequencing (Illumina, USA).

RNA-seq data analysis

Sequence reads were aligned using the bowtie2 software to generate a bam file, with mature miRNA sequences as the mapping reference. Bedtools v2.25.0 [73] and Bioconductor [74], implemented in the R statistical programming language [75], were used to retrieve read counts mapped to mature miRNA sequences from the alignment file. These read counts were then used to assess miRNA expression levels. For comparison between samples, the CPM + TMM normalization method was used. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment were performed by DIANA [76]. All numerical graphs were generated using Origin software, Mircosoft Excel, and SRplot [77].

Sample preparation for LC-MS/MS

For LC-MS/MS analysis, each 10 µg of proteins was reduced and alkylated using a Mini MS Sample Prep Kit (#A40006, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins were digested with 0.2 µg of Trypsin/Lys-C Protease Mix (#A40009, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 37 °C for 16 h, and peptides were desalted with a peptide clean-up column.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Proteome analysis was performed using the Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with the EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). An autosampler was used to load 10 µL aliquots of the peptide solutions into a Trap column (PepMap 100 C18 3 μm 75 μm x 2 cm nanoViper, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and EASY-Spray column (15 cm x 50 μm PepMap RSLC C18 2 μm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The peptides were separated with a linear gradient from 5% to 40% mobile phase B (100% water (A) and 80% acetonitrile (ACN) (B), and each contained 0.1% formic acid) at 300 nL/min, and injected for MS analysis. MS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap at 120,000 resolution, with a scan range of 400–1,600 m/z. Precursor ions with charges of + 2 to + 8 were isolated for MS/MS sequencing. MS/MS scans were acquired in the Ion Trap using higher energy collision-induced dissociation (HCD) fragmentation with a normalized collision energy of 33% and the first mass set to 120 m/z.

Protein identification and label-free quantification

The identification of proteins was performed with Proteome Discoverer 2.4 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a SEQUEST HT search engine using the UniProt Homo Sapiens (TaxID = 9606) database. A precursor mass tolerance of 25 ppm, fragment mass tolerance of 0.8 Da, and maximum missed cleavage of 2 were set. Oxidation (methionine, + 15.995 Da) and carbamidomethyl (cysteine, + 57.021 Da) were set as dynamic and static modification, respectively. Abundances were based on area and normalized based on total peptide amount. For proteomics analysis, false discovery rate (FDR) control was applied using a target–decoy strategy implemented in Proteome Discoverer. Identification-level validation was performed based on q-values at the PSM, peptide, and protein levels, applying strict (1%) and relaxed (5%) FDR thresholds.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel and OriginPro 2024b (OriginLab, v.10.1.5.132), wherein descriptive statistics, including means and standard errors (S.E.), were computed for the dataset. The comparison of variables between two groups—namely, healthy controls with term birth (n = 5) and patients with preterm labor (n = 5)— was conducted using the independent two-tailed t-test with a significance level of α = 0.05. Additionally, within each group, a paired t-test was employed to assess differences in variables. This combined use of independent and paired t-tests facilitated a comprehensive examination of inter-group and intra-group variations, ensuring a thorough analysis of the collected data.
