Unexpected [18F]-fluciclovine hyperuptake in radiation necrosis after proton therapy: illustrative case
Authors: Kazuaki Kamata, Seisuke Tanigawa, Yasuhiro Takeuchi, Takanari Okamoto, Ichita Taniyama, Hayato Takeuchi, Takumi Yamanaka, Norihiro Aibe, Sachimi Yamada, Kei Yamada, Naoya Hashimoto

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND[18F]-Fluciclovine PET is increasingly used for visualizing malignant gliomas and delineating tumor infiltration. More recently, its utility has been reported in differentiating tumor recurrence from radiation necrosis (RN). Amino acid PET tracers exploit the upregulated amino acid transport mechanisms in tumor cells and have been widely used in neuro-oncology to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Established tracers such as [11C]-methionine, [18F]-fluoroethyl-l-tyrosine, and [18F]-fluorodopa have demonstrated utility in the evaluation of malignant gliomas, particularly in differentiating tumor recurrence from treatment-related changes.1 Besides these traditional tracers, [18F]-fluciclovine (anti-1-amino-3-[18F]-fluorocyclobutane-1-carboxylic acid, also known as [18F]-FACBC) positron emission tomography (PET) has been increasingly adopted for the visualization of malignant gliomas and the assessment of tumor infiltration. In Japan, [18F]-fluciclovine received regulatory approval for tumor visualization in suspected newly diagnosed malignant gliomas and has been covered by national health insurance since 2024. This recent commercial availability is expected to lead to its widespread clinical adoption. Its favorable pharmacokinetic properties, including low physiological uptake in normal brain parenchyma, result in high lesion-to-background contrast, enabling both visual interpretation and quantitative assessment of tracer uptake in intracranial lesions. Recent studies indicate that [18F]-fluciclovine offers diagnostic performance comparable to that of the aforementioned established amino acid PET tracers. Furthermore, accumulating evidence has suggested the usefulness of [18F]-fluciclovine PET in distinguishing tumor recurrence from radiation necrosis (RN), a well-recognized and diagnostically challenging complication following radiotherapy.2 However, most investigations have focused on lesions after surgery or radiation therapy, and the uptake characteristics of [18F]-fluciclovine PET in newly identified intracranial contrast-enhancing lesions remain unclear.

Here, we report a case in which a newly developing intracranial lesion demonstrated glioblastoma-mimicking imaging progression and marked [18F]-fluciclovine uptake after proton therapy for ethmoid sinus carcinoma. Although preoperative imaging strongly suggested a de novo high-grade glioma, histopathological and molecular analyses of the resected specimen confirmed RN. We present the imaging findings of this case and discuss their implications in the context of the existing literature.

Illustrative Case

The patient was a 57-year-old man with a history of right ethmoid sinus carcinoma (T4aN0M0, squamous cell carcinoma) diagnosed via biopsy. He initially received two cycles of PCE (paclitaxel, carboplatin, and cetuximab) chemotherapy, followed by concurrent proton therapy and cisplatin. A total proton dose of 70 Gy (relative biological effectiveness [RBE]) was delivered in 35 fractions (constant RBE 1.1) to the primary tumor involving the bilateral nasal cavities, right orbit, and anterior skull base. Approximately 1 year after completing proton therapy, he underwent neck dissection for a right cervical lymph node metastasis. Since then, the primary tumor had remained in remission with no evidence of further recurrence. Four years 6 months after completing proton therapy, surveillance MRI revealed a new 1-cm contrast-enhancing lesion in the left frontal lobe with associated vasogenic edema on T2-weighted imaging (Fig. 1A–C). According to the radiotherapy treatment plan, the enhancing lesion was located within the intracranial dose distribution of the proton therapy, and RN was therefore considered. The left frontal lobe lesion was, however, situated in a relatively lower-dose region compared with the right frontal lobe, prompting consideration of a de novo primary brain tumor in addition to RN. Although the patient remained asymptomatic, a follow-up MRI study obtained 3.7 months after referral demonstrated rapid progressive enlargement of both the enhancing lesion and the surrounding edema (Fig. 1D–F), which heightened suspicion for a high-grade glioma. To further characterize the lesion, early-phase [18F]-fluciclovine PET was performed 15 minutes after tracer administration. The study showed uptake corresponding to the enhancing region, with semiquantitative values of the standardized uptake value (SUVmax 2.96, SUVmean 1.68, and SUVpeak 2.16) (Fig. 2). The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was markedly elevated at 22.77. The background region of interest was placed in contralateral normal frontal white matter, avoiding edema, gliosis, vascular structures, and potential areas of prior radiation exposure, and was selected for its homogeneous appearance on MRI. Despite the absence of overt focal neurological deficits preoperatively, the rapid radiographic progression and elevated fluciclovine uptake raised concern for a high-grade glioma, and resection was performed via a bifrontal craniotomy through a bicoronal skin incision. Intraoperatively, clear 5-ALA fluorescence was observed within the lesion (Fig. 3A and B). Ex vivo evaluation also confirmed the presence of fluorescence in the resected specimen (Fig. 3C), further reinforcing the preoperative suspicion of a high-grade glioma. Despite the intraoperative 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) fluorescence suggesting a neoplastic process, the histopathological examination revealed coagulative necrosis and foamy macrophages, without microvascular proliferation or palisading necrosis, and immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated only sparse MIB-1–positive cells (Fig. 4). Molecular testing showed no alterations associated with glioma, including mutations of IDH or TERT promoter. Collectively, these histopathological and molecular findings established a final diagnosis of RN. The patient remains under close radiological surveillance; follow-up imaging at 3 months postoperatively showed no evidence of residual or newly developed necrotic lesions. Discussion

Observations

Amino acid transport is upregulated in brain tumors to support rapid cellular proliferation and metabolic demands. Tumor cells rely on amino acids such as glutamine for energy production, nucleotide synthesis, and redox homeostasis, resulting in increased expression of transporters including ASCT2 and LAT1.3,4 [18F]-Fluciclovine is a synthetic amino acid analog transported primarily via these systems, and its uptake reflects enhanced amino acid transport activity rather than intracellular metabolism. [18F]-fluciclovine exploits this mechanism and offers high lesion-to-background contrast due to its low physiological uptake in normal brain parenchyma.1,5–7 While recurrent tumors often demonstrate increased uptake via LAT1 overexpression, RN represents a complex microenvironment of vascular injury, blood-brain barrier disruption, and inflammatory cell infiltration.8–11 These processes, along with potential ASCT2-mediated transport in nonneoplastic tissue, may alter local amino acid dynamics and lead to tracer accumulation even in the absence of viable tumor cells.

Clinical studies have evaluated [18F]-fluciclovine PET for distinguishing tumor recurrence from RN, primarily using quantitative metrics such as SUV and TBR.12–14 Previous reports have proposed various SUVmax thresholds for identifying tumor progression, ranging from 1.3 in stereotactic radiosurgery–treated metastases to 4.66 in glioblastoma cases.12–14 While SUV reflects normalized absolute tracer accumulation, TBR emphasizes lesion-to-background contrast. However, the optimal thresholds for differentiating de novo lesions remain underinvestigated, particularly given that [18F]-fluciclovine’s high contrast may influence these relative metrics.

In our case, while the SUVmax (2.96) exceeded some previously proposed thresholds for recurrent metastatic tumor,12 it fell within the reported range for RN (1.2–4.3), highlighting the limitations of SUV-based interpretation.14 Given these inconclusive findings, we evaluated the TBR. Notably, the TBR in the present case (22.77) was essentially comparable to the median TBR (22.68) of our glioblastoma cohort (Table 1). However, this deceptively high TBR was primarily driven by the remarkably low physiological uptake of [18F]-fluciclovine in normal brain parenchyma, rather than high absolute tracer accumulation. Because this low background significantly reduces the denominator in ratio calculations, even moderate SUV values can yield disproportionately inflated TBRs that mimic high-grade glioma. This diagnostic pitfall underscores the need for caution when relying on relative metrics in the postradiation setting. In cases in which TBR is markedly elevated but SUV demonstrates only moderate uptake, clinicians should be cautious in interpreting these findings as definitive evidence of tumor activity. Such discrepancies may reflect disproportionately elevated relative uptake due to low background signal rather than true hypermetabolism. Therefore, instead of proceeding directly to surgical intervention, it is important to incorporate additional clinical and radiological factors, including symptom progression and the rate of radiographic change, into the decision-making process. In selected cases, careful observation with close imaging follow-up may represent a reasonable alternative. In contrast, in the present case, the lesion demonstrated rapid radiographic progression, with a 21.2-fold increase in volume over 112 days (from 555.19 to 11,780 mm3) and an estimated volume doubling time of 25.4 days. This growth rate is notably faster than the reported median doubling times for glioblastoma (31–49.6 days).15,16 Although RN typically follows a more protracted or fluctuating course,17–19 the rapid radiographic progression strongly raised concern for a high-grade glioma, ultimately leading to surgical intervention. Intraoperatively, the lesion exhibited 5-ALA fluorescence, which further mimicked a high-grade glioma. It is important to note, however, that RN has been reported to exhibit false-positive fluorescence due to blood-brain barrier disruption and associated inflammatory changes.20 Histopathological analysis ultimately confirmed RN. This discrepancy highlights the inherent difficulty of clinical decision-making in such cases and emphasizes that even aggressive growth kinetics and intraoperative fluorescence are not specific for malignancy.

Interestingly, RN developed in the left frontal lobe despite it receiving a lower physical dose than the right frontal region. In proton therapy, biological effects can be influenced by increases in RBE and linear energy transfer (LET) toward the distal edge of the beam.21–23 While the lack of precise LET quantification in our treatment planning system precludes definitive conclusions, this case suggests that LET-dependent effects or individual vascular vulnerability may contribute to asymmetric RN development. These factors underscore the difficulty in predicting radiation-induced injury based on physical dose distribution alone in proton therapy.

The present case is distinct from prior reports of amino acid PET imaging in RN in several important respects. First, the lesion developed as delayed intracranial RN following proton therapy for a head and neck malignancy rather than treatment of a primary intracranial tumor. Second, although [18F]-fluciclovine PET demonstrated only moderate SUV, the TBR was markedly elevated due to extremely low physiological brain uptake, thereby closely mimicking high-grade glioma. This highlights a clinically important interpretive pitfall that has not been widely emphasized in prior studies of amino acid PET imaging.2,12–14 Third, the lesion exhibited rapid radiographic progression and intraoperative 5-ALA fluorescence, further reinforcing the false impression of a malignant neoplasm.

In conclusion, newly detected intracranial enhancing lesions require cautious interpretation of [18F]-fluciclovine metrics, as a high TBR does not exclude RN. In particular, cases demonstrating discordance between moderate SUV and markedly elevated TBR should prompt careful consideration of this potential pitfall. Accurate clinical decision-making requires integration of absolute SUV values, longitudinal imaging changes, clinical symptoms, and radiotherapy dose distribution to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary surgical intervention.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that [18F]-fluciclovine uptake is not specific to tumor tissue. Markedly increased relative uptake, including a high TBR, can occur in intracranial RN developing as a delayed effect of prior radiotherapy, even in the absence of brain targeted treatment. Awareness of this diagnostic pitfall is important, as lesions demonstrating discordance between moderate SUV and markedly elevated TBR may not represent true tumor activity. In such cases, careful interpretation should be based on comprehensive assessment of the clinical course, longitudinal imaging changes, and treatment history.
