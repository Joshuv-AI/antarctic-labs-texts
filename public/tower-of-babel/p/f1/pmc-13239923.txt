Introduction

The LITESPARK‐005 trial showed that belzutifan significantly improved progression‐free survival and objective response rates in patients with advanced clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) who had previously received immune checkpoint inhibitors and antiangiogenic therapies [1]. The European Association of Urology guidelines consistently include belzutifan as an alternative to everolimus in patients who are intolerant or progress after immunotherapy and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR)‐targeted therapy [2].

Current evidence for belzutifan is largely confined to clear cell RCC, and its efficacy in non‐clear cell RCC has not yet been established. TFE3‐rearranged RCC is a rare and frequently aggressive histological type for which published research in the metastatic setting is limited to VEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors [3] and mechanistic targets of mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors [4].

Herein, we describe a case of metastatic TFE3‐rearranged RCC treated with fourth‐line belzutifan therapy, resulting in improvement in respiratory symptoms and radiographic reduction in pulmonary metastases.

Case Presentation

A 33‐year‐old Japanese woman presented with gross hematuria. She had no notable medical history and was not taking any regular medications. Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed a 10‐cm left renal mass and multiple pulmonary nodules of up to 12 mm, suggestive of renal cancer with lung metastases. The disease was staged as cT2bN0M1 (Stage IV), and the patient was classified as intermediate‐risk, according to the International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium criteria [5]. She underwent an open left nephrectomy.

Histological examination revealed a predominantly alveolar‐to‐papillary architecture with proliferating tumor cells with weakly eosinophilic cytoplasm and prominent nucleoli. The tumor extended beyond the renal capsule and invaded the renal pelvis. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated nuclear TFE3 positivity, and the tumor was diagnosed as a TFE3‐rearranged RCC (Figure 1).

(B) Tumor extension beyond the renal capsule. (C) Tumor invasion into the renal pelvis. (D) Immunohistochemistry demonstrating nuclear TFE3 positivity.

The patient received postoperative lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab as first‐line therapy for residual pulmonary metastases. However, 6 months later, CT revealed mediastinal nodal metastases and local recurrence in the surgical bed, indicating progressive disease. Second‐line treatment with cabozantinib was administered. Three months later, CT revealed disease progression with > 10 pulmonary nodules, although the mediastinal nodal lesions and local recurrence remained stable (Figure 2A–C); at that time, third‐line axitinib was administered. During treatment, cough, C‐reactive protein, and Krebs von den Lungen‐6 levels increased, and chest radiography revealed a progression of pulmonary nodules along with increased opacities in the lower lung fields (Figure 2D). These observations raised suspicion of an increasing metastatic burden with carcinomatous lymphangitis or drug‐induced interstitial lung disease (ILD).

(B) Abdominal CT showing local recurrence in the surgical bed. (C) Chest CT showing mediastinal lymph node metastasis. (D) Disease progression during axitinib treatment. Chest radiography performed 2 months after axitinib administration revealing increased nodular lesions and bilateral infiltrative opacities. (E–G) Response 1 month after belzutifan therapy. Belzutifan (120 mg once daily) was initiated as fourth‐line therapy. Owing to clinical concerns regarding rapid disease progression, the treatment was initiated without prior discontinuation of axitinib or administration of corticosteroids. In addition, darbepoetin alfa was administered concurrently to preemptively manage the anticipated treatment‐induced anemia. The cough rapidly improved, and the elevated C‐reactive protein levels promptly declined, reaching normal levels by week five (Figure 3).

Association between belzutifan‐based treatment and longitudinal changes in hemoglobin, C‐reactive protein, and Krebs von den Lungen‐6 (KL‐6). Time courses of hemoglobin, C‐reactive protein, and KL‐6 following treatment with belzutifan. The X‐axis shows the duration of belzutifan and darbepoetin alfa treatments.

After 1 month (Figure 2E–G), CT showed no substantial changes in the size of the lymph node metastases or local recurrence. However, the pulmonary metastases decreased in both number and size. Krebs von den Lungen‐6 levels had also decreased and were within the normal range by week nine. After 3 months of treatment, chest radiography demonstrated improvement in the bilateral infiltrative opacities and nodular lesions (Figure 2H). No grade ≥ 3 adverse events attributable to belzutifan use were observed. Although the patient developed grade 2 lower‐extremity edema, it was managed by reducing the darbepoetin alfa from 360 μg to 40 μg. Anemia remained well controlled throughout the treatment period.

Discussion

In the present case, fourth‐line belzutifan was associated with symptomatic improvement and tumor shrinkage. Although distinguishing between carcinomatous lymphangitis and drug‐induced ILD remains challenging, clinical improvement was observed without corticosteroids or antibiotics, suggesting the potential contribution of belzutifan.

Belzutifan is an inhibitor of hypoxia‐inducible factor 2α (HIF‐2α). The rationale for using belzutifan in this case was based on its established mechanism in von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease, in which loss of VHL protein function leads to constitutive activation of the HIF pathway [6]. This mechanism, also relevant to > 80% of sporadic clear cell RCC cases [7], presents specific management challenges. Because belzutifan‐induced HIF‐2α inhibition can suppress erythropoietin and create a risk of anemia following nephrectomy, we administered darbepoetin alfa preemptively. This approach was associated with no grade ≥ 3 adverse events and allowed for the continuation of full‐dose therapy.

Evidence of TFE3‐rearranged RCC remains limited, and an optimal standard systemic treatment has not yet been established. TFE3‐rearranged and TFEB‐altered RCCs are rare, often aggressive, and tend to affect younger patients [8]. In advanced disease, VEGFR‐tyrosine kinase inhibitors [3] and mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors [4] have been used with varied outcomes. TFE3‐rearranged RCC has a molecular background distinct from that of VHL‐inactivated clear cell RCC. The Proline‐Rich Coiled‐Coil‐TFE3 fusion protein upregulates HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α, although tumor development may depend more on HIF‐1α via metabolic reprogramming [9]. Prior research suggests that HIF‐2α–related pathways could contribute to tumor behavior in TFE3‐rearranged RCC. Although the underlying mechanism warrants further molecular investigation, our findings support further exploration of belzutifan treatment in this rare subtype with limited treatment options. Prospective multi‐institutional studies are needed to determine the efficacy and safety of HIF‐2α inhibition in non‐clear cell RCC.

This study had some limitations. First, per the fifth World Health Organization classification [10], the tumor was diagnosed as TFE3‐rearranged RCC solely by immunohistochemistry and was not confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization or RNA‐based assays. Second, a baseline CT scan was not performed immediately before the initiation of belzutifan therapy. Third, the short follow‐up period precluded assessment of response durability and survival impact. Fourth, mixed pulmonary findings precluded definitive differentiation between carcinomatous lymphangitis and other etiologies such as ILD. Finally, improvement in cough was assessed subjectively and not quantified using a standardized symptom score.

Conclusion

Fourth‐line belzutifan therapy in a patient with TFE3‐rearranged RCC led to radiographic regression of pulmonary metastases and symptomatic improvement, while nodal and local lesions remained stable. This suggests its therapeutic potential for this rare disease.
