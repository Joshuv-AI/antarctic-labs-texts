Phytogenic synthesis of copper nanoparticles by using leaf extract of Eucalyptus globulus and its antifungal activity against Fusarium oxysporum and Fusarium Solani
Authors: Nazar Abbas, Shakil Ahmed, Madeeha Ansari, Saud Alamri, Alanoud T Alfagham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhytogenic synthesis of nanoparticles with plant extracts offers a novel approach to nanotechnology and provides prospects for their applications in medicine, agriculture, or environmental science. However, their antifungal activities have been least explored hitherto. The CuNPs were synthesized using bioactive molecules present in Eucalyptus globulus L. leaf extract, and their antifungal activity against selected Fusarium species was tested. Copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) were synthesized by mixing plant leaf extract and CuSO4 with a 1:1 ratio. They had acquired attention due to their characteristic absorbance peak at 580 nm, confirming CuNPs characterized through UV-VIS spectroscopy.ResultsAnalysis of SEM affirmed the shape of CuNPs as square and triangular with a particle size of 13–88 nm. EDX analysis revealed a high level of purity, emphasizing the presence of Copper (Cu), Oxygen (O), and Carbon (C) atoms. FTIR spectroscopy revealed significant phytochemical bands, highlighting the nanoparticle’s capacity to diminish Cu ions, which encompass polyphenols (3738.05 cm−1), surface hydroxyl groups (3419.79 cm−1), and C-OH stretching (1529.85 cm−1). The antifungal activity of copper nanoparticles was evaluated in vitro against Fusarium oxysporum and Fusarium solani. Various concentrations of copper nanoparticles (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 ppm) were tested. Results indicated that the inhibition rate increased as the concentration of copper nanoparticles rose from 20 ppm to 100 ppm when cultured on media of Malt Extract Agar (MEA). At the highest concentration (100 ppm), the inhibition rates were 94.89% for F. solani and 80.12% for F. oxysporum. Notably, the media containing F. solani exhibited a higher inhibition rate at 100 ppm compared to the media containing F. oxysporum, demonstrating the effectiveness of CuNPs in treating infectious diseases caused by the fungus pathogens under examination.ConclusionThe synthesized CuNPs can be an effective environmentally friendly method currently available for fungicides. This study suggests the search for effective innovative NPs synthesis and their application in addressing various urgent problems of agriculture and environmental management.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Copper nanoparticles, Plant metabolites, Fusarium spp., Biocontrol

Introduction

Fungal diseases pose a significant threat to agricultural production and necessitate the development of advanced antifungal agents. In the meantime, synthetic fungicide overuse has resulted in resistant fungal strains, which cause environmental contamination and expose humans and animals to health hazards [1, 2]. The genus Fusarium represents a group of filamentous fungi responsible for a wide array of diseases in several important crops worldwide. It exhibits a great threat to the health, productivity, and quality of crops since Fusarium infections and can lead to high yield loss, deleterious effects on the post-harvest product, and food contamination. Consequently, crop failure caused by this pathogen affects economic stability, food safety, and human health [3, 4]. Fusarium species show a high degree of genetic variation, possess adaptability features, and can produce mycotoxins, which make them difficult to control [5]. The current disease management strategies have failed, so a relatively advanced mode of action is urgently required in crop protection through synthesized CuNPs [6].

Of the available advanced strategies, nanoparticles have found wide application in agriculture because of their unique characteristics such as high surface area-to-volume ratio, controllable surface chemistry, and extreme reactivity. While these technologies improve efficiency, they may also pose risks by potentially amplifying the effects of harmful substances if not managed responsibly [7]. They may be used for delivering harmful agrochemicals, preventing or decreasing the loss of nutrient element uptake along with water retention, and providing antimicrobial and antifungal properties to crops [8]. All these innovative approaches may guarantee a shift to new balanced and sustainable crop protection systems [9]. Among various metallic nanoparticles, copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) are the most important studied nanoparticles than all other metallic nanoparticles [10]. Special attention is given to copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) because of their unique properties, including optical characteristics, cost-effectiveness, thermal conductivity, mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, and catalytic capabilities, distinguishing them from their source metal [11, 12]. Copper nanoparticles exhibit unique phytochemical characteristics that make them beneficial in different agricultural fields. These particles function as potent antimicrobial and antifungal agents because of their high surface area-to-volume ratio and enhanced reactivity [13].

CuNPs target a wide range of pathogens and protect crops due to their ability to damage internal cellular membranes and enzymatic processes, such as DNA replication and transcription, electron transfer, and protein transport [14, 15]. CuNPs are stable and resistant to degradation, remain effective for a long time in different environments [16], and can be used in almost any agricultural practice, such as soil treatment, nutrient production, crop growth promotion, targeted delivery of agrochemicals and micronutrients, and resource optimization [17]. They also prevent soil-borne diseases, promote seed germination, and ensure crops are resistant to droughts, salinity, and other abiotic stressors, through multiple mechanisms of action such as disruption of cell membrane integrity, generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), interference with cellular processes, and induction of oxidative stress [18]. CuNPs inflict damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, disrupting cellular homeostasis and reducing fungal viability [19]. Additionally, CuNPs increase membrane porosity, impairing ion transport and nutrient uptake, further compromising fungal cell integrity [20].

Eco-friendly synthesis of nanoparticles is a sustainable option for their production for both industrial and agricultural applications. In this regard, phytogenic synthesis is considered a sustainable and eco-friendly method of nanoparticle fabrication, which has multiple benefits compared to conventional ones such as chemical and biological methods. Phytogenic synthesis involving plant extracts represents an emerging field within nanotechnology and biotechnology to provide ecologically compatible and sustainable manufacturing of nanoparticles [21]. Plant extracts contain a variety of phytochemicals, such as flavonoids, terpenoids, and polyphenols, which exhibit reducing and stabilizing powers when placed in synthesizing metal nanoparticles [22]. The extracted compounds are feasible substitutes for harsh and hazardous chemical reagents used in the conventional synthesis process. These entities become involved in synthesis by facilitating ion reduction and serving as capping agents during nucleation and particle formation processes [23]. Phytochemicals contained in plant extract also render the nanoparticles biocompatible and stable while granting unique properties and therapeutic values [24].

The leaf extract of Eucalyptus globulus is perfect for phytogenic synthesis because its phytochemicals are natural reducing agents that facilitate the conversion of metal ions into their nanoparticle forms while the chemicals stabilize the nanoparticles and prevent them from agglomeration by forming a protective cover [25]. Moreover, E. globulus leaf extract is compatible with nanoparticle size and composition control. Being biocompatible and environmentally friendly, the use of E. globulus leaf extract for medical, agricultural, and environmental green innovations is one of the most prospective ways of their development [26]. The potential of phyto-synthesized nanoparticles in agricultural practices. Despite initial excitement over their biocompatibility and eco-friendly nature, practical challenges emerged, such as inconsistent synthesis protocols and limited understanding of long-term impacts on soil health and plant growth [27]. This study highlights the need for further research and development to address the limitations of Phyto-synthesized nanoparticles and enhance their applicability in real-world scenarios.

Although there are more and more considerations of advantages that nanoparticles might bring to agriculture, some issues like potential impact on the environment, bioavailability, or soil health still matter for comprehensive research. Previous research has focused on ZnO nanoparticles and their efficacy against fungal infections [28]. However, there is a lack of information on how Eucalyptus globulus derived CuNPs affect and control fungal infections. Therefore, we hypothesized that capping agents increase the efficacy of reduced CuNPs against antifungal infection. The specific objectives of this study were (i) phytogenic synthesis of copper nanoparticles by using Eucalyptus globulus leaf extract, and their characterization, (ii) assessing their antifungal effect on Fusarium pathogens. The novelty of this research lies in its focus on the long-term interaction between phytogenic copper nanoparticles and fungi, assessed through an extended incubation period, which differs from short-term evaluations typically used for other purposes. This approach provides unique insights into the stability and efficacy of nanoparticles, contributing to developing innovative, eco-friendly strategies for crop protection and food security in modern agriculture.

Materials and methods

The green synthesis, antifungal, and characterization of copper nanoparticles was done in the Applied Environmental Biology Research Laboratory, Department of Botany, University of Punjab, Lahore. Eucalyptus globulus Labill. leaf extract and CuSO4.5H2O were used to synthesize copper nanoparticles by the green synthesis method. Characterization of the synthesized nanoparticles was done using scanning electron microscopy, UV-visible spectroscopy, and particle size analysis. All of the reagents used in the study were of laboratory grade. All the study’s reagents, including CuSO4.5H2O, were procured from Merck Co., Germany, and Sigma Aldrich Chemical Co., USA.

Phytogenic synthesis of copper nanoparticles

Eucalyptus globulus L. leaves were collected from the research area Botanical Garden, Institute of Botany, University of the Punjab Lahore Pakistan. The Botanical Garden is part of the institute and many identified plant specimens are growing in this area. The collected leaves were protected using polythene bags. Once collected, the leaves were washed thoroughly with tap water and left to air dry in a shaded area at room temperature for several days. Under hygienic conditions, the dried leaves were ground into a fine powder using an electric blender. The powdered leaves were then stored in an airtight container for future use. To prepare the plant leaf extract, 10 g of the Eucalyptus globulus Labill leaf powder were mixed with 100 milliliters of deionized water and boiled at 80 °C for 20 min. The mixture was filtered using Whatman filter paper No.1, following the method [29]. Additionally, a 1 mM copper sulfate (CuSO4) solution was prepared by dissolving 0.05 g of CuSO4·5H2O in 200 milliliters of deionized water. This solution was stored in a beaker and kept in a refrigerator for subsequent experiments. To synthesize copper nanoparticles, 10 ml of leaf extract was combined with 10 ml of a pre-prepared copper sulphate reagent in a vial, which was then left overnight at ambient temperature. The formation of nanoparticles was initially indicated by a color change in the solution.

Copper nanoparticles characterization

To confirm the synthesis of CuNPs, the solution of the resultant was analyzed using a UV-vis spectrophotometer (BMS: UV-2800 Hitachi, Japan) over a range between 300 and 800 nm. For this purpose, the synthesized solution of copper nanoparticles was transferred to the Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm/4° for 10 min. The resulting pellets were resuspended in deionized water using a vortex mixer and then subjected to centrifugation three more times under the same conditions to purify the nanoparticles. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) determined the functional groups of the copper nanoparticles. The infrared spectroscopy method was used to obtain the spectrum. The description of functional groups present on the copper NPs was studied at the spectrum range of 4000–400 cm−1 in this technique. The sample was prepared by dissolving copper NPs, prepared at optimized conditions, uniformly in a dry medium of KBr. A transparent disc was formed by compressing the sample. The sample was dried and subjected to FTIR (Shimadzu: IRTracer-100, USA) analysis. To determine the crystal structure of synthesized pure CuNPs, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using dried samples. The sample drop was taken on a carbon-coated grid to prepare the sample film. The extra solution was wiped off. This film was allowed to dry and observed on the SEM (Apreo S, Thermo Scientific, USA) grid. Scanning electron microscopy on various magnifications was conducted to determine the size and shape of the CuNPs. An energy dispersive x-ray (EDX) study was also done to measure the study elemental composition of synthesized CuNPs.

In vitro antifungal activity of phytogenic copper nanoparticles

Pure cultures of Fusarium oxysporum Schlecht. and Fusarium solani Mart. were obtained from the Fungal Culture Bank of Pakistan at the Institute of Agricultural Sciences (IAGS), University of the Punjab, Lahore, Pakistan. Malt Extract Agar (MEA) medium, sourced from Sigma-Aldrich, was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions and dispensed into sterile Petri plates under aseptic conditions in a laminar airflow cabinet. The medium was inoculated with pure colonies of each Fusarium species using sterilized inoculating needles. The inoculated plates were incubated at room temperature to facilitate the complete growth of the fungal cultures.

To prepare copper nanoparticle (CuNP) solutions at concentrations of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 ppm, a 1000 ppm stock solution was prepared by dissolving 1 g of CuNPs in 1 L of deionized water, with dispersion ensured using sonication. The stock solution was diluted with deionized water to achieve the desired concentrations. For 20 ppm, 2 mL of stock solution was mixed with 98 mL of water; for 40 ppm, 4 mL of stock solution was mixed with 96 mL of water; for 60 ppm, 6 mL of stock solution was mixed with 94 mL of water; for 80 ppm, 8 mL of stock solution was mixed with 92 mL of water; and for 100 ppm, 10 mL of stock solution was mixed with 90 mL of water. Each solution was thoroughly mixed to ensure uniformity to assess their antifungal activities against F. oxysporum and F. solani. The prepared media was dispensed into Petri plates under the laminar airflow’s fluorescent light, and 5 mL of each CuNP concentration (20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 ppm) was added to the respective plates. Control plates received no treatment, while one positive control received 5 mL of a plant extract and another positive control received 5 mL of a CuSO4 solution. A standard fungicide was also applied. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 48 h.

Afterward, equal-sized agar blocks containing fungal mycelium were used to inoculate each Petri dish. Following, the Petri dishes were incubated under normal conditions for 23 days. The radial growth of Fusarium species was measured in each Petri plate by observing the colony size of each fungus treated with the different CuNP concentrations. Analysis of data

The data that was received during the experiment was analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance. To conduct a statistical analysis of the antifungal ability of copper nanoparticles, Duncan’s multiple range test was employed at 0.05% significance. Statistical data were analyzed using Statistics 8.1.

Antifungal activity against Fusarium Solani

The control treatment demonstrated the highest colony growth of F. solani. In contrast, the treatment with plant extract resulted in a slight inhibition, with a percentage inhibition rate of 5.42%. The copper sulfate solution exhibited a higher inhibition rate of 30.72%. Among the treatments, copper sulphate solution proved to be more effective in inhibiting the growth of F. solani compared to the plant extract.

The inhibition rates varied with different concentrations of copper nanoparticles (CuNPs). At 20 ppm and 40 ppm, the inhibition rates were 69.61% and 80.50%, respectively, with the 40 ppm treatment being more effective than the 20 ppm treatment. Similarly, the inhibition rates for 60 ppm and 80 ppm concentrations were 77.26% and 88.61%, respectively, showing greater effectiveness at higher concentrations. The highest inhibition rate of 94.89% was observed at 100 ppm. Antifungal activity against Fusarium oxysporum

The plant extract solution exhibited an inhibition rate of 5.42%, while the copper sulfate solution had a significantly higher inhibition rate of 30.72%. Copper sulfate’s inhibition rate surpassed that of the plant extract solution. When evaluating the copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) solution, the inhibition rate was 30.52% at a 20 ppm concentration, increasing to 48.69% at a 40 ppm concentration. The inhibition effect of the CuNPs solution on the F. oxysporum colony was more pronounced at 40 ppm compared to 20 ppm. Furthermore, the inhibition rates for CuNPs at 60 ppm and 80 ppm were 59.33% and 65.46%, respectively, with 80 ppm showing greater efficacy than 60 ppm. At a 100 ppm concentration, CuNPs achieved the highest inhibition rate of 80.12%. This data indicates that the concentration of copper nanoparticles increased from 20 ppm to 100 ppm, progressively enhancing the inhibition growth rate of the F. oxysporum colony, as illustrated in Fig. 7.

Comparison between F. solani and F. oxysporum of growth Inhibition

The percentage inhibition rates against the phytopathogenic fungi Fusarium oxysporum Schelet and Fusarium solani Mart. were observed by applying various concentrations of copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) solutions, specifically 20 ppm, 40 ppm, 60 ppm, 80 ppm, and 100 ppm, to media containing these fungi. At a concentration of 100 ppm, the inhibition rates were 94.89% for F. solani and 80.12% for F. oxysporum. The lowest inhibition rate occurred at 20 ppm for both fungi. At 80 ppm, the inhibition rates were 88.61% for F. solani and 65.46% for F. oxysporum. Notably, the inhibition rates decreased when the concentration dropped from 60 ppm to 40 ppm in both fungi. Additionally, both plant leaf extract and copper sulfate were tested for their antifungal activities in the media containing these fungi. Copper sulfate exhibited a higher inhibition rate in media with F. solani compared to F. oxysporum. Overall, F. solani showed a greater inhibition rate compared to F. oxysporum. Discussion

The purpose of the study was to synthesize and characterize copper nanoparticles using leaf extract from Eucalyptus spp., as well as their antifungal activity against phytopathogenic fungi F.solani and F.oxysporum. The results clearly showed some unique characteristics of CuNPs, synthesized with plant extracts, and their high efficiency as antifungal agents. UV-visible spectroscopy is vital in the confirmation of nanoparticle formation and assessing its optical properties. In particular, when nanoparticles are exposed to light, they interact with the electromagnetic field proving results in plasmon resonance. giving a characteristic absorption peak which is used to evaluate the size, shape, and concentration of nanoparticles developed in the solution. In the current research, it was observed at 580 nm in the UV-visible spectrum proving that CuNPs were formed. The result also coincides with the works conducted by [31] and [32].

The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of the CuNPs further explains the capping agents of the synthesized CuNPs. The observed peaks at 3419.79, 1213.23, 1237.23, and 1629.85 cm−1 corresponding to O-H, N-H, C-N, and C = O bond vibrations, respectively, were due to the presence of proteins, phenolic compounds, and flavonoids found in the plant extract that play a vital role in the stabilization of the CuNPs which coincides with studies of [33] and [34]. The stretching of O-H bonds in alcohols and phenols at 3419.79 cm−1 indicates that hydroxyl groups present in the extract are involved in the reduction between copper ions and alcohol groups [35]. The band at 1213.23 cm−1 relates to the N-H band and suggests the presence of primary amines from the proteins in the leaf extract [36, 37]. The band at 1237.23 cm−1 indicated the presence of the C-N stretching of the subjacent aromatic ring to the amino groups. This leads to the conclusion that amino acids and proteins play a role in the reduction of the copper ions and the capping and stabilization of the NPs [38]. The band at 1629.85 cm−1 showing the carbonyl group indicates the presence of flavonoids and phenolic acid. This confirms the previous explanation in which flavonoids and phenolic acids play a vital role in the reduction and stabilization of the nanoparticles through chelation and electrostatic interaction [39]. At 1282.66 cm−1, the band relating to the C-O bond showed that the organic compounds including flavonoids, phenolic acids, and carboxyl group participate in the capping and stabilization of CuNPs [40].

It was determined through XRD analysis that CuNPs were crystalline and their peaks corresponded to the copper crystal planes belonging to the face-centered cubic structure [41]. Based on the Debye–Scherrer equation, the average size of CuNPs was calculated to be 57.32 nm. Studies on green synthesis of CuNPs using various plant extracts have consistently demonstrated the formation of FCC crystal structures with characteristic diffraction peaks corresponding to (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of copper [42, 43].

The value is reliable due to the SEM data, which demonstrated that the nanoparticles were generally sized between 13 and 88 nm. The morphology and dimensions of copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) in this study were assessed using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM). The analysis revealed the presence of square and triangular CuNPs. Previous studies reported different shapes, with spherical CuNPs documented by [44] and [45], while [46] observed triangular nanoparticles. The size of CuNPs in this study ranged from 13 to 88 nm, which is comparable to the average size of 38.62 nm reported by [29]. When CuNPs were synthesized using a mixture of a reagent and leaf extract from Eucalyptus champiana, the sizes were found to be between 45 and 50 nm [47]. Phytochemicals such as ketones, terpenoids, and amides, which capped the nanoparticles, contributed to the observed sizes and aggregation into pellets [29]. Furthermore [48], noted that these phytochemicals are present on the nanoparticle surfaces and facilitate their aggregation.

The antifungal activity of CuNPs against F. solani and F. oxysporum was concentration-dependent. CuNPs showed higher inhibition rates than plant extract and copper sulphate solutions; thus, CuNPs as antifungal agents are more effective. In previous work, the antifungal activity of some metal nanoparticles was examined against particular species of Fusarium with comparable results. The same results were observed by [49] who gave the observation during his work on the antifungal activities of CuNPs with media containing fungus F. solani and also showed a higher percentage inhibition rate at 100 ppm concentration.

Copper nanoparticles (CuNPs) have been demonstrated to possess potent antimicrobial properties. In media containing the fungus F. oxysporum, the application of varying concentrations of CuNPs resulted in increased inhibition rates, escalating with higher concentrations [50]. Similarly [51], observed comparable inhibition effects when CuNPs were applied to media with different Fusarium species [52]. reported that CuNPs could inhibit 99.9% of microbial growth, highlighting their strong antimicrobial efficacy. The antimicrobial activity of CuNPs is attributed to Cu+ ions, which inactivate DNA and ribosomes, impairing their functionality. When copper ions penetrate fungal and bacterial cells, they disrupt enzymes and proteins, leading to antimicrobial effects [53].

The antifungal activity of copper nanoparticles is a result of a combination of physical interactions and biochemical processes [54]. In particular, one of the most important mechanisms involves the direct physical interaction between CuNPs and the fungal cells through any contact. As a result, the fungal cell membrane is irreversibly damaged, and the content of the cell will start to leak. This process ultimately leads to the death of the fungal cell [55]. In addition to this mechanism, the CuNPs are highly reactive and have a large specific surface. The latter characteristic ensures that CuNPs are highly efficient in their interaction with the cell wall. In the case of fungi, the main components of the cell wall are chitin, glucan, and some proteins [56]. If the CuNPs come into contact with a fungal cell, the cell wall is disrupted, and the fungi are not able to grow further [57]. The complexity of these mechanisms supports that such nanoparticles possess distinguishing functional properties against the fungi and can be effectively used in the appropriate concentrations [58].

Conclusion

The synthesis of copper nanoparticles by using Eucalyptus globulus L. leaf extract has been proved during this study to present an advancement in nanotechnology that might influence a range of scientific fields, particularly agriculture and environmental science. This study examined the CuNPs synthesis alongside its antifungal activity against selected Fusarium species, which shed light on the potential of the synthesized nanoparticles as a safer alternative to available fungicides. As a result, CuNPs of a specific size, shape, and surface chemistry were ultimately synthesized. UV-vis spectroscopy, scanning, and transmission electron microscopy as well as XRD and FTIR provided insight into the physical and chemical properties of the fabricated CuNPs. The concentration-dependent inhibition of the CuNPs tested against the fungal growth of selected Fusarium species also indicated that the synthesized CuNPs can serve as an effective environmental friendly alternative to currently available fungicides, many of which produce significant noxious environmental side-effects. Due to their biodegradability, low-level toxicity to non-target organisms, and inability to accumulate in the environment, CuNPs can be used to promote sustainable agriculture practices. Thus, the evidence provided by this study suggests the search for effective innovative NPs synthesis methods and their application in addressing various urgent problems of agriculture and environmental management.
