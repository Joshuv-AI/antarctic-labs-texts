Restrictive and Binge‐Purge OSFED Variants: Clinical Features and Comparisons With Classical Eating Disorders
Authors: Linda Booij, Emily Mizzi, Samantha Ginsberg, Howard Steiger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCurrent diagnostic nomenclature includes “atypical” eating disorders (EDs) under the term other specified feeding or eating disorder (OSFED). Previous studies suggest that individuals with OSFED have similar psychological and concurrent symptoms to individuals with “classical” EDs. Our study compared people with OSFED to those with classical EDs on indices reflecting ED pathology, concurrent psychological symptoms, personality traits, functional impairment, illness duration, and pretreatment motivation. We also compared OSFED variants characterized solely by restrictive eating behaviors (OSFED‐R) to variants in which binge‐eating and/or purging symptoms predominated (OSFED‐B/P).MethodsParticipants were 836 treatment‐seeking adults (185 AN‐Restricting type [AN‐R], 165 AN Binge‐eating/purging type [AN‐B/P], 276 BN, 104 OSFED‐R, and 106 OSFED‐B/P) starting outpatient ED treatment. Data were analyzed with generalized linear models.ResultsOverall, findings in individuals with OSFED were comparable to those in people with a “classical” ED diagnosis on self‐reported measures of concurrent psychological symptoms, personality traits, functional impairment, illness duration, and treatment motivation. However, relative to people with AN‐R, individuals with OSFED‐R reported higher levels of psychological ED symptoms (p < 0.001). The OSFED‐B/P group had even more ED‐related psychological symptoms than did the OSFED‐R group (p = 0.02), but did not differ from the AN‐B/P and BN groups on any of our indices.DiscussionFindings extend previous research, underscoring the point that OSFED syndromes have serious clinical implications, comparable to those associated with full‐threshold AN and BN. Results also highlight the relevance of examining heterogeneity within the OSFED category in function of a restrictive vs. binge/purge distinction.Keywords: comorbidity, eating disorders, functional impairment, other specified feeding or eating disorder, treatment classification

Background

Under the label “other specified feeding or eating disorder” (OSFED), the current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM‐5‐TR) recognizes “atypical” maladaptive eating syndromes that do not meet full criteria for any of the “classical” feeding or eating disorders (EDs) (APA 2022). OSFED variants include: atypical anorexia nervosa (AN), in which all criteria for AN are met except that, despite significant weight loss, affected individuals are not underweight; bulimia nervosa (BN) of low frequency and/or limited duration, in which all criteria for BN are met except that binge‐eating and compensatory behaviors occur, on average, less than once a week and/or for less than 3 months; binge‐eating disorder (BED) of low frequency and/or limited duration, in which all criteria for BED are met except that, again, binges occur less than once a week and/or for less than 3 months, on average; and purging disorder, in which affected individuals recurrently purge through such means as self‐induced vomiting or laxative misuse in the absence of binge eating (APA 2022).

Several studies have compared OSFED variants to their classical ED counterparts on ED severity and associated symptomatology. Results indicate that OSFED variants are comparable to or even rival their classical ED counterparts for severity of eating‐related preoccupations, concurrent psychopathology, and medical complications, and show comparable treatment responses (Steiger and Booij 2025; Walsh et al. 2023; Wilkop et al. 2023; Withnell et al. 2022). In other words, OSFED syndromes seem to have similar clinical implications to those of their “classical” ED counterparts. Given this point, there is reason to regard the former group of syndromes as constituting an even greater social and health problem, as OSFEDs affect many more people than do classical EDs—one study estimating that, worldwide, 24.6 million people had OSFED in 2019, compared to 17.3 million with BED, and 13.6 million with AN or BN (Santomauro et al. 2021). The preceding suggests that it would be an error to regard the OSFED diagnoses as representing less‐severe syndromes than classical EDs.

The present study aimed to extend previous research comparing clinical presentations between people with OSFED and “classical” EDs. Drawing on a large sample of treatment‐seeking adults in a specialized outpatient ED program, we hypothesized that people with OSFED diagnoses would show comparable levels of psychological symptoms to those seen in individuals with AN or BN. To previous investigations, our study added self‐reported measures of emotion dysregulation, personality traits, functional impairment, illness duration, and pretreatment motivation. In addition, based on previous research identifying differential loadings of personality traits in individuals with restrictive vs. bulimic ED forms (e.g., Legg and Turner 2021; Martinussen et al. 2017), we subdivided individuals in the OSFED category into those showing only restrictive eating (OSFED‐R) and those in whom binge and/or purge symptoms were also present (OSFED‐B/P). Our analyses compared OSFED‐R and OSFED‐B/P groups to each other and to their respective “classical” ED counterparts—that is, AN‐Restricting type (AN‐R), on the one hand, and AN‐Binge‐eating/purging type (AN‐B/P) and BN, on the other.

Methods

Participants

Participants in this research ethics board‐approved study were adults treated between 2018 and 2025 at the outpatient program of the Eating Disorders Continuum of the Douglas Mental Health University Institute in Montreal, Canada. We included people with AN, BN, and OSFED, but excluded people with BED, because patients with BED are rarely offered treatment in our program. We also excluded people who did not complete an Eating Disorder Examination‐Questionnaire (EDE‐Q) at the start of treatment. ED diagnoses and subtype variants (OSFED‐R vs. OSFED‐B/P) were determined by experienced clinicians using semi‐structured interviews, which were then validated through multidisciplinary team consensus. Decisions concerning subtype variants were established using additional information obtained from the EDE‐Q. Individuals were classified as OSFED‐R if they met OSFED criteria but did not report recent binge‐eating or purging behaviors, and as OSFED‐B/P if they met OSFED criteria and also exhibited evidence of binge‐eating and/or purging. Due to the limits of historical data on weight loss, we opted not to diagnose Atypical AN. As for DSM‐5 OSFED subvariants, 91 individuals reported the presence of at least one binge in addition to purging behaviors in the past 3 months, suggesting BN of low frequency. The remaining 15 individuals reported purging without any binge‐eating, suggesting purging disorder. Given the small size of the “purging disorder” sample, and prior evidence that people with purging disorder exhibit similar psychological tendencies to those with BN (Krug et al. 2022; Steiger and Booij 2025), we combined the BN of low frequency and purging disorder subgroups into one OSFED‐B/P group.

Clinical Measures

Self‐report assessments of eating and concurrent symptoms were conducted at the start of treatment using paper‐ or web‐based questionnaires. In both formats, ED symptoms were assessed using the EDE‐Q, version 6.0 (Fairburn and Beglin 2008). A recent study showed good psychometric properties of the global severity score but limited support for the original 4‐factor structure (Dufour et al. 2025). In our dataset, Cronbach's alpha for the EDE‐Q global severity scale was 0.92. BMI was derived from EDE‐Q items or, when unavailable, anthropometric data extracted from patient files. Illness duration was calculated by subtracting each participant's reported age of onset from the demographic age.

Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Questionnaire (GAD‐7; Spitzer et al. 2006) and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9; Kroenke et al. 2001), respectively. Our Cronbach's alphas for the GAD‐7 and PHQ‐9 were respectively 0.89 and 0.86. Emotion regulation was assessed using the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS‐18) (Victor and Klonsky 2016). The DERS‐18 had a Cronbach alpha of 0.88 in our dataset. Personality traits were assessed using the Big Five Inventory (BFI‐44), which includes subscales measuring Openness, Conscientiousness, Extraversion, Agreeableness, and Neuroticism (John et al. 1991). Our Cronbach's alphas for these scales were 0.84, 0.79, 0.86, 0.71, and 0.77, respectively. Functional impairment was assessed using the Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS) total score (Sheehan and Sheehan 2008). Cronbach's alpha was 0.81. The Autonomous and Controlled Motivation Questionnaire (ACMTQ) (Zuroff et al. 2007), applied in an ED‐adapted form by Steiger et al. (2017), measures motivation for therapy. We used the Autonomous Motivation subscale—previously shown to predict better ED treatment outcome (Sansfacon et al. 2020). Cronbach's alpha was 0.86.

Statistical Analyses

Using IBM SPSS version 29, we conducted a series of generalized linear models to examine group differences on measures of eating and concurrent pathology, functional impairment, illness duration, and motivation. For each model, group (AN‐R, AN‐B/P, BN, OSFED‐R, and OSFED B/P) was the fixed factor, and the measure of interest was the dependent variable. Primary group comparisons were OSFED‐R vs. AN‐R, OSFED‐B/P vs. AN‐B/P, OSFED‐B/P vs. BN, and OSFED‐R vs. OSFED‐B/P. Bonferroni correction was applied to all paired comparisons (i.e., α = 0.05/10). P values reported below are Bonferroni‐adjusted. Effect sizes for the significant comparisons were calculated using partial eta squared (η 2). Since the AN‐R group was slightly younger than the other groups (see below), and age was associated with illness duration, we analyzed illness duration using age as a covariate.

Demographic Variables

Individuals with AN‐R were slightly younger than individuals in any of the other diagnostic groups (p < 0.004). There were no age differences between the AN‐B/P, BN, and OSFED‐B/P groups (p > 0.65). Most of the sample (95.8%) identified as female. Approximately 78% reported themselves to be of European descent, 4.3% Middle Eastern, 3.4% Hispanic/Latino, 3.2% African/Caribbean, 2.8% Asian, and 8.3% other ancestries. Given the distributions obtained, we did not investigate group differences as to gender and ancestry.

ED Pathology

Both AN groups had predictably lower mean BMIs than did the other groups (BN, OSFED‐R, or OSFED‐B/P) (p < 0.001). In addition, the OSFED‐B/P group had a lower BMI than the BN group (Wald χ 2 = 11.71, p = 0.006; η 2 = 0.02, 95% CI = [0.001–0.063]). There was no BMI difference between the OSFED‐R and OSFED‐B/P groups (p > 0.99).

Individuals with AN‐R had lower EDE‐Q scores than people with OSFED‐R (Wald χ 2 = 36.24, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.08, 95% CI = [0.031–0.147]), who in turn had lower scores than individuals with OSFED‐B/P (χ 2 = 9.53, p = 0.02, η 2 = 0.044, 95% CI = [0.005–0.107]). The corresponding effect sizes for the preceding comparisons were large and small‐medium, respectively. EDE‐Q scores did not differ between the OSFED‐B/P, BN, and AN‐B/P groups (p > 0.99).

Concurrent Clinical Features

Individuals with AN‐R had a shorter illness duration than any of the other groups (p < 0.02). The difference in illness duration did not, however, survive the control for age (p > 0.39). There were no significant differences in GAD‐7, PHQ‐9, DERS‐18, BFI‐44, SDS, and ACMTQ scores between individuals with OSFED‐R vs. AN‐R, OSFED‐B/P vs. AN‐B/P, OSFED‐B/P vs. BN, and between OSFED‐R vs. OSFED‐B/P (all p > 0.11). Overall, the AN‐R group reported the lowest scores on measures of psychological symptoms. However, across all groups, levels of anxiety, depression, and functional impairment were within the moderate clinical range, according to established norms for the GAD‐7, PHQ‐9, and SDS (Kroenke et al. 2001; Sheehan and Sheehan 2008; Spitzer et al. 2006).

Discussion

The present study compared treatment‐seeking individuals with OSFED to those with “classical” EDs (AN and BN) on indices reflecting ED pathology, concurrent psychological symptoms, personality traits, functional impairment, illness duration, and pretreatment motivation. We noted that people with OSFED displayed comparable, or in some cases higher levels of eating and concurrent psychological symptoms when compared to individuals with AN or BN. Specifically, individuals with restrictive OSFED forms reported higher levels of psychological ED symptoms (i.e., preoccupations around eating, shape, and weight) than did individuals with AN‐R. In contrast, individuals with OSFED marked by binge‐eating and/or purging symptoms (OSFED‐B/P) showed similar levels of eating preoccupations to individuals with AN‐B/P or BN. Those with OSFED‐B/P reported higher levels of eating pathology than did people with OSFED‐R. Irrespective of a restrictive or a binge‐purge presentation, individuals with OSFED had comparable levels of anxiety, depression, emotion dysregulation, personality traits, functional impairment, and treatment motivation to those in the “classical” ED groups. All in all, as has previous research (see Steiger and Booij 2025; Walsh et al. 2023; Wilkop et al. 2023; Withnell et al. 2022), our findings suggest that eating and concurrent psychological disturbances in OSFED variants are no less severe than those in the “classical” EDs. Our findings, furthermore, highlight the relevance of examining a restrictive vs. binge/purge distinction within the OSFED category.

The present study is among the largest to date in characterizing OSFED in adults receiving treatment in a specialized eating disorder setting. The relatively large sample size, along with the broad set of behavioral measurements used, allowed us to achieve a detailed comparison of people with OSFED variants to those with their corresponding classical ED counterparts. However, results should be interpreted in light of the following limitations. Our sample was predominantly female of European ancestry. Future studies are needed to investigate whether results can be generalized to more diverse backgrounds. Furthermore, our study was conducted in a sample of individuals seeking and eligible for treatment in a specialized eating‐disorder program. Results may not be generalizable to individuals with EDs who do not seek help or who do not need specialized eating‐disorder care. Furthermore, although individuals with OSFED had a similar duration of illness prior to treatment to that in those with AN or BN, longitudinal studies are needed to examine whether OSFED may merely represent prodromal or residual forms of their “classical” ED counterparts. Longitudinal treatment outcome data are also important for clarifying whether individuals with OSFED‐R or OSFED‐B/P benefit from interventions to the same extent as those with AN or BN.

To conclude, our results confirm that OSFED variants warrant serious clinical attention. Clinical disturbances on the dimensions studied here seem to be about as severe in OSFED variants as they are in threshold AN or BN—and OSFED variants are far more numerous. Our findings also highlight the possible clinical relevance of distinguishing OSFED variants based on the presence or absence of binge‐purge behaviors. All in all, our findings corroborate previous indications that OSFED variants are not inherently less severe than are “classical” EDs, and should not, by default, receive lesser priority for specialized treatment.
