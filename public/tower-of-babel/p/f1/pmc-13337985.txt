Reply to: Commentary on “The food additive 4-hexylresorcinol impairs intestinal stem cell function and exacerbates inflammatory bowel disease in Drosophila melanogaster via suppression of MAPK signaling pathway”
Authors: Xinxin Fan, Jinhua Yan, Yu Yuan, Yi Chen, Haiyang Chen
Published: 2026

Dear Editors and Professor Kim,

We thank Professor Kim for his thoughtful and constructive commentary on our article. His critique has helped us identify several important limitations and areas where our wording could be more precise. Below, we respond to the main concerns raised, organized thematically.

Exposure relevance of the 4-HR concentrations used in Drosophila

Professor Kim notes that the 4-HR concentrations used in our fly experiments (0.1, 1, and 10 mM) are considerably higher than the residue levels of this food additive in crustacean meat (approximately 1–2 mg/kg as reported by Codex, EFSA and JECFA) [1–4]. We fully agree that caution is needed when extrapolating from such high concentrations to realistic human dietary exposure. The primary goal of our study, however, was to establish a mechanistic proof-of-concept in a genetically tractable model organism. In Drosophila, higher compound concentrations are often required to achieve sufficient internal exposure because of the chitinous exoskeleton and different metabolic handling. Importantly, the same concentrations did not affect intestinal stem cell (ISC) homeostasis under steady-state conditions (Figure 1 in our original paper), arguing against non-specific cytotoxicity. We acknowledge the concentration discrepancy as a limitation and agree that future studies should test chronic, low-dose exposure in mammalian models before any translation to human dietary risk.

Acute versus chronic exposure models

Professor Kim correctly points out that our study used short-term injury models (bleomycin-treated flies and 7-day DSS-treated mice [5]) and therefore does not directly address long-term or chronic dietary exposure. We accept this criticism. Our conclusions regarding “long-term” effects were overstated. The current experiments were designed to dissect the effect of 4-HR on the acute injury-repair response, which we believe is a relevant first step. However, we fully agree that chronic colitis models and prolonged low-dose feeding studies are needed to assess the potential relevance for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) epidemiology.

Directness of evidence for MAPK/ERK pathway inhibition

Professor Kim questions whether our data conclusively demonstrate that 4-HR directly inhibits MAPK1/ERK2 enzymatic activity. We appreciate this technical concern. Our study showed direct physical binding between 4-HR and MAPK1 by molecular docking and bio-layer interferometry, and we observed reduced p-ERK levels in vivo together with a functional rescue by ERK overexpression (Figure 6 in our original paper). However, we did not perform in vitro kinase activity assays using purified MAPK1 protein. Therefore, while the evidence strongly indicates that 4-HR interferes with MAPK/ERK signaling, we agree that the wording “directly inhibits” is too strong. We have therefore moderated this statement to reflect that 4-HR binds to MAPK1 and is associated with reduced ERK activation, leaving the precise mechanism of enzymatic inhibition for future studies.

Wording and extrapolation of conclusions

Finally, Professor Kim suggests that our translational conclusions – linking 4-HR directly to human IBD pathogenesis – are not fully supported by the current experimental evidence [6]. We accept this suggestion. Our findings demonstrate that under the tested conditions, 4-HR impairs ISC-mediated epithelial repair in two model systems. Whether this translates into clinically relevant risk for IBD patients will require further investigation using more physiologically relevant exposure paradigms and chronic disease models.

Summary

We are grateful to Professor Kim for his rigorous and constructive critique. We acknowledge the three main limitations discussed above. We stand by our experimental data but we acknowledge the limitations regarding exposure relevance, chronicity, and mechanistic directness as identified by Professor Kim. We hope that this exchange will help readers interpret our findings more accurately.
