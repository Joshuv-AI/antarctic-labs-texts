Acute kidney injury and morbi‐mortality associated with “triple whammy” combination: Systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Dulce María Calvo, Luis Carlos Saiz, Leire Leache, Maria Concepción Celaya, Marta Gutiérrez‐Valencia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims“Triple whammy” (TW) refers to the concomitant use of diuretics, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASI) and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). The aim of this article is to analyse the association between TW exposure and acute kidney injury (AKI), among other morbi‐mortality outcomes.MethodsWe included studies published up to August 2023 in the Cochrane Library, Medline or EMBASE, among others. The risk of AKI within 12 months after TW vs. not exposed to TW was the main outcome.ResultsWe included five case–control studies, involving 410 130 participants. The risk of AKI was significantly higher among those exposed to TW vs. not exposed (odds ratio [OR] 2.01, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.30–3.10; four studies; I 2: 90%; low certainty of evidence). Three studies provided data on all‐cause mortality, not showing a significantly higher risk with TW exposure (very low certainty of evidence).ConclusionThe available evidence reveals that TW may lead to a significantly higher risk of AKI and related hospitalizations, with no detrimental effect on mortality.Keywords: acute kidney injury, drug safety, nephrotoxicity, systematic review

INTRODUCTION

The term “triple whammy” (TW) corresponds to the concurrent use of diuretics, renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASI) and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). 1 These therapeutic classes have an impact on kidney function in various ways, such as by causing hypovolaemia and decreasing glomerular perfusion and thus filtration rate. 2 In some contexts, metamizole (dipyrone) is occasionally employed as an alternative to NSAIDs for patients at risk of kidney injury or with pre‐existing renal disease, owing to its perceived lower nephrotoxicity due to its different mechanism of action, although there is controversy on whether it is less nephrotoxic. 3 , 4 Therefore, some studies also include metamizole together with NSAIDs in the TW combination.

The TW combination of drugs has been linked to an elevated risk of acute kidney injury (AKI). 2 AKI is a common and potentially serious clinical condition, particularly among hospitalized patients and those with chronic illnesses. It is characterized by a sudden deterioration in renal function, often resulting in the accumulation of metabolic waste products, fluid imbalances and electrolyte disturbances. AKI is not only associated with increased hospital stay and healthcare costs, but also contributes significantly to patient morbidity and mortality. Early recognition and prevention of modifiable risk factors remain essential components of effective management. 5 , 6

One notable preventable cause of AKI is drug‐induced nephrotoxicity, particularly through the concurrent use of certain medications like TW combination. This interaction compromises renal autoregulation, particularly in patients with underlying risk factors such as dehydration, heart failure or pre‐existing kidney disease. In this regard, Loboz et al. discovered a strong relationship between the number of TW drugs and the worsening of kidney function. 7 In particular, the TW combination has been linked to a 30% increased risk of AKI in some studies. 8 , 9 Furthermore, the probability of developing AKI seems to be higher during the first 30 days of TW exposure. 10

AKI has significant social and economic implications, with an estimated mortality prevalence of up to 60% worldwide. 11 However, these data are highly variable according to the country, setting, patient demographics and AKI diagnostic criteria and definitions used, among other factors. 11 The economic impact of AKI amounts to some 5% of the hospital budget and 1% of total health expenditure, which is at least partly due to an increase in hospital stay, monitoring and need for dialysis. 12 , 13

The number of hospitalizations for AKI has exponentially increased over time, from 15 885 in 1997 to 207 287 in 2015. 14 Iavecchia et al. discovered that diuretics and RAASIs were responsible for 54.1% of inpatient AKI. 15 According to García Camín et al., the incidence of outpatient AKI attributable to TW drugs was 3.4 per 1000 users/year, with a mortality of 11.3% during hospital stay and 40.3% at 12 months. 16

Despite awareness of these risks, TW combination remains frequently prescribed, underscoring the need for greater vigilance. Understanding the clinical implications of triple therapy‐induced AKI is crucial to guide more effective prescribing practices. Research examining patient characteristics, comorbidities and pharmacological profiles associated with AKI in this context could contribute to a clearer risk–benefit assessment. This knowledge can support improved therapeutic decisions and the implementation of preventive measures in clinical settings, especially in primary care and outpatient management where these drug combinations are commonly initiated. The risk of AKI with the TW combination has been assessed in studies with different designs, contexts and populations, but they have not been reviewed together and the magnitude of the effect has not been quantified in an aggregate form. In addition, there is limited evidence about the effect of the combination on other relevant clinical outcomes such as hospitalization due to AKI, need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) or mortality. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to study the detrimental effect of TW on AKI and other relevant clinical outcomes. The review aims at analysing the evidence on the impact of TW exposure on the risk of AKI and other morbi‐mortality outcomes.

METHOD

The protocol of the systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42020213680) and published elsewhere. 17 The review was conducted following the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Review of Interventions 18 and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement. 19

Study eligibility criteria

Clinical trials and analytical observational studies comparing the exposure to TW combination vs. non‐exposure to TW in adults (≥18 years) were eligible. There were no restrictions regarding the publication date, setting or language. Animal studies were excluded.

Exposure

The TW combination corresponds to the simultaneous use of at least (1) a loop diuretic, thiazide or other diuretics; (2) a renin–angiotensin aldosterone system inhibitors (RAASI), including angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI e.g., enalapril, lisinopril), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB; e.g., losartan, valsartan) and/or direct renin inhibitors (e.g., aliskiren); and (3) an NSAID or metamizole. The non‐exposure to TW comprises the use of none or less than three of the mentioned drug classes.

Outcome variables

AKI was the main outcome. The following secondary outcomes were also analysed: (1) hospitalization due to AKI; (2) serious adverse events (SAEs); (3) requirement of RRT; and (4) all‐cause mortality. Primary and secondary events occurring within 12 months after the last prescription of the TW combination were considered. Studies must provide data for at least a primary or a secondary outcome to be eligible for inclusion.

The definitions of AKI used within the different studies were considered acceptable. In the case of studies that did not provide a specific AKI definition, the criteria established by the Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and the guideline of the National Institute for Health and Excellence (NICE) were adopted. 20 , 21 According to KDIGO, AKI is defined as an increase in serum creatinine ≥26 μmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) within 48 h, a ≥50% increase in serum creatinine known or suspected to have occurred in the last 7 days, or a drop in urine output to <0.5 mL/kg/h for more than 6 h plus 25% of the estimated glomerular filtration rate in the last 7 days. 20 , 21

An SAE was considered as any event that led to death, was life‐threatening, resulted in permanent or substantial disability, required hospitalization or prolongation of existing hospitalization, or was a congenital anomaly or birth defect, 22 occurring with any of the three drug classes of the TW combination. SAEs were considered independently of the AKI event.

Sources of information and search strategy

Literature searches were conducted in the following sources: Cochrane Library, Medline, Epistemonikos, EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS), Bireme, and Scientific Electronic Library Online (Scielo).

ClinicalTrials.gov, Spanish Clinical Trials Registry (REec), EU Clinical Trials Registry, EU post‐authorization study registry (ENCePP), and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) of the World Health Organization were also consulted. A search in PROSPERO was carried out to identify completed or ongoing reviews. In addition, the reference list of all included studies and of reviews, clinical practice guidelines and position papers were reviewed to identify other relevant studies.

Searches were designed by combining terms related to kidney injury and TW drugs. The Medline and EMBASE search strategies are shown in the Supplementary material. Searches were carried out in February 2021 and updated in August 2023.

Screening and data extraction

Two independent reviewers carried out the screening of papers based on their title and abstract, checking all the eligibility criteria in the first stage. Then, full texts from each paper considered for inclusion were reviewed. Two reviewers independently extracted the data from the included trials with a pre‐planned data extraction form. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or by a third author when necessary.

When it was necessary to complete the information, the authors of the included studies were contacted for additional data.

Risk of bias and certainty of the evidence assessment

The risk of bias of the included studies was assessed using ROBINS‐I tool, 23 , 24 since all the included studies were non‐randomized studies. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) methodology 25 was followed to evaluate the certainty of the evidence. The assessment of the risk of bias and certainty of the evidence was performed independently by two reviewers, and the discrepancies were discussed.

Data analysis

Data synthesis and analysis were conducted using the inverse variance method and a random effects approach. We used RevMan version 5.4 for the analyses. 17 The main analysis consisted of the comparison between TW combination of any type vs. non‐exposure to TW. A secondary analysis was planned considering only the exposure to the TW combination that included metamizole (and not an NSAID) vs. non‐exposure to TW, to explore the potential role of metamizole as a less nephrotoxic alternative in the TW combination.

The odds ratio (OR) and adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were used to summarize the results. When a study reported both unadjusted and adjusted effect estimates, adjusted data were used in the analyses. Also, we performed an additional meta‐analysis using the rate ratio (RR) reported in the included studies, where available.

The I 2 statistic was used to describe the heterogeneity, with an I 2 > 60% indicative of substantial heterogeneity.

We planned to perform subgroup analyses for all the outcome variables in participants with (1) > 75 years; (2) previous chronic kidney disease (CKD); and (3) previous cardiovascular disease.

We also planned to conduct sensitivity analyses for all the outcomes (1) excluding studies with high risk of bias; and (2) including only those studies that used KDIGO's definition of AKI, when applicable.

Nomenclature of targets and ligands

Key protein targets and ligands in this article are hyperlinked to corresponding entries in http://www.guidetopharmacology.org, the common portal for data from the IUPHAR/BPS Guide to PHARMACOLOGY, and are permanently archived in the Concise Guide to PHARMACOLOGY 2023/24. 26 , 27

RESULTS

A total of 1041 references were initially identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates, 985 references remained for screening. In the first phase, 957 references were excluded after screening of titles and abstracts. In the second phase, the full texts of the 28 remaining references were reviewed, of which 22 were excluded. Consequently, six references were included in the review, 8 , 10 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 which corresponded to five different studies (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of the literature search.

The characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1. Three of them were nested case–control multicentre studies: two conducted in United Kingdom and one in Spain. The remaining ones were a case–control study carried out in a single centre from Australia, and a retrospective cohort study performed in a single centre in France. No clinical trial fulfilling the inclusion criteria was identified. The number of participants in each study was highly variable, ranging from 1721 to 480 537 participants.

The comparator used varied between the different studies. In the study by Lapi et al., 10 the comparator was a diuretic plus an antihypertensive, in Bories et al. 31 two TW drugs, while Calvo et al. 30 and Dreischulte et al. 8 compared TW with the use of one or two drug classes from TW. Mangoni et al. compared TW with none to two of these pharmacological classes. 28 Additionally, Mangoni et al. analysed whether extreme heat influences the risk of AKI associated with TW and provided results for both heat‐wave periods and non‐heat‐wave periods. 28 We considered the non‐heat wave period for the analysis, since it was more comparable to the rest of the included studies and had a larger sample size.

Results on primary outcome: acute kidney injury (AKI)

AKI occurring within the 12 months after the last prescription of the TW combination was evaluated in four of the included studies. 8 , 28 , 29 , 31 TW significantly increased the risk of AKI (OR 2.01, 95% CI 1.30–3.10; I 2: 90%; low certainty of the evidence; Figure 2). The result of the rate ratio meta‐analysis was similar, with an RR of 1.42 (95% CI, 1.15–1.76, I 2: 49%) (Figure A1).

Forest plot for AKI with TW vs. non‐exposure to TW.

Results on secondary outcomes

Hospitalization due to AKI

Hospitalization due to AKI was analysed by Calvo et al., 30 being significantly higher among people exposed to TW compared to those not exposed to TW (aOR 1.36, 95% CI 1.32–1.40). The risk of hospitalization due to AKI was higher with the TW that included metamizole than with the TW that included an NSAID as compared to non‐exposure to TW (aOR TW with metamizole vs. no TW: 1.45; 95% CI 1.40–1.51; aOR with TW including an NSAID vs. no TW: 1.25; 95% CI 1.21–1.29; (P‐value for interaction <0.0001).

SAEs were not analysed in the included studies.

Requirement of RRT

The study by Calvo et al. 30 showed no significant differences between exposure to TW and non‐exposure to TW in the requirement of RRT during hospitalization (aOR 0.94, 95% CI 0.83–1.06). 30

All‐cause mortality

Two studies provided data on all‐cause mortality, 28 , 30 although it was not possible to conduct meta‐analyses due to the heterogeneity among the studies in reporting the results. Calvo et al. 30 found that, among the participants hospitalized due to AKI, all‐cause mortality during hospitalization was significantly lower in the participants exposed to TW than among those not exposed to TW (aOR 0.81, 95% CI 0.71–0.93). Mangoni et al. 28 found no significant differences in in‐hospital mortality (aOR 0.36, 95% CI 0.11–1.20).

Subgroup analyses

It was only possible to perform the subgroup analysis relative to participants older than 75 years, with only the study by Calvo et al. 30 providing the necessary data. The authors found that, in people older than 75 years, the risk of hospitalization due to AKI was significantly higher among people exposed to TW compared to those not exposed to TW (aOR 1.37, 95% CI 1.32–1.41).

Risk of bias and certainty of the evidence data

The risk of bias assessment following the ROBINS‐I tool is shown Figure 4 for the main outcome (AKI), and in supplementary material for the secondary outcomes (Figure A2, A3, A4). All included studies showed an overall moderate risk of bias for the primary outcome (AKI), 8 , 10 , 28 except for Bories et al., 31 which shows a serious risk of bias due to confounding (no adjustment for covariates). In the rest of the studies, we found a moderate risk of bias in Domain 1 (bias due to confounding), since despite several confounding factors being considered in the analysis of AKI, additional confounding by other variables cannot be ruled out. The Domain 2 (selection bias) was rated as moderate because apart from new (incident) users of TW drugs, prevalent users were also considered. The rest of the domains (3 to 7) were rated as low risk of bias in all studies. 8 , 10 , 28

DISCUSSION

Key findings

The results of this systematic review indicate a significantly higher risk of AKI and hospitalization due to AKI with the TW combination, with a low certainty of evidence. Sensitivity analyses that restricted study inclusion to those that used KDIGO's definition of AKI and excluded a study with a high risk of bias yielded similar results.

The two studies 28 , 30 that provided information on all‐cause mortality showed, although with a very low certainty of evidence, that TW was not associated with a significantly higher risk, even when adjusting for confounding factors. Nevertheless, it was not possible to conduct meta‐analysis of this variable since the data available were heterogeneous. Moreover, the likelihood of unknown residual confounders limits the ability to draw firm conclusions. Therefore, the potential effect of TW on all‐cause mortality remains open to further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up periods. The results also show no association with the need for RRT, although with very low certainty of evidence, and no data on SAEs were reported in the studies included.

Our findings are clinically relevant for patients, healthcare professionals and decision‐makers. The concurrent use of RAASI and diuretics is common in the management of hypertension and heart failure. NSAIDs, which are widely available over the counter, are frequently used by patients without medical supervision. As a result, the combination of these three drug classes, often referred to as the “triple whammy” (TW), occurs frequently in clinical practice. However, it is important to note that not all patients exposed to this combination will develop AKI. The risk of AKI is influenced by multiple factors, including the duration of drug exposure, dosage and individual patient characteristics. These include advanced age, pre‐existing CKD, dehydration, hypertension, cardiovascular comorbidities, atherosclerosis, high dietary sodium intake and the use of other nephrotoxic agents. Clinicians should be particularly vigilant when treating patients with these risk factors, ensuring regular monitoring of renal function and reassessing the necessity of continuing all three medications concurrently.

Based on the evidence presented in this review, we recommend that clinicians avoid prescribing this drug combination when safer alternatives are available, especially in high‐risk patients. Moreover, patients should be explicitly counselled on the potential renal risks of NSAID use, particularly when they are already receiving RAASI or diuretics. Patient education on the risks of self‐medication and the importance of seeking medical advice before taking NSAIDs is essential. Our findings also support the integration of alerts related to this drug combination into electronic clinical decision support systems to enhance prescribing safety. Interprofessional collaboration between physicians and pharmacists is crucial to optimizing pharmacotherapy and minimizing preventable drug‐induced kidney injury.

Results from other related studies and reviews

Some studies that did not fulfil our inclusion criteria provide information on the use of TW combination and associated risks. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Mattioli et al. 32 conducted a post‐hoc analysis based on data from a large Italian active pharmacovigilance database, characterizing AEs related to the exposure to TW leading to emergency department visits and/or hospitalizations, with a focus on the AEs related to AKI. Over a 12‐year study period, 80 of more than 60 000 pharmacovigilance reports corresponded to AEs potentially related to TW, and only nine of them had kidney manifestations. 30 Another retrospective study by Piekarska et al. assessed the rate of TW cases in a community pharmacy setting in Poland, and found 12 TW cases among 14 243 prescriptions over 5 months. 33 Loboz et al., in a cross‐sectional study of patients admitted to a general medical ward of a teaching hospital, found higher serum creatinine levels and lower creatinine clearance values in patients with three TW drugs as compared to two, one or none of the TW drugs. 7

To our knowledge, there are few systematic reviews that examine the association between NSAID exposure and AKI. 34 , 35 One of them was conducted in 2017 and included ten studies, and another was conducted in 2014 and included five observational studies. Both reported a significantly higher risk of AKI with NSAID exposure. However, none of them analysed the risk of AKI with the TW exposure.

Strength and limitations

This review analyses the effect of TW on AKI and other clinically relevant morbi‐mortality outcomes. The review was performed based on a previously registered protocol and following a robust methodology. A broad and updated literature search was performed, including the most relevant databases. Additionally, all the review processes were conducted in parallel by two reviewers.

Our review has several limitations, and thus the findings should be regarded with caution. First, it was not possible to include adjusted data from all the studies in the meta‐analysis for AKI, as the included studies provided different adjusted estimators (Dreischulte et al. 8 and Lapi et al. 10 reported adjusted RR and Mangoni et al. 28 adjusted OR), some adjusted data did not correspond to the comparison of interest, or some studies did not provide adjusted results. 31 However, studies proving adjusted data show similar results in their unadjusted data, so this is not expected to be especially relevant to the results. Furthermore, the meta‐analysis of AKI revealed a significant heterogeneity, what might be expected when combining studies with different designs, populations, settings, comparators or follow‐up periods. However, the fact that the sensitivity analysis confirms the results for the main variable reinforces the robustness of our results. Also, we acknowledge the potential risk of publication bias in our systematic review, as the possibility exists that studies with non‐significant or negative results may be underreported or unpublished. In addition, meta‐analysis of all‐cause mortality could not be performed, since available data referred to different study populations, and therefore have different interpretations.

Finally, all the evidence found comes from observational studies, providing an initial lower certainty of the evidence than other studies with more robust designs, such as randomized controlled trials. Obtaining information from clinical trials on this topic is not feasible, and the observational nature of the studies is an inherent limitation when assessing safety issues such as drug–drug interactions. However, well‐designed observational studies with large sample sizes that adequately address confounding factors are the highest quality evidence that can be expected for this research question, and this is what future research should focus on to improve knowledge on this area.

CONCLUSION

Evidence of low certainty suggests that TW exposure may be associated with a higher risk of AKI and hospitalization due to AKI. Exposure to the TW combination should be a matter of concern, since it could have important implications for patients. Our review findings should encourage physicians, pharmacists and stakeholders to handle these drugs with caution to improve patient safety. Therefore, vigilance should be strengthened when these drugs are used simultaneously to minimize the risk of developing an avoidable kidney injury and other adverse events.
