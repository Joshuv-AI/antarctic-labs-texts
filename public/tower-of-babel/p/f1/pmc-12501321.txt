Nutritional and qualitative characteristics of beef patties incorporated with hydrated coffee cherry pulp powder
Authors: Rehab F M Ali, Ayman M El-Anany
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of more sustainable and healthful meat products is urgently needed. The purpose of the present investigation was to evaluate the impact of utilizing different levels of CCPP as a fat substitute on the nutritional, quality, and sensory attributes of burger samples. The chemical composition, functional characteristics, total phenolics, total flavonoids, anthocyanin, and antioxidant activity of CCPP were assessed. The proximate composition, cholesterol content, cooking qualities, and sensory aspects of produced burgers were investigated. CCPP is a nutrient-rich by-product that contains a crude fiber content of 36.66%, alongside significant levels of ash (6.97%), protein (10.16%), and carbohydrates (44.4%). CCPP exhibits several functional properties that enhance its application in food applications. Its water absorption capacity (WAC), oil absorption capacity (OAC), emulsifying activity, and foaming capacity are significant, with values of 3.25 ml H2O/g, 2.80 mL oil/g, 43.5%, and 21.50%, respectively. TPC of CCPP was reported to be 24.70 mg GAE/g, with significant phenolic components which contribute to antioxidant activity of the CCPP. Incorporating CCPP into burger enhanced nutritional profiles by increasing fiber, protein, and ash content while significantly reducing fat levels. The 50% and 75% CCPP substitutions received over all acceptability scores of 8.25 and 8.22, respectively, suggesting strong consumer preference for these formulations.Subject terms: Proteins, Chemistry, Agriculture

Introduction

The transition towards meat substitutes is increasingly recognized as a vital strategy for enhancing sustainability in food systems1. Various alternatives, including plant-based, insect, fungi, and single-cell proteins, present promising solutions to mitigate environmental impacts1,2. However, the success of these substitutes hinges on understanding their nutritional profiles, technological feasibility, and consumer acceptance, which are critical for market penetration and dietary shifts3. In this regard, coffee cherry consists of several components, each contributing to its overall quality and potential applications. The skin, or pericarp, changes color from green to red as it ripens, indicating optimal harvest time and correlating with sugar accumulation, particularly sucrose, which is crucial for coffee quality4. Beneath the skin lies the sweet pulp, which is rich in bioactive compounds, making coffee cherry extracts valuable as superfoods with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties5. The mucilage layer, primarily composed of pectin, plays a role in the processing of coffee cherries6. The parchment and silverskin layers, often considered by-products, contain significant nutritional value and can be utilized in various industries, including food and pharmaceuticals6. Finally, the coffee bean, the endosperm, is the primary product, with its flavor profile influenced by the ripening process and metabolic changes7.

The processing of coffee cherries into beans generates significant waste, primarily through wet and dry methods. In the dry process, cherries are sun-dried, leading to the removal of skin, pulp, mucilage, parchment, and silverskin during de-hulling. This waste can be managed and repurpose effectively8. The dry processing method produces various residues, including husk, pulp, and parchment, which are rich in carbohydrates and bioactive compounds9.

Coffee Cherry Pulp powder (Cascara powder), a by-product of coffee production, presents both environmental challenges and opportunities for value-added applications. With over 430 g of pulp generated per kg of coffee cherries, the disposal of this waste can lead to significant environmental issues due to its high content of caffeine, polyphenols, and tannins8,10. However, innovative uses have emerged, such as the production of coffee vinegar through fermentation, which optimizes the pulp’s nutrient content11. Additionally, extraction methods utilizing natural deep eutectic solvents have been developed to recover valuable compounds like caffeine and chlorogenic acid from the pulp12. Furthermore, coffee pulp can be transformed into non-wood paper, demonstrating its potential as a sustainable resource13. These applications highlight the dual nature of coffee cherry pulp as both a waste product and a valuable resource, suggesting a need for further exploration of its benefits and uses in various industries. Coffee pulp (CP), a significant byproduct of coffee processing, constitutes about 28% of the dry weight of coffee cherries, generating approximately two tons of CP for every ton of coffee produced7,14. This waste, often overlooked, holds potential economic value through various applications14. Pleissner et al.15, found that coffee cheery pulp contains substantial quantities of protein (9–11%), lipids (2–17%), cellulose (13–27%), tannin (4.5%), pectin (6.5%), reducing sugar (12.4%), as well as non-nitrogenous extract (57–63%). CP can be processed into cascara tea, a functional drink rich in antioxidants, caffeine, and phenolic compounds16.

Coffee pulp contains high levels of carbohydrates, hemicellulose, and antioxidants, making it suitable for energy food products and functional drinks like cascara tea17. Spent coffee ground (SCG) is rich in dietary fibers and polyphenols, which enhance the nutritional value of baked goods, such as cookies, while extending their shelf life18. In this scenario, Pérez Calvo et al.17 combined coffee pulp with bars to generate functional bars with valuable nutritional qualities.

The growing consumer interest in safe and health-enhancing foods has led to a significant trend in product reformulation within the food industry. This strategy focuses on replacing traditional ingredients with healthier alternatives, which has been shown to positively impact consumer health and acceptance19,20. Research indicates that consumers appreciate both ingredient reduction (e.g., fat and sodium) and nutrient addition (e.g., omega-3) in processed meats21. Successful reformulation can enhance product appeal without compromising sensory qualities, as demonstrated in studies involving various food product22.

The partial substitution of meat with plant-based products is increasingly recognized as a viable strategy to reduce meat consumption while maintaining desirable attributes of meat. This approach is gaining traction among consumers as it allows for dietary adjustments without complete elimination of meat, thus promoting acceptance and adherence. Below are key aspects of this strategy23,24. Studies indicate that substituting animal meat with plant-based meat analogues can enhance satiety and improve diet quality, with increases in dietary fiber and reductions in saturated fat25,26. The reformulation of burgers to reduce saturated fat content is a significant area of research, driven by health concerns associated with high animal fat intake. Various studies have explored alternative ingredients that can replace animal fats while maintaining sensory and physicochemical properties. This transition not only aims to enhance the nutritional profile of burgers but also to cater to consumer preferences for healthier options27–29. In this regard, replacing 25% to 50% of animal fat with pijuayo flour improved tenderness and flavor, with a notable preference for the 25% reduction among consumers30. Fenugreek Seed Gum demonstrated effective fat-like properties, allowing for a 57% reduction in cooking shrinkage and improved texture in low-fat burgers31. The use of oat meal and chia powder not only replaced 50% of animal fat but also enhanced water retention and cooking yield, leading to better overall quality32,33.

The potential of using hydrated CCPP as a fat replacer in beef burgers or processed meat is promising, given its nutritional profile and functional benefits. Although no direct research has been conducted on its application in meat products. Therefore, the current investigation aimed to investigate the impact of adding varying quantities of CCPP as fat replacer on the nutritional and quality properties of beef burger.

Phenolic content (mg GAE/g)), flavonoids content (mg of catechin equivalents (CE) per g DW), anthocyanin content (mg/100 g) and antioxidant activity (%) of Coffee Cherry Pulp powder (Cascara powder)

The total phenolic content (TPC) of CCPP was recorded to be 24.70 mg GAE/g (Table 1), this value significantly enhances its antioxidant properties. This high TPC is crucial as phenolic compounds are known for their ability to scavenge free radicals, thereby providing protective effects against oxidative stress56. High TPC correlates with increased scavenging activity against free radicals, demonstrating potent antioxidant effects10. Cascara extracts have shown significant inhibition of lipid peroxidation, further supporting their antioxidant potential10. The antioxidant properties of phenolic compounds in Cascara contribute to anti-aging effects by protecting skin cells from oxidative damage10,57. This finding aligns with those obtained by Machado et al.6, who reported that TPC of coffee pulp was 2.37 g GAE/ 100 g). A study using an aqueous two-phase system (ATPS) for extracting polyphenols from coffee pulp reported a TPC of 434.63 mg GAE/g, which is significantly higher than the TPC of CCPP58. Another research found that coffee pulp had a TPC of 40.6 mg GAE/g, which is also higher than that of CCPP50. While CCPP has a lower TPC compared to some other coffee by-products, it still represents a valuable source of phenolic compounds. The differences in TPC can be attributed to the extraction methods and the specific coffee by-product analyzed. The extraction method significantly impacts the TPC, with techniques like ATPS and optimized solvent extraction yielding higher phenolic contents58. The variety of coffee and the specific part of the coffee plant used (e.g., pulp, mucilage) also contribute to variations in TPC59.

The total flavonoid content (TFC) of CCPP, was recorded at 9.82 mg CE per g DW, highlighting its potential as a source of bioactive compounds. This value reflects the significant antioxidant properties attributed to cascara, which can be harnessed for various applications. The presence of various phytochemicals, including flavonoids, contributes to its antioxidant properties, making it a valuable ingredient in functional foods60. The TFC of coffee pulp, reported by Machado et al.6 as 1.23 g CE per 100 g, highlights the nutritional potential of this by-product. In this regard, CCPP’s flavonoid content is higher than that of other coffee by-products, such as silverskin and parchment, which have lower concentrations of bioactive compounds6. Different extraction methods yield varying flavonoid levels, for instance, Soxhlet extraction of coffee cherry pulp resulted in a significantly higher TFC of 296.8 mg QE/g extract10. The high flavonoid content positions CCPP as a valuable ingredient for food, cosmetic, and pharmaceutical industries, promoting sustainability in coffee processing61.

The total anthocyanin content of CCPP, has been recorded at 16.68 mg/100 g. This finding aligns with research indicating that coffee pulp is a significant source of anthocyanins, particularly cyanidin-3-rutinoside, which is the predominant pigment identified in various studies. Anthocyanins from coffee pulp exhibit various biological activities, including antioxidant properties and enzyme inhibition, which could be beneficial in food formulations62. The potential for using CCPP as a natural colorant in food products is supported by its anthocyanin content63. The DPPH radical scavenging activity of CCPP, has been reported to be 78.50%. This high level of antioxidant activity indicates the potential of CCPP as a functional ingredient in food applications. Studies indicate that cascara from different coffee varieties, such as arabica and robusta, exhibits strong DPPH inhibition, with values ranging from 53 to 78%37,64, Cascara is rich in phenolic compounds, which contribute to its antioxidant properties. For instance, TPC in cascara can reach up to 80 mg GAE/100 g37. The presence of flavonoids and other phytometabolites further enhances its antioxidant potential, with specific extracts showing IC50 values as low as 9.59 mg/L57. The DPPH radical scavenging activity of CCPP at 78.50% positions it favorably among various natural and synthetic antioxidants. The ethyl acetate fraction of Coffee arabica leaves exhibited an IC50 of 28.2 µg/ml, indicating a stronger antioxidant activity than CCPP65.

The high content of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and anthocyanins in CCPP extracts significantly enhances their antioxidant properties, making them appealing to health-conscious consumers. These compounds exhibit strong free radical scavenging activity, as evidenced by DPPH assays, which measure the ability to neutralize free radicals, thus contributing to the health benefits of the final product.

The fiber content in CCPP can enhance the nutritional value of food products, making them healthier options for consumers47. In this regard, Papageorgiou et al.18, reported that the incorporation of CCPP into formulations can lead to increased dough stiffness and improved shelf life of products, as observed in studies on bakery items. The cooking process of beef burger samples results in moisture loss, which affects the nutritional profile and overall quality. Specifically, burgers with 100% CCPP exhibited a moisture loss of only 6.22%, compared to 10.28% in control samples. This reduction not only preserves moisture but also increases the concentration of protein and fat, thereby improving the nutritional profile of the burgers54.

The incorporation of CCPP into beef burgers significantly enhances their protein content, with levels rising from 17.54 to 19.64% as CCPP inclusion increases (Table 2). This trend highlights the nutritional benefits of utilizing coffee by-products in meat formulations, promoting sustainability in the food industry. Beef burgers with CCPP exhibited higher protein levels compared to control samples without CCPP. The highest protein content (19.64%) was recorded in samples with 20% CCPP, indicating a direct correlation between CCPP levels and protein enhancement (Table 2). Coffee by-products, including CCPP, are recognized as potential sources of alternative proteins, essential amino acids, and bioactive peptides38. The cooking process significantly increases protein content in burger samples, owing to moisture reduction process. Additionally, burgers incorporated with CCPP possessed greater protein contents than control samples. This phenomenon can be attributed to the unique properties of CCPP and its nutritional benefits. In this regard, Ariz et al.70, reported that cooking process reduces moisture and concentrates protein levels in burgers. Plant-based burgers, including those with coffee by-products, have been found to have a protein profile similar to that of beef burgers, while also being richer in minerals and polyunsaturated fatty acids71. The incorporation of CCPP into beef burgers significantly reduces fat content, demonstrating its potential as a fat replacer. Specifically, burgers with 20% and 15% CCPP exhibited fat levels 52.35 and 4.09 times lower than a control burger with 20% beef fat, respectively. Hydrated CCPP serves as an effective natural fat replacer, enhancing moisture and fiber content while significantly lowering fat levels in food products. This dual functionality not only improves the nutritional profile but also maintains desirable sensory attributes in various formulations. Similar studies have shown that other plant-based ingredients, like pijuayo pulp, can also effectively lower fat levels in burgers by replacing animal fat without compromising sensory qualities30,72. Other fat replacers, such as inulin, have demonstrated similar benefits, reducing fat content while maintaining desirable textural properties73. The incorporation of CCPP into burger formulations significantly mitigates fat loss during cooking, attributed to the fiber content of CCPP that enhances fat retention. CCPP contains soluble and insoluble fibers that can absorb and retain fat during cooking, reducing the amount of fat lost compared to burgers without CCPP. Similar studies have shown that other high-fiber ingredients, such as pea fiber and oat flour, also improve fat retention in meat products, indicating a broader trend in utilizing fibers for fat reduction33,74. Reducing fat content in burgers can lead to healthier products, potentially lowering risks associated with high saturated fat intake, such as heart disease30,33.

The ash content in beef burger samples is a significant indicator of mineral content, with uncooked samples showing a range from 1.75 to 3.18% (Table 2). statistically, the addition of CCPP significantly increased the ash content, with the highest level of 3.18% achieved at a 20% CCPP supplementation. This enhancement is attributed to CCPP’s rich mineral profile, which contributes to the overall nutritional value of the burgers. This trend is echoed in research on non-conventional food plants (NCFPs), which also contribute to increased ash content and overall nutritional value in beef burgers75. The ash content of burgers supplemented with 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% CCPP was found to be 1.21, 1.41, 1.62, and 1.81 times higher than that of control samples without CCP, indicating a clear trend of enhanced mineral content due to the addition of CCPP. These findings indicate that the addition of CCPP contributes to higher ash content, which is indicative of increased minerals in the burgers.

The incorporation of CCPP into beef burgers significantly enhances their fiber content (Table 2), aligning with the trend of improving the nutritional profile of meat products. The observed increases in fiber content 13.66, 15.05, 16.66, and 18.05 times higher than control samples at 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% incorporation demonstrate the potential of CCPP dietary fibers to address nutritional deficiencies in traditional meat formulations. Increased dietary fiber intake is significantly associated with reduced cardiovascular disease (CVD) risks and enhanced gut health. Beyond this threshold, a decline in appearance values indicates the necessity for careful moderation in fat replacement to maintain sensory quality. Similar studies on other fat replacers, such as pijuayo pulp and peel flours, indicate that a 25% fat reduction can improve sensory attributes like tenderness and flavor, while higher substitutions may detract from overall liking30,72. Methods

Coffee Cherry Pulp powder (Cascara powder) (CCPP) was purchased from privet coffee producer, in Jizan, Saudi Arabia. Lean beef and beef fat were was purchased from the fresh meat section of Al Tamimi store in Buraidah, Qassim, Saudi Arabia. The reagents and chemical substances used in the present investigation have been ordered from Sigma Aldrich Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). All chemicals and solvents were categorized as analytical.

Hydrated coffee cherry pulp powder

To prepare hydrolyzed CCPP, a series of systematic actions must be undertaken to ensure quality and efficacy. The process begins with washing the CCPP to eliminate contaminants, followed by draining excess water. Hydration is achieved by combining three parts distilled water with one-part CCPP for 24 h at room temperature (25 °C), after which any remaining water is drained before use80. Once hydrated, the CCPP is used immediately in preparing meat burgers,

Processing of beef burger samples containing hydrated CCPP

The burger samples were made using standard techniques68. The lean meat and beef fat were ground using an 8 mm plate by a kitchen mincer (Panasonic Meat Grinder 1700 W, MK-GX1710KTZ, Japan). The meat and fat mixture were subsequently combined with other ingredients (Table 5). The fat appropriate for each formulation was added. All burger samples were made with 65% lean beef. Consequently, the control burger was made with 65% lean meat and 20% beef fat.

Analytical methods

The total viable count (TVC) of CCPP was measured according to the procedures described by Watson et al.35.

Proximate analysis which includes moisture, ash, fat, protein, crude fiber and carbohydrate contents. Were conducted using standard procedure of AOAC81, AOAC 925.09B, AOAC method no. 930.22, AOAC 950.36, AOAC method no. 950.36, AOAC method no. 950.37).

The Association of Official Analytical Chemists81 procedures were implemented to determine the proximate parameters (carbohydrate, lipids, protein, and ash) of samples. The micro-Kjeldahl technique is utilized to measure nitrogen content, which is then converted to crude protein by multiplying the nitrogen value by 6.25 (OAC method no. 950.36). Moisture content was determined using weight difference methods, typically involving drying samples to a constant weight (AOAC 925.09B). Ash content was similarly assessed through incineration, allowing for the quantification of inorganic residues (AOAC method no. 930.22). Crude fat is extracted using petroleum ether, following AOAC protocols, which ensures effective lipid recovery AOAC 950.36. Crude fiber content was determined using a digestion technique (AOAC method no. 950.37), Carbohydrate content is calculated by difference, subtracting the sum of other proximate components from 100%82.

Energy values (kcal/100 g) were estimated using the Atwater conversion factor20

Foam capacity (FC %) The technique provided by Li et al.84 was utilized to estimate the foam capacity (%) of CCPP.

0.5 g of CCPP was mixed with 50 mL of deionized water, and measured the starting liquid height. The foam height was measured every 15 min by stirring at 12,000 r/min for two minutes using a high-speed disperser. Foamability (FC) was measured by comparing the volume of the foam solution after shearing to its initial volume.

Emulsification capacity, measured in milliliters of oil per gram of CCPP was determined as described by El-Adawy et al.80.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), Anthocyanin, DPPH radical scavenging activity

Dried samples (5 g) of CCPP were combined with 100 mL of 70% ethanol and 1% HCl for 72 h at room temperature 25 °C. The mixture was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper to separate the liquid extract from solid residues. The resulting ethanolic extract was stored in capped vials at −25 °C to maintain its integrity for subsequent analyses

The Folin-C Assay was used to evaluate TPC, following earlier protocols described by Kupina et al.85. The TPC was expressed as mg of gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry weight.

TFC was estimated using the aluminum chloride calorimetric technique based on the approach provided by Alsayari et al.86,87. TFC is measured in mg of catechin equivalents (CE) per g DW.

The assessment of anthocyanins concentration was conducted in accordance with the method described by Alshammai et al.56, involves calculating their content as cyanidin 3-glucoside equivalents. The anthocyanin content was determined using an ethanolic HCl solution, which facilitates the extraction of anthocyanins from samples. The absorbance was measured at 530 nm.

The Alsayari et al.86 method for free radical scavenging of stable 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radicals was used to quantitatively assess the extracts’ antioxidant activity.

Ahmed and Ali’s78 method were used to analyze phenolic components in an ethanolic extract of CCPP. HPLC analysis was performed using a Prodigy ODS-2 column (250 mm × 3.2 mm, Phenomenex, Cheshire, UK) with a G1315A diode array detector.

Determination of cholesterol

Cholesterol concentration was determined using previously published techniques by ref. 68. Five grams of fat substances from beef burger samples were extracted with petroleum ether. The petroleum ether was removed by evaporation at 50 °C. The extracted fat was weighed and saponified with aqueous ethanolic KOH solution. The mixture was allowed to cool at room temperature before adding 10 mL of petroleum ether. The mixture was shaken vigorously for one minute. After the layers had separated, the ether layer was transferred to a clean test tube and the solvent was evaporated at 50 °C. To generate a chromophore for colorimetric analysis, acetic acid, ferrous sulfate and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) were added. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm compared to the reagent blank.

Sensory evaluation

Qassin University Ethics Committee authorized the sensory evaluation procedures. Prior to the sensory assessment, panelists were given guidelines to ensure the reliability of their results. All panelists received information about the components in the beef burger samples. Before the study started, the panelists provided informed, written consent. Panelists were asked to participate in a sensory evaluation training session for Check-All-That-Apply (CATA) items. In accordance with ElAnany and Ali68, 50 of healthy judges (20 females and 30 men, aged 25–40 years) were randomly selected from an independent company (producer for burger products) in Qassim, Saudi Arabia, accomplished a sensory evaluation, which is consistent with methodology in sensory analysis. The beef burger samples were a cooked and then cut into equal-sized portions to ensure uniformity in the samples presented to each panelist. Three cut of each formulated beef burger samples were served to the panelist.at 50 °C. Cooked burger samples were placed on white labeled plates with code numbers. Sensory properties of patties were evaluated in terms of appearance, color, texture, juiciness, flavor, and general acceptability (1: not like, 9: extremely like). To mitigate residual flavor effects during sensory evaluations, panelists were provided with plain water between samples, adhering to ISO standards in a controlled environment (ISO-DP ISO 6658:2017). Sensory evaluations were carried out in separate booths under controlled settings (white fluorescent lighting and a room temperature of 22 °C).

Statistical analysis

A one-way ANOVA test (Analysis of variance) was applied for data from five replicates (n = 5), (except sensory results (n = 50), using SPSS software version 21.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Duncan multiple range tests were performed at the 0.05 level, as described by Gomez and Gomez89.
