A Parallel Group, 1:1 Randomised, Pilot Study Comparing the Clinical Efficacy and Bleeding Risk Between Low Dose and Standard Dose Intrapleural Tissue Plasminogen Activator for Adults With Pleural Infection
Authors: Ken Ka Pang Chan, Christopher Chan, David Shu Cheong Hui, Ai Ling Tan, Y C Gary Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA pilot study to assess the feasibility of a randomization trial protocol comparing low (2.5 mg) and standard (10 mg) doses of intrapleural tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) with deoxyribonucleases (DNase) in unresolved pleural infection. The proposed protocol comparing low‐dose and standard doses of intrapleural tPA (with DNase) therapy will be feasible and acceptable.MethodsA single‐centre, two‐arm, double‐blinded, randomised controlled feasibility study which includes subjects with unresolved pleural infection eligible for intrapleural tPA/DNase injection, with follow‐ups till 3 months after hospital discharge. Recruited subjects will be randomised in a 1:1 ratio to receive a maximum of 6 doses of intrapleural tPA (with DNase) starting at either 2.5 or 10 mg. A clinical decision is allowed at or after the third dose of tPA to continue the assigned regimen (blinded) or convert to open‐label use of 10 mg doses of tPA to complete the course based on the clinical response.DiscussionThe main outcome is the feasibility of the trial protocol, based on the percentage of eligible patients enrolled, retained to discharge, and completing 3 months of follow‐up. Other important outcomes include survival at 3 months follow‐up and without the need for surgical intervention, the need for additional pleural interventions, the number of decisions to convert to open‐labelled use of 10 mg intrapleural tPA, clinical and radiographic response after the treatment course, safety profiles, especially bleeding complications and the number and reason for protocol violation. Feasibility outcomes will be reported as descriptive data. Comparison of outcomes between the two treatment groups will be analysed on an intention‐to‐treat basis. Safety outcomes will be reported descriptively for each group. The reported estimates of recruitment rates, adherence, follow‐up completeness, and variability and event rates for key clinical and bleeding outcomes will inform the design of future studies that incorporate the current study design. Trial registration: NCT05766124, ClinicalTrials.gov Keywords: intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy, pilot feasibility study, pleural infection, tissue plasminogen activator

Introduction

Pleural infection is associated with prolonged hospitalization and poor clinical prognosis. Its management frequently requires pleural drainage and use of intrapleural fibrinolysis therapy, with a combination of intrapleural tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and deoxyribonuclease (DNase). Various doses of intrapleural tPA with clinically efficacious outcomes have been reported in the literature, and the optimal dose of tPA remains undefined. There was no randomised controlled trial (RCT) comparing the traditional “standard” dose (10 mg) and reduced dose (2.5 mg) of tPA.

The TRAPPE study is a pilot study to serve as preparatory work before proceeding to a full‐scale, multicentre RCT. The feasibility of the study design involving flexibility of dose escalation and various clinical parameters will be tested for suitability as study outcome measurements before proceeding to a full‐scale RCT.

Background and Rationale

Pneumonia is one of the commonest diseases that requires hospitalization and it ranks second as the cause of death in Hong Kong [1]. Parapneumonic effusion (PPE) is frequently associated with pneumonia and leads to a worse prognosis. Complicated PPE (CPPE) and empyema, collectively known as pleural infection, can increase mortality and consumption of healthcare resources [2, 3]. Complete evacuation of pleural fluid can be challenging even with adequate large‐bore chest drain placement, due to the viscous nature of the fluid and extensive septations partitioning the infected fluid into multiple locules in the pleural space [4, 5]. Antibiotic treatment and drainage of infected pleural effusion can only cure 70% of pleural infections [6, 7]. Surgical decortication is associated with unavoidable bleeding, anesthetic risk and a high rate of chronic post‐thoracotomy pain [8]. The advent of intrapleural fibrinolysis therapy by a combination of tPA and DNase can significantly improve drainage of pleural fluid and avoid surgery in more than 90% of patients [4, 7, 9, 10].

The optimal dose of intrapleural tPA (to be used with DNase) remains undefined. The original ‘standard’ dose (10 mg) used in the landmark MIST‐2 RCT was chosen empirically [7] and had not been subjected to conventional dose‐escalation assessment or long‐term pharmacovigilance follow‐up [11]. tPA accounts for the majority of the cost of the combination tPA/DNase therapy and has potential bleeding risks.

Rates of bleeding following intrapleural tPA/DNase therapy vary from < 5% to as high as 17% in published studies [7, 9, 10, 12]. Clinically significant bleeding warrants additional intervention including packed cell transfusion, radiological interventions, and even surgery. This risk is therefore one of the driving forces urging the need to find a safer and lower effective dose. It is likely that the risk of pleural bleed is dose‐dependent. A study using 20 mg of intrapleural tPA was associated with a doubling of serious bleeding complications (28%; versus 10 mg of tPA) [12]. Studies of de‐escalation of tPA doses appear to show a reducing trend in bleeding rates with lower starting doses of intrapleural tPA: 4.9% and 2.9% when using 5 mg and 2.5 mg of tPA respectively [13, 14].

The multicentre dose de‐escalation ADAPT and ADAPT‐2 studies have confirmed that a starting dose of 5 mg and 2.5 mg intrapleural tPA were safe and effective. They employed a pragmatic approach to begin therapy with a lower tPA dose (2.5 mg or 5 mg) and allow escalation to the conventional dose (10 mg) if attending clinicians are concerned with a lack of clinical response [13, 14]. In the ADAPT‐2 study, a starting dose of tPA at 2.5 mg remained efficacious, with only 2 patients (2.9%) requiring surgery and a low rate of bleeding complications (2.9%) [14], comparable with the original RCT using 10 mg of tPA [7]. The lower dose of tPA, with potentially lower bleeding risk, provides an attractive alternative, especially in patients who require therapeutic anticoagulation and those with acquired coagulopathy due to overwhelming sepsis [15, 16, 17].

There are no high‐quality RCT data comparing therapeutic outcomes and bleeding rates between low (2.5 mg) and standard doses (10 mg) of intrapleural tPA in pleural infection. The lack of this important evidence is also reflected by heterogeneous practice in using different doses, ranging from 1 to 10 mg, of intrapleural tPA from a recent international survey among experts in pleural medicine. The respondents would consider using a lower starting dose of tPA (with the possibility of escalation if clinically needed) if a median of 80% of patients could be successfully treated at that dose [16].

A multicentre RCT involving low and standard doses of intrapleural tPA is required to compare their therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles [16]. Such studies are challenging to design. Clinically relevant and patient‐oriented outcomes [13, 14] are clearly preferable over conventional radiographic clearance [7]. There are no set definitions of clinical resolution of pleural infection for several reasons. These patients are complex; separating the pleural infection from the concurrent/underlying source of (usually lung) infection is difficult as inflammatory parameters and blood markers are confounded. Patients typically have multiple comorbidities; clinical ‘improvement’ or ‘deterioration’ has to be considered by taking into account multiple parameters. Decisions to stop/step down antibiotics or to refer to surgical decortication, to insert more or to remove chest drains, and when to discharge home all need to be individualised and cannot be governed by set rules. Clinical practice varies across institutions and among different countries [16]. For these reasons, the study dose is preferably blinded, given the element of subjective decision on treatment success or failure. Therefore, a unique and pragmatic trial design is necessary to allow the clinicians to determine if the randomised and blinded dose is providing the expected clinical response. Allowing the clinicians to revert to an open‐label use of ‘standard’ 10 mg doses of tPA at any point after the initial 24 h of treatment would alleviate any concern from physicians and patients about the delay in receiving effective treatment doses.

Given the high success rate and relatively low bleeding complication rate, a full‐scale non‐inferiority study will require a very large cohort. Before that is undertaken, a pilot feasibility study is necessary to assess this pragmatic trial design, including its acceptability to patients and clinicians (in the form of enrolment rates), the practical running of the protocol (e.g., compliance) and completion rates. The likelihood of clinicians reverting to open‐labelled ‘standard’ 10 mg dosing will also provide useful data to guide future trial design. Such RCT should allow escalation of the tPA dose if clinicians believe there is inadequate clinical responses that met pre‐set criteria [13, 14, 16]. This unique approach would alleviate any concern from participants and enrolling clinicians that patients may be disadvantaged in receiving lower doses of treatment.

This pilot study serves as preparatory work before proceeding to a full‐scale, multicentre RCT. The feasibility of the study design involving flexibility of dose escalation and various clinical parameters will be tested for suitability as study outcome measurements before proceeding to a full‐scale RCT. This eventual full study and the ultimate goal will provide novel insight into the clinical outcomes by using different doses of intrapleural tPA in treating pleural infection. Establishing the optimal dose of intrapleural therapy will inform physicians and translate to better patient outcomes [18].

Objective

In this pilot RCT, we aim to test the feasibility and acceptability of a study design that incorporates a low starting dose of intrapleural tPA with a dose escalation approach, including recruitment, protocol adherence, and follow‐up procedures. We will also explore clinical outcomes to generate preliminary estimates of effect by comparing the lower (2.5 mg) and standard doses (10 mg) of intrapleural tPA (with DNase) in patients with unresolved pleural infection despite chest tube drainage and systemic antibiotics.

Trial Design

This is a single‐centre, two‐arm, double‐blinded, feasibility RCT.

Methods

Participants, Interventions and Outcomes

The clinical trial will be conducted at the Department of Medicine & Therapeutics, Prince of Wales Hospital, Hong Kong. The site principal investigator (PI) will screen patients with unresolved pleural infection requiring intrapleural tPA/DNase to be included in the study. Potential patients fulfilling the following eligibility criteria will be approached and offered inclusion in the study. Informed consent will be obtained prior to enrolment. After consent, participants will be assigned to a study arm according to the pre‐generated randomization sequence. All participants will receive standard care for their conditions as per their treating clinicians. Enrolment and screening logs will be maintained.

Study Setting

This study will enrol 30 consecutive eligible patients with unresolved pleural infection requiring intrapleural tPA/DNase.

Participant Withdrawal Criteria

Participants can withdraw at any time from the study and do not need to provide the research staff with a reason. There may be reasons for the PI to decide to withdraw a participant from the study. This could be due to an inability to comply with the study protocol, such as availability for follow‐ups or other compliance issues. A participant may also be withdrawn in their best interests. In all cases, the study withdrawal form will be completed. All participant data up until the time of withdrawal will be retained. Withdrawn participants will not be replaced. Where considered clinically necessary, withdrawn participants will be asked to return to the clinic for a safety follow‐up appointment(s).

Participant Timeline

Follow‐up appointments will be arranged for patients 1 month after discharge or completion of antibiotic (whichever is earlier), and 3 months.

Sample Size

In this study, a total sample size of 30 patients (15 in each arm) has been selected pragmatically as appropriate for a single‐centre pilot feasibility randomised trial, allowing assessment of key protocol parameters, including monthly recruitment rate, consent/eligibility ratio, retention to discharge and 3‐month follow‐up (among survivors), protocol adherence/compliance, and data completeness for core clinical outcomes.

Recruitment

The study will enrol participants for a period of 18–24 months. This is based on the number of potentially suitable participants seen in the recruiting centre (around 1–2 per month).

Treatment Description

After consent, all patients will be randomised to receive intrapleural tPA of either low or standard dose in a 1:1 ratio. These drugs will be injected twice daily via the chest drain for up to 6 doses. After the second dose of tPA at the assigned doses and before each injection of the remaining doses of tPA, the attending physician will be allowed to continue with the assigned dose of tPA or escalate to 10 mg tPA based on the clinical response, without knowing the previous doses and not breaking the blinding (Figure 1).

Study protocol flowchart of the TRAPPE pilot randomized controlled trial. Adult patients with unresolved pleural infection after chest drain insertion and antibiotics are randomized in a 1:1 double‐blinded manner to receive intrapleural tPA 2.5 mg plus DNase or tPA 10 mg plus DNase. After the first two blinded doses, clinicians review the patient’s clinical response before each subsequent dose and may continue the assigned blinded dose or escalate to open‐label 10 mg tPA if clinically indicated, up to a maximum of six doses. The key feasibility endpoints are the proportion of eligible patients successfully randomized, the proportion retained to hospital discharge, and the proportion completing 3‐month follow‐up or death, whichever occurs first. Follow‐up is performed at 1 and 3 months after discharge.

Additional pleural interventions for failed medical treatment are allowed if unresolved pleural infection and substantial residual pleural collection amendable to further drainage are still present after the course of intrapleural tPA/DNase. Poor radiographic response in a patient showing acceptable symptom improvement is not an indication for additional pleural interventions. Choices of pleural intervention include therapeutic thoracentesis (volume > 200 mL), chest drain insertion, and surgical referral for surgical decortication, which will be chosen at the discretion of the attending physician. For additional drain placement, the size of the drain, the volume of fluid drained, and additional intrapleural tPA/DNase (standard dose) through the drain will be recorded. The following management will not be allowed under the study conditions, including intrapleural antibiotic therapy, intrapleural saline/water irrigation (simple water/saline flushes to maintain chest drain patency are acceptable, if necessary), and use of systemic corticosteroids solely for pleural infection.

Assignment of Intervention Allocation

Sequence Generation

All recruited patients with proven pleural infection fulfilling the inclusion/exclusion criteria will be randomised to receive intrapleural tPA at a starting dose of either low dose (2.5 mg) or standard dose (10 mg) in a 1:1 ratio. Eligible patients will be randomised according to a pre‐generated sequence of 30 subject numbers using the True Random Number Generator on Random.org. The list will only be accessible by an independent research nurse not involved in patient care or outcome assessment.

Blinding

The commercial product of tPA is available in powder form and it must be reconstituted into a solution with sterile water, with a final concentration of 1 mg tPA per mL. The dose of tPA instilled can be controlled by adjusting the volume of tPA aspirated. Before intrapleural instillation of tPA through the chest drain, the tPA must be mixed with normal saline to make up to a 40 mL solution for easy injection and flushing the dead space of the drain. Therefore, by altering the volume of the diluent during this mixing process, the blinding of tPA can be accomplished by standardising the final volume of the instillation solution. The colourless nature of the tPA solution also facilitates blinding the staff who inject the drug.

The first two tPA doses will be pre‐prepared in a trial numbered pack and dispensed to the ward at randomization. The subsequent four doses will be prepared after the decision on whether an escalation of the dose is required. The independent nurse reconstitutes and dilutes tPA to a standard 40 mL volume, labels the syringe as ‘intrapleural tPA (research medication)’ without specifying the dose to the clinical team, and stores it at 4°C with the patient study number. According to the information provided by the manufacturer, reconstituted tPA remains stable for up to 24 h, after which any unused solution will be discarded and a new vial will be prepared.

The research team, clinicians, clinical nursing staff, and patients will be blinded to the treatment arm. A copy of the randomization code will be held only by a research nurse performing randomization, who is not involved in data collection and patient care. This staff will be responsible for preparing the tPA of assigned doses. The treatment allocation will not be recorded in the clinical notes, but it will be clearly documented in the study proforma for the purpose of urgent unblinding. Access to this code for a patient before the completion of the trial will only be permitted in exceptional circumstances, such as being necessary to determine the best acute medical care for a patient, and only after discussion with the PI.

Data Collection, Management and Analysis

Data Collection Methods

Data from paper case report forms is being transposed into the study Excel database for the designated study statistician. This will be re‐identifiable. Consent to this study is extended, so data collected from this study will be used in future related studies.

Data Management

All procedures for the handling and analysis of data will be conducted using International Council for Harmonization Good clinical practice guidelines and local policies and procedures for the handling and analysis of data. Data collected will be stored at sites on password‐protected computers accessible only by the site research staff and held in the department where the PI is based. All physical documentation will be stored in a secure, locked filing cabinet with restricted access in private offices within the PI's department in line with the local policy guidelines for research data archiving. All data associated with this trial will be archived for 15 years after the completion of the project. After the archival period, personal data in both paper and electronic records will be shredded and erased respectively.

Confidentiality

Patient privacy and confidentiality will be maintained, as any information that identifies participants will be available only at the enrolment study site and only to designated study investigators, all of whom will either have signed a confidentiality agreement or be employees of the hospital.

Statistical Methods

A full statistical analysis plan will be finalised before the completion of data collection and will be uploaded to an open‐access platform. In general, data will be analysed on an intention‐to‐treat basis for all randomised subjects. The demographics, feasibility metrics (e.g., recruitment rates, protocol adherence, follow‐up completeness) and clinical characteristics will be summarised using descriptive statistics for each treatment group. The pre‐defined criteria for trial feasibility include > 50% of eligible patients are successfully randomised, > 95% of randomised participants are retained to discharge, and > 80% of randomised participants complete 3 months of follow‐up or until death, whichever earlier. Changes in parameters before and after treatment within the same subject will be summarised descriptively and, where appropriate, explored using paired t‐test or Wilcoxon signed rank test, recognising that these analyses are exploratory in the context of a pilot study. Clinical and safety outcomes between the intervention groups will be presented as between‐group estimates (e.g., mean or median differences, risk differences, and corresponding confidence intervals); exploratory independent t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test and simple categorical comparisons may be performed but will not be used for definitive hypothesis testing. Time‐to‐event data (e.g., time to treatment failure or additional intervention) will be described using Kaplan–Meier curves and median times with confidence intervals; formal proportional hazards modelling will not be a primary focus given the limited sample size. All patients who received at least a single fibrinolytic dose will be included in the safety analysis, with adverse events summarised descriptively by treatment group.

An interim analysis will be performed after 10 subjects have been enrolled (one‐third of the target recruitment) to assess the practicality of continuing the trial and the likelihood of achieving key feasibility objectives. The purpose is to assess the rate of recruitment, protocol adherence, and data completeness to determine the feasibility of fulfilling the enrolment target and key feasibility criteria, and review the rate and nature of AE in both groups descriptively to identify any safety concerns that might warrant study modification or cessation.

Missing Data

Missing data may exist either in the form of total non‐response (e.g., attrition due to death or patient withdrawal) or item non‐response (when some but not all of the required information is collected from the patient). Efforts shall be made to minimise the missing data due to item non‐response. This will maximise proper and complete data collection. Throughout the trial, participants will have regular contact with the respiratory department and the study team. This will maximise proper and complete data collection. The research team will document as accurately as possible the reasons for any non‐completion or missing data, thereby minimising truly absent data.

Data Monitoring

Source data will be scrutinised to ensure robust data is provided. Data entered in the case report forms (CRFs) transcribed from source documents must be consistent with the source documents or the discrepancies must be explained. Any discrepancies should be resolved with the PI or documented as File Notes. Procedure deviations and/or violations may be determined at this time and must be reported according to local procedure/policy. Source documents are filed at the PI's site. After the data have been entered into the study database, a system of data validation checks will be implemented and applied to the database. The study database will be updated in accordance with the resolved query reports. All changes to the study database will be documented.

A Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB) consists of experienced respiratory physicians and trial statisticians who are all independent from the sponsor of the study and have no competing interests, will be established to ensure the safety of study participants through study procedures, reviewing adverse events and serious adverse events, and considering new data (recently published studies) that may determine the validity of study continuation. All deaths, anticipated or unanticipated, will be discussed with the DSMB. The committee determines whether significant benefits or risks have been uncovered which may have an impact on the feasibility and/or ethical conduct of the study. The DSMB will also help to ensure the scientific integrity of the study by reviewing the quality of the data it uses to make its decisions. The DSMB provides recommendations and determines whether the study should continue, be suspended, or be terminated.

Harms

Side effects of intrapleural tPA have been described about the use of intrapleural tPA, including pleural bleeding (ranging from mild local bleeding to severe systemic bleeding) and pain. The participants will be monitored as per standard of care guidelines and during follow‐ups throughout the study. The hemodynamic status, drain output and its nature, Hb level, clotting profile, breathlessness, and pain score will be monitored regularly. In the event of suspected or confirmed significant bleeding complications, additional blood checks, imaging, and thoracic surgery consultation will be triggered to ensure timely management.

Auditing

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the study design comparing the two starting doses of intrapleural tPA in patients with unresolved pleural infection. The findings of this study may inform future study design and implementation of multicentre RCTs involving patients with unresolved pleural infection.
