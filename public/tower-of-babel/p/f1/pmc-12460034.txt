Ex Vivo Evaluation of Sample Yields among New Semi-automatic, Conventional Semi-automatic, and Full-core Biopsy Needles
Authors: Yoshiki Endo, Hirofumi Sekino, Yoko Nihei, Ryo Yamakuni, Daichi Kuroiwa, Shiro Ishii, Kenji Fukushima, Hiroshi Ito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose: To evaluate the sample weights obtained using a new semi-automatic biopsy needle and a new full-core biopsy needle, and to compare them with those obtained using a conventional semi-automatic needle under identical ex vivo conditions.Material and Methods: A total of three biopsy needles were compared: the semi-automatic biopsy needles TEMNO Elite™ (Merit Medical Systems, South Jordan, UT, USA) and STARCUTⓇ (TSK Laboratory, Tochigi, Japan) and the full-core automatic biopsy needle CorVocet™ (Merit Medical Systems), with throw lengths of 15 mm, 20 mm, and 25 mm. Two materials were used for sampling: kamaboko (a type of Japanese fish cake) and chicken breast muscle. For each material, 20 specimens were collected using each needle type, resulting in 100 samples per material. All procedures were conducted under identical conditions.Results: The TEMNO Elite™ needle yielded the heaviest samples among all tested needles in both kamaboko (9.21 ± 0.95 mg) and chicken breast (5.92 ± 0.51 mg), significantly surpassing all others (p < 0.05). STARCUTⓇ collected significantly more tissue than CorVocet™ (15 mm) in both materials (p < 0.05), while CorVocet™ (25 mm) collected more than STARCUTⓇ in chicken breast (p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between STARCUTⓇ and CorVocet™ (20 mm) in both materials. For CorVocet™, sample weight increased with throw length in both materials.Conclusions: The TEMNO Elite™ needle collected the heaviest biopsy specimens among all tested needles in both model materials.

Introduction

In recent years, precision medicine based on immunohistochemical and molecular information has gained widespread recognition in cancer treatment [1]. Compared with surgical biopsy, percutaneous image-guided needle biopsy is simpler and less invasive [2]. Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated that image-guided needle biopsy is effective not only at the time of initial diagnosis but also during disease progression, allowing for immunohistochemical diagnosis and cancer gene mutation analysis [2-5]. Consequently, there is an increasing clinical demand for minimally invasive biopsy methods that can obtain larger tissue samples. Semi-automatic and full-core biopsy needles are primarily used for these procedures. Various manufacturers produce biopsy needles with distinct features, and several studies have compared their performances [6-9].

Recently, two new biopsy needles have become available in Japan from Merit Medical: the semi-automatic biopsy needle TEMNO Elite™ (Merit Medical Systems, South Jordan, UT, USA) and the full-core biopsy needle CorVocet™ (Merit Medical Systems). The CorVocet™ is a full-core automatic biopsy needle capable of ejecting up to 25 mm of tissue and extracting cylindrical tissue samples. Both products claim to offer improved tissue sampling capacity compared to traditional models. This study aims to evaluate the sample weight obtained using these two new biopsy needles and compare it with conventional needles under identical conditions.

Material and Methods

Ethical approval

Institutional ethics approval was deemed unnecessary since this study did not involve human or animal subjects.

Characteristics of biopsy needles

The biopsy needles used in this study and their characteristics are as follows, and photographs of each are shown in Fig. 1.

(a), (b) TEMNO EliteTM appearance and magnified view of needle tip. The notch length is approximately 19 mm. (c), (d) STARCUT® appearance and magnified view of the needle tip. The notch length is approximately 17 mm. (e), (f) CorVocetTM appearance and magnified view of throw length adjustment knob.

New semi-automatic cutting biopsy needle - TEMNO Elite™

The characteristic feature of this biopsy needle is the use of a new technology called Total Core™ biopsy Technology. This technology utilizes the conventional side-notch mechanism and reduces the amount of metal surrounding the notch, thereby expanding the inner lumen and increasing the sample volume. Additionally, the needle includes the use of Sample Assist, a hands-free function that releases the sample from the biopsy needle with a button press on the handle, simplifying sample retrieval. The notch length for sampling in these biopsy needles is approximately 19 mm.

Aspiration-type semi-automatic cutting biopsy needle - STARCUTⓇ (TSK Laboratory, Tochigi, Japan)

A characteristic feature of this biopsy needle is the incorporation of aspiration into the biopsy procedure, requiring an extension tube, a three-way stopcock, and the needle itself. The notch length for sampling in these biopsy needles is approximately 17 mm. STARCUTⓇ has been reported to collect larger sample volumes compared to other semi-automatic needles, such as MISSION™ (Becton, Dickinson and Company, NJ, USA), SuperCoreⓇ (Argon Medical Devices, Plano, TX, USA), TEMNO EvolutionⓇ (Merit Medical Systems), FINE COREⓇ (Dr. Japan, Tokyo, Japan), and Quick-CoreⓇ (IZI Medical Products, Owings Mills, MD, USA). Based on these findings, STARCUTⓇ was considered a representative conventional semi-automatic needle for this study [7]. The biopsy procedure was as follows:

1. A syringe was attached to an extension tube equipped with a three-way stopcock.

2. Once the needle reached the target tumor, the plunger was pulled back to create negative pressure.

3. The stopcock was then closed to preserve the negative pressure.

4. With the negative pressure maintained, the cutting mechanism of the biopsy needle was engaged.

The disadvantages of this biopsy needle are that the procedure is somewhat complex due to the addition of suction, and there is a potential risk of contusion to the sample tissue.

Full-core automatic biopsy needle - CorVocet™

This needle uses a full-core biopsy system. The advantage of this design lies in the structure of the cannula, which securely contains the tissue to be harvested, allowing for larger tissue collection in a single biopsy than conventional products that use notch sampling systems. Additionally, the throw length can be adjusted from 10 mm to 25 mm in 1 mm increments using a rotary knob on the handle. This adjustment feature allows the needle to be fired safely and precisely, even when vital organs are located near the lesion. The biopsy needle can only target the lesion by fine-tuning the throw length, minimizing the risk of injury to adjacent structures. It also permits biopsies of moderately hard lesions or tissues that are not as hard as bone based on subjective experience.

Biopsy procedure

Three biopsy needles were compared: TEMNO Elite™ and STARCUTⓇ (semi-automatic), and CorVocet™ (full-core automatic), with CorVocet™ offering throw lengths of 15 mm, 20 mm, and 25 mm. All needles were 18 G in size. Two different materials were used to evaluate the sample collection performance of biopsy needles: kamaboko and chicken breast muscle. Kamaboko, a homogeneous and elastic fish cake, was selected for its standardized texture and reproducibility, making it suitable for controlled bench testing. In parallel, chicken breast was used to simulate a more biologically relevant tissue environment with fibrous and heterogeneous characteristics. For each material, 20 samples were collected per biopsy needle under identical conditions. All biopsies were performed by a board-certified radiologist in their fifth year of practice, with needles sequentially rotated after each biopsy. The samples were collected by rubbing the material onto small medicine paper. After each sample collection, the puncture site was marked, and subsequent biopsies were taken at new sites to avoid overlap with the marked area. During biopsies with the STARCUTⓇ needle, suction was applied using a 5 mL syringe and a 50 cm extension tube.

Measurement of sample weight

Sample weights were measured using an electronic scale (Shimadzu Analytical Balance AUW220D; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a readability of 0.01 mg. First, the weight of the medicine paper was measured and recorded as the “pre-weight.” After the biopsy, the sample weights were measured together with the pre-weighed medicine paper and recorded as the “post-weight.” The sample weights (mg) were calculated by subtracting the pre-weight from the post-weight.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was set at 20 specimens per group, based on effect sizes reported in prior studies [7]. To confirm the adequacy of this sample size, a post hoc power analysis was performed using effect sizes, calculated from observed group means and pooled standard deviations. Pairwise effect sizes were expressed as Cohen's d to quantify the magnitude of differences between needle types. In general, Cohen's d values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 are interpreted as small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively. A power value of ≥0.80 was considered acceptable [10]. This analysis was applied to all pairwise comparisons across both materials. Levene's test for homogeneity of variances was first conducted to assess whether the assumption of equal variances was satisfied for comparing sample weights across different biopsy needles. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify whether the sample weights followed a normal distribution. Based on the results of Levene's test, Welch's ANOVA followed by the Games-Howell post hoc test was applied to the kamaboko data due to unequal variances. For chicken breast, where homogeneity of variances was confirmed, standard one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used. For CorVocet™, an additional sub-analysis examined the relationship between throw length (15 mm, 20 mm, and 25 mm) and sample weight using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Given the small sample size (n = 20), a simple linear regression model was also developed to predict sample weight based on throw length. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0.0.0). Effect sizes (Cohen's d) and post hoc power analyses were calculated using custom scripts based on standard statistical formulas.

Results

The sample weights obtained using each biopsy needle for both kamaboko and chicken breast are summarized in Table 1. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that all sample weights were normally distributed in both materials (all p > 0.05). Levene's test indicated significant heterogeneity of variances in kamaboko (p = 0.029), while variances were homogeneous in chicken breast (p = 0.590). Therefore, Welch's ANOVA was applied to the kamaboko dataset, and standard one-way ANOVA was used for the chicken breast dataset.

In kamaboko, Welch's ANOVA revealed significant differences in sample weights among the needle types (F = 163.90, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons using the Games-Howell test are summarized in Table 2. TEMNO Elite™ needle collected significantly heavier samples than all other needles (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). STARCUTⓇ collected significantly heavier samples than CorVocet™ (15 mm) (p < 0.05), but there was no significant difference between STARCUTⓇ and CorVocet™ (20 mm) (p > 0.05).

In chicken breast, one-way ANOVA showed significant differences in sample weights among the needle types (F = 56.89, p < 0.001). Post hoc comparisons using Tukey's HSD test are summarized in Table 3. TEMNO Elite™ needle collected significantly heavier samples than all other needles (p < 0.05 for all comparisons). STARCUTⓇ collected significantly heavier samples than both CorVocet™ (15 mm) (p < 0.05), whereas CorVocet™ (25 mm) yielded significantly more tissue than STARCUTⓇ (p < 0.05).

The results of the post hoc power analysis are summarized in Table 4. Most comparisons showed high statistical power (≥0.9), except for the comparison between STARCUTⓇ and CorVocet™ (20 mm) in kamaboko (power = 0.113). The same comparison in chicken breast demonstrated moderate power (0.756).

For CorVocet™, a sub-analysis was performed to examine the relationship between throw length (15 mm, 20 mm, and 25 mm) and sample weight. In both kamaboko and chicken breast, Pearson's correlation revealed a strong positive correlation between throw length and sample weight (kamaboko: r = 0.897, p < 0.01; chicken breast: r = 0.857, p < 0.01). A simple linear regression model was developed using kamaboko data:

y = 0.255x + 0.688

where x is the throw length (mm), and y is the predicted sample weight (mg). The model achieved an R2 value of 0.796.

A corresponding regression model for chicken breast was:

y = 0.16x + 1.461

with an R2 value of 0.681, also indicating a strong linear relationship. These results support the association between longer throw length and increased sample yield across different tissue types.

Discussion

TEMNO Elite™ represents a new type of semi-automatic biopsy needle, while CorVocet™ is a newly introduced full-core automatic biopsy needle. TEMNO Elite™ collected the heaviest sample among all tested needles in this study. When comparing TEMNO Elite™ with the conventional semi-automatic STARCUTⓇ, the average sample weight was 9.21 mg ± 0.95 mg for TEMNO Elite™ and 5.84 mg ± 0.45 mg for STARCUTⓇ in kamaboko, and 5.92 mg ± 0.51 mg and 4.93 mg ± 0.46 mg, respectively, in chicken breast. These results indicate that TEMNO Elite™ collected approximately 57% more tissue than STARCUTⓇ in kamaboko and 20% more in chicken breast. This difference may be attributed to the longer and deeper notch design of TEMNO Elite™, known as Total Core™ Biopsy Technology, which enables greater sample collection than conventional semi-automatic needles.

In contrast, when comparing TEMNO Elite™ with the full-core automatic CorVocet™ set to a throw length of 25 mm, the average sample weight for CorVocet™ was 6.97 mg ± 0.60 mg in kamaboko and 5.50 mg ± 0.53 mg in chicken breast, approximately 76% and 93% of the weight obtained with TEMNO Elite™, respectively. The notch shape of STARCUTⓇ and TEMNO Elite™ is a half-moon or similar shape, with notch lengths of 17 mm and 19 mm, respectively. In contrast, CorVocet™ has a maximum cylindrical extraction cavity of approximately 24 mm and a throw length of 25 mm, so it was expected that CorVocet™ would yield the heaviest sample given the internal structure of these needles. However, the results differed from this expectation, particularly in kamaboko, suggesting that extraction cavity size alone may not be the primary determinant of sample yield. A comparison between STARCUTⓇ and CorVocet™ further supports this finding. Although CorVocet™ has a larger internal cavity, no significant difference in sample weight was observed between CorVocet™ set to a throw length of 20 mm and STARCUTⓇ in kamaboko, and the same result was obtained in chicken breast.

To support the interpretation of such findings, post hoc power analysis results are summarized in Table 4. Most pairwise comparisons across both materials demonstrated high statistical power (≥0.9), particularly those involving TEMNO Elite™ and STARCUTⓇ. However, one exception was the STARCUTⓇ vs. CorVocet™ 20 mm comparison in kamaboko, where power was markedly low (0.113), likely due to a minimal mean difference. In contrast, the same comparison in chicken breast yielded a moderate power (0.756), though no significant difference was ultimately observed (p > 0.05). These findings suggest that tissue properties may influence not only sampling efficiency but also statistical detectability.

This discrepancy implies that the effectiveness of the CorVocet™ needle may vary depending on tissue properties. Other factors, such as the notch design and interaction between the material and cavity, may be more critical in determining sample yield. According to a previous report, CorVocet™ collected significantly larger samples than the conventional full-core automatic needle (Tru-Core™; Argon Medical Devices, Inc., Frisco, TX, USA) in liver biopsies, indicating that CorVocet™ outperforms traditional needles in this category [9]. However, the current study suggests that the test material, kamaboko, should be interpreted with caution for evaluating sample yield with full-core automatic needles. Kamaboko, a food product with a nearly uniform structure, has a volume expected to correlate closely with its mass. Thus, if the extraction cavity were fully filled, CorVocet™, with its larger cavity, should theoretically yield the heaviest sample. The fact that CorVocet™ did not perform as expected suggests that the material did not sufficiently interact with or fill the cavity. In contrast, chicken breast, which contains a natural fibrous and heterogeneous structure, may better simulate actual biopsy targets. Overall, the results suggest that with relatively soft and uniform materials like kamaboko, the semi-automatic TEMNO Elite™ may outperform the full-core automatic CorVocet™ in terms of sample yield. However, the difference between the two needles was smaller in chicken breast, which has more biological resemblance than kamaboko. This finding suggests that CorVocet™ may yield larger samples in tissues that are harder and more fibrous than chicken breast, such as actual tumors.

A statistically significant correlation was found between the throw length and the amount of sample collected for CorVocet™ (p < 0.01). A strong correlation coefficient (r = 0.897) was identified in kamaboko for the predictive formula, and the high coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.796) confirms the predictive strength of the model. A similar correlation was also observed in chicken breast (r = 0.857), supporting the generalizability of the throw length-yield relationship across different materials. Previous research has shown that an increased cancer detection rate significantly correlates with the number of samples examined [11], suggesting that the positive predictive value improves with the amount of collected samples. CorVocet™ enables biopsies of lesions located near vital organs relatively safely. By adjusting the throw length, an appropriate amount of tissue can be obtained, which is expected to be useful for pathological diagnosis.

This study has several limitations. First, this study used kamaboko and chicken breast as substitute materials, which differ from real organs or tumors regarding structural and mechanical properties. As previously mentioned, the sample yield may vary depending on the hardness and composition of the tissue. Second, although TEMNO Elite™ and CorVocet™ are reported to reduce tissue and cellular damage compared to conventional needles, this study did not evaluate tissue damage. Last, the study did not assess the suitability of each biopsy needle for different types of samples. CorVocet™ may be particularly useful for organs with dense, relatively hard fibrous components or lesions with sclerotic changes, such as cirrhosis or prostate cancer.

In conclusion, using kamaboko and chicken breast samples, TEMNO Elite™ collected the heaviest samples among the STARCUTⓇ and CorVocet™ biopsy needles. Further studies are needed to evaluate sample damage and diagnostic accuracy for actual tumors using these biopsy needles.
