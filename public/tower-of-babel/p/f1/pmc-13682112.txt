The role of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR/ErbB1) in diabetic retinopathy and its therapeutic implications
Authors: Marwan Malluhi, Dina Tluli, Saghir Akhtar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic retinopathy (DR) remains a major cause of preventable vision loss worldwide and arises from the convergence of chronic inflammation, oxidative injury, and metabolic dysregulation that ultimately compromises vascular and neuronal integrity of the retina. Although intravitreal anti-VEGF agents have improved the management of DR, significant limitations such as incomplete therapeutic response, repeated administration and high cost underscore the need for alternative or adjunctive strategies.Main bodyEmerging preclinical evidence suggests that dysregulated signaling via the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) plays a critical role in DR pathogenesis. EGFR and some of its ligands are expressed across multiple retinal cell types and play important roles in normal development and in regulating homeostasis in adult retinas. However, in hyperglycemia/diabetes enhanced EGFR signaling promotes vascular hyperpermeability, pericyte loss, glial reactivity, inflammatory amplification, and pro-fibrotic remodeling that contributes to both early microvascular dysfunction and later proliferative changes in experimental models. Several preclinical studies indicate that pharmacologic blockade of EGFR or downstream signaling pathways attenuates angiogenesis, inflammation, and gliosis in models of DR.ConclusionsThese emerging preclinical findings implicate EGFR as a promising therapeutic target to complement or extend beyond VEGF-centered approaches. Further validation of the role of EGFR and its ligands in mediating retinal pathology in clinically relevant models will be essential for advancing EGFR-targeted interventions toward clinical translation.Keywords: Diabetes, Retinopathy, EGFR, VEGF, Therapy, Diabetes complications

Introduction

Diabetes Mellitus (DM) is a group of chronic endocrine diseases characterized by persistent hyperglycemia [1]. Clinically, DM is classified into at least four categories, namely Type 1 (T1DM), Type 2 (T2DM), gestational diabetes, and other types arising from specific identifiable causes [1]. In T1DM, an autoimmune response destroys insulin-producing β-cells in the pancreas, leading to an absolute insulin deficiency [1, 2]. In contrast, T2DM begins with insulin resistance of primarily skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver, followed by a progressive, nonautoimmune decline in pancreatic β-cell function and insulin secretion [1]. Uncontrolled chronic hyperglycemia in both T1DM and T2DM can lead to a range of macrovascular and microvascular complications including cardiovascular [3], renal [4], neuronal [5], and retinal pathologies [6]. In addition to chronic hyperglycemia, the classical risk factors for diabetes complications include smoking, dyslipidemias and hypertension [7].

This review focuses on diabetic retinopathy (DR), the primary microvascular complication of diabetes and a leading cause of new cases of blindness among adults aged 20–74 years in developed countries [6, 7]. Approximately one-third of individuals with diabetes develop DR [8]. Its prevalence correlates strongly with both disease duration and glycemic control, as maintaining near-normoglycemia can delay or prevent its onset and progression [7]. However, recent studies have also suggested that intensive therapies that result in rapid reductions in Hemoglobin A1C (HbA1C), such as with glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, can be associated with initial worsening of DR at least in the early stages but may ultimately be protective in late or advanced stages [9–12]. Of note, GLP-1 agonist use has been associated with a 2-fold increase in incidence of neovascular age-related macular degeneration among patients with diabetes [13].

In DR visual symptoms progress from normal vision to blurred or distorted vison and impaired color perception, ultimately culminating in complete blindness in severe stages of DR [8, 14]. The early stage is referred to as non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) which includes several structural anatomic retinal alterations, such as the loss of retinal pericytes, thickening of capillary basement membrane, and microaneurysm formation [15]. Pericytes are mural cells embedded in the basement membrane of small blood vessels, where they play a crucial role in vascular stability and blood flow regulation; their degeneration weakens the vessel walls and disrupts microvascular integrity [16]. These changes occur alongside neuronal dysfunction and impaired retinal blood flow, even before clinical signs appear [17]. NPDR is often asymptomatic or accompanied by minor visual changes, however progressive pericyte loss and microvascular occlusion lead to chronic retinal ischemia which results in proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) [18]. At this stage, visual alterations are significantly more pronounced where damage to the retinal blood vessels is more severe, stimulating the growth of new abnormal blood vessels (neovascularization) through the process of angiogenesis [18]. This occurs through the upregulation of growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), both of which are expressed in response to hypoxia and are known to promote neovascularization [19, 20]. Additionally, diabetic macular edema (DME) is a common complication of both NPDR and PDR, where the blood-retinal-barrier (BRB) is compromised with increased vascular permeability culminating in fluid accumulation in the macula, the central region of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision [14]. DME is a common cause of visual impairment in DR patients [18].

The current standard of care for DR centers on intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, with agents such as bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting VEGF-A [21]. Among various other inflammatory mediators, VEGF plays a central role in promoting both neovascularization and vascular leakage in PDR [20]. Under hyperglycemic and hypoxic conditions, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) upregulates VEGF expression which drives angiogenesis via ligand binding and downstream signaling [22]. By sequestering VEGF and/or inhibiting its receptor signaling, these agents decrease fluid extravasation, resolve edema, and inhibit angiogenesis associated with PDR in the optic disc, retina and iris [23].

Despite their widespread use in clinical practice, anti-VEGF therapies are limited by several key challenges. One major drawback is their relatively short intraocular half-life, which often necessitates frequent intravitreal injections to sustain therapeutic efficacy [21]. This repeated administration contributes to the high overall cost of treatment and poses significant accessibility barriers for some patient populations [24]. Additionally, the need for repeated injections increases the risk of injection-related complications, such as endophthalmitis, a rare but potentially vision-threatening infection [25]. Furthermore, anti-VEGF therapy does not elicit a uniform response across all patient populations [26]. The proportion of non-responders after three injections has been demonstrated to be as high as 65% [26]. These non-responders only had a 3.2-letter gain in best visual acuity at 12 months following anti-VEGF treatment compared to 12.8-letter gain in responders [26]. Consequently, pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP), a form of laser therapy, can be used as an alternative or in combination with anti-VEGF agents, depending on patient preference and DME status [27]. However, even with this approximately 32–39% of PDR patients develop vitreous hemorrhage after PRP and may require vitrectomy as an additional surgical intervention [28]. Given the limited durability, high cost, procedural risks, and suboptimal efficacy of current anti-VEGF therapies there is an urgent and compelling need to explore alternative therapeutic strategies for DR. Recently, Lamivudine, an inflammasome inhibitor traditionally used to treat human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), has demonstrated efficacy comparable to -or potentially exceeding- that of VEGF inhibitors in improving visual acuity [29]. However, this therapeutic application has not yet received FDA approval and requires further validation. Another potential therapeutic target, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), has emerged from recent preclinical studies in DR and is the subject of this review.

Epidermal growth factor receptor signaling

EGFR, the first receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) to be identified, regulates key physiological cellular processes including cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and survival [30, 31]. As part of the four-membered ErbB family, EGFR (also known as ErbB1 or HER1) interacts with other members, including ErbB2 (HER2/Neu), ErbB3/HER3, and ErbB4/HER4 [3, 4, 31]. Ligand binding induces a conformational change in the receptor, dimerizing as homodimers or heterodimers with other ErbB family members [32] (Fig. 1b). After receptor dimerization, the intracellular tyrosine kinase domain of EGFR undergoes either trans- or auto-phosphorylation at specific tyrosine residues within its C-terminal tail of both receptors [33]. These phosphorylated residues serve as docking sites for various adaptor and effector proteins which activate downstream signaling pathways such as the Extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK1/2) pathway, the Phosphoinositide 3-Kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway, and the Janus Kinase (JAK)/ Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT) pathway [4, 32].

(a) EGFR/ErbB family of receptors and their ligands. This family of RTKs comprises four members (EGFR/ErbB1, ErbB2, ErbB3, and ErbB4), each harboring a modular extracellular region consisting of two leucine-rich ligand-binding domains (domains I and III), and two cysteine-rich dimerization arms (domains II and IV) [34]. The intracellular region includes a transmembrane segment, a tyrosine kinase domain and a regulatory C-terminal domain. ErbB2 lacks a cognate soluble ligand and acts primarily as a dimerization partner, while the catalytically inactive ErbB3 signals exclusively through heterodimerization. (b) Ligand-induced activation of EGFR signaling. EGFR resides on the cell surface as a low-activity monomer under basal conditions [35]. Proteolytic ectodomain shedding by ADAM family metalloproteases liberates soluble, receptor-competent ligands [36]. Ligand binding disrupts the domain II–IV autoinhibitory tether, driving homo- or heterodimerization and asymmetric kinase domain activation, which enables trans-autophosphorylation of C-terminal tyrosine residues and downstream signaling through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, JAK/STAT, and RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK pathways [35] (not shown). Additionally, ligand-independent transactivation of EGFR signaling can also occur (not shown)

It should be noted that ErbB3 lacks intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity due to mutations in its kinase domain, rendering it incapable of phosphorylating itself or other proteins [4, 32]. To function, ErbB3 relies on dimerization with other ErbB family members through heterodimerization, this compensates for its kinase deficiency and allows for activation of downstream signaling pathways [4]. Moreover, ErbB2 is unique within the family, as it has no known soluble ligand and is considered an outlier structurally [37, 38]. Unlike EGFR, ErbB2’s extracellular domain adopts a conformation that resembles a ligand-activated state even in the absence of a ligand [38] (Fig. 1a). This property explains its ability to cause ligand-independent cell transformation when overexpressed.

There are at least 7 different cognate ligands for EGFRs (see also Fig. 1) most of which are known to initiate distinct signaling cascades as part of normal retinal physiology, including Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF) and Transforming Growth Factor-α (TGF- α) [39], both of which specifically bind to EGFR; Heparin Binding (HB-EGF) [40], which can interact with both EGFR and ErbB4; Amphiregulin (AREG) [41], Betacellulin (BTC) [42], and Epiregulin (EREG) [43], which predominantly bind to EGFR but may also show activity with other ErbB family members. Epigen, the least characterized ligand, signals predominantly through EGFR [4, 44, 45]. Neuregulins (NRGs) are ligands for ErbB3 and ErbB4 but through heterodimerization can also engage other ErbBs (see Fig. 1a and section below for a discussion on the role of ligands in DR).

EGFR signaling is known to play a critical role in oncogenesis, and EGFR inhibitors are now standard-of-care agents across multiple malignancies including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), glioblastoma, head and neck cancer, and colorectal carcinoma [46]. Beyond cancer, dysregulated EGFR signaling has also been implicated in the pathology of diabetes complications, including cardiovascular [3], renal [4], neuronal and ocular manifestations [47–49]. EGFR is also expressed in multiple retinal cell types (see Fig. 2) and emerging pre-clinical evidence implicates EGFR in DR pathology. Herein, we firstly review the underlying pathology of DR, discuss the emerging preclinical evidence for the role of EGFR in DR pathology and then explore the possibility of using of EGFR-targeted therapies as complementary or alternative approaches to existing DR treatments.

EGFR family receptor expression in retinal cell types. Human retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells express all four ErbB receptors, with ErbB3 at the lowest level [50]. Photoreceptors express EGFR ligands that act on neighboring glial cells [51–53], though EGFR expression directly in photoreceptor has not been reported. Human retinal müller cells (RMCs) express EGFR [54, 55], with expression of other ErbBs yet to be characterized. Vascular endothelial cells express ErbB2, ErbB3, and ErbB4 under physiological conditions [56], with ErbB1/EGFR expression additionally reported in the diabetic retinal vasculature [57]. Diabetic microvascular endothelium is thought to express all ErbB receptors [3]. Pericytes express EGFR and ErbB4 receptors, which are activated by ligands secreted from adjacent endothelial cells [58]. Microglia express ErbB2, ErbB3, and ErbB4 based on CNS studies [59]

Pathophysiology of diabetic retinopathy

The pathogenesis of DR is complex and likely involves multiple mechanisms leading to neuronal, glial and vascular dysfunction [20]. A key mechanism involves hyperglycemia induced damage to the microvasculature and neuronal architecture in the retina, the light sensitive part of the eye [60]. The retina is known as a site of “neuro-vascular coupling”, where neurons, glial cells, and vascular endothelial cells interact to maintain retinal homeostasis and vision [61]. In diabetes, this delicate balance is disrupted, leading to both neural degeneration and microvascular pathology including disruption and leakiness of the BRB [62]. Chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes is responsible for oxidative stress, inflammation, and the activation of multiple signaling pathways including the polyol pathway, the advanced glycation end products and their receptor (AGE/RAGE) pathway, the protein kinase C (PKC) pathway, and the hexosamine pathway, all of which contribute to microcirculatory disturbances and retinal nerve degeneration through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [63].

In the eye, RAGEs are constitutively expressed on endothelial cells, pericytes, glial cells and RPE cells, with expression notably increased under diabetic conditions [64]. Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells function as a critical metabolic interface, transporting nutrients and waste between photoreceptors and the choroidal vasculature [65]. Photoreceptors (rods and cones) are the retina’s primary light-sensing neurons [66, 67]. Retinal Müller cells (RMCs) are the principal glial cells of the retina, maintaining retinal homeostasis by regulating ion balance and neurotransmitter uptake [68]. Microvascular endothelial cells form the inner BRB, playing a pivotal role in maintaining the immune privilege of the retina and facilitating selective nutrient transport [69]. Pericytes regulate capillary diameter to control blood flow, maintain the integrity of the BRB, and degrade extracellular matrix components to support vascular homeostasis [58]. In the retina, microglia serve as immune sentinels that monitor neuronal health, clear cellular debris, and help maintain synaptic stability and visual function [70].

In pericytes and endothelial cells, AGE-RAGE binding culminates in increased pro-inflammatory NF-κB activity, ROS formation and the downregulation of crucial tight junction proteins, such as claudin-5, ZO-1 and occludin [71]. Simultaneously, the polyol pathway becomes overactive due to elevated intracellular glucose, resulting in a 27% increase in glucose metabolism [72]. Pericytes, endothelial cells, and RMCs cells constitutively express aldose reductase and take up glucose independently of insulin, making them the primary cells vulnerable to this pathway [73]. Aldose reductase converts glucose to sorbitol, which accumulates and draws water into the cell, resulting in osmotic stress [72]. This process consumes NADPH, weakening antioxidant defenses and contributing to redox imbalance, further reducing cell survival [74]. The hexosamine biosynthetic pathway is also upregulated under hyperglycemia, diverting fructose-6-phosphate to produce UDP-GlcNAc, which modifies transcription factors like Sp1 [75]. This modification increases the expression of Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), promoting inflammatory signaling and extracellular matrix accumulation, both detrimental to pericyte function [75]. Additionally, increased glucose levels elevate diacylglycerol (DAG) levels, activating specific PKC isoforms, particularly PKC-δ [76]. This leads to enhanced VEGF expression, heightened vascular permeability, and stimulates proinflammatory gene expression [76].

Retinal inflammation mediated by cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) contributes to apoptosis of retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and compromises photoreceptor integrity [77]. IL-1β activates the pro-inflammatory NF-κB signaling cascade and promotes the generation of ROS, leading to endothelial cell apoptosis and disruption of the BRB, thereby enhancing vascular permeability [78]. IL-1β also stimulates IL-6 secretion and induces capillary angiogenesis via p38/MAPK pathways [79]. Similarly, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) exacerbates endothelial cell injury and increases endothelial permeability, resulting in vascular leakage with ensuing ischemia and neuronal dysfunction [67]. Thus, consistent with the notion that diabetes patients undergo chronic low-grade systemic inflammation [77], these inflammatory mediators may act synergistically in a cascade amplification manner, sustaining the inflammatory milieu and accelerating the progression of DR. Collectively, these pathways converge and amplify oxidative stress, inflammation and VEGF expression, explaining the vascular and neural compromise of the retinal microenvironment in DR.

So what is the role of EGFR signaling in the normal retina and DR pathology? The retina is a complex neurosensory tissue lining of the eye, that comprises multiple cell types including RPE cells, RMCs, microglia, pericytes and endothelial cells, all of which are known to express EGFR family of receptors (see Fig. 2). Consistent with this wide expression pattern, EGFR is known to be critical for the development of retinal tissue during embryogenesis and in maintaining homeostasis in adult retinas [80, 81]. The ubiquitous expression and key physiological functions of EGFR in the retina also suggest that its dysregulation in diabetes could potentially have marked pathological impact on multiple cell populations. The emerging preclinical evidence implicating EGFR in the pathology of DR across several retinal cell types is therefore reviewed in subsequent sections.

Role of EGFR in diabetes-induced retinal vascular dysfunction

Vascular endothelial cells

Retinal endothelial dysfunction and increased permeability of the microvasculature is an important pathological process in DR. EGFR is already recognized as a critical regulator of diabetes-induced endothelial dysfunction in both the microvasculature and the macrovasculature [3, 82–85]. For example, in the mesenteric microvasculature of diabetic rats, early mRNA expression analyses demonstrated multiple gene expression changes (>1100) including increased expression of EGFR that were associated with diabetes-related vascular dysfunction [82, 86]. Pharmacological inhibition with the selective EGFR TK inhibitor, AG1478, at onset of diabetes normalized more than 90% of all gene expression changes and prevented the development of vascular dysfunction implying that EGFR signaling may act as an early contributor to the development of vascular complications associated with diabetes [86]. It is likely that EGFR acts through heterodimerization with other members of the EGFR family of receptors, notably ErbB2, leading to downstream PI3K/AKT pathway activation that is known to mediate of diabetes-induced vascular complications [87, 88]. Heterodimerization with other EGFR receptors is also implicated. Indeed, hyperglycemia activates all ErbB receptors, and treatment with pan-ErbB inhibitors that inhibit phosphorylation of all EGFR receptors restored vascular function in a rat model of T1DM [89, 90]. Additionally, dysregulated EGFR signaling is also implicated in diabetes-induced corneal pathology [47]. Thus, diabetes-induced alterations in EGFR signaling are already implicated in vascular and ocular complications. Indeed, EGFR is expressed across several retinal cell populations (see Fig. 2), including vascular endothelial cells, and pericytes.

Preclinical evidence already exists implicating EGFR as a mediator of vascular dysfunction in the retina (See Tables 1 & 2). For instance, in mouse models of early- and late-stage DR, selective EGFR inhibition markedly reduced key vascular pathological features of the disease [48]. A 20 mg/kg administration of AG1478, a reversible and competitive TK inhibitor of EGFR, by oral gavage inhibited neovascular tuft formation without interfering with physiological revascularization, indicating a targeted anti-angiogenic effect [48]. These benefits occurred independently of changes in blood glucose levels, suggesting that EGFR inhibition may modulate aspects of DR pathology beyond hyperglycemia control [48]. This is further supported by findings from the oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) model, a well-established surrogate for proliferative retinal neovascularization, in which a single intravitreal injection of AG1478 (2 µg/µL) at postnatal day 15 significantly reduced pathological neovascular tufts compared to vehicle-injected controls [48].

Summary of selected in vitro models directly mimicking diabetes and other ocular/retinal models supporting the role of EGFR signaling in diabetic retinopathy

These anti-angiogenic effects of EGFR inhibition in the retina may in part be influenced by regulation of EGFR expression by non-coding RNAs such microRNAs that are also known mediators of DR [104]. For example, miR-335-3p is known to negatively regulate EGFR expression in adult retinal pigment epithelial cell lines-19 (ARPE-19) [105, 106]. Furthermore, the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) KCNQ10T1, that augments hyperglycemia-induced EGFR expression, is upregulated in the aqueous humour of DR patients and HRECs and negatively correlates with miR-1470 expression [101]. High KCNQ1OT1 expression is linked to advanced DR stages, promoting apoptosis and inhibiting vascularization through its regulation of the miR-1470/EGFR axis [101]. KCNQ1OT1 acts as a competitor/sponge for miR-1470 and silencing of KCNQ1OT1 restores miR-1470 function to reduce EGFR levels and retinal pathology associated apoptosis and cell migration [101]. Thus, post-transcriptional regulation of EGFR appears to play an important role in vascular pathology associated with DR.

Pericytes

Structural alterations in vascular support cells or “pericytes” also play a critical role in in BRB integrity and disease progression [107, 108]. These cells are found in the vasculature throughout the human body, but their density varies according to the functional demands of different tissues [107]. In retinal vasculature, where stringent barrier regulation is necessary in maintaining the BRB [108], the endothelial cell to pericyte ratio is as high as 1:1, shifting to ~4:1 in DR [109]. In addition to providing structural support, pericytes also facilitate the local delivery of metabolites across the BRB [110]. In DR, pericyte loss takes place as a progressive process where some persist but adopt a dysregulated, stress-induced phenotype characterized by “pericyte bridging”, where pericytes become abnormally motile and detach from capillary walls into the perivascular space [107]. This phenomenon has been observed in diabetic models where pericyte migration has been proven as a significant mechanism of continued pericyte loss and altered pericyte-endothelial interactions leading to vascular dysfunction [111, 112].

Although the direct mechanistic involvement of EGFR in pericyte dysregulation is largely under studied, evidence suggests it may play a contributory role. In a high-fat, high-sugar diet rat model of T1DM, increased EGFR expression was observed in surviving pericytes and retinal endothelial cells, alongside elevated fibroblast growth factor 7 (FGF7) [92]. EGFR upregulation led to aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade, driving pathological cellular changes such as excessive proliferation, migration, and invasion [92]. These cellular changes mirror the typical bridging features of pericytes in DR, suggesting that EGFR signaling contributes to pericyte dysfunction. Notably, pericyte behavior appears to be further modulated through epigenetic regulation of EGFR-associated pathways. miR-199a-3p, a microRNA normally downregulated under diabetic conditions, was shown to suppress FGF7 expression and, consequently, inhibit the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway [92]. Restoration of miR-199a-3p levels in vitro effectively reduced pathological behaviors of endothelial cells and pericytes, further implicating EGFR as a mediator of diabetes-induced retinal microvascular dysfunction [92]. Taken together, these findings point to a potential crosstalk between FGF–EGFR signaling pathways in mediating pericyte pathology and further implicate EGFR in the DR-induced vascular dysfunction.

EGFR in retinal Müller cell activation, and retinal epithelial cell mediated apoptosis and neuronal degeneration

The retina contains a diverse population of neuroglial cells, including RMCs, microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes, of which RMCs are by far the most abundant, comprising approximately 90% of total retinal glia and serving as critical regulators of retinal morpho-functional organization. [113, 114]. These uniquely bipolar cells span the full thickness of the retina, integrating neurons into discrete functional units known as retinal columns, and function as living optical fibers that minimize light scatter and channel photons to photoreceptors [113]. Beyond their structural role, RMCs maintain extracellular ion homeostasis, particularly potassium buffering, regulate glutamate recycling and glucose metabolism, and contribute critically to blood-retinal barrier maintenance [115].

In DR, RMCs are the primary glial cell type to undergo reactive gliosis, a stereotyped stress response initially characterized by the secretion of neurotrophic factors that attenuate retinal oedema and protect retinal ganglion cells [116]. However, under conditions of chronic hyperglycaemia, this initially protective response gives way to a sustained pro-inflammatory and neurotoxic phenotypic shift that actively drives neurodegeneration and fibrosis [116, 117]. EGFR is expressed in human RMCs [54, 55] (see also Fig. 2), and is required for cell proliferation, repair and for RMC activation in conditions of hyperglycemia [40, 54, 55]. For example, retinal cell regeneration is thought to require endothelin B induced Src-dependent transactivation of EGFR [54]. High glucose leads to nuclear translocation of EGFR to induce the transcription intermediary factor (TIF2) and elicit cyclin-D1 dependent proliferation of human RMCs [55]. Hyperglycemia also led to RMC activation as evidenced by increased glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) that could be inhibited by a selective inhibitor of EGFR, Erlinotib [55], implying a key role of EGFR in glial cell activation.

A critical downstream consequence of RMC activation is a marked upregulation of TNF-α secretion whereby hyperglycemia-induced activation of RMCs results in a > 20-fold increase in TNF-α secretion compared to normal glucose [118]. Elevated TNF-α levels have also been observed in the vitreous fluid of PDR patients [119]. This is consistent with the pro-inflammatory cytokines’ well-established role in the pathogenesis of DR where it can act on retinal endothelial cells, pericytes, RMC, and epithelial cells to promote leukocytosis, tight junction disruption, BRB breakdown, and neuroinflammation [67, 120]. Data in a rat model of spinal cord injury also supports that EGFR inhibition reduces activation-associated glial cell morphological changes and production of inflammatory cytokines, namely, TNF-α [121], and by the fact that EGFR mediated signaling in neuroglial cells is known to contribute to other neurodegenerative pathologies [122, 123].

There appears to be significant interplay between TNF-α and EGFR in the pathogenesis of DR. Although TNF-α is not a canonical EGFR ligand, it can induce transactivation of EGFR receptors [93]. Recent work demonstrated that TNF-α released from RMC’s under hyperglycemic conditions activates the EGFR/p38/NF-κB/p62 signaling cascade in RPE cells (see also Fig. 4), leading to excessive mitophagy and apoptosis [93]. Furthermore, EGFR activation in RPE cells contributes to cell proliferation, migration, and inflammatory extracellular matrix remodeling [50, 124, 125]. The precise intermediate steps of TNF-induced EGFR transactivation, whether through ligand shedding or direct receptor crosstalk, is insufficiently delineated. Current evidence suggests a likely mechanism involving TNF-α induction of sheddase enzymes such as matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP-9) in RPE cells [126], which is known to cleave membrane-bound EGFR ligands, though this has been demonstrated in non-ocular cell types [127]. Furthermore, inhibition of ERK1/2, which activates RMCs and therefore the release of TNF-α, attenuates upregulation of MMP-9 as well as other biomarkers of inflammation in diabetic rats [128].

The in vivo significance of this TNF-α/EGFR axis was directly confirmed in diabetic mice, where oral gefitinib (4 mg/kg/day) and TNF-α neutralisation (10 mg/kg/day) each independently attenuated blood-retinal barrier breakdown, with combination treatment producing superior protection [93], implying that RPE apoptosis in DR is not simply a consequence of autonomous EGFR overactivation, but is substantially mediated through TNF-α-driven EGFR transactivation. Mechanistic summary of cell-specific EGFR-mediated retinal pathology in diabetic retinopathy, including endothelial, RPE, photoreceptor and RMCs. Chronic hyperglycemia acts as the initiating driver, activating EGFR signaling and interconnected metabolic pathways across retinal cell types. In retinal endothelial cells, hyperglycemia stimulates EGFR through a RhoA/ROCK/YAP cascade, leading to upregulation of AREG and CCN2, both transcriptional targets of YAP, with CCN2 functioning as a profibrotic matricellular protein [57]. AREG likely reinforces EGFR activation via autocrine signaling in endothelial cells, sustaining the RhoA/ROCK/YAP-CCN2 axis, and may also contribute to paracrine signaling to neighboring müller cells, as it is upregulated as a YAP target and part of the endothelial secretome shown to activate müller cells, although its specific role has not been directly tested [57]. This endothelial-müller cell crosstalk amplifies CCN2 transcription and secretion, promoting müller cell activation into a myofibroblast-like phenotype, indicated by α-SMA expression and extracellular matrix deposition (not shown), ultimately contributing to retinal fibrosis [57]. Concurrently, chronic hyperglycemia activates multiple pathways in RMCs, including AGE/RAGE [132], polyol [133], hexosamine [134], and oxidative stress via ROS [135], driving VEGF release from RMCs and other retinal cells. VEGF further enhances ERK1/2 signaling, a downstream effector of EGFR (black dotted line), forming a bidirectional amplification loop [136]. Additionally, VEGF may increase expression of EGFR in RMCs (black dotted line) though this was only demonstrated in a non-diabetic model [102]. Activated ERK1/2 promotes glial activation and TNF-α release, which induces EGFR transactivation in RPE cells [93, 99]. This likely occurs via upregulation of EGF ligand-cleaving enzymes such as ADAMs and/or MMPs (grey dotted line), rather than direct receptor activation, consistent with evidence that TNF-α induces MMP-9 in RPE cells [126] (see main text for further discussion). This TNF-α-mediated EGFR activation triggers downstream p38/NF-κB signaling, promoting transcription of p62 and induction of mitophagy, which contributes to apoptosis [93]. EGFR activation also increases VEGF expression and secretion in RPE cells [99]. As RPE cells and photoreceptors function as a coupled unit, loss of RPE integrity leads to secondary photoreceptor degeneration [137]. Notably, inhibition of ERK1/2 signaling in RMCs mitigates this degeneration [138]. In DR, EGFR is involved in multiple interconnected feedback loops across retinal cell types linking inflammation, VEGF signaling, neurodegeneration, and fibrosis

The relationship between EGFR and VEGF signaling in RMCs further elaborates this complexity. Vitreous VEGF levels in DR may reach up to sevenfold higher than in non-diabetic controls [139], and VEGF treatment of cultured rat RMCs induces approximately 19-fold upregulation of EGFR expression [102], a response exceeding that produced by EGF itself, the receptor’s canonical ligand, strongly suggesting that elevated ERK1/2 phosphorylation observed in the diabetic retina is driven at least in part by VEGF-induced EGFR upregulation rather than direct VEGF receptor signaling alone. U0126-mediated ERK1/2 inhibition reduces VEGF expression in RMCs of diabetic rats [136]., implicating a bidirectional VEGF–EGFR crosstalk loop in which each pathway amplifies the other (Fig. 3). While current evidence for this crosstalk is largely confined to RMCs, similar mechanisms may plausibly operate in astrocytes and microglia, warranting further investigation.

Complementing these RMC-centered mechanisms, in vivo and ex vivo evidence from diabetic mouse models has identified a paracrine signaling axis through which endothelial EGFR activation drives RMC transition toward a myofibroblast-like phenotype [57]. This process is mediated through EGFR-dependent secretion of CCN2, a profibrotic matricellular protein, and AREG, whose expression is regulated downstream of an EGFR/RhoA/ROCK/YAP transcriptional program in endothelial cells [57]. YAP, a nuclear transcriptional coactivator and downstream effector of the Hippo pathway, governs genes controlling cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis in ocular tissues [140]. Erlotinib-mediated EGFR inhibition significantly suppressed retinal AREG mRNA expression in diabetic mice, while selective pharmacological blockade of YAP with verteporfin similarly downregulated AREG alongside established YAP target genes including PAI-1 and CYR61 [57]. Collectively, these findings suggest that hyperglycemia-induced endothelial EGFR activation increases AREG expression and CCN2 secretion to drive RMC activation, linking endothelial EGFR dysregulation to retinal fibrosis (Fig. 3) [57].

The role of YAP is further reinforced by evidence that EGFR promotes YAP nuclear translocation in RMCs through proteasomal degradation of the Hippo scaffold protein SAV1, enhancing expression of profibrotic mediators such as CTGF and CYR61, while systemic or intravitreal EGFR inhibition markedly attenuated retinal fibrosis in diabetic mice [49] (See Table 1). Non-diabetic models of proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR) corroborate this axis: in ARPE-19 cells, EGF upregulated YAP and RhoA, yet YAP nuclear translocation persisted upon RhoA silencing, indicating that EGF can drive YAP activation independently of this canonical upstream regulator [97]. Downstream effectors, including CTGF, CYR61, Cyclin D1, c-Myc, and Bcl-xL, are strongly implicated in cell proliferation, survival, and fibrotic progression [97]. Importantly, the inhibition of EGFR with intraperitoneal 80 mg/kg/day erlotinib or conditional knockout of EGFR in ARPE-19 cells significantly reduced YAP activation and fibrotic markers [97]. Thus, across both diabetic and non-diabetic contexts, these findings establish the EGFR/YAP signaling axis as a plausible therapeutic target.

Pathological mechanisms in RMCs ultimately converge on RPE integrity and photoreceptor survival. As a critical intermediary, the RPE sustains photoreceptor homeostasis through multiple interdependent functions including protein recycling, phagocytosing shed outer segments, mediating selective nutrient transport, and providing photoprotection. Under diabetic conditions, RPE apoptosis disrupts this support axis, precipitating progressive photoreceptor degeneration and irreversible vision loss [141–143]. Notably, inhibition of ERK1/2 within RMCs reduces photoreceptor neurodegeneration [138], highlighting the EGFR/ERK1/2 axis as a key mechanism in RMC-induced neuro-glial-vascular dysfunction that characterizes advanced DR. Current evidence supports a multifactorial model in which RPE damage is one important, rather than the sole, driver of neurodegeneration.

Role of EGFR ligands in diabetic retinopathy

EGFR ligands (see Fig. 1) exhibit unique binding dynamics, receptor affinities, and patterns of expression, which can influence the magnitude, duration, and specificity of EGFR-mediated responses [144, 145]. In the context of DR, where pathological signaling may be driven by differential ligand activity, an understanding of their individual roles is of importance. Of the 7 canonical ligands for EGFR family of receptors, the expression of only EGF, BTC, AREG and NRG1 has been reported to be specifically elevated in models of DR [57, 94, 95, 146].

In a T1DM mouse model [94], BTC administration increased retinal vascular permeability alongside elevated levels of a cleaved soluble BTC isoform (Table 1). The sheddase ADAM-10, which mediates BTC cleavage, is upregulated in the retinas of both diabetic mice and humans, and notably does so under low VEGF conditions, implicating a VEGF-independent pathway [94]. A single intravitreal BTC injection (200 ng) in both normoglycemic and hyperglycemic mice induced retinal hemorrhage and vascular leakage, hallmark features of diabetic macular edema, establishing BTC as an independent mediator of blood-retinal barrier breakdown [94]. Transgenic mice with systemically elevated BTC further developed lens abnormalities and disrupted retinal lamination, demonstrating that BTC overactivity directly compromises ocular tissue integrity [100]. Corroborating these findings, human diabetic retinal samples exhibited increased levels of both membrane-bound and soluble BTC, affirming its clinical relevance [94].

BTC/EGFR signaling also underlies the paradoxical worsening of DME observed with acute intensive insulin therapy, a phenomenon observed in human patients [91, 147]. In diabetic mice, insulin therapy exacerbates retinal vascular permeability approximately five-fold, likely driven by ADAM-10-mediated BTC cleavage and downstream EGFR activation, which disrupts tight junction proteins including ZO-1 in RPE and endothelial cells [91]. In vitro, AG1478 reduced insulin-induced EGFR phosphorylation and preserved tight junction integrity in ARPE-19 cells; in vivo, intraperitoneal cetuximab (1 mg), a selective anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody, substantially attenuated insulin-induced vascular leakage in diabetic mice [91]. Collectively, these findings imply targeted BTC/EGFR inhibition represents a potential therapeutic strategy that might mitigate both diabetic retinopathy and the adverse retinal vascular consequences of insulin therapy.

The expression of AREG appears to be increased in primary retinal vascular endothelial cells derived from mice bearing T1DM [57]. AREG expression is likely involved in a signaling loop involving EGFR/RhoA/ROCK/YAP pathway that ultimately leads to RMC activation and retinal fibrosis (see Fig. 3). Similarly, an early immunostaining study explicitly reports elevated EGF levels in the human retina with PDR [95]. The direct evidence for the expression or the role of other canonical EGFR ligands is DR remains unknown; however, non-diabetic models sharing key pathological features with DR, including compromised BRB, fibrosis, glial activation, and neovascularization, provide supporting mechanistic evidence for EGFR ligand involvement in DR (Tables 1 and 2). For example, in non-hyperglycemic conditions, EGF activated RPE cells induced EGFR/AKT signaling resulting in cell proliferation, migration, and EMT as evidenced by upregulation of N-cadherin, α-SMA, and vimentin [103]. Selective pharmacological EGFR inhibition with erlotinib or siRNA-mediated knockdown abolished these effects, restoring epithelial morphology and suppressing EMT-associated gene expression [103]. EGF may also contribute to PDR through the vitreous microenvironment, where ischemia-driven neovascularization breaches the inner-limiting membrane, exposing endothelial cells to vitreous-stored growth factors [95, 148]. Vitreous stimulation of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) enhanced proliferation, migration, and EGF/VEGF expression, with EGF effects approximately threefold greater than VEGF, and AG1478-mediated EGFR inhibition suppressed endothelial proliferation and tube formation [149]. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as HUVECs may not accurately represent the molecular properties of retinal endothelial cells [69], and whether the observed responses reflect EGF-driven EGFR activation or other vitreous-derived factors such as TNF-α is not known [149].

Evidence from non-diabetic retinal disease models also support a pathogenic role for HB-EGF in DR. Its expression is induced by oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines, and overnutrition, driving EGFR signaling in the context of obesity and diabetes [150]. In murine choroidal neovascularization (CNV) models, HB-EGF and its activating enzymes are upregulated before VEGF elevation, clustering around nascent vessels, and conditional knockout significantly reduced choroidal lesion size and neovascular growth [98]. HB-EGF-deficient mice in the OIR model similarly developed fewer and smaller pathological vessels, establishing HB-EGF as an early driver of retinal neovascularization acting upstream of VEGF [98]. These findings extended to a primate model, where intravitreal CRM-197, a selective HB-EGF inhibitor, significantly attenuated vascular leakage and lesion size in laser-induced CNV in common marmosets [98]. In PVR, HB-EGF was detected in epiretinal membranes colocalizing with the RPE marker cytokeratin [99]. In human RPE cells, oxidative stress-induced HB-EGF enhanced VEGF secretion via EGFR signaling with broad EGFR inhibition proving more effective than anti-HB-EGF antibodies alone [99]. HB-EGF-driven RPE proliferation is dependent on ERK1/2 and PI3K/AKT, whereas migration on p38 MAPK, and VEGF upregulation on both p38 MAPK and PI3K/AKT [99] (Fig. 4, Table 1). Upstream, ADAM8 regulates HB-EGF bioavailability through ectodomain shedding and its absence in retinal hypoxia models enhanced physiological revascularization and reduced pathological tuft formation [151], implicating ADAM8-mediated HB-EGF/EGFR activation in hypoxia-driven angiogenesis.

Beyond the classical EGFR ligands, EGFL7, an angiogenic factor with EGF-like structural domains, represents a potential novel EGFR ligand of relevance to DR [96, 130]. In both human retinal vascular endothelial cells and diabetic rat models, EGFL7 appears to be consistently upregulated and promotes endothelial proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis via the PI3K/AKT/VEGFA signaling pathway [96]. Its role in modulating EGFR pathway activity is further supported by findings that EGFL7 deletion significantly reduced phosphorylation of EGFR, AKT, and ERK, indicating that EGFL7 acts as an upstream regulator of downstream EGFR signaling in DR [96].

Neuregulins (NRGs): are they potentially protective in retinal pathologies?

In contrast to the pro-pathogenic EGFR ligands described above, NRGs represent a four member subgroup within the EGF ligand family, namely NRG1-4 that bind to ErbB3 and ErbB4 [152] and may have opposing or protective roles in DR. NRGs are broadly expressed across tissues and regulate cell growth, differentiation, survival, and intercellular communication, with established anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties [153, 154]. In a clinical study of 7 patients with PDR, NRG1β1 protein was elevated 3-fold in the vitreous humor relative to non-diabetic controls, and NRG1 transcripts were detected largely in retinal glial and endothelial cells, suggesting that local retinal cells rather than systemic circulation serve as the primary source of the ligand [146]. On the hand, NRG2 transcripts were primarily noted only in glial cells implying a differential and/or context dependent role of the different NRGs. However, elevated NRG1β1 protein was not consistently observed in a larger independent validation cohort of 32 patients with PDR. This may reflect variations in disease progression as the study was performed at only a single time point. The fact that NRG was detected in both the vitreous and fibrovascular membranes suggests a potential role in neuroglial remodeling [146]. Since in cell culture models, NRG1-ErbB signaling promotes neurite outgrowth and neuronal survival in dorsal root ganglia and motoneurons [155, 156], it might have a neuroprotective role in DR though this requires validation in clinically relevant models.

NRG4 may similarly confer neuroprotection as in clinical cohorts, T2DM patients with microvascular complications exhibited significantly lower serum NRG4 and each unit decrease was associated with approximately 1.9-fold higher odds of developing complications that included DR [157]. A further reduction was observed in patients with diabetic neuropathy, though differences within the DR subgroup did not reach statistical significance [158]. Data on NRG3 in DR remain sparse. Collectively, the limited available evidence points toward a neuroprotective role for NRGs in retinal pathology though causal evidence from DR-specific models is still required.

Conclusions and perspectives

The collective preclinical evidence presented herein supports a potential role of EGFR/ErbB receptor signaling cascades in the development of DR and other retinal diseases (see also Fig. 4). Beyond the well-established VEGF-driven mechanisms, retinal EGFR signaling can lead to BRB breakdown, glial activation, inflammation, and neovascularization that contribute to DR pathology. Pharmacological inhibition with selective small-molecule inhibitors (e.g., AG1478, Erlotinib, Gefitinib) or siRNA-mediated gene silencing, has demonstrated multifaceted pathophysiological effects in preclinical models of diabetes, including reduction of neovascular tuft formation, attenuation of inflammatory markers and preservation of retinal function (Tables 1 and 2).

EGFR and other ErbB receptors are expressed across multiple cell types in the healthy retina (Fig. 2) and serve essential physiological roles in regulating proliferation, differentiation, migration, and survival. While EGFR signaling is required for normal retinal development, function, and repair [159], its chronic overactivation is directly associated with the development of DR in preclinical models. Although longitudinal studies mapping EGFR expression across distinct stages of disease progression are lacking, available evidence suggests that overactivation occurs early in the disease course, as EGFR inhibition appears to be most efficacious at this stage [49]. Early pharmacological targeting may therefore warrant investigation during the initial non-proliferative stages, where EGFR inhibition could reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine release, limit pericyte loss, and attenuate BRB disruption before irreversible neovascularization, neurodegeneration, and fibrosis are established. In this context, EGFR-targeted therapy could represent a preventative or disease-modifying approach, complementing existing anti-VEGF and anti-inflammatory strategies, though robust preclinical and clinical validation is an essential prerequisite.

Several EGFR inhibitors with established clinical profiles might represent attractive repurposing candidates for DR. The small-molecule inhibitors gefitinib and erlotinib, FDA-approved for NSCLC since the early 2000s [160, 161], have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical DR models (Table 1, Fig. 4), as has the FDA-approved monoclonal antibody cetuximab [91, 162]. However, repurposing must account for the dual physiological and pathological roles of EGFR in the eye, necessitating temporally and spatially controlled delivery to optimize efficacy and limit systemic exposure. While the known adverse effects of systemic EGFR inhibition, including dermatological toxicity and diarrhea, are generally manageable in oncology, they present meaningful challenges in the setting of chronic ocular disease.

From a translational perspective, local intravitreal delivery of EGFR-targeted therapies represents a strategically attractive approach that could circumvent the systemic toxicity associated with oral or intravenous EGFR inhibitors. Preclinical studies have already established the feasibility of this approach, demonstrating that intravitreal administration of small-molecule EGFR inhibitors [49, 94, 163] and neutralizing antibodies directed against EGFR ligands, including HB-EGF [99], can achieve therapeutically relevant target engagement within the retina without systemic exposure. Interestingly, the encapsulation of EGFR inhibitors within sustained-release polymer nanospheres has provided early proof-of-concept for this delivery paradigm, with Robinson et al. demonstrating enhanced optic neuronal regeneration in a nerve-crush injury model [163], findings that underscore the capacity of optimized intraocular pharmacokinetics to enhance therapeutic efficacy while preserving ocular safety.

Building on this foundation, newer generation of intravitreal delivery platforms including other biodegradable sustained-release nanoparticles, and injectable hydrogels [164–166], offers potential opportunities to tailor EGFR inhibition to the stage-specific pathophysiology of DR. In early to intermediate disease, where the predominant drivers of retinal injury are endothelial dysfunction, pericyte loss, and chronic neuroinflammatory signalling rather than established neovascularization, these platforms are particularly well suited to providing continuous, low-level receptor blockade over extended periods, substantially reducing the injection burden that has impeded patient adherence to existing intravitreal therapies. At more advanced disease stages, stimuli-responsive hydrogels and cell-targeted nanoparticles and liposomes offer the further capability of spatially and temporally controlled drug release, enabling cell-type-specific EGFR inhibition within the retinal neurovascular unit. Collectively, these delivery platforms can address the key pharmacokinetic and targeting limitations for ocular translation of EGFR-directed therapies, and represent a key complementary strand of development alongside mechanistic validation of EGFR as a therapeutic target in a chronic, multifactorial disease such as DR.

A further rationale for EGFR-targeted therapy lies in its mechanistic convergence with VEGF signaling. Substantial crosstalk exists between HB-EGF/EGFR and VEGF pathways, whereby EGFR activation converges on shared downstream effectors to upregulate VEGF expression, establishing a self-amplifying pro-angiogenic feed-forward loop (Fig. 4). This interdependence suggests that VEGF blockade alone may incompletely suppress angiogenic signaling, particularly in persistent or recurrent disease. Combined EGFR and VEGF inhibition could therefore modulate a broader network of angiogenic, and inflammatory pathways implicated in DR progression. While dual EGFR/VEGF inhibition is FDA-approved in NSCLC [167], this approval is disease- and mutation-specific and does not constitute evidence of retinal efficacy or safety; independent clinical investigation in the retinal context would therefore be required before conclusions regarding therapeutic benefit can be drawn.

Additional angiogenic pathways also warrant consideration as co-targets. Angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2), a key mediator of vascular destabilization and inflammation, is upregulated in DR and acts permissively to amplify growth factor-driven angiogenesis downstream of both VEGF and EGFR [168]. The recent FDA approval of Faricimab, a bispecific antibody targeting Ang-2 and VEGF-A, establishes a clinical precedent for multi-target approaches in ocular disease [169], and underscores the biological plausibility of next-generation fusion proteins directed against EGFR in combination with VEGF-A and/or Ang-2. By addressing multiple pathological mechanisms simultaneously, such strategies could complement or reduce reliance on adjunctive treatments such as pan-retinal photocoagulation, thereby minimizing associated complications including choroidal effusion, retinal detachment, and macular edema [170].

Looking ahead, several research priorities require attention. Comprehensive evaluation of all ErbB receptor family members in DR pathophysiology, development of highly selective isoform-specific inhibitors, and optimization of nanoparticle-based delivery formulations with assessment of long-term ocular safety and efficacy represent near-term imperatives. Identification of retinal biomarkers, including phosphorylated EGFR and ligand expression profiles, will be critical for patient stratification and therapeutic monitoring. Rational drug combination strategies incorporating EGFR inhibitors alongside Ang-2, VEGF, antioxidant, or anti-inflammatory agents may yield additive or synergistic benefits warranting systematic evaluation. To date, no clinical trials of EGFR inhibitors specifically targeting DR have been reported, and the field remains firmly at the preclinical stage. Nevertheless, further validation of the role of EGFR and its ligands in mediating retinal pathology in clinically relevant models will be essential for advancing EGFR-targeted interventions toward clinical translation.
