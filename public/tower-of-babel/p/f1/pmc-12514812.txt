Hospital readmission disparity measure for evaluating hospital performance and penalties
Authors: Yajun Yi, Henry John Domenico, Leta Yi, Wei Xiang, Xiayu Xiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe excess readmission ratio (ERR) is currently used to determine penalties for hospitals with excessive readmission rates. To determine whether the effectiveness of alternative measures, such as the Risk-Standardized Readmission Rate (RSRR), or Excess Days in Acute Care (EDAC), or differences between paired measures, would provide comparable or superior assessments.MethodsA retrospective national study on readmission measures. This study analyzed national readmission data from 3,047 hospitals for the fiscal year (FY) 2022. We measured the agreements and disparity scores in hospital performance assessments, as well as the changes in penalty status between the paired readmission measures.ResultsThe ERR and RSRR measures showed a significantly high degree of agreement, ranging from 83.5 to 93.8% across the six applicable conditions/procedures. In contrast, marked differences emerged when comparing EDAC with ERR or RSRR, showing varying levels of disparities. If the EDAC measure were adopted, between 15.9 and 25.2% of hospitals would experience a change in their penalty status. Specifically, using EDAC would reduce financial penalties for 11.4–16.6% of small community hospitals and safety-net hospitals for at least one of the targeted conditions.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that integrating disparity scores into the current ERR-based measurement system could have significant implications for promoting equity and informing policy decisions. By doing so, the evaluation of individual providers and hospital care quality could become more comprehensive, insightful, accurate, and equitable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01726-w.Keywords: Readmission, Readmission measures, The excess readmission ratio (ERR), The Risk-Standardized Readmission Rate (RSRR), Excess Days in Acute Care (EDAC), Disparity score

Introduction

In the United States, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) has imposed three measures to report and evaluate 30-day hospital readmissions for targeted conditions and procedures. The three measures currently used by CMS are Excess Readmission Ratio (ERR), Risk-Standardized Readmission Rate (RSRR), and Excess Days in Acute Care (EDAC).

In general, the ERR and RSRR measures are very similar. However, the target audiences for ERR are medical professionals such as providers, researchers, and stakeholders, while RSRR is a consumer-oriented readmission measure for patients and caregivers, including over 1,000 more hospitals. The RSRR formula involves the national observed readmission rate, while ERR is the measure of a hospital’s relative performance that CMS uses in the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) hospitals.

Both RSRR and ERR are defined only inpatient hospitalizations, while EDAC captures the excess days that a hospital’s patients spent in acute care within 30 days after discharge. The EDAC measure incorporates the full range of post-discharge use of care, including emergency department (ED) visits, observation stays, and unplanned inpatient readmissions [1].

Since 2012, ERR has been incorporated into theHRRP and implemented nationwide to evaluate hospital performance. The HRRP attempts to drive hospitals to reduce readmissions by imposing a penalty of up to 3% of their base Medicare payments for higher-than-expected ERR [2]. The HRRP has imposed more than $3 billion in financial penalties to date [3].

There are both intended and unintended effects resulting from the implementation of HRRP. In the first 3 years of the HRRP, safety-net hospitals (SNHs) reduced their readmissions for the three target conditions by 1.77–2.86%.4 Further studies have shown that readmission rates declined from 21.5 to 17.8% for targeted conditions and 15.3–13.1% for non-targeted conditions in the time period from 2007 to 2015 (per Medicare) [4]. Hospital care quality has generally improved. Greater emphasis has been placed on care coordination, discharge planning, and implementing care transition teams [5–7].

However, in the same period, the total number of hospital revisits per 100 patient discharges for target conditions increased [8]. A substantial portion of the estimated reductions in readmissions after implementation of the HRRP was the result of increases in coded severity rather than underlying improvements in the delivery of care [9].

HRRP is associated with increased 30-day mortality rates for patients [10] and may disproportionately affect SNHs, which serve a higher proportion of low-income and minority patients [11].

HRRP hospitals could intensify efforts to manage patients who return within 30 days of discharge to emergency departments(ED) and observation units. [9–13] There were questions about whether hospitals could “game” the system by inappropriate discharges or be unfairly penalized with the ERR-based measure under HRRP [12–19].

The fairness issue remains unresolved [20]. Of 2845 hospitals ranked for heart failure, 387 of 1422 (27.2%) that received penalties under the HRRP based on the readmission measure would be down-classified to no penalty by using the EDAC measure, whereas 382 of 1423 (26.8%) that received no penalty would be up-classified to a penalty [3].

Significantly fewer small and rural hospitals would receive penalties for the two targeted conditions if the EDAC measure were used instead of the ERR measure, suggesting that these providers were unfairly penalized with the use of ERR to assess readmission in HRRP;3 Therefore, many clinicians and researchers have expressed skepticism regarding the program’s effects and concerns about these unintended consequences [11, 21, 22].

It is important to identify a better or new measure from the current readmission metrics to mitigate the unintended effects of the ERR-based HRRP. Wadhera et al. [3] found only moderate agreement between performance rankings using ERR and EDA. The penalty status of about 25–28% of HRRP hospitals would change if the EDAC measure were used instead of the ERR measure to evaluate performance.

However, it remains unclear how to effectively quantify and interpret the disparities between different readmission measures. For instance, a hospital with a high disparity score from a low ERR/RSRR rate but a high EDAC value represents a particularly complex scenario that warrants further investigation. Currently, no studies have identified HRRP hospitals with the largest disparities between ERR and EDAC measures and analyzed their implications, or examined how variations in the proportions of patients dually eligible for Medicare and Medicaid contribute to performance differences across these metrics. These gaps highlight the need for a deeper understanding of how discrepancies between paired readmission metrics impact hospital evaluations and the potential for penalization under the HRRP.

To address these questions, we defined three key objectives: (1) to compare hospital performance rankings under each readmission measure and identify hospitals with the largest disparities; (2) to determine whether replacing the ERR measure with EDAC or RSRR changes which hospitals incur financial penalties under the HRRP; and (3) to evaluate the relationship between hospital types and their likelihood of penalization under different readmission measures.

Measures

The ERR is the ratio of predicted-to-expected readmissions for a given condition, and it is used to determine penalties for HRRP hospitals. RSRR is computed by the ERR ratio multiplied by the national observed readmission rate for the condition. EDAC captures the total number of days that a patient spends in ED, in observation status, or admitted as an unplanned readmission within 30 days of discharge after an index hospitalization. EDAC is only applied to three specific conditions (AMI, HF, and PN). Both RSRR and EDAC are based on a larger set of hospital populations (n=4,848) compared to HRRP hospitals (n=3,047). The analyses of hospital performance between paired readmission measures were conducted within a common set of HRRP hospitals.

Statistical analysis

To assess the overall agreement of hospital performances between paired readmission measures such as ERR vs. EDAC, EDAC vs. RSRR, and ERR vs. RSRR, we employed quintile group alignment analysis to rank measure performance and used the weighted Kappa statistic to assess agreements between paired readmission measures [26, 27].

We derived the distances of ranking positions between two measures using quintile group alignment analysis for each condition in three or six 5 × 5 contingency tables. Hospitals were organized into quintiles based on the sorted measure values for each relevant condition/procedure. For a given CMS condition, hospitals with the lowest EDAC or ERR values were categorized into group 1(quintile 1), representing the “best-performing” hospitals. In contrast, those with the highest EDAC or ERR values were placed in group 5 (quintile 5), indicating the “worst-performing” hospitals. We calculated the proportion of hospitals that would be reclassified into a different performance group if another measure were used. We also examined associations of the readmission performance rankings for the three measures (ERR, RSRR, and EDAC).

For each hospital, we determined whether the ranking positions changed between paired readmission measures and calculated the quintile group number difference as a new measurement called the disparity score. Therefore, all hospitals were categorized into three classes based on the disparity scores: (1) hospitals with perfect agreement (disparity score = 0, i.e., no quintile group change between paired readmission measures); (2) hospitals with partial agreement (disparity score: 1–3, indicating the quintile group changes ranging from 1 to 3); and (3) hospitals with perfect disagreement (disparity score = 4, indicating the maximal group change). The overall agreement includes both perfect and partial agreements.

Net reclassification analysis and the replicated ERR-based stratified methodology are described in the online appendix statistical analysis Section [23]

Results

Reclassification of performance rankings

The performance distributions and descriptive statistics of the three readmission measures were summarized in Table 1, Supplement Tables 1 and 2 [23], and Supplement Fig. 1[23]. To evaluate the overall agreement of hospital performances, we calculated the Kappa statistic to demonstrate the percentages of the overall agreement for the three paired readmission measures (p<0.0001, Fig. 1).

Overall agreement across three pairwise comparisons

The measures with the highest agreement were ERR and RSRR (95.7% − 98.4%) across all six CMS applicable conditions/procedures (Fig. 1). These nearly perfect overall agreements suggest that ERR and RSRR are nearly identical measures of readmission. The overall agreements between ERR and EDAC or RSRR and EDAC also demonstrated high agreement percentages, ranging from 75.9 to 77.5% (Fig. 1), across the three applicable conditions (AMI, HF, PN); however, there were some discrepancies, accounting for 22.5–24.1%. These findings align with the comparisons made across the three readmission measures, as shown in Table 1 and Supplement Tables 2 and 3. Similar performance patterns were observed across the cohorts when comparing ERR and RSRR measures; however, the performance between ERR/RSRR and EDAC was notably inconsistent. Specifically, some hospitals demonstrated strong performance under ERR but poor performance under EDAC [23].

To further assess the agreement in performance rankings between ERR and EDAC measures, the 3,047 HRRP hospitals were ranked by quintile group alignment analysis for each condition (AMI, HF, and PN) in three 5 × 5 contingency tables (Table 2). We also conducted a similar assessment for the reclassification of RSRR performance based on EDAC, as well as the reclassification of ERR performance based on RSRR. An assessment of RSRR performance reclassification using the EDAC measure (Supplement Table 4) revealed patterns similar to those observed when reclassifying ERR performance using EDAC. In contrast, minimal changes were noted when ERR performances were reclassified using the RSRR measure across the CMS readmission cohorts(Supplement Table 5).

In Table 2, each column represents a count of hospitals falling within a specific group ranking based on the readmission ERR measure, and each row displays the numbers and percentages (%) of hospitals that would (or would not) be reclassified based on their EDAC measure.

When a hospital’s performance is ranked into five groups by two measures (ERR and EDAC), three potential classification outcomes exist, as shown in Table 2, based on disparity scores: perfect agreement (dark gray cells, with a minimal disparity score of 0), partial agreement (light gray and unshaded cells, with disparity scores ranging from 1 to 3), and perfect disagreement (cells containing underlined values, with a maximal disparity score of 4).

The average percentages of perfect agreement in hospital performance between ERR and EDAC across the three conditions (AMI, HF, and PN) remained consistent and stayed below 50%, ranging from 35% − 37.2% (Table 2). When including the partial agreements, the overall agreement percentages between ERR and EDAC increased to a range of 76.4–77.4% (Fig. 1).

For AMI, of the 369 hospitals ranked in group 1 with ERR measure (Table 2), there were 175 hospitals with unchanged performance status (disparity score = 0) and 194 (52.6%) reclassified to a lower performance group (2 to 5). In contrast, 187 (50.7%) of 369 hospitals from group 5 were reclassified to a higher performance group (1 to 4) when the EDAC measure was used. Similar results for the HF and PN cohorts are described in the online appendix statistical analysis Section. [23]

We summarized the hospital counts with perfect disagreement (disparity score = 4) between ERR and EDAC, resulting in 13 hospitals for AMI, 10 hospitals for HF, and 18 hospitals for PN. In total, 41 hospitals experienced performance ranking group shifts, transitioning from the best to the worst or vice versa during reclassification (Table 2).

Out of these 41 hospitals with perfect disagreements between ERR and EDAC measures, a higher number, 30 hospitals, initially held the best ERR performance but shifted to the worst EDAC performance (including 7 hospitals for AMI and HF, and 16 hospitals for PN), while fewer hospitals,11 in total, had the worst ERR performance and the best EDAC performance (comprising 6 hospitals for AMI, 3 for HF, and 2 for PN). Remarkably, one hospital exhibited the same pattern of perfect disagreement (best ERR performance and worst EDAC performance) for two conditions, namely HF and PN.

Reclassification of HRRP penalty status

Of the HRRP hospitals assessed for penalty status in AMI, HF, and PN, the changes for either ERR- or RSRR-based penalties reclassified by EDAC were very similar (Fig. 2, Supplemental Fig. 2) [23]. When using the EDAC measure, there was only a small percentage of changes (range 7.3–13.2%) in the HRRP hospitals with the use of ERR or RSRR measures that would be subject to either penalties or non-penalties (p>0.05).

Reclassification of ERR-based penalty status based on the EDAC measure for AMI, HF, and PN

Some hospitals received no penalty with the use of the ERR or RSRR measures which would be up-classified to penalty status with the EDAC measure. The percentages of these hospitals (“Up-Classified to Penalty”), e.g., 7.3% for AMI, 11.9% for HF, and 12.8% for PN, are displayed as orange bars in Fig. 2.

Similarly, a comparable percentage of the hospitals that received penalties based on ERR would be down-classified to no penalty using the EDAC measure. The percentages of these hospitals (“Down-Classified to No Penalty”), e.g., 8.6% of hospitals for AMI, 12.2% of hospitals for HF, and 12.4% of hospitals for PN are displayed as grey bars in Fig. 2.

Overall, 15.9%, 24.1%, and 25.2% of hospitals for AMI, HF, and PN, respectively would change their ERR-based penalty status with either an up-classification or down-classification if the EDAC measure were used to evaluate performance.

Penalty status changes in subsets of HRRP hospitals

We further examined the proportion of the association of American medical colleges (AAMC), community hospitals, and hospitals with different socioeconomic status (SES) groups that would be up-classified and down-classified when the EDAC measure was used rather than ERR(Supplemental Tables 6 and Supplemental Fig. 4) [23].

For community hospitals, the EDAC measure resulted in more down-classification than up-classification for AMI by 1.7%, HF by 2.0%, and PN by 6.0% (Supplemental Table 6). However, for AAMC hospitals, the EDAC measure changed distribution compared to the ERR-based penalty status, and hospitals with an up-classification to penalty status increased 11.9% for AMI (p>0.05), 26.7% for HF (p<0.001), and 19.8% for PN (p<0.01). Thus, the results indicate that 19.8–29.7% of AAMC hospitals that did not receive a penalty based on the ERR measure would be penalized with the EDAC measure. In comparison, a much lower percentage of AAMC hospitals (ranging from 3 to 7.9%) would be down-classified from penalty status to no penalty status. The results suggest that using the EDAC measure could lead to significantly more AAMC hospitals being up-classified to penalty status while community hospitals would see down-classification of penalty status for HF and PN.

CMS started assessing a hospital’s performance relative to other peer hospitals with a similar proportion of patients who are dually eligible for Medicare and full Medicaid benefits in FY 2019. CMS divides hospitals into 5 quintiles or peer groups based on the percentage of dual-eligible stays (Medicare and Medicaid): quintile 1 (≤ 13.71% dual-eligible stays), quintile 2 (13.72% − 18.27%), quintile 3 (18.27% − 22.96%), quintile 4 (22.99% − 30.95%), and quintile 5 (≥ 31.00%). This classification has quintile 1 encompassing a high socioeconomic status (SES) group and quintile 5 encompassing a low-SES group or SNHs.

For hospitals with penalty status with EDAC, there are two types of changes, i.e., down-classification from penalty to no penalty (grey bars in Supplemental Fig. 4) [23] and up-classification from non-penalty to penalty (red bars in Supplemental Fig. 4) [23]. Although there was no statistically significant change in penalty status for any condition and any peer groups (p > 0.05), there was an overall trend of increased up-classification being more pronounced from peer group 5 (range − 2.0% to − 5.1%) to peer group 1 (range, 0.3–2.7%).

Among the 610 hospitals with high-SES (peer group 1), the EDAC measure was associated with a small net increase in penalty status (up-classification, 0.3% for AMI, 2.0% for HF, 2.7% for PN). A total of 609 hospitals in peer group 5 were classified as being low-SES or SNHs in FY 2022. The EDAC measure in peer group 5 was associated with a decreasing up-classification in penalty status (net-up classification: -5.1% for AMI, -3.3% for HF, and − 2.0% for PN) indicating that these hospitals would not be subject to penalties in FY 2022 if the EDAC measure were applied. The results suggest that the current ERR-based stratified payment adjustment method increases the number of SNHs that are penalized compared to EDAC.

Discussion

There are several noteworthy findings in this study. First, we confirmed that the EDAC measure would change HRRP hospitals’ performance and penalty status compared to ERR for the 3 conditions [3]. In addition, we revealed two new relationships (RSRR vs. EDAC and ERR vs. RSRR). ERR and RSRR had almost perfect agreement with each other on the readmission performance ranking and penalty status assignments for the six targeted cohorts (Table 2; Fig. 2, Supplemental Table 4, Supplemental Table 5, Supplemental Fig. 2, and Supplemental Fig. 3) [23]. Second, in addition to confirming the relationship between paired readmission measures,3 we further demonstrated a novel way of measuring disparity levels per hospital. We found that about 0.4%-2.8% of HRRP hospitals had maximal disparity scores and identified the 41 HRRP hospitals that showed the largest performance gap (disparity score = 4, Table 2). This perfect disagreement pattern in those hospitals indicates a concern regarding suboptimal quality management when making extra efforts to transition index and/or readmitted inpatients to outpatient settings. Third, 19.8–29.7% of AAMC hospitals that did not receive a penalty under ERR would be penalized if EDAC were used by the HRRP. However, a down-classification in penalty status was more common with community hospitals (Supplemental Table 6) [23] and hospitals that provide care for patients who are at a socioeconomic disadvantage (Supplemental Fig. 4) [23].

It is not surprising that the ERR and RSRR measures had the highest agreement in the analysis because the ERR used in HRRP and the consumer-oriented RSRR are calculated using the same methodology and reporting period, but they are calculated among different sets of hospitals. RSRR includes more hospitals that are excluded from ERR, such as hospitals in U.S. territories and Veterans Affairs hospitals (Supplemental Table 2). Therefore, the ERR values used in the RSRR formula are not identical to those used in HRRP. There is another difference between ERR and RSRR in the hospital-level readmission rate calculation formulas. The RSRR formula involves the national observed readmission rate, while ERR is the measure of a hospital’s relative performance that CMS uses in the payment reduction calculations to assess hospitals’ excess readmissions for each condition or procedure.

Certain factors may have opposing effects on changes in ERR and EDAC values. EDAC captures the total number of days in multiple readmission types that a patient spends in ED, in observation status, or admitted as an unplanned readmission, while ERR counts only the first inpatient revisit using binary metrics that do not account for the length of stay. Notably, the increasing use of ED visits and observation stays will increase EDAC but not ERR or RSRR. Therefore, under HRRP, there is a concern that hospitals could make their ERR and RSRR rates seem lower without accurately reflecting the quality of care.

Higher mortality following index discharges has a greater impact on ERR than on EDAC. Patients who die post-discharge have less opportunity for readmission but are counted as being at the same risk for readmission as those who survive the full measurement period. Thus, hospitals with higher post-discharge death rates may perform better (lower ERR) under the current HRRP because a patient who dies shortly after discharge cannot be readmitted [28, 29]. However, EDAC quantifies stay length in days during readmissions and accounts for the risk of death and survival time after index discharge (Supplemental Table 2) and is not influenced by higher post-discharge death rates.

The majority of hospitals penalized were large hospitals, teaching hospitals, and SNHs with low SES [30]. The current study’s findings demonstrate that the EDAC measure would substantially change the distribution of hospital performance (Table 2) and financial penalties under the HRRP (Fig. 2) for AAMC hospitals, community hospitals (Supplement Table 6), and SNHs (Supplement Fig. 4).

Prior research suggested that differences between hospitals are likely related to both case mix (medical complexity) and socioeconomic diversity of the patient population [31, 32]. In this study, we found that the EDAC measure could significantly up-classify penalty status in AAMC hospitals and down-classify penalty status in community hospitals (Supplement Table 6). These community hospitals are more likely to receive penalty cuts under the EDAC measure. Community hospitals are more commonly non-teaching and physician-owned hospitals, located in rural regions, and hospitals with fewer hospital beds. The results increase concerns that the ERR measure may disproportionately penalize hospitals that care for vulnerable populations.

CMS ERR stratifies hospitals into five peer groups based on their respective proportions of patients who are dually eligible for Medicare and Medicaid. CMS compares one hospital’s performance against other hospitals treating patient populations of similar socioeconomic status (within one of five individual peer groups).

Our analysis of the distribution of penalties using the ERR versus EDAC measures across the five hospital peer groups indicates that there were no significant changes in penalty status within individual peer groups (p > 0.05, Supplement Fig. 4). However, an overall increasing trend of down-classified penalties was observed from peer group 1 (high-SES group) at 8.9% (95% CI, 8.6–9.2%) to peer group 5 at 12.5% (95% CI, 8.4–16.5%). After balancing out the up-classified penalties, on average, about 3.5%of SNHs in peer group 5 would be down-classified from penalty status to non-penalty if EDAC were used (Supplement Fig. 4). The result suggests that the current ERR method puts some SNHs at a slightly higher risk of incurring penalties, which is aligned with other studies [33].

Wadhera et al.3 had previously compared how performance rankings and financial penalties change when using the one-paired readmission measure. They had also found that significantly fewer rural hospitals would be penalized with the use of the EDAC measure for the three targeted conditions.

In this study, we compared changes in performance and penalty status using three-paired readmission measures. This was done not only by switching readmission measures but also by introducing a new metric, the disparity score, to quantify the difference between the differences of paired readmission measures for individual HRRP hospitals.

We also compared several new types of hospitals, such as AAMC and community hospitals, with a particular focus on five hospital groups categorized by the percentage of dual-eligible patients for Medicare and full Medicaid benefits. These categories, implemented in the current HRRP, were not examined in Wadhera’s study [3]. These categories, implemented in the current HRRP, were not examined in Wadhera’s study.

Both the EDAC and ERR measures have their own advantages and limitations. The ERR measure is a straightforward metric valued for its simplicity and familiarity, allowing hospitals to directly track performance on readmission events by focusing on six specific conditions or procedures. However, the ERR measure provides an incomplete picture as it is limited to unplanned inpatient readmissions, with potential for ‘Gaming’ and disproportionately penalizing hospitals that serve vulnerable populations.

In contrast, the EDAC measure offers a more comprehensive perspective on broad readmission types, with the potential to reduce “Gaming” and minimize the impact on rural and small hospitals (Supplemental Table 2). However, the EDAC measure has its limitations as it only applies to three target conditions (AMI, HF, and PN) and is more complex to calculate and interpret.

Our findings, along with other studies [3]indicate only moderate agreement between EDAC and ERR measures in hospital performance rankings. This suggests that the two metrics capture somewhat different aspects of care quality. Therefore, ERR cannot simply be replaced with EDAC, highlighting the importance of the disparity score measure established in this study. This measure quantifies the gap between ERR and EDAC and serves as a warning signal when a hospital exhibits a maximal disparity score. The hospital can then conduct a more comprehensive assessment of post-discharge care using EDAC data to gain deeper insights. By properly utilizing the disparity score measure alongside ERR, we can enhance the incentives for hospitals to reduce avoidable readmissions.

This study has limitations. First, there is no agreed-upon method to determine the penalty status of HRRP hospitals using a non-ERR-based method. We developed EDAC and RSRR for penalty calculation based on the algorithms of the CMS stratified method, but others may take a different approach. Second, we were unable to compute hospitals’ specific penalties when assessing the association between the penalty amounts and hospital performance changes from the three measures; to do so would require additional (claims) data. However, the penalty status as a qualitative indicator is a strong proxy for the penalty. Third, although the data presented represents penalties for FY 2022, it is unknown whether the same or a subset of the same 30 hospitals (Table 2) with perfect disagreements will maintain this performance gap and be penalized with EDAC as the program ramps up in FY 2023 and FY 2024.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that CMS could enhance its reporting by simultaneously presenting penalty rates from the ERR measure alongside the new measure (disparity scores). This combined approach would provide a more comprehensive assessment than relying solely on the ERR measure. Incorporating the disparity scores into the HRRP framework could also support efforts to track individual hospital quality management models designed to address inequities.
