Brain network localization of anhedonia
Authors: Chenglong Liu, Yu Song, Xufeng Zhao, Zhili Wang, Liping Yan, Yongqiang Yu, Jiajia Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnhedonia, encompassing a broad spectrum of deficits in reward processing, is highly prevalent in major depressive disorder (MDD) and constitutes one of its core symptoms. While substantial progress has recently been made in mapping neuropsychiatric symptoms to specific brain networks, focused efforts to examine network localization of anhedonia are limited. We initially synthesized extant neuroimaging literature to identify brain locations with structural or functional alterations related to anhedonia. By integrating these affected brain locations with large-scale discovery (1113 healthy individuals) and validation (1093 healthy individuals and 255 MDD patients) resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging datasets, we then applied novel functional connectivity network mapping to construct an anhedonia network. The anhedonia network was composed of the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, insula, lateral prefrontal cortex, and striatum, principally implicating the canonical ventral attention and subcortical networks. Further analyses revealed that the trait and state anhedonia networks preferentially involved the default and limbic networks respectively, in addition to the commonly affected ventral attention and subcortical networks. Our findings may not only advance the understanding of the neurobiology underlying anhedonia from a network perspective, but also potentially contribute to more targeted and effective intervention strategies for anhedonia.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Psychiatric disorders

Introduction

Anhedonia is traditionally defined as a decrease in an individual’s ability to experience pleasure or interest, which has been extended by the modern framework to encompass a broader spectrum of deficits in reward processing [1, 2]. Anhedonia is highly prevalent in major depressive disorder (MDD) and constitutes one of its core symptoms [3]. Conventional antidepressants, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, have shown a limited clinical benefit on anhedonia, which is prominently associated with treatment-resistant depression [4]. Moreover, there is strong evidence that anhedonia could serve as an independent risk factor for suicide [5–7]. These findings suggest that anhedonia requires careful assessment and targeted treatment, highlighting the need to elucidate its neurobiology to improve therapy development. Although substantial preclinical and clinical research indicates that dysfunction in neural reward circuitry along with alterations in multiple relevant neurotransmitter systems are implicated in anhedonia [4, 8–11], a complete picture of its brain substrates is yet to be unveiled.

Continuing improvements in in vivo neuroimaging techniques and analytic approaches have enabled a more precise examination of brain structure and function in health and disease [12–19]. Taking advantage of neuroimaging tools, numerous studies have documented brain structural and functional alterations linked to anhedonia, with the anterior cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, insula, striatum, thalamus, and amygdala being preferentially affected [4, 20–24]. However, the extent and nature of such changes have varied significantly across studies. The marked heterogeneity in previous results may be reconciled by an increasingly recognized notion that abnormalities in distinct brain locations that cause the same symptom can map to a common brain network [25]. Motivated by this perspective, brain localization of a neuropsychiatric symptom has recently shifted from a dominant region-based approach to an updated network-based paradigm. In this instance, a novel and well-validated functional connectivity network mapping (FCNM) approach has been developed to achieve network localization of a disease, a symptom or a psychological process, by integrating brain locations of interest (e.g., lesion, structural damage, functional abnormality, and neural activation) with large-scale functional brain connectome data [26–33]. The FCNM approach has enjoyed considerable success in mapping a variety of neurological and psychiatric symptoms to common symptom-specific brain networks [34–44]. Despite these myriad points of interest, focused efforts to examine brain network localization of anhedonia with the FCNM approach have been limited.

To address this missing gap, this exploratory study initially synthesized extant neuroimaging literature to identify brain locations with structural or functional alterations related to anhedonia. By integrating these affected brain locations with large-scale discovery and validation resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) datasets, we then applied the FCNM approach to construct an anhedonia network. Materials and methods

Study search and selection

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we conducted a comprehensive literature search on the PubMed and Web of Science to select neuroimaging studies reporting brain locations that were associated with anhedonia, published before October 1st, 2024. For completeness, we used a combination of many search terms: “anhedonia”, “MRI”, “fMRI”, “magnetic resonance imaging”, “PET”, “positron emission tomography”, “SPECT”, “single photon emission computed tomography”, “ASL”, “arterial spin labeling”, “neuroimaging”, “FC”, “functional connectivity”, “ReHo”, “regional homogeneity”, “ALFF”, “fALFF”, “amplitude of low frequency fluctuation”, “CBF”, “blood flow”, “glucose metabolism”, “VBM”, “voxel-based morphometry”, “DBM”, “deformation-based morphometry”, “GMV”, “gray matter”, “grey matter”. The reference lists of relevant reviews and meta-analyses were also hand-searched to identify additional studies.

Since a single study may contain multiple contrasts (i.e., group comparison in a neuroimaging measure between individuals with and without anhedonia or between individuals with high and low anhedonia, or correlation analysis between a neuroimaging measure and anhedonia severity), we focused our analysis on contrasts rather than studies. Inclusion criteria for the contrasts were: (1) to compare structural or functional neuroimaging measures between individuals with and without anhedonia or between individuals with high and low anhedonia; (2) to correlate structural or functional neuroimaging measures with scale scores on anhedonia; (3) to conduct analyses in a voxel-wise manner and at a whole-brain level rather than a region of interest level; (4) to report coordinates of significant brain clusters in a standard reference space (Talairach or Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] space). Two investigators (C.L. and Y.S.) performed the study search and screening procedures independently. A flowchart of the study selection process is shown in Figure S1 in the Supplementary materials. This protocol was registered on PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/, registration number: CRD420251023285). Coordinates of peak voxels of significant clusters reported in each contrast were extracted, with coordinates in Talairach space converted to MNI space.

Since anhedonia is a multidimensional construct, we further classified the contrasts into two categories that focused on trait and state anhedonia, respectively. This classification was based on rating scales used in the literature. Specifically, the Chapman Scale for Physical Anhedonia (CPAS), Chapman Scale for Social Anhedonia (CSAS) and Temporal Experience of Pleasure Scale (TEPS) are typically adopted to assess trait anhedonia, and the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS) is often used to evaluate state anhedonia [1, 45].

Discovery and validation datasets

Our study used the Anhui Medical University Dataset (AMUD) as a discovery dataset and the Human Connectome Project (HCP) as a validation dataset. The AMUD included 1113 healthy adults of Han Chinese (643 female, mean age = 32.66 ± 12.78 years, 1110 right-handedness), who were enrolled from local universities and communities through poster advertisements. Participants with neuropsychiatric or severe somatic disorders, a history of head injury with loss of consciousness, MRI contraindications, or a family history of psychiatric diseases among their first-degree relatives were excluded. The HCP dataset is available for download to anyone agreeing to the open access data use terms (https://db.humanconnectome.org/). 1093 healthy young adults (594 female, mean age = 28.78 ± 3.69 years) from the HCP were included in the validation dataset. The full details regarding the sample have been reported in previous studies [46–48]. The HCP protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Washington University in St. Louis, MO, USA, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

In addition to the above-mentioned healthy samples, we included a MDD sample as another validation dataset. The MDD dataset included 255 MDD individuals of Han Chinese (161 female, mean age = 40.99 ± 12.67 years, 252 right-handedness), who were recruited consecutively from Affiliated Psychological Hospital of Anhui Medical University. Following the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), two well-trained psychiatrists confirmed the diagnosis of MDD. The exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of other psychiatric disorders such as substance-induced (e.g., drug or alcohol) mood disorder, bipolar disorder, anxiety disorder, schizophrenia, substance abuse or addiction; (2) a history of severe physical or neurological disease; (3) a history of head injury with loss of consciousness; (4) contraindications for MRI such as pregnancy. All patients were receiving their regular antidepressant medications, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, or noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressants. For both the AMUD and MDD datasets, the protocols were approved by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, and all participants provided written informed consent after being given a complete description of the study. Demographic information of the discovery and validation datasets is provided in Table S1 in the Supplementary materials.

fMRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Resting-state blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI data of the AMUD and MDD datasets were collected on a 3.0-Tesla General Electric Discovery MR750w scanner, and those of the HCP dataset on a 3.0-Tesla Siemens Skyra scanner. The fMRI parameters of the three datasets are provided in Table S2 in the Supplementary materials. Participants with poor image quality (e.g., visible artifacts) or incomplete brain coverage were excluded.

The AMUD and MDD resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI, http://rfmri.org/dpabi) [49]. The first 10 volumes for each participant were discarded to allow the signal to reach equilibrium and the participants to adapt to the scanning noise. The remaining volumes were corrected for the acquisition time delay between slices. Then, realignment was performed to correct the motion between time points. Head motion parameters were computed by estimating the translation in each direction and the angular rotation on each axis for each volume. The data of all participants were within the defined motion thresholds (i.e., maximal translational or rotational motion parameters less than 2 mm or 2° for AMUD and less than 3 mm or 3° for MDD). We also calculated frame-wise displacement (FD), which indexes the volume-to-volume changes in head position. Several nuisance covariates (the linear drift, the estimated motion parameters based on the Friston-24 model, the spike volumes with FD > 0.5 mm, the global signal, the white matter signal, and the cerebrospinal fluid signal) were regressed out from the data. Since global signal regression can enhance the detection of system-specific correlations and improve the correspondence to anatomical connectivity, we included this step in the preprocessing of resting-state fMRI data [50]. Next, the datasets were band-pass filtered using a frequency range of 0.01-0.1 Hz. In the normalization step, individual structural images were first co-registered with the mean functional images; the transformed structural images were then segmented and normalized to MNI space using a high-level nonlinear warping algorithm, i.e., the diffeomorphic anatomical registration through exponentiated Lie algebra technique [51]. Then, each filtered functional volume was spatially normalized to MNI space using the deformation parameters estimated during the above step and resampled into 3-mm isotropic voxel. Finally, all data were spatially smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6 × 6 × 6 mm3 full-width at half maximum (FWHM).

The HCP resting-state fMRI data were minimally preprocessed with echo planar imaging gradient distortion correction, motion correction, field bias correction, spatial transformation and normalization into MNI space, and artifact removal using independent component analysis (ICA) + FIX [47, 52]. Additionally to the HCP minimal preprocessing, several procedures were further performed using the DPABI software. Briefly, nuisance variables including the global, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid signals were regressed out. Then, the data were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz). Finally, the data were spatially smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

Functional connectivity network mapping

With the FCNM method, we constructed an anhedonia network based on the extracted coordinates of brain locations related to anhedonia reported in prior studies (Fig. 1). First, 4-mm radius spheres centered at each coordinate of a contrast were created and merged together to generate a combined seed mask specific to that contrast (henceforth referred to as the contrast seed) in accordance with previous FCNM research [28–30, 53–55]. Second, based on the preprocessed resting-state fMRI data, we computed a contrast seed-to-whole brain functional connectivity (FC) map for each subject, by calculating Pearson’s correlation coefficients between time courses of the contrast seed and each voxel within the whole brain, followed by Fisher’s Z-transformation to improve normality. Third, the subject-level FC maps were entered into a voxel-wise one-sample t test to identify brain regions functionally connected to each contrast seed. We only considered positive FC as the biological meaning of negative FC is still a matter of debate [50, 56]. Fourth, the resulting group-level t maps were thresholded and binarized at P < 0.05 corrected for multiple comparisons. Note that a voxel-level family-wise error (FWE) correction was used for the AMUD and HCP datasets, while a less stringent voxel-level false discovery rate (FDR) correction was used for the MDD dataset due to its reduced sample size. Finally, the binarized maps were overlaid to produce a network probability map, which was thresholded at 50% to yield the anhedonia network. Furthermore, we used the same analytic procedure to construct trait and state anhedonia networks based on the extracted coordinates of brain locations related to trait and state anhedonia, respectively.

Association with canonical brain networks

To ease interpretability, we examined the spatial associations between the anhedonia networks and eight well-established canonical brain networks. The seven cortical networks were the visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, limbic, frontoparietal, and default networks from the study by Yeo et al. [57]. The subcortical network included the amygdala, hippocampus, basal ganglia, and thalamus from the Human Brainnetome Atlas [58]. The proportion of overlapping voxels between each anhedonia network and a canonical network to all voxels within the corresponding canonical network was computed to estimate their spatial association.

Validation analyses

We carried out several validation analyses to test the robustness of our results. First, we performed the same analyses by using two independent validation datasets (i.e., the HCP and MDD datasets) to assess the impact of dataset selection. Second, we repeated the FCNM procedure with use of 1-mm and 7-mm radius spheres to evaluate the effect of seed size. Third, a validation analysis was conducted by excluding the contrasts in healthy individuals and focusing solely on the contrasts in patient populations. Fourth, given the rather small number of the brain structural contrasts, we sought to rule out their influence by repeating our analyses using the brain functional contrasts only. To quantify the spatial similarity between the anhedonia networks from the main and validation analyses, we calculated a Dice coefficient, defined as 2 × (overlapping voxels) / (network #1 voxels) + (network #2 voxels). Fifth, to examine the impact of threshold selection, we repeated the FCNM procedure using 45% and 55% overlap thresholds.

Results

Included studies

After a comprehensive literature search and selection, a total of 20 studies with 29 contrasts (17 contrasts on MDD, 2 contrasts on obsessive-compulsive disorder, 2 contrasts on schizophrenia, 1 contrast on bipolar disorder, and 7 contrasts on healthy subjects) were included in our analyses. Among them, there were 18 group comparison contrasts (i.e., anhedonia vs. non-anhedonia or high vs. low anhedonia) from 12 studies and 11 correlation contrasts (i.e., neuroimaging-anhedonia correlations) from 8 studies. Moreover, 15 contrasts from 11 studies were included in the trait anhedonia analysis and 9 contrasts from 6 studies in the state anhedonia analysis. With respect to neuroimaging modality, 27 brain functional contrasts and 2 brain structural contrasts were included in our analysis. Sample and imaging characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Tables S3–S5 in the Supplementary materials.

Anhedonia network

The anhedonia network was mainly composed of the bilateral dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, insula, lateral prefrontal cortex, and striatum (nucleus accumbens, caudate, and lentiform nucleus) (Fig. 2A). Concerning canonical networks, the anhedonia network principally involved the ventral attention (overlapping proportion: 17.1%) and subcortical (20%) networks (Fig. 2B).

Trait anhedonia network

The trait anhedonia network primarily consisted of the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, prefrontal cortex, insula, striatum (nucleus accumbens and caudate), precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex, and inferior parietal lobule, as well as the right lateral temporal cortex (Fig. 3A). Regarding canonical networks, additionally to the ventral attention (9.9%) and subcortical (9%) networks, the default network (19.9%) was the most prominently affected canonical network (Fig. 3A).

State anhedonia network

The state anhedonia network chiefly comprised the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, insula, striatum (nucleus accumbens, caudate, and lentiform nucleus), thalamus, and amygdala (Fig. 3B). As to canonical networks, besides the ventral attention (10.9%) and subcortical (41%) networks, the limbic network (21.4%) was an additional preferentially affected canonical network (Fig. 3B).

Validation analyses

First, we found that the anhedonia networks derived from the HCP and MDD datasets were similar to that from the AMUD (Figure S2 in the Supplementary materials). Second, when repeating the FCNM procedure with use of 1-mm and 7-mm radius spheres, we observed that the resulting anhedonia networks were nearly identical to that using the 4-mm radius sphere (Figure S3 in the Supplementary materials). Third, the anhedonia network based solely on the contrasts in patient populations was largely consistent with our main result (Figure S4 in the Supplementary materials). Fourth, when repeating our analyses using the brain functional contrasts only, the resultant anhedonia network was similar to that in our main analysis (Figure S5 in the Supplementary materials). The Dice coefficients between the anhedonia networks from the main and validation analyses are shown in Table S6 in the Supplementary materials. Fifth, using the 45% and 55% overlap thresholds yielded anhedonia networks that had larger and smaller spatial extents than that based on the 50% threshold (Figure S6 in the Supplementary materials). However, the core areas of the anhedonia network were still present across these thresholds. For completeness, we constructed the anhedonia network based on negative FC and found that the resultant network mainly involved the visual, default, and frontoparietal networks (Figure S7 in the Supplementary materials). In addition, as most of the studies included in our analysis assessed consummatory anhedonia and only a limited number of studies evaluated anticipatory anhedonia, we conducted brain network localization for consummatory anhedonia. As shown in Figure S8 in the Supplementary materials, the consummatory anhedonia network and its affected canonical brain networks shared some degree of similarities with our main result.

Discussion

Through the combination of the novel FCNM approach and large-scale functional brain connectome data, we investigated brain network localization of anhedonia, based on brain locations with structural or functional alterations in relation to anhedonia reported in previous literature. Results showed that the anhedonia network was composed of the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, insula, lateral prefrontal cortex, and striatum, principally implicating the canonical ventral attention and subcortical networks. Further analyses demonstrated that the trait and state anhedonia networks preferentially involved the default and limbic networks respectively, in addition to the commonly affected ventral attention and subcortical networks. Our findings may not only advance the understanding of the neurobiology underlying anhedonia from a network perspective, but also potentially contribute to more targeted and effective intervention strategies for anhedonia.

Though neuroimaging has been frequently used to find brain aberrations in psychiatric illnesses, the results are often irreproducible, limiting their translation into clinical applications [59–61]. The lack of reproducibility may stem from many factors, such as small sample sizes resulting in limited statistical power, high clinical and demographic heterogeneity of study populations, experimental design variability, and data analytics diversity [59, 60]. To alleviate the impact of these factors, one effective tool is to perform a coordinate-based neuroimaging meta-analysis, which can synthesize results from different studies to find the more consistently affected brain regions linked to a disease or a symptom [62]. However, there is an increasing agreement that a disease or a symptom does not map to a single brain region but rather to an interconnected network, as neuropathological processes typically occur in a distributed fashion [63]. Therefore, brain localization of a disease or a symptom has gradually transitioned from a regional approach to a connectomic method. This transformation has benefited fundamentally from the development of the FCNM approach, which combines brain locations of interest (e.g., lesion, structural damage, functional abnormality, and neural activation) and large-scale brain connectome data to localize a disease or a symptom to a common network [26–33, 62]. Leveraging the FCNM method, researchers have mapped many neuropsychiatric diseases and symptoms, some of which have eluded traditional regional localization, to specific brain networks [34–44]. While several coordinate-based neuroimaging meta-analyses have reported regional brain abnormalities related to anhedonia [22, 24], our work complements and extends these earlier efforts by applying the FCNM approach to further identify the network substrates of anhedonia from a connectomic perspective.

We found that the anhedonia network was mainly composed of the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, insula, lateral prefrontal cortex and striatum, some of which are important components of the neural reward circuit [64, 65]. Since the anterior cingulate cortex receives information about rewarding outcomes [66], previous research has demonstrated that anhedonia in depression and schizophrenia patients can be attributed to dysfunction in the anterior cingulate cortex [67]. In the earlier two meta-analyses, the anterior cingulate cortex has also been shown to be associated with anticipatory anhedonia [22, 24]. The insula is a complex brain region that is central to the coordination of taste, internal perception, emotion, and decision-making [68]. In terms of reward processing, the insula is also a key region responsible for integrating reward and surprise information [69, 70]. Hyperactive responses to uncertainty in the anterior insula may mediate the correlation between high intolerance of uncertainty and dysfunction in reward anticipation [71]. The striatum can be subdivided into different parts, which subserve distinct functions [72]. The dorsal striatum is involved in reward and decision making, while the ventral striatum plays a major role in reward anticipation and consumption [73, 74]. Empirical evidence indicates that stress-related anhedonia is associated with ventral striatum reactivity to reward [75]. Lynch et al found that changes in frontostriatal connectivity could predict future anhedonia symptoms [76].

The trait anhedonia network additionally affected the precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex and lateral temporal cortex. The precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex, a critical hub for multimodal integration, demonstrates extensive structural connectivity and functional coupling with multiple brain regions [77, 78]. Lee et al reported weaker correlations between precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex activity and anhedonia in schizophrenia patients compared to healthy controls [79]. It has been evident that the precuneus may be associated with trait anhedonia following childhood trauma [80, 81]. A task fMRI study demonstrated that responses in the lateral temporal cortex under high-reward versus low-reward conditions were attenuated in MDD patients relative to healthy controls [82]. The state anhedonia network additionally affected the orbitofrontal cortex, thalamus, and amygdala. As a key cortical area of the neural reward circuitry, the orbitofrontal cortex is critically implicated in emotion and reward processing [83]. The paraventricular thalamic nucleus can mediate aversion and reward-related behaviors [84], acting as an interface to the brain’s reward circuitry [85]. With regard to the amygdala, prior studies have reported that the neural pathway from the anterior cingulate cortex to the basolateral amygdala is associated with reward, and blockade of astrocyte glutamate transporter proteins in the central amygdala could induce a lack of pleasure [86, 87].

The anhedonia network principally involved the canonical ventral attention and subcortical networks. The ventral attention network is assumed to associate with visuospatial cognition [88]. Schneider et al revealed that ventral attention network activity correlated with pupillary dilation, thereby linking this network to heightened arousal and reward anticipation [89]. Scientific advances at the interface of subcortical brain anatomy and network science have highlighted the possible contribution of subcortical structures to large-scale network communication [90]. Compared with non-anhedonia children, anhedonia children were found to exhibit reduced functional connectivity between large-scale networks and subcortical areas [91]. By using data-driven clustering to differentiate subtypes of MDD, Wang and colleagues found that the anhedonia-dominated subtype of MDD was characterized by abnormal hypoconnectivity in the subcortical network and prominent anhedonia symptoms [92]. Our further analyses demonstrated that in addition to the commonly affected ventral attention and subcortical networks, the trait and state anhedonia networks preferentially involved the default and limbic networks, respectively. Emerging evidence suggests that reward experience enhances task-dependent connections between the default mode network and the ventral striatum [93]. Lee et al found that altered structural connectivity of large-scale brain networks, including the default mode and central executive networks, contributed to the progression of trait anhedonia in schizophrenic patients [94]. The limbic network is traditionally thought to be associated with emotional and memory processing [95, 96]. A recent study revealed that MDD patients with high anhedonia had a single abnormally increased functional connectivity component in a frontal-limbic circuit; moreover, anhedonia symptoms could mediate the relationship between this disrupted component and suicidal ideation [97], emphasizing the intimate link between state anhedonia and the limbic network.

There are several limitations of the current study that we need to acknowledge. First, our literature search was not limited to a certain disease given our hypothesis that anhedonia maps to a common network irrespective of disease contexts, yet we cannot exclude the possibility that distinct diseases have different and specific anhedonia networks. Future research should focus on investigating the anhedonia networks in different diseases and examining their variations across diseases (e.g., comparing the anhedonia networks in MDD vs. schizophrenia). Second, we utilized resting-state fMRI data from two healthy samples and a MDD sample to conduct network localization of anhedonia. It seems more appropriate to use fMRI data from a sample that is comparable to the selected studies’ participants in terms of demographic and clinical profiles. However, there is relevant literature indicating that sample selection does not have a significant impact on network localization [43, 98, 99]. Third, we included a few studies on anhedonia in healthy individuals as well as pooled studies on brain structural and functional alterations together for analysis, which may influence the interpretation of our results. Our validation analyses based on the patient contrasts or brain functional contrasts yielded results similar to those in our main analysis, indicating a negligible effect. Fourth, the classification of anhedonia was based on rating scales used in the literature. While this classification is standard, it may not be perfectly discrete. More refined classification methods would likely add value to future studies. Fifth, we did not consider each study’s sample size and effect size in our FCNM analysis, since there has been no consensus yet on how to account for these factors. Further investigation of their influences, in concert with analytical advances in the future, will help address this issue. Sixth, due to the limited number of the contrasts related to anticipatory anhedonia, we did not map the anticipatory anhedonia network. The similarities and differences between the consummatory and anticipatory anhedonia networks warrant further investigation but are beyond the scope of this study. Finally, an overlapping threshold of 50% was used to identify brain areas that were functionally connected to 50% of the contrast seeds in accordance with previous research [54, 55]. We selected this relatively lenient threshold due to the fact that no consensus has been reached on the threshold selection and we could not establish the anhedonia network at a more rigorous threshold.

In conclusion, this study combined the novel FCNM approach and large-scale functional brain connectome data to construct the anhedonia network along with its trait and state forms. Our findings may not only improve our understanding of the neurobiology underlying anhedonia from a network perspective, but also potentially contribute to more targeted and effective intervention strategies for anhedonia. The current identification of these anhedonia networks may expose unique treatment targets and reliable biomarkers for different forms of anhedonia. That said, these anhedonia networks may hold translational potential to refine brain stimulation targets for noninvasive neuromodulation approaches (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation and transcranial current stimulation), promoting precision medicine in psychiatry. In addition, they may serve as reliable biomarkers that have prognostic utility in monitoring symptom progression and the effects of pharmacological or psychological interventions in individuals with anhedonia.
