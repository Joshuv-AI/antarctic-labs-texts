Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analysis reveal distinct tumor microenvironment signatures in primary and recurrent hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Zhimou Cai, Jinhong Zhang, Yaodong Ding, Zhenglin Xu, Zhi Liu, Zhiwei Qiang, Zhaoyue Lu, Lin Chen, Weiping Wen, Wenbin Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC) is a rare, highly aggressive malignancy, with recurrence playing a pivotal role in treatment failure and cancer-related mortality. The tumor microenvironment (TME) characteristics and molecular mechanisms driving recurrence in HPSCC remain poorly defined.Materials and methodsPrimary HPSCC (P-HPSCC) samples (n = 6) and recurrent HPSCC (R-HPSCC) samples (n = 3) were analyzed. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics were employed to dissect the cellular composition and molecular characteristics of the TME. Key regulatory networks were identified through gene expression analysis, pathway enrichment, and intercellular communication profiling. Functional validation experiments were performed to confirm the biological relevance of the pathways identified.ResultsScRNA-seq analysis revealed that malignant epithelial cells (maEpCs) in recurrent tumors exhibited increased stemness, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and immune evasion programs. Mechanistically, IGF2BP2 was selectively upregulated in R-HPSCC and functioned as an m6A reader to stabilize SMAD3 mRNA, thereby activating transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) signaling and facilitating malignant progression. Integrated spatial transcriptomics and functional assays demonstrated that cancer-associated fibroblasts in recurrent tumors promote progression via FN1-mediated signaling. Concurrently, the recurrent TME underwent myeloid remodeling toward an immunosuppressive state, characterized by increased infiltration of SPP1+ tumor-associated macrophages and further enrichment of LAMP3+ dendritic cells with impaired antigen-presenting capacity, alongside aggravated CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, marked by upregulation of CTLA4 as a potential immune escape driver.ConclusionsThis study identifies recurrence-associated cellular and molecular features in HPSCC. Recurrent tumors exhibited maEpC reprogramming via IGF2BP2-mediated activation of TGF-β signaling, coupled with FN1-dependent stromal activation and immune evasion marked by myeloid immunosuppressive remodeling and CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, characterized by CTLA4 upregulation. Collectively, these findings reveal a pro-recurrent TME and suggest potential therapeutic targets in R-HPSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s11658-026-00991-z.Keywords: Hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, Tumor recurrence, Immune remodeling, Tumor microenvironment, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Spatial transcriptomics

Introduction

Although hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (HPSCC) accounts for less than 5% of all head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cases, it remains one of the most prognostically unfavorable subtypes, with a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 30–40% [1, 2]. As a highly aggressive and lethal cancer, HPSCC exhibits a strong tendency for recurrence, even after aggressive treatment [3]. Notably, nearly 50% of patients experience recurrence within 1 year of diagnosis, with few eligible for curative therapy, making recurrence the primary cause of treatment failure and cancer-related mortality in HPSCC [4, 5]. Due to the disease’s rarity, research on recurrent HPSCC (R-HPSCC) is limited, and the biological mechanisms underlying its rapid recurrence post-treatment remain poorly understood. Additionally, the risk factors for recurrence and optimal management strategies have yet to be clearly defined.

The tumor microenvironment (TME), composed of diverse cell types and their spatiotemporal interactions, plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis, progression, and therapeutic response [6, 7]. Emerging evidence suggests that tumor recurrence is not merely a continuation of the primary disease but rather reflects a significant reprogramming of both tumor cell states and the TME under therapeutic pressure [8–10]. In HPSCC, recurrent tumors are often associated with a more aggressive clinical phenotype; however, the cellular and molecular factors driving these phenotypic changes remain poorly understood. Specifically, how recurrent tumor cells are supported by tumor-intrinsic reprogramming and complex intercellular signaling networks within the TME has not been fully explored. Furthermore, there is increasing recognition that investigating the TME and the remodeling of its cellular regulatory networks can significantly aid in the discovery and identification of therapeutic targets in tumors [11]. These knowledge gaps highlight the urgent need for an in-depth investigation of the recurrence process. A systematic characterization of the cellular composition and functional states of distinct TME components is critical for elucidating the mechanisms driving HPSCC recurrence, which will not only improve our understanding of HPSCC pathogenesis, but also facilitate the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

Despite the clinical benefits of immune checkpoint blockers (ICBs) in various cancers in recent years [12, 13], their efficacy in HNSCC, including HPSCC, remains suboptimal [14]. The therapeutic efficacy of ICBs is limited by the immune microenvironment [15]. Consequently, dissecting the immune landscape of the HPSCC TME is essential for understanding the mechanisms of immune evasion that hinder ICB effectiveness and for guiding the development of more potent immunotherapeutic strategies, particularly in the recurrent setting. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has become a pivotal tool for exploring TME cellular components, their heterogeneity, and interactions. It provides unparalleled insights into intratumoral transcriptional diversity and facilitates the precise characterization of distinct cell populations’ roles in drug resistance and tumor prognosis [16, 17]. While several studies, including our previously established comprehensive single-cell atlas of primary HPSCC (P-HPSCC), have examined immune cell and tumor cell heterogeneity in P-HPSCC [18–21], a comprehensive single-cell immune landscape of R-HPSCC is still lacking. Notably, systematic single-cell comparative studies between P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC remain scarce, and spatial information, essential for understanding cell interactions within the tissue architecture, is largely absent.

To investigate the dynamic remodeling of the TME during HPSCC recurrence and uncover its key driving mechanisms, this study integrated scRNA-seq, high-definition spatial transcriptomics (ST), and functional validation experiments to systematically characterize and compare the cellular composition, molecular programs, and intercellular communication networks in P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC. By dissecting tumor cells, stromal components, and diverse immune cell populations at both single-cell and spatial resolutions, this study elucidated tumor-intrinsic programs associated with HPSCC recurrence and identified that the TME of R-HPSCC is typically accompanied by stromal activation and immune dysfunction. Our findings provide a comprehensive cellular and molecular framework for understanding HPSCC recurrence and offer insights into potential therapeutic vulnerabilities in R-HPSCC. Moreover, the systematic analysis of immune cell dynamics during HPSCC recurrence presented in this study offers a theoretical foundation and strategic direction for the future development of novel immunotherapeutic approaches targeting this refractory malignancy, thus informing early intervention and precision treatment for HPSCC recurrence.

Methods

Human specimens and ethical approval

This study enrolled six patients diagnosed with HPSCC and three patients with R-HPSCC following prior treatment. All patients were recruited from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). From the six treatment-naïve HPSCC patients, six fresh primary tumor samples and two adjacent normal mucosal tissue samples were collected. In addition, three fresh recurrent tumor tissue samples were obtained from the three patients who had developed R-HPSCC after undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Patient demographic and clinicopathological variables, including age, sex, tumor stage, and grade, is provided in Tables S1–2.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions

Both tumor and corresponding adjacent tissues were cut into fragments smaller than 1 mm3 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) containing with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Enzymatic dissociation was carried out with the MACS Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) at 37 °C under continuous rotation for approximately 30 min. The digested mixture was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer (BD Falcon, USA) to obtain a single-cell suspension. Cells were then centrifuged at 330 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by red blood cell lysis using a buffer (Solarbio, China) on ice for 15 min. Following two PBS washes, cells were resuspended in PBS-based sorting buffer containing 2% FBS. A 10 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was taken for counting using a hemocytometer under an inverted microscope, and cell viability was assessed by the 0.1% trypan blue exclusion assay.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and read processing

ScRNA-seq libraries were constructed from the prepared cell suspensions following the manufacturer’s protocol for the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (10 × Genomics). This process involved generating barcoded Gel Beads-in-Emulsion (GEMs) for reverse transcription. After breaking the GEMs, the cDNA was purified, fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and adaptor-ligated, Following polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, libraries underwent paired-end sequencing (150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The raw sequencing output was processed using CellRanger (version 5.0.1), yielding gene–barcode matrices that were used for downstream analyses (Table S3).

Preprocessing and batch correction of scRNA-seq data

Quality control (QC) metrics were computed for each sample using the Seurat R package (v4.3.0) [22]. Cells were retained if they met the following criteria: (1) number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA) within the 5th–95th percentile range; (2) total UMI counts (nCount_RNA) within the 5th–95th percentile range; (3) mitochondrial gene percentage below 20%. Genes detected in fewer than 0.1% of cells were excluded. Filtering criteria were determined on the basis of sample-specific QC metric distributions.

DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) [23] was applied to identify and remove putative doublets, using a pN parameter of 0.25 and an expected doublet frequency of 8‰. Cell cycle phases, including G1, S, and G2/M, were inferred using the Seurat “CellCycleScoring” algorithm together with the “cc.genes.updated.2019” reference gene set.

Gene expression counts were log-normalized with a scale factor of 10,000, followed by scaling with regression of mitochondrial gene percentages and cell cycle scores to minimize technical variability. Highly variable genes (2000) were selected using the variance-stabilizing transformation method. Finally, donor-specific batch effects were corrected using Harmony integration.

Dimensionality reduction and clustering

Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was applied to the top 15 Harmony-corrected principal components, determined by an elbow plot analysis of their standard deviations, to visualize the integrated dataset. To systematically resolve cellular heterogeneity, a two-tiered clustering strategy was employed. First, major cell lineages were identified through low-resolution clustering (resolution = 0.3). Each major lineage was then subjected to high-resolution subclustering (resolution = 0.6) to define finer cellular subtypes and states. Cell type annotation was performed by cross-referencing cluster-specific gene expression patterns with established cell-type markers.

Copy number variants and mapping analysis for cancer cells

To identify large-scale chromosomal copy number variations (CNVs) and distinguish malignant cells, the InferCNVpy package (v1.0.0) was used. A reference set of presumed diploid “normal” cells, including all annotated lymphoid immune cells and fibroblasts from initial clustering, was curated. The raw UMI count matrix was subset to include these reference cells alongside all epithelial cells of uncertain malignant status.

The analysis was performed with the log-transformed expression matrix, with genes ordered according to genomic coordinates (GRCh38). To reduce technical and biological noise, the denoise method was applied. A sliding window of 100 genes was used to smooth the expression signal across the genome, enhancing the detection of broad chromosomal alterations. A hidden Markov model (HMM) was applied to infer discrete CNV states for each cell. CNV scores, representing the degree of genomic instability, were calculated for each cell by InferCNVpy. The resulting CNV score matrix was clustered using the Leiden algorithm to group cells on the basis of shared copy number alteration profiles. Cell clusters exhibiting significantly elevated CNV scores and spatially coherent CNV patterns were annotated as malignant.

Transcription factor regulon analysis

To identify transcription factor (TF) regulons specific to the cell clusters, pySCENIC analysis [24] was performed. The single-cell gene expression matrix was first converted into loom format, and the standard three-step workflow was followed. Gene regulatory networks (GRNs) were inferred, motifs were enriched using human cisTarget databases, and regulon activity was calculated using AUCell. Regulon specificity scores (RSS) were computed for each cluster to identify cell-type-associated regulons. The top regulons, ranked by RSS, were visualized in a heatmap generated with the R package pheatmap.

Pathway analysis

Pathway activity across cell groups was quantified using gene set variation analysis implemented in the GSVA R package (version 1.42.0) [25]. Hallmark gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Pathways with significantly different activity scores were identified using the limma R package.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across cell subpopulations were identified using the Seurat FindMarkers procedure on the basis of the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was then carried out using clusterProfiler (v4.2.2) [26]. Gene identifiers were mapped from gene symbols to ENTREZ IDs on the basis of the org.Hs.eg.db database, followed by adjustment of p-values using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Cell‒cell communication analysis

Intercellular communication among distinct cell types was inferred using the CellChat R package (v1.6.1) [27]. Cell subpopulations were defined and grouped to create CellChat objects. Pre-processing steps were conducted using default parameters, and the “CellChatDB.human” ligand-receptor interaction database was utilized for the analysis. Ligand–receptor interaction and signaling pathway networks were inferred using the computeCommunProb and computeCommunProbPathway functions, respectively.

Cell developmental trajectory

The developmental trajectory of CD8⁺ T cells was reconstructed with the Monocle R package (v2.30.1) [28]. A CellDataSet object was created from raw UMI counts using the negbinomial.size() expression family. Genes used for pseudotime ordering were selected on the basis of cluster-specific DEGs identified by Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function (log2FC ≥ 0.1, adjusted p < 0.01). Cells were subsequently arranged along the inferred trajectory using the DDRTree dimensionality reduction method. Following trajectory reconstruction, genes exhibiting dynamic expression changes over pseudotime were identified using the differentialGeneTest function.

Signature scoring calculation

Signature scores were derived by averaging the expression values of multiple genes after scaling and centering, using Seurat’s “AddModuleScore” function. The signature gene list included cytotoxicity and exhausted scores for CD8+ T cells, M1 and M2 polarization, angiogenesis, phagocytosis, polarization, and inflammatory scores for macrophages, as well as differentiation, activation, antigen presentation, migration, and tolerance scores for dendritic cells. A list of the genes involved is provided in the supplementary materials (Tables S4–6).

Spatial transcriptomic profiling with Visium HD

To explore ST at a higher resolution, the Visium HD Spatial Gene Expression platform was applied. Visium HD enables near single-cell spatial resolution through a densely packed array of contiguous 2 × 2 µm barcoded features [29]. Small HPSCC tissue specimens were processed as formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples in accordance with the Visium HD FFPE Tissue Preparation Handbook (CG000684, 10 × Genomics). RNA integrity was evaluated by determining the proportion of RNA fragments exceeding 200 nucleotides (DV200) using RNA isolated from tissue sections. Only samples with a DV200 > 30% were deemed suitable for downstream processing. Subsequent steps included probe hybridization, ligation, preparation of Visium HD slides, probe release and extension, and finally, probe-based library construction. To minimize potential batch effects, one tissue section from each P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC case was co-embedded on the same Visium HD chip for processing. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus-25B system using a paired-end read strategy.

Spatial transcriptomics processing with Seurat, Banksy, and RCTD

Visium HD sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the Space Ranger pipeline (v4.0.1). Gene-barcode matrices generated by Space Ranger were imported into R (v4.4.0) for downstream analyses using the Seurat package (v5.1.0) [30]. The pipeline produced data at the native 2 μm resolution, along with binned aggregations at 8 μm, 16 μm, and 32 μm resolutions. After evaluation, the 16 × 16 μm bin size was selected for subsequent analysis to optimize the balance between cellular resolution and transcript counts per bin.

For each sample, the gene-barcode matrix underwent processing through a Seurat-based analysis workflow to normalize and stabilize variance. To enable integrated dimensional reduction and clustering across multiple samples within the spatial context, the Banksy algorithm (k_geom = 50) was applied within Seurat’s spatial framework. The top 1000 highly variable genes were used to compute Harmony-corrected PCA embeddings, which were subsequently employed to generate a UMAP visualization on the basis of the top 10 principal components. Unsupervised clustering was performed using a graph-based approach on these components, and cluster-specific marker genes were identified using the FindAllMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). DEGs were defined by an absolute log2 fold change greater than 0.5 and an adjusted p-value below 0.05.

Cell type annotation was performed using matched scRNA-seq data, rather than relying on public scRNA-seq references. To reduce potential batch effects and biological discrepancies associated with external reference datasets, a reference-based deconvolution was performed using robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) [31]. Specifically, scRNA-seq profiles from primary and recurrent tumors were mapped onto their corresponding spatial sections. The final cell type annotations were determined by integrating evidence from the unsupervised Banksy/Seurat clustering results with the RCTD-predicted cell type proportions.

Establishment of the IGF2BP2-overexpressing FaDu cell line and molecule treatment

FaDu cells, a human hypopharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma cell line, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Cat. #HTB-43). Lentiviral particles were employed to establish stable IGF2BP2-overexpressing FaDu cell lines. FaDu cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The following day, the medium was exchanged for 1 mL of fresh culture medium supplemented with lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 and 5 μg/mL polybrene (Cat. #58-58-2, Solarbio, China); 24 h later, the viral supernatant was removed and replaced with complete medium, and 72 h after transduction, cells were subjected to puromycin selection (2 μg/mL) for approximately 1 week to generate stable cell populations. Overexpression efficiency was verified by fluorescence microscopy, quantitative PCR (qPCR), and Western blot.

To suppress SMAD3 phosphorylation, FaDu cells overexpressing IGF2BP2 were treated with the selective SMAD3 inhibitor SIS3 (0.5 μM; Cat. #A170854, Ambeed, China). To block the interaction between IGF2BP2 and its downstream molecules, FaDu cells were treated with CWI1-2 (0.5 μM). Under both treatment conditions, cells were plated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and incubated with the indicated compounds for 24 h.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Cat. #9109, Takara, Japan), and RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically. Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Hifair® Ⅱ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat. #11119ES60, Yeasen, China). qPCR was subsequently carried out with Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat. #11202ES08, Yeasen, China). Each reaction contained SYBR Green Master Mix (1 ×), 0.2 μM of each primer, and 2 μL of diluted cDNA. Amplification was performed under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Table S7.

mRNA stability assay

FaDu cells were plated in 6-well plates and allowed to grow for 24 h to reach approximately 50% confluence. Cells were subsequently exposed to actinomycin D (5 μg/mL; Cat. #HY-17559, MCE, USA) and collected at 0, 3, and 6 h after treatment. Total RNA was extracted for subsequent qRT-PCR analysis, and mRNA expression levels at each timepoint were quantified and normalized to GAPDH. The mRNA degradation rate was estimated following previously published protocols [32].

Western blot

Total cellular proteins were extracted using a commercial lysis buffer for Western blot and immunoprecipitation (Cat. #P0013, Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Cat. #B6169, Uelandy, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Biosharp, China) and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system (Saizhi, China). Antibody information is provided in Table S8.

RNA immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (RIP-qPCR)

RIP-qPCR was conducted to examine RNA–protein interactions. Approximately 107 cells were lysed in IP lysis buffer containing protease and RNase inhibitors. After DNase I treatment to remove genomic DNA, the cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with IGF2BP2 (Cat. #11601-1-AP, Proteintech, China) or control immunoglobulin (Ig)G (Cat. #AC005, ABclonal, China). Immune complexes were captured using pre-balanced Protein A/G magnetic beads (Cat. #88804, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and thoroughly washed. Co-precipitated RNAs, along with input controls, were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Cat. #9109, Takara, Japan) and purified via ethanol precipitation. Reverse transcription was carried out using a commercial first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Cat. #11119ES60, Yeasen, China). qPCR was subsequently performed with a SYBR Green Master Mix (Cat. #11202ES08, Yeasen, China) on a QuantStudio real-time PCR platform. Standard amplification conditions were applied, followed by melting curve analysis to verify product specificity. The relative abundance of SMAD3 mRNA in immunoprecipitated fractions was determined using the percent-input method, by comparing Ct values between IP and corresponding input samples and correcting for the input dilution factor (1/10).

Wound healing assay

Cell migratory capacity was evaluated by a scratch-based wound healing assay. Cells were cultured in 6-well plates until a confluent monolayer was formed, after which a linear wound was introduced using a sterile pipette tip. Detached cells were removed by gentle washing, and serum-free medium was subsequently applied to minimize proliferation-associated effects. Wound closure was documented at the indicated timepoints, and migratory activity was quantified by calculating the relative change in wound width using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Transwell invasion assay

Cell invasive capacity was examined using Transwell chambers (8-μm pore size) pre-coated with Matrigel. Approximately 1 × 105 cells were placed into the upper compartment under serum-deprived conditions, whereas complete medium containing the corresponding stimulants was added to the lower chamber. Following incubation, cells that penetrated the Matrigel barrier and reached the underside of the membrane were fixed and stained, and subsequently quantified by microscopic analysis across randomly selected fields.

Tumorsphere formation assay

Single-cell suspensions were prepared in stem cell culture medium (DMEM/F12, Cat. #PM150312, Procell, China) supplemented with 1 × B27, 20 ng/mL bFGF (Cat. #HY-P5321, MCE, USA), and 20 ng/mL EGF (Cat. #HY-P7109, MCE, USA). Cells were plated in ultra-low attachment 6-well plates at an initial density of approximately 1 × 104 cells per well and maintained for about 10 days, during which the culture medium was refreshed at 2–3-day intervals. Tumorsphere formation and growth were monitored and imaged under a microscope.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation qPCR (MeRIP-qPCR)

FaDu cells were treated with the small-molecule compound CWI1-2, which binds to IGF2BP2 and inhibits its interaction with m6A-modified target mRNAs. The m6A modification level of SMAD3 mRNA was then assessed by MeRIP-qPCR to investigate the effect of IGF2BP2 on m6A RNA methylation of SMAD3. The control group was treated with an equal volume of vehicle. Cells were established in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and stabilized overnight. Treatment was initiated by adding CWI1-2 at a final concentration of 0.5 μM for 24 h, while control cultures received the corresponding vehicle.

Total RNA was isolated from FaDu cells using TRIzol reagent. A 100 μg RNA sample was subjected to immunoprecipitation with an anti-m6A antibody (Cat. #61496, Proteintech, China) or an anti-IgG control immobilized on Protein A/G magnetic beads in the presence of RNase and protease inhibitors. Following collection of the bead-associated RNA, samples were purified and eluted according to standard procedures. The resulting RNA was subsequently reverse-transcribed and analyzed by qPCR to assess m6A enrichment.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining and multiplex IHC

Paraffin-embedded tumor sections were processed for IHC. After removal of paraffin and rehydration, antigen retrieval was achieved by heat treatment in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Sections were then allowed to cool and incubated with primary antibodies (Table S8) at 4 °C overnight. Signal detection was performed using a goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG-HRP polymer (Cat. #PK10006, Proteintech, China) for 1 h at 25 °C, followed by visualization with a diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogenic substrate. Slides were subsequently counterstained, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted according to standard histological procedures. To quantify target protein expression, the percentage of positively stained cells and staining intensity was assessed in five randomly selected fields at 400 × magnification. Cell counting and scoring were performed by two independent pathologists according to established protocols [33].

Multiplex IHC (mIHC) was performed using a commercial kit (Absin, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequential rounds of staining were carried out with the primary antibodies listed in Table S8. After each round of staining, the signal was developed, and the antibody was eluted before the next cycle. Images from multiplex immunofluorescence staining were generated using a TissueFAXS Spectra system (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria).

Xenograft tumor model and treatment

To evaluate the effect of FN1 blockade on rHPSCC in vivo, a human rHPSCC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model was established following previously published protocols from our group [34]. Mice were randomly assigned to either an FN1 inhibitor group or a control peptide group (n = 4 per group). The FN1 inhibitor group received intraperitoneal injections of H-Arg-Gly-Asp-Ser-OH (RGDS, 5 mg/kg; Cat. #A107716, Ambeed, China), while the control peptide group received Arg-Gly-Glu-Ser (RGES, 5 mg/kg; Cat. #A745704, Ambeed, China). Treatments were administered three times per week for 3 weeks, during which tumor volume and body weight were monitored every other day. Tumor tissues were subjected to IHC staining for CD44, Ki67, vimentin (VIM), and E-cadherin.

The cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model was generated by subcutaneously injecting exponentially growing FaDu cells transduced with lentiviral empty vector (oeNC) or IGF2BP2-overexpressing construct (oeIGF2BP2) into the right dorsal flank of BALB/c nude mice (1 × 107 cells/mL). In the two oeIGF2BP2 groups (n = 4 per group), mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of IgG (5 mg/kg) or SIS3 (5 mg/kg). Tumor size was measured every 3 days, and tumors were harvested on day 27. IHC staining was performed to assess the expression of p-SMAD3, CD44, VIM, and E-cadherin.

Tumor size was assessed by caliper-based measurements of length and width, and tumor volume was estimated according to the formula: VT = (L × W2)/2. At experimental endpoints, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised for measurement and subsequent analyses, including IHC staining. Details of the antibodies used are listed in Table S8.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical evaluation was conducted using GraphPad Prism, and comparisons between two groups were performed with an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).

Results

Distinct single-cell transcriptomic landscapes between P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC

Following the overall workflow (Fig. 1A), scRNA-seq profiles were generated from six treatment-naïve primary patients and three recurrent patients, comprising six P-HPSCC tissues, two matched adjacent mucosal (AM) tissues, and three R-HPSCC tissues. After standard data processing and QC, a total of 89,476 cell transcriptomes were obtained, including 44,019 cells from primary lesions, 16,790 cells from adjacent normal mucosa, and 28,667 cells from recurrent lesions (Fig. S1A).

On the basis of the expression of canonical marker genes, eight major cell types were identified: epithelial cells (EpCs, n = 36,968), T & NKT cells (n = 18,246), neutrophils (n = 9559), fibroblasts (n = 7,799), myeloid cells (n = 7613), B cells (n = 5597), endothelial cells (n = 2665), and mast cells (n = 1029) (Figs. 1B, S1B–D). The accuracy of cell type identification was confirmed by DEGs and marker gene expression (Figs. 1C, S1B, D). While all identified cell types were present across patients and samples, their proportions varied significantly, highlighting inter-tumoral molecular heterogeneity and a complex ecosystem in HPSCC (Figs. 1D, E, S2).

Heterogeneity of malignant EpCs in P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC

The CNV score of each cell was calculated using InferCNVpy. EpCs were grouped on the basis of shared copy number alterations, and the resulting CNV score matrix was clustered using the Leiden algorithm (Fig. S3A, B). EpCs with significantly elevated CNV scores and spatially consistent CNV patterns were identified as malignant EpCs (maEpCs) (Fig. S3C). CNV score analysis revealed that maEpCs from the R-HPSCC group exhibited higher CNV scores, indicating a greater degree of genomic instability (Fig. S3D). Dimensionality reduction and clustering of maEpCs revealed distinct segregation patterns across P-HPSCC, R-HPSCC, and AM tissues, while maEpCs within the same sample category further formed multiple subclusters, indicating marked transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 2A).

To explore the molecular and biological differences between tumor cells in P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC, DEG and pathway enrichment analyses were performed on maEpCs from both groups. The results showed that R-HPSCC maEpCs exhibited high expression of multiple stemness-related genes, including CD44, KLF4, IQGAP1, YAP1, and ALCAM, suggesting enhanced stem-like characteristics. Additionally, several immune suppression-related genes, such as NECTIN2, MIF, GPNMB, CXCL1, and CD59, were significantly upregulated in R-HPSCC maEpCs, while the expression of antigen presentation-related genes (e.g., HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C) was markedly reduced (Figs. 2B, C, S3E). Notably, R-HPSCC maEpCs showed high expression of “don’t eat me” signal-related genes, including CD47 and CD24 (Fig. 2B, C). These molecules bind to SIRPα and Siglec-10 on macrophages, respectively, mediating evasion of phagocytosis and facilitating immune evasion [35]. GSVA revealed that R-HPSCC maEpCs were significantly enriched in hallmark pathways related to cancer progression and malignant transformation, such as UV_response_DN and EMT, angiogenesis-associated pathways such as hypoxia and angiogenesis, and pathways involved in tumor immunosuppression and stemness, including TGF-β, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)/nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), and interleukin (IL)6/Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK-STAT3) signaling (Fig. 2D). Additionally, mapping of maEpCs to HPSCC tumor cell subsets previously defined in our earlier study [18] revealed that tumor cell subsets with stemness and EMT characteristics, such as immortal cancer and metastasis cancer − 1, were predominantly enriched in R-HPSCC maEpCs (Fig. S3F–H). These results suggest that R-HPSCC maEpCs possess enhanced EMT features, immune evasion capacity, and stem-cell-like properties, which may drive tumor recurrence and progression through mechanisms such as invasion, metastasis, immune evasion, and maintenance of stemness. In contrast, P-HPSCC maEpCs displayed distinct molecular characteristics, with significant enrichment in pathways related to cell proliferation, including E2F_targets, MYC_targets_V1/2, and G2M_checkpoint, as well as metabolic pathways such as fatty acid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. These results suggest that active cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming are key features of the primary tumor (Fig. 2D).

Subsequently, pySCENIC analysis was performed to identify key TFs regulating the phenotypic differences between primary and recurrent maEpCs. The results revealed aberrant activation of SMAD3 in R-HPSCC maEpCs (Fig. 2E). As a critical downstream effector of the TGF-β signaling pathway, SMAD3 has been shown to promote malignant progression in various cancers by maintaining the self-renewal capacity of cancer stem cells and inducing EMT in tumor cells [36–38]. Previous studies also indicated that m6A RNA methylation can regulate the expression and stability of SMAD3 mRNA in cancer cells [39]. Interestingly, during DEG analysis, the m6A “reader” protein gene IGF2BP2 was specifically upregulated in R-HPSCC maEpCs (Fig. 2B), suggesting that IGF2BP2 may contribute to tumorigenesis and progression in R-HPSCC maEpCs by regulating SMAD3. Correlation analysis of gene expression in maEpCs revealed a strong association between IGF2BP2 and SMAD3, supporting this hypothesis (Fig. 2F). More compellingly, coordinated expression of IGF2BP2, SMAD3, and CD44 was observed across maEpC subsets, with the three molecules tending to be synchronously upregulated or downregulated within the same subgroup, suggesting their co-localization in a shared functional phenotype (Fig. S3I). On the basis of these findings, RIP-qPCR was performed in FaDu cells. RIP-qPCR analysis showed a significant enrichment of SMAD3 mRNA in anti-IGF2BP2 immunoprecipitates compared with IgG controls, providing evidence for a direct interaction between IGF2BP2 and SMAD3 mRNA (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, a FaDu cell line with stable IGF2BP2 overexpression was established (Fig. S3J, K). After transcriptional arrest induced by actinomycin D, residual SMAD3 mRNA levels were measured at 0, 3, and 6 h. The results indicated that IGF2BP2 overexpression effectively delayed SMAD3 mRNA degradation compared with the control group (Fig. 2H), demonstrating that IGF2BP2 significantly enhances SMAD3 mRNA stability.

IGF2BP2 promotes tumor progression via m6A-dependent stabilization of SMAD3 mRNA

Upregulation of IGF2BP2 markedly enhanced the migratory and invasive behavior of FaDu cells, as demonstrated by wound healing and Transwell assays (Fig. 3A, B). In parallel, IGF2BP2 overexpression induced coordinated changes in EMT marker expression, with increased N-cadherin and vimentin levels and reduced E-cadherin expression (Fig. 3C). Beyond its effects on cell motility, IGF2BP2 overexpression also promoted tumor cell stemness, as evidenced by increased sphere-forming capacity and elevated expression of stemness-associated proteins (Fig. 3D, E). Importantly, treatment with SIS3 substantially attenuated both the EMT-associated phenotypes and the stemness-promoting effects induced by IGF2BP2, indicating that these functional alterations are dependent on SMAD3 phosphorylation. Further Western blot analysis revealed that, compared with the control group, phosphorylated SMAD3 (p-SMAD3) and TGFB1 protein levels were significantly elevated in IGF2BP2-overexpressing FaDu cells (Fig. 3F). In contrast, SIS3 treatment strongly inhibited IGF2BP2-induced SMAD3 phosphorylation and reduced TGFB1 production. These results suggest that IGF2BP2 induces EMT and maintains stemness characteristics in HPSCC cells via SMAD3 phosphorylation, highlighting the critical role of the IGF2BP2-SMAD3 axis in tumor recurrence and metastasis.

To investigate whether IGF2BP2 promotes tumor progression through SMAD3 activation in vivo, a CDX model was established using FaDu cells with IGF2BP2 overexpression or corresponding controls. Mice bearing oeIGF2BP2 tumors were treated with either IgG or the SMAD3 phosphorylation inhibitor SIS3. Tumors derived from oeIGF2BP2 cells exhibited significantly accelerated growth compared with the oeNC group, whereas pharmacological inhibition of SMAD3 phosphorylation by SIS3 markedly attenuated tumor growth and reduced final tumor burden (Fig. 3G). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that oeIGF2BP2 tumors displayed elevated levels of p-SMAD3, CD44, and vimentin, while E-cadherin expression showed no obvious change. Notably, SIS3 treatment markedly decreased the expression of p-SMAD3, CD44, and vimentin (Fig. 3H). These results demonstrate that IGF2BP2 promotes tumor growth and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in vivo in a SMAD3 phosphorylation-dependent manner, supporting the functional IGF2BP2–SMAD3 axis in HPSCC progression.

To further investigate the interaction between IGF2BP2 and SMAD3, MeRIP-qPCR assays were conducted. The results revealed that IGF2BP2 overexpression significantly elevated the m6A modification level of SMAD3 transcripts in FaDu cells compared with the control group. Conversely, when IGF2BP2 binding to m6A-modified transcripts was disrupted by CWI1-2, the m6A modification level of SMAD3 was substantially reduced (Fig. 3I). These results confirm that IGF2BP2 recognizes SMAD3 through m6A modification, acting as a “reader” to stabilize SMAD3 mRNA, thereby activating the downstream TGF-β signaling pathway and promoting tumor progression.

Enhanced tumor cell–fibroblast communication in recurrent tumors

To investigate and compare the intercellular communication patterns between primary and recurrent maEpCs and various cellular subpopulations within their respective TME, intercellular communication analysis was conducted using the CellChat tool. The results revealed significantly more interactions between maEpCs and other cell types in R-HPSCC compared with P-HPSCC (Fig. S4A). Specifically, signaling from maEpCs to CD8+ T cells was markedly enhanced in R-HPSCC (Fig. S4B), indicating a potential immune evasion mechanism in recurrent tumors. Additionally, crosstalk between maEpCs and fibroblasts was the most prevalent, with R-HPSCC maEpCs receiving more signals from fibroblasts than their primary counterparts (Fig. S4B), suggesting that fibroblasts may contribute to the malignant progression of R-HPSCC. Given the close interplay between fibroblasts and maEpCs, we next characterized fibroblast heterogeneity in HPSCC to delineate their associations with maEpC functions and tumor microenvironment remodeling.

Six distinct fibroblast subsets were defined on the basis of marker gene expression, tissue origin, and functional enrichment characteristics (Figs. 4A–C, S4C). The subset, derived primarily from tumor tissue and marked by high expression of cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) markers such as FAP and metalloproteinases, as well as ECM-related genes such as POSTN, was classified as extracellular matrix CAFs (mCAFs). GO analysis of mCAFs indicated significant enrichment in terms related to extracellular matrix organization and collagen fibril organization, consistent with their defining features. Another subset, identified by elevated expression of stem-cell-related genes including SCRG1 and SOX9, demonstrated functional enrichment in connective tissue and mesenchyme development, and was classified as developmental CAFs (dCAFs). A subset expressing high levels of antigen presentation genes, such as CD74 and HLA-DRA, was enriched in GO terms related to immune response regulation, antigen-receptor-mediated signaling, and antigen processing and presentation via MHC class II, and was thus designated as antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs). The myofibroblastic CAF (myoCAF) subset, characterized by high expression of classical myofibroblast markers such as ACTA2 and genes encoding contractile proteins (TAGLN, MYLK, MYL9, and MYL11), was functionally enriched in muscle system processes, myofibril assembly, and muscle contraction. Another subset, primarily derived from AM tissue, exhibited high expression of elastic fiber differentiation genes such as elastin (ELN), fibulin-1 (FBLN1), and microfibril-associated protein 4 (MFAP4), with GO terms associated with elastic fiber assembly, and was designated as elastic fibroblasts (Elastic Fbs). The final subset, characterized by high expression of pericyte marker genes (e.g., RGS5 and KCNJ8) and angiogenic features (e.g., PDGFA, EGFL6, and GMFG), was defined as pericytes, with functional associations confirmed by GO analysis related to endothelium development, endothelial tube morphogenesis, and endothelial cell migration. Notably, mCAFs were present in both P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC, whereas dCAFs were specifically enriched in R-HPSCC, suggesting a potential role for this subset in HPSCC tumor recurrence (Fig. 4C).

Following the identification of fibroblast subpopulations, a detailed cell–cell communication analysis was conducted between maEpCs and each fibroblast subset in both P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC to dissect the specifics of their signaling interactions. The results revealed stronger communication between maEpCs and dCAFs, mCAFs, and myoCAFs in R-HPSCC compared with P-HPSCC (Figs. 4D, S4D). Visium HD ST sequencing (ST-seq) confirmed that each CAF subset was spatially adjacent to maEpCs in both tumor types. Notably, dCAFs, mCAFs, and myoCAFs formed distinct niches surrounding the leading edge of cancer nests, facilitating frequent signaling interactions (Fig. 4E). Differential and activity analyses of intercellular signaling pathways (Fig. S4E, F) revealed significant differences in both the frequency and intensity of cell–cell communication between maEpCs and each CAF subset between primary and recurrent tumors. Specifically, pathways involving FN1, collagen, and laminin were significantly activated in R-HPSCC, suggesting their critical role in tumor recurrence. Further comparison of ligand-receptor pair signaling differences between maEpCs and each CAF subset in primary versus recurrent tumors revealed that interactions were primarily mediated by FN1, COL6A1/2/6, and COL1A1/2 along with their respective protein receptors (Fig. 4F). In the R-HPSCC group, signaling pairs involving dCAFs, mCAFs, and myoCAFs binding to maEpCs via the FN1 ligand and receptors such as SDC1, SDC4, and CD44 were significantly enhanced compared with P-HPSCC. The FN1-CD44 ligand-receptor pair plays a key role in tumor cell migration and invasion [40, 41]. Additionally, SDC1 and SDC4 are essential for maintaining tumor cell stemness and promoting metastasis [42–44]. To evaluate the in vivo role of FN1 signaling, a human rHPSCC PDX model was established, and mice were treated with either the FN1 inhibitor RGDS or a control peptide, RGES. RGDS treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth and reduced tumor size compared with the control peptide group (Fig. 4G). IHC analysis showed a notable decrease in the expression levels of CD44, Ki-67, and VIM—markers associated with stemness and proliferation—while E-cadherin expression remained unchanged in the RGDS-treated group, indicating enhanced cell adhesion and reduced invasive potential (Fig. 4H). These results emphasize the critical role of FN1 in tumor progression and suggest that targeting FN1 signaling could be a promising therapeutic strategy for R-HPSCC. In conclusion, these results suggest that CAFs interact with maEpCs through FN1 signaling to enhance their stemness and invasive/metastatic capabilities, contributing significantly to HPSCC recurrence and progression. This specific FN1-mediated interaction highlights the pivotal regulatory role of FN1 in maEpC-CAF communication, positioning it as a potential novel molecular target for therapeutic intervention.

CD8+ T cells display distinct states in primary versus recurrent HPSCC

Subclustering analysis of T/NK cells revealed distinct subsets on the basis of functional marker genes (Fig. 5A, B) [45]. CD8+ T cells were classified into CD8+ ISG+ T cells (CD8A, IFIT2, IFIT3), exhausted CD8+ T cells (PDCD1, ENTPD1), and two cytotoxic subsets: cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1 (GZMA, GZMH) and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_2 (CRTAM, TNFSF9). For CD4+ T cells, subsets included naïve CD4+ T cells (IL7R, TCF7) and two Treg subsets: Tregs (FOXP3, IL2RA) and ISG+ Tregs (FOXP3, ISG15, IFI6). Proportional analysis revealed significant differences in the distribution of these subsets among AM, P-HPSCC, and R-HPSCC tissues. Notably, the proportion of exhausted CD8+ T cells was significantly higher in R-HPSCC compared with P-HPSCC (Figs. 5C, S5A). Comparative analysis of exhaustion scores across the four CD8+ T-cell subsets further confirmed that exhausted CD8+ T cells exhibited the most prominent exhaustion phenotype (Fig. 5D), indicating that the TME of R-HPSCC is associated with a more pronounced immunosuppressive state.

Clustering analysis of T cells based on gene expression matrices revealed distinct patterns across tissue sources. T cells from AM and P-HPSCC preferentially clustered together, suggesting shared functional states and molecular characteristics. In contrast, T cells from R-HPSCC exhibited a distinct transcriptional profile, indicating a unique process of immune editing and potentially the activation of immune evasion mechanisms (Fig. 5E). To further investigate immunological differences in the TME between primary and recurrent lesions, differential gene expression analysis of CD8+ T cells from P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC was performed. CD8+ T cells from R-HPSCC showed significant upregulation of core exhaustion-associated TFs, including CBLB, ICOS, NR4A3, TOX, and FOXP1, along with exhaustion-related genes such as ENTPD1, TNFRSF9, CTLA4, and CXCL13 (Figs. 5F, S5B). Conversely, cytotoxic effector genes (GZMH, GZMM, GZMB, and NKG7) were significantly downregulated in R-HPSCC CD8+ T cells (Fig. 5F). These results indicate profound impairment of CD8+ T-cell function in the R-HPSCC TME. Subsequently, exhaustion-related gene expression and exhaustion scores across distinct CD8+ T-cell subsets from different tissue sources were evaluated. Compared with AM, exhaustion scores of CD8+ ISG+ T cells and exhausted CD8+ T cells were significantly elevated in P-HPSCC (Fig. 5G). In R-HPSCC, both cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1/2, exhausted CD8+ T cells, and CD8+ ISG+ T cells exhibited higher exhaustion scores than those in AM. Furthermore, exhaustion scores for cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1/2 and exhausted CD8+ T cells were significantly higher in R-HPSCC than in P-HPSCC, indicating a more pronounced exhausted phenotype (Fig. 5G). Regarding exhaustion-associated genes, PDCD1 was expressed similarly in CD8+ T cells from both groups without significant differences, while CTLA4 and TIGIT were aberrantly upregulated in R-HPSCC (Fig. S5B). Notably, high expression of LAG3 and TIGIT was observed in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1/2 in both groups. In R-HPSCC, TOX expression in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1/2 and CTLA4 in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1 was significantly elevated compared with P-HPSCC (Figs. 5H, S5C). In the exhausted CD8+ T-cell subset, exhaustion markers were broadly elevated in both groups, with TIGIT, TOX, and ENTPD1 showing substantially higher levels in R-HPSCC compared with P-HPSCC (Figs. 5H, S5C). These results suggest that CD8+ T-cell exhaustion in P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC is linked to distinct inhibitory molecular programs. The pronounced upregulation of CTLA4 in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells from R-HPSCC may represent an early molecular event marking the initiation of CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, highlighting the critical role of CTLA4 in shaping the exhausted state of CD8+ T cells in the recurrent TME.

Monocle was utilized to infer the immune states and transitional trajectories of CD8+ T cells. CD8+ ISG+ T cells were primarily localized to the early trajectory, while exhausted CD8+ T cells and most cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_2 occupied distinct terminal states characterized by high exhaustion and cytotoxicity scores, respectively (Fig. 6A, B). Notably, when analyzed separately, CD8+ T cells from R-HPSCC predominantly localized to the terminal states, whereas those from P-HPSCC were more enriched in early and intermediate transitional states (Fig. 6C). Trajectory analysis divided CD8+ T cells into seven distinct states, with states 1 and 6 at opposite terminal ends. State 1, primarily composed of exhausted CD8+ T cells, was mostly derived from R-HPSCC, while states 5–7 mainly consisted of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1/2, with the highest contribution from P-HPSCC-derived CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6D). These results suggest that CD8+ T cells in R-HPSCC are predominantly in terminal exhausted states, whereas those in P-HPSCC are more frequently found in earlier and cytotoxic stages.

By analyzing transcriptomic dynamics along pseudotime, CD8+ T cells were categorized into four distinct differentiation stages (Fig. 6E). CD8+ ISG+ T cells were mainly enriched in stage 1, characterized by elevated expression of cytotoxicity-related gene GNLY and interferon-stimulated genes such as ISG20, IFITM1/2/3, and IFIT1/2/3, indicating an adaptive immune response phenotype. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of Toll-like receptor and interferon-γ response pathways at this stage. Stage 2 exhibited high expression of classical cytotoxic markers (GZMH, GZMK, and NKG7), with substantial enrichment of functions related to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and cell killing, signifying a robust cytotoxic phenotype. Stage 3 represented the onset of exhaustion, marked by upregulation of TIGIT, CTLA4, ICOS, and NR4A3, with pathways enriched in the negative regulation of lymphocyte activation and tolerance induction. Stage 4 corresponded to terminal exhaustion, characterized by increased expression of exhaustion-associated TFs (TOX, FOXP1, SOX4) and exhaustion-associated markers (LAYN, ENTPD1, CXCL13, IL2RA, TNFRSF18), where exhausted CD8+ T cells were predominantly localized. Tissue mapping indicated that AM-derived CD8+ T cells were concentrated in stage 1, P-HPSCC cells in stages 2–3, and R-HPSCC cells almost exclusively in stage 4, further confirming that most CD8+ T cells in R-HPSCC reside in an exhausted state. Analysis of TF expression along the pseudotime trajectory revealed progressive upregulation of exhaustion-related TFs, including TOX, FOXP1, NR4A1, HIVEP1, NFKB1, and IKZF4, particularly in CD8+ T cells from R-HPSCC. This suggests that the TME of R-HPSCC may promote CD8+ T-cell exhaustion through the activation of key exhaustion-related TF networks. Notably, immune checkpoint molecules were initiated earlier in CD8+ T cells from R-HPSCC than in those from P-HPSCC, with CTLA4 showing the most prominent upregulation at the intermediate stage (Fig. 6G). This was consistent with its increased expression in cytotoxic CD8+ T cells_1 from R-HPSCC. Additionally, mIHC confirmed a significant enrichment of CD8+ CTLA4+ T cells in R-HPSCC compared with P-HPSCC, with a notably higher cell count observed in recurrent tumors (Fig. 6H).

Our findings reveal significant heterogeneity in the evolution of CD8+ T-cell exhaustion between P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC. In contrast to P-HPSCC, CD8+ T cells in R-HPSCC exhibit a markedly more pronounced exhausted phenotype, characterized by an early onset immunosuppressive program linked to CTLA4. These results provide valuable molecular evidence for the immune evasion mechanisms driving tumor recurrence and emphasize the importance of considering recurrent disease as a distinct immunological state. Furthermore, CTLA4 emerges as a potential immunotherapeutic target for R-HPSCC.

Dysregulated myeloid cell function characterizes recurrent HPSCC

Unsupervised clustering of myeloid cells revealed nine distinct subpopulations on the basis of consensus functional markers, including monocytes, four subsets of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) (NLRP3+ TAMs, SPP1+ TAMs, C1QC+ TAMs, and LILRB1+ TAMs), and four dendritic cell subsets (cDC1, cDC2, LAMP3+ DCs, and pDCs) (Figs. 7A, B; S6A). These subsets were detected across different tissue samples; however, the myeloid cell composition varied significantly between tissue types (Fig. 7C). Notably, SPP1+ TAMs were significantly enriched in R-HPSCC (Figs. 7C; S6B). Gene signature scoring revealed that, compared with other TAM subsets, SPP1+ TAMs exhibited notably enhanced angiogenic potential coupled with relatively reduced phagocytosis-related activity, suggesting a critical role in recurrence-associated immunosuppression and tumor remodeling (Fig. 7D). Macrophages are traditionally categorized as proinflammatory M1 or antiinflammatory M2 phenotypes [46]. However, analysis of inflammatory and polarization signatures in TAMs revealed that M1 and M2 features were not mutually exclusive in HPSCC (Fig. 7E). Specifically, both NLRP3+ and SPP1+ TAM subsets displayed characteristics of both M1 and M2 macrophages, reflecting the high phenotypic plasticity of macrophages within the HPSCC TME. Interestingly, while C1QC+ TAMs exhibited the strongest phagocytic potential and prominent proinflammatory features, they also showed relatively high M2 polarization scores. This finding further highlights that TAM phenotypes in the HPSCC TME are more complex than the classical M1/M2 dichotomy, consistent with our previous report [18]. Notably, comparative analysis of TAM polarization across groups revealed that, compared with P-HPSCC, TAMs in R-HPSCC exhibited significantly reduced M1 scores and markedly increased M2 scores (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7F). These results suggest that recurrent tumors may promote the establishment of a more immunosuppressive, M2-like myeloid microenvironment, dampening inflammatory responses and antitumor immunity while fostering a myeloid niche that facilitates immune evasion.

Within the dendritic cell (DC) population, a LAMP3+ DC subset was identified by integrating the expression profiles of signature genes related to maturation (LAMP3, MARCKSL1, IDO1, UBD), activation (CD80, CD83, CD40), migration (CCR7, SLCO5A1, FSCN1), and immunosuppression (CD274, PDCD1LG2, CD200, EBI3, IL4I1, SOCS2), alongside corresponding functional scores (Figs. 7G; S6C, D). This LAMP3+ DC subset, recognized for its immunoregulatory and tolerogenic functions [47, 48], was significantly enriched in both P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC, with further increase observed in R-HPSCC (Figs. 7C; S6B), suggesting a prominent immunomodulatory role of LAMP3+ DCs in the recurrent TME. As professional antigen-presenting cells, DCs initiate adaptive immune responses by presenting tumor antigens to T cells. Quantitative analysis of antigen presentation capacity revealed a significant impairment in DCs from R-HPSCC (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7H), further supporting the role of DC dysfunction in immune evasion during tumor recurrence. In summary, as the disease progresses from AM to P-HPSCC and further to R-HPSCC, myeloid cells gradually remodel toward an immunosuppressive, pro-angiogenic, and antigen-presentation-deficient phenotype, fostering a microenvironment that supports immune evasion and tumor growth during recurrence.

Discussion

HPSCC, though relatively rare, is marked by an aggressive clinical course and a high propensity for early recurrence, which significantly contributes to treatment failure and cancer-related mortality in affected patients [49]. The present study employed an integrated single-cell transcriptomic analysis of P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC, complemented by ST and functional validation experiments, to comprehensively characterize the cellular composition, molecular programs, and intercellular communication networks driving tumor recurrence. Through systematic analysis of maEpCa, fibroblasts, and various immune cell populations, a profound and coordinated remodeling of the TME during HPSCC recurrence was identified. Notably, recurrent tumors displayed convergent features of increased tumor cell stemness, immune evasion, and stromal activation, along with widespread immune dysfunction marked by CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and myeloid cell-mediated immunosuppression. These findings provide mechanistic insights into the biological underpinnings of HPSCC recurrence, highlighting that recurrence represents a distinct pathological and immunological state, rather than merely a continuation of the primary disease. Notably, this study uncovered key molecular regulatory axes and intercellular interaction networks in R-HPSCC, providing a potential theoretical foundation and intervention targets for therapeutic strategies. Given the current lack of effective clinical treatments for R-HPSCC, these discoveries offer a vital basis for the development of precision treatment approaches in the future.

This study presents a comparative analysis of maEpCs from P-HPSCC and R-HPSCC, revealing that the IGF2BP2–SMAD3 axis is selectively activated during HPSCC recurrence. This axis functions as a key tumor-intrinsic regulatory program that drives the maintenance of tumor cell stemness and induces EMT. IGF2BP2, a canonical m6A “reader” protein, has been shown in various tumor types to exert oncogenic effects by stabilizing m6A-modified transcripts of downstream target genes during tumor progression [50]. Yu et al. [51] demonstrated that in HNSCC, IGF2BP2 directly binds to the mRNA of the EMT-related transcription factor Slug, enhancing its stability and thereby promoting EMT, tumor invasion, and lymph node metastasis. Similarly, Ouyang et al. [52] reported that in gastric cancer, IGF2BP2 drives EMT and enhances tumor cell migration and invasion by stabilizing HMGA1 mRNA. Beyond EMT, the role of IGF2BP2 in sustaining cancer stem cell programs has been well documented. For instance, Li et al. [53] confirmed that IGF2BP2 recognizes the SOX2 transcript to stabilize it, promoting self-renewal and stemness in colorectal cancer. In hepatocellular carcinoma and esophageal cancer, IGF2BP2 overexpression was shown to stabilize CDC45 and OCT4 mRNA via m6A modification, promoting tumor cell stemness and exerting tumorigenic effects [54, 55]. However, the specific role of IGF2BP2 and its downstream targets in HPSCC, particularly in the context of tumor recurrence, has not been systematically explored. This study demonstrates that IGF2BP2 is selectively upregulated within tumor cells in R-HPSCC and exhibits a strong transcriptional and functional association with SMAD3, a central effector of the TGF-β signaling pathway. Mechanistically, IGF2BP2 stabilizes SMAD3 transcripts through an m6A-dependent pathway, thereby sustaining SMAD3 activity and reinforcing downstream transcriptional programs related to EMT and tumor cell stemness. Although a recent study by Peng et al. [56] suggested that IGF2BP2 may regulate components of the TGF-β–SMAD signaling pathway through epitranscriptomic modifications, the functional positioning of the IGF2BP2–SMAD axis in tumor recurrence has not been clearly defined. Our findings extend this knowledge by showing that the IGF2BP2–SMAD3 axis not only regulates transcription, but also drives key malignant phenotypes, including tumor cell migration, invasion, and stemness. Notably, pharmacological inhibition of SMAD3 phosphorylation using SIS3 effectively blocked IGF2BP2-driven malignant progression, indicating that this progression depends on the intact SMAD3 signaling cascade. This dependency was validated not only in vitro but also in vivo, where pharmacological inhibition of SMAD3 phosphorylation significantly reduced IGF2BP2-driven tumor growth and reversed associated malignant phenotypes in xenograft models. In the context of tumor recurrence, this IGF2BP2-centered regulatory signal confers a selective advantage to tumor cells by simultaneously enhancing cellular plasticity, maintaining stem-like properties, and facilitating immune evasion. This enables tumor cells to adapt to therapeutic pressure and hostile microenvironmental conditions. The IGF2BP2–SMAD3 regulatory mechanism provides a previously unrecognized layer of regulation within the TGF-β/SMAD signaling pathway in R-HPSCC, offering an explanation for the convergence of stem-like and aggressive phenotypes observed in recurrent tumor cells. In summary, this study highlights the IGF2BP2–SMAD3 axis as a critical, mechanistically coherent, and potentially targetable regulatory node in R-HPSCC, unveiling the essential role of m6A-dependent post-transcriptional regulation in tumor cell state transitions and disease recurrence.

Our previous work demonstrated that mCAFs play a pivotal role in driving malignant progression in P-HPSCC [18]. Our data further highlight that, beyond intrinsic tumor-regulatory programs, signals derived from CAFs in the stroma serve as a critical extrinsic driver of HPSCC recurrence. Integrated single-cell and ST analyses reveal that R-HPSCC is marked by selective enrichment of dCAFs, accompanied by a widespread intensification of communication between CAFs and maEpCs. Bartoschek et al. [57] first proposed that dCAFs in breast cancer represent a CAF subpopulation with transcriptional characteristics related to development, suggesting they may originate from tumor cells undergoing EMT and play a pivotal role in maintaining the niche of malignant stem cells. However, the specific functions of this CAF subset during tumor initiation and progression remain inadequately explored. In the context of R-HPSCC, the enrichment of dCAFs suggests their involvement in shaping a stromal niche conducive to tumor cell survival and evolutionary adaptation. The functional role and formation mechanisms of dCAFs in tumor recurrence warrant further investigation. At the spatial level, CAFs co-localize with maEpCs at the invasive tumor front, creating a structural reorganization that establishes a pro-tumorigenic niche. This niche enables sustained bidirectional signaling between tumor cells and the surrounding stroma, reinforcing malignant phenotypes associated with recurrence. At the molecular level, this study identified FN1-mediated signaling as the dominant communication axis linking CAFs and maEpCs in R-HPSCC. Compared with primary tumors, recurrent lesions exhibit significantly elevated FN1 expression across multiple CAF subsets, which engages tumor cells through CD44 and integrin receptors, propagating downstream signaling pathways. The FN1–CD44 axis is closely associated with cell adhesion, migration, and stemness maintenance in various cancers [40, 41]. More recently, single-cell analyses of skull base chordoma have identified FN1 as a recurrence-associated extracellular matrix component that facilitates tumor invasion and regeneration [9], emphasizing the potential commonality of FN1-driven stromal remodeling in tumor recurrence. Our findings extend this understanding by positioning FN1-mediated signaling as a critical stromal-derived input that reinforces tumor cell plasticity in the recurrent TME via CAF–tumor cell interaction networks. The convergence of FN1–CD44 signaling with stemness-related transcriptional programs in maEpCs suggests that stromal-derived extracellular matrix signals may synergize functionally with tumor-intrinsic regulatory networks, such as the IGF2BP2–SMAD3 axis. Through coordinated tumor–stroma crosstalk, CAFs contribute to the sustained reinforcement of tumor cell states characterized by stemness and invasiveness, providing a favorable microenvironment for immune evasion and therapeutic tolerance, and promoting disease recurrence. Notably, PDX models further support the causal role of FN1 in sustaining tumor growth. Functional experiments confirmed that targeting FN1 significantly inhibits tumor growth and suppresses malignant features, highlighting the critical role of FN1 in tumor recurrence and progression. These findings indicate that CAF-driven FN1 signaling serves as a key extrinsic driver of tumor cell plasticity and recurrence in HPSCC, highlighting its significant potential as a therapeutic target in R-HPSCC.

In addition, the immune microenvironment of R-HPSCC is marked by significant functional impairment. Compared with primary tumors, CD8+ T cells in R-HPSCC exhibit an immunosuppressive phenotype, characterized by reduced cytotoxicity and aberrant activation of immune checkpoints, alongside a marked increase in the infiltration of exhausted CD8+ T cells within the TME. Pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed earlier upregulation of multiple exhaustion-related TFs and genes in CD8+ T cells from R-HPSCC, suggesting the premature onset of this immunosuppressive state. Notably, the dysfunction of CD8+ T cells in R-HPSCC is particularly marked by the early and preferential upregulation of CTLA4, rather than the classical PD-1-dominant exhaustion phenotype. These observations suggest that R-HPSCC is governed by a distinct immunosuppressive mechanism, where CTLA4-mediated inhibitory signaling plays a more central role in limiting CD8+ T cell function, contrasting with the classical terminal exhaustion pathway. The early upregulation of CTLA4 and the contraction of functional CD8+ T-cell subsets suggest that immune evasion in R-HPSCC may occur prior to the onset of classical terminal exhaustion [58]. Clinical data have shown limited response rates and suboptimal overall efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors in patients with recurrent HNSCC [59]. In light of these findings, it is hypothesized that a dual-target therapeutic strategy incorporating CTLA4 inhibition—such as combining a CTLA4 inhibitor with a PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitor—may be more effective in reversing the early established immunosuppressive state in R-HPSCC and restoring antitumor immune responses, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes. Notably, the recently conducted phase III clinical trial CheckMate 651 explored dual immune checkpoint blockade targeting CTLA4 and PD-1 in recurrent/metastatic HNSCC [60]. Although the trial did not meet its primary endpoint of overall survival, the combination regimen demonstrated a favorable safety profile, providing a critical reference for evaluating the potential of dual PD-1/CTLA4 blockade in recurrent HNSCC. On the basis of our findings, R-HPSCC represents a distinct HNSCC subtype characterized by an early established, CTLA4-dominant immunosuppressive phenotype, whose response to combined immune checkpoint blockade warrants further investigation. Additionally, increased NKT cell infiltration was also observed in R-HPSCC. However, owing to the relatively limited number of detected NKT cells, the functional heterogeneity and immunomodulatory properties of this population could not be reliably characterized in the present study. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to further clarify the functional role of NKT cells in R-HPSCC.

In parallel with adaptive immune suppression, R-HPSCC also undergoes extensive reprogramming of myeloid cells, characterized by a functional shift toward an immunosuppressive, tumor-promoting state. Monocytes, macrophages, and DCs in R-HPSCC exhibit transcriptional programs linked to defective antigen presentation, impaired T-cell priming, and elevated expression of immunoregulatory mediators. These myeloid cell populations, rather than supporting effective antitumor immunity, appear to contribute to the establishment of a tolerogenic microenvironment that hinders immune surveillance. Given the capacity of dysfunctional myeloid cells to promote T-cell exhaustion and undermine the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade, these findings suggest that myeloid-driven immunosuppression plays a critical role in reinforcing immune evasion and treatment resistance in R-HPSCC.

This study has several limitations. First, while multiple independent biological samples were included for each disease group, the recurrent tumors were not derived from patient-matched P-HPSCC samples, limiting the ability to directly trace clonal evolution and cellular dynamics during disease recurrence. Second, the relatively small sample sizes of the scRNA-seq and ST-seq datasets may constrain the comprehensive capture of recurrence-associated cellular heterogeneity and spatial interactions. Expanding patient cohorts and incorporating broader spatial sampling in future studies will be essential to refine these observations. Furthermore, in vitro mechanistic validation was primarily conducted using the FaDu cell line, owing to the limited availability of well-characterized HPSCC models. In addition, the current study employed a pharmacological inhibition strategy rather than a genetic loss-of-function approach to investigate the IGF2BP2–SMAD3 axis. In the PDX model, although RGDS treatment effectively suppressed tumor progression, it should be noted that RGDS broadly targets the conserved RGD motif shared by multiple integrin ligands, including osteopontin and vitronectin. Therefore, the observed antitumor effects may partly reflect broader disruption of RGD-dependent integrin signaling networks rather than selective inhibition of FN1 alone. Further studies using more specific genetic perturbation strategies will help to clarify the precise contribution of FN1-mediated signaling in R-HPSCC progression. Finally, although our data suggest that CTLA4-centered immunosuppression constitutes a therapeutic vulnerability, the clinical efficacy of dual immune checkpoint blockade in R-HPSCC requires validation through prospective studies.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that R-HPSCC represents a selectively reassembled tumor ecosystem rather than a mere expansion of residual tumor cells. Under sustained therapeutic and microenvironmental pressures, tumor recurrence appears driven by the preferential retention and reinforcement of cellular programs conferring phenotypic plasticity, ecological adaptability, and immune evasion. By integrating m6A-dependent post-transcriptional regulation, stromal signaling, and immunosuppression into a unified mechanistic framework, this study provides a comprehensive explanation for the pronounced aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance of R-HPSCC, while also uncovering multiple interconnected vulnerabilities with potential therapeutic relevance. These insights contribute to the molecular understanding of HPSCC recurrence and establish a foundation for developing more effective, integrative therapeutic strategies.
