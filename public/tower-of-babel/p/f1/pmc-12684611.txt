Disparities in Access to Vascular Stroke Imaging and Carotid Revascularization: A Population Study
Authors: Harshil Shah, Tina He, Naomi Dyck, Jillian Stang, Dana Nicol, Christiane J. McIntosh, Stephen B. Wilton, Shelagh B. Coutts, Nishita Singh, Michael D. Hill, Aravind Ganesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDecisions on imaging with computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, and ultrasound in stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) may be influenced by factors ranging from location‐based resource considerations to patient characteristics. We investigated disparities in vascular imaging utilization and carotid revascularization over 7 years in a Canadian province (Alberta, population: 4.4 million).MethodsWe used provincial administrative data encompassing patients presenting to hospital or emergency/urgent‐care facilities with TIA or ischemic stroke from April 1, 2016 to March 31, 2023. We related the vascular imaging received to year, age, sex, event diagnosis, comorbidities, distance to a comprehensive stroke center, region, and stroke center type using mixed‐effects logistic regressions. We similarly examined disparities in imaging for recurrent events and in receipt of carotid endarterectomy/stenting.ResultsAmong 47 963 patients (median age, 72, interquartile range, 61‐82, 47.6% female) with stroke/TIA, patients who were female, older, and experienced minor stroke/TIA (versus major stroke) had lower odds of receiving computed tomography angiography or any neurovascular imaging, as did those presenting to nonstroke centers or rural sites (eg, 35.8% rural versus 75.3% urban, adjusted odds ratio [any imaging]:0.56, 95% CI, 0.34–0.94). Odds of receiving vascular imaging increased over time, including computed tomography angiography (2016:49.1% versus 2023:79.9%, adjusted odds ratio per‐year since 2015 [computed tomography angiography], 1.18 [95% CI, 1.16–1.19]). Female sex and absent neurovascular imaging carried lower odds of carotid revascularization (2.4% female versus 4.4% male, adjusted odds ratio, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.52–0.63]).ConclusionsDespite increasing utilization of neurovascular imaging, patients who are female, older, rural, or with minor stroke/TIA remain less likely to receive neurovascular imaging, with expected implications for receiving carotid revascularization. Female patients are less likely to undergo carotid revascularization even after accounting for receipt of imaging.

Up to 20% of stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA) events are attributed to large artery atherosclerotic stenosis of the common or internal carotid arteries, after which there is a high risk of recurrent stroke due to the unstable atherosclerotic plaque. 1 Carotid revascularization procedures, either stenting or endarterectomy, are efficacious in secondary prevention, especially if done urgently. 2 Vascular imaging of the head and neck plays a crucial role in establishing the presence and extent of contributory extracranial or intracranial vessel disease (most commonly atherosclerosis), thereby informing decisions on carotid procedures and medical therapies such as antithrombotic regimens and statins. 3

There are 3 noninvasive vascular imaging modalities that are used in stroke imaging: computed tomography angiography (CTA), ultrasound, and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). However, worldwide, there has been a general preference for CTA, 4 perhaps because the lumen diameter is well shown and best approximates digital subtraction angiographic images. Other advantages include relative accessibility and speed of completion in the acute setting, as well as acceptable quality of imaging for key parenchymal and extra/intracranial vascular structures. 5 Although CTAs are often the preferred vascular imaging in stroke, there is variation in use based on the center type and geography (urban or rural). 6

Disparities among regions may also extend to access to subsequent carotid procedures. A Canadian study using discharge data from 1994 to 1997 revealed marked regional variation in carotid endarterectomy rates, attributed at the time to the differing views of indications and inconsistency of published guidelines. 7 As indications and guidelines become consistent and well‐known, regional variations may persist due to resource limitations. 8

In Alberta's publicly funded health care system, patients do not have to pay for physician visits, stroke investigations, or medically indicated procedures such as carotid revascularization. However, patients with financial barriers are known to struggle with balancing competing priorities (eg, wage loss, transportation and parking fees, and outpatient medication costs) when seeking to access or adhere to postacute or secondary prevention care. 9 Other patient characteristics, such as race and sex, are associated with disparities in imaging following stroke/TIA and carotid revascularization. In the United States, Black patients receive diagnostic imaging less often than White patients after ischemic stroke, 10 and female patients are less likely to receive carotid revascularization procedures. 11 However, these prior studies were not population‐based and may involve selection bias and other confounding factors.

We aimed to examine whole population‐level disparities in receipt of vascular stroke imaging and subsequent carotid revascularization based on demographic and geographic factors among all patients presenting with stroke/TIA in the province of Alberta, Canada.

METHODS

All data used for this study are stored in a deidentified database and the programming code is stored as a STATA do‐file. Requests for access to the data or code, accompanied by a proposal of appropriate rigor, will be considered by the corresponding author.

Data Source

This study used population‐level administrative data for Alberta, a Western Canadian province with a population of 4.4 million. 12 We obtained data for all patients with ischemic strokes or TIA from April 1, 2016 to March 31, 2023 (N=47 963). This was derived from the Discharge Abstract Database, maintained by Alberta Health, for patients admitted to the hospital, and the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System, for patients seen at emergency departments or other care centers and were not hospitalized. These databases use standardized definitions of ischemic stroke and TIA or minor stroke (ie, patients presenting with ischemic stroke and who were discharged home directly from the emergency department) based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), which are 92% to 97% accurate. 13 , 14 The use of imaging and imaging type was recorded for each episode of care and enumerated. We used the Canadian Classification of Health Interventions codes to identify all carotid revascularization procedures (including endarterectomy and stenting) or recurrent episodes that occurred until March 31, 2024. Each patient analysis considers their entire episode of care journey through our provincial health care system, including interfacility transfers or presentations to a different facility within days of the original episode. Of course, if a patient was only referred for neurovascular imaging weeks later, this would not be counted within the same episode of care in our administrative data. However, this would not meet the threshold for timely neurovascular imaging when considering the typical timeframe for urgent carotid revascularization.

Analysis

The vascular imaging modality during the index event for each patient received was determined (MRA versus CTA versus ultrasound). CTA/MRA was enumerated if patients received either imaging modality, regardless of whether they also received ultrasound. Next, the type of center (comprehensive, primary, other), distance to closest comprehensive stroke center, and region type (urban or rural) was classified for the 107 hospitals in the province using the 2016 census information, which provides a list of urban and rural communities.

Univariable analyses were done for categorical variables using a χ2 test, with age dichotomized at the median. We also examined the association of the year of stroke with CTA, vascular imaging, and carotid procedure use using logistic regression. Mixed multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the effect size of the association between use of CTA or any vascular imaging for both the index and subsequent events and key demographic and health service factors. The hospital of initial presentation was used as the random effect variable. The fixed variables included year, age, sex, distance to the closest comprehensive stroke center, region type, index event diagnosis, comorbidities (atrial fibrillation, chronic renal failure requiring dialysis, diabetes, heart failure, hypertension, and/or coronary artery disease), and center type (comprehensive stroke center, primary stroke center, or other). A similar regression model was conducted to investigate the effect size of the association between receiving carotid procedures and the abovementioned variables. Finally, this regression was repeated to also adjust for the primary neurovascular imaging modality received (CTA/MRA, ultrasound, or none of the above) for any carotid procedures, carotid stenting, and carotid endarterectomy. For this study, the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting observational studies were used. 15

Ethics Approval

The University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (REB22‐0233) approved this study and waived the need for informed consent for this population analysis.

RESULTS

This study consisted of 47 963 patients (median age: 72 years, interquartile range: 61–82, 22 809 [47.6%] female) with 22 853 (47.6%) presenting with TIA/minor stroke (47.6%) and 25 110 (52.4%) presenting with ischemic strokes (Table 1). Forty thousand one hundred twenty‐three (83.7%) of them presented to urban hospitals, with 17 891 (37.3%) of patients presenting to Comprehensive Stroke Centers (CSCs). Twenty‐nine thousand five hundred sixty‐three (61.7%) of the patients had CTA as their most sophisticated vascular imaging, with 14 922 (31.1%) receiving no vascular imaging.

Four hundred ninety‐four (1.0%) of patients received MRA, of which 262 (53.0%), also had CTA imaging. A total of 1638 (3.4%) patients received carotid revascularization. The median follow‐up time was 57 months.

Over the years, there was a general trend of increased utilization of vascular imaging for patients with stroke/TIA (P het <0.001), with the most sophisticated vascular imaging being CTA more commonly and ultrasound less commonly (Figure 1).

Univariable analyses revealed that female patients were less likely to receive CTA (58.2% of females versus 63.9% male, P het <0.001) or any vascular imaging (66.4% female versus 71.1% male, P het <0.001). Further univariable analyses also showed significant heterogeneity in receiving CTA and vascular imaging across age groups, sex, urban versus rural regions, index event diagnosis, and center type (P het <0.001). These differences generally persisted across all 7 years, particularly for rural versus urban regions (Figure 2). Aside from age, these variables, in addition to primary imaging modality, were also significantly associated with receiving carotid revascularization procedures (P het <0.001). Patients with atrial fibrillation, coronary artery disease, chronic renal failure, diabetes, and hypertension also showed significant heterogeneity in receiving carotid revascularization procedures.

Upon examining the utilization of CTAs using mixed‐effects multivariable logistic regressions, we confirmed that odds of receiving CTA imaging increased over time (49.1% in 2016 versus 79.9% in 2023, adjusted odds ratio [aOR] per year since 2015: 1.18 [95% CI, 1.16–1.19]). Furthermore, patients who were female (58.2% of females versus 63.9% of males, aOR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.77–0.84]) or older than 71 years (57.1% of the older patients versus 65.3% of the younger patients, aOR, 0.63 [95% CI, 0.60–0.67] were less likely to receive CTAs. Those with minor stroke/TIA as the diagnosis also had lower odds of receiving CTA (48.3% in minor stroke/TIA versus 72.9% in ischemic strokes, aOR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.44–0.49]). Patients with chronic renal failure (45.3% versus 62.4%, aOR, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.52–0.62]), dialysis (44.5% versus 61.5%, aOR, 0.34 [95% CI, 0.28–0.40]), heart failure (54.1% versus 62.5%, aOR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.70–0.80]), diabetes (60.4% versus 61.4%, aOR, 0.94 [95% CI, 0.89–0.99]), and coronary artery disease (55.2% versus 61.9%, aOR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.82–0.95]) were less likely to receive CTAs than patients without the condition. Conversely, patients with hypertension (62.8% versus 58.1%, aOR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.23–1.36]) were more likely to receive CTAs than those without it. Finally, center type and region type (ie, urban versus rural) did not show any significant differences in odds of receiving CTA (Table 2). The estimated variance of the random intercept for the presenting hospital site was 0.88 (95% CI, 0.64–1.22). When the receipt of any vascular imaging (regardless of modality) was examined, multivariable regression models revealed that patients with diabetes and coronary artery disease did not have any significant differences in odds of receiving imaging. Furthermore, patients who presented to rural sites (35.8% versus 75.3%, aOR, 0.56 [95% CI, 0.34–0.94]) as opposed to urban sites had lower odds of receiving any vascular imaging, as did those who presented to primary stroke centers (69.1%, aOR, 0.39 [95% CI, 0.10–1.54]) or other stroke centers (51.4%, aOR, 0.11 [95% CI, 0.03–0.41]) versus comprehensive stroke centers (85.4%). The estimated variance of the random intercept for the presenting hospital site was 0.80 (95% CI, 0.57–1.11). Otherwise, all other results were similar to those of receiving CTA (Table 2).

During the follow‐up period (up to March 31, 2024), 6208 patients in our study's cohort had recurrent events. Similar mixed‐effects regression models were created for this population (Table 3). The odds of receiving CTA for recurrent events increased over time (49.7% in 2016 versus 92.5% in 2023, aOR per year since 2015: 1.17 [95% CI, 1.13–1.21]) and any vascular imaging (61.1% in 2016 versus 85.0% in 2023, aOR, 1.10 [95% CI, 1.06–1.14]). Older age (55.0% for older patients versus 64.0% for younger patients, aOR, 0.79, [95% CI, 0.69–0.91]) decreased the odds of CTA but not vascular imaging. Female patients continued to have reduced odds of both CTA (55.2% in females versus 62.3% in males, aOR, 0.70 [95% CI, 0.62–0.80]) and any vascular imaging (64.6% versus 69.8%, aOR, 0.75 [95% CI, 0.65–0.85]). Patients presenting to a rural region (37.7% in rural versus 74.1% in urban, aOR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.24–0.77]) had reduced odds of receiving any vascular imaging. Minor stroke/TIA as index event diagnosis, chronic renal failure, dialysis, and center types not categorized as comprehensive or primary stroke center had significantly reduced odds of receiving either CTA or any vascular imaging for recurrent events. The estimated variance of the random intercept for the presenting hospital site was 0.89 (95% CI, 0.60–1.34) for CTA imaging and 0.79 (95% CI, 0.51–1.20) for any vascular imaging.

Of the 47 963 patients in our cohort, 1638 (3.4%) underwent carotid procedures. Female patients were less likely to receive carotid procedures (2.4% female versus 4.4% male, aOR, 0.57, [95% CI, 0.52–0.63]). There were no significant variations in the odds of receiving carotid procedures based on year, age group, region type, or distance to the closest comprehensive stroke center. Those with atrial fibrillation (2.8% versus 3.6%, aOR, 0.68, [95% CI, 0.60–0.76]) were less likely to have carotid procedures than patients without it. Patients with hypertension (4.2% versus 1.8%, aOR, 2.18 [95% CI, 1.75–2.71]) or coronary artery disease (4.4% versus 3.3%, aOR, 1.32, [95% CI, 1.17–1.50]) had increased odds of receiving carotid procedures. We did note that patients seen in centers not categorized as comprehensive or primary stroke centers (1.6%, aOR, 0.48, [95% CI, 0.35–0.65]) had significantly lower odds of receiving carotid procedures compared with patients from CSCs (5.4%). Patients who had no vascular imaging (1.4%, aOR, 0.39, [95% CI, 0.32–0.49]) or ultrasound (1.0%, aOR, 0.21, [95% CI, 0.10–0.44]) were less likely to receive any carotid procedures than those who received CTA/MRA (4.7%), even after accounting for all the factors above (Table 4). The estimated variance of the random intercept for the presenting hospital site was 0.07 (95% CI, 0.02–0.20). When investigating carotid stenting and carotid endarterectomy separately, we identified that patients have been more likely to receive carotid stenting over the years (5% in 2016 versus 14.1% in 2022, aOR, 1.06, [95% CI, 1.01–1.12]) as well as if they had diabetes (11.1% versus 10.6%, aOR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.05–1.27]). Patients with minor stroke/TIA (4.0% versus 14.1%, aOR, 0.70, [95% CI, 0.55–0.90]) were less likely to receive carotid stenting, as were those who presented to primary stroke centers (5.8%, aOR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.28–0.79]) or other centers (4.1%, aOR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.19–0.37]) as opposed to CSCs (15.6%). Otherwise, results were similar to those observed when all carotid procedures were analyzed together (Table 5). The estimated variance of the random intercept for the presenting hospital site was 0.02 (95% CI, 0.00–0.79) for stenting and 0.16 (95% CI, 0.07–0.37) for carotid endarterectomy.

DISCUSSION

Using population‐level data from an entire Canadian province, we found that 60.7% of the patients with ischemic stroke or minor stroke/TIA received CTA, with increased utilization over the years, ranging from 49.1% in 2016 to 77.9% in 2023. We also note a prominent reduction in the percentage of individuals receiving no vascular imaging, from 40.5% of patients in 2016 to 14.0% in 2023. This highlights the increased uptake of appropriate vascular imaging, possibly due to the increasing use of carotid procedures and better adherence to guidelines.

One of the most concerning findings from this study was the significantly lower odds of female patients receiving CTA or any vascular imaging compared with male patients. Previous studies have reported female patients receiving brain imaging and angiography less frequently than male counterparts. 16 Although certain situations, such as pregnancy, may make clinicians concerned about the fetal risk of radiation exposure, current guidelines highlight that CTA may still be used given the negligible risks of the radiation and there is no known harm caused to the fetus by CT contrast dye. 17 Our analysis also revealed that female patients had lower odds of receiving carotid procedures; this may potentially relate to the concern that female patients have more surgical risks and reduced benefits than male patients. Although a systematic review from the early 2000s did conclude that female patients had higher rates of operative stroke and death when undergoing carotid endarterectomy, multiple other studies have shown no difference in complication rates or benefits. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

We also found that compared with patients presenting to hospitals in urban regions, patients in rural sites were less likely to receive vascular imaging. This may be attributed to the limited diagnostic imaging capabilities and access to specialist care for rural sites 22 ; however, integrated stroke systems of care should strive to ensure that patients initially seen at such sites are still connected to appropriate imaging through other facilities in the system. We also found that patients presenting to centers other than CSCs (present only in 2 large urban centers in our province) were less likely to receive vascular imaging. Furthermore, our findings revealed that the failure to receive vascular imaging was associated with lower odds of carotid procedure and that patients presenting to centers other than CSCs and primary stroke centers had lower odds of receiving carotid procedures even after adjusting for imaging received. When we investigated carotid stenting and carotid endarterectomy separately, the results revealed that patients at non‐CSC centers were less likely to receive carotid stenting but not carotid endarterectomy. This may perhaps relate to the greater relative ease of scheduling patients presenting at our CSCs for stenting due to timely angiography suite availability, compared with challenges in timely operating room availability for endarterectomy, which have been noted in a recent interview study of physicians treating patients with stroke. 4 This may have conferred a greater advantage for patients presenting to CSCs versus non‐CSCs for stenting compared with endarterectomy. Previous literature has reported regional variation in carotid revascularization; however, we did not specifically find any significant disparities between urban versus rural centers after taking into account center type and presenting hospital. 11 , 23

Our analysis also found that older patients were less likely to receive CTA or any vascular imaging. Vascular imaging is pivotal in this demographic, because previous studies have illustrated that carotid procedures in elderly patients are safe and provide significant benefits. 24 , 25 We also found significantly lower odds of receiving CTA or any vascular imaging in patients with index diagnosis of minor stroke/TIA. Although this may be due to the milder and transient nature of these presentations, international stroke guidelines emphasize that all patients with acute disabling or nondisabling stroke, as well as TIAs, should undergo neurovascular imaging to identify carotid stenosis; in the Canadian guidelines, CTA is recommended as the first‐line imaging, although MRA and ultrasound are mentioned as appropriate alternatives. 26 , 27 , 28 Patients with specific comorbidities, namely, chronic renal failure, were less likely to receive CTAs. Although clinicians may be concerned about using intravenous contrast in patients with known renal disease due to the risk of contrast‐induced nephropathy, either ultrasound or MRA may be used instead; however, our results reveal that patients with these comorbidities are less likely to receive any modality of noninvasive vascular imaging. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that imaging‐associated renal complications are rare, and the benefits of prompt imaging often outweigh the risks. 29 , 30 Finally, patients with atrial fibrillation did not have underutilization of neurovascular imaging but did have lower odds of carotid procedures. This likely reflects these patients receiving appropriate neurovascular imaging to determine the cause of the stroke, regardless of the risk factors of an embolic source. Subsequently, the patients may be expected to have lower odds of carotid procedures if the stroke cause is judged by their physicians to be embolic.

There are several limitations to our study. Patient ethnicity or race or both as well as smoking status were not collected in our administrative data. We did not have information about stroke cause or the presence of significant carotid disease on imaging, both of which would influence decisions to pursue revascularization. However, it would, of course, be impossible to know the carotid disease status for the patients who did not receive vascular imaging in the first place, and it was essential for us to include these patients as well in the analysis to understand disparities at a population level. We also did not have data on whether patients were offered carotid procedures or vascular imaging but refused them. We did not specifically quantify stroke severity beyond diagnostic classification into major stroke, minor stroke, or TIA. However, it is essential to note that neurovascular imaging, especially CTA, is pivotal even for TIAs and minor strokes to establish stroke cause and guide management for secondary prevention. 31 Furthermore, this study did not examine the long‐term clinical outcomes that may arise due to disparities in neurovascular imaging or carotid procedures. The study also investigated a timeframe that may have been affected by the pandemic. It is known that during the COVID‐19 pandemic, there were reduced rates of ischemic stroke presentations and higher rates of out‐of‐hospital deaths. 32 This may cause the data set in this study to not capture patients who would have otherwise presented for assessment and treatment in a health facility. Finally, because we examined patients presenting with stroke or TIAs in Alberta, our findings may not be generalizable to other regions with varying health resources and systems; however, as a population study, the results have provided us invaluable insights into disparities in stroke imaging and subsequent carotid procedures. Our results may be most applicable to public health care systems or mixed private/public systems where patients do not have to pay for the neurovascular imaging or carotid revascularization indicated.

In conclusion, we found disparities in the utilization of vascular imaging based on whether the hospital was in an urban or rural region, further modulated by patient characteristics, such as sex, age, and center type, despite increasing utilization over time. We discovered that female patients had significantly lower odds of vascular imaging and carotid procedures. Further studies must investigate the morbidity and mortality end points due to these disparities and analyze contributory factors that may be addressed to improve patient health outcomes.
