Hungry for equity in the Green Mountain State: food insecurity among college students in Vermont
Authors: Trishnee Bhurosy, Jamia Danzy, Lara Scott, Florence Onyeje Abraham, Maxine Cromis, Pindar Yawulda Mbaya, Sophie Lyras, Nicole Reilly, Tammy Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are limited data on food insecurity among college students from different marginalized backgrounds, both in Vermont and other predominantly White spaces, and what their institutions could do to improve food security and other basic needs. Thus, the aims of this study were to 1) examine food insecurity prevalence and its correlates among underrepresented college students, 2) assess their reasons for using campus food pantries and strategies for improving access to culturally appropriate foods on campus, and 3) determine their perspectives regarding how their institutions can address their basic needs.MethodsUsing a mixed methods cross-sectional research design, a convenience sample of full-time students or trainees ages 18 and older enrolled at four institutions of higher education in Vermont, United States, were recruited through flyers, course announcements, newsletters, listservs, and social media posts. An online survey provided estimates of food insecurity, on-campus food pantry use, and participant perspectives on institutional strategies to address basic needs through open-ended questions. Food insecurity was assessed by the 10-item United States Department of Agriculture food security survey module. Descriptive statistics assessed frequencies and percentages along with means and standard deviations. Binary logistic regression was conducted to estimate the odds of food insecurity. Inductive coding was used to analyze qualitative data and identify emergent themes.ResultsAround 40% of all participants experienced food insecurity in the past year, with higher odds observed in students aged ≥ 30, Pell Grant recipients, first-generation students, Black students, and those who used a food pantry. The most cited reason for using a campus food pantry was ease of getting to the pantry while lack of awareness was the most reported barrier. Over 50% of participants recommended gathering feedback from students about their preferences to improve access to culturally appropriate foods. Common themes from qualitative analyses included: food access support, safe and affordable housing access, mental health and healthcare access, financial aid access, reliable and affordable transportation access, non-financial academic support, and general support services.ConclusionsInstitutions should prioritize food assistance programs targeting college students and incorporate mental health care, housing support, and financial aid for comprehensive safety nets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28092-8.

Introduction

Food insecurity, defined as the limited or uncertain access to sufficient nutritious food, is higher among college populations than in United States (US) households, making it a public health priority [1]. In particular, food insecurity estimates among college students ranged from 10% to 75% [2] and have been reported to be associated with high body mass index, poor overall health and diet quality, low academic performance, and fewer days of sleep sufficiency [3, 4]. However, the burden of food insecurity varies across diverse groups of students. For instance, food insecurity rates are as high as 74% among those from marginalized backgrounds [4–6].

Traditionally, the term ‘historically marginalized’ was broadly defined based on race and/or ethnicity [7]. More recently, individuals from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds and geographically underrepresented areas have also been considered [8]. In addition, other groups of students have historically received little attention in terms of understanding their food access needs such as gender minority groups, first-generation students, single parents, low-income students, and international students [9–12]. These historically marginalized students might be more vulnerable to food insecurity due to unique challenges such as limited access to educational resources and social support, discrimination, fear in seeking food assistance because of their legal status or identity, and family rejection [13–16]. Consequently, given the association between food insecurity and low academic performance, food insecurity creates an additional barrier to education, retention, and socioeconomic mobility for students from historically marginalized groups. Addressing the problem of campus food insecurity is thus an important step in reducing socioeconomic disparities in general.

On-campus food pantries are the primary safety nets to address food insecurity among college students, with some student groups more likely to use them than others [17]. In response to the widespread issue of college food insecurity, data from Swipe Out Hunger in 2024 showed that 76% of their institutional partners had a food pantry serving their students. In addition to providing food, some pantries also offered diapers, infant formulas, and assistance with applying for food stamps [18]. While food pantries were originally intended to provide short-term emergency food access to individuals, recent evidence showed that some subsets of students, including racial or ethnic minorities, recipients of student loans, first-generation to college, and international students, were more likely to rely on food pantries long-term than others [17]. Given that diverse college students rely on these food pantries to acquire food short-term or long-term, more research is needed to understand whether foods available from these pantries meet their needs.

While campus food pantries are essential safety nets, they act as a band-aid solution to address hunger and the cultural needs of recipients [17, 19, 20]. Food pantries provide a temporary solution due to multiple factors, including operating on a very tight budget, lack of resources and labor, low awareness of cultural food staples among food pantry staff, heavy reliance on donations, and language barriers [17, 21, 22]. Although it is challenging for food pantries to consider everyone’s preferences, studies have shown that adopting a few strategies can improve overall client satisfaction. In a non-collegiate adult sample, participants shared that provision of multilingual pantry services and recognition of certain cultural celebratory foods, such as Rosca de Reyes, improved pantry reach and utilization [23]. Moreover, individuals who received culturally relevant foods from food pantries were more likely to use the food and reported better mental health [24]. Thus, by expanding food pantry offerings and services through student input, campuses can create more inclusive environments [25].

The present study was conducted in the predominantly White rural state of Vermont for the following reasons. To the best of the authors knowledge, this was the first study to examine food insecurity rates across different institutions of higher education in Vermont. Vermont has seen a steady increase in the number of students from diverse backgrounds in recent years. For example, at the University of Vermont, the percentage of students of color and international students increased from 11.3% in 2014 to 14.5% in 2023 [26]. About 25% of Vermont residents who identified as a gender minority in 2022 were between the ages of 18 and 24 [27]. In addition, 18% of all undergraduates reported being parents and 2,757 college students were single mothers in Vermont in 2021 [28]. Over 3,600 graduate students, about 500 medical students, and about 80 postdoctoral trainees study at the main colleges and institutions of Vermont, with 47% of students being the first in their family to graduate [29, 30]. Vermont’s institutions of higher education are also home to international students from over 60 countries [31–33].

Thus, this study sought to capture how food insecurity affects different groups of college students with varying social, cultural, economic, and nationality backgrounds in a predominantly White rural state. These groups included first-generation college students, Black, Indigenous, and Persons of Color (BIPOC), individuals identifying as a gender minority, Pell Grant recipients, single parents, and international students. In this study, we defined college students as any student or trainee enrolled full-time at a higher education institution in Vermont, including undergrads, graduate students, medical students, postdoctoral trainees, and non-degree students. The aims of the current study were to: (1) examine the prevalence of food insecurity and its correlates among college students in Vermont; (2) assess students’ reasons for using campus food pantries and strategies for improving access to culturally appropriate foods on campus; and (3) explore the perceptions of students regarding strategies that their institutions can adopt to improve their basic needs.

Methods

This study was determined to be exempt from human subjects’ approval by the University of Vermont Institutional Review Board (STUDY00003203). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study through an online form.

Research design, setting, and sample size

This study employed a mixed-methods cross-sectional survey design. A convenience sampling approach was used to recruit college students from the University of Vermont, Vermont State University, Saint Michael’s College, and Champlain College in the state of Vermont, US. The University of Vermont and Vermont State University are public universities while Saint Michael’s College and Champlain College are private, nonprofit colleges. The University of Vermont and Champlain College are located in Burlington, the most urban area of the state with the highest costs of living, with relatively better access to certain culturally relevant food retailers and public transportation. Saint Michael’s College is located in Colchester, a suburban setting with moderate access to diverse food retailers and slightly lower costs of living than Burlington. In contrast, Vermont State University includes multiple campuses at both urban and rural sites of Vermont, where access to diverse foods and community resources may be limited.

Participant recruitment

Participant recruitment across different institutions was possible due to collaborations with multiple stakeholders, including the Offices of the Dean of Students, food pantry leaders, campus ministry services, interfaith leaders, and student-centered groups that focus on improving social justice initiatives. Participants were recruited by announcements through university listservs, flyers posted in different buildings across campuses, newsletters, course announcements, and through university social media accounts. The sample size was calculated from a power analysis involving four factors: a prevalence of food insecurity among minority students of 74% from previous research [34], a desired precision of 5%, a type I error of 0.05, and a recruitment goal of at least 3% from each institution. Given this calculation, our projected sample size was 600, accounting for attrition. The sample size for this current study was 900.

Inclusion criteria for participants were ages 18 and older and being enrolled full-time at one of the four participating institutions of higher education in Vermont. Participants could include undergraduate students, graduate students, medical students, postdoctoral trainees, and non-degree students such as those in certificate programs. Every participant who completed the online survey had the opportunity to enter a drawing to win one of 50 $50 checks.

Data collection

The online survey was conducted via Qualtrics (Provo, UT) and took about 10 min to complete. To ensure survey data quality, captcha was enabled to remove “bots” and duplicate entries were tracked and removed. Data was collected from October 2024 to May 2025.

Survey instrument and measures

The survey was collaboratively developed with community partners from the participating sites and consisted of three sections: sociodemographic characteristics, food security status, and food pantry use and awareness. The survey items were pilot-tested with five students and four community partners who provided feedback on ways to improve the quality of the survey.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sociodemographic questions included age, the name of the institution the participant was affiliated with, student type, gender, race, ethnicity, single parent status (yes or no), first-generation status (yes or no), recipient of Pell Grants (yes or no), and international student status (yes or no). We explored age as a categorical variable: 18–20, 21–23, 24–26, 27–29, and 30 years or above; student type as undergraduate, graduate, medical or postdoctoral trainee, and students in non-degree programs; gender as female, male, gender minority, not disclosed; race as White, Asian, Black, Mixed, American Indian, and other; and ethnicity as Hispanic or Non-Hispanic.

Food security status

The prevalence of food insecurity over the last 12 months was assessed using the 10-item validated United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Adult Food Security Survey Module [35]. The survey module includes questions that assess an individual’s anxiety over food supply, reduced quality and quantity of food consumed, and skipping meals due financial constraints. The number of affirmative responses (e.g., often true, sometimes true, yes) were added to obtain a score of 0 to 10. Students were then grouped into one of four food security categories: high food security (i.e., the household can steadily access enough food they want, defined as having a food security score zero), marginal food security (i.e., food access issues do not significantly impact dietary quality or quantity, defined as having a food security score 1–2), low food security (i.e., reduced diet quality and variety, defined as having a food security score 3–5), or very low food security (i.e., acute or chronic reductions in diet quantity, defined as having a food security score 6–10). The food security status of participants was dichotomized as being food secure (high food security or marginal food security status) and food-insecure (low food security or very low food security status), in line with recommendations by the USDA Economic Research Service [36].

Food pantry use and awareness

The survey also asked participants to report on their usage of campus food pantries in the past year, factors that have influenced them to use campus food pantries, preferences for culturally appropriate foods through campus food pantries and dining halls, and an open-ended question on what additional support or resources from their institution would help them meet their basic needs.

Analyses

SPSS (version 29.0, SPSS Inc, Armonk, NY, 2023) was used to conduct all quantitative data analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to assess frequencies and percentages along with means and standard deviations for sociodemographic characteristics, the prevalence of food insecurity among all participants and by different identities (e.g., food prevalence in BIPOC students), and food pantry usage. Variance Inflation Factors ranged from 1.0 to 1.5, indicating minimal multicollinearity. A binary logistic regression was conducted to estimate the odds of food insecurity. Significant factors were reported with a p-value of less than 0.05.

MAXQDA (version 24.0, VERBI, Germany, 2025) was used to organize and code participants’ responses to the open-ended question on strategies their college or university could implement to address their food security and other basic needs. Following the procedures described by Mayring [37], data were analyzed using inductive coding, i.e., categories that emerged from the data. The data were inductively coded by two researchers independently (FOA, MC). FOA, MC, and TB met to discuss the codes, categories, definitions of categories, and rules for coding text within a category. A coding manual was then developed in consensus by the three researchers to identify the main themes. FOA and MC used the coding manual to categorize the data under each theme. In addition to the inductive category development, the frequency with which each theme was reported by participants was used to determine its relative importance. Our final steps included conceptualizing and understanding the relationships among the themes or concepts that emerged from the data through a visual representation (Fig. 1) [38].

About 45% of participants were females, while 17.2% identified as a gender minority. Almost 23% of the students were first-generation college students, and 22.1% were Pell Grant recipients. Approximately 63% identified as White, while 14.8% identified as BIPOC. In addition, about 5% of participants identified as Hispanic.

Food insecurity rates

About 40% of participants were food insecure. All institutions that participated had high rates of food insecurity (Table 1), with the highest rate observed at Vermont State University (47.1%). Food insecurity rates were 48.3% among medical students or postdoctoral trainees, 47.4% among graduate students, and 38.5% among undergraduate students. Regarding gender, food insecurity rates were the highest among gender minority students (52.2%). Food insecurity rates were high among first-generation (66.2%), single parents (59.5%), and international students (55.0%), with the highest observed among Pell Grant recipients (68.8%). While food insecurity rate was 47.0% among White students, 63.4% of BIPOC students were food insecure, with the highest rates observed among Black students (81.8%). Almost 62% of Hispanic students were food insecure.

Correlates of food insecurity

Table 2 shows the associations between multiple student characteristics and food insecurity. College students who were 30 years or older had higher odds of being food insecure compared to those aged 18 to 20 years old [Odds Ratio (OR) = 1.92; 95% Confidence Interval (CI) = 1.10, 3.37]. Black students had higher odds of being food insecure than White students (OR = 5.25; 95% CI = 1.36, 20.3). Among students who held marginalized identities, first-generation students (OR = 2.14, 95% CI = 1.45, 3.15) and Pell Grant recipients (OR = 2.56, 95% CI = 1.71, 3.82) had higher odds of being food insecure than those who were not. The odds of being food insecure were also higher among those who used a campus food pantry (OR = 3.03, 95% CI = 2.07, 4.45).

Reasons for using or not using a campus food pantry

As shown in Table 3, the most cited reasons for using a campus food pantry were that it was easy to access the pantry (19.1%), its location and hours (15.2%), the quality of food items (10.8%), and having helpful pantry staff or volunteers (10.6%). Being not aware of the existence of a campus food pantry was the most reported reason among respondents for not using a food pantry (25.7%). Other reasons that prevented students from not using a food pantry on campus included not being able to visit the pantry when it was open (20.6%) and stigma associated with using a food pantry (11.9%).

Strategies for improving access to culturally appropriate foods on campus

In terms of strategies for improving access to culturally appropriate foods on campus, over half of participants (53.6%) recommended gathering feedback from students about their food preferences and cultural needs (Table 4). Other approaches mentioned were ensuring food labeling includes cultural information and dietary preferences (38.1%), partnering with cultural student organizations to co-host events (32.8%), hosting food events featuring dishes from different cultures (31.6%), providing recipes or cooking classes that highlight diverse cuisines, and hiring diverse staff who are knowledgeable about cultural staples (29.0%). Participants also stressed the importance of expanding Halal options, Kosher foods, spices and fresh produce such as okra in dining facilities and food pantries.

Institutional strategies to address food security and other basic needs

Seven main themes emerged from the qualitative data: food access support, safe and affordable housing access, mental health and healthcare access, financial aid access, reliable and affordable transportation access, non-financial academic support, and general support services (Fig. 1). These themes are reviewed below in terms of the most frequently mentioned to the least commonly mentioned.

Food access support

Participants shared that they needed fresh produce, including access to iron-rich foods, a variety of options in both dining halls and food pantries, dining halls to be open for longer hours, and coupons to grocery stores. Several individuals stressed the importance of receiving food assistance, especially for those who do not meet the minimum wage requirements or eligibility criteria (e.g., international student) for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.

"I cannot always afford to utilize on-campus supports like the food pantry. Instead, I need supports and solutions also local to me and fellow commuters so that our gas expenses of getting to campus don’t cost as much as full-cost grocery trip down the road. Mental health and healthcare access

In addition to food access and housing support, mental health and better access to healthcare resources were raised by participants. For instance, students suggested increasing the availability of free mental health counseling sessions throughout the academic year, over summer and breaks, hiring more mental health therapists, improving access to medical insurance for international students and their dependents, and having more resources available for individuals with physical health conditions and disabilities.

"My college no longer provides good access to counseling services. I rely on free counseling to get through the semester and having more therapists is essential."

Financial aid access

In general, participants shared that they faced a lot of barriers in getting financial aid, including scholarships. Examples of barriers were the length of time students had to wait for their financial aid to get approved, lack of tuition assistance and scholarships, and limited educational grants. Participants suggested that their institutions could investigate addressing these barriers while advocating for better financial support at the state and federal levels.

"If tuition wasn’t so expensive. I don’t receive much financial aid because of my parents income, but they can’t actually support me all through university. The way we do financial aid leaves so many people behind and makes college cost prohibitive, even a state university."

In terms of other non-financial resources, participants shared that it would be helpful to have access to non-stigmatized help to manage college demands, particularly in the context of neurodivergence and disability. Discussion

These findings suggest that food insecurity among college students is not only widespread but also deeply shaped by identity, context, and access to resources. As such, addressing these issues requires moving beyond one-size-fits-all solutions toward more equitable and student-informed strategies. The current study goes beyond previously published studies on college food security in three ways. First, to the best of our knowledge, this study was the first to provide food insecurity estimates among various diverse groups of students at four institutions of higher education in Vermont, which is a predominantly White rural state. Second, this study surveyed college students on strategies to improve access to culturally appropriate foods. The current study also expanded the existing literature by including the lived experiences and perspectives of students regarding food security and other basic needs such as housing, mental health, and financial aid—issues that are intricately connected to each other. Ensuring that college students have reliable access to food would also entail having stable, affordable and safe housing, mental health support, and sufficient financial aid (Fig. 1). These findings demonstrated the urgency of addressing food insecurity from both a public health and health equity lens, requiring comprehensive institutional and policy responses.

The overall high rate (40.1%) of past-year food insecurity in college students reported in this study aligns with prior work conducted in other parts of the US [1–3]. The finding that students who identified as Black, compared to other racial/ethnic groups, reported higher food insecurity rates also supports prior research [4–6]. Importantly, this study provides new insight into a broader definition of marginalized populations by finding high rates of food insecurity (52.2–68.8%) among often “hidden” or “invisible” populations who self-identified as gender minorities, Pell Grant recipients, first-generation, single parents, international students. Further, student groups who are often overlooked in college food insecurity surveys, medical students and postdoctoral trainees, also reported high rates of food insecurity (47.4–48.3%). Critically, older students (often postdoctoral trainees and medical students) had higher odds of reporting past year food insecurity, underscoring the urgent need to address this issue in populations that might easily slip through current safety nets [39].

As might be expected, the most often reported reason for using a campus food pantry was ease of getting to the pantry while lack of awareness was the most reported barrier—corroborating what other recent studies reported [17, 22]. While many colleges and universities have established food pantries in response to the rising rates of food insecurity on their campuses, food-insecure students might not be aware of these resources and might face other challenges, including reliable access to transportation, in accessing them. Our findings also showed that being aware of the existence of on-campus food pantries was not enough to improve access for food-insecure students due to barriers such as inconvenient operating hours and perceiving the needs of other students being more urgent than theirs.

It might also be worthwhile reflecting that about half of the respondents recommended gathering feedback from students about their preferences to improve access to culturally appropriate foods. This is especially important for institutions in predominantly White states where the needs of minority students could be overlooked [13]. Moreover, some groups such as international students might be especially vulnerable in current political climates in the US [40] and struggling to access food due to high stress levels, reduced mobility out of fear of deportation, and lack of resources from their institutions [41]. In addition, international students on student visas are generally limited to on-campus work of up to 20 h per week during the academic term [42], which can significantly constrain income and access to financial resources. For example, international students in this current study shared that they would like more financial aid, scholarships, and job opportunities from their institutions. These structural limitations may further exacerbate food insecurity and reduce dietary quality, particularly for students without family support in the US. As such, seeking their input on how to provide culturally appropriate foods and other resources would be critical.

This study’s findings indicated that institutional efforts to address the basic needs of food access, housing, mental health, financial aid, and other daily needs (e.g., reliable transportation) were insufficient. From a research perspective, future studies could look at identifying how to create and implement comprehensive safety net programs for the most vulnerable college students. While some needs could be addressed at the institutional level, others need to be addressed at the federal, state, and community levels. In particular, study findings suggested a need for policy research that evaluates institutional and systemic strategies to improve food security and other basic needs among the most vulnerable college students. For instance, to date, there has been limited research on how federal nutrition assistance policies could be revised to expand their services to international students. In addition, it will be important to continue engaging college students through community participatory research to determine what foods currently in pantries meet student needs and improve access to culturally appropriate foods and other basic needs. Future research could also consider tracking students over multiple semesters to measure whether food insecurity affects academic performance, retention, or graduation rates. This might help higher education institutions better understand the longer-term impacts and justify increased institutional support. From a practical standpoint, colleges and universities could assess their financial aid structures, partner with community or state organizations to provide additional emergency funding and improve access to mental health resources given the direct connections between food security and mental health. Cultural competency could also be prioritized in dining services, food pantries, mental health and counseling services to improve a sense of belonging among students in the college community.

While the current study had several strengths, including a large sample of students ranging from undergraduates to medical students and post-doctoral trainees, which represented diverse sociodemographic characteristics, the study had some limitations. Firstly, data presented in this study represented the views of college students from only four institutions of higher education in Vermont, and as such, might not be representative of all college students in the US. Due to the use of convenience sampling, survey respondents might not be broadly representative of the total student population at each institution. In addition, survey participation might have been influenced by an interest in food security or lived experiences with food insecurity, making them more likely to participate and potentially introducing self-selection bias. Survey responses were based on self-report, and underreporting food insecurity might have occurred due to factors such as stigma and social desirability bias. While we ran the survey during two consecutive semesters that included the winter break, data were not collected during summer and might not have captured seasonal variations in food insecurity, suggesting the importance of considering the timing surveys when interpreting research.

Conclusions

This study’s findings highlight existing disparities in food access and other basic needs among college students in Vermont. There is an urgent need to revise existing programs and policies to be more inclusive for college students. Given that food insecurity does not exist in isolation, food assistance programs targeting college students should incorporate mental health care, housing support, and financial aid to provide comprehensive safety nets for the next generation of scholars. Collaborative partnerships across campus departments will likely be necessary for effective and holistic change. Interventions could center cultural responsiveness, communication, and emergency funding, especially for students who are already stretched thin. Future research could assess the best practices and strategies to get buy-in from university administrators and those who oversee resources on campus. In addition, it will be critical to continue conducting research on co-designing programs with students, informed and enriched by their lived experiences.
