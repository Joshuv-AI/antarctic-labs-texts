Weight Bias Internalisation and Health‐Related Quality of Life in Adolescents: What Role Do Family Meals Play?
Authors: Jaume Broto‐Puig, Miriam Latorre‐Millán, Eva Penelo, Anna Bach‐Faig, David Sánchez‐Carracedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWeight bias internalisation (WBI) has been associated with poorer health‐related quality of life (HRQoL), however, evidence in community‐based adolescent samples remains scarce. Family meals have shown protective effects on adolescent health, yet their role in the relationship between WBI and HRQoL has not yet been explored. This study investigated associations between WBI, HRQoL, and family meals in Spanish adolescents.MethodWe conducted a cross‐sectional study with a representative sample of 1016 adolescents aged 11–16 from Terrassa, Spain (random multistage cluster sampling). Validated instruments were used to assess WBI (WBIS‐M), HRQoL (KIDSCREEN‐27), and family dinner quality and frequency. Anthropometric data were objectively measured. Adjusted regression models were conducted.ResultsLower WBI and a higher frequency and quality of family dinners were associated with higher HRQoL scores across KIDSCREEN‐27 domains. Further analyses revealed that family dinners moderated the effect of WBI on two HRQoL factors, attenuating its negative impact. However, this moderating impact became substantially weaker at higher levels of WBI.ConclusionsThis study of Spanish adolescents is the first to show a negative association between WBI and HRQoL and that this relationship is moderated by the frequency and quality of shared family meals, suggesting that supportive family environments can buffer WBI's negative impact, although this protective role is attenuated at the highest levels of WBI, highlighting the vital need for early prevention, and warranting further investigation.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex, multifactorial disease characterised by excessive adiposity and associated with an increased risk for numerous non‐communicable diseases, making it a leading risk factor for disability (WHO Regional Office for Europe). Overweight and obesity affect almost 60% of adults and nearly one in three children and adolescents (29% of boys and 27% of girls) in the WHO European Region (WHO Regional Office for Europe). In Spain, the prevalence of obesity and overweight among children and adolescents aged 2 to 17 is 30% (Gutiérrez‐González et al. 2024).

Children and adolescents with higher body weight experience weight stigma, defined as the social devaluation and denigration of individuals because of their excess body weight, which can lead to negative attitudes, stereotypes (e.g., being lazy, gluttonous, lacking willpower and self‐discipline, incompetent, unmotivated to improve their health, non‐compliant with medical treatment, and personally responsible for their higher body weight), prejudice, and discrimination (Rubino et al. 2020). In Spain, 43.2% of adolescents (81.8% in adolescents with obesity) reported experiencing weight‐related stigma from different sources, such as peers, school, family, healthcare providers, media, and society in general, with girls being particularly affected (Anastasiadou et al. 2024). This pervasive weight stigma is driven by negative social perceptions of obesity, reinforced by the thin ideal, and the misattribution of its causes and management to personal responsibility (Crandall et al. 2001).

Adolescence is a unique and formative period during which adverse events can increase vulnerability to mental health problems (WHO 2025). Weight stigma negatively impacts the psychosocial and physical health of adolescents (Puhl and Lessard 2020), particularly when internalized. Weight bias internalisation (WBI) is defined as the process of becoming aware of and agreeing with negative weight‐based stereotypes, applying them to oneself, and engaging in self‐blame and self‐devaluation for one's weight (Corrigan et al. 2006). High levels of WBI were found in 19.4% of Spanish adolescents (50.7% among those with obesity), with girls again being particularly affected (80,8%, and 96.1% among those girls with obesity) (Anastasiadou et al. 2024).

WBI significantly contributes to multiple adverse health‐related outcomes in adolescents, worsening their psychosocial, physical and behavioural health (Butt et al. 2023). Additionally, weight stigma contributes to weight gain over time, leading to a bidirectional relationship with obesity (Ma et al. 2021). Overall, the evidence shows that weight stigma, particularly WBI, can contribute to worsening obesity‐related problems and create additional barriers to effective obesity care and prevention (Sánchez‐Carracedo 2022).

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) is an individual's or a group's perceived physical and mental health over time (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2022). Previous research has found reduced HRQoL in children and adolescents with overweight and obesity, with impairments in their physical and social functioning (Tsiros et al. 2009). In addition, WBI has been found associated with decreased overall functioning and lower HRQoL (Pearl and Puhl 2018), primarily in samples of adults seeking weight loss. However, to our knowledge, no previous research has examined the association between WBI and HRQoL in community‐based samples of adolescents.

It is well‐established that greater family meal frequency protects children and adolescents against a poorer diet, obesity, risky behaviours, poorer mental health and well‐being, and poorer academic outcomes (Snuggs and Harvey 2023). Better‐quality and more frequent family meals can also alleviate the consequences of adverse life experiences, such as food insecurity (Barreiro‐Álvarez et al. 2024). While prior studies have explored the benefits of family meals (Skeer et al. 2023), none have specifically investigated whether their frequency and quality can buffer the negative association between WBI and HRQoL in adolescents.

The main goal of this study is to examine the relationship between WBI and HRQoL in a representative sample of Spanish adolescents, and to assess the potential moderating effect of family meals on this association.

Methods

Study Design

This was a cross‐sectional survey‐based study, conducted in the framework of the first assessment of a funded wider longitudinal study on weight stigma in adolescents.

Participants

A total of 1016 adolescents were recruited through a random multistage cluster sampling to be representative of students from the first to fourth year of compulsory secondary education in the city of Terrassa, corresponding to 64 classes from seven public and nine grant‐aided schools. More details on the sampling and the sample flow can be found elsewhere (Anastasiadou et al. 2024).

Instruments

Anthropometric and sociodemographic measures. Participants' weight (kg) and height (m) were measured by trained research staff using precision stadiometers and portable scales without shoes. Weight status categories were determined based on zBMI scores, according to the WHO growth reference criteria (de Onis et al. 2007): underweight (zBMI < −2 SD), normal weight (zBMI between −2 SD and 1 SD), overweight (zBMI between 1 SD and 2 SD), and obesity (zBMI > 2 SD) 1 . Participants reported information about gender, age (grouped as < 13, 13 to < 15, ≥ 15 years), their parents' family origin (grouped as Spanish or non‐Spanish), and parents' educational level based on the International Labour Organisation's classification system (comprising less than basic, basic, intermediate and advanced, subsequently dichotomised as low (basic or less than basic) and high (intermediate or advanced).

KIDSCREEN‐27. We used the Spanish‐validated version of this questionnaire (Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2007). It is an international questionnaire for assessing HRQoL among children and adolescents aged 8–18 years. It includes 27 items answered using a Likert‐type scale and grouped into the five following dimensions: physical well‐being (five items), psychological well‐being (seven items), autonomy and parent relations (seven items), and social support and peers (four items), and school environment (four items). Higher derived T‐scores indicate better HRQoL. McDonald's omega (ω) ranged from 0.79 to 0.84 for the referenced sample, and from 0.76 to 0.88 for ours, both reflecting a good internal consistency.

Weight Bias Internalisation Scale (WBIS‐M). This questionnaire measures WBI across the body weight statuses (Pearl and Puhl 2014). We used the previously validated Spanish version for adolescents which showed an excellent internal consistency (ω = 0.93) (Andrés et al. 2022), in our sample as well (ω = 0.95). This version has 10 items that are rated on a one‐to‐seven‐point Likert scale. Higher scores indicate greater WBI. According to previous research (Anastasiadou et al. 2024), the raw score was dichotomised into two categories: low (WBIS‐M < 4) and high (WBIS‐M ≥ 4) levels of WBI, in order to describe its distribution according to sociodemographic, anthropometric, HrQOL and family dinner variables. It was treated as a continuous variable for the rest of the analysis.

Children's Family Dinner Index (FDI‐C). The FDI‐C assesses the quality of family dinners through eight items, with responses based on level of agreement or frequency scored from zero to four representing the following components: communication (conversations during family dinners), enjoyment of sharing food and time with family, presence of digital distractions, and dinner logistics (helping in the kitchen or at the table) (Skeer et al. 2023). Previous studies have shown that adolescents report sharing dinner with their families more often than other meals, therefore, researchers have focused particularly on family dinners (Skeer et al. 2023). It has a unidimensional structure, and higher scores (range 0–32) indicate higher quality of family dinners. As described in the Supporting Information S1, an eight‐item and four‐first‐order‐factor plus one‐second‐order‐factor model was analysed using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and the maximum likelihood robust (MLR) method of estimation, showing a satisfactory fit for the model in the present sample [CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.983, RMSEA = 0.031 (90% CI: 0.014–0.047), SRMR = 0.020, χ 2 (16) = 31.34]. Standardized factor loadings ranged from 0.31 to 0.91; internal consistency reliability, as measured by McDonald's omega, was acceptable (ω = 0.75).

Frequency of Family Dinners (FDF). The weekly frequency of family dinners (ranging from zero to seven) was also assessed. This measure has been used in previous studies of Spanish adolescents (Barreiro‐Álvarez et al. 2024). This measure was dichotomised: 0–4 versus 5–7 family dinners per week.

Procedure

Questionnaires were administered in classrooms via an online platform, supervised by trained staff. Weight and height were measured privately by trained research staff using portable scales and precision stadiometers, following standardized protocols and the Catalan Public Health Agency's recommendations to minimise potential adverse effects (Bujalance et al. 2023). Data were pseudo‐anonymised to preserve confidentiality. Data collection took place between April and May of 2022. Data collection was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of the World Medical Assembly of Helsinki guidelines (World Medical Association).

Statistical Analysis

Sociodemographic and anthropometric associations with dichotomised WBIS‐M scores were assessed using chi‐square (χ 2) and Student's t‐tests. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the associations between the dichotomised WBIS‐M and all the studied variables considered. To identify potential predictors of HRQoL factors through the KIDSCREEN‐27, we used multiple regression models with stepwise backward procedure and Wald test, considering probability for entry and removal as 0.05 and 0.10, respectively. In these models, the category “non‐binary” was not included for gender as a predictor because of its very low size in the study sample (n = 3). Finally, the moderator effect of both quality (FDI‐C score) and frequency (FDF score) of family dinners on the relation between WBIS‐M scores and each HRQoL dimension score was analysed with hierarchical multiple linear regression models; when the interaction term between WBIS‐M and the family dinner measures was statistically significant, simple effects at the minimum, median and maximum level of FDI‐C and FDF were estimated. Statistical analyses were conducted using Mplus v.8.11 (Muthén & Muthén, CA, USA) and Stata v.19 (StataCorp LLC, TX, USA).

Results

Table 1 shows the distribution of sociodemographic and weight‐related variables within the sample. Among the 1016 adolescents of the sample (age range: 11–17 years old), nearly half were females (48.9%) and aged 13–14 (49.6%); most of the participants had normal weight (71.5%), and were from Spanish parents (73.2%) with advanced (39.7%) or intermediate (35.2%) education level. Compared to participants with lower WBIS‐M scores, those with scores of at least 4 were more likely to be girls (75.6% vs. 42.3%), have excess weight (overweight 26.9% vs. 17.8%; obesity 19.8% vs. 4.7%) and have a higher weight (BMI 23.1 ± 4.8 vs. 20.5 ± 3.3; zBMI 0.82 ± 1.29 vs. 0.20 ± 1.04).

Lastly, Table 5 and Figure 1 show that FDI‐C and FDF moderated the effect of WBIS‐M on psychological well‐being, and FDI‐C moderated the effect of WBIS‐M on school environment (p ≤ 0.044). In all three cases, the magnitude of the negative coefficients for WBIS‐M on the aforementioned HRQoL dimensions was greater at higher levels of FDI‐C or FDF. At the same time, higher levels of family dinner quality and frequency were consistently associated with higher HRQoL scores across the WBIS‐M range, although this effect became markedly smaller at higher levels of WBIS‐M. For physical well‐being, the moderating effect of FDI‐C showed a similar trend, although the result was marginally significant (p = 0.056).

Discussion

This study offers novel evidence on the relationship between WBI and HRQoL in adolescents, a population underrepresented in the literature, especially in Mediterranean countries. While previous research has shown that WBI negatively impacts the psychological and physical health in adults (including its association with disordered eating, depression, anxiety, low self‐esteem, and adverse health behaviours) (Pearl and Puhl 2018), our findings extend this body of knowledge by showing that similar patterns are also evident in a representative community‐based sample of Spanish adolescents. Specifically, we found that higher levels of WBI are consistently related to lower HRQoL across different domains.

These results highlight that adolescents with lower levels of WBI consistently report higher HRQoL, particularly in the areas of psychological well‐being, physical well‐being, and autonomy & relationships with parents. Although the associations were weaker regarding the school environment and peer and social support, the overall pattern aligns with prior research in adult populations (Pearl and Puhl 2018; Zagaria et al. 2023), and extends these observations to a representative sample of adolescents. This study contributes to the limited literature on this topic by revealing these associations in a Mediterranean context, where cultural norms regarding food and body image may differ from those in North America or Northern Europe (Abdoli et al. 2024). The observed results across multiple HRQoL domains suggest that WBI is a pervasive factor influencing adolescent well‐being, regardless of specific life domains. This pattern is consistent with previous studies in diverse populations, including individuals seeking weight loss (Walsh et al. 2018), adult women (Zagaria et al. 2023), college students (Purton et al. 2019), and adults experiencing food insecurity (Cuauro et al. 2023), however, our study is among the first to quantify this relationship in a community adolescent population using validated instruments and objective anthropometric data.

In addition, we found some domains of HRQoL more negatively associated with female gender and older age, and, with a more marginal effect, with non‐Spanish origin and zBMI. Regarding gender influence, these results align with previous research in adolescents in whom girls and adolescents with overweight are more likely to report WBI and lower HRQoL (Anastasiadou et al. 2024). This gender gap may be attributed to stronger societal pressures on girls regarding body image (Stewart and Ogden 2021).

On the flip side, we found higher frequency and quality of family dinners were associated with lower WBI and higher HRQoL, across all domains, and furtherly, more frequent and higher‐quality of shared family dinners attenuated the negative effect of WBI on some psychosocial HRQoL dimensions. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that family functioning and parental support can mitigate the effects of weight stigma on disordered eating behaviours (Hooper et al. 2023). Likewise, previous studies have linked frequent family meals to protective effects against obesity (Berge et al. 2015) and disordered eating in adolescents, particularly among girls (Loth et al. 2015), and have emphasised their positive influence on psychosocial well‐being (Fulkerson et al. 2007), and the importance of communication and enjoyment during mealtimes (De La Torre‐Moral et al. 2021). Consistent with this literature, our findings suggest that the benefits of shared family meals extend to broader dimensions of adolescent well‐being, highlighting that the quality of family interactions may be as important as, or even more important than, meal frequency itself. This underscores the need for public health strategies to move beyond simply promoting meal frequency and place greater emphasis on mealtime quality.

However, our findings also indicate that the moderating effect showed by family meals is substantially attenuated at higher levels of WBI. This result is consistent with previous findings indicating that positive family and parenting factors were associated with lower levels of disordered eating behaviours primarily among adolescents without experiences of weight stigma, whereas these associations were not observed among those reporting weight‐stigmatising experiences (Hooper et al. 2023). Although the aforementioned study focused on experienced rather than internalized weight stigma and examined disordered eating rather than HRQoL, both sets of findings suggest that supportive family contexts may have a reduced influence when weight‐related stigma is more pronounced. One possible explanation is that elevated WBI reflects more deeply internalized maladaptive cognitions and emotional distress that may be less responsive to potentially beneficial psychosocial influences, consistent with heterogeneous intervention findings and the remaining challenge of achieving sustained reductions in WBI (D'Adamo et al. 2024). This insight further reinforces the importance of early prevention‐focused approaches targeting WBI during development, including detection and quickly support before maladaptive cognitions and emotional distress become more deeply internalized.

Given the high prevalence of high levels of WBI in our sample (approximately 20%), its negative impact on HRQoL, and the challenges associated with higher levels of WBI highlighted by our moderation analyses, we suggest aligning with international guidelines (Rubino et al. 2020; Tanas et al. 2022; Nutter et al. 2024) by prioritising the reduction of weight stigma and shifting the narrative about obesity as a priority when designing health promotion prevention and intervention strategies for adolescents, regardless of their weight status. This requires coordinated efforts across cultural, educational, and healthcare sectors to reduce weight stigma and improve adolescent HRQoL. Addressing weight stigma requires a broader societal commitment to challenge and change the dominant narrative around obesity, shifting from blame and bias to a more compassionate care of obesity, recognising social and biological factors beyond personal responsibility, promoting health strategies that focus on outcomes rather than weight, using non‐stigmatising language and imagery about body weight and obesity, and engaging in legislative and policy efforts to reduce it.

In this sense, our findings also support the importance of accounting for the role of family meals, as higher frequency and quality of family dinners were associated with a weaker negative effect of WBI on HRQoL. Understanding this interaction is essential for a comprehensive assessment of adolescent well‐being. Therefore, public health strategies should not only encourage regular family meals but also foster environments that support positive communication, emotional connection, and body acceptance at the family level. These proposed actions align with the ‘Get Involved’ campaign by the Catalan Public Health Agency (ASPCAT) (Agència de Salut Pública de Catalunya (ASPCAT) 2023), which encourages families to share at least one meal per day and respectful conversations during mealtimes, free from digital distractions (e.g., mobile phones or television). At a broader societal level, the campaign promotes body acceptance by discouraging negative comments about physical appearance (whether directed at children, adolescents, or others) and encourages young people to critically evaluate media and social media messages, particularly those that idealise certain body types or promote unhealthy dietary practices, especially among girls.

These efforts should be expanded to include legislation and policy efforts to reduce weight stigma in educational and healthcare settings, as well as in the media (including advertising, promotions, and the entertainment industry), where stigma often originates, as recommended by international consensus (Rubino et al. 2020; Nutter et al. 2024). To effectively reduce overweight/obesity, disordered eating, and WBI, and to improve adolescent HRQoL, collaboration is needed among healthcare professionals, educators, and families. Moving forward, longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to corroborate and better understand these insights and assess their applicability across diverse populations and contexts.

This study has several strengths. It is the first to assess the association of WBI on HRQoL in a community‐based sample of adolescents, and to examine the role in this association of both the frequency and quality of family dinners through moderation analysis. We used validated instruments for Spanish adolescents (WBIS‐M, KIDSCREEN‐27, and FDI‐C), objective anthropometric measurements, and a representative sample from an urban area in Catalonia.

However, some limitations should be noted. The cross‐sectional design prevents causal inference. While the sample was representative of Terrassa, an urban area in Catalonia, it may not be generalisable to other regions or rural populations (in which sociodemographic may differ). The measure of family dinner quality includes the frequency of family conversations and the level of adolescent participation in them, but not their content. Finally, we used a WBIS‐M cut‐off score of ≥ 4 based on previous studies (Anastasiadou et al. 2024; Pearl et al. 2020; Prunty et al. 2020), but this threshold may require further validation.

This study provides for the first time robust evidence that WBI has a significant negative relationship with all dimensions of HRQoL in adolescents, including physical well‐being, psychological well‐being, autonomy and parent relationships, peers and social support, and school environment. The frequency of family dinners, and particularly their quality, were associated with lower levels of WBI and better HRQoL. Moreover, quality and frequency of family dinners also moderated the negative effect of WBI on HRQoL, suggesting that supportive family meal contexts may help attenuate its negative effect; although this influence appears to diminish at higher levels of WBI.

These insights encourage the development of culturally sensitive and emotionally supportive interventions that address weight stigma as a complex phenomenon, promoting body acceptance and mental well‐being in adolescents, especially among girls. Future research should use this novel knowledge to scale and adapt these strategies across diverse populations and settings and develop qualitative and longitudinal research to confirm and expand these findings.
