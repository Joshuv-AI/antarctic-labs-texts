ENO3-mediated glycolytic reprogramming participates in β-glucan-induced dendritic cells activation and anti-tumor responses
Authors: Jun Ding, Yongzhe Hao, Meng Yuan, Jie Pan, Yewen Xie, Xiao Sun, Yongling Ning, Chunjian Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDendritic cells (DCs) are key initiators of antitumor immunity, yet the metabolic drivers governing their activation remain incompletely defined. Here, we show that the fungal immunomodulator β-glucan induces robust immune-metabolic reprogramming in bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs), characterized by coordinated increases in glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid cycle activity. This metabolic shift is essential for BMDCs activation, as inhibition of glycolysis or oxidative metabolism diminishes β-glucan-induced costimulatory molecule expression, proinflammatory cytokine production and T-cell priming. Multi-omics profiling identifies enolase 3 (ENO3) as a key glycolytic regulator selectively upregulated by β-glucan. ENO3 knockdown reduces glycolytic flux, decreases mitochondrial ATP and ROS production, and consequently impairs BMDCs maturation and CD8⁺ T-cell responses. In DC-specific ENO3-knockout mice, ENO3 deficiency significantly compromises the antitumor efficacy of β-glucan, which characterized by accelerated tumor growth, impaired DCs activation, reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and an immunosuppressive tumor milieu. Introduction

DCs are central orchestrators of antitumor immunity owing to their exceptional antigen-presenting capacity and sentinel-like ability to detect pathogenic and danger signals [1]. Under steady-state conditions, peripheral DCs remain in an immature state yet express a broad repertoire of pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) that allow rapid detection of microbial products and inflammatory cues. Engagement of these receptors by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) initiates DCs maturation, characterized by enhanced antigen processing and presentation through upregulated major histocompatibility complex (MHC-I/II) molecules, increased expression of costimulatory receptors, and robust secretion of cytokines and chemokines. These coordinated changes drive DCs migration to draining lymph nodes, where they prime naïve T cells and initiate effective tumor-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell responses [2–4]. Among the diverse PAMPs, fungal-derived β-glucans are well-established ligands for the pattern recognition receptor Dectin-1 on DCs [5], and β-glucan–activated DCs have been shown to promote Th1 and Th17 polarization as well as enhance cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) activity, thereby strengthening antitumor immunity [6–8]. These potent immunostimulatory properties highlight β-glucan as a promising immunomodulator and adjuvant for cancer immunotherapy.

Over the past decade, it has become increasingly evident that immune cell activation is accompanied by profound metabolic rewiring that dictates cellular phenotype and effector function. Recent studies have begun to delineate the metabolic programs required for DC activation, particularly the metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis [9–11]. Upon stimulation with Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, DCs undergo a rapid glycolytic surge that actively supports maturation by fueling endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi expansion and sustaining high-level cytokine and protein biosynthesis [12, 13]. Similarly, activation of the STING pathway in DCs promotes glycolysis-driven ATP production, amplifying type I interferon responses essential for antitumor immunity [14]. Conversely, inhibition of glycolysis has been shown to impair DCs migration and maturation [15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that β-glucan can trigger macrophage activation, concurrent with a metabolic shift toward heightened glycolysis and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity, as evidenced by increased [13C] incorporation into pathway intermediates [16]. Furthermore, β-glucan recognition drives extensive metabolic reprogramming, characterized by upregulated glycolytic flux, sustained enhancement of oxidative phosphorylation, and TCA cycle remodeling. This coordinated immunometabolic reconfiguration is essential for the establishment of the trained immunity phenotype [17, 18]. Although metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of immune activation, the exact role of glycolysis in β-glucan–induced dendritic cell activation and subsequent antitumor immunity remain poorly understood. Enolase 3 (ENO3) is a key metalloenzyme in the glycolytic pathway that catalyzes the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate, representing a critical regulatory node controlling glycolytic flux [19]. ENO3 has been associated with clinical prognosis and metabolic remodeling in several diseases; it contributes to tumor progression in colorectal cancer [20], drives vascular dysfunction in diabetes by enhancing glycolysis [21], and modulates inflammatory and epithelial homeostasis in colitis through regulation of ferroptosis [22]. Moreover, clinical analyses suggest correlations between ENO3 expression and immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment [23]. While the immunometabolic functions of the related isoenzyme ENO1 in DCs have been described [24], the role of ENO3-mediated glycolysis in DCs activation and immune regulation has not been explored.

Here, we demonstrate that β-glucan triggers extensive immunometabolic reprogramming in DCs, characterized by coordinated enhancement of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation. Through integrated transcriptomic and metabolic profiling, we identify ENO3 as a glycolytic enzyme selectively upregulated by β-glucan. ENO3 proves essential for the glycolytic surge, mitochondrial activity, and the maturation and T cell–priming capacity of β-glucan–activated DCs in vitro. Importantly, DC-specific deletion of ENO3 inhibited the antitumor efficacy of β-glucan in vivo, leading to impaired DCs maturation, reduced intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and a shift toward an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Collectively, our findings establish ENO3 as a fundamental metabolic checkpoint that bridges β-glucan sensing to DC-driven antitumor immunity, highlighting its therapeutic potential for enhancing DC-based immunotherapy strategies.

Materials and methods

Mice and cell lines

C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice were purchased from Cavens Lab Animal (Changzhou, China). CD4 and CD8 ovalbumin (OVA) T-cell receptor transgenic OT-I and OT-II mice were kindly provided by Prof Hai Qi (Tsinghua University). Dendritic cell (DC)-specific ENO3 knockout mice on a C57BL/6 J background were generated by crossing ENO3flox/flox mice with CD11c-Cre transgenic mice, and all genetic engineering procedures were performed by Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc (Shanghai, China). All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. Experiments utilized female mice at 6–8 weeks of age. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Changzhou Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (Approval No. CZZYY 19–1–2411–1). Mice were euthanized by inhalation of carbon dioxide (CO2) in a closed chamber, following the guidelines set by the IACUC. The Lewis lung cancer cell line (LLC) derived from C57BL/6 mice was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37℃ in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Reagents

Β-glucan (whole glucan particles, WGP) was purified from the cell walls of Saccharomyces cerevisiae and provided by Biothera (InvivoGen). Structurally, WGP consists of a β-(1,3)-D-glucan backbone with approximately 3%−6% of the backbone glucose units bearing α,β-(1,6)-linked side branches. Recombinant mouse Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (Flt3) ligand was purchased from Peprotech. Fluorescein-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against CD11c, MHC-II, CD40, CD80, CD86, OVA257–264-H-2 Kb, CD45, CD11b, F4/80, Gr-1, PD-L1, PD-1, CD3, CD4, CD8 and IFN-γ; isotype control monoclonal antibodies; and carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) were purchased from Biolegend. Leukocyte activation cocktail (LAC) was purchased from BD Pharmingen. Microbead-conjugated mAbs against CD4, CD8 and CD11c were purchased from Miltenyi Biotec. [13C]6-glucose were obtained from MCE. Anti-ENO3 (Cat. no. PA5-117935) and the prestained protein ladder (Cat. no. 26617) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific, anti- GAPDH (Clone 13E5, Cat. no. 4970) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, and anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Cat. no. ab97051) was obtained from Abcam. 2-NBDG, Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM), 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), oligomycin, DAPI, OVA, hyaluronidase, collagenase type IV and deoxyribonuclease were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

BMDCs preparation and culture

Bone marrow cells were isolated from femurs and tibiae of mice and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 100 ng/ml Flt-3 ligand (Flt3L). Flt3L-stimulated BMDCs consisted mainly of conventional DC subsets (cDC1 and cDC2) and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), as previously described [6, 25]. Fresh medium was replenished on day 3. On day 6, the dendritic proliferating clusters were collected and purified by anti-CD11c microbeads to obtain immature BMDCs for further experiments.

Tracer treatment and stable isotope-resolved metabolomics analyses

Immature BMDCs stimulated with or without WGP (100 μg/mL) were maintained in glucose-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with [13C]6-glucose (10 mM) and 10% dialyzed FBS for 48 h. Aliquots of the medium were taken at 0 and 48 h and the cells were rinsed in cold PBS. Cell samples were quenched and metabolites were extracted using a methanol–water system. Briefly, 300 μL of pure water was added to cell pellets, followed by vortexing for 30 s. The samples were then flash-frozen in dry ice and subjected to three repeated freeze–thaw cycles in liquid nitrogen. After vortexing for another 30 s, the samples were sonicated for 15 min in an ice-water bath. Subsequently, 250 μL of the lysate was mixed with 750 μL of ice-cold methanol (−40℃), vortexed and incubated for 1 h. After centrifugation, 900 μL of the supernatant was collected and dried under a vacuum concentrator. The dried metabolite extracts were reconstituted in 180 μL of ultrapure water and vigorously vortexed. The solution was then filtered to remove particulates. After a final centrifugation, the clarified supernatant was transferred to injection vials for HPIC-MS/MS analysis. The remaining cell lysate was reserved for protein content quantification to normalize metabolite abundances. Metabolite identities were confirmed by matching the accurate mass and retention time against authentic standards. The isotopic enrichment (13C-labeling) of metabolites was quantified using Xcalibur and IsoCor software to correct for natural isotope abundance.

Seahorse glycolytic stress assay

Immature BMDCs were seeded into XFe96 microplates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and were treated with or without WGP. The culture medium was replaced with XF DMEM base medium (Agilent, Cat No. 103575–100) supplemented with glucose (10 mM), pyruvate (1 mM) and glutamine (2 mM). The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were measured using an XFe96 analyzer after sequential injection of the compounds of the seahorse XF cell glycolysis stress Test kit (Cat No. 103020–100, Agilent) (Glucose 100 mM, Oligomycin 2 µM and 2-DG 500 mM) for ECAR test or the seahorse XF cell mito stress Test kit (Cat No. 103015–100, Agilent) (oligomycin 1.5 µM, FCCP 2 µM, and Rot-AA 0.5 μm) for OCR test. The ECAR and OCR were automatically recorded and calculated by the Seahorse XF96 software (Agilent) following sequential injection of the metabolic modulators.

In vitro metabolic function assays

Glucose uptake assay: BMDCs (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates and stimulated with WGP (100 μg/mL) for 24 h. After two washes, cells were incubated with 2‑NBDG (100 μM) in glucose‑free RPMI‑1640 at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were then washed three times with PBS, stained with anti-CD11c antibody and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) measurement: BMDCs were treated with β-glucan for 24 h, then harvested and resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium containing TMRM (20 μM). Cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, washed with PBS, stained with anti-CD11c antibody and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) detection: mtROS levels were measured using MitoSOX™ Red staining. BMDCs pretreated with β-glucan were incubated with MitoSOX™ (5 µM) Red in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. cells were washed twice with PBS, stained with anti-CD11c antibody and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry.

Lactate quantification: Culture supernatants from β-glucan-stimulated BMDCs were collected at 24 h. L-lactate levels were measured using the L-Lactate Assay Kit (Eton Bioscience, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA interference

ENO3-specific siRNA (ENO3siRNA) and negative control siRNA (NCsiRNA) were synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China). For RNAi experiments, BMDCs were transfected with ENO3siRNA or NCsiRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by β-glucan stimulation. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-PCR and Western blot.

Flow cytometry

In vitro, BMDCs were pretreated with either 2-DG (500 μM) or oligomycin (20 nM) for 30 min, or were transfected with NCsiRNA and ENO3siRNA for 48 h prior to β-glucan stimulation. In vivo, tumors and draining lymph nodes (DLNs) were collected from euthanized mice and minced into pieces smaller than 1 mm3, Tissues were enzymatically digested at 37 °C for 30 min with continuous rotation using a solution containing collagenase type IV, hyaluronidase, and DNase I. The resulting suspensions were filtered through 70-μm cell strainers and washed twice with staining buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FCS and 1 mM EDTA).

Cells were blocked with an Fc-blocking monoclonal antibody (Miltenyi Biotec) for 15 min on ice and stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies against CD11c, CD40, CD80, CD86, MHC-I, MHC-II, CD45, CD11b, CD3, CD4, CD8, Gr-1, PD-1 and F4/80 for 30 min. After two washes with PBS, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry on a FACS Canton II (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) and data were analyzed with FlowJo V10.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

BMDCs or tumor samples were treated with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and total RNA was isolated and reverse transcribed with TaqMan Reverse Transcription Reagents (Applied Biosystems). The indicated cytokine mRNA levels were quantified by qRT-PCR amplification and data were acquired on an ABI ViiA 7 real-time PCR system. Relative quantification of mRNA expression was calculated by the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method. The mRNA relative expression was normalized to the expression of GAPDH, and analyzed using the 2^(−ΔCt) method. The primer sequences are indicated in Supplemental Table 1.

In vitro T cell priming assay

CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from the lymph nodes and spleens of OT-II and OT-I mice were purified with magnetic-activated cell-sorting beads. The immature BMDCs, which were pretreated with 2DG or were transfected with ENO3siRNA, were cultured with purified CFSE-labeled OT-I or OT-II T cells at a 1:10 ratio in the presence of β-glucan and 50 µg/ml OVA. After 72 h, T cell proliferation and IFN-γ production were assessed by flow cytometry.

Mouse tumor models

The ENO3flox/flox and ENO3ΔCD11c mice were subcutaneously implanted with LLC cells (3.5 × 105 per mouse). Beginning on day 8 post-inoculation, when palpable tumors had developed, mice were treated with 200 μL of β-glucan (4 mg/mL in PBS; total 800 µg) or 200μL of PBS as vehicle control given every day using an intragastric gavage needle. Tumors were measured with a caliper twice weekly, and the volume was calculated as tumor size (mm3) = ab2/2, where a is the length of the longest axis, and b is the length at a right angle from a. Mice with tumors < 300 mm3 were considered to be surviving.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tumors were fixed in 4% PFA, dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After rinsing with PBS, sections were blocked with Fc receptor blocker, and incubated with blocking buffer (5% goat serum, 2.5% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS). Tumor sections were then incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibody (1:100, anti-mouse CD3; or anti-mouse CD4; or anti-mouse CD8a) in blocking buffer overnight at 4℃. After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Slides were washed and incubated with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated tyramide (TSA reagent) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. The tissue sections were counterstained with DAPI and mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse C1 fluorescence microscope or a Pannoramic MIDI slide scanner (3DHISTECH).

Western blot

Cells (1 × 10⁷) were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with complete protease inhibitor cocktail. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was subjected to 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes according to the manufacturer’s protocols (Bio-Rad Corp, USA). Membranes were blocked and subsequently incubated with primary antibodies against ENO3 and GAPDH, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Multiple group comparisons were performed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

β-glucan induced an increase metabolic reprogramming in BMDCs

Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of cellular metabolism in DCs maturation, especially glucose metabolism. Our previous work demonstrated that β-glucan effectively activates DCs and augments Th1 and CTLs responses to potentiate antitumor immunity [6]. To determine whether metabolic reprogramming is involved in β-glucan–induced DCs maturation, we first examined early metabolic alterations by assessing glucose uptake. Glucose-deprived BMDCs were incubated with 2-NBDG, and we found that β-glucan stimulation significantly enhanced glucose consumption of BMDCs compared to PBS controls (Fig. 1A). To obtain a comprehensive overview of the metabolic alterations, we performed stable isotope-resolved metabolomics using uniformly 13C-labeled glucose ([13C]₆-glu) as a tracer. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that β-glucan markedly remodeled multiple nodes of central carbon metabolism, prominently affecting glycolysis and its interconnected pathways, including TCA cycle and pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) (Fig. 1B). Strikingly, we observed that the levels of most glycolytic intermediates, such as glucose 6-phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate, 3-phosphoglycerate, pyruvate, and lactate were significantly increased in β-glucan-treated BMDCs, as visualized by heatmap analysis. Concurrently, the levels of TCA cycle intermediates, such as citrate, cis-aconitate, isocitrate, fumarate, and malate, were also significantly upregulated in β-glucan-treated BMDCs (Fig. 1C). These data indicate that β-glucan elicits a coordinated metabolic reprogramming in BMDCs, characterized by concurrent upregulation of glycolytic and mitochondrial pathways.

To functionally validate these observations, we monitored real-time metabolic flux using extracellular flux analysis. β-glucan stimulation significantly elevated both basal glycolytic activity and maximal glycolytic capacity, as reflected by increased extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Fig. 1D). Notably, oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis demonstrated parallel increases in basal and maximal respiration (Fig. 1E). This coordinated enhancement of glycolytic and oxidative metabolism suggested that augmented glycolysis may provide additional pyruvate to support mitochondrial oxidative metabolism. In line with this possibility, we found that β-glucan-treated BMDCs exhibited enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential, indicative of improved mitochondrial fitness, assessed by TMRM staining which was evaluated using laser confocal microscopy (Fig. 1F, H upper) and flow cytometry (Fig. 1G, H lower). Collectively, these findings establish that β-glucan triggers extensive immunometabolic rewiring in BMDCs, defined by simultaneous enhancement of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, which might provide the metabolic foundation for DC activation.

Blockade of glycolytic metabolism inhibits β-glucan-induced BMDCs activation

Glycolytic augmentation is a well-recognized hallmark of metabolic rewiring in activated immune cells. To determine whether glycolytic activity is required for β-glucan–mediated BMDCs activation, we stimulated BMDCs with β-glucan in the presence of 2-DG, a competitive inhibitor of hexokinase that blocks glycolysis at its first enzymatic step. As expected, 2-DG treatment effectively suppressed glycolytic flux, as reflected by a pronounced reduction in lactate production (Supplementary Fig. 1). Functionally, we found that glycolytic inhibition 2-DG markedly impaired β-glucan-induced BMDCs activation, resulting in substantially reduced expression of the costimulatory molecules CD40 and CD80, as well as MHC class II (MHC-II) (Fig. 2A). Moreover, 2-DG treatment reciprocally modulated β-glucan-induced cytokine expression at both the transcriptional and protein levels, abrogating IL-12b mRNA and IL-12p40 secretion while concurrently upregulating IL-10 mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 2B), consistent with a shift toward a regulatory DCs phenotype. To further assess the role of glycolysis in DC-mediated T cell priming, we co-cultured β-glucan-stimulated BMDCs with OVA-specific naïve T cells in the presence or absence of 2-DG. We found that the proliferation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells induced by β-glucan-stimulated BMDCs was significantly reduced following 2-DG treatment (Fig. 2C, D), and there was a marked decrease in interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production by CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 2D). These findings indicate that glycolytic metabolism is indispensable for the ability of β-glucan-activated BMDCs to prime robust T cell proliferation and effector differentiation.

Given that β-glucan triggers not only glycolytic activation but also enhanced oxidative phosphorylation in BMDCs, we next interrogated the functional contribution of mitochondrial respiration. We pretreated BMDCs with oligomycin, an inhibitor of the mitochondrial ATP synthase complex (Complex V) (Fig. 2E), prior to β-glucan stimulation. Oligomycin treatment did not affect the surface expression of the co‑stimulatory molecules CD86, CD80, or CD40 on β‑glucan‑stimulated BMDCs (Fig. 2F). However, it significantly attenuated the production of key pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑12p40, TNF‑α, and IL‑6) (Fig. 2G) and markedly reduced MHC‑II surface expression (Fig. 2F). These results establish that β-glucan-induced BMDCs activation depends on an integrated metabolic program involving both glycolysis and mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.

β-glucan upregulates the glycolytic gene ENO3 to drive metabolic reprogramming in BMDCs

Given the critical role of glycolysis in β-glucan-induced BMDCs activation, we next sought to identify the key metabolic regulators underlying this process. Transcriptomic profiling revealed 484 significantly upregulated genes in β-glucan-treated BMDCs compared with BMDCs. As showed in Fig. 3A, Intersection of these genes with a curated list of 113 metabolic genes from glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and the PPP, we identified ENO3 as a key candidate gene. ENO3 encodes β-enolase, which catalyzes the penultimate step of glycolysis, converting 2-phosphoglycerate (Glycerate-2P) to phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) (Fig. 3A). Consistent with transcriptomic results, β-glucan significantly increased the ENO3 mRNA and protein levels, confirmed by qPCR, Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3B-D).

To evaluate the functional role, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of ENO3 in BMDCs. ENO3 siRNA1 with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected based on RT-PCR screening and confirmed by Western blot analysis, using for all subsequent experiments (Supplemental Fig. 2 A). Glycolytic stress tests demonstrated that ENO3 knockdown significantly attenuated ECAR, as reflected by reductions in basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve (Fig. 3E). Consistently, intracellular lactate production, a direct indicator of glycolytic flux, was also substantially reduced in ENO3-deficient BMDCs stimulated with β-glucan (Fig. 3G). These findings demonstrate that ENO3 is essential for β-glucan-induced glycolytic enhancement.

We next assessed whether ENO3 deficiency impacts mitochondrial function in β-glucan-activated BMDCs. We found that although ENO3 knockdown did not significantly alter basal or maximal OCR, it reduced ATP production (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, although mitochondrial membrane potential measured by TMRM staining remained unchanged between the two groups, mitochondrial ROS production was significantly decreased in ENO3-deficient BMDCs following β-glucan stimulation (Fig. 3H). These results suggest that ENO3 contributes to both glycolytic and mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming in β-glucan-stimulated BMDCs.

Knockdown of ENO3 attenuates β-glucan-induced activation and functional capacity of BMDCs

Given the established link between glycolytic metabolism and DC activation, we hypothesized that ENO3 mediates β-glucan-driven BMDCs maturation. We found that ENO3 knockdown significantly decreased CD86 and CD40 expression in β-glucan-stimulated BMDCs, whereas CD80 and MHC-II levels remained unchanged (Fig. 4A). These results indicate that ENO3 contributes to the maturation phenotype of BMDCs stimulated by β-glucan. In addition, ENO3 knockdown markedly reduced the secretion of IL-12p40 and IL-6 (Fig. 4B). At the transcriptional level, ENO3 deficiency significantly downregulated the expression of IL-12b, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 2B), suggesting that ENO3 preferentially regulates specific pro-inflammatory cytokines, while the expression of other cytokines may be maintained through compensatory mechanisms.

To determine whether ENO3 influences T cell priming, we first assessed the antigen cross-presentation capacity of BMDCs. The OVA257–264 peptide (SIINFEKL) complexed with MHC-I molecules is required for initiating CD8+T cell cross-priming. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that WGP treatment induced the highest percentage of CD11c⁺ SIINFEKL⁺ BMDCs, and this formation of the MHC‑I-peptide complex was markedly reduced upon ENO3 knockdown (Supplemental Fig. 2 C). Subsequently, we cocultured BMDCs with OVA-specific CD4⁺ or CD8⁺ T cells. As depicted in Fig. 4C, ENO3 knockdown did not impair the ability of β-glucan-induced BMDCs to enhance CD4⁺ T cell proliferation or IFN-γ production. In contrast, ENO3 deficiency significantly attenuated CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that ENO3 specifically supports the ability of β-glucan-activated BMDCs to prime cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cell responses, linking glycolytic metabolism to adaptive immune programming.

DC-specific deletion of ENO3 attenuates β-glucan-mediated tumor suppression

To clarify the function of ENO3 in DC-mediated antitumor immunity in vivo, we constructed DC-specific ENO3-deficient mice (ENO3flox/floxCD11c-Cre, designated as ENO3ΔCD11c) and used littermate ENO3flox/flox mice as wild-type controls (Ctrl). Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were subcutaneously inoculated into ENO3ΔCD11c and control mice. After palpable tumors developed, mice were given oral administration of β-glucan (WGP) or PBS daily for treatment (Fig. 5A). As expected, compared to Ctrl mice group, tumor growth was significantly inhibited and tumor weight was markedly reduced after β-glucan treatment (Fig. 5B, D). In contrast, compared with β-glucan-treated control mice, ENO3ΔCD11c mice showed a blunted therapeutic response to β-glucan, resulting in accelerated tumor progression (Fig. 5B) and significantly increased final tumor volume and weight (Fig. 5C, D). These results confirm that the expression of ENO3 in DCs is indispensable for the anti-tumor effect of β-glucan, highlighting its key role in mediating the functional link between DCs metabolic reprogramming and efficient anti-tumor immune responses.

DC-specific deletion of ENO3 impairs the immunostimulatory effects of β-glucan in the tumor microenvironment

To investigate the mechanism by which ENO3 regulates the immune landscape in vivo, we performed a comprehensive immunophenotyping of tumor-infiltrating leukocytes (CD45⁺ cells) in all four experimental groups. Notably, while there was no significant difference in the overall frequency of tumor-infiltrating DCs between the Ctrl + WGP and ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP groups (Supplemental Fig. 3 A), the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of MHC-II and CD86 were significantly reduced on these tumor-infiltrating DCs from ENO3-deficient mice treated by WGP, indicating a compromised maturation status (Fig. 6A). Analysis of myeloid populations revealed that DC-specific ENO3 knockout selectively decreased the frequency of CD11bhiGr-1hi granulocytes within tumors, whereas the frequency of CD11binterGr-1inter myeloid cells remained unchanged (Fig. 6B). Additionally, compared with the Ctrl + WGP group, the frequencies of total CD3+ T cells and CD3+CD8+ T cells in tumors were significantly decreased in ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP group, whereas the frequency of CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cells was comparable between groups (Fig. 6C, D). Furthermore, we demonstrated a significant increase in the percentage of PD-1⁺CD8⁺ T cells, an indicator of T cell exhaustion, in the tumor microenvironment of ENO3CD11c + WGP mice relative to Ctrl + WGP mice (Fig. 6E). However, ENO3 knockout in DCs did not significantly alter the frequency of CD11b+F4/80+ tumor-infiltrating macrophages or affect the surface expression of MHC-II and CD206 on these macrophages (Supplemental Fig. 3B). In tumor-draining lymph nodes (DLNs), the frequencies of total CD3⁺ T cells were also significantly decreased in the ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP group compared to the Ctrl + WGP group, despite similar proportional distributions of CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T cell subsets between the two groups (Supplemental Fig. 3 C).

To validate the flow cytometry findings on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and gain spatial resolution of T cell populations within the intact tumor microenvironment, multiplex immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections was conducted. Consistent with flow cytometric results, co-staining for CD3, CD4, and CD8 demonstrated a significant reduction in the infiltration of total CD3⁺ T cells and CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells in tumors from ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP mice relative to Ctrl + WGP controls, while CD3⁺CD4⁺ T cell infiltration remained comparable between the two groups (Fig. 6F). These results confirm that DC-specific ENO3 deficiency disrupts the recruitment and/or maintenance of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells within tumors, thereby compromising a crucial mechanism of β-glucan-induced antitumor immunity.

To further assess whether ENO3 deficiency modulates the cytokine milieu in tumors, qRT-PCR was used to quantify the expression of immune-related genes in tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. 6G, the mRNA levels of key antitumor mediators, including IL-12B, IL-6, and CD8α, were comparable between the Ctrl + WGP and ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP groups. In contrast, the mRNA expression of TNF-α and IFN-γ, which are critical for antitumor immunity, were markedly downregulated in tumors from ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP mice. Furthermore, the mRNA expression of immunosuppressive molecules such as IL-10 and CD274 was significantly upregulated in the ENO3ΔCD11c + WGP group (Fig. 6G). Taken together, our findings reveal that DC-specific ENO3 deficiency reverses β-glucan-mediated activation of the tumor immune microenvironment and promotes the establishment of an immunosuppressive niche.

Discussion

Glucose metabolism is a fundamental process supporting both energy production and biosynthetic demands in immune cells, and is indispensable for orchestrating DCs activation and maturation. Beyond supplying ATP, glycolysis and its associated pathways generate metabolic intermediates that contribute to macromolecule synthesis and maintain cellular redox homeostasis [9]. Glucose metabolism proceeds through three interconnected and central pathways—glycolysis, TCA cycle, and PPP—which collectively coordinate energy generation, redox balance, and the provision of biosynthetic precursors required for cellular function [26]. Under aerobic conditions, glucose is metabolized into pyruvate, which enters mitochondria to fuel the TCA cycle and couples with oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for efficient ATP generation. Previous studies have shown that immature DCs primarily rely on OXPHOS to meet their energy demands [27], whereas activated DCs rapidly transition toward elevated glycolysis, which provides essential metabolic intermediates to support the synthesis of activation-related molecules [28]. In our perivous study, we employed Saccharomyces cerevisiae-derived particulate β-glucan to induce BMDCs activation by targeting the C-type lectin receptor Dectin-1 [6]. Consistent with previous reports establishing β-glucan as a potent inducer of glycolytic metabolism in M1 macrophages and myeloid DCs [7, 16], our study demonstrates that β-glucan stimulation similarly enhances glucose uptake and coordinately activates both glycolytic and oxidative metabolic pathways in BMDCs.

Functionally, our data highlight the essential role of glycolytic flux in β-glucan–induced DC maturation. Pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis with 2-DG substantially attenuated β-glucan-induced expression of co-stimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, CD86, MHC-II) and impaired proinflammatory cytokine IL-12p40 secretion, while increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 production—indicating a shift toward a less inflammatory phenotype. The corresponding decline in T-cell priming capacity reinforces the dependence of DC immunostimulatory function on intact glycolytic remodeling. Interestingly, inhibition of OXPHOS by oligomycin predominantly reduced cytokine production without strongly affecting surface costimulatory receptors. Given that cytokine synthesis is a metabolically demanding process requiring abundant amino acids, nucleotides, and reducing equivalents, interruption of mitochondrial function likely restricts the supply of TCA cycle–derived biosynthetic precursors, thus selectively limiting cytokine production [29, 30]. These results indicate that β-glucan-activated BMDCs activation required a coordinated engagement of both glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration. This metabolic configuration contrasts with the predominant glycolytic commitment observed in LPS-stimulated DCs, where oxidative phosphorylation is often suppressed [31]. This balanced metabolic program may enable prolonged antigen presentation and sustained activation capacity, consistent with the known role of Dectin-1 signaling in inducing trained immunity characterized by durable metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming [17].

A central finding of our study is the identification of ENO3 as a previously unrecognized regulator of DC immunometabolism. While several glycolytic enzymes have emerged as metabolic checkpoints shaping immune responses [32–34], the role of ENO3 (β-enolase) in immune cells had remained unexplored. Currently, immunometabolic functions of enolase isoforms ENO1 and ENO2 have been documented [24, 35]. We found that ENO3 expression was specifically and robustly upregulated upon β-glucan stimulation. ENO3 knockdown impaired glycolytic reprogramming and reduced mtROS generation without altering oxygen consumption, suggesting that ENO3-driven glycolysis may support mitochondrial redox signaling by maintaining pyruvate-derived substrate availability [36]. Functionally, we found that ENO3 deficiency attenuated β-glucan-induced the expression of CD86 and CD40, and selectively impaired production of IL-12p40 and IL-6. Given the pivotal role of IL-12 in Th1 differentiation and CTLs activation [37], we assessed the capacity of ENO3-deficient BMDCs to prime T cells. Importantly, ENO3-deficient DCs stimulated by β-glucan showed a selective defect in activating CD8⁺ T cells while preserving CD4⁺ T-cell activation. This selective impairment stands in marked contrast to the broad immunosuppression caused by the hexokinase inhibitor 2-DG, reflecting their distinct positions within the glycolytic pathway [38]. Whereas 2-DG imposes a global blockade at glycolysis initiation, ENO3 operates downstream, enabling more precise regulation of DC effector functions. This highlights the unique position of ENO3 within the glycolytic pathway as a more selective metabolic regulator of DC effector function, particularly in supporting cross-presentation and CD8⁺ T-cell priming.

With a long history of use in traditional Asian medicine, β-glucan is now recognized for its immunomodulatory functions in antitumor immunity. Our previous work established that β-glucan not only activates DCs to promote Th1 and CTLs responses but also reverses the immunosuppressive phenotype of tumor-educated DCs (TEDCs), thereby restraining tumor growth and diminishing immunosuppressive cells in the tumor microenvironment [6, 39]. Given the central role of DCs metabolism in immune activation, we further validated the physiological relevance of ENO3 in DC-mediated antitumor immunity. We demonstrated that ENO3 is essential for the antitumor efficacy of β-glucan. β-Glucan significantly inhibited tumor growth in control mice, whereas this effect was markedly reduced in DC-specific ENO3-deficient mice.

In the tumor microenvironment, we showed that although the frequency of tumor-infiltrating DCs was unchanged, ENO3-deficient DCs showed reduced MHC-II and CD86 expression in vivo. This partially diverges from our in vitro findings, where ENO3 knockdown decreased CD86 and CD40 but not MHC-II. Such differences likely reflect the influence of the tumor microenvironment, where hypoxia, nutrient limitation, and immunosuppressive cytokines increase the metabolic demands of DCs, rendering MHC-II expression more dependent on ENO3-driven glycolytic support. In addition, we observed a significant reduction in CD11bhiGr-1hi granulocytes in ENO3ΔCD11c mice, whereas CD11binterGr-1inter myeloid cells remained unchanged. These two populations represent functionally distinct subsets, CD11bhiGr-1hi cells often include inflammatory neutrophils or granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (G-MDSCs), while CD11binterGr-1inter cells represent transitional or less differentiated myeloid populations [40]. The reduction of CD11bhiGr-1hi granulocytes could reflect impaired inflammatory recruitment due to weakened DC activation or reduced production of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 or TNF-α—both decreased in ENO3-deficient tumors. Most importantly, we further found that ENO3-deficient mice following β-glucan treatment exhibited markedly reduced infiltration of CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells in tumors and DLNs, while CD4⁺ T cell frequency remained unaffected, reinforcing the notion that ENO3-dependent metabolic programming of DCs preferentially supports cytotoxic T cell responses. PD-1 is a key negative regulator of CD8+T cell activation and is highly expressed by exhausted T cells in cancer [41]. The upregulation of PD-1 on tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells in β-glucan-treated ENO3-deficient mice suggests a shift toward a more exhausted phenotype. This is consistent with the observed decrease in TNF-α and IFN-γ expression, as exhausted CD8⁺ T cells typically lose the ability to produce these effector cytokines.

Despite these findings, several limitations merit further exploration. First, we did not define the specific metabolic intermediates altered by ENO3 deficiency, nor fully elucidate the crosstalk between ENO3 and the Dectin-1-Syk-CARD9 signaling pathway. Second, the decreased mtROS and cytokine production in ENO3-deficient DCs hint at a potential mechanism that ENO3 may support NF-κB activation by sustaining redox and metabolic homeostasis essential for signal propagation, which demands further experimental support. Finally, analysis of public Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets revealed that ENO3 was not induced by β-glucan (GEO accession: GSE42189) but was upregulated by LPS (GEO accession: GSE228649) in human DCs, indicating stimulus-dependent regulation. Future studies involving modulation of ENO3 in human DCs under diverse stimulatory conditions are warranted to assess its translational potential.

In summary, our results demonstrate that β-glucan elicits a distinct immunometabolic program in DCs, characterized by the coordinated activation of glycolysis and oxidative metabolism. This metabolic reprogramming is orchestrated by ENO3, which functions as a metabolic checkpoint integrating glycolytic flux with mitochondrial function and immune effector programs. ENO3 enables β-glucan-induced DCs to sustain high metabolic output, cytokine production, and cross-priming capacity, thereby facilitating effective CD8⁺ T-cell–mediated tumor immunity. These results not only deepen our understanding of β-glucan-induced DCs activation but also identify ENO3 as a promising target for enhancing metabolic and cancer immunotherapies.
