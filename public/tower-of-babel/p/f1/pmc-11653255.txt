Photoexcitation and One-Electron Reduction Processes of a CO2 Photoreduction Dyad Catalyst Having a Zinc(II) Porphyrin Photosensitizer
Authors: Teruyuki Honda, Takumi Ehara, Ren Sato, Tomohiro Ogawa, Yusuke Kuramochi, Akiharu Satake, Kiyoshi Miyata, Ken Onda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe have explored the photophysical properties and one-electron reduction process in the dyad photocatalyst for CO2 photoreduction, ZnP-phen=Re, in which the catalyst of fac-[Re(1,10-phenanthoroline)(CO)3Br] is directly connected with the photosensitizer of zinc(II) porphyrin (ZnP), using time-resolved infrared spectroscopy, transient absorption spectroscopy, and quantum chemical calculations. We revealed the following photophysical properties: (1) the intersystem crossing occurs with a time constant of ∼20 ps, which is much faster than that of a ZnP single unit, and (2) the charge density in the excited singlet and triplet states is mainly localized on ZnP, which means that the excited state is assignable to the π–π* transition in ZnP. The one-electron reduction by 1,3-dimethyl-2-phenyl-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzo[d]imidazole occurs via the triplet excited state with the time constant of ∼100 ns and directly from the ground state with the time constant of ∼3 μs. The charge in the one-electron reduction species spans ZnP and the phenanthroline ligand, and the dihedral angle between ZnP and the phenanthroline ligand is rotated by ∼24° with respect to that in the ground state, which presumably offers an advantage for proceeding to the next CO2 reduction process. These insights could guide the new design of dyad photocatalysts with porphyrin photosensitizers.

Introduction

Toward a sustainable society, CO2 photoreduction using sunlight, also known as artificial photosynthesis, is one of the most promising approaches to provide clean energy sources.1−3 With its high demand, many CO2 photocatalytic reduction reactions have been reported using semiconductors,3 covalent organic frameworks,4 and metal complexes.5 As an intensively investigated photocatalytic system, a series of dyad catalysts consisting of a [Ru(diimine)3]2+ sensitizer and a fac-[Re(diimine)(CO)3Cl] catalyst connected with an alkyl chain (Ru–Re) achieved highly efficient CO formation with a high quantum yield (ϕCO = 46%) and a high turnover number (TONCO > 2915).2,5 Nevertheless, frequently used precious metal complexes are not ideal photosensitizers in terms of their photophysical properties as well as their costs.6

As photosensitizers, metal porphyrins have many advantages such as high absorption coefficients and visible-light absorption,7 and they typically consist of earth-abundant elements. In fact, natural photosynthesis uses magnesium porphyrin derivatives known as chlorophyll for photosensitization.7,8 Dyad metal complex systems for CO2 photoreduction using a metal porphyrin photosensitizer have also been developed.9−15 There are several reports on dyad photocatalysts composed of zinc(II) porphyrin and [Re(diimine)(CO)3Cl]. Gabrielsson et al. reported the dyad photocatalyst in which a zinc(II) porphyrin and a fac-[Re(bpy)(CO)3Cl] (bpy = 2,2′-bipyridine) are connected by a saturated methylene spacer in the amide-bridge linker.10 This dyad catalyst attained a TONCO of 260 for CO production.11 Matlachowski et al. reported that the rigid xanthene-bridged dyad of a zinc(II) porphyrin and [Re(bpy)(CO)3Cl] showed a TONCO of 200.12 Despite those developments, one of the major issues is low TONCO because porphyrin photosensitizers have relatively less chemical stability; porphyrins tend to be decomposed by hydrogenation of the C=C bonds during photoirradiation.11,13

Recently, a dyad photocatalyst with direct connection of a zinc(II) porphyrin (ZnP) and a fac-[Re(phenanthroline)(CO)3Br] (ZnP-phen=Re, Figure 1)16,17 significantly improved the performance of ϕCO (=8%), TONCO (>1300), and CO selectivity (>99.9%). The long lifetime of the lowest triplet state (T1) allowed an efficient quenching with an electron donor, which exhibited a high Stern–Volmer constant Ksv = 180 000 M–1. It was hypothesized that one-electron-reduced ZnP efficiently transfers an electron to the Re catalyst moiety (phen=Re). This efficient electron transfer from the zinc(II) porphyrin to the Re catalyst likely suppresses the electron accumulation on the porphyrin because electron accumulation is known as a possible decomposition pathway of the porphyrin.11,13,16,18

Molecule structures of ZnP-phen=Re and ZnP.

Scheme 1 shows the initial process prior to the CO2 reduction reaction assumed in the previous work.17 After photoexcitation, the lowest excited singlet state (S1) is formed in ZnP (process 1), and S1 undergoes an intersystem crossing to T1 (process 2). T1 is quenched by 1,3-dimethyl-2-phenyl-2,3-dihydro-1H-benzo[d]imidazole (BIH),19 and a one-electron reduced species (OERS) is generated. The electron on ZnP moves to phen=Re (process 3). Those processes are followed by the catalytic cycle. In this dyad, it is expected that the two units are close enough to have an interaction with each other, which would affect the electron/structure properties. However, the properties of each process have never been quantitatively examined, such as the rate constant of each process or the structure and electron distribution in each excited state. In order to reveal the modulation in excited-state dynamics on the zinc(II) porphyrin in the presence of the connection to the rhenium complex and the loss process governing the total quantum efficiency, it is necessary to investigate each process at the molecular level in real time.

Predicted Mechanism on the Formation of OERS in the Photocatalytic CO2 Reduction Reaction Using ZnP-phen=Re

Time-resolved infrared spectroscopy (TR-IR) is an ideal tool for quantifying the multistep processes of CO2 photoreduction. TR-IR allows us to obtain the information on changes in the electron density of the central metal in a metal carbonyl complex by monitoring the CO stretching vibrations. We previously investigated the processes of Ru–Re behind the formation of the OERS and its reactivity using TR-IR in the presence of BIH and triethanolamine,5,20 and almost all the processes in Ru–Re CO2 photoreduction systems have been revealed.5,21,22 In the previous works, the OERS was generated via the two processes: (i) the reductive quenching of the excited ruthenium unit by BIH following intramolecular electron transfer from the Ru unit to the Re unit and (ii) the reduction of Ru–Re in the ground state by BI• produced by deprotonation in process (i). The time constants of processes (i) and (ii) were several tens of nanoseconds and several microseconds, respectively. It is essential to reveal how those processes were affected by the porphyrin-based systems.

In this paper, we investigated the initial processes of the CO2 photoreduction reaction of ZnP-phen=Re and the photophyical properties of ZnP for reference (Figure 1) using TR-IR as well as vis–NIR transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy and quantum chemical calculations.23 We succeeded in (1) observing the intersystem crossing with a time constant of ∼20 ps, which is more than 50 times faster than that of zinc(II) porphyrin alone, (2) detecting small red shifts of CO stretching vibration peaks in S1 and T1, indicating π–π* transition in ZnP and slight structural distortion, and (3) determining the two pathways for OERS generation in the presence of BIH reductatnt: the ∼100 ns reduction by BIH and the ∼3 μs reduction by BI•. Under a CO2 atmosphere, the same spectral changes were observed as those without CO2, indicating that the reaction between ZnP-phen=Re and CO2 occurs later than 1 ms. Our discovery disentangled the perturbation on the electronic properties of ZnP caused by the presence of the Re catalyst and vice versa.

Experimental Section

Materials

The complexes, ZnP-phen=Re and ZnP, were synthesized according to the previous report.16,17 The reductant, BIH, was synthesized according to the previous report.19 The solvent, N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMA), was purchased from Kanto Chemical Co.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The steady-state IR absorption spectra were measured using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Spectrum 3, wavenumber resolution ≈ 4 cm–1). The sample solutions were sealed in a home-built infrared cell equipped with BaF2 windows with an optical path length of 0.5 mm. The concentration of sample solutions was 0.3 mM in DMA.

Transient Absorption Spectroscopy (TA)

TA measurements were carried out by pump–probe methods. For the probe pulse, a visible region pulse (460–720 nm) was generated by focusing a part of the output of the Ti:sapphire regenerative amplifier (Spectra-Physics, Spitfire Ace, central wavelength = 800 nm, pulse duration ≈ 120 fs, repetition rate = 1 kHz, pulse energy = 3.5 mJ/pulse) on a sapphire crystal (3 mm thickness). For the pump pulse in the picosecond range up to 1 ns delay time, the 400 nm subpicoscond pulse was generated by second harmonic generation using a BBO (β-BaB2O4) crystal from the other part of the output of the amplifier. The fluence of the pump pulse at the sample position was 0.94 mJ/cm2. For the pump pulse after 1 ns delay time, the 410 nm nanosecond pulse was obtained from the optical parametric oscillator pumped by the third harmonic generation of a Nd:YAG laser (EKSPLA NT242, central wavelength = 1064 nm, pulse duration ≈ 3 ns, repetition rate = 1 kHz) synchronized with the amplifier using a picosecond delay generator (Stanford Research Systems, DG535). The polarization of the pump and probe pulses were set to the magic-angle configuration (∼54.7°). The probe pulse which passed through the sample solution was dispersed by a polychromator (JASCO, CT-10TP, 300 grooves/500 nm). The spectra were recorded using a multichannel detection system equipped with a CMOS sensor (UNISOKU, USP-PSMM-NP). The concentration of the sample solutions was 0.1 mM in DMA, and all measurements except for the CO2 reaction were conducted under nitrogen gas after bubbling with argon gas for 30 min. The absorbance at 400 and 410 nm for this sample was 0.36 and 0.6, respectively.

Time-Resolved Infrared Spectroscopy (TR-IR)

The experimental setup for the home-built pump–probe TR-IR measurement system has been reported previously.24−28 Briefly, a broadband mid-IR probe pulse (pulse duration ≈ 120 fs, bandwidth ≈ 150 cm–1, tunable range = 1000–4000 cm–1) was generated by difference frequency generation between the signal and idler pulses from the optical parametric amplifier (Lighconversion TOPAS-Prime) pumped by the regenerative Ti:sapphire amplifier (Spectra-Physics, Spitfire Ace). The same pump pulses as the TA measurements were used for the pump pulses before and after 1 ns delay time. The polarization angles of the pulse for the pump and probe were set to the magic angle (∼54.7°). The sample solutions were continuously circulated through a home-built infrared cell equipped with BaF2 windows with an optical path length of 0.5 mm. A probe pulse passed through an optical cell was dispersed by a 19 cm polychromator followed by detection using a 64-channel mercury–cadmium–telluride infrared detector array. The concentration of the sample solutions was 0.1 mM in DMA, and all measurements except for CO2 reaction were conducted under nitrogen gas after bubbling with argon gas for 30 min. The absorbance at 400 and 410 nm for this sample was 0.36 and 0.6, respectively.

Quantum Chemical Calculations

Quantum chemical calculations were performed using the Gaussian 16 package, revision C.01.29 Ground singlet states (S0), excited singlet states (S1), excited triplet states (T1), and doublet states (OERS) were calculated based on density functional theory (DFT), and the S1 state was calculated based on time-dependent (TD) DFT. We employed the LanL2DZ basis set and the ωB97XD functional. Infrared vibrational spectra were calculated based on the optimized geometries for each state. The vibrational spectra were appropriately scaled with the scaling factor to take into account the wavenumber shifts caused by anharmonicity.30

Global Analysis

The data obtained from TA and TR-IR were evaluated by the global analysis using the software Glotaran (version 1.5.1).31 We adopted sequential kinetic models for all of the data. EAS (evolution associated spectrum) represents a spectral component associated with each kinetic process.

Results and Discussion

Photophysical Properties in the Excited State

Transient Absorption Spectroscopy

To investigate the initial excited-state behavior in ZnP-phen=Re, we first discuss the TA spectra observed in the picosecond (ps) range. Figure 2a shows the TA spectra of ZnP-phen=Re in a DMA solution after photoexcitation at 400 nm. Immediately after photoexcitation at 0 ps, the excited-state absorptions (ESA) were observed at 500, 600, and 650 nm, and the ground-state bleach (GSB) was observed at 575 and 620 nm. We conducted the global analysis assuming a sequential model with three components (Figure 2b) and obtained the time constants of τEAS1 = 0.2 ± 0.1 ps, τEAS2 = 17 ± 1.3 ps, and τEAS3 > 1000. The fitting results of the global analysis at 500 and 575 nm are shown in Figure S1 as examples. These results show a better fit and demonstrate the validity of the model. We also conducted the nanosecond to microsecond (ns to μs) TA measurements and revealed that the lifetime of the long-lived species is approximately 100 μs (Figure S2).

(a) Temporal evolution of TA spectra in the ps range of ZnP-phen=Re in a 0.1 mM DMA solution after photoexcitation at 410 nm and (b) EASs and their time constants obtained from the global analysis of TA data.

We found that the dynamics observed in ZnP-phen=Re was unique compared to the typical dynamics in zinc(II) porphyrin systems. EAS2 and EAS3 in ZnP-phen=Re display the features corresponding to the TA spectrum of S1 and T1 in ZnP (Figure S3).32 Since quick internal conversion (IC) from S2 to S1 typically occurs in less than picoseconds in porphyrin dyad systems,9,33 we reasonably assigned EAS1, EAS2, and EAS3 to the S2, S1, and T1, respectively. Based on these assignments, the time constants of 0.2 ± 0.1 and 17 ± 1.3 ps are attributed to IC and intersystem crossing (ISC) from S1 to T1, respectively.32,34 Note that the ISC time constant of 17 ps is approximately 50 times faster than the typical ISC time constants in ZnP, 1–2 ns.35 We hypothesize that the acceleration of ISC is due to enhancement of the spin–orbit coupling by the heavy atom effect of the Re ion located near the ZnP.

Time-Resolved Infrared Spectroscopy (TR-IR)

The wavenumber of CO stretching vibrations in a metal carbonyl complex is known to be sensitive to the charge on the central metal of the complex.5,10,11,36,37 We measured the TR-IR spectra in the picosecond range in the CO stretching vibrational region, 1850–2050 cm–1, of ZnP-phen=Re (Figure 3a). Immediately after photoexcitation, GSBs were observed at the same wavenumbers as the peaks in the FT-IR spectra (1890, 1917, and 2019 cm–1, Figure S4), and ESAs were observed at 1860 and 2000 cm–1. These red shifts became smaller within 50 ps. We conducted global analysis assuming a sequential model with two components on the TR-IR data (Figure 3b). The fitting results of the global analysis at 2005 and 2020 cm–1 are shown in Figure S5 as examples. Since the time constant of EAS1 of 21 ± 2.1 ps was close to that of ISC from S1 to T1 estimated from the TA spectra (17 ± 1.3 ps), we assigned EAS1 and EAS2 to S1 and T1, respectively. IC from S2 to S1 could not be observed in the measurements due to the limitation of the instrumental response function (∼200 fs). We also conducted TR-IR in the nanosecond to μs range and confirmed that the lifetime of the EAS2 was longer than 10 μs (Figure S6).

Quantum Chemical Calculations and Spectral Simulations of TR-IR Spectra

Comparing TR-IR spectra to spectral simulations using quantum chemical calculations allows us to obtain a deeper understanding on excited-state characters in metal complexes.26,28 We conducted DFT calculations for S0 and TD-DFT calculations for S1 and T1 in ZnP-phen=Re. We first calculated the IR spectrum of the structure optimized in S0 and compared it to the FT-IR spectrum measured in a DMA solution in the range of 1850–2050 cm–1. We conducted the calculations using several combinations of a basis set and a functional and adopted the LanL2DZ basis set and the ωB97XD functional, which best reproduces the experimental FT-IR and TR-IR spectra (Figure S7). Since the calculated spectrum reasonably corresponds to the experimental spectrum, we discussed the molecular geometry based on the calculated structure. According to the optimized structure shown in Figure S8a, the dihedral angle between the ZnP plane and the phenanthroline (phen) planes is 102° (Figure S8b) and the distance between the Re ion and the Zn ion is 5.5 Å (Figure S8c), indicating that the Re ion is located just above the ZnP. This small distance between the Zn and Re ions must enhance the spin–orbit coupling and accelerate the ISC rate in ZnP-phen=Re.

Figure 4a shows the calculated difference IR spectrum between S1 and S0 and the TR-IR spectrum of S1 measured at 3 ps after photoexcitation in the CO stretching vibrational region of 1850–2050 cm–1. The calculated difference spectrum reproduces well the TR-IR spectrum, which ensures that the calculations are reliable enough to discuss the excited-state properties. The S1 optimized geometry shows that the dihedral angle between the ZnP and phen planes is 108°, which is slightly larger than that in S0. The natural transition orbitals (NTOs) of S1 shown in Figure 4c indicate that the transition is mainly attributed to the π–π* transition in ZnP with slight extension of the orbitals toward the phen. Figure 4b shows the calculated difference IR spectrum between T1 and S0 and the TR-IR spectrum of T1 measured at 1000 ps after photoexcitation. The NTOs of T1 show a π–π* transition like those of S1 (Figure 4c), and the dihedral angle between the ZnP and phen planes in the T1 geometry was 102°, indicating the similarity to the optimized geometry in S0. Because the dihedral angle corresponds to the degree of π-conjugation across the porphyrin unit and the phen ligands, we argue that the interaction between the two units in T1 gets weakened compared to the interaction at the optimized geometry in S1. This is consistent with the smaller peak shift in the CO spectrum observed in T1. The picture of S1 with π–π* excitation of ZnP in the dyad system is consistent with the fact that the emission from S1 can mainly be explained by the emission from ZnP with accrelated decay of ∼20 ps.38

(a) TR-IR spectra at 3 ps (red line) and calculated difference IR spectra between S1 and S0 (blue line). (b) TR-IR spectra at 1000 ps (red line) and calculated difference IR spectra between T1 and S0 (blue line). (c) NTOs for S1 and T1 at the optimized geometries of ZnP-phen=Re.

In general, the wavenumber shifts of the CO stretching vibrations of metal carbonyl complexes upon photoexcitation are thought to originate from the difference in charge on the central metal. However, in the case of ZnP-phen=Re, the wavenumber shift is presumed to result mainly from the difference in structure in the vicinity of the Re ion because there is little difference in NTOs on the Re ion between the electronic states. Concerning the origin of the difference in structure, we propose that these distinct behaviors of the S1 and T1 states are governed by the interplay between exchange interactions and the distribution of the electronic wave function. In the S1 state, electron delocalization is favored to reduce exchange interactions. This delocalization extends toward the phen moiety, enhancing π-conjugation with the ZnP moiety and resulting in an increased dihedral angle. Conversely, in the T1 state, electron localization is promoted to enlarge the exchange interactions. The electron density predominantly localized on the ZnP moiety, leading to a relatively unchanged dihedral angle. These structural adjustments in both states reflect the system response to the electronic requirements imposed by the different configurations.

Production and Properties of OERS

TA in the Presence of BIH

To explore the formation of the OERS, we conducted TA measurements of ZnP-phen=Re in the presence of an electron donor, BIH. Figure 5a shows the TA spectra in the ns−μs range of ZnP-phen=Re in a DMA solution with 0.05 M BIH after photoexcitation at 410 nm. At 0 ns, ESAs were observed around at 500, 600, and 650–700 nm and GSBs were observed at 575 and 630 nm. Because these ESAs and GSBs are almost the same as the spectrum observed in ZnP-phen=Re without BIH (Figure S2a), the TA spectrum at around 0 ns is assigned to the T1 state. Figure 5b compares the temporal profiles of TA signals at 500 nm in the presence and absence of BIH. A quick decay component with a time constant of approximately 1–2 μs, followed by a much slower decay component, was observed in the presence of BIH. In addition, the peak position of the ESA at 500 nm showed a red shift associated with the quick decay. This spectral evolution can be attributed to the electron reduction process of ZnP-phen=Re.

(a) Temporal evolution of TA spectra in the ns−μs range of ZnP-phen=Re in a 0.1 mM DMA solution with 0.05 M BIH after photoexcitation at 410 nm. (b) Temporal profiles of TA spectra at 500 nm with BIH (red line) and without BIH (black line). (c) EASs and their time constants obtained from global analysis of the TA spectra with BIH.

We conducted a global analysis of the TA spectra assuming a sequential model with two components (Figure 5c). Note that OERS is produced by two processes: reductive quenching of T1 by BIH and reduction of S0 by BI• produced via the deprotonation of BIH•+;19 thus, the two-component sequential model is a coarse model. The adoption of this phenomenological model was necessitated by the difficulty in distinguishing between the spectra of T1 and the spectra of the OERS due to their closely similar spectral profiles. Because the shape of EAS2 corresponds to that of electrochemically produced OERS,17 EAS1 and EAS2 are assigned to T1 and OERS, respectively. The time constant of generation of the OERS from these measurements was estimated to be 980 ± 90 ns; as mentioned above, this is an apparent value due to the coarse model. The experimental fact that the lifetime of T1 is ∼100 μs while the quenching time constant of T1 by BIH is ∼1 μs, indicating that almost all T1 are converted into OERS.39,40

TR-IR in the Presence of BIH

For a deeper understanding of the OERS generation processes, we measured the TR-IR spectra of ZnP-phen=Re in a DMA solution in the presence of 0.05 M BIH after photoexcitation at 400 nm (Figure 6a). At 0 ns, the two ESA were observed at 2007 and 1905 cm–1 and GSB was observed at 1890, 1925, and 2020 cm–1. The shape of the spectra at 0 ns corresponded to the T1 spectra in the ns−μs range of TR-IR measurements. Up to 1 μs, the ESA at 2007 cm–1 gradually red shifted to 1999 cm–1. A new ESA appeared at 1875 cm–1, and the intensity of GSB at 1925 cm–1 was increased. Based on the discussion of the TA spectra, this spectral change is attributed to the production process of OERS by BIH and the spectra after 1 μs were assigned to the OERS.

Quantum Chemical Calculations

DFT calculations were performed to thoroughly evaluate the properties of the one-electron reduced species observed by TA and TR-IR measurements. In the calculations, the open-shell UHF was used due to its charge being −1 and spin multiplicity being 2. Figure 7a shows the comparison of the experimental and calculated vibrational spectra, which were reasonably consistent. Figure 7b shows the electron distribution of a singly occupied molecular orbital (SOMO). In this result, the electron distribution spans ZnP and the phen part of phen=Re. According to the optimized geometries, the dihedral angle between ZnP and phen in the OERS structure was 126°, which is larger than the dihedral angle of 102° in S0. Regarding the dihedral angle, the electron wave functions at the meso position of ZnP and phen seem to be mixing (Figure 7b). This increases the tendency of the structure to become planar. However, due to steric hindrance, this likely results in an angle of 24°. This large difference in the dihedral angle between S0 and OERS presumably stabilizes OERS and makes its lifetime long enough to proceed the next step.

(a) TR-IR spectrum at 100 μs (red line) of ZnP-phen=Re with 0.05 M BIH and calculated difference IR spectra between OERS and S0 (blue line) in ZnP-phen=Re and (b) the electron distribution of the SOMO. It can be observed the electron wave functions are mixing in the meso position of ZnP.

Mulliken charge analysis was performed to examine the difference in charge density of S0 and the corresponding charge density of OERS in more detail, with the results presented in Table S2. In these data, the most negatively charged ligand is ZnP, and the next one is phen. As can be seen from SOMO, in the OERS, the electron is primarily located at the ZnP part and spans the phen part. The smaller amount of CO peak shifts in ZnP-phen=Re compared to previous reports is due to the electron being located at the ZnP part rather than the Re metal center.

Total Picture of the Initial Processes of CO2 Photoreduction

The initial process of ZnP-phen=Re after photoexcitation was clarified below (Scheme 2). Photoexcitation at 400 nm generates S2 of ZnP, followed by IC to S1 with a time constant of ∼0.2 ps. Subsequently, ISC from S1 to T1 occurs with the time constant of ∼20 ps, which is approximately 50 times faster than that of 1–2 ns observed in the pristine ZnP. The lifetime of T1 is ∼100 μs, indicating that T1 has a sufficiently long lifetime as a photosensitizer. Both S1 and T1 show the π–π* transition of ZnP with slight π-conjugation extension to phen resulting from the perturbation caused by docking the phen=Re catalyst block. With the addition of BIH, the OERS is generated by redutive quenching of T1 with a time constant of ∼100 ns. In parallel, the OERS is directly generated by the reduction of ZnP-phen=Re in the ground state by BI• with the time constant of ∼3 μs. The electron in the OERS is primarily located at the ZnP part and spans to the phen part.

Proposed Mechanism of the Initial Process in the Photocatalytic CO2 Reduction Reaction Using ZnP-phen=Re in a DMA Solution with 0.05 M BIH

From these results, we summarize the advantages of ZnP-phen=Re compared to other CO2 photoreduction dyad catalysts using a porphyrin sensitizer. First, due to the Re ion being close to ZnP, the ISC is accelerated and energy loss processes from S1 such as radiative and nonradiative deactivation were efficiently suppressed. Second, the electron transfer from ZnP to phen=Re occurs immediately after the reduction because of the π-conjugation of ZnP and phen. Third, OERS is stable because of the large difference in structure between S0 and the OERS. These advantages originate from the strong electronic coupling and the congested structure thanks to the direct connection between the photosensitizer part and the catalyst part. This characteristic also makes a difference in the initial processes from those of the Ru–Re systems.

Conclusion

We have investigated the process from photoexcitation to generation of the OERS in ZnP-phen=Re, which is an efficient CO2 photoreduction catalyst having zinc(II) porphyrin as a photosensitizer, using time-resolved spectroscopies, TR-IR and vis–NIR TA, and quantum chemical calculations. Immediately after photoexcitation, after a quick IC from S2 and S1 within 0.2 ps, the ISC from S1 to T1 occurs with the time constant of ∼20 ps. This fast ISC compared to general porphyrins originates from the acceleration by the heavy atom effect of the Re ion that is located just above ZnP, and suppresses the energy loss processes from S1. The charge distribution in the excited state is mostly localized on ZnP, that is, the π–π* transition in ZnP. The dihedral angle between ZnP and phen is slightly changed from S0 to S1 but that in T1 becomes closer to that in S0, which causes the wavenumber shift of the CO stretching vibrations in phen=Re. In the presence of BIH, OERS is generated via the two distinct paths: one is the reductive quenching of T1 by BIH with the time constant of ∼100 ns and the other is direct reduction of S0 by BI• with the time constant of ∼3 μs. The charge distribution in the OERS spans ZnP and phen, indicating that electron transfer occurs immediately after the reduction by BIH or BI•. The fact that the dihedral angle is rotated by 24° with respect to that of S0 would make the OERS stable. The measurement under CO2 implied that the next reaction process occurs later than 1 ms, which is the repetition time of the measurement. These characteristics of the initial process of CO2 photoreduction were revealed by the combination method of TR-IR, TA, and quantum chemical calculations. And, based on this knowledge, novel molecular designs of dyad catalysts having a porphyrin photosensitizer would be realized.
