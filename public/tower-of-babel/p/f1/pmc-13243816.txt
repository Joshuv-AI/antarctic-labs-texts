How to Foster Challenging Interdisciplinary Collaborations: Can Philosophy Support Neuroscientists?
Authors: M. Kunze, C. Brun, J. Badaut, M. Darnaudéry, F. Gross, L. Peltier, T. Pradeu, I. Sarto‐Jackson, J. P. Konsman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNew conceptual and technological developments bring neuroscientists closer to other disciplines and other fields in neuroscience with different traditions. Although some neuroscientists may underrate the potential benefits of successful interdisciplinary collaborations, others may be unaware of the typical difficulties of such collaborations or are not trained in skills that render them fruitful. Here, we argue that interdisciplinary interactions have long been part of neuroscience, although they are often challenging, because neuroscientists may be confronted with concepts, assumptions, and interpretative horizons that differ from their own. This can lead to misunderstandings and little mutual appreciation. Using the historical development of brain imaging techniques, we distinguish between different types of interdisciplinary interactions and illustrate some of their benefits. In addition, we present various challenges for collaborations at the interface between traditional laboratory‐type approaches and those of clinical or computational neuroscience or of ecological field approaches. To address these challenges, we invite neuroscientists to consider philosophers as collaboration partners with complementary expertise, which includes special consideration of language use, underlying assumptions and proficiency in conceptual analysis. This expertise can be used by neuroscientists to increase their understanding and address some difficulties in interdisciplinary interactions more effectively. The benefits of these interactions can be expected to outweigh challenges in the dialogue with philosophers. Importantly, neuroscientists can choose between reading philosophical literature, participating in joint events with philosophers, and integrating philosophers into neuroscience projects. This may allow neuroscientists to explore unforeseen possibilities to improve or initiate collaborations with scientists from other fields and disciplines.

Introduction

In the last few decades, important technological and conceptual advances have changed the landscape of neuroscience and extended the range of research questions that can be addressed. This progressively increased the methodological diversity of neuroscience, with novel observation and intervention techniques contributing to rapid progress in many fields, such as cellular and molecular neuroscience, electrophysiology, or cognitive neuroscience. As a result, today's neuroscience includes different research traditions that can be traced back to different historical developments of scientific investigations of the nervous system. Besides the initial fields of neuroanatomy, neurophysiology, neuroendocrinology, and neuropharmacology, and the medical disciplines of neurology and psychiatry, additional fields 1 such as behavioral neuroscience, parts of the cognitive sciences, computational and theoretical neuroscience, and others are now combined under the umbrella term “neurosciences” 2 (Cowan et al. 2000; Stahnisch 2016), which is accepted as an independent discipline and encompasses many fields. Thus, in their daily practice, neuroscientists can be confronted with collaboration partners trained in other traditions, with different research questions, basic assumptions, methodologies, and interpretative horizons or frameworks. Moreover, neuroscientists are also concerned with interdisciplinarity as broad research questions require collaboration with other scientific disciplines such as physics, psychology, or computer science. However, the concrete experience of scientists with interdisciplinary projects seems to show that the expected and advertised advantages of such collaborations are often not achieved. Indeed, misunderstandings, difficult communication, and a perceived limited true collaboration render interdisciplinary projects challenging (Waldman 2013; Hall et al. 2018; MacLeod 2018; Gohar et al. 2019; Mazzocchi 2019). Moreover, the meaning of “interdisciplinarity” is often ambiguous, and it is frequently met with resistance despite incentives to position one's work as “interdisciplinary” (Ledford 2015). At the interface between different fields of neuroscience, problems may arise that are similar to those encountered between neuroscience and other disciplines (Lélé and Norgaard 2005; Contreras Kallens et al. 2022). In this, fields are characterized mostly by social interactions of interest groups, whereas disciplines correspond to well‐defined academic structures with their own buildings and diploma‐granting powers. Such pitfalls are often underestimated by academic institutions or funding agencies that promote or support interdisciplinary projects without providing accompanying support. We propose that many research questions in contemporary neuroscience require a multiperspectival view and the integration of different levels of description. Thus, they typically benefit from interdisciplinary exchange and collaboration. To address challenges typical for such interactions, we invite neuroscientists to consider philosophers as helpful partners and interlocutors.

For that purpose, we first highlight that interdisciplinary collaborations have always been part of neuroscience and occurred in different forms (Section 2), then illustrate how typical challenges make such interactions often complicated (Section 3), and finally suggest that philosophers can support neuroscientists in such collaborations because of their complementary proficiencies (Section 4). Importantly, neuroscientists can benefit from philosophical contributions in different ways (Section 5).

Different Forms of Interdisciplinarity in Neuroscience

In the 20th century, progress in understanding in neuroscience was often achieved by addressing novel and complex questions from the perspective of different disciplines, and biologists, physicians, chemists, and physicists often collaborated in interdisciplinary ways. Moreover, method development in neuroscience has often taken advantage of interdisciplinary collaborations (e.g., the development patch‐clamp recordings, Verkhratsky and Parpura 2014, or brain imaging as described below). These collaborations also helped to challenge conceptual one‐sidedness or biased interpretations and offered opportunities for better or more relevant controls. Overall, this allowed for broadening of the discipline‐specific scope and resulted in a more complete picture on the phenomenon of interest.

In neuroscience, interdisciplinarity occurs in many flavors because its historical development united parts of different disciplines into neuroscience as a novel and independent one. Thus, today's neuroscience comprises various fields with different origins, in which different research traditions continue to live. Consequently, exchange or collaboration between members of these fields (interfield collaborations) in many aspects resembles those of interdisciplinary collaborations.

Here, the term “interdisciplinary collaboration” will be used as an umbrella term to refer to interdisciplinary and interfield collaborations to address these ambiguities; we first specify our understanding of interdisciplinarity to distinguish it from other forms of interactions neuroscientists can be engaged in (Section 2.1), then specify various forms of interdisciplinary interactions to illustrate their diversity in neuroscience (Section 2.2), and finally use the development of brain imaging as a historic illustration of how interdisciplinary collaborations and challenges advanced the understanding of this method and clarified methodical limitations to excessive interpretations (Section 2.3).

Descriptions of Interdisciplinarity

Without a doubt, many neuroscientists already interact with scientists from other fields or disciplines, for example, when they interact with members of the statistics department to take advice, to find the most appropriate statistical approach, or to implement new kinds of data analysis. In such cases, the neuroscientist will typically accept the domain‐specific “authority” of the statistician and the proposed solution regarding the analysis. In contrast, neuroscientists can also participate in teams composed of scientists from different fields or disciplines, who jointly address research questions for which no discipline alone has sufficient expertise and in which all parties benefit from mutual exchange and interactions. Only the latter type of interactive collaboration is considered “interdisciplinary” here. Even though the concept of interdisciplinarity is often not made clear (Gozzer 1982; Choi and Pak 2006; Alvargonzalez 2011), the supposed relevance and expected benefits of interdisciplinarity are widely advertised (Jacobs and Frickel 2009; MacLeod et al. 2019). Much has already been written about interdisciplinarity in general (for summary, see Huutoniemi et al. 2010), but in this article, we restrict the use of the term “interdisciplinary” to projects or collaborative activities that involve scientists from different fields or disciplines and exceed mere service‐providing. These collaborations may be driven by a common interest in a technological application or research question or by a better understanding of a complex phenomenon through mutual exchange between fields or disciplines (Choi and Pak 2006; Mazzocchi 2019). Accordingly, in interdisciplinary approaches, as we understand them, scientists from different disciplines or fields join their domain‐specific skills and knowledge to advance the shared understanding of a phenomenon by interaction and exchange, which in turn can reveal misconceptions and biases in disciplinary perspectives. In this sense, interdisciplinary approaches differ from multidisciplinary and transdisciplinary 3 approaches, and distinguishing properties of interdisciplinarity are prolonged and active interactions, along with complementarity of approaches.

Different Forms of Interdisciplinarity

Next, we suggest that interdisciplinary exchange and collaboration can play out in different forms and describe how these forms differ. We take the establishment of brain imaging techniques for neuroscientific research as a historical example to illustrate that neuroscience has long been interdisciplinary. These methods are well‐established tools today, but their implementation in neuroscience required important technical and conceptual developments that were facilitated and sometimes even made possible by interdisciplinary research. Moreover, these examples can help neuroscientists recognize similar examples of interdisciplinary interactions in their own practice.

Methodological Interdisciplinarity

The development of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) from nuclear magnetic resonance was, in hindsight, a masterpiece of cooperation between physicists, chemists, engineers, and physicians to repurpose and further develop a technique that originated in analytical chemistry for advanced medical diagnosis and research (Leach 2004; Wehrli 2004). Following general distinctions of different forms of interdisciplinarity suggested before (Huutoniemi et al. 2010), this type of collaboration can be described as methodological interdisciplinarity, because different methods or approaches are combined in a novel, integrated manner, rather than being merely juxtaposed (Huutoniemi et al. 2010, 84; Table 1). The aim of this form of interdisciplinarity can be to progressively develop methods for novel purposes (e.g., for medical diagnosis and research) and typically requires interactions between developers and users. Methods converting nuclear magnetic resonance signals into images of the structural properties of a brain in a living organism were initially validated by comparison to postmortem anatomical planes (Bobinski et al. 2000; Osechinskiy and Kruggel 2009). A subset of these techniques has been labeled functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) as the signals reflect localized changes in brain physiology during neuronal activation (Raichle 2009). This subsequent application of MRI to study the functioning brain was, among others, made possible by a joint effort between engineers, radiologists, and cognitive psychologists (Bandettini et al. 1992; Kwong et al. 1992). Thus, methodological interdisciplinarity develops novel methods, which allow for the exploration of domains in previously impossible ways (e.g., having a means to assess activation of brain structures in a non‐invasive way in humans) and may initiate new collaborations.

Empirical Interdisciplinarity

The technological progress obtained with fMRI techniques allowed measurements with reasonable temporal and spatial resolution for the study of brain correlates of cognitive, physiological, and pharmacological processes and for diagnostic use (Matthews et al. 2006) and fostered interactions between neuroscientists and cognitive scientists. In this type of interdisciplinary interaction, the research question (e.g., correlations between brain activity and mental processes) is jointly approached by several disciplines, and progress in understanding is achieved at the interface, requiring contributions from both sides. This kind of interdisciplinarity is close in spirit to empirical interdisciplinarity in which different types of empirical findings are integrated to gain more insight into how phenomena observed in different fields can be linked (Huutoniemi et al. 2010). The expected benefit of empirical interdisciplinarity is gained from integrating and relating different types of factors or levels (e.g., mental processes and increased activity of brain areas during a task). This should allow for a broader consideration of the phenomenon of interest and increased understanding.

Conceptual Interdisciplinarity

Finally, the advancement in imaging techniques was accompanied by progressive changes in conceptual frameworks presupposed in the application of advanced fMRI techniques for specific scientific and medical purposes. This form of interdisciplinarity has been termed conceptual or theoretical interdisciplinarity and can include a comparison of concepts, models, or theories across disciplines and fields (Huutoniemi et al. 2010). This can result in mutual correction, cross‐fertilization, or the establishment of new relationships between disciplines and fields and new research strategies. Conceptual changes are often essential for employing new technologies and repurposing applications established for one research question to another type of scientific investigation. For example, when scientists realized the possibility to observe brain activity by fMRI while cognitive functions are being exerted, this inspired some changes in the research questions being investigated, putting more emphasis on “thinking, consciousness, and social cognition” and complementing the cognitive‐centered perspective by a brain‐centered one (Sutterer and Tranel 2017). Moreover, the reinterpretation of spontaneous low‐frequency fluctuations in the absence of explicit tasks or stimuli as “background activity” instead of “noise” led to the systematic investigation of functional connectivity of parts of the brain by resting state fMRI (Wei et al. 2024). Thus, another benefit of conceptual interdisciplinarity is to challenge or modify categories, concepts, or notions or the relationships between those, which can then be explored experimentally.

Overall, these different forms of interdisciplinarity focus on developing novel methods, a multiperspectival view on a phenomenon, or the integration of different types of concepts, theories, or explanations, even though a clear‐cut separation between the types is often not possible.

Joint Interdisciplinary Contributions to the Study of Human Cognitive Abilities by fMRI

To underpin our claim that interdisciplinary interactions give rise to interesting novel questions or challenge even well‐established methods or concepts, we describe examples of concrete contributions of fMRI as a tool to study the relation between brain activities and complex cognitive functions. The availability of fMRI measurements opened new opportunities at the interface between cognitive science and biological neuroscience. However, the high expectations were also met with reservations and resistance, with challenges often originating from different background assumptions regarding how the brain functions.

In this context, diverging perspectives of other research traditions challenged established concepts (Section 2.3.1) and methodological assumptions (Section 2.3.2) of brain imaging at the time. Providing a short overview, we illustrate that in the development of brain imaging techniques in general and fMRI in particular, external critiques had their share in challenging strategies and concepts (Thagard 2009).

Clarifying the Relationship Between Local and Global Brain Activity at Different Levels of Description

A first debate readdressed the questions of if and where perception and cognitive processes are located in the brain and whether localized activity changes revealed by fMRI measurements reflect such processes. Most fMRI approaches seem to assume that some regions of the brain are particularly important for the ability of interest and thus expect that strong changes in regional brain activity are found during the exertion of the ability. This assumption seemed intuitive, because the general idea that cognitive functions are exerted by specific and restricted brain regions can be traced back to the 19th century when local brain damage was linked to specific deficits (e.g., of language production by Paul Broca) (Finger 2001). Thus, the results of initial fMRI studies were interpreted to support such brain localizationist theories. However, another tradition of the 19th century claimed that the entire brain is equally important for individual functions (equipotentiality) and had supporters in the 20th century (Lashley 1950; Finger 2001). This tradition promoted the idea that cognitive processes require widespread activity in the brain and proposed that understanding of how a certain cognitive function is made possible requires considering the whole brain (or at least large portions of it) (holism) (Mundale 2002; McCaffrey 2023). Such emphasis on the relevance of widespread activation across the brain during the performance of certain cognitive functions appeared less compatible with initial findings of fMRI studies. However, over time, the idea that widespread activation of brain networks across the entire brain is highly relevant for the performance of certain cognitive functions has gained some traction (Sutterer and Tranel 2017; Duffau 2018; Arshavsky 2023). Scientists supporting the latter tradition have argued that the conclusion that a brain structure is sufficient for a function cannot be drawn from temporal correlations between some cognitive processes and local increases in brain activity but that such claims require invoking other types of findings (such as the functional consequences of lesions of that brain structure) (Sutterer and Tranel 2017; Duffau 2018).

This debate about the way the brain functions and a more appropriate interpretation of fMRI data received input from other disciplines as well, namely, from cognitive and computational sciences asking whether information processing is computed in a modular way or addressing the question of whether the architecture of cognition is modular.

At the level of information processing, cognitive scientists first suggested that computation in the brain can be understood as linear processing that can use independent computational units, whereas later concepts referred to parallel distributed processes 4 and artificial neural networks 5 to argue for computation at the level of the entire brain (Clayden 2013). This focused on the dynamics of network activity motivated the application of network theory in cognitive science and neuroscience. To better understand the relation between brain activity and brain circuitry, concepts were developed that combined functional integration and functional specialization as two fundamental principles of functional organization underlying information processing (Friston 1994, 2002). These principles were related to activity patterns observed by brain imaging and Friston referred to “localizationism” and “[dis]connectionism” as a historical foundation of his concepts. Thus, descriptions can be made at the level of material connections (connectome), brain activity (patterns), and functional interactions between brain areas. Consequently, the temporal correlation between remote neurophysiological events as measured by brain imaging was distinguished from the influence that one neural system exerts over another and describes different aspects of activity patterns that are obtained as signals by fMRI (Friston 2002).

Another input was driven by questions such as if cognitive capacities can be considered composite of independent elements that can be affected independently, if such elements could be functionally connected, modified and newly combined during evolution, and if such elements could be performed by localized neuronal circuits in individual brain areas (Carruthers 2006; Croston et al. 2015). Although some scholars used fMRI data to claim separability of such elements, critics argued that fMRI data also provided evidence for large interindividual differences in brain activity patterns, which exceed regional borders and that localized brain activity presents with some plasticity upon functional damage (Prinz 2006). Interestingly, in addition to this debate in cognitive neuroscience a similar debate occurred in philosophy of mind with some parties postulating that at least some functions of the mind itself are modular, which could be encapsulated and anatomically localized, and independent of higher level cognitive processes (e.g., Fodor 1983). If one can accept that the brain is closely‐linked to the mind, this further supported brain localizationist theories from a more conceptual point of view and some neuroscientists using brain imaging referred to Fodor (e.g., Wojciulik et al. 1998; Coltheart 1999; Kanwisher 2010; Bertolero et al. 2015). In contrast, neuroanatomical and electrophysiological data were used by philosophers to argue against the criteria proposed by Fodor (Prinz 2006), whereas some neuroscientists and philosophers alike argued for the modularity of the mind (Keane 2018), and the modularity of the mind hypothesis (Mason et al. 2008; Sutton and Schier 2014). A mutual influence of neuroscience and philosophy could be considered conceptual interdisciplinarity, when the insights of one discipline influenced the interpretation of results in another discipline.

Interpretation of Results Obtained by In Vivo Brain Imaging

The second example concerns the relationship between fMRI signals obtained by blood oxygen level‐dependent (BOLD) nuclear magnetic resonance measurements and images of brain activity, which are generated as output of functional brain imaging experiments. The interpretation of the relation between neuronal activity and fMRI signal has not been straightforward to establish. Limitations to a direct relation between brain activity and metabolism, based on the relationship between neural activity and increased cerebral blood flow, have been discussed early on (Roy and Sherrington 1890) and, later, regarding glucose metabolism (Sokoloff 1977). However, a better understanding of the relationship between raw fMRI data and their interpretation was made possible by contributions in which methodological interdisciplinarity involving electrophysiologists played an important role. Over time, it has become clear that changes in oxygen consumption reflected by the BOLD signal cannot be used as a direct proxy for neuronal activity, because no linear relationship with electrophysiological activity exists, and the course of temporal and spatial changes of the BOLD signal does not solely reflect local electrophysiological processes (Lauritzen 2005; Logothetis 2008; Theriault et al. 2023). These systematic investigations integrating different perspectives revealed that the simple interpretations of neuronal metabolism underlying BOLD brain imaging needed to be nuanced and gave rise to the development of advanced approaches to distinguish neuronal from vascular effects (Tsvetanov et al. 2021) and to statistical and conceptual improvements (Elliott et al. 2021) of fMRI measurements. Moreover, it became increasingly clear how difficult it is to establish whether an fMRI signal reflects function‐specific processing or neuromodulatory changes (Gusnard et al. 2001; Szameitat et al. 2011) and how challenging it is to relate an fMRI signal to excitatory or inhibitory processes (Logothetis 2008). Altogether, these developments changed the interpretation of fMRI measurements in cognitive neuroscience because they modified the original interpretation of fMRI signals. On the one hand, this was an important methodical refinement, but on the other hand, it also changed the underlying assumption of these measurements, which, in turn, changed the interpretation of results.

Consequences of Interdisciplinary Collaborations for fMRI Measurements

These lines of critique have led some to doubt that real progress has been made by this type of fMRI measurements, and some authors have argued that the relationships between mind and brain are too complex to be addressed by formulating questions from a single perspective (Cooper and Shallice 2010). However, these challenges were in the end beneficial for neuroscience because they urged neuroscientists and cognitive scientists to specify the questions more clearly so that they can be addressed by fMRI in the context of cognitive neuroscience (Coltheart 2013; Mather et al. 2013) and to use fMRI more specifically to discriminate between alternative models by formulating hypotheses about the expected outcomes (Price 2018). Moreover, these discussions led to further improvement of approaches (Poldrack et al. 2017; Elliott et al. 2021; Peelen and Downing 2023). In summary, the establishment and further developments in brain imaging techniques have only been possible due to the contribution of many scientists from diverse disciplinary origins and research fields working together in interdisciplinary ways (Bandettini 2012). This overview illustrates how interdisciplinary collaborations can be important for the development of novel methods and support the claim that interdisciplinary collaborations have always been part of neuroscience.

Of note, similar collaborations occur in contemporary neuroscience as well. As neuroscience shares many assumptions with other disciplines of the natural sciences (e.g., physics and chemistry), methodological and empirical interdisciplinarity between neuroscientists and other natural scientists occurs more regularly than with other disciplines. Some of these collaborations have been indispensable for the invention of novel technologies that are highly important for neuroscience today, such as stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy (Muller et al. 2012; Vicidomini et al. 2018), single cell sequencing (Boulgakov et al. 2020; Piwecka et al. 2023), further developments of modern brain imaging techniques, extending those discussed above (Philiastides et al. 2021; Vizioli et al. 2021; Stangl et al. 2023), advanced multielectrode recordings (Chen et al. 2021; Urai et al. 2022), or advanced mass‐spectrometric analyses (e.g., of the lipid compositions of brain parts) (Xu and Li 2019; Yoon et al. 2022). However, neuroscience also has some similarities with the social sciences regarding empirical approaches (e.g., systematic investigation of cohorts or interventions), and neuroscientists can be involved in empirical interdisciplinarity, whereas its overlap with disciplines of the humanities (e.g., philosophy) appears rather low, collaborations occur rarely and remain mostly at the level of conceptual interdisciplinarity. Altogether, different forms of interdisciplinary exchange and collaborations can complement limitations of established approaches in different research traditions within neuroscience, aid the spread of better methods between scientific communities (Smaldino and O'Connor 2022) and provide a more complete picture of phenomena of interest, because diverging perspectives can complement each other (Brignol et al. 2024).

Difficulties in Interdisciplinary Exchange as an Obstacle for Collaborations in Today's Neuroscience

Contemporary neuroscience offers many opportunities to engage in and benefit from interdisciplinary collaborations and projects. However, in practice, interdisciplinary‐type collaborations can be challenging and do not occur frequently. Supposedly, interdisciplinary collaborations are not very attractive for many practically working neuroscientists despite high praise for interdisciplinarity and promises of its benefits. This reservation can be caused by the scientists' own experience in previous interactions with scientists from other disciplines, which either have not been as fruitful as expected, remained in a sort of service‐providing state, or were very challenging because of misunderstandings and the lack of perceived equal partnership. Some of these complications and dissatisfaction with interdisciplinarity in neuroscience have been described recently (Wudarczyk et al. 2021; Brignol et al. 2024; Ibanez et al. 2024; Linsley et al. 2025). Such experiences, supposedly, lower positive expectations of interesting and fruitful interdisciplinary collaborations, which, in turn, reduce the motivation to initiate and establish new interdisciplinary collaborations or projects because the time and energy spent on these activities may not seem worth it. Moreover, for some other disciplines like those from the social sciences or the humanities, the gap to neuroscience appears so wide that neuroscientists are reluctant to enter discussions, exchange, or active collaborations, even though this might reveal interesting aspects. Thus, neuroscientists can be expected to benefit from a better understanding of some typical challenges frequently observed in interdisciplinary collaborations that enable them to evaluate potential and risks of interdisciplinary collaborations to be established and to consider them during the implementation of such collaborations. For that purpose, we first summarize some problems frequently observed in interdisciplinary collaborations from the literature that can be related to situations in neuroscience (Section 3.1) and then illustrate how underlying differences between fields of neuroscience may render collaborations difficult from a practice‐oriented perspective (Section 3.2). 6

Problems of Interdisciplinary Collaborations in Neuroscience

To better understand why successful and fruitful interdisciplinary collaborations are difficult to put into practice and why the promised benefits of interdisciplinarity often cannot be accessed, we summarize here some challenges that have been observed in interdisciplinary collaborations. For a first orientation, we refer to Lélé and Norgaard (2005) who list the following challenges for interdisciplinary collaborations in the life‐sciences (Figure 1, central column) and add examples from neuroscience between brackets 7 : (1) differences in values that are—often unrecognized—associated with a particular study, such as the choice of questions, theoretical positions, variables, and the styles of research (e.g., neuroscientists can disagree about the relevance of mechanistic understanding underlying the BOLD signal, because the searches for differences between brain activity patterns can be informative independently of the underlying mechanisms, whereas claims of the mechanisms of information transfer between brain regions make stronger assumptions about the mode of information transfer); (2) differences in theories or explanatory models including their underlying assumptions when studying the same phenomenon (e.g., disagreement in the explanatory power of fMRI data can originate from different interpretations what a difference in the fMRI activity pattern between patients and healthy individuals reflects, when some scientists use these abnormal brain states either for diagnosis whereas others use it for mechanistic explanations. In the first case, the abnormalities reflect characteristic deviations somewhat independently of etiology, whereas in the second case, these abnormalities serve as bridge between a genetic basis and the symptoms of the patients); (3) epistemic differences including differences in methods, notions of adequate proof, and other fundamental assumptions (e.g., fields of neuroscience differ in their appreciations of correlations or in their preference for detailed investigations of individual patients compared to large scale comparisons requiring sophisticated statistical analyses); and (4) differences that are external to science and find their origin in the way in which society interacts with and organizes academia, which affects the relationship between disciplines and fields and thus interdisciplinary research (e.g., emphasizing neuroscience with the “decade of the brain” [1990] or providing important funding such as for the “blue brain project” [2005] can have detrimental effects on other interdisciplinary projects by fostering a perception of unjust preferential treatment).

Challenges for interdisciplinary collaborations: Three of the four major challenges for interdisciplinary collaborations have been described previously by Lele and Norgaard (central column). These challenges originate in part from different research traditions (left column), which give rise to conceptual differences, different epistemic cultures, and language use, and can foster attitudes or behavior that can negatively affect social interactions. Moreover, these challenges can foster negative attitudes towards interdisciplinary collaborations that are detrimental for initiating, establishing and implementing these collaborations (right column). The negative attitudes also contribute to maintenance of separated research traditions (red arrow). The fourth challenge, societal forces that are external to science and find their origin in the way in which society interacts with and organizes academia, is not linked to the research traditions in the same way.

It is revealing that most of these challenges refer to differences in concepts, assumptions and interpretations that underlie concrete scientific practice. Accordingly, it has been suggested that some of the problems in interdisciplinary exchange are related to the way in which their scientific activities are structured around conceptual and methodological frameworks (MacLeod 2018). Moreover, values, theories, underlying assumptions, and epistemic preferences are rarely made explicit in scientific practice, even though they shape the way research is planned and performed (Lélé and Norgaard 2005; MacLeod 2018). Thus, field‐specific practices remain opaque to outsiders (Brown et al. 2015; MacLeod 2018), because little effort is made to make assumptions accessible to scientists from other disciplines or fields.

In what follows, we discuss potential causes for the challenges described above (Figure 1, left column) and explore their detrimental consequences for interdisciplinary collaborations (Figure 1, right column) from the perspective of contemporary neuroscience. The beneficial effects of interdisciplinary collaborations are often explained by complementary approaches, which would compensate for inherent limitations and biases of individual fields or disciplines (Darden and Maull 1977). However, such approaches are often embedded in different research traditions from which they cannot easily be disentangled. These traditions reflect alternative ways to address research questions, describe phenomena, and methodological preferences that have proven successful. Moreover, these research traditions can also differ in the way knowledge is produced and validated in practice, and in the way, this knowledge is shared (e.g., how investigations are performed, research questions are studied, and results are published and thus how results contribute to explanations and understanding). These aspects have been studied for physics and molecular biology and designated “epistemic cultures” (Knorr‐Cetina 1999). Indeed, different fields of contemporary neuroscience can also be described as independent research traditions with different historical origins and epistemic cultures (Choudhury 2010). Neuroscientists from different fields can interact in various ways with their study objects or tools (e.g., investigating laboratory animals and interacting with human patients or computers), act in different social contexts (e.g., a hospital, a laboratory, or long‐term field observations), and use different formats of publishing (e.g., case‐studies, studies describing interventions, or reports on field observations).

Moreover, these research traditions typically correspond to different scientific communities that remain separated from other research traditions to a certain extent. This separation can reinforce the establishment of shared concepts, assumptions, and interpretations and make a field internally more homogenous. However, this social separation is not always apparent and a neuroscientist and a collaboration partner from another field may consider themselves as part of the same broad neuroscience community but, in fact, belong to rather independent communities with different thinking traditions and styles.

Finally, we want to point out specifically that prolonged social separation is often linked to different language use, which, in turn, can be expected to contribute to difficulties in interdisciplinary exchange. For example, misunderstanding between neuroscientists can occur when one scientist uses the term “brain activity” to refer to some metabolic activity in a certain brain area, whereas the other scientist refers to information processing at the level of computing. Another interesting example for a disagreement about terminology and underlying concepts between two fields of neuroscience (computational and biologically oriented neuroscience) was a discussion of whether neuronal networks in vitro can show intelligence and sentience (Kagan et al. 2022; Balci et al. 2023). Such “language problems” can be particularly detrimental when they impede communication and thus negatively affect joint activities such as planning of experiments or interpreting of results (e.g., in the context of the Human Brain Project (Aicardi and Mahfoud 2024)). However, language and concepts are typically generated, established, and developed further by shared communicative practice within a field, whereas questions of adequate terminology often receive less attention. In summary, any separation of fields or disciplines that is fostered by different origins, research traditions or social separation can be expected to have negative effects for interdisciplinary collaborations.

Social separation together with the challenges described above can also give rise to attitudes and behaviors that impede interdisciplinary collaborations. A lack of mutual understanding can result in a low appreciation of alternative approaches when they are perceived as less appropriate concepts, less reliable strategies, or less rigorous forms of investigation, even though the underlying differences just reflect prioritizing another subset of factors, other compromises between ideal and possible, or a focus on a different level of explanation. Indeed, each partner can foster an attitude of overestimation of one's own research tradition and low appreciation for other fields because of insufficient acquaintance with or prejudice against other research traditions. As a consequence, the benefits of interdisciplinary collaborations are underestimated, and collaborators are not treated as equal partners but as service providers. A study on computational neuroscience and systems biology as fields with similarity in the use of computation (e.g., in modeling) described these as rather separated in social terms (de Schutter 2008). Overall, we think that the relevance of the potentially detrimental effects of separated research traditions on interdisciplinary collaborations is widely underestimated in neuroscience.

Examples of Complicated Interfield Relations in Neuroscience

But how do the problems described above in general terms affect neuroscientists, when they are involved in interdisciplinary collaborations? To illustrate this, we consider the relationships between two sets of fields in contemporary neuroscience that are often perceived as separated and for which interdisciplinary collaborations could be advantageous. In the first example, three fields of neuroscience have a widely overlapping understanding of their study objects, but diverge in their research questions and goals, which, in turn, result in differences in some basic assumptions and a prioritization of some causal factors. In the second example, we discuss the relation between two fields with different historical origins that remained rather separated for a relatively long period, thus resulting in rather different concepts of the research object and the most relevant level of description. These descriptions can also be interpreted as possible explanations for why these fields appear separated today.

Challenging Relations Between Fields of Neuroscience Applying Laboratory‐Type Investigations, Ecological Field Approaches, or Developing Clinical Applications

Even among empirically oriented fields of neuroscience such as laboratory‐type neuroscience, clinical neuroscience, and neuroscience of wild‐living animals (sometimes referred to as ecological neuroscience; Bucci‐Mansilla et al. 2021), different assumptions, methodological preferences, and interpretative horizons seem to exist. Without a doubt, working in a “wet lab” context is often linked to a special framework with assumptions and priorities that are not always easy to reconcile with those of studies emphasizing the importance of a complex ecological environment or clinical practice. To illustrate this, we first describe characteristics of laboratory‐type experiments and those of clinical and ecological neuroscience, to then pinpoint some important differences and highlight some of the above‐mentioned challenges together with potential problems for collaborations. Keeping these in mind, some possible collaborative interactions are sketched.

Laboratory‐Type Investigations to Reduce Various Sources of Variability

At the beginning of the 21st century, mainstream neuroscience widely studied cultured cells (e.g., neurons), brain slices, and model organisms under well‐defined conditions and with standardized tests. This type of approach, which resembles “biological neuroscience” in opposition to cognitive science (Gold and Stoljar 1999), aims at identifying parts, activities, and mechanisms (e.g., proteins, their enzymatic activities, or processes involving many of these entities) to obtain a better understanding of their role in brain function and behavior. The power of this approach is related to reducing complexity by investigating parts (in cultured cells and brain slices), minimizing interindividual differences by lowering the influence of genetic background and environmental variability, and aiming at higher reproducibility by tightly controlling experimental conditions. Overall, this approach can thus reach a high level of internal validity or validity of a causal claim in a controlled setting. These approaches can be summarized by the reduction of complexity and variability, and the undeniable success of such “reductionistic approaches” has occasionally led to their equation with the scientific method (Glasgow 2008). However, these approaches primarily reflect a set of underlying assumptions, preferences, and interpretations, which have obvious strengths but suffer from a questionable generalizability of findings to all populations living in various conditions (Campbell 1957; Jimenez‐Buedo and Miller 2010; Dirnagl et al. 2022). Consequently, a certain disregard of, and lower appreciation for, other types of investigations may prevail in these fields. Thus, fields favoring this “reductionistic approach” research style may be considered typical for a group with its own framework (e.g., concept or methods), which demarcates it from other disciplines or fields, and with a certain epistemic culture (Knorr‐Cetina 1999).

Ecological Field Approaches

Other approaches emphasize the importance of studying an organism's behavior and related brain processes in a natural environmental context to investigate specific research questions (e.g., in ecological field approaches; Costa and Sinervo 2004; Kihlstrom 2021). Such approaches focus on the modulatory role of complex and ecologically relevant environments and on the correspondence between ecologically relevant phenotypes and the living conditions of a species.

For example, the physiological response to hypoxia can be investigated by studying specific human populations, such as Tibetans, living in high altitude for millennia, or animal species, such as elephant seals that dive deep, and can thus be expected to have developed evolutionary adaptations to the hypoxic conditions of their natural habitats (Murray et al. 2018; Piot et al. 2023). Obviously, such approaches differ from studies of the detrimental effects of hypoxia on mammalian brains in laboratory environments, which are often investigated in rodents by triggering hypoxia artificially to mimic disease states (e.g., arterial occlusion or stroke models), by inactivating specific genes of interest or by the application of certain chemicals. Ecological field approaches allow for a better understanding of the physiological role of, and the evolutionary context in which, certain mechanisms have emerged in a species living in habitats, which cannot be explored in model organisms. The study of physiologically effective systems that protect organisms living in their natural environments provides highly valuable information. However, such ethological approaches cannot take advantage of the typical tools used in “reductionistic approaches,” and thus, they are often limited by the variability of different study objects and the lack of internal controls. But, scientists in this tradition often weigh these limitations as less important and may have a low appreciation for studies that were performed outside the normal habitat of the species of interest. Although ecological and reductionist approaches pursue different goals, namely external validity or relevance and internal validity or reproducibility, respectively (Sullivan 2009), joint research projects could implement different forms of interdisciplinarity. On the one hand, drawing on the experience from laboratory research, new tools to investigate free‐living animals (e.g., elephant seals, Kendall‐Bar et al. 2022, or monkeys, Testard et al. 2024) could be developed as a form of methodological interdisciplinarity. On the other hand, the results obtained in animals living in their natural habitat, which may be too extreme for other organisms to live in, might compensate for the limitations of studies in lab‐raised model organisms (Piot et al. 2023), and thus foster empirical interdisciplinarity. So although historically ecological and reductionist approaches have developed in opposite directions, technological improvements could enable future interdisciplinary research projects.

Clinical Neuroscience

Experiments in translational neuroscience aim at a better understanding of pathophysiological processes and at improving patient care. However, in this research domain, the generalizability of results obtained in model organisms or relatively small groups of patients need not hold up under conditions encountered in the general population (Fernandez 2013; Kympouropoulos 2023). Moreover, findings considered relevant for humans in general may come into tension with the application to individual patients, and the priority of their successful therapy may question advice exclusively based on “reductionistic approaches” that aim at reducing interindividual variability. In this respect, beneficial therapeutic options for a relatively small (sub)group of patients can be considered real and valuable progress (e.g., Villéga et al. 2024). Such observations may depend on specific environmental conditions, a specific subpopulation of patients, or a combination of different factors that may be difficult to come across in clinical trials or laboratory conditions. Hence, it may be harder for such approaches to meet the gold standards of a randomized clinical trial or a meta‐analysis of evidence‐based medicine, although in some domains of clinical neuroscience (e.g., psychiatry), this problem is occasionally addressed by stratification methods (Hampel et al. 2023). Nonetheless, considering and incorporating clinical observations into laboratory practice and corroborating results in model organisms should have the potential to further improve “bench‐to‐bedside” research by also transferring information from ‘bedside‐to‐bench’ (van der Laan and Boenink 2015). This type of interdisciplinarity could excel in empirical interdisciplinarity between clinicians (e.g., neurologists or psychiatrists) and experimental neuroscientists but could also give rise to theoretical interdisciplinarity.

Altogether, these examples illustrate that fields can have rather different perspectives even when working with similar concepts of the study object, and that interdisciplinarity should come with the acknowledgement that different disciplines and fields are well justified to use different methods. However, combining the strengths of different approaches can be a demanding task due to the different goals of the respective traditions, particularly when differences in background assumptions are not acknowledged.

Different Concepts and Levels of Investigation Complicate Relationships Between Biologically Oriented and Computational Neuroscientists

The example illustrates relationships between computational and biologically oriented neuroscientists and indicates how different origins influence concepts and underlying assumptions and form obstacles to interdisciplinary collaboration.

Here, “biologically oriented neuroscience” encompasses research described in the previous part, and we only provide a short description of computational neuroscience before comparing the fields. Computational neuroscientists typically 8 work with computers and address research questions by means of mathematical analyses, computational models, or predictions, which often can be performed without direct contact with living organisms. Thus, their studies often address brain activity at levels which can be addressed by computational approaches and without even considering the relevance of other levels. Although computational scientists typically bring special computational and engineering skills, their contributions often focus on technical inputs (Akil et al. 2016) and add different perspectives. Thus, this research tradition differs clearly from more experimentally oriented fields in terms of methods, research goals and underlying concepts, and can be described as a different epistemic culture.

Parts of these differences can be retraced to the early phase of computational neuroscience in the 20th century, when a rapid development of informatics was linked to the investigation of logical and mechanical requirements for new types of machines in which the resemblance to behaving animals was also considered important (e.g., early cybernetics) (Wiener 1948). At the same time biologically oriented neuroscience lacked the tools, except for electrophysiological approaches, to study the brain at the level of neurons or neuronal circuits. Thus, biologically oriented and computational neuroscience developed rather independently and remained widely separated despite being later united under the umbrella of neuroscience. Accordingly, contemporary computational neuroscience resulted from a merger of two relatively independent disciplines, in which computational traditions are still operating, and which includes scientists from other disciplines, such as physicists, mathematicians, or engineers.

As a consequence, computational neuroscientists often use partially different terminology (e.g., “computation,” “model,” or “signaling”) and underlying concepts (e.g., the concept of causation) than biologically oriented neuroscientists, because they have different education and training. These can also contribute to a situation in which empirically working neuroscientists sometimes struggle to understand some basic principles of computational neuroscience (e.g., when it brings together neuroscience and information theory; McDonnell et al. 2011), whereas scientists with a computational background may lack a thorough understanding of the material basis of the brain and its function.

We use these two fields of neuroscience to illustrate how different research traditions can justify different concepts of the research object and underlying assumptions, which in turn give rise to differences in field‐specific practices. For that purpose, we describe the often‐presumed functional similarity between computers and brains (Section 3.2.2.1) and different perspectives on algorithms, models, and the outcomes of mathematical and computational analyses (Section 3.2.2.2). The differences described here contribute to a situation in which the collaboration between computational neuroscientists and empirically working neuroscientists is often challenging in practice. 9

Different Origins of Fields Resulting in Diverging Interpretations of the Relationship Between Brain Activity and Computation

Because one historical root of computational neuroscience lies in the development of informatics and computational theory, the level of signal and information processing is often prioritized when brain functions are studied in this field, which is also driven by the idea of a functional similarity between brains and computers. However, disagreement about the presumed similarity between brains and computers, the utility of the metaphor of “brains as computers”, and the relevance of such similarity and metaphors for scientific practice can make interdisciplinary collaborations challenging.

The concept of a similarity between brains and computers is rather old, because many decades ago Warren McCulloch claimed that propositional logic can be used to describe the functioning of the central nervous system and that brains can be considered as computing devices that need to be better understood (McCulloch 1965). In the 1980s, David Marr concluded that electrophysiological recordings along with microanatomy cannot sufficiently explain perception and cognition and proposed that full explanations require separate analyses at the so‐called computational level, algorithmic level, and neuronal implementation level (Marr 1982). Moreover, some authors proposed that “electrical and chemical signals are used in the brain to represent and process information” in the brain (Sejnowski et al. 1988, 1299). Indeed, computational neuroscience was expected to be able to play an essential role in simulating and predicting the behavior of nervous systems (Stern and Travis 2006), which led many computational neuroscientists to the assumption that nervous systems perform computations and process information (Piccinini and Shagrir 2014). Thus, the underlying assumption of “computational neuroscience” has broadened from a metaphor with heuristic potential to the name of an entire field of neuroscience that uses computer models to perform analyses and develop novel concepts about neuronal functions and circuits (Miłkowski 2018; Brette 2022).

However, this metaphor also makes assumptions about the brain, its functioning, and the most important level of description that differs from those of biologically oriented neuroscience. It seems to rest on the ideas (1) that logical thinking in the brain is a form of information processing, (2) that information processing is the purpose of computer programs, and (3) that information processing may be implemented by neural systems (Kass et al. 2018). Moreover, in many prominent views of computational neuroscience, the idea prevails that neuronal processes and circuits can be described and recapitulated by computational models. In such models, neurons are often described as if their spiking displays exclusively all‐or‐none digital or binary activities (similar to digital computing in a Turing machine), which, in turn, determine network activity (“computation”). Thus, large parts of computational neuroscience concentrate on signaling, signal propagation, or information transfer, and use models to investigate signal patterns in large data sets obtained by large‐scale recordings. Even though contemporary computational models try to recapitulate different aspects of human brain activity (e.g., digital and analog signal transduction), the description mostly remains at the activity level (Gebicke‐Haerter 2023).

However, such descriptions that remain at the level of signaling patterns and computation cannot easily be reconciled with the perspective of biologically oriented neuroscientists, because for the latter (i) this activity is dependent on a material basis in life neurons and neuronal networks, which may differ between regions of the brain; (ii) neuronal activity is modulated by other signaling molecules, such as neuropeptides; and (iii) the contribution of other cell types (e.g., astrocytes) to complex signaling activity is widely disregarded in such computational models (LeBeau et al. 2005; van den Pol 2012; Ripoll‐Sanchez et al. 2023). Thus, for biologically oriented neuroscientists, it is important that neuronal networks can be “fine‐tuned” by modulating synaptic and glial functions and by linking otherwise disconnected neural circuits. Consequently, computational and biologically oriented neuroscientists may disagree about what the primary level of description, the major research question or the proper depiction of the study object, should be (e.g., either at the level of the brain as the material substrate producing these activities or at the level of neuronal network activity with internal logic and boundary conditions).

Accordingly, scientists from these research traditions bring concepts and assumptions that motivate their preferences. Nonetheless, a special focus on the level of neuronal activity patterns can be justified when it is motivated by obtaining a description of the phenomenon at this level, as a heuristic to study phenomena that occur at shorter time periods than can be investigated by interventionist approaches, as a way to test sufficiency claims at this level, or to explore structural restrictions of signal propagation. However, to obtain a complete picture of brain function and the complexity of its structure, a broad variety of causal factors and the integration of different levels are necessary. Similarly, biologically oriented approaches that disregard or downplay the relevance of the level of information processing as an important and partially independent level of description fall short. Thus, all descriptions that focus only on one level or disregard relevant factors remain limited to a certain extent, whereas interdisciplinary collaborations are an opportunity to emphasize and investigate the relevance of one level but concomitantly consider other factors and perspectives as well. In this context, some critique of well‐established approaches in computational neuroscience by biologically oriented neuroscientists can reflect disagreement about background assumptions. It can be particularly challenging for interdisciplinary collaborations when background assumptions are not sufficiently specified.

Different Perspectives on the Power of Computational Tools and Mathematics and Their Contributions to the Understanding of Brain Processes

The use of mathematical models, algorithms, and predictions as favored ways of investigation is another aspect in which computational and experimental neuroscientists differ. Historically, mathematical and later computational models made important and independent contributions to neuroscience and allowed for the investigation of systems that are too complex to study otherwise (e.g., the mathematical model by Hodgkin and Huxley demonstrated sufficiency conditions for action potentials and applying the cable equation to dendrites made too complex data sets manageable; for review, Kotaleski and Blackwell 2010).

In contemporary neuroscience, computational models come in various forms and can differ in their underlying assumptions, their reference to mathematics or statistics, and the purpose they are used for (e.g., testing sufficiency, exploring mechanisms of processing, or generating hypotheses) (Kass et al. 2018; Kriegeskorte and Douglas 2018; Beer et al. 2024). Moreover, they can be sufficiently complex to study large‐scale network dynamics (Bassett et al. 2018) and to convert theoretical insights into models that can be tested empirically (Kriegeskorte and Douglas 2018; Heiney et al. 2021). Finally, they can be generated by the analysis of data sets obtained from living organisms or the combination of theoretical modules reflecting some biological elements (e.g., theoretical representations of neurons or synapses) together with some underlying assumptions and boundary conditions. Thus, computational neuroscientists use the potential of models and computational approaches in many ways as a heuristic or strategic tool to extract information from complex data sets, without the need to understand the underlying biological mechanisms in detail.

Although such approaches are often motivated by a scarcity of interventional data, they are typically driven by the conviction that mathematical models can reveal critical variables and relations (Weichwald and Peters 2021). Accordingly, it often remains unclear to outsiders, whether (i) modeling presumes just a similarity in input–output relations, which can be mimicked by a computational algorithm, or (ii) the structural properties of neurons, networks and brains actually allow the realization of processes, which are similar to computational processing to computational processing (Kanwisher et al. 2023). Thus, computational neuroscientists sometimes seem to treat algorithms, mathematical processes, and results of computational predictions as if they are implemented in neurons or brain circuits, which is often not easy to reconcile with the framework of biological neuroscience (Kanwisher et al. 2023). Thus, referring to such approaches can contribute to misunderstandings and complicated relations between computational and biologically oriented neuroscientists, when the collaboration partners differ in the concepts and underlying assumptions of mathematical or computational models. Such models can be considered as summarizing descriptions of phenomena of interest that were obtained through experiments studying the brain systems (e.g., changing activity patterns over time), as inherent properties of brain activities that can be described independently of their material basis (e.g., at the level of complex firing patterns), or as independent mathematical equivalents of physical processes that are underlying empirically observable phenomena.

Interdisciplinary projects between neuroscientists from these fields can be challenging, when it is unclear to which extent algorithms or models are expected to correspond to real processes in the brain or are considered “merely” mathematical representations of the material processes of the brain. As a consequence, these scientists may disagree in their appreciation of these types of results. For example, for computational neuroscientists a demonstration of differences in information‐based comparisons (e.g., Granger causality) might suffice to formulate a claim, whereas empirically working scientists may insist that independent validation or corroboration experiments in an organism should be performed. This can be interpreted as a preference of the computational scientist for internal validity within the data set (e.g., by data splitting and comparison by shuffling analyses), whereas the experimental scientist focuses more on external validity and wants to see predictive power of the model or a corroboration of the analyses in independent experiments. Another example of diverging interpretations is the revived debate on the distinction of and the relationship between correlation and causation in computational inferences and their interpretation, which appeared in the context of computational analyses of results obtained by advanced functional MRI techniques (Moreau and Dumas 2021; Novembre and Iannetti 2021). As a consequence, members of these fields of neuroscience can disagree whether finding a mathematical solution or the best computational model is sufficient to claim better understanding or whether empirical validation of predictions and the corroboration by interventionist approaches are necessary to justify mechanistic claims. Thus, making the underlying assumption of one's own methods explicit should facilitate productive interdisciplinary collaborations and can reveal some underlying causes for disagreement about the relevance of results and novel findings.

Overall, the collaboration between computational and biologically oriented neuroscientists can be made challenging by diverging assessments of specific contributions of computational analyses, the irreplaceable role of the latter for some research questions, the type of information that can be obtained by computational approaches, and the explanatory power of such results (Teufel and Fletcher 2016; Chirimuuta 2018, 2021).

Intermediate Conclusion

The examples above illustrate that interdisciplinary collaborations can be more complicated than expected when interacting scientists belong to different research traditions. Some problems can be a consequence of a low awareness of differences in underlying conceptual frameworks and of difficulties in communication between individuals and groups with different backgrounds. A better understanding of disciplinary characteristics can be expected to raise mutual respect for different traditions without fearing to compromise the value of one's own research approach and thus to facilitate the exploration of joint research strategies to integrate the advantages of different disciplines and fields. Moreover, increased awareness for terminological and conceptual ambiguities can help neuroscientists to recognize communication barriers. This could increase appreciation for methods and approaches established in other fields and the opportunities they offer, raise the expectations of advantages accessible by interdisciplinary collaboration, and mitigate communication problems that complicate collaborations.

Thus, neuroscientists could benefit from recognizing that some of the problems rendering interdisciplinary collaborations complicated are often rooted in different research traditions. The examples above illustrate that diverging preferences of such research traditions are often motivated by differences in goals, research objects, or phenomena of interest and can reflect compromises between the ideal and the possible. Some of these problems can be addressed by an increased awareness of scientists and more time devoted to further clarifications, but others might require support from individuals with complementary training.

Closer Cooperation With Philosophers Can Improve Interdisciplinary‐Type Collaborations in Neuroscience

As described above, partners in interdisciplinary collaborations are often confronted with challenges that are not directly related to their scientific training but linked to underlying differences in assumptions, methods, and conceptual frameworks, which can be exacerbated by misunderstandings due to unclear or only seemingly shared terminology. This may convince the reader that interdisciplinary collaborations are often more challenging than anticipated and can complicate otherwise exciting and promising projects.

Hence, identifying causes for misunderstandings, addressing critical points or searching for common ground in joint projects may be essential to improve this type of collaboration. Unfortunately, in many neuroscience curricula, not much attention is being paid to terminology and language use, reflections on underlying assumptions and conceptual differences between research traditions. However, if too few interdisciplinary collaborations limit the advance of neuroscience, efforts should be undertaken to facilitate exchange and foster the implementation of such collaborations (Nancarrow et al. 2013; O'Rourke and Crowley 2013). For that purpose, neuroscientists can either acquire additional skills or request external support from partners with complementary expertise to compensate for their limitations.

Philosophers are good candidates to be such partners, because their training typically emphasizes capacities that can be expected to be beneficial to support interdisciplinary collaborations. While basic philosophical education typically makes philosophers proficient in the analysis of arguments, concepts, and language use in general, philosophers of science bring to the table information about concepts and argumentations frequently used in scientific practice. Their understanding has been developed by observation of scientific practice, analyses of scientific texts, and direct exchange with scientists (Kaiser et al. 2014). Some philosophers even participate in scientific projects for an extended period for which the term “philosophy in science” has been coined (Pradeu et al. 2024). Moreover, some subfields of philosophy of science such as the philosophy of biology and the philosophy of neuroscience offer a wide variety of contributions, which address similar topics as neuroscience but from a different perspective and under different assumptions. 10 Thus, we propose that intensifying collaborations between neuroscientists and philosophers might be a promising strategy to support interdisciplinary collaborations, because these can facilitate identifying typical sources of misunderstanding between scientists more easily (Kaiser et al. 2014; Laplane, Duluc, et al. 2019; MacLeod 2018). Moreover, philosophers have also addressed problems of interdisciplinary research themselves and emphasized the importance of conceptual clarification and rigorous argumentation to facilitate interdisciplinary dialogues (Hoffmann et al. 2013; Holbrook 2013; O'Rourke and Crowley 2013). Importantly, the contributions of philosophers to neuroscience do not necessarily depend on their scientific excellence but rather on complementary expertise to clarify or work out problems, ambiguities and underlying assumptions.

To illustrate how philosophers could contribute to neuroscientists' interdisciplinary collaborations, we describe ways of addressing language use and terminological ambiguities. Working out differences between disciplines or fields in the meaning of some terms and concepts and making neuroscientists aware of these can be a first step in promoting interdisciplinarity.

A first type of philosophical contributions to the use of language and underlying concepts in neuroscience has already been described by Max Bennett and Peter Hacker in their book “Philosophical Foundations of Neuroscience.” The authors argue that the “province [of analytic philosophy] is not the domain of empirical truth or falsehood, but the domain of sense and nonsense” and that “[t]he solution proposed may be philosophical clarifications on the one hand, or empirical discoveries and scientific theories, on the other” (Bennett and Hacker 2005, 399). Such contributions address the relation between the use and meaning of terms. Becoming aware of terminological inaccuracies and conceptual inconsistencies can be enlightening for neuroscientists and help improve their accuracy in language use, which, in turn, can be expected to improve clear communication and, hence, foster interdisciplinary activities.

A second type of philosophical contribution deals with differences in language use between different social groups (e.g., fields of neuroscience). Sometimes scientists employ the same term for different things (e.g., using the term “model” may refer to a computational program, a material object, a graphical illustration, or an organism; Frigg and Hartmann 2020), whereas in other cases, different terms seem to be used for a similar construct or notion with nuances (e.g., the distinction between fear and anxiety and between “state anxiety” and “trait anxiety,” made in psychology, are widely disregarded in many fields of neuroscience using animal behavioral experiments; Goes et al. 2009; Harro 2018). But it can also happen that when the same words are being used in two or more fields or disciplines, they may either have overlapping but somewhat different meanings for the parties involved or refer to different levels of description, for which the term “boundary object” has been coined 11 (Star 1989a, 1989b). For example, it has been observed that the term “learning” has up to 59 different meanings in different fields (e.g., neuroscience, psychology, computer science, or behavioral ecology) and is used to apply to a wide variety and different approaches, but the authors suggest that the definitions can vary across disciplines as long as they are coherent with a core concept of learning (Barron et al. 2015). In such cases, a philosophical contribution can raise awareness for potential misunderstandings but also illustrate that some terminological differences can be justified by the study object or the level of description. Thus, interdisciplinary collaborations may benefit from philosopher's request for precision and even active translational efforts aiming at better compatibility of terminology between fields.

In a third type of contribution, philosophers can identify inconsistencies in the way a term is used, which may reflect an unclear relation between the term und the underlying concept. Indeed, when some neuroscientists and philosophers realized that there were problems in the use of terms, such as “representation,” “code,” “computation,” or “model organisms,” they identified different forms of use and suggested clarifications while acknowledging input from philosophy (Barack and Krakauer 2021; Baker et al. 2022; Kunze and Malfatti 2025). However, philosophers can also address terms and concepts linked to “meta‐scientific” or epistemic questions. For example, a request for better clarification of different causal concepts at work in neuroscience has recently been stated and some authors noted that the extent to which claims of causality can be favored by the notion of mechanism differs across fields of neuroscience (e.g., neurobiology, computational neuroscience, or psychiatric disorders) (Barack et al. 2022) and, most recently, Ross and Bassett (2024). Thus, “causality” does not only seem to have different meanings in these fields but also different underlying concepts. This is reflected by different functions “causality” has in explanations (Chirimuuta 2018; Rolls 2021; Barack et al. 2022) and has consequences for the evaluation of differences between causation and correlation (Chirimuuta 2018; Moreau and Dumas 2021). Thus, some potential problems in interdisciplinary collaborations between biologically oriented and computational neuroscientists, described above, could be the consequence of differences in the concept of causality. A philosopher's contribution could be to illustrate how differences in the perceived strength of causal claims, in the understanding of the relation between observations and hypothesis testing, and in the burden of proof between fields and disciplines could be traced back to differences in the underlying concept of causality. In a similar way, several recent papers involving philosophers highlight some underlying assumptions that are characteristic for different fields of neuroscience, either by reflecting on different ways in which computational models are used to explain in neuroscience (Brun et al. 2025), or by elaborating on the use of different forms of models in neuroscience (Beer et al. 2024). Overall, philosophical contributions can highlight such inconsistencies and suggest improvements, which may be very effective when the expertise of philosophers can be combined with that of neuroscientists. Moreover, clarifying the relationships between terms and concepts thereof can raise awareness for the potential causes of disagreement between fields and disciplines.

In a fourth type of contribution, philosophers support neuroscientists to address differences between fields and disciplines regarding terminology and language‐use at a meta‐level, and to develop general strategies to promote interdisciplinary collaborations. One approach could be to aim at a sort of lingua franca or “mediation language” that is valid in all neuroscientific fields and even shared with other disciplines, but this would imply avoiding all terminological ambiguities and working out clear definitions. However, concepts and terms are often so intertwined with the methodology and theory of fields or disciplines that finding common ground might require accepting the use of broad or vague terms and concepts. Consequently, such terms may appear inherently imprecise (e.g., the contemporary use of “self,” “stress,” and “computation”), because they refer to different things and play various heuristic roles in different fields. Indeed, some of the underlying concepts even seem to have little common measures between disciplines (e.g., resilience between psychology, ecology and the social sciences). Occasionally, they become close to incommensurable, which can make them very difficult to use in interdisciplinary projects (see, e.g., Annerstedt 2010; Olsson et al. 2015). However, the ambiguities of some concepts can also be utilized intentionally as vague concepts at the interface of fields or disciplines (boundary objects) with the potential to facilitate converging developments as common long‐term research strategies of different communities (Löwy 1992; Neto 2020). Thus, it is unclear in how far the goal of a lingua franca type of language can be realized for neuroscience, because a single common language might be difficult to reach, especially when the use of boundary objects seems unavoidable. However, it might be helpful to consider a more loose concept of lingua franca as a kind of parlance with some common core vocabulary that is widely accepted and reflects shared ideas that support communication between different communities, similar to the situation in ancient Mediterranean communities, or as a “creole” (Galison 1997; Thagard 2009; Mazzocchi 2019).

Finally, collaborations with philosophers could be an effective strategy to tackle problems linked to argumentation, because analyzing arguments is a proficiency, which philosophers typically acquire in an early stage of their training. For neuroscientists, it may be important to understand that some types of arguments may be well‐established in one discipline or field but less accepted in another. Taken together, we propose that intensified exchange between neuroscientists and philosophers has the potential to improve interdisciplinary collaborations of neuroscientists, because philosophers can address some of the most challenging aspects of interdisciplinary collaborations, which were described above with some examples.

Despite these benefits, it is important to keep in mind that collaborations between neuroscientists and philosophers are also interdisciplinary. Thus, this exchange can come with some of the problems discussed before. However, additional efforts from both partners can be expected to favor the benefits of philosophical inputs for neuroscientists. How such collaboration between neuroscientists and philosophers can be realized in practice, and how the suggested benefits could play out is explored in the next part.

Different Forms of Collaboration of Neuroscientists With Philosophers

The description above suggests that neuroscientists could benefit from interactions with philosophers in challenging interdisciplinary collaborations, because these scholars are typically trained in skills that are complementary to those of many neuroscientists. This exchange should enable them to jointly tackle challenges that are particularly problematic for interdisciplinary collaborations.

In challenging interdisciplinary collaborations, progress may only be possible if all partners collaborate to recognize and highlight differences in the orientation of research questions, underlying assumptions, and interpretative horizons. In this context, scientists need to specify their own conceptual framework and explain terms and concepts that are well established within their respective communities. Thus, in interdisciplinary collaborations, neuroscientists can benefit from philosophical contributions that help them in clarifying terms, developing a common language, and referring to shared concepts. For this, philosophers can identify different word‐use or ambiguous terminology, highlight potential conceptual ambiguities, and support communication and exchange by facilitating the clarification of terms and arguments (as described above in Section 4). Moreover, they may raise awareness for differences between fields and disciplines due to different research traditions and request clarification when perceiving misunderstandings because of different underlying assumptions or interpretations, entraining predecisions.

However, from a practical point of view, neuroscientists may ask, in which phases of their research such interactions could be most beneficial (5.1), how philosophical contributions can be realized when they do not feel comfortable exchanging with philosophers (5.2), and how such contributions could be beneficial for challenging interdisciplinary collaborations more concretely (5.3).

Different Ways in Which Philosophers Can Support Interdisciplinary Collaborations

From a conceptual perspective, neuroscientists may benefit from interactions with philosophers in various ways during interdisciplinary collaborations. A neuroscientist can either expect to improve expertise, skill set, and methodological repertoire or aim at better preparation for or organization of an interdisciplinary project. Accordingly, the contributions of philosophers can be to prepare scientists for challenging interdisciplinary collaborations, to support them in such collaborations, and to foster the integration of findings obtained in different fields or disciplines (Table 2).

By interacting with philosophers, individual neuroscientists can try to increase their own skill set by acquiring or improving abilities beneficial for interdisciplinary collaborations. This can encompass the ability to explain research with an emphasis on underlying assumptions, preferences, and interpretative horizons, and an awareness of some often‐underestimated challenges of bringing together different research traditions. Moreover, philosophical input can be expected to render neuroscientists more careful in their language use.

By involving philosophers in their interdisciplinary long‐term collaborations, neuroscientists can hope to reduce misunderstandings in communication between partners, bridging gaps between groups by increasing mutual understanding and, hence, to favor direct interdisciplinary practice. From this perspective, the contributions of philosophers can vary, and different forms may be best suited depending on the project, a distinct phase of such a project, and the collaborating partners. Whereas some benefits can be obtained even before the project starts (preparatory phase), others can occur during the planning phase, and yet others may happen in the execution and interpretation phase. In the preparatory phase, the exchange with philosophers can help neuroscientists (i) to specify their contribution to the project regarding the research object and question together with their underlying assumptions and interpretative horizons; (ii) to identify unclear or ambiguous terminology which, although well‐established in one field, can come with undesired associations or ambiguities to members of other fields; and (iii) to specify their arguments because some of them might be well‐established in one field but appear less convincing in others. During the planning phase of experiments, major goals could be to raise awareness for methodological and conceptual biases of one's own field, for potential benefits of complementary approaches, and for the advantage of addressing a complex phenomenon from multiple perspectives. This should reduce any feelings of superiority of one's field and raise curiosity for other approaches. In the execution and interpretation phase of projects, philosophers can support collaborating scientists when they discuss results and jointly interpret them to obtain an integrated perspective that is acceptable for all partners. In this context, fostering fruitful communication between collaborating scientists can be a major challenge and may require paying attention to terminological ambiguities and underlying preferences. Thus, philosophical contributions can be a request for clarification of terminology or argumentation and sometimes consist in retracing disagreements about interpretation to differences in underlying assumptions or priorities of fields and disciplines (cf. Section 3.2). Overall, neuroscientists can hope to obtain a more complete picture of a complex phenomenon of interest by interdisciplinary approaches when philosophical contributions foster the integration of different perspectives provided by collaboration partners. In addition, the philosopher's contribution can problematize formulations that seem to result from the perceived superiority of one field over another. However, all forms of interdisciplinary projects can be expected to benefit from prolonged exchange with a philosopher precisely because of their interdisciplinary character.

Philosophers' contributions can be short or long term and can take place in different phases of projects of interdisciplinary collaborations. However, in all forms of collaborative interactions, the involvement of philosophers neither implies that neuroscientists give up control nor that the decisions or judgments should be handed over to philosophers. Instead, philosophers should be considered as collaboration partners who can be perceived as listeners and questioners, and by this means as external support for interdisciplinary collaborations, but not as teachers or “know‐it‐alls.”

Different Ways of Interaction Between Neuroscientists and Philosophers

Neuroscientists trying to reach the benefits of interdisciplinary collaborations described above and looking for philosophical input to support such collaboration may wonder how to integrate philosophers in such interdisciplinary collaborations and which formats are suitable. Somewhat similarly to interdisciplinary collaborations described above, collaborations between neuroscientists and philosophers can be realized in different forms. Such collaborations can occur at a single time point, during a collaborative meeting seeking support, or as long‐term involvement of a philosopher in a scientific project, and they can involve minimally two interested individuals or groups of scholars sharing common interests. Thus, neuroscientists searching for philosophers as collaboration partners should be aware that they can consider and choose among various forms of interaction with philosophers. Here, we propose three forms, which differ in the kind and duration of interaction 12 , the extent to which specific skills are actively practiced (“training”‐like), and in the type of input the philosopher can provide to support a concrete project. Accordingly, the impact on mutual understanding of collaboration partners and interdisciplinary skill sets of neuroscientists can be expected to differ as well.

Philosophy of Science Applied to Neuroscience

This approach emphasizes the wealth of knowledge about scientific methods, approaches, and concepts that have accumulated in philosophy of science and that is available to motivated neuroscientists in the form of literature and qualified individuals (Figure 2A). From the early 20th century on, and due to the positivistic orientation (e.g., of the Vienna circle), philosophy of science has dealt with formalizing scientific methodology and establishing criteria, such as empirical confirmability, to distinguish meaningful scientific statements from metaphysical or non‐scientific ones (Neurath et al. 1996). However, in its present‐day form, philosophy of science often provides detailed analyses of scientific practice and reflects on models and explanations used in knowledge acquisition in neuroscience but from a philosophical point of view (Bechtel and Huang 2022). This modern‐day approach remains mainly in an external position of describing and commenting on scientific processes without attempting to intervene in or prescribe scientific practice (Kaiser et al. 2014) and thus differs from work by early 20th century philosophers of science who often tried to prescribe idealized forms of scientific investigations. Awareness of the internal workings of scientific research and different modes of knowledge production in general can help neuroscientists to recognize similar processes in their own interdisciplinary practice. Moreover, philosophers of science have reflected on various topics relevant for (neuro)scientific practice, some of which have been mentioned above, such as “models in science,” “causality,” or “reductionism.” These contributions are about concepts fundamental to (neuro)science and can be starting points on which scientists can then build further. Neuroscientists can take advantage of this external general perspective to recognize and avoid certain types of errors made in the past and respond to constructive criticism of the underlying assumptions of their practice. Importantly, this type of information transfer between philosophers and neuroscientists regarding philosophy of science does not require direct exchange and can be achieved by the effort of the neuroscientist to read the existing literature in philosophy of science. For example, two recent papers published in high‐impact factor journals that were written by neuroscientists contain many references to publications by philosophers of science, which the authors considered valuable (Rolls 2021; Levenstein et al. 2023).

Different opportunities for neuroscientists to engage in interactions with philosophers. (A) Philosophy of science applied to neuroscience: Scientists can take advantage of the available ideas provided by philosophers of science, who observe, conceptualize, and reflect on scientific practice to develop descriptions, criteria, and suggestions for improvement. (B) Philosophy for neuroscientists: Neuroscientists and philosophers sharing interests in similar broad research questions can exchange and discuss topics of interest at interdisciplinary events or in special issues of neuroscientific journals, to experience the benefits of the complementary skills of these two disciplines. (C) Philosophy in neuroscience: Neuroscientists involve philosophers directly in their specific research projects, in which philosophers can clarify assumptions, discuss concepts, and develop novel frameworks. Philosophy for Neuroscientists

Another approach is to focus on the direct exchange between scientists and philosophers when they share an interest in a topic or a research question and philosophers present their points of view to neuroscientists on certain occasions (Figure 2B). This can raise the interest of scientists for philosophical methods when they are applied directly to specific research questions. By this, scientists become familiar with the thinking style(s), frameworks, and methods philosophers utilize in practice. In addition, observing different approaches and comparing various tools should enable neuroscientists to become more aware of some of the characteristic hurdles of interdisciplinary practice. Such exchanges between neuroscientists and philosophers can occur in lectures or interdisciplinary exchange events (e.g., through networking events at neuroscience conferences), because their expertise allows philosophers to mediate between scientists and positions of philosophers reflecting on their practice (Kaiser et al. 2014). Moreover, philosophers can make some scientific assumptions and terms explicit, propose how they may be articulated better when they are used in different scientific disciplines, and indicate standards for good scientific methods and theories (de Haro 2020). For this purpose, the philosopher William Wimsatt, who has been proposed to do “philosophy for science” (Griesemer 2008, 2011), suggested that philosophers need to “study the heuristic techniques that scientists use to explore, model and analyze complex systems” instead of starting with particular idealizations (Wimsatt 2007, 8), and has also engaged in debates with neuroscientists (Wimsatt 1976). More recently, van Stee (2022) has proposed that an analysis of the tasks employed in cognitive neuroscience and their conceptual implications can be considered philosophy for cognitive neuroscience. Such interactions do not require a constant investment of time and energy but can provide a wealth of information in workshop‐like events. This type of interaction may result in joint papers reflecting a personal interaction between neuroscientists and philosophers based on meetings at different levels (Barack et al. 2022; Ross and Bassett 2024).

Philosophy in Neuroscience

Finally, scientists can benefit from direct participation of philosophers in their research projects (Figure 2C). This approach includes philosophers structurally in long‐term projects, in which they continuously participate and either make their own thematic contributions (e.g., theories or suggestions for experiments) that are directed to and can be used by scientists (Laplane, Mantovani, et al. 2019; Pradeu et al. 2024) or foster interdisciplinary interactions of neuroscientists. In this context, getting to know each other better can improve the learning process and facilitate the acquisition of important interdisciplinary skills. Moreover, such research projects can be expected to benefit from the intellectual contributions of philosophers because the latter can broaden the perspective of neuroscientists and might even reveal aspects that were previously unnoticed. As such, it attempts to avoid a merely unilateral relationship between philosophy and the sciences (Kaiser et al. 2014; Pradeu et al. 2024). Such unilaterality can be mitigated by publications written by philosophers for neuroscientists (van Gelder 1998; Thagard 2009; Barwich 2019) and joint publications between neuroscientists and philosophers (Craver et al. 2016; Rosenbaum et al. 2016; Barwich 2019; Mok et al. 2021; Bickle et al. 2022; Barwich and Severino 2026).

Whereas the first approach (philosophy of science) applied to neuroscience primarily tries to extract relevant information from philosophical literature without necessarily exchanging with the philosopher, the second approach (philosophy for neuroscientists) aims at taking advantage of occasionally organized interactions between scientists and philosophers. The third approach (philosophy in neuroscience) counts on a more long‐term involvement of philosophers in neuroscientific practice. Neuroscientists may need to consider which format is best suited to expand their horizon or for their interdisciplinary collaborations. From the perspective of a neuroscientist interested in interdisciplinary research, philosophers of neuroscience have potentially interesting things to say, but these may be obscured by formulations (terms, phrases, or metaphors) not familiar to neuroscientists. However, efforts of neuroscientists to familiarize themselves with these can be expected to pay off. Given that philosophers engaging in “philosophy for neuroscientists” share an interest with neuroscientists and take inspiration from their approaches, it should be easier for neuroscientists to engage in productive exchange with these philosophers. “Philosophy in neuroscience” seems like a promising, but highly challenging, approach, as philosophers take scientific problems as a starting point to mobilize their own “toolkit” to propose improvements and alternative solutions to neuroscientists. In the long run, this approach may be highly rewarding, but it is important to keep in mind that it requires more continuous collaboration and mutual understanding. Some neuroscientists may have philosophical training or philosophical inclinations or may already be engaged in exchanges with philosophers, but for others this particular opportunity may be of help to develop specific skills fostering interdisciplinarity. Obviously, all forms of collaborative integration of philosophers in neuroscience teaching and research should ensure equal partnership and without the need to fully master the other discipline.

Finally, neuroscientists may wonder if philosophers are interested in this type of collaboration, because the benefit for philosophers might be unclear in some forms of collaboration. However, in philosophy, the switch to a more practice‐oriented philosophy of science (philosophy of science in practice; Boumans and Leonelli 2013) has led to more reflection of philosophers on their role in exchange and participation in collaborations with scientists. Thus, it has been suggested to distinguish reflective approaches in philosophy of science from more collaborative ones, which require regular interactions between philosophers and scientists around a common problem (Kaiser et al. 2014). In this context, the interactions between neuroscientists and philosophers can thus be beneficial for both sides. However, neuroscientists must not forget that the interaction with philosophers is also interdisciplinary. Thus, neuroscientists may be confronted with different styles of argumentation and critique that reflect different academic cultures. Consequently, these interactions may benefit from personal acquaintance and long‐standing interactions, which foster trust and allow continuous learning of the other language and thinking tradition (O'Rourke and Crowley 2013) (famous pairs of philosophers and neuroscientists are John Bickle & Alcino Silva, Max Bennett & Peter Hacker, Carl Craver & Shayna Rosenbaum).

Bridging Scientific Fields or Disciplines by Philosophical Insights

Finally, we want to illustrate how philosophical contributions might support neuroscientists in their interdisciplinary projects. For that purpose, we use the often‐challenging collaboration between clinical and biologically oriented neuroscientists as an example and explore potential benefits, which philosophical insights could provide for planning and executing an interdisciplinary project on an inherited human disease of the brain involving the use of model organisms (MO).

As described above, both of these fields have their own research traditions, often appear socially separated, seem to ask partially different research questions, and members of these groups frequently also seem to disagree on the use of MO. Whereas biologically oriented neuroscientists typically consider MO as useful tools for many research questions, clinical neuroscientists often criticize excessive trust in extrapolating results obtained in MO to humans. However, in the context of a joint project, diverging perspectives on the utility of using MO can complicate already challenging tasks, such as defining research questions, jointly designing experiment setups, and agreeing on the distribution of money for different parts of the project.

To foster effective collaborations, philosophers should be able to support neuroscientists in overcoming the typical challenges described above. This may include raising attention for the relevance of terminological and conceptual ambiguities to improve communication between collaboration partners, the relevance of different research traditions, or some conceptual and practical consequences of such differences, as we describe below.

In the type of collaboration described, a philosopher could point out that it cannot be taken for granted that scientists of different fields always mean the same when they speak about “MOs” or “the use of MOs.” It may turn out that a biologically oriented neuroscientist uses MO to refer to a species considered typical for a taxonomic group from which findings can be extrapolated to other species of this group, whereas a clinical neuroscientist may think of a MO species that is expected to represent humans (for details, see Kunze and Malfatti 2025 or Bolker 2009). Recognizing such conceptual differences can clear up misunderstandings and help neuroscientists to formulate and address critiques more specifically.

Moreover, a philosopher could support collaboration among neuroscientists by retracing parts of the disagreement about various aspects of MO use to differences in research traditions of their fields. Clarifying that diverging evaluations of power and limitations of MO‐based approaches do not reflect a different understanding of scientific rigour but can be linked to differences in research questions, underlying assumptions, and priorities of the respective fields can suggest that such differences are not inherently problematic but a consequence of complementary approaches.

Regarding a human disease, biologically oriented neuroscientists typically investigate underlying mechanisms (e.g., etiology or disease progression) and try to support causal claims, whereas clinical neuroscientists often focus their attention on the variability of symptoms, interindividual differences of patients, and the need for therapeutic options. Thus, clinical neuroscientists evaluating MO‐based models of human diseases often prioritize aspects that are directly related to the state of patients and consider the variability of symptoms, whereas biologically oriented neuroscientists use MO to focus on differences to a healthy organism and the possibility to obtain a mechanistic understanding of the disease (Kunze and Malfatti 2025). Accordingly, some clinical neuroscientists consider the use of MOs misleading, because of potentially important species differences, insufficient overall similarity to human patients, and criticize their use in studying human diseases. Instead, many biologically oriented neuroscientists consider MOs excellent tools to investigate conserved biological processes. However, this positive evaluation by the latter can be explained, at least in part, by a high appreciation for low interindividual variability and for the opportunity to test mechanistic hypotheses by interventions, which is often not possible in humans. Thus, a preference for using MOs or directly studying human patients often reflects prioritizing some advantages over others, while weighing the limitations of the preferred approach against some inherent limitations of alternative approaches. However, disentangling scientific disagreement between neuroscientists that are based on field‐specific preferences originating in different research traditions can help collaborating neuroscientists to decide what they can agree on and where specific attention needs to be paid to.

Such contributions can induce a shift of debates from being about scientific rigor towards a common search for benefits of complementary strategies, more appropriate controls, and optimal approaches when dealing with a given research question. Moreover, neuroscientists recognizing the independent value of different research traditions can be expected to spend more time and energy to jointly design experiments that satisfy the expectations of all involved partners. This can facilitate specifying the role of experiments performed in MOs for research projects and to take most benefit from MO‐use without exaggerating the significance for human patients. In this context, a philosopher can also highlight that the use of MOs is based on similarity and that studies in MOs can provide insights that may be plausible or promising for human patients but can never be taken for granted (Kunze and Malfatti 2025). Jointly accepting such generalized insight can help neuroscientists find a balance between over‐ and underestimating the trustworthiness of findings obtained in MO for conditions in humans.

When teams of scientists meet to present results, discuss their interpretation, and agree on the next steps, a philosopher could indicate that different research traditions typically have established ideas about necessary controls and what constitutes good evidence, and that this can give rise to disagreements about practical decisions. For example, disagreement on the question of which control experiments are most important might be influenced by the research question, because biologically oriented scientists often focus on the testability of claims by interventionist approaches, whereas clinical neuroscientists prefer to explore some of the risks of transferring insights between species. Thus, disagreement can be understood as a preference for the trustworthiness of mechanistic or causal claims in MOs (internal validity) or for high relevance of similar findings in human conditions (external validity). Recognizing the legitimacy of different types of concern can pave the way for jointly elaborating experimental setups that allow both fields to contribute to a more complete understanding of the phenomenon of interest.

Philosophical contributions such as those described above can provide helpful insights during different phases of interdisciplinary projects (cf. Section 5.1) and can be realized in different ways of interaction between neuroscientists and philosophers (cf. Section 5.2). Thus, these contributions can be expected to facilitate the interactions between collaborating neuroscientists when they are successful in raising awareness and fostering communications.

Outlook

In this paper, we tried to show how interdisciplinary collaborations have been the basis of many innovations and insights in neuroscience and that today different forms of interdisciplinarity continue to contribute to the progress of neuroscience. Thus, a lack of such interdisciplinarity would generate disciplinary or field‐specific islands of knowledge with very limited interconnections, and hence limit the power of corroboration by different methods or approaches to increase the robustness of findings (Wimsatt 2007). This also applies to different fields of contemporary neuroscience with different research traditions. By means of interdisciplinary collaboration, neuroscientists often can obtain a more complete picture of the phenomenon of interest and avoid biased interpretations and premature generalizations. However, interdisciplinary exchange or collaboration is often challenging and typically requires special commitment and effort to initiate and foster it. We suggest that different research traditions of fields and disciplines, and little contact between members of these groups can give rise to various challenges of interdisciplinary collaborations, because of differences in underlying concepts and assumptions, and practical aspects of research. This, in turn, can foster misunderstanding and communication problems but can also be expected to lower the appreciation of different approaches and the expectation to reach additional benefits by interdisciplinary collaborations. We conclude that some limitations of scientific education are linked to a lack of skills that are required for effective interdisciplinary collaborations. In this context, philosophers can support neuroscientists in improving their skills and, in turn, interdisciplinary exchange and collaborations. This contribution does not require a deep understanding of neuroscience or specific topical insights, but rather the proficiency of philosophers in skills that are typically part of their training.

Neuroscientists, along with their scientific societies, as well as programs promoting and calling for interdisciplinarity should be aware of and acknowledge the opportunities, but also of the difficulties of interdisciplinary research endeavors. These institutions should help neuroscientists develop skills not only to submit interdisciplinary projects but also to effectively conduct them. Personal experience of fruitful and satisfying interdisciplinary interactions is obviously important (Brown et al. 2015), but providing neuroscientists with skills to collaborate better and more effectively is also essential. We are convinced that progress in neuroscientific understanding will increasingly require the ability to work interdisciplinarily. However, such endeavors require properly trained neuroscientists as well as mediators to facilitate such interactions. Philosophers are certainly not the solution for all problems, but their specific sets of skills and methods can make them valuable partners in these endeavors. Increased interactions with philosophers could be achieved by reintroducing or fostering philosophy of science courses in neuroscience curricula and specific funding opportunities for interdisciplinary sessions in both neuroscience and philosophy conferences.
