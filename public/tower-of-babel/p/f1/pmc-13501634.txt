Functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres based on microenvironment sensing promote the sequential repair of infected bone defects by regulating ROS balance
Authors: Xinyu Zhang, Fan Wang, Xinhe Li, Xiaoqian Ding, Anan Jang, Zijie Wang, Xu He, Hui Gou, Jindong Tan, Wang Han, Xiaoyu Han, Dingqun Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe key to treating infectious bone defects (IBD) is regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) balance to achieve “anti-infective first, then osteogenesis” temporal sequential repair. However, sensing microenvironmental changes to regulate ROS remains challenging. This study prepared a nanoparticle (PBD-ICG nanoparticlces) capable of sensing pH changes to enable protonation and deprotonation with “triple-element synergy” acid-base-driven assembly method, and loaded onto GelMA/HAMA hydrogel microsphere established a functional-responsiveness hydrogel microsphere (PGI) based on microenvironment sensing.ResultWith ultrasonic cavitation, indocyanine green transition to excited state, enabling electron transfer or energy transfer with H2O or 3O₂ in microenvironment to generate ROS. Under 1 W ultrasound, ROS release 74% to anti-bacteria and restores acidic infected microenvironment to normal weakly alkaline state. Simultaneously, nanoparticles sense pH changes, triggering internal polyaniline transform to emeraldine base and bovine serum albumin conformational changes. This process releases deferoxamine to inhibit excess ROS with 72% efficiency, regulating ROS balance and promoting vascularized osteogenesis. In vitro experiments confirmed a 9.19-fold increase in angiogenesis. In vivo studies revealed 82.6% antibacterial efficacy, followed by 46.5% improvement in osteogenesis efficacy, achieving temporal sequential treatment.ConclusionSummarizing, this system regulates ROS balance through sensing of microenvironment to achieve functional-responsive temporal sequential repair, providing novel strategies for IBD synergistic repair.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04584-y.

Introduction

Infected Bone Defect (IBD) refers to the coexistence of extensive bone tissue loss with persistent bacterial infection, accompanied by a highly complex and distinctly time-dependent dynamic pathological process [1]. In the early stages of infection, the body primarily exerts its anti-infectious effects through various mechanisms, including the induction of an inflammatory response and phagocytosis by macrophages [2], during this process, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by the body through respiratory bursts can further contribute to the elimination of pathogens [3]. However, it is noteworthy that during the mid-stage of infection, due to pathogen immune escape and biofilm formation [4], a persistent infection establishes a microenvironment with a low pH (pH = 5.5) [5, 6]. On this basis, the continuous release of microbial metabolites and bacterial toxins repeatedly stimulates the affected site, thereby triggering recurrent immune responses [7]. Unfortunately, as a substance with “dual properties”, the excessive ROS generated by uncontrolled release far exceeds the clearance capacity of the body’s antioxidant system, leading to oxidative stress. These adverse factors not only cause invasion of the osteocyte lacuna–canaliculi network during the late stages of infection but also create a hypoxic environment by eroding vascular endothelial cells. This inhibits osteoblast function and impedes new bone formation [8], ultimately forming a vicious cycle of “persistent infection-oxidative stress-nonunion” [9]. In summary, therapeutic strategies for IBD should align with its pathological temporal sequence, implementing dynamic and intelligent synergistic treatments. Therefore, in the treatment of IBD, there is hope that by targeting ROS, breaking the infection and nonunion vicious cycle and regulating the balance between anti-infection and tissue repair using ROS, and thus systematically advancing subsequent repair processes. However, achieving an intelligent switching for the “sequential regulation” of ROS constitutes a current research frontier and challenge in the treatment of IBD.

For this reason, the ideal therapeutic strategy for IBD should involve the efficient release of ROS in the early stages to exert antimicrobial effects, then inhibits excessive ROS production in later stages to alleviate oxidative stress and promote cell proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis, to provide an optimal repair environment for bone regeneration. But it is difficult to achieve above-mentioned precise “temporally sequential regulation” of ROS homeostasis through single response pattern. Ultrasound (US), as a physical therapeutic modality with excellent spatial-temporal controllability and tissue penetration capabilities [10], can serve as an ideal external “remote control” [11]. Currently, numerous studies have utilized sonodynamic responses to promote ROS release [12], achieving targeted antibacterial effects [13], However, the uncontrolled sustained production of ROS undoubtedly exacerbates oxidative stress [14], which in turn impedes the bone repair process during the later stages of IBD [15]. While traditional ROS-antioxidant therapies (such as vitamin C and N-acetylcysteine) effectively scavenge ROS [16, 17], their non-selective scavenging disrupts the anti-infective processes crucial during the early stages of IBD [18]. In contrast to relying solely on external stimuli, utilizing the dynamic changes of the body’s own pathological microenvironment as a trigger provides a safer and more reliable pathway for responsive therapy. Hence, developing a “dual-responsive” strategy capable of sensing both external regulatory means and changes in the IBD microenvironment is crucial for highly matching the pathological process and promoting osteogenic repair [19, 20].

The key to the realization of the dual-response regulation strategy lies in the construction of an intelligent drug delivery system that can accommodate and execute this complex task, and hydrogel microspheres are regarded as an ideal platform due to their excellent biocompatibility [21]. GelMA hydrogels, as an effective biological carrier, have been widely used in the treatment of infectious diseases to exert antibacterial effects due to their excellent plasticity and drug-loading capacity [22, 23]. but conventional homogeneous hydrogel microspheres often exhibit limited response modes [24–26], which makes it challenging to achieve “temporal sequential” drug release and multi-response capabilities [27]. Indocyanine green (ICG), as an organic sonosensitizers [28], has been demonstrated to inhibit bacterial respiratory chain electron transport by generating ROS under US irradiation to exert a bactericidal effect [29]. Deferoxamine (DFO) as an FDA-approved clinical therapeutic, it can bind to free iron to form non-toxic chelates, suppress Fenton reaction thereby reducing excessive ROS production. Concurrently, it upregulates HIF-1α and its downstream target, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [30], promoting angiogenesis with excellent biosafety [31]. Polyaniline-BSA (PANI-BSA), as a nanoparticle exhibiting “acid-base isomerism,” undergoes conformational adjustments in its framework across varying pH environments, thereby sensing changes in the infection microenvironment [32]. Therefore, based on the unique technical advantages of US and the acidic physiological microenvironment (pH = 5.5) at the infection site [5, 6], this study proposes to construct a functionally responsive hydrogel microsphere that can sense the microenvironment. The core of this design lies in loading nanoparticles capable of protonation/deprotonation through automatic pH sensing onto the surface of the microsphere, to achieve division of labor and synergy for different treatment stages of IBD. The first stage utilizes US applied to ICG to trigger rapid ROS release, thereby exerting an antibacterial effect. Subsequently, after the infection is controlled, nanoparticels sensing pH changes in the micro-environment intelligently and PANI transforming from the emeraldine salt (ES) state to the emeraldine base (EB) state, resulting framework undergo a hydrophobic “collapse” and conformational adjustment [32, 33], disrupting the electrostatic and hydrogen bond interaction network between PANI-BSA-DFO, enabling DFO release and initiates the second stage program to suppress exceed ROS generation, promoting the angiogenesis and osteogenesis processes synergistically. This mechanism achieves the temporal sequential activation of the therapeutic function and the adaptive balance regulation of ROS, effectively promoting the staged repair of infectious bone defects.

In summary, the temporal pathological characteristics of infectious bone defects demand an evolution in therapeutic strategies from a static, single-mode approach to a dynamic, synergistic paradigm. Intelligent temporal sequential regulation centred on ROS balance is the pivotal breakthrough required to achieve this transformation. This study developed functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres based on the dynamic pathological process of IBD and its special infection microenvironment (Schematic Diagram 1). Firstly, pH-responsive nanoparticles (PANI-BSA-DFO, PBD nanoparticles) were synthesized through a ternary synergistic strategy driven by intermolecular acid-base interactions. Then, ICG was electrostatically adsorbed to construct dual-responsive nanoparticles (PBD-ICG nanoparticles). Subsequently, these nanoparticles were loaded onto the surface of gelatin/hyaluronic acid (GelMA/HAMA) composite hydrogel microspheres synthesized by microfluidic technology, ultimately forming functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres(PGI) based on microenvironment sensing. Under the influence of external US, this hydrogel microsphere system rapidly releases ROS to exert anti-infection effects, and intelligently senses the pH changes in the infection microenvironment at the bone defect site, adaptively regulating the balance of ROS, thereby achieving the sequential repair of IBD “anti-infection-promoting bone formation”. The structural and dual-response capabilities of the functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres after synthesis were systematically evaluated. In addition, the sequential therapeutic ability of this microsphere for infectious bone defects was studied and discussed. Overall, this study innovatively proposes a sequential repair concept for IBD based on ROS homeostasis regulation, and the existence of functional-responsive nanoparticles enables the hydrogel microspheres to precisely match the needs of different pathological stages of IBD, providing a simple and efficient new solution for the dynamic treatment of IBD and related infection traumas.

Materials and methods

Preparation of PBD nanoparticles

Preparation of PANI-BSA assemblies based on previous studies [34], the method is as follows. Firstly, a BSA-Fe³⁺ complex solution was prepared by adding FeCl₃ aqueous solution (1998 µL, 0.1 g/mL) to the BSA solution (27 mL, 0.0074 g/mL) and stirring continuously for 30 min. Prepare the aniline/HCl solution by mixing HCl solution (285 µL, 36–38% by mass), aniline (285 µL), and double-distilled water (3 mL), then sonicate at 37 °C for 30 min. To synthesize the PANI-BSA assembly, the aniline/HCl mixture was added to the BSA-Fe³⁺ complex solution in an ice bath. The color of the mixture turned deep green within 6 h, after which the resulting mixture was placed in a 4 °C refrigerator overnight. Next day, centrifuge at 18,000 rpm for 20 min and remove the supernatant. Disperse the sediment in water and transfer it to a 100 kDa ultrafiltration tube. Obtain the PANI-BSA assembly by ultrafiltration at 3,500 rpm for 5 min followed by five washes with water. Finally, resuspend the PANI-BSA nanoparticles in 3 mL of double-distilled water, add 199.8 µg of DFO (66.6 µg/mL), incubate on a shaking incubator at 4 °C for 24 h, then centrifuge at 18,000 rpm for 20 min. After removing the supernatant, the PBD nanoparticels are obtained.

Preparation of PBD-ICG nanoparticles

Incubate the prepared PBD nanoparticles with an ICG working solution (diluted from a 1% ICG stock solution to 100 µg/mL) at room temperature on a shaking incubator for 24 h. The ICG stock solution consists of 1% (w/v, 10 mg/mL) ICG dissolved in deionized water. Then centrifuge at 15,000 rpm for 15 min, wash three times with double-distilled water, collect the precipitate, and obtain PBD-ICG nanoparticles.

DFO encapsulation efficiency assay: After co-incubating 66.6 µg/mL DFO with PANI-BSA nanoparticles at 4 °C for 24 h, the mixture was centrifuged at 18,000 rpm for 10 min. The 1 mL supernatant was taken as the test sample. Then, 100 µL of 0.1 M FeCl₃ solution was added to form an iron complex. Subsequently, 1 mL of DFO solutions at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, and 10.0 µg/mL were prepared to form a five-point standard curve. FeCl₃ was added to each solution under the same conditions to form iron complexes for subsequent analysis. Finally, a high-performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC) was used to plot the calibration curve and determine the concentration of the test sample. The HPLC parameters are as follows: Column: Cadenza CD-C18, 150 × 4.6 mm, 3 μm; Detection wavelength: 430–435 nm; Mobile phase A: 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 5.5); Mobile phase B: Acetonitrile (ACN); Gradient elution; Run time: 5–10 min; Column temperature: 40 °C.

ICG coating efficiency assay: After co-incubating 100 µg/mL ICG with the nanoparticles for 24 h, centrifuge at 4 °C and at 4 °C and 18,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a fully black 96-well plate, and its fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 780 nm and an absorption wavelength of 820 nm. The ICG drug encapsulation efficiency was calculated based on the free ICG concentration determined from a standard curve.

Analysis of DFO release curves: Samples were placed in three different pH environments (pH 5.5, pH 6.5, and pH 7.5) and incubated for 1, 3, 5, and 7 days. US intervention was performed during the first 3 days of incubation, using the same US parameters as those used for cell sonication. Subsequently, the supernatant was diluted 10-fold, and 100 µL of 0.1 M FeCl₃ solution was added to form iron complexes. Detection was performed by HPLC at 430 nm, and concentrations were determined based on the calibration curve.

Preparation of GelMA hydrogels and HAMA hydrogels

Synthesis of GelMA Hydrogel: Disperse 20 g of gelatin in 200 mL of carbonate buffer solution (0.3427 g of NaCO₃ and 3.0915 g of NaHCO₃ were dispersed in 200 mL of deionized water). Place the mixture in a 50 °C oil bath and stir until completely dissolved. Then take 2 mL of MA (methacrylic anhydride) and slowly add it to the gelatin solution, taking care to avoid exposure to light. Continue the reaction in an oil bath for 3 h, then add 100 mL of PBS to terminate the reaction. Finally, centrifuge at 25 °C and 7000 rpm for 15 min to remove unreacted MA. Transfer the GelMA into 8 kDa dialysis bags and dialysize at 38 °C for 2 days. Dispense the dialysed GelMA into multiple Petri dishes, freeze overnight at -80 °C, and dry in a freeze dryer to obtain the final product. Synthesis of HAMA Hydrogel: Dissolve 10 g of hyaluronic acid (HA) in 500 mL of deionized water solution, mechanically stir until the solution becomes clear and transparent. Next, under an ice-water bath, slowly add 20 mL of MA to the solution while stirring thoroughly. Then, gradually add 20 mL of 5 M NaOH to the system. Allow the system to react overnight at 4 °C. Centrifugation was performed at 7000 rpm for 15 min the following day. The supernatant was transferred to a dialysis bag with an 8 kDa molecular weight cutoff and dialyzed at room temperature for 3 days. After dialysis, transfer to a 50 mL centrifuge tube, freeze overnight at -80 °C, then freeze-dry in a freeze dryer for later use.

Preparation of GelMA/HAMA composite hydrogel microspheres

Synthesis of microspheres using microfluidic technology based on the team’s prior experience [35], The specific steps are as follows. Take the oil phase (1.5 g “Span 80”, paraffin oil 30 g; mix thoroughly, then sonicate for 5 min to eliminate air bubbles) and the aqueous phase (Gelma 0.15 g, HAMA 0.05 g, 3 mL of 0.25% Lithium Phenyl (Engineering For Life, Suzhou, China) (2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl) phosphinate in PBS). Connect the aqueous and oil phases to the microfluidic device and adjust to an appropriate flow rate. Under the combined effects of shear stress and hydrophobic contact, pregel droplets form, which subsequently crosslink into hydrogel microspheres under UV light. The final product is obtained by washing three times with acetone and ultrapure water, respectively. The product was frozen overnight at -80 °C and subsequently freeze-dried in a freeze dryer.

Preparation of PGI hydrogel microspheres

Incubate the equal proportion of PBD-ICG nanoparticle suspension with GelMA/HAMA composite hydrogel microspheres under light-protected and room-temperature conditions for 24 h. During the incubation, ensure that the pH of the suspension is less than or equal to 6.0. After the incubation, wash three times, collect the microspheres and freeze-dry for storage.

The characterization of nanoparticles and hydrogel microspheres

The structure, morphology, and physicochemical properties of the functional-responsive microspheres were examined using an inverted microscope, scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Clara, Tescan), infrared spectroscopy, and Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (LSCM; Leica). For FTIR experiments, samples were ground into fine powder, uniformly mixed with potassium bromide, dried, and analyzed. Absorption peaks between 400 and 4000 cm− 1 were read on the instrument’s computer.

Detection of cellular reactive oxygen species

Use the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit with CM-H2DCFDA to measure ROS levels in cells following intervention with the material in combination with US. Using a 96-well plate and with 1 × 10⁴ cells per well, measurements were taken at different time points. OD values within the 400–700 nm wavelength range underwent measurement via the microplate reader. By comparing OD values, the levels of intracellular ROS were assessed.

Detection of the material’s ability to generate and inhibit ROS

ESR (Jesfa200, JEOL, Japan) was used to detect ROS generation. After 5 min of ultrasonic treatment (1.0 W, 50% duty cycle, 1 MHz) and without ultrasonic treatment, ·OH was detected using DMPO (Chronon Chemicals), and 1O₂ was detected using TEMP (Chronon Chemicals). ESR (Jesfa200, JEOL, Japan) was used to monitor the ability to inhibit ROS: Add the test samples to the Fenton reaction system separately, and detect ·OH in the reaction system using DMPO (Chronon Chemicals).

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were provided by Chongqing Medical University. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, GIBCO) supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution and 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C and 5% CO₂, changed the culture medium every two days. Besides that, BMSCs were isolated from 4-week-old SD rats (Chongqing Medical University Animal Research Center), and all animal studies were approved by the Chongqing Medical University Animal Research Committee. To summarize, the muscle and connective tissue were removed from the rat femur and tibia, the bone ends should then be trimmed, and rinse the bone marrow cavity with culture medium (α-MEM) and collect the cell suspensionand. The bone marrow suspension was cultured in α-MEM medium (GIBCO), which supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 3 days, the medium was changed, Subsequently, medium changes were performed every two days, and the cells were cultured to the third generation for use.

Bacterial/cell co-culture model

Refer to previous experimental methods [36], HUVEC cells and P2-generation BMSCs were seeded at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 6-well culture plate, and these cells were cultured overnight in DMEM or α-MEM medium (with 10% fetal bovine serum and without penicillin-streptomycin solution) at 37℃ in a CO2 incubator. The next day, HUVECs and BMSCs were treated with DMEM and α-MEM culture medium (added 10% antibiotic-free fetal bovine serum) containing S.aureus (MOI 10) respectively, under a co-culture environment of 37℃ and 5% CO2 incubator. Subsequent experiments and analyses were conducted according to the intervention conditions.

Evaluation of antibacterial activity in vitro

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Escherichia coli (E. coli) were used as examples of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, respectively. A bacterial suspension with a concentration of 108 CFU/mL was divided into five groups and subjected to different interventions: Control group, PBD nanoparticle intervention group (addition of 1% PBD nanoparticles), PGI microsphere intervention group (1% PGI microspheres), US intervention group (US parameters: power 1.0 W, aera 9.6 cm2, duty cycle 50%, frequency 1 MHz and 10 min), and PGI@US intervention group (US intervention after addition of 1%PGI microspheres, and parameters are similar as US group). After intervention, the samples were placed in a bacterial incubator for shaking for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of bacterial suspension from each group was spread on LB solid medium using a plate spreader. After spreading, the plates were incubated in a bacterial incubator for 24 h to observe colony growth. The crystal violet staining procedure followed identical conditions as the plate coating method. After intervention, the bacterial suspension was directly incubated in a 12-well plate for 48 h to form biofilms. Upon completion of incubation, the plate was removed, the supernatant was discarded, and the plate was gently rinsed three times with saline. The biofilm was then fixed with 4% polyformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by removal of the fixative. The plate was rinsed twice with saline and the waste solution was discarded. Crystal violet dye was subsequently added and stained for 10 min. The dye was removed, and the biofilm was gently rinsed until the supernatant became colorless. After drying, the biofilm formation in each experimental group was observed.

Detection of cell activity

Different interventions were applied to HUVEC and BMSC cells to observe their effects on proliferation, viability, toxicity, adhesion, and morphology. Cellular analysis was performed using cytoskeleton staining, live/dead staining, CCK-8 assays, and LDH toxicity tests. First, after subjecting cells to different interventions for 24 h, cell proliferation and cytotoxicity were assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime) and LDH assay kit (Beyotime) in 96-well plates. After co-culturing HUVEC cells with the materials for 1, 3, and 5 days, cell viability was assessed using a live/dead staining kit (L3224, Invitrogen). Following removal of the culture medium and washing with PBS, a composite dye (containing calmodulin-dependent protease AM and propidium iodide) was added. Cells were incubated for 15 min before observation and documentation under a confocal microscope. The effects of microspheres on the adhesion and morphology of BMSCs were assessed using cytoskeletal staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China). After co-culturing BMSCs with the materials for 1, 3, and 5 days. After washing the cells three times with PBS buffer, fix them with 4% paraformaldehyde for 25 min, and then permeabilize them with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. All of these steps were carried out at room temperature. BMSCs were stained with Alexa Fluor 594 phalloidin (Dilution ratio: 1:400) and 4,6-diamino-2-phenylindole dichloride (DAPI, 8 mg/mL), then examined and imaged using a copolymer microscope (Andor, England).

Cell scratch assays and cell migration assays

Six-well plates are used for cell scratch assays and cell migration assays. Create scratch wounds using the tip of a 100 µL pipette and the cell density is 5.5 × 10⁵ cells per well, staining all cells with a live/dead staining kit to assess migration rates across different groups after 24 h and then imaging. Transwell migration assays were conducted using Transwell chambers. Specifically, 0.4 μm Transwell chambers were placed in six-well plates. The upper chamber was seeded with 5 × 10⁴ cells and subjected to different intervention conditions. After 24 h, Crystal violet was used to stain the cells for 15 min after they had been fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Results were then obtained using a microscope.

Vascularization capacity assessment

The capacity of the material to promote vascularization was comprehensively evaluated through angiogenesis assays, immunofluorescence (IF) staining, and Western blot (WB) analysis. In the angiogenesis assay, 20 µL of matrix gel was first added to each well of a 24-well plate at 4 °C and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 30–60 min. Subsequently, 4 × 10⁵ HUVECs were seeded into each well and subjected to different group interventions (intervention conditions as previously described). After 8 h of incubation, cell nuclei and actin were labeled, and cells were observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope. ImageJ software is used to analyze vascular-related parameters. After subjecting cells to different intervention conditions for 2 days, immunofluorescent staining was performed on the various groups. Firstly, the cells undergo fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Then were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. After that, cells were incubated with primary antibodies. Dilution ratios for VEGF (Proteintech) and CD31(Proteintech) with 1:200, HIF -1α (Abcam, UK) with 1:100. After that, primary antibody incubation and secondary antibody binding were performed, and protein expression was examined using confocal microscopy. The WB experiment first involved lysing cells from different groups using cell lysis buffer, extracting proteins, and measuring protein concentration with the Bicinchoninic Acid Assay Kit. Proteins were then denatured by boiling in a metal bath for 10 min. Prepare electrophoresis gel and buffer. Add marker and target proteins to the upper gel. Run electrophoresis at 60 V for the upper gel and 100 V for the lower gel. Transfer the gel at 250 mA for 120 min. Wash the membrane in Tris-Buffered Saline Tween (TBST). Block with rapid blocking solution for 30 min. Incubate with primary and secondary antibodies separately. Finally, expose for visualization.

Osteogenesis-related capability testing

The osteogenic potential of BMSCs was validated through alkaline phosphatase staining, alizarin red staining, immunofluorescence staining, and Western blot analysis. Perform the ALP assay using a 6-well plate. Induce BMSCs with osteogenic induction medium for 7 days across different intervention groups, followed by ALP staining (37 °C, 20 min) and microscopic imaging. For ARS staining, a 24-well plate was used. Cultivated for 21 days using osteogenic induction medium for BMSCs in different intervention groups, followed by ARS staining (37 °C, 30 min) and microscopic observation. After 5 days of osteogenic induction and intervention in confocal dishes, immunofluorescence staining was performed following the same procedure as before. Protein expression was then examined using confocal microscopy. Immunofluorescent staining was performed on the various groups. Firstly, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then permeabilized in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After that, they were blocked into 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, and incubate with primary antibodies. Dilution ratios for OPN (Proteintech) and RUNX2 (Abways) are all 1:200, and BMP2 (Abcam, UK) diluted 1:100. After that, primary antibody incubation and secondary antibody binding were performed, and protein expression was examined using confocal microscopy. The Western blot (WB) procedure followed the same steps as before: protein extraction and quantification were conducted after cell intervention, followed by electrophoresis, membrane transfer, and antibody incubation. Finally, protein expression was visualized on a chemiluminescent imaging system.

Establishment of animal models

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: IACUC-CQMU-2025-0251). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats weighing 300 g were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection (dose: 30 mg/kg) of pentobarbital/saline solution (2%). A 3 mm diameter, 2.5 mm deep bone defect was created on the lateral femoral condyle by removing hair around the femoral neck to fully expose the femur. All animals were randomly divided into six groups: the simple bone defect group, the infected bone defect (IBD) group, the IBD + PGI group, the IBD + US group, the IBD + PGI@US group, and the IBD + Vancomycin (Van) group. Except for the group with simple bone defects, all other groups received an injection of 200 µL of S. aureus suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL). The PGI injection group received 500 µL/kg, and then the wound was sutured. The vancomycin intervention group received 40 mg/kg administered via continuous injection for three days. US intervention parameters were set at 0.1 W/cm2, 1 MHz, 50% duty cycle and 10 min with continuous intervention for three days. n = 6 indicates biological replicates.

Evaluation of micro-CT

Collected bone tissue samples respectively at 3 days, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks after modeling. The femurs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at previous 3 day. Subsequently, these femurs were imaged using a micro-CT scanner, and the following parameters were then used to generate 3D reconstructions: BV/TV, bone density, trabecular number, trabecular separation and trabecular thickness.

Histological analysis

After Micro-CT analysis, rat femur specimens were prepared as paraffin-embedded tissue samples for subsequent histological evaluation. Samples were immersed in 12.5% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for eight weeks to decalcify and soften the tissue, with the decalcifying solution replaced every two days. Subsequently, the samples were dehydrated in alcohol at different concentration gradients, washed with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sagittal sections of the lateral condyle of the femur were cut at a thickness of 5 μm and perform H&E/Masson staining and immunofluorescence staining. The general procedure is as follows: Air-dry the femur sections, then incubate them in 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min, followed by blocking in 5% goat serum for 30 min. Dilute the primary antibody in goat serum at the appropriate ratio and incubate overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were: IL-6 (Affinity, China), OCN (Affinity), and CD31 (Affinity). The primary antibody was incubated over the night at 4 °C, then the fluorescent secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature in the dark. Stain cell nuclei with DAPI. Cover the slide and observe under a laser microscope.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1), while image plotting and data analysis were performed using Origin 2021, GraphPad Prism, and ImageJ software. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. To evaluate differences between multiple groups, a one-way or two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed and multiple comparisons between groups were performed using the Tukey test. Significant differences were determined based on P < 0.05.

Result

Synthesis and characterization of PBD nanoparticles and PBD@GelMA/HAMA-ICG functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres

Based on previous research methodologies, this study employed a strategy combining oxidative polymerization with protein self-assembly to prepare PBD-ICG composite nanoparticles. In brief, a BSA-Fe³⁺ complex is first prepared to serve as an initiator and assembly template, then added aniline undergoes polymerization under Fe³⁺ oxidation. The resulting PANI chains undergo in situ self-assembly with BSA through hydrophobic interactions. Building upon this foundation, DFO and ICG molecules were further incorporated via electrostatic adsorption and stabilized within the PANI-BSA-formed tertiary structure. Following synthesis, we first examined the constructed PB and PBD nanoparticles and verified via XPS whether DFO was successfully loaded onto the nanoparticles (Figure S1, Supporting Information). As there are no differentiating elements between PB and PBD, we present the results for elemental content. The nitrogen content in PB nanoparticles is 5.34%, while that in PBD nanoparticles is 3.61%. The carbon content increases from 79.19% in PB nanoparticles to 81.66% in PBD nanoparticles. This shift in elemental composition stems from the significant carbon content in DFO. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to examine the morphology of PBD-ICG nanoparticles (Fig. 1A). The elemental distribution was analyzed using Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) on representative samples. The images show that the nanoparticle surfaces exhibit characteristic Na elements with a distinct distribution following the introduction of ICG, and the quantitative data further confirm the presence of the corresponding characteristic elements (Fig. 1B, C). In addition, particle size analysis and zeta potential measurements were performed on PBD nanoparticles and PBD-ICG nanoparticles (Fig. 1D). The results of the dynamic light scattering (DLS) test indicated that the particle size of PBD nanoparticles was 125.6 nm, with a potential of-26.44 mV. After ICG incorporation, the particle size increased to 169.9 nm, and the potential decreased to-29.67 mV, representing a reduction of 3.23 mV compared to the original PBD nanoparticles (Figure S2, Supporting Information). This negative charge characteristic facilitates further adsorption on the microsphere surface. After drug loading, the encapsulation efficiencies of DFO and ICG were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and a microplate reader, respectively. The corresponding concentrations were calculated based on peak areas (Table S1, Figure S3, Supporting Information) and fluorescence intensities (Table S2, S3, Figure S4, Supporting Information). The results showed that the drug encapsulation rates for DFO and ICG were 88.84% and 50.31% respectively. Based on this, HPLC was used to analyze the release rates of DFO under different pH conditions and over varying time periods, and it was found that (Figure S5, Table S4,5, Supporting Information), the release rate of DFO from PBD-ICG nanoparticles is highest at a pH of 7.5. Furthermore, considering that the PGI system must first be stimulated by US in an acidic environment to function, it is essential to test the stability of DFO under acidic conditions, this ensures the feasibility of the second-stage repair therapy. DFO content was measured in the supernatant of PBD-ICG nanoparticles treated with US in an acidic environment (pH 5.5) using HPLC (Table S6, Supporting Information), comparison with the results of the DFO release curve suggests that the increase in DFO release induced by US is significantly lower than the release variation caused by pH changes. This fully confirms that, under acidic infection microenvironments, US-triggered PGI systems do not cause a large amount of premature DFO release, and that the sequential release of the system is stable and reliable. In summary, by taking advantage of electrostatic adsorption, PBD nanoparticles were co-incubated with ICG solution to endow the nanoparticles with US responsiveness, thereby constructing dual-function responsive nanoparticles (PBD-ICG nanoparticles) capable of sensing microenvironmental changes.

After the synthesis of nanoparticles, we modified gelatin (Gel) and hyaluronic acid (HA) using methacrylic anhydride (MA), and constructed covalently crosslinked hydrogels GelMA and HAMA through UV-initiated radical polymerization. The results from the H Nuclear Magnetic Resonance Spectra (1H-NMR) indicate that MA has been successfully grafted onto the molecular chains of gelatin and hyaluronic acid (Fig. 1E). Therefore, we utilized microfluidic technology and UV crosslinking to construct GelMA/HAMA composite hydrogel microspheres were fabricated as a micrometer-scale drug delivery platform, Then, through electrostatic adsorption, the PBD-ICG nanoparticles were loaded onto the surface of the hydrogel microspheres, ultimately forming a complete functional-responsive hydrogel microsphere capable of sensing changes in the microenvironment (PBD-ICG@GelMA/HAMA, PGI). As shown in the optical microscope images (Figure S6, Supporting Information), microspheres exhibit uniform distribution in solution, presenting as spherical structures with consistent morphology and size. We used a confocal laser scanning microscope to observe the PGI at a wavelength of 810 nm, as shown in Fig. 1F, The nanoparticles exhibited green fluorescence, and during the synthesis of the GelMA/HAMA composite hydrogel microspheres, Cy5.5-NHS was introduced to make GelMA/HAMA show red fluorescence, it was clearly observable that the surface of the red-fluorescent microspheres was uniformly covered with green fluorescence, indicating the successful loading of PBD-ICG NPs and the complete structure of this functional-responsive hydrogel microsphere. Furthermore, SEM analysis revealed that compared to pure GelMA/HAMA, nanoparticles on the surface of PGI microspheres obviously, indicating the successful loading of PBD-ICG nanoparticles (Fig. 1G). This laid a crucial foundation for the subsequent temporal treatment of the PGI hydrogel microspheres. To verify whether the target material was successfully loaded onto the hydrogel microspheres, we analyzed and validated the material using a Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) (Fig. 1H), compared to the absorption peaks of BSA, the PANI-BSA assembly exhibits the C-N stretching vibration peak of aniline at 1303 cm⁻¹ and the structural vibration peak of -NH⁺ at 1147 cm⁻¹ [37]. Furthermore, compared to pure PB nanoparticles, both PBD nanoparticles and PGI hydrogel microspheres containing DFO exhibit sharp -OH stretching peaks at 3645–3300 cm⁻¹, this is attributed to the characteristic structure C(= O)N-(OH)R of hydroxamic acid (HOAt) in DFO. And the vibrational absorption band of the sulfonic acid group (R-SO₃H) is also observed at 1385–1340 cm⁻¹. Additionally, after loading ICG, the PGI hydrogel microsphere group exhibits a new alkenyl C = C characteristic peak at 1675–1640 cm⁻¹. It can be observed that a stretching vibration absorption peak of the SO₂ group (RSO₂OR’) from the sulfonate ester is present at 1190–1120 cm⁻¹. Meanwhile, further energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of the PGI hydrogel microspheres revealed that (Fig. 1I), in addition to the fundamental elements C, N, and O carried by GelMA and HAMA, the microspheres also contained sulfur (S)—a common element in both DFO and ICG, as well as sodium (Na)—an element specific to ICG. This effectively demonstrated the successful encapsulation and loading of the target drugs. Considering that the effective release of nanoparticles is a crucial prerequisite for PGI microspheres to exert their effects in vivo, we continuously monitored nanoparticle release over 9 days. Owing to the intrinsic fluorescence of ICG, we directly measured the fluorescence intensity at 810 nm in the supernatant using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Figure S7, Supporting Information). Results showed the fastest increase in fluorescence intensity at 5 days and with stabilized level between 5 and 9 days.

Dual responsiveness and ROS balance capabilities of functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres PGI

The construction of the ROS balance system in PGI microspheres is central to achieving balanced antibacterial and anti-inflammatory capabilities, and the prerequisite for its effectiveness lies in its exceptional dual-response capability. Firstly, we validated the pH responsiveness of PBD-ICG nanoparticles. Near-infrared absorption spectroscopy revealed that the absorption peak of nanoparticles shifted significantly to the left as the pH increased (Fig. 2A), indicating that PANI underwent deprotonation during pH elevation, transitioning from the conductive open ES state to the insulating compact EB state. Furthermore, at pH 6.5 and 7.5, the main chain collapse induced by deprotonation of PANI reduced the particle size of PBD-ICG nanoparticles from 144.7 nm to 63.88 nm (Figure S8, Supporting Information). The above data directly demonstrate that molecular conformational changes occur within nanoparticles during the acid-base environmental transition [38], this effectively promoted the release of DFO in nanoparticles. On this basis, we further investigated the effects of pH changes at the microsphere level, as demonstrated by the shear rate-viscosity curve (Figure S9, Supporting Information). To better account for the dynamic structural changes in the material during use, we selected the intermediate shear rate value—specifically, the change in the viscosity coefficient of the PGI hydrogel at a shear rate of 0.543 s⁻¹. This is illustrated by the results of the rate-viscosity curve, at a shear rate of 0.543 s⁻¹, the viscosity of PGI decreased significantly from 245,000 mPa/s at pH 5.5 to 22,751 mPa/s at pH 7.5, This effectively demonstrates the effect of pH changes on this hydrogel microsphere system.

To further investigate the effects of changes in microenvironmental pH on hydrogels, PGI hydrogels were incubated for 24 h under different pH conditions, after which their strain modulus was measured. The results show that as the pH increases, the corresponding loss modulus also gradually increases, and the PGI hydrogel incubated at pH 7.5 exhibits the highest loss modulus (Figure S10, Supporting Information), indicating that the PGI hydrogel exhibits the highest flow properties under these conditions, which may be attributed to conformational changes in the nanoparticles leading to the disruption of the network structure on the hydrogel surface. Furthermore, considering previous studies that have shown pH changes to have a certain influence on the GelMA/HAMA hydrogel system itself, this factor may also contribute to the disruption of the PGI hydrogel network structure under alkaline conditions, thereby promoting an increase in its loss modulus. SEM results (Figure S11, Supporting Information) demonstrate that as pH increases, the microsphere surface becomes wrinkled and the surface-loaded nanoparticles are significantly reduced. This may be attributed to the interchain electrostatic repulsion of the functional groups (e.g., carboxyl, amino, etc.) in GelMA/HAMA hydrogels, which changes with increasing pH. Consequently, the cross-linked network becomes loosened or even partially disintegrated, leading to gel softening [39]. But clearly, this clearly provides favorable conditions for nanoparticles to exert their effects in physiological environments. Secondly, we validated the US responsiveness of PGI microspheres at the cellular level. We found that under US intensity of 1.0–1.5 W, the combined intervention of PGI and US effectively eliminated the negative effects caused by bacterial infection (Fig. 2B). The cell viability was optimal and nearly consistent with the uninfected group, whereas inappropriate ROS release induced by other US intensities resulted in low cell viability. Accordingly, we selected an US intensity of 1 W for subsequent studies. To further investigate the pH sensitive of PGI microspheres directly, we incubated PGI microspheres in varying pH conditions for 24 h, then added the supernatant to FeCl3 solution to monitor DFO release. Figure 2C demonstrates that when pH exceeded 7.0, a distinct dark-colored complex formed in the FeCl3 solution. This clearly indicates that DFO in the nanoparticles can effectively release DFO and form complexes with iron ions under weakly alkaline conditions.

Based on the above experiments, we validated the capability of US-triggered ROS generation and PBD nanoparticles inhibit excess ROS product. To directly capture free radical signals, electron spin resonance (ERS) technology was employed. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, after US irradiation, the ERS spectra of both the ICG group and the PGI group clearly exhibited the characteristic quadruplet peaks of hydroxyl radicals (·OH) (peak intensity ratio 1:2:2:1) and the characteristic triplet peak of singlet oxygen (¹O₂) (peak intensity ratio 1:1:1). This conclusive evidence demonstrates that the PBD-ICG component loaded onto the surface of PGI microspheres can effectively generate ROS under US irradiation, establishing the basis for its antibacterial activity. Additionally, as a control, no characteristic signals for ·OH or ¹O₂ were detected in PGI hydrogel microspheres under conditions without US stimulation, ruling out the possibility of intrinsic ROS generation (Figure S12, Supporting Information), and under different pH conditions, both US triggered ICG and ICG alone can promote the efficient generation of ROS (Figure S13, Supporting Information). After that, we separately added materials from different groups to the Fenton reaction system at pH 7.5 for observation. As illustrated in Fig. 2F, compared to the Control group, nanoparticles or microspheres containing DFO significantly suppress the ·OH production in the reaction system. This comparative data strongly demonstrates that as normal pH microenvironment, for materials containing DFO, the ROS content in the Fenton reaction system is significantly reduced.

Antibacterial effects of PGI hydrogel microspheres

For IBD, effective clearance of the infected environment is a prerequisite for promoting subsequent osteogenic repair. Preliminary experiments have demonstrated that PGI hydrogel microspheres can effectively release ROS under ultrasonic stimulation. Therefore, E. coli and S. aureus were selected to validate the antimicrobial properties of PGI microspheres, as representatives of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively. Bacterial plate-count experiments demonstrated that co-culturing pathogenic bacteria with either PGI hydrogel microspheres or PBD nanoparticles alone for 12 h did not effectively inhibit bacterial growth. 10 min of US intervention alone also showed only limited inhibitory effects on colony growth, with results unsatisfactory. Under the combined PGI and US intervention, both S. aureus and E. coli exhibited significantly reduced colony counts, effectively demonstrating the superior antibacterial performance of PGI hydrogel microspheres when subjected to US treatment (Fig. 2G). It is well known that in chronic infection settings, pathogenic bacteria develop the ability to form biofilms to evade antibiotics and immune responses [4]. Therefore, the biofilm formation capability was validated through crystal violet staining, as shown in Fig. 2H, the control group exhibited a completer and more robust biofilm. Moreover, S. aureus demonstrated enhanced biofilm formation capacity throughout the experiment. However, encouragingly, the combined intervention of US and PGI hydrogel microspheres significantly disrupted biofilm formation, this suggests that this therapeutic system can effectively impede biofilm formation and eliminate early-stage biofilms that have already formed. Quantitative analysis using a microplate reader similarly demonstrated (Fig. 2I, J) that the combined US and PGI intervention group exhibited lower absorbance values, indicating stronger biofilm disruption capacity. The inhibition rates against S. aureus and E. coli biofilms were 75.53% and 95.22%, respectively. It is also noteworthy that US intervention alone can partially disrupt biofilm integrity. Although the effect is not ideal, we speculate that this may be due to the cavitation effect or mechanical wave vibrations generated by US, which physically open the biofilm to some extent, thereby further enhancing the antibacterial properties of PGI. Finally, the efficacy of the PGI@US intervention strategy was visually validated through bacterial cell co-culture. As shown in Fig. 2K, pH changes in the cell/bacterial co-culture supernatants across different intervention groups were monitored continuously for 72 h as an indicator of the infection microenvironment status. The supernatant pH in the PGI@US intervention group exhibited a significant shift toward the control group’s pH level by 48 h, providing strong support for the antibacterial effect of the combined PGI and US intervention.

PGI hydrogel microspheres exhibit excellent biocompatibility and effectively recruit cells

Extensive prior literature has demonstrated that GelMA and HAMA hydrogels contain organic components similar to those found in the body and exhibit reliable biocompatibility [21], but considering that this study involves the loading of multiple drugs onto the GelMA/HAMA hydrogel and its need injected into defect sites within the body, testing its biocompatibility is essential. Materials were divided into five groups to conduct comprehensive safety testing: the Control group, the PBD nanoparticle group, the PGI hydrogel microsphere group, the US intervention group, and the PGI@US intervention group. By co-culturing each group of materials with bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) for 1 day, 3 days, and 5 days, and applying appropriate US parameters (1 MHz, 1 W, 50% duty cycle, 10 min) to intervene in specific groups. First, cell viability staining and cytoskeletal staining were performed on each group of cells, followed by confocal laser scanning microscopy to observe their morphology. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, cells maintained good conditions and adhered well to the microspheres over the course of cultivation. It was also observed that cells on the microspheres retained excellent morphology under US intervention. Based on this foundation, the proliferation capacity and cellular status were assessed using a cell counting kit (CCK-8) and a LDH assay kit. Data from Fig. 3C-F demonstrate that cells in all groups exhibited robust viability with no significant differences. The duration of the 5-day intervention did not impair cellular proliferation capacity. This provides favorable foundational conditions for subsequent cell recruitment and promotion of vascularization and osteogenesis at defect sites, while also indirectly confirming that the ROS levels generated under US exposure do not pose a threat to cells. Additionally, we examined the effects of PGI@US treatment on cellular ROS production at different time points to demonstrate the safety of this therapeutic approach. As shown in (Figure S14, Supporting Information), although PGI@US intervention directly increases intracellular ROS levels, the time-course curve indicates that ROS spontaneously recovers to pre-intervention levels within 10 h post-treatment. This effectively demonstrates that under normal physiological microenvironments, the spontaneous dissociation of PGI-balanced microspheres releases DFO to inhibit the excessive production of ROS. This directly confirms that the ROS dose generated by US acting on PGI hydrogel microspheres poses no threat to cells, highlighting the excellent biosafety of PGI hydrogel microspheres.

PGI hydrogel microspheres exerts a pro-angiogenic effect

In this study, DFO was loaded onto nanoparticles capable of sensing pH changes to facilitate protonation and deprotonation, and subsequently loaded onto functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres, aiming to release DFO under specific conditions. Based on this, we validated the pro-angiogenic capability of the constructed hydrogel microspheres. First, we validated the tubule-forming capacity of HUVECs under different conditions, cells were seeded onto matrix gel and cultured for 8 h under different intervention conditions, skeletal and nuclear staining was performed to thoroughly observe cell tubule formation. As shown in the fluorescence microscope image in Fig. 5A, the control group exhibited almost no formation of intact lumens, with most lesions appearing as punctate or dendritic nodules. Conversely, both the PBD NPs culture group and the PGI@US intervention group demonstrated superior tube-forming capacity, exhibiting superior canal density and morphology compared to other groups. Further quantitative analysis revealed that the total tube length and number of tube-forming nodes in Group PBD and PGI@US were significantly increased (Fig. 5B, C). This directly reflects that HUVECs exhibited a more favorable tube-forming trend following combined PGI and US intervention, demonstrating superior tube-forming efficacy. Subsequently, we investigated the expression levels of vascularization-related proteins. Western blotting revealed (Fig. 5D) that the expression of VEGF and HIF-1α was significantly elevated in both the PBD group and the US combined with PGI group (Fig. 5E-G). Although CD31 expression showed no statistically significant difference in the PBD group, VEGF expression was markedly increased following US combined with PGI intervention compared to the Control group. Immunofluorescence staining further corroborated these findings (Fig. 5H-J). Statistical analysis revealed that the PBD and PGI@US groups exhibited significantly higher mean fluorescence intensities (Figs. 5K-M), providing more direct evidence that our intervention was effective. Bones are formed through endochondral ossification and intramembranous ossification, both of which require the presence of osteoblasts and new blood vessels [40], hence, in bone defects, promoting the occurrence of vascularization is essential for facilitating bone healing. In summary, the excellent pro-angiogenic effects of the functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres PGI create a more ideal repair environment for osteogenesis in bone defect sites.

PGI@US system promotes osteogenic differentiation

In the previous section, we demonstrated the exceptional vascularization capacity of the functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres, a further investigation was conducted to verify and explore whether PGI microspheres can effectively promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. Similarly, the experiment was divided into five groups: Control, PBD, PGI, US, and PGI@US, with corresponding interventions administered to each group. Firstly, BMSCs from different intervention groups were cultured in osteogenic induction medium for 7 days and 21 days, after which alkaline phosphatase staining (ALP) and alizarin red staining (ARS) were performed. As demonstrated by the ALP staining results in Fig. 6A, BMSCs treated with PGI@US exhibited significantly deeper staining intensity compared to the Control, US, PBD, and PGI groups. Additionally, ARS conjugated to calcitonin also exhibited a deeper red color in the PGI@US group (Fig. 6B), indicating higher calcium nodule formation in this group. On this basis, to further validate the osteogenic potential of the functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres PGI, BMSCs cultured under five different intervention conditions were treated with osteogenic induction medium for 7 days. WB analysis and IF staining were subsequently performed to assess the expression levels of relevant osteogenic proteins. Western blot results revealed (Fig. 6C) that compared with the other four groups, the PGI@US intervention group exhibited significantly elevated expression levels of osteopontin (OPN), BMP2, and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) in BMSCs (Fig. 6G-I). Additionally, as shown in Fig. 6D-F, immunofluorescence staining of BMP-2, RUNX2, and OPN in BMSCs post-intervention revealed that the PGI@US group exhibited markedly elevated expression levels of these molecules compared to the other four groups, with the highest mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6J-L). In view of the above results, we conclude that under US intervention conditions, PGI functional-responsive microspheres can significantly enhance the expression of osteogenesis-related molecules and promote osteogenic differentiation.

US combined functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres PGI exert antibacterial effects and alleviate inflammatory responses in vivo

Based on the promising antibacterial, angiogenic, and osteogenic potential demonstrated in in vitro experiments, we further established animal models to validate their therapeutic efficacy in vivo. For comprehensively and systematically evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of functional-responsive hydrogel microspheres PGI for infectious bone defects in vivo, we constructed a 3 mm defect diameter in the lateral condyle of rat femurs (Figure S15A, Supporting Information). Rats were divided into six experimental groups randomly: the simple bone defect group, the IBD group, the IBD + PGI group, the IBD + US group, the IBD + PGI@US group, and the IBD+ Vancomycin (Van) group. Except for the bone defect group, the other five groups received an injection of S. aureus (1 × 108 CFU/mL) into the defect site to establish the IBD model and undergo interventional treatment. The specific process is shown in Fig. 7A. After three days of treatment, bacterial plate count experiments were taken from the tissue surrounding the defect site in rats to observe infection status. As shown in Figure S16 (Supporting Information), infection was not well controlled in the group receiving PGI microspheres alone. However, the PGI@US treatment group showed considerable reduction in colony counts compared to both the IBD group and the PGI intervention group. This indicates that PGI microspheres cannot effectively exert their anti-infective capabilities in the absence of US, consistent with the conclusions of our in vitro antimicrobial experiments. Moreover, it was surprising to find that the colony count in the PGI@US treatment group even lower than the Van group. This demonstrates that PGI microspheres combined with US therapy exhibit superior anti-infective capabilities compared to antibiotic treatment, along with greater therapeutic controllability and lower risks of bacterial resistance. Considering that the initiation of the second phase of bone healing in the PGI treatment system depends on changes in the microenvironmental pH, we used an implantable pH sensor to monitor local tissue pH levels at the defect sites of rats in the IBD and PGI@US groups 3 days after model induction and plotted the pH change curves (Table S7, Figure S17, Supporting Information), The results showed that the pH around the infected tissue had already decreased by 24 h, and the rate of decline was greater between 24 and 48 h, which is consistent with findings reported in previous studies [41]. Analysis of the local tissue pH results for the PGI@US group revealed that the microenvironment pH exhibited minimal variation between 0 and 24 h (ΔpHPGI@US=0.65<ΔpHIBD=0.925), and showed a trend toward recovery after 48 h. This provides some evidence supporting the feasibility of using pH changes to initiate the second phase of osteogenic therapy.

PGI hydrogel microspheres promote vascularization and osteogenesis in infected bone defects in vivo

Before performing in vivo interventions, we first analyzed the expression of relevant proteins in the infectious bone defect (IBD) model at different time points using immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Figure S19, Supporting Information, we established an infectious bone defect model at the lateral condyle of the rat femur and collected samples at 3 days, 4 weeks, and 8 weeks post-surgery to evaluate the dynamic changes in key proteins. Results revealed a significant inflammatory peak in the defect area at 3 days post-surgery due to infectious factors. By 4 weeks and 8 weeks post-surgery, although inflammatory cell infiltration remained detectable, expression levels of both vascularization and osteogenesis related markers were markedly upregulated. Notably, in the group treated with injection of PGI microspheres alone for 8 weeks, although its vascularization and osteogenesis effects were superior to the IBD group, it still lagged significantly behind the PGI@US group. This may be due to the PGI microspheres lacking US stimulation, preventing timely control of the infected environment and consequently delaying bone healing. However, as the infection was cleared by the autoimmune response and the PGI microspheres degraded, the encapsulated PBD nanoparticles were gradually released over time, ultimately exerting a certain osteogenic effect. The experimental results in this section demonstrate that following effective infection control, PGI@US intervention can autonomously release PBD nanoparticles to promote vascularization and osteogenesis, significantly enhancing bone healing capacity in infected bone defects.These findings indicate that the repair progression of IBD exhibits distinct temporal characteristics, the primary biological events differ at different points in time. Therefore, based on the aforementioned characteristics, the PGI microsphere is expected to align with this repair sequence, thereby demonstrating excellent antibacterial and osteogenic effects. In addition, histological sections and H&E staining of rat heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues under different treatment conditions confirmed that the materials exhibited good biocompatibility and did not cause any significant adverse reactions (Figure S20, Supporting Information).

Following completion of the intervention in the infected environment, the bone promoting effect of PGI hydrogel microspheres was further evaluated. Direct observation of bone tissue healing at the defect site after 4 or 8 weeks under different intervention conditions revealed that after PGI@US intervention in IBD rats, the defect site was nearly completely healed by week 8 (Figure S15C, Supporting Information). Subsequently, bone tissue underwent decalcification for subsequent sectioning and staining. Figure 7D and F show Masson staining indicating that in the 4-week IBD group, there was almost no new bone tissue formation around the defect site. Even in the simple defect group, only minimal bone tissue generation was observed. Improvement in the IBD group was not seen until week 8, whereas the PGI@US treatment group demonstrated excellent osteogenic effects. By week 4, substantial new bone tissue had already ingrown around the defect site. By week 8, the defect area were completely filled with new-formation bone and the statistical analysis of the new bone area fully confirmed this conclusion (Fig. 7E, G). Next, immunofluorescence staining was performed for the angiogenic marker CD31 and the osteogenic marker OCN, as shown in Fig. 8A. These markers were labeled with red fluorescence, revealing significantly higher fluorescence intensity in the PGI@US intervention group. Quantitative analysis of the markers confirmed this finding (Figure S18F, G, Supporting Information). This demonstrates that the combined intervention of PGI microspheres and US effectively promotes vascularization and osteogenesis. Although preliminary in vitro experiments indicated that DFO alone is effective in promoting vascularized bone regeneration, and the extent of new bone formation in the group treated with DFO alone for IBD were unsatisfactory (Figure S21, Supporting Information). This was attributed to the inability to effectively control the infectious microenvironment in the early stages, as well as the short duration of action of free DFO, which is rapidly metabolized and cleared, thereby failing to effectively keep pace with the pathological progression of IBD. These findings further confirm the feasibility of establishing a PGI therapeutic system to address the treatment of infectious bone defects.

Discussion

Infected bone defects, as clinically intractable conditions, exhibit severely impaired self-repair capabilities due to constraints imposed by complex infection microenvironments. The infection induced inflammatory cell infiltration and cellular metabolic byproducts in the pathological region, creating a microenvironment characterized by weak acidity. Persistent infection further accumulates excessive reactive oxygen species, significantly impeding bone repair. Therefore, the repair of infectious bone defects must follow a sequential principle, first eliminate the infectious microenvironment and then promote vascularized osteogenesis to achieve phased, sequential repair of infectious bone defects. Based on the drug properties of DFO, the pH responsiveness of PANI, and the US sensitivity of ICG, this study constructed functionalized PBD-ICG nanoparticles via a ternary synthesis method, This enables DFO to stably “reside” between molecular chains through intermolecular hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions. Thus, we speculate that this process further promotes the release and action of DFO from the PGI hydrogel microspheres, thereby synergistically inhibiting the excessive production of ROS. At the same time, it is worth noting that the particle size of the nanoparticles underwent significant changes following ICG modification, which may be attributed to the following three factors: (1) Modification via electrostatic adsorption may lead to the formation of additional core-shell structures in the nanoparticles, thereby affecting their particle size [42]; (2) As a typical tricarbocyanine compound, the hydrophobic moieties of ICG may drive spontaneous avoidance of contact between the hydrophobic regions and water in aqueous solutions, triggering particle aggregation and resulting in an increase in particle size [43]; (3) DLS measures the hydrated particle size. The increased hydrophobicity of PBD-ICG nanoparticles leads to the formation of a thicker hydration layer on their surface, which increases the hydrated radius and results in an overestimation of the particle size by DLS. However, this unexpected result does not affect the inherent functionality of the PBD-ICG nanoparticles; therefore, no significant adjustments were made to the size and structure of the nanoparticles in this study.

By further loading PBD-ICG nanoparticles onto GelMA/HAMA microspheres, the nanoparticles can be equipped with micron-scale carriers to enhance their therapeutic efficacy. Series characterization confirmed that PGI microspheres serve as an excellent carrier for efficient delivery and release of nanoparticles. This study makes DFO in the PGI microsphere system effectively binds to free iron, inhibiting the Fenton reaction and suppressing ROS production [36]. And experimental results demonstrate that the functional responsive hydrogel microspheres exhibit outstanding dual responsiveness. Under US stimulation, it efficiently generates ROS (·OH/1O₂), and fully demonstrates its ability to exert highly effective antibacterial action. While pH elevation induces the collapse of the nanoparticle framework to release DFO, thereby suppressing excessive ROS production. This intelligent system, which integrates external responses with physiological microenvironmental changes, achieves dynamic equilibrium controllability of ROS, effectively aligning with the sequential repair demands of IBD, establishing a solid foundation for the functional performance of PGI microspheres in vivo, thereby providing a reliable mechanism to suppress excessive ROS generation in the microenvironment.

As an initial step in sequential therapy, studies have demonstrated that PGI@US can generate ROS and effectively inhibit bacterial growth. However, it is noteworthy that following ultrasonic excitation of PBD nanoparticles, low levels of •OH and ¹O₂ generation remain detectable. This is likely attributed to the generation of hydrogen or hydroxyl radicals and hydrogen peroxide (ROS) through acoustic dissociation of water induced solely by ultrasonic action [44]. Previous studies have demonstrated that DFO can chelate free iron to inhibit the Fenton reaction, thereby suppressing ROS production [45]. And as a pro-angiogenic drug, DFO has been demonstrated in preliminary studies to effectively reduce HIF-1α degradation, thereby promoting angiogenesis in vascular endothelial cells [30, 31], and a rich blood supply provides an optimal regenerative environment for bone healing, thereby significantly promoting bone regeneration [46]. Before that, vascularization is an indispensable prerequisite and sustained motor for the osteogenic process. In addition to the ability of vascular endothelial cells themselves to secrete multiple osteogenic factors such as VEGF and BMP-2 [47], and the specific subtypes of bone-resorbing vascular cells (H-type) also directly regulate osteoblasts through the Notch signaling pathway [48]. Therefore, in this study, DFO plays two key roles: firstly, it inhibit the Fenton reaction, thereby reducing the sustained overproduction of ROS and maintaining oxidative stress homeostasis. Secondly, it independently exerts a reparative effect by upregulating the HIF-1α pathway, promoting the expression of proangiogenic genes, and consequently driving angiogenesis and bone regeneration to facilitate subsequent bone tissue repair. This ensures the therapeutic efficacy of IBD treatment across various pathological conditions. Here, we used experiments such as ERS to verify the role of DFO in inhibiting ROS production, which provides a foundation for the second phase of sequential repair. Before that, Verification of the expression of the relevant genes also confirms the pro-angiogenic and osteogenic effects of PGI@US intervention. However, it is worth noting that during in vitro experiments to validate angiogenesis, the PBD nanoparticle group exhibited angiogenic effects similar to those of PGI@US, which is attributed to the excellent angiogenic effects of the DFO drug within the nanoparticles. But related anti-infection experiments indicate that intervention with free DFO or PBD nanoparticles cannot achieve a satisfactory antibacterial effect. This suggests that the development of a PGI hydrogel microsphere system is essential for achieving both anti-infective and osteogenic effects.

The presence of an infected environment severely impedes the bone healing process and even leads to nonunion. Therefore, effectively eliminating the infected environment at the defect site is a necessary precondition for promoting bone repair [49]. Vancomycin (Van), as a broad-spectrum antibiotic, can effectively achieve anti-infective effects, some recent studies suggest that Van carries a risk of treatment failure and the emergence of resistant bacteria, but it remains a first-line drug for the clinical treatment of infections [50–52]. Therefore, in the design of this study, Van group was included in the animal experiments to objectively observe and validate the antimicrobial effects of the PGI@US therapeutic system. Although Van effectively suppresses infection and inflammatory responses in this study, its reparative effects remain limited. Conversely, this study demonstrates that PGI@US intervention not only exhibits antibacterial effects comparable to Van, but also displays superior osteogenic promotion. Previous research has shown that this osteogenic promotion is largely attributable to the vascularization and osteogenic effects of DFO within the hydrogel microspheres [53]. Although this study has demonstrated that the PGI@US sequential treatment system exhibits excellent antibacterial and osteogenic effects both in vitro and in vivo, it must be acknowledged that significant challenges remain in its clinical application and translation. Firstly, although this PGI hydrogel microsphere system has undergone multiple biosafety evaluations at the cellular level and in vitro H&E staining assessments, more comprehensive safety evaluations are still needed in the future; this is a prerequisite for future clinical translation. Furthermore, since bone tissue serves as the primary structural component supporting the body’s biomechanics, related mechanical testing is essential for further material development and functional evaluation. And further optimization is required for the appropriate material size, drug encapsulation capacity, and suitable US parameters for clinical use. It is worth noting that during in vitro experiments, we found that US treatment alone can produce a significant osteogenic effect on BMSCs. Although this effect is weaker than the PGI@US treatment system, it still holds important implications. Based on previously published literature, we believe this effect stems from the biological effects of low-intensity US itself [54], which may promote osteogenic differentiation and bone formation by activating VEGF and the RUNX2/Sox9 signaling pathways in BMSCs, the underlying mechanisms of this process remain unclear [55]. Therefore, considering the biological effects of US, the additional mechanisms it exerts during treatment require further exploration and clarification. This will facilitate more precise research, clinical translation, and therapeutic applications of US combined with materials in the future.

In summary, this study demonstrates that the multifunctional response hydrogel system designed based on microenvironment sensing shows promise for the repair of infectious bone defects. However, given the limited scope of the current research, its clinical translation still faces numerous challenges. Regarding materials, the long-term in vivo biosafety and metabolic pathways of nanoparticles and hydrogels require further validation. Regarding US parameters, given the disparity between experimental conditions and actual clinical scenarios, further exploration is needed to determine safe and effective treatment parameters based on tissue variability in the human body and to avoid potential tissue damage. Future work should involve systematic safety evaluations, parameter optimization, large-animal studies, and further mechanistic investigations to provide new insights for the precision treatment of infected bone defects.

Conclusion

Overall, this study innovatively constructed a kind of functional-responsive microsphere based on microenvironment sensing (PGI), which achieved stepwise and sequential treatment of infectious bone defects by regulating the ROS homeostasis. The mechanism of its action lies in the following: Firstly, through external US stimulation, the “ICG” part of the PGI hydrogel microspheres is precisely triggered, inducing the explosive release of ROS, which efficiently kills pathogens and regulates the infection microenvironment. Once the pH of the infection microenvironment returns to the normal physiological level, the microsphere with pH-responsive function disintegrates, DFO released from the nanoparticles directly inhibits the production of excessive ROS, while structural changes in the microspheres under alkaline conditions accelerate DFO release, thereby synergistically enhancing the inhibition of ROS and synergistically promoting vascularization and osteogenesis. This functional-responsive strategy overcomes the limitations of traditional antimicrobial materials prone to inducing oxidative stress damage by precisely regulating the equilibrium state of ROS, and plays a powerful role in promoting vascularized bone regeneration. Through systematic in vitro cell experiments (including antibacterial efficacy, cell compatibility, and osteogenic differentiation assessment) and in vivo rat infection-induced bone defect models, the PGI microspheres demonstrated remarkable antibacterial activity and excellent ability to promote vascularized osteogenesis. This study proposes a functional-responsive temporal sequential treatment strategy for infectious bone defects—first combating infection, then promoting bone formation—offering a novel approach for intelligent synergistic therapy of IBD.
