Different clinical forms of Plasmodium vivax infection result in unique T cell epitope-specific immune responses
Authors: Camila Medeiros Costa, Gregório Guilherme Almeida, Cecília S Lindestam Arlehamn, Camila Pereira Queiroz-Glauss, Victor Hugo Gonçalves Gunderman, Marcela Marísia Mayrink Pereira, Laurence Rodrigues Do Amaral, Dhelio Batista Pereira, Mauro Shugiro Tada, Olindo Assis Martins-Filho, Ricardo Tostes Gazzinelli, Lis Ribeiro Do Valle Antonelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria remains a significant public health concern despite a steady decline in symptomatic cases. In Brazil, endemic regions are primarily concentrated in the Amazon region, where most cases are attributed to Plasmodium vivax. Although malaria does not confer long-term sterilizing immunity, clinical immunity is achieved, leading to asymptomatic (ASY) infections. Assessing cellular immunity against P. vivax is challenging due to the lack of well-defined antigens. Hence, we developed a library of 310 P. vivax peptides (MPv310) to evaluate antigen-specific responses of CD4+ T cells and their memory subpopulations. The specific cytokine response, IFN-γ and TNF, and upregulation of activation markers, CD69 and CD154, were assessed in P. vivax–infected individuals, including symptomatic before treatment (SY-I) patients, symptomatic after treatment (SY-T) patients, ASY individuals, and uninfected individuals. CD4+ T cells were stimulated with peptides and analyzed by flow cytometry or ELISpot. MPv310 stimulation led to increased frequencies of CD154+ and CD69+CD154+ cells in symptomatic before treatment and SY-T patients and IFN-γ+TNF+ cells in SY-T patients. Cytokine response was also associated with ASY status, with higher frequencies of IFN-γ+TNF+ among CD4+ T cells and of IFN-γ+ in the effector memory subset. Distinct antigen-specific activation patterns were also observed among symptomatic and ASY individuals when the peptide pool was divided into smaller pools representing specific antigens. This study reveals that MPv310 and different sets of minipools stimulate CD4+ T cells in symptomatic and ASY infections, having potential to be used as a new tool to assess immune mechanisms associated with different clinical presentations of the disease and protection against malaria.Keywords: T cells, Plasmodium vivax, peptides, cytokines, cell activation

Introduction

Malaria is a febrile disease transmitted through the bite of Anopheles mosquitoes infected with Plasmodium protozoan. Malaria is still considered a major public health problem, present in over 85 countries. In 2019, 241 million cases and 627,000 deaths were reported worldwide, with 900,000 cases occurring in the Americas. The Amazon Basin, which covers 9 countries in South America, accounts for over 90% of the confirmed cases recorded annually in the Americas.1–3 Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax are the main causative parasites of malaria,4 and the latter is the most globally widespread parasite, accounting for >70% of cases in the Americas.

Malaria symptoms caused by P. vivax include fever, headache, and malaise, among others. However, a high percentage of the population in endemic areas develops asymptomatic (ASY) infection with low levels of parasitemia, usually indetectable by conventional diagnostic methods. The persistence of untreated ASY carriers of P. vivax is a major challenge for diagnosis and treatment that represents an obstacle to achieving eradication.5,6

The immunological mechanisms that favor ASY infections remain unclear. It is speculated that genetic factors, previous infections, and an efficient acquired immunity play crucial roles in ASY infections.7,8 It is well accepted that malaria does not induce long-term immune protection.9,10 While both humoral and cellular immunity contribute to parasite control, assessing acquired immunity against P. vivax is particularly challenging due to the lack of in vitro culture methods and limited immunological tools.11 After infection, individuals acquire only partial immunity, suggesting impaired immune memory development.

In immune animals and partially immune humans, T cells play an important role in the defense against malaria. Cytotoxic T cells appear to eliminate infected hepatocytes upon antigen presentation by major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I). Studies in mice infected with P. berghei have shown that molecules such as IFN-γ and nitric oxide are essential for the activation of Plasmodium-specific CD8+ T cells.12,13 In addition, it is well established that CD8+ T cells are vital for protection during liver stage of Plasmodium infection. However, other studies with P. berghei and P. yoelli infections have demonstrated that CD8+ T cells make little contribution to the control of the parasite blood stage, mainly due to the lack of MHC-I on erythrocytes.14,15 Although most investigations have been performed in mice, CD8+ T cells from humans living in malaria-endemic regions or from sporozoite-vaccinated volunteers have been found to recognize MHC-I–restricted epitopes from P. falciparum pre-erythrocytic antigens.16,17 Recent findings have shown that human CD8+ T cells generated during infection with P. vivax specifically eliminate parasitized reticulocytes through an MHC-I–mediated, granulysin-driven mechanism, possibly contributing to protection.18 Otherwise, T helper (Th) cells also contribute to protection, mainly during the blood stage of P. vivax infection.9,19 Parasitized erythrocytes are indirectly targeted by CD4+ Th cells that may orchestrate secreted antibody responses or the antiparasitic activity of phagocytes. A robust Th1 response and high levels of IFN-γ are key signatures of vivax malaria.20 Th1 cell–associated IFN-γ may also either directly activate inducible nitric oxide synthase in infected hepatocytes21 or potentiate the cytotoxic activity of P. falciparum–specific CD8+ T cells via upregulation of MHC-I molecules on infected liver cells.22 Additionally, studies on controlled human malaria infections have linked lower parasite burdens with the presence of cytotoxic Th1-like CD4+ T cells.23,24

Although some evidence has been obtained in humans, the precise requirements for cytotoxic CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets, as well as the specific effector mechanisms by which these may confer protection, remain poorly understood. Moreover, most investigations have concentrated on P. falciparum infection; correspondingly, studies in humans infected with P. vivax are relatively scarce, attributable to difficulties in culturing P. vivax continuously in vitro and the limited availability of well-characterized protein antigens. Indeed, most of the research evaluating acquired immune responses during human P. vivax infection employs individual recombinant antigens or a small set of epitopes, under the assumption that they adequately represent the parasite.25–27

To address this issue, our study aimed to assess antigen-specific CD4+ T cell responses in symptomatic (SY) and ASY P. vivax–infected individuals using both comprehensive and small peptide pools. Our findings demonstrated that these peptides were recognized by P. vivax–specific T cells during infection. Notably, only ASY individuals exhibited memory CD4+ T cells producing IFN-γ and TNF upon antigen stimulation, suggesting that the absence of excessive inflammation during acute malaria enables these individuals to control parasitemia through a more efficient parasite-specific T cell response.

Methods

P. vivax peptide selection and synthesis

To identify promising peptide candidates to compose the megapool MPv310, we employed 3 strategies (Fig. 1). First, we selected P. vivax antigens that had previously been tested for eliciting T cell responses (proliferation and/or cytokine release).25,26,28–30 A list of 44 antigens (Table S1) was used to retrieve the protein sequences available on PlasmoDB, from Sal-1 (40 sequences) or P01 (4 sequences) strains. These sequences were analyzed to predict T cell epitopes using the tool “TepiTool”31, from the Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource. In addition, we included 32 T cell epitopes previously tested in immunoassays and reported in the Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource. Finally, 47 epitopes were selected among genes expressed by merozoites. Some of the previously described epitopes were longer than 15 amino acids. For these, HLA class II binding prediction was performed to identify the optimal MHC binding sequence. We defined epitopes of 15 residues, with up to 10-residue overlaps, and predicted binding using the 7-allele method.32 The final set of 310 epitopes included no more than 20 epitopes per antigen, and duplicates were removed. The peptide library was commercially synthesized as crude material on a 1 mg scale by A&A Ltd. Lyophilized peptides were dissolved in 100% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and combined to form the megapool, as previously described.33 The MPv310 was also subdivided into 19 minipools, each containing a distinct protein or protein family. Specific sequence identities and reference numbers for all antigen pools used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Strategy employed to build the peptide libraries. Three approaches were combined to generate the libraries (from left to right): protein sequences from 44 P. vivax antigens were used to generate HLA-DR-binding 15-mer peptides using the 7-allelle method available on Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource; peptides were previously tested in immunoassays; and the epitope of merozoite was predicted by TepiTool. Epitopes were selected based on predicted affinity on HLA-DR at a maximum number of 20 peptides per antigen to avoid overrepresentation. The peptide list is described in Table S1.

Study approval

All participants provided written informed consent for participation in the study. The protocols were approved by the Ethical Committee on Human Research at René Rachou Institute, Oswaldo Cruz Foundation (CEP-IRR- CAAE: 59902816.7.0000.5091). Patients provided written informed consent before their inclusion in the research.

Study population

SY individuals with acute P. vivax infection (symptomatic before treatment [SY-I]) were selected among those who had a confirmed diagnosis of thick smear and sought medical care at the Centro de Pesquisa em Medicina Tropical de Rondônia. These individuals received treatment with chloroquine and primaquine, and a new blood sample was collected between 30 and 45 days after diagnosis (symptomatic after treatment [SY-T]). ASY individuals were identified by active search in Candeias do Jamari (8°47′41.6′′S 63°42′10.9′′W), municipality from Rondônia, by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), among volunteers who reported not have had fever or presented only mild symptoms that were not sufficient to lead them to seek medical assistance or treatment in the past 2 weeks. In addition, healthy subjects with negative qPCR were invited to participate in the study. These participants were included in the control (CTL) group and did not have any disease or reported previous malaria. Individuals with inflammatory, chronic, and/or autoimmune disease; pregnant women; or those under 18 yr of age were not included in this study. Controls were residents of Belo Horizonte, a nonendemic area, and Porto Velho and Candeias do Jamari, malaria-endemic areas.

Blood samples from 17 SY-I patients, 21 SY-T patients, 29 ASY individuals, and 27 CTL individuals were used in this study. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained by density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, blood was centrifuged at 500 g, 10 min, and plasma was removed and stored at −80 °C until use. Cells were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (1:1, v/v) and slowly layered over 15 mL Ficoll-Paque (Cytiva). Cells were then centrifuged at 410 g, 40 min, and the PBMC was carefully removed and washed twice with cold PBS. PBMCs were cryopreserved in 90% v/v fetal calf serum with 10% v/v DMSO and kept in liquid nitrogen until use.

Quantification of soluble mediators

For quantification of CXCL10, IFN-γ and TNF, Luminex Bio-Plex Pro human cytokines 27 assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories) was performed. Briefly, capture antibody-coated microspheres were incubated overnight with standards, controls (provided by the kit), or plasma samples. After incubation, biotinylated detection antibodies were added and incubated for 30 min and then a solution of streptavidin conjugated with phycoerythrin was added to each well for a second incubation of 30 min. Beads were washed and acquired using the Luminex 200 device (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Exponent 26 software (Bio-Rad). Bio-Plex Manager software (Bio-Rad) was used for analysis. The final concentration of each plasma biomarker was estimated by 5-part logistic regression according to the standard curve inserted in each experimental batch, and the results were expressed in pg/mL.

Cell phenotyping

Cells were thawed and kept at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 4 h in RPMI with 10% fetal calf serum to allow the restoration of cellular metabolism. Cells were then incubated with peptide pool library (+ MPv310) or DMSO diluted in RPMI (− MPv310) with a final concentration of 5 µg/mL. After 5 h of incubation, 2.5 µg/mL of brefeldin A and monensin were added and the cells were cultured for additional 10 h. Cell suspensions were incubated with antibodies of interest (anti-CD3 BV650, clone UCHT1; anti-CD4 BV605, clone RPA-T4; anti-CD8 Alexa Fluor 700, clone SK1; anti-CD45RO BV786, clone UCHL1; anti-CD27 phycoerythrin, clone 323; anti-CD154 APC-eFluor 780, clone 24-31; anti-CD69 BV510, clone FN50; IFN-γ BV605, clone 4S.B3; TNF PERCP-Cy5.5, clone Mab11) and acquired by the flow cytometer LSRFortessa. The frequencies of CD4+ T cells and their subsets (naive, effector [Eff], effector memory [EM], and central memory [CM]), and the expression of activation markers (CD69, CD154) and cytokines (IFN-γ, TNF) was analyzed in FlowJo v.10 (TreeStar).

IFN-γ ELISpot assay

ELISpot plates (Millipore) were activated with PBS for 1 min, coated with mouse anti-human IFN-γ capture antibody (0.004 µg/mL; clone 1-D1K; Mabtech), and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Plates were washed thrice with PBS and incubated with culture medium (RPMI 1640 with 5% glutamine) with 10% penicillin-streptomycin for 1 h. Peptide pools or DMSO in a final concentration of 5 μg/mL diluted in RPMI and 2.5 × 105 fresh PBMCs were added per well. Cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 24 h. After incubation, cells were discarded and plates were washed with PBS thrice, and then biotinylated mouse anti-human IFN-γ antibodies (1 μg/mL; clone 7-B6-1) were added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Plates were washed with PBS thrice, and streptavidin alkaline phosphatase conjugate solution was added, following incubation at room temperature for 1 h. Plates were washed with PBS, and 5-Bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl phosphate solution (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to wells and incubated for 30 min. Finally, plates were rinsed with deionized water to stop the reaction. Data were then acquired using the Mabtech IRIS reader. For each well, images on detection channel (White LED ring light) were taken and number of spots per well were automatically counted using the Apex software version 1.1.58. Spots’ size and intensity thresholds (15 and 22, respectively) were chosen and consistently used throughout all the experiments. Responses were considered positive when higher number of spots were found upon P. vivax peptide libraries stimulation compared with nonstimulated condition within the same group. Background signal was subtracted from stimulated condition when comparisons were performed among groups.

Statistics

The data were organized into tables using Excel software (Microsoft) and analyzed by GraphPad Prism v.8 software (GraphPad Software). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess whether the variables in each clinical group presented parametric distribution. The Wilcoxon test was used to verify the difference between cell frequencies, paired by volunteers, for each group, comparing samples without or with MPv310 stimulation. For multiple comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-test was used to compare cytokine expression between the clinical groups (values of MPv310 minus background).

For ELISpot, the differences (values of peptide pools minus background) in the number of spots between groups were determined by Mann-Whitney test. The comparative analyses of categorical data, comprising the frequency of responders and the signatures (proportion of participants above the global median cutoff), were carried out using Fisher’s exact test. Significant differences (P < 0.05) were indicated with asterisks. The signature analysis was conducted by converting each variable expressed as a continuous value into categorical data. For this purpose, the global median values were employed as cutoffs to classify participants as having either low (below the cutoff) or high (above the cutoff) IFN-γ spot numbers. The following cutoffs (expressed as absolute numbers of spots per 2.5 × 106 cells) were applied: MPv310 = 79; m1 = 0; m2 = 57; m3 = 64; m4 = 112; m5 = 90; m6 = 60; m7 = 49; m8 = 81; m9 = 103; m10 = 64; m11 = 0; m12 = 43; m13 = 78; m14 = 56; m15 = 177; m16 = 110; m17 = 76; m18 = 37; m19 = 48. The overall signatures were visualized using plots with delta lines that display the proportion of individuals with ELISpot responses above the respective global median cutoffs. Colormap constructs were generated in Microsoft Excel using conditional formatting to highlight each volunteer’s response relative to the global median of the corresponding minipool. The color gradient represents the intensity of the ELISpot response.

Decision tree algorithms were generated using Weka (Waikato Environment for Knowledge Analysis) (version 3.6.11; University of Waikato). This machine learning approach identifies the minimal set of variables necessary for optimal group classification by selecting root and branch attribute nodes. Groups that show significant differences among the number of minipools were considered as attributes for subgroup classification. The previously defined cutoffs were then applied to the dataset to construct the decision tree.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Peripheral blood samples were obtained from 77 participants including SY-I patients (n = 17) and SY-T patients (n = 21), ASY P. vivax–infected individuals (n = 29), and CTL individuals (n = 27). There were no differences in age between SY-I or SY-T patients and ASY individuals. However, CTL individuals were significantly younger than SY-I patients, SY-T patients, and ASY individuals. In addition, ASY individuals had more previous self-reported malaria episodes than the other groups and, as expected, had lower parasitemia than SY-I patients, estimated by qPCR (P = 0.001) (Table 1).

ASY P. vivax–infected individuals produce lower levels of TNF than SY patients and CTL individuals

SY P. vivax–infected patients are associated with high levels of soluble inflammatory mediators. To assess the extent of systemic inflammation in our cohort, we measured the levels of CXCL10, TNF, and IFN-γ during active P. vivax infection and after treatment (Fig. 2). Our findings revealed that SY-I patients exhibited elevated levels of CXCL10 and IFN-γ in comparison with SY-T patients and CTL individuals. Conversely, both ASY and CTL individuals displayed reduced levels of CXCL10 compared with SY-T patients. Notably, ASY individuals had lower levels of TNF relative to the other groups, and higher levels of IFN-γ compared with SY-T patients. Additional profiling of 24 cytokines (Fig. S1) provides a broader view of the inflammatory environment across these groups.

ASY P. vivax–infected individuals do not display altered proportions of CD4+ memory T cell subsets

We previously found that ASY infection by P. vivax does not alter the overall numbers of leucocytes in blood, while active SY infection leads to reduction of the blood counts of lymphocytes and increase in monocytes.34 To characterize the CD4+ memory T cell subsets (naive, CM, EM, and Eff T cells), the expression of CD45RO and CD27 on PBMCs from SY-I patients, SY-T patients, and ASY individuals as well as CTL individuals was evaluated ex vivo without prior stimulation of the cells (Fig. 3A). Our results showed a similar overall subset composition in each group (Fig. 3B). However, higher frequencies of EM CD4+ T cells were observed among SY individuals compared with CTL individuals, even 30 to 45 d after treatment (Fig. 3C, lower left), while SY-I patients showed a lower frequency of naive CD4+ T cells compared with CTL individuals (Fig. 3C, upper left). ASY infection had no impact on the frequencies of CD4+ memory T cell subsets compared with CTL individuals.

MPv310 induces expression of CD69 and CD154 on CD4+ T cells from SY P. vivax–infected patients

To assess CD4+ T cell function, we investigated the expression of CD69 and CD154 on CD4+ T cell subsets upon stimulation with the peptide library MPv310. Antigen-responding CD154+ T cells were mostly CD27− T cells, thus being represented by EM and Eff CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4A). Stimulation with MPv310 induced the expression of CD154 on CD4+ T cells as well as on EM and Eff compartments from both SY-I and SY-T groups. The same was observed in cells coexpressing CD69 and CD154 (Fig. 4B). While the induction of these markers was also observed in ASY, the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.07) (Fig. 4B). Although SY and ASY individuals exhibited higher frequencies of responders compared with CTL individuals, no statistical differences were found between the clinical groups.

MPv310 induces IFN-γ and TNF production by CD4+ T cells from SY-T patients and ASY individuals

To further investigate the presence of P. vivax–specific CD4+ T cells during SY and ASY infection, after SY infection, and in CTL individuals, we evaluated the production of IFN-γ and TNF upon stimulation with MPv310 peptide library (Fig. 5A, B). Although a greater number of SY-T patients and ASY individuals respond to MPv310-producing IFN-γ+CD4+ and TNF+CD4+ T cells, no significant differences were observed between stimulated and unstimulated cultures when they were compared as a group (Fig. 5B, left and center). Furthermore, we observed that MPv310 induced the production of IFN-γ by EM CD4+ T cells in ASY individuals (Fig. 5B, left). Significant differences were observed in the frequencies of TNF-producing naive CD4+ T cells between stimulated and unstimulated culture from ASY individuals (Fig. 5B, center). Eleven out of 21 and 14 out of 29 SY-T patients and ASY individuals, respectively, displayed increased frequencies of TNF-producing cells upon MPv310 stimulation. On the other hand, only 5 out of 18 SY-I patients responded to the peptide library producing TNF.

Production of IFN-γ by CD4+ T cells reveals acquired immune response during SY and ASY P. vivax infection

IFN-γ production by P. vivax–specific cells was also assessed by ELISpot. The MPv310 stimulation induced expansion of IFN-γ+ cells only in SY-T patients (Fig. 6A). Greater numbers of spots obtained in the MPv310 condition, after background was subtracted, were also observed in SY-T patients when compared with CTL individuals (Fig. 6B). Antigen-specific response, after background was subtracted, was observed when 11 out of 19 minipools were used as stimuli: m4 (AMA-1 peptides), m5 (TRAg peptides), m6 (MSP-1 peptides), m9 (PvRBP2b peptides), m11 (PvRBP2c peptides), m12 (Roph2 peptides), m13 (RON2 peptides), m15 (Pv24), m17 (MSA180 peptides), m18 (PvRALP1), and m19 (PvRAMA peptides) (Fig. 6C, E). Minipool 6 was able to induce antigen-specific response regardless of the clinical form. m9 was able to induce an antigen-specific response in ASY individuals and SY-I patients and m17 in ASY individuals and SY-T patients. On the other hand, m4, m5, m11, and m12 induced higher antigen-specific response only in SY-I patients compared with CTL individuals. The other 3 minipools (m15, m18, and m19) induced an antigen-specific response in SY-T patients compared with CTL individuals. Finally, m13 induced a lower antigen-specific response in ASY individuals compared with CTL individuals. As expected, the radar plots revealed that the CTL group exhibited low frequencies of responders across all peptide pools. In contrast, the ASY group showed higher recognition rates, particularly in response to m4, m9, m13, m15, and m17, with the highest responder frequency observed for m6. In the SY-I group, all peptide pools, except m18, elicited responder rates exceeding 40%. The SY-T group exhibited the most robust response, with MPv310, m15, m17, m18, and m19, being recognized by more than 50% of individuals. Statistically significant differences in responder frequencies were observed for several peptides among groups (Fig. 6D). No significant response was observed with the remaining minipools (Fig. S2). Altogether, our data show that the MPv310 and small peptide pools were able to reveal heterogeneous memory T cell patterns associated with P. vivax infection status.

ASY status is discriminated by a selected set of peptide pools

To further refine the characterization of the IFN-γ production by P. vivax–specific cells, the median for each minipool across all patients was calculated. The median value was used as a cutoff to categorize patients as either low or high IFN-γ producers. All minipools elicited greater IFN-γ production by ASY individuals, SY-I patients, and SY-T patients compared with CTL individuals. Compared with SY-I patients, ASY individuals exhibited higher frequencies of responders for m10, m17, m18, and MPv310. In contrast, SY-T patients showed higher frequencies of responders exclusively for m14, m15, m17, m18, and MPv310. Finally, when compared with SY-T patients, ASY individuals displayed significantly higher IFN-γ production in response to m4 and m10. (Fig. 7A).

To identify the minimal set of variables capable of discriminating clinical groups based on IFN-γ produced upon P. vivax–specific stimulation, at individual levels, decision trees were built using the predefined global median cutoff of spot-forming unit count for each minipool (Fig. 7B). The overall performance of the minipools was evaluated by compiling the reactivity profiles of individual samples based on the selected attribute sets (Fig. 7B, C). To assess classification accuracy, the “true infection” status of each sample was compared with its “classified infection” classification, and the percentage of agreement was calculated (Fig. 7C). Data analysis revealed that 60 samples were correctly classified among the ASY, SY-I, and SY-T groups. ASY infection was correctly identified in 39 samples (90.7%), while SY-I and SY-T status were correctly classified in 12 (44%) and 9 (60%) samples, respectively (Fig. 7C). A total of 25 samples were misclassified. The agreement between true and predicted classifications was further assessed using the Kappa index, which showed a moderate agreement (κ = 0.485) (Fig. 7C). Minipool-based classification for the differential diagnosis of P. vivax infection achieved an overall accuracy of 70.58% (Fig. 7B). Another decision tree was constructed to distinguish ASY from CTL individuals (Fig. S3). In this binary model, ASY infection was correctly identified in 100% of cases and CTL infection in 53% (Fig. S3), with 39 correct classifications out of 58 samples and reaching an overall accuracy of 87.93%.

Discussion

P. vivax is one of the parasites that cause malaria in humans and accounts for high morbidity rates worldwide. This species has special characteristics, such as the development of hypnozoites in the liver and rapid formation of gametocytes,35 allowing the transmission before the symptoms onset. P. vivax exclusively infects reticulocytes that correspond to 1% to 2% of the total erythrocytes in peripheral blood36 and have quick maturation and short life.37 Thus, although the first culture of P. vivax was reported in 1912,38 there is no effective methodology for its long-term maintenance in vitro. In fact, this is considered the main factor that hinders the understanding of the mechanisms of adaptive immunity, as parasite antigens are not easily obtained.

Although the maintenance of antibody levels suggests that Plasmodium triggers humoral memory immune response,39,40 the generation of memory T cells able to recognize parasite antigens is necessary for the development of long-lasting protection.19 Moreover, experimental models indicate that Th1 response plays an important role in protection during Plasmodium infections.41 During acute human infection by P. vivax, the induction of the Th1 cytokines, IFN-γ and TNF, and chemokines associated with this profile, such as CXCL10, is still controversial.42,43 While one of these studies shows that levels of TNF and the other that levels of IFN-γ are not induced during acute infection, the latter shows that ASY infection produces higher levels of TNF and IFN-γ than SY infection.43–45 Although in our study circulating levels of TNF are not increased during acute malaria and decreased during ASY infection compared with CTL, higher levels of IFN-γ and CXCL10 are found in SY infection but not in ASY infection. The lack of increment or even the decrease in the production of inflammatory mediators during ASY P. vivax infection suggests that systemic inflammation requires a certain parasitemia threshold. Furthermore, alterations in CD4+ T cell memory subsets are also triggered by inflamed microenvironment-induced parasitemia, with expansion of effector memory and contraction of naive cells. In contrast, ASY infection does not display either increased inflammatory factors, expansion of effector memory cells or high parasitemia, but they are still able to develop clinical immunity. Exploring antigen-specific responses will allow us to understand how ASY status is achieved.

The evaluation of CD4+ T cell responses against P. vivax antigens is scarce, and most studies have evaluated the cellular response after stimulation with recombinant proteins or small number of peptides.29,45–49 No substantial responses were observed with these approaches, probably due to the limited number of cells specific to determined epitopes in the circulation and the lack of immunodominance. Another approach used purified P. vivax–infected reticulocytes as stimulus in cell cultures.18,45,50 However, these experiments had important limitations such as the volume of blood required to purify enough number of infected cells. To overcome these issues, a comprehensive library containing 310 peptides of potential T cell epitopes derived from P. vivax was built. CD4+ T cells from most SY patients, before and after treatment, respond to MPv310 stimulation expressing CD154, an activation marker transiently expressed on the surface of the T cells, which is downregulated upon binding to its receptor on antigen-presenting cells.51 Analysis of activation and costimulatory molecules have been used to assess recent TCR engagement.52 Expression of CD154 on CD4+ T cells in humans infected with P. vivax was not previously described. However, studies have reported induction of this molecule after P. berghei infection in mice53 and after administration of P. vivax circumsporozoite protein-based vaccines in rhesus monkeys. In the latter, the expression of CD154 is concomitantly induced with at least 2 of 3 cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF).54 MPv310 also induced coexpression of CD154 and CD69 in Eff and EM CD4+ T cell subsets from SY infection, before and after treatment. Although no statistical difference was observed in ASY infection, 8 out of 15 volunteers responded to MPv310 expressing these molecules. Previous study compared the coexpression of CD69 and CD154 with the well-known combination OX40 and CD25 upon stimulation of PBMCs from HIV- and cytomegalovirus-infected individuals with peptide libraries. The data demonstrated that both combinations served as similar readouts of antigen-specific CD4+ T cell response.52 Thus, the coexpression of CD154 and CD69 by CD4+ T cells upon MPv310 stimulation strongly supports that both SY and ASY P. vivax infections elicit adaptive memory immune response.

Th1 cytokines are an essential effector molecule for the elimination of Plasmodium and the development of antimalarial immunity in humans.55–61 Ioannidis et al.20 demonstrated that, based on chemokine receptors expression, distinct populations of Th1-polarized CD4+ T cells are altered during SY and ASY P. vivax infection.

Other studies have assessed cytokine production upon peptides or recombinant proteins’ stimulation, and the results are controversial. MPv310 did not induce a substantial increase in the frequency of IFN-γ– or TNF-producing cells in any of the scenarios analyzed by flow cytometry. In agreement with our data, induction of TNF is not observed when PvDBPII epitopes are used as stimulus.25 In contrast, recombinant PvTRAg55, PvRON2, VIR, and PvMSP119 induce the production of TNF measured in culture supernatant.30,62–64 Moreover, induction of effector and memory CD4+ T cells producing TNF is observed in RTS,S vaccinees after in vitro restimulation with PfCSP peptides.65 These conflicting results suggest that production of this cytokine is dependent on different stimuli. The absence or reduced cytokines production during acute P. vivax infection has been attributed to high expression of inhibitory molecules, such as CTLA-4, PD-1, and TIM-3, on CD4+ T cells. The role of these molecules in immunosuppression during malaria is demonstrated by the simultaneously blockage of their signaling leading to restoration of cytokine production.45

On the other hand, contemporaneous expressions of IFN-γ and TNF were found in CD4+ T cells from SY-T patients and in memory CD4+ T cells from ASY individuals. Although multifunctional T cells were not previously described during P. vivax infection, their expansion has been associated with effector function and immunity in several parasitic diseases.66–69 In agreement with the discussion above, the induction of these cytokines by MPv310 only happened when parasitemia was subpatent or absent, suggesting that the high parasite load suppresses Th1 response.

Although no difference was found when intracellular IFN-γ was assessed, high numbers of IFN-γ-producing cells were revealed by ELISpot when MPv310 was used in cultures from SY-T cells. Interestingly, smaller pools composed of peptide from single proteins or family proteins were able to restimulate CD4+ T cells to produce IFN-γ. While m2, m8, and m14, containing peptides from PvDBP, PvMSP-9, and Pv12, did not trigger IFN-γ production in any group assessed here, other researchers have described an effective CD4+ T cell response using epitopes from the same proteins.25,26,29 Stimulation with recombinant PvMSP119 induces a discrete production of IFN-γ during acute infection and convalescence, and higher frequency of IFN-γ+CD4+ T cells in treated patients compared with CTL individuals.70 Similarly, the MSP-1 library (m6) induced IFN-γ production by SY-I patients, SY-T patients, and ASY individuals, suggesting that antigen-specific response is induced regardless of the parasite load and maintained after parasite clearance. Stimulation with PvTRAg induces higher frequencies of IFN-γ–, IL-2–, IL-4–, or IL-10–producing CD4+ T cells in treated individuals.71 Although our data do not show a PvTRAg-specific response in SY-T patients, a significant IFN-γ production was observed during acute infection. In our hands, the induction of antigen-specific response by PvTRAg seems to be triggered by a certain level of parasitemia. López et al.30 demonstrated that although 3 epitopes of PvRON2 induce TNF, IL-10, and/or IL-6, none of them induce IFN-γ response during acute malaria, corroborating our data. Moreover, m13, which contains PvRON2 epitopes, was the minipool that elicited lower IFN-γ production exclusively by CD4+ T cells from ASY individuals. A previous study72 defined that PvRON2 is expressed in P. vivax schizonts and suggested its role in red blood cell invasion. The presence of conserved structural domains and immunogenic features, such as coiled-coil motifs and tandem repeat regions, had already suggested its antigenic potential. The responses induced by individual minipools, but not detected when MPv310 is used in cultures, suggest that the MPv310 needs to be improved to elicit antigen-specific response. The library can be improved by removing antigens that inhibit T cell responses and the ones that are not recognized by T cells from P. vivax–infected or previously infected individuals.

Furthermore, the ability of selected minipools to induce IFN-γ production by T cells from distinct clinical groups was investigated at individual levels. When the global median IFN-γ response per minipool was used as a stratification threshold, key differences in reactivity profiles were revealed. Across all minipools, ASY, SY-I, and SY-T groups demonstrated higher IFN-γ responses compared with the CTL group, confirming the presence of antigen-specific memory responses. However, the frequency of responders varied between groups. ASY individuals exhibited overall lower responder rates compared with SY-I patients, except for a few minipools (m6, m18, MPv310), possibly reflecting a more regulated immune activation in the absence of symptoms. In contrast, the SY-T group showed higher responder frequencies only in a subset of minipools (m15–m19 and MPv310), suggesting the presence of memory cells that may persist after parasite clearance.

In agreement with these data, the decision tree was able to identify ASY individuals with high accuracy, but the model’s performance was less robust for distinguishing SY-I and SY-T patients. The good accuracy and moderate agreement by the Kappa index highlight the utility of minipool-based classification in complex infection contexts. Distinct responses between SY and ASY individuals triggered by different sets of minipools are expected, as these responses depend on factors such as parasite load, inflammatory environment, and other host-related variables. Importantly, considering the clinical relevance of distinguishing ASY infection from CTL individuals, the binary model achieved a high classification accuracy, with 100% correct identification of ASY individuals. These findings underscore the potential of minipool-specific IFN-γ responses as a valuable tool for identifying subclinical P. vivax infections, which are often missed by standard diagnosis tests and that contribute to transmission in endemic areas.

Altogether, MPv310 and a distinct set of peptides induce P. vivax–specific CD4+ T cells response in SY-I patients, SY-T patients, and ASY individuals. The deconvolution of this peptide library reveals that cells from individuals with different clinical forms respond to distinct combinations of epitopes, which can segregate them with good accuracy. Therefore, this study raises the potential for developing tools to evaluate the adaptive immune response in P. vivax infection and to support the diagnosis of ASY cases, which is considered a major obstacle for malaria elimination.
