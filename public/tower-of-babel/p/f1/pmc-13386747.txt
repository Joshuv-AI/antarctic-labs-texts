Rare hilar giant cystic cholangiogenic adenoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Wei Jiang, Hong Su, Shaoyong Liang, Zongding Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaparoscopic resection of giant hilar biliary cystadenoma has been rarely documented in clinical literature. Here, we report a case of a 51-year-old female patient whose laboratory results were unremarkable, while abdominal imaging identified a large cystic lesion located in the hepatic hilum. The patient underwent laparoscopic tumor resection under general anesthesia. During surgery, the tumor was noted to significantly compress nearby hilar structures—including the portal vein and gallbladder—and had a communication with the left hepatic duct. The tumor was successfully resected en bloc, and pathological analysis confirmed it to be a hilar biliary cystadenoma. Postoperatively, the patient recovered uneventfully without any complications and was discharged in good condition. No tumor recurrence was detected at the 6-month follow-up visit. This case underscores the potential for misdiagnosis of large hilar biliary cystadenomas and shows that laparoscopic resection is a viable therapeutic option for these lesions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04942-y.Keywords: Cholangiogenic adenoma, Laparoscopic resection, Substantial cystic mass, Misdiagnosis, Case report

Background

Hepatic cystic space-occupying lesions encompass a range of conditions, including hepatic cysts, hepatic abscesses, hepatic cystic echinococcosis, and hepatic cystadenomas. Diagnosis and disease nature dictate the choice of treatment, with prognostic outcomes varying significantly—ultimately leading to distinct short- and long-term clinical results. Clinical assessment relies heavily on patients’ medical history, clinical manifestations, and imaging findings, particularly liver-specific radiological features.

Most hepatic cysts are asymptomatic; however, as cysts enlarge, become infected, or cause mass effects, patients may present with abdominal distension and pain. In contrast, hepatic abscesses are often accompanied by systemic symptoms such as chills, fever, and localized abdominal pain [1]. Patients with hepatic cystic echinococcosis typically have a relevant epidemiological history (e.g., exposure to echinococcus cysts) and are usually asymptomatic in the early stages. Radiologically, these lesions often appear as unilocular or multilocular cysts, sometimes with calcified components [2].

Hepatic cystadenoma is a rare entity, but it shows a predilection for female patients and is generally asymptomatic. The radiological density of cystic fluid varies by cyst type [3]. These tumors predominantly arise from intrahepatic bile ducts; they are rare in the gallbladder and extremely uncommon in the hilar bile duct, with a known potential for malignant transformation. Surgical resection remains the only curative treatment, as incomplete removal is associated with a risk of recurrence [3]. This report presents a rare case of large hilar biliary cystadenoma successfully treated with laparoscopic resection. This minimally invasive approach offers advantages such as minimizing hospital stay, alleviating patient discomfort, and facilitating postoperative recovery—making it a valuable therapeutic option for such lesions.

Case presentation

A 51-year-old female patient presented to our outpatient clinic with a 3-month history of progressive abdominal pain in the mid and upper abdomen. Physical examination revealed a large, immovable mass in the right upper abdomen. Enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed a huge multilocular cystic mass measuring approximately 14.2 × 17.4 × 22.2 cm in the right upper abdomen, predominantly involving Couinaud hepatic segments Ⅳ, Ⅴ and Ⅵ, with a clear boundary, multiple internal septa, and focal sparse calcification in the capsule wall. After contrast administration, the posterior capsule wall and internal septa showed mild enhancement. The mass displaced the adjacent liver, pancreas, right kidney, and right colon, and the gallbladder was not clearly visualized (Fig. 1A–C). Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) further confirmed a large multilocular abnormal signal lesion in the right upper abdomen (Fig. 1B, C), with no dilatation of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts and normal biliary structures (Fig. 1D–F). Based on these radiological features, a preoperative presumptive diagnosis of biliary cystadenoma originating from the hepatic hilum/extrahepatic bile duct was made. The patient was subsequently admitted to our hospital for further surgical treatment.

Her past medical history included 5 years of hypertension, which was well-controlled with sustained-release nifedipine, and her blood pressure remained stably within the normal range. She had no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, substance abuse, genetic disorders, or infectious diseases. She was postmenopausal after regular menstrual cycles. She had no previous surgical history and no history of residence or travel to echinococcosis-endemic areas.

Relevant laboratory findings were as follows: hemoglobin was 144.0 g/L, white blood cell count was 5.09 × 109/L, platelet count was 198.0 × 109/L, the international normalized ratio was 0.96, glucose was 6.16 mmol/L, total bilirubin was 11.8µmol/L, direct bilirubin was 4.2µmol/L, serum albumin was 53.7 g/L, blood urea nitrogen was 6.0 mmol/L, alanine aminotransferase and aspartate aminotransferase were not elevated.The initial laboratory tests showed that tumor biomarkers were normal.

Following preoperative preparation, laparoscopic exploration and hilar tumor resection were performed under general anesthesia. Intraoperatively, a solid-cystic tumor was identified in the hepatic hilum, which caused significant compression and dense adhesion to adjacent anatomical structures including the ligamentum teres hepatis, branches of the left and right portal veins, left hepatic duct, gallbladder, and right hepatic duct. The tumor pedicle was found to be closely connected to the left hepatic duct. To prevent intraoperative mucus spillage and intra-abdominal contamination, the surrounding tissues and organs were fully protected with surgical gauze before manipulating the cyst. A small opening was carefully created on the cyst wall using an electrosurgical knife and ultrasonic scalpel, and a suction device was promptly inserted into the cyst cavity to aspirate the mucinous fluid in a controlled manner.

The resected specimen showed mixed polycystic and solid features, with thickened cyst walls, abundant mucinous fluid accumulation, and no bile components. Given our surgical strategy of en bloc complete tumor resection, intraoperative cyst fluid analysis (cytology, tumor markers) and frozen-section margin assessment were not performed (Fig. 2A).

Postoperative histopathological examination of the resected lesion was performed, accompanied by immunohistochemical staining. The tumor cells were immunoreactive for CK7 (+), CK19 (+), CEA (+), Syn (+), Vimentin (+), Desmin (+), ER (+), PR (+), and CgA (+), with a Ki-67 proliferation index of 3%. Based on these morphological and immunophenotypic features, a diagnosis of biliary cystadenoma was established (Fig. 2B-C). The patient recovered smoothly without any postoperative complications and was discharged from the hospital thereafter (Fig. 2D–F). At the 6-month postoperative follow-up, the patient remained free of relevant clinical symptoms, and routine CT reexamination confirmed no signs of tumor recurrence (Fig. 3A–C).

Discussion and conclusion

Intraoperatively, the lesion was found to occupy the hepatic hilum and the left posterior portion of the liver. To obtain an adequate surgical field, the large cystic cavity was aspirated to reduce its volume.

Importantly, careful manipulation prevented mucus from leaking into the abdominal cavity, which was regarded as a critical step to avoid intra-abdominal contamination. After evacuating the cystic fluid, the surrounding tissues in the surgical field were mobilized, and the residual tumor mass was elevated. Due to dense adhesions between the tumor and hepatic hilar structures, the hepatic round ligament was divided along its course to facilitate identification of the left branch of the portal vein. Suture repair was used to effectively control bleeding from the dissected portal vein branch, although dissection from the left hepatic side remained technically difficult. The tumor and adjacent hepatic parenchyma were then transected along the identifiable hepatic boundary, exposing an intact cyst wall that could be easily and thoroughly dissected. The gallbladder, right branch of the portal vein, and left hepatic duct were gently separated and preserved. During the procedure, an isolated bile duct opening communicating with the left hepatic hilum was identified on the tumor surface.

Considering the dense adhesions surrounding the hepatic hilum and technical difficulty of left-sided dissection, we prepared several backup hemostatic strategies, including standby laparoscopic Pringle maneuver, selective portal vein clamping, and immediate availability of vascular sutures. Intraoperative laparoscopic ultrasound was also used to identify the portal vein branches and avoid vascular injury. Minor hemorrhage from the portal vein branch was controlled by precise suture repair, and no conversion to open surgery was required. An isolated small bile duct opening connecting the tumor to the left hepatic hilum was identified intraoperatively. This ductal opening was securely ligated with 4 − 0 absorbable sutures and closed by interrupted sutures to maintain biliary integrity, without the need for stent placement or biliary-enteric reconstruction.

Following cholecystectomy, we applied surgical clips to the segment of the left hepatobiliary duct adjacent to the tumor. The resected specimen was placed into a specimen retrieval bag, which was then extracted through a small abdominal incision. A drainage tube was also positioned at the surgical site to facilitate fluid evacuation. The total operative time was roughly 4 h, with an estimated blood loss of 50 mL—no blood transfusion was required during the procedure. Postoperatively, the patient was managed with an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol.

She achieved an uneventful recovery without any perioperative complications and was discharged smoothly from the hospital [4].

Imaging diagnosis of hilar tumors poses unique challenges: contrast-enhanced abdominal CT often fails to clearly delineate the relationship between the tumor and intrahepatic/extrahepatic biliary tract, as well as the gallbladder. In contrast, MRI—boasting superior soft tissue resolution—excels at depicting intratumoral structural details, such as multilocularity, capsular formation, internal septations, and papillary projections—key pathological characteristics that MRI captures with greater precision.

Notably, MRCP addresses these aforementioned limitations, offering distinct advantages for preoperative imaging assessment of large cystic-solid hilar lesions. In line with existing literature, this type of cystic tumor is prone to misdiagnosis due to overlapping imaging manifestations with hepatic cysts and cystic echinococcosis [5–7]. The shared radiological features between these entities significantly heighten the risk of diagnostic error. For hilar biliary cystadenoma specifically, its CT findings are characterized by mixed cystic-solid radiological manifestations, focal cyst wall thickening, and well-defined margins.

The slight enhancement of the posterior capsule wall and septa observed on preoperative imaging is consistent with the pathological features of biliary cystadenoma. Pathological examination confirmed that the cyst wall and septa consisted of fibrous connective tissue and benign stromal components with low cellularity and mild vascularity, which accounts for the mild enhancement pattern rather than obvious heterogeneous enhancement suggestive of malignancy. Gross examination revealed that the resected hilar cystic mass contained abundant mucinous fluid, with a smooth fibrous capsule and thin internal septa, consistent with the typical gross features of biliary cystadenoma. Histologically, the cyst was lined by benign biliary-type epithelial cells without atypia or malignant features, and the cyst wall was composed of mature fibrous stroma. Immunohistochemically, the positive expression of CK7 and CK19 confirmed the biliary epithelial origin, while ER and PR positivity indicated the characteristic ovarian-like stroma of biliary cystadenoma; the low Ki-67 proliferation index (3%) further verified its benign biological behavior. The mild vascularity within the fibrous capsule and septa accounts for the slight enhancement on imaging, and the presence of mucinous fluid within the tumor corresponds well with the cystic morphology and non-solid enhancement pattern observed preoperatively. The above radiological-pathological correlation strongly supports the diagnosis of hilar biliary cystadenoma.

Additionally, tumor-associated infection can elevate the risk of abscess formation. In contrast, hepatic cysts are characterized by well-demarcated margins, thin cyst walls, and homogeneous cystic fluid [8].

Hepatic cystic echinococcosis presents with varying radiological patterns across different stages—including unilocular, multilocular, or polycystic forms—along with characteristic findings such as daughter cysts, cyst wall collapse, and calcification [9]. Diagnostic confirmation often relies on the patient’s epidemiological history of residence in or exposure to echinococcosis-endemic areas [10].

Hepatic cystic tumors represent a heterogeneous group of lesions with overlapping radiological features, making preoperative differential diagnosis a key clinical challenge. The primary differential diagnoses for our case of hilar biliary cystadenoma include simple hepatic cysts, cystic hepatocellular carcinoma, metastatic mucinous adenocarcinoma, hepatic abscess, and hydatid cysts. Simple cysts typically present as unilocular, thin-walled lesions without septa or enhancement, lacking mucinous components or epithelial lining on pathology—distinct from our case’s multilocular structure, mucinous fluid, and biliary-type epithelial cells positive for CK7/CK19. Cystic hepatocellular carcinoma often exhibits irregular thickening of the cyst wall, solid nodular components, and positive expression of HepPar-1 (which was negative in our specimen), while metastatic mucinous adenocarcinoma may show CK20/CDX2 positivity (absent in our case) and a history of extrahepatic primary tumors [11]. Hepatic abscesses are usually accompanied by systemic inflammatory responses and rim enhancement [12], which were not observed here.

Treatment indications for hepatic cystic tumors are guided by lesion type, size, symptoms, and malignant potential.Biliary cystadenomas, particularly mucinous subtypes, carry a well-documented risk of malignant transformation to cystadenocarcinoma (estimated at 5–30%), thus surgical resection remains the gold standard regardless of symptom status [13]. In contrast, asymptomatic simple cysts require no intervention unless they cause mass effects or complications. For symptomatic or large lesions, percutaneous interventions (e.g., aspiration, sclerotherapy, or drainage) are sometimes considered for palliative relief; however, these approaches are associated with high recurrence rates in mucinous cystic tumors due to incomplete elimination of the epithelial lining and septal structures.

Percutaneous biopsy, while potentially informative, carries inherent risks including mucinous fluid leakage (which may lead to peritoneal dissemination or chemical peritonitis) and sampling bias—especially for lesions with focal malignant changes or heterogeneous stromal components, as observed in biliary cystadenomas [14]. In our case, the hilar location of the tumor (adjacent to critical vascular and biliary structures) and presence of mucinous fluid further supported surgical resection over percutaneous modalities, as it allowed for complete lesion removal, definitive pathological diagnosis, and preservation of hepatic hilar function.

Owing to the low prevalence of hepatobiliary cystadenoma in routine clinical practice and the absence of pathognomonic clinical symptoms or physical signs, early laboratory tests and imaging evaluations often produce inconclusive, non-specific findings. This translates to poor clinical awareness and insufficient recognition of the disease among most clinicians. A thorough review of the existing literature shows that many patients with this condition have undergone repeated palliative procedures, which clearly underscores the high risk of preoperative misdiagnosis and inappropriate clinical management [15–18]. Accordingly, accurate differentiation of biliary cystadenoma from complicated and atypical hepatic cysts is critically important in daily clinical work. Making this key diagnostic distinction directly shapes the selection of treatment plans and has a substantial bearing on the patient’s long-term prognosis.

Particular clinical vigilance is warranted for middle-aged to elderly female patients with a hepatic cyst, those with radiological features suggestive of a solitary or multilocular cystic lesion with internal septations or papillary projections, and patients with postoperative recurrent hepatic cysts or elevated tumor marker levels. Accordingly, for preoperative hepatic cystic lesions of uncertain pathological nature, intraoperative rapid frozen-section pathological examination should be obtained when clinically indicated. This critical step helps minimize the risk of diagnostic errors and inappropriate management of biliary cystadenoma.

Hepatobiliary cystadenomas predominantly originate from the intrahepatic bile ducts, and radical resection—encompassing the entire tumor plus a margin of adjacent normal liver parenchyma—is clinically imperative for curative intent. Extrahepatic biliary cystadenomas, by contrast, are exceedingly rare. In the present case, the patient presented with an unusually large tumor burden. Notably, laparoscopic resection offered distinct benefits including shorter hospital stay, minimized surgical trauma, and accelerated postoperative recovery. Importantly, during the dissection phase, we identified a well-demarcated interface between the tumor capsule and surrounding normal liver tissue—a key finding that provided valuable intraoperative reference. Resecting the tumor along this demarcated plane markedly minimized intraoperative blood loss and shortened the overall operative time. Furthermore, to our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of a rare giant hilar hepatobiliary cystadenoma successfully managed with laparoscopic resection.

Limitations of this case report should also be acknowledged. First, given the extremely large volume of the tumor, the limited operative space of laparoscopy made it difficult to absolutely ensure the integrity of the tumor capsule during resection, which carries a potential risk of cyst fluid leakage and abdominal implantation. Second, intraoperative ultrasound was not applied in this case to locate and mark the vital peritumoral anatomical structures, and the surgical dissection relied largely on the surgeon’s empirical judgment without real-time intraoperative imaging navigation.

Taken together, the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of hilar bile duct cystadenoma rely on a combined evaluation of multiple imaging modalities. Laparoscopic tumor resection performed along the safe dissection plane adjacent to the tumor capsule can serve as one of the optional surgical approaches for selected eligible patients, which effectively accelerates postoperative recovery for properly indicated individuals.
