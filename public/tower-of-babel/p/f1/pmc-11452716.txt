Influence of physiological conditions on hemodynamics of internal carotid artery aneurysms
Authors: Lei Zhou, Shanjie Dong, Amir Alahdadi Kheiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of body physiology on the rupture risk of cerebral saccular aneurysms. Comprehensive hemodynamic analyses were conducted using computational fluid dynamics to assess the potential for aneurysm rupture under three physiological conditions: rest, normal activity, and exercise. Contours of wall shear stress, oscillatory shear index, and pressure were analyzed and compared to identify regions at high risk of rupture. Additionally, statistical analysis was performed to evaluate the rupture risk of aneurysms. Blood flow dynamics during the peak systolic phase were also examined under these conditions. Our findings indicate that the dome area, where blood pressure is highest and the incoming blood flow first contacts the aneurysm sac, is the critical region with a heightened risk of rupture.

Introduction

Internal Carotid Artery (ICA) saccular aneurysms are a common type of cerebral aneurysm that can pose a significant risk of rupture, leading to potentially life-threatening conditions such as subarachnoid hemorrhage. Understanding the hemodynamics within these aneurysms is crucial for assessing their rupture risk and informing treatment strategies. In particular, the impacts of pulsatile flow rate and body physiology on hemodynamics in ICA saccular aneurysms have gained attention due to their potential influence on aneurysm growth and rupture1–3.

Computational analysis, specifically Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating the complex flow patterns and hemodynamic parameters within cerebral aneurysms. CFD involves the numerical simulation of fluid flow and provides quantitative information about variables such as pressure, velocity, and wall shear stress. By utilizing CFD, researchers can analyze the intricate details of blood flow within ICA saccular aneurysms and gain insights into the factors that contribute to their rupture risk4–6.

Pulsatile flow rate, which refers to the variation in blood flow velocity and pressure throughout the cardiac cycle, plays a crucial role in determining the hemodynamics within ICA saccular aneurysms. The pulsatile nature of blood flow influences parameters such as wall shear stress, vorticity, and flow velocity distribution, all of which are known to be associated with aneurysm progression and rupture. Understanding how changes in the pulsatile flow rate affect these hemodynamic parameters is essential for accurate rupture risk assessment7,8.

Moreover, the body physiology of an individual, including factors such as blood pressure, heart rate, and vessel compliance, can significantly impact the hemodynamics within ICA saccular aneurysms. Changes in body physiology can alter the flow conditions and lead to variations in wall shear stress, flow stability, and energy dissipation within the aneurysm. Consequently, studying the relationship between body physiology and hemodynamics can provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying aneurysm growth and rupture9–11.

By employing computational analysis techniques, researchers can investigate the impacts of pulsatile flow rate and body physiology on the hemodynamics of ICA saccular aneurysms. CFD simulations allow for the evaluation of flow patterns, pressure distributions, wall shear stress profiles, and other relevant parameters under different flow conditions and physiological states12,13. This computational approach provides a comprehensive understanding of how changes in pulsatile flow rate and body physiology can affect the hemodynamics within the aneurysm and ultimately influence the risk of rupture14–16.

In conclusion, the impacts of pulsatile flow rate and body physiology on the hemodynamics of ICA saccular aneurysms are critical factors in rupture risk analysis17–19. Computational analysis, particularly CFD, offers a powerful approach to investigate these relationships, providing quantitative insights into the complex flow patterns and hemodynamic parameters within the aneurysm20–22. The use of CFD simulations enables researchers to assess the influence of pulsatile flow rate and body physiology on aneurysm hemodynamics, contributing to a better understanding of aneurysm behavior and facilitating informed clinical decision-making23–25.

The recognition of the aneurysm rupture risk in different body physiology is critical since the blood hemodynamic is changed by the body activity. This study has tried to investigate the role of Pulsatile Flow Rate on the hemodynamic factors related to the saccular aneurysm rupture inside two patient-specific ICA aneurysms. Three different cardiac cycles of rest, normal, and exercise conditions of the body are investigated in this research. Computational technique is applied for the analysis of the blood inside the selected cerebral aneurysms.

Computational fluid dynamic is applied for the modeling intra-aneurysm blood. Navier-stokes equations are solved via a simple algorithm for the incompressible blood flow inside vessels and aneurysms27,28. To calculate blood viscosity, the Casson model is applied since the blood is assumed non-Newtonian flow30,31. A transient model of the blood cycle is also considered by applying the unsteady pressure outlet and mass flow rate at the outlet and inlet, respectively (Figs. 2 and 3)32,33. As noticed in Fig. 3, three different inlet mass flow rates related to the body physiology are examined in this work. Four different stages are introduced in this figure for future comparisons. ANSYS-FLUENT is used for the modeling of the blood inside the vessel33.

Figure 1 also demonstrates the applied grid for these cerebral aneurysms. Poly-hexacore grid is produced for the select saccular aneurysm. The size of the grid on the parent vessel and sac section is almost equal while the grid resolution distribution on the cross-section area shows that the grid size near the wall is less than the center of the vessel. Prism layers are specialized mesh elements used to capture the boundary layer of fluid flows. They help in accurately resolving the velocity gradient near the wall, which is crucial for predicting wall shear stress and other important flow characteristics. This is mainly due to the importance of hemodynamic data on the vessel and aneurysm wall. The number of grid cells for these aneurysms is about 1,400,000 cells.

Figures 4 and 5 display the contour of wall shear stress on selected saccular aneurysms in different physiology conditions at four stages. As expected, the value of the wall shear stress on the sac wall is directly proportional to the mass flow rate of incoming blood into the sac section area. Therefore, peak systolic is the stage in which the maximum mass flow rate in the cardiac cycle. The shape of the selected cases is not identical and case C75 has more curvature than case C16 which is more like a sphere. The sac volume of C75 is four times more than C16: In addition, the ostium section area of C75 is about two times more C16. Comparisons of the high-risk region where WSS on the sac surface is maximum indicate that high WSS occurs in the dome area in C75 while the neck area is critical in C16. This evaluation indicates that the critical region is related to the size of the aneurysm and ostium section area. Besides, the blood cycle of the body changes from rest to exercise, and expansion of the high-risk region is initiated from the critical region.

Conclusion

The present study has focused on the hemodynamic characteristics of the blood inside the cerebral aneurysm in different body physiology. Comprehensive hemodynamic analyses are performed to disclose the role of the cardiac cycle on the blood hemodynamic inside the cerebral saccular aneurysms. Casson model, as a robust non-Newtonian, is used for the modeling of the blood viscosity in the vessel and aneurysms. The transient blood flow interaction with the aneurysm and vessel wall is modeled via a one-way Fluid-Solid Interaction approach. A comparison of the blood flow in three different body physiologies of rest, normal, and exercise is performed to disclose the risk of aneurysm rupture in various stages of the cardiac cycle.
