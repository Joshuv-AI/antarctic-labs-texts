Association of CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers with overall anemia in U.S. youth: NHANES study
Authors: Menmei Zhang, Rui Zhang, Yuhong Tian, Huabin Wang, Yuan Shi, Yan Li, Luyu Ren, Ruihan Liu, Xueyun Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Evidence suggests a link between inflammation and anemia. This study investigates potential associations between novel inflammatory biomarkers and anemia. Data were obtained from 11,564 participants aged ≤ 18 years from NHANES 2005–2018. Six indices were evaluated: systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), pan-immune-inflammation value (PIV), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR). Logistic regression, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses were performed. Among 11,564 children (mean age 11.8 ± 3.8 years, 51.2% male), 571 cases of anemia were identified. After full adjustment, higher levels of SII, PIV, PLR, and MLR remained significantly associated with increased odds of anemia, whereas the associations for SIRI and NLR were attenuated and no longer statistically significant (both P > 0.05). Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest quartile was associated with greater odds of anemia for SII (OR = 1.65, 95% Cl 1.14–2.38), PlV (OR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.17–2.30), PLR (OR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.19–2.43), and MLR (OR = 1.63, 95% Cl 1.19–2.23). Restricted cubic spline analyses indicated linear associations between all six CBC-derived markers and anemia. Sensitivity stratified analyses showed significant interactions for SII (race, P = 0.03), PIV (age, P = 0.01; sex, P = 0.04), and NLR, whereas MLR, PLR, and SIRI demonstrated consistent associations across subgroups. These results suggest that CBC-derived inflammatory indices are independently associated with overall anemia prevalence in children and adolescents, highlighting their potential as accessible markers for risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06975-5.

Introduction

Anemia represents a critical global public health burden, disproportionately affecting children due to its implications for growth, cognitive development, and long-term morbidity [1]. One-fourth of the global population is estimated to be anemic, with cases increasing rapidly for women, expectant mothers, young girls, and children younger than age 5 [2]. Despite long-term preventive strategies, anemia and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) in young children persist stubbornly. Iron deficiency remains a significant cause of anemia in children [3], but non-iron deficiency etiologies (such as chronic inflammation, infection, obesity-related low-grade inflammation, and hemoglobinopathies) are increasingly recognized as important factors that may be missed by standard hemoglobin screening [4]. Therefore, understanding the low-cost, readily available inflammatory markers associated with anemia in children can improve risk stratification and inform targeted assessments.

Current scientific progress emphasizes the applicability of novel inflammatory indices derived from routine complete blood counts as economical and noninvasive assessment tools for diverse health endpoints. Examples include the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), which have been associated with inflammatory conditions in adults, including metabolic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer [5, 6]. For instance, elevated NLR correlates with increased anemia risk in adult cohorts by reflecting neutrophil-dominated inflammation that impairs iron utilization [7]. More recently, composite indices like the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), Pan-Immune-Inflammation Value (PIV), and Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI) have emerged, integrating multiple blood cell counts to provide a holistic view of systemic inflammation; studies in adult populations link higher SII and SIRI levels to anemia severity, suggesting their potential as prognostic tools [8]. However, pediatric research remains nascent, with limited investigations into these markers. Preliminary studies indicate that NLR and PLR may associate with iron deficiency anemia in children with chronic diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease or obesity, but generalizability to broader populations is constrained by small sample sizes and lack of population-based data [9].

Despite these advancements, significant knowledge gaps persist, particularly regarding the application of novel inflammatory markers to childhood anemia in large, representative cohorts. Current literature predominantly focuses on adult or disease-specific pediatric groups, overlooking the heterogeneity of the general pediatric population and the potential of markers like PIV and SIRI, which remain underexplored in this context [10]. Moreover, existing studies often rely on single-marker approaches, neglecting the integrative power of multi-index analyses, and lack longitudinal NHANES data to account for temporal trends and confounders like diet and infection [11]. This study leverages NHANES 2005–2018 data to evaluate the association between systemic inflammation-related biomarkers (SII, PIV, SIRI, NLR, PLR, MLR) and overall anemia in U.S. children and adolescents, with the aim of improving population-level risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), administered by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), represents a periodic national assessment program aimed at evaluating the health and nutritional well-being of non-institutionalized children and adults residing in the United States. As a division of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the NCHS is tasked with supplying vital epidemiological information to guide national health policy formulation [12].NHANES employs a nationally representative, cross-sectional design using a multistage, clustered probability sampling methodology [13]. The survey integrates comprehensive household interviews with physical examinations conducted by trained health professionals [14, 15]. Participants included in the analysis were younger than 19 years and had completed both the interview and physical examination components at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC). We initially included all participants from NHANES 2005–2018. After excluding participants aged 19 years or older and those with missing data on inflammatory biomarkers, hemoglobin, or covariates, 11,564 children and adolescents were included in the final analysis, including 571 participants with anemia and 10,993 without anemia. Anemia ascertainment

In this study, anemia was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [16]. Specifically, anemia was determined based on age- and sex-specific hemoglobin thresholds: hemoglobinf < 115 g/L for children aged 5–11 years, < 120 g/L for adolescents aged 12–14 years, and for participants aged 15–18 years, < 120 g/L for females and < 130 g/L for males. Participants were categorized as anemic or non-anemic according to these cutoffs.

Calculation of CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers

Complete blood counts were quantified using an automated hematology analyzer (Coulter® DxH 800), with results reported in ×10³ cells/µL. Six CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers were computed as follows: Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII): Platelet count × Neutrophil count / Lymphocyte count. Pan-Immune-Inflammation Value (PIV): SII × Monocyte count. Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI): Neutrophil count × Monocyte count / Lymphocyte count. Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR): Neutrophil count / Lymphocyte count. Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR): Platelet count / Lymphocyte count. Monocyte-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (MLR): Monocyte count / Lymphocyte count.

Covariates

In accordance with previous literature, a range of potential covariates were assessed, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, poverty income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), history of asthma [17, 18]. Dietary intake data—including daily total energy, protein, carbohydrate, dietary fibre, total fatty acids, dietary iron, vitamin B12, and vitamin C—were obtained from two 24-hour dietary recall interviews [19]. Race and ethnicity were categorised as Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races. PIR was calculated by dividing household (or individual) income by the poverty threshold for the survey year. Following U.S. federal standards, we classified participants into three household income tiers: low (PIR ≤ 1.3), middle (PIR > 1.3 to 3.5), and high (PIR ≥ 3.5) [20]. Complete blood count (CBC) and differential counts for all participants were measured using the Beckman Coulter DxH 800 instrument at the NHANES Mobile Examination Center (MEC). Asthma data were obtained from the questionnaire item “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told {you/SP} that {you have/s/he/SP has} asthma?” [21] For other continuous variables with missing values less than 6%, the missing values were replaced by the mean or median values.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Free Statistics software version 2.2 [22] and R software version 4.3.2 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage). All analyses were conducted in accordance with the complex, multistage probability sampling design of NHANES. Appropriate sampling weights, stratification, and clustering were incorporated to obtain nationally representative estimates. Because complete blood count (CBC) variables were derived from laboratory examination data, the corresponding examination sample weights (WTPH2YR) were applied.

To account for the combination of multiple NHANES cycles (2005–2018), the 2-year sampling weights were adjusted following NHANES analytic guidelines by dividing the original weights by the number of cycles included. Survey design variables, including strata (SDMVSTRA) and primary sampling units (SDMVPSU), were also incorporated in all analyses. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers and anemia (Tables 1, 2 and 3). In the multivariable models, six inflammatory indices were analyzed either as continuous variables or as quartile-categorized variables (Table 3). Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

After applying NHANES sampling weights, the analytic sample of 11,564 participants represented approximately 50 million U.S. children and adolescents in the civilian noninstitutionalized population during the study period. Compared with participants without anemia, those with anemia were more likely to be female (75.4% vs. 47.8%, P < 0.001) and older (mean age 13.4 vs. 11.8 years, P < 0.001). Significant differences in race/ethnicity distribution were observed, with a higher proportion of non-Hispanic Black participants in the anemia group (41.1% vs. 12.4%) and a lower proportion of non-Hispanic White participants (28.8% vs. 58.2%) (P < 0.001). The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Relationship between CBC and anemia

In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), higher SII, PIV, PLR, and MLR remained independently associated with greater odds of anemia. Among the continuous variables, PLR showed the strongest association (OR = 2.44, 95% CI 1.60–3.72, P < 0.001), followed by MLR (OR = 1.69, 95% CI 1.24–2.31, P = 0.001), SII (OR = 1.51, 95% CI 1.16–1.97, P = 0.002), and PIV (OR = 1.35, 95% CI 1.11–1.65, P = 0.003). In quartile analyses, the highest quartile was associated with increased odds of anemia for SII (OR = 1.65, 95% CI 1.14–2.38, P = 0.008), PIV (OR = 1.64, 95% CI 1.17–2.30, P = 0.004), PLR (OR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.19–2.43, P = 0.004), and MLR (OR = 1.63, 95% CI 1.19–2.23, P = 0.003). By contrast, the associations for SIRI and NLR were attenuated after full adjustment and no longer reached statistical significance. PIV showed the most consistent graded association across quartiles.

Sensitivity analysis

Forest plots demonstrated significant interaction effects for SII (P for interaction with race = 0.03), PIV (P for interaction with age = 0.01; P for interaction with sex = 0.04), and NLR across subgroups (Fig. 3). The association between SII and anemia was strongest among non-Hispanic Black children. Adolescents (older than 12 years) exhibited a greater anemia risk associated with PIV compared to younger children, while males showed a stronger PIV–anemia association than females. In contrast, MLR showed no significant interactions across subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05). The associations for PLR and SIRI remained consistent in both direction and magnitude across all examined strata (p for interaction > 0.10).

Discussion

In this nationally representative cross-sectional study using NHANES data from 2005 to 2018, we investigated the associations between novel systemic inflammatory markers—SII, PIV, SIRI, NLR, PLR, and MLR—and the prevalence of anemia in US children. Our results showed that higher levels of SII, PIV, PLR, and MLR were independently associated with increased odds of anemia after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and clinical covariates, whereas the associations for SIRI and NLR were attenuated after full adjustment. Among these markers, PLR showed the strongest continuous association, while PIV demonstrated the most consistent graded association across quartiles. These relationships were generally consistent across multiple subgroups, although effect modification was observed for selected biomarkers in subgroup analyses. These associations were generally consistent across multiple subgroups, although significant effect modification was observed for selected biomarkers, including SII by race and PIV by age and sex. Collectively, our findings provide epidemiological evidence of an association between systemic inflammatory indices and anemia in children and adolescents.

Anemia of inflammation (AI), also referred to as anemia of chronic disease, is a hypoproliferative anemia that develops in the context of acute or chronic immune activation, typically characterized by impaired erythropoiesis and restricted iron utilization despite adequate iron stores [23]. The pathophysiology of AI is multifactorial and can be summarized in four main mechanisms. First, dysregulation of the hepcidin–ferroportin axis leads to iron sequestration in macrophages and enterocytes, causing functional iron deficiency despite sufficient iron stores [24]. Second, pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-1β, suppress erythropoiesis by reducing erythropoietin (EPO) production, impairing progenitor responsiveness, and inhibiting red cell maturation [25, 26]. Third, chronic inflammation shortens erythrocyte lifespan via oxidative stress, macrophage activation, and immune-mediated clearance [27, 28]. Fourth, systemic immune and metabolic dysregulation, characterized by neutrophilia, monocytosis, lymphopenia, and platelet activation, alters hematopoietic balance—changes reflected by indices such as SII, PIV, NLR, PLR, and MLR [29–31]. Consistent with these mechanisms, our study observed that elevated inflammatory biomarkers were significantly associated with overall anemia in pediatric populations. However, because the present study did not distinguish iron deficiency anemia, inflammation-related anemia, or other anemia subtypes, these findings should be interpreted as epidemiological associations rather than direct evidence of specific pathogenic pathways. Composite inflammatory indices may provide a practical approach for population-level risk assessment, but their role in identifying specific anemia subtypes requires further investigation.

The inflammatory markers assessed in this study serve as surrogate indicators of such immune–hematopoietic imbalance. SII integrates neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, reflecting innate immune activation, adaptive immune suppression, and thrombopoietic drive [32]. PIV incorporates neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and platelets, offering a broader view of inflammatory burden [33]. Ratios such as NLR, PLR, and MLR each capture distinct aspects of immune dynamics: elevated NLR indicates neutrophilia with lymphopenia (pro-inflammatory stress with impaired regulation) [34–36], higher PLR suggests platelet activation with reduced adaptive immunity, and increased MLR reflects monocyte-driven inflammation relative to lymphocytes. These easily obtainable blood count–derived markers thus mirror immune cell shifts central to the pathogenesis of inflammation-related anemia.

This study has several strengths. First, the use of NHANES data ensures a large sample size and national representativeness, enhancing the generalizability of our findings to the US pediatric population. Second, by simultaneously evaluating multiple novel systemic inflammatory indices, we provide a more comprehensive assessment of the inflammatory milieu than traditional single-parameter analyses. Third, we applied rigorous statistical methods, including natural log transformation of skewed variables, multivariable adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders, and restricted cubic spline modeling to explore non-linear dose–response relationships. Fourth, the consistency of associations across subgroups supports the robustness and stability of our findings.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design of NHANES limits causal inference. Second, NHANES measures inflammatory markers with a single blood sample, which may not fully capture temporal variations. Third, due to the nature of the NHANES database, specific diagnostic biomarkers (such as ferritin, transferrin saturation, hepcidin, and serum vitamin B12 levels) and disease diagnoses (such as inflammatory bowel disease) are missing in some participants, making it difficult to classify iron deficiency anemia or macrocytic anemia. To address these issues, we conducted an additional analysis of microcytic hypochromic anemia in participants aged 12 and older, defined by mean corpuscular volume and serum iron levels. The results were consistent and more significant, further supporting the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Tables 1 & Supplementary Figure S1). Future studies should incorporate more detailed clinical and laboratory data to further elucidate the relationship between CBC-derived inflammatory biomarkers and specific anemia subtypes.

Our findings suggest that systemic inflammatory status may be an underappreciated correlate of pediatric anemia. Integrating blood count-derived inflammatory indices into epidemiologic evaluation may help identify children at higher risk of anemia. However, their clinical utility for etiologic classification or therapeutic decision-making requires further validation.

Conclusions

In conclusion, elevated CBC-derived inflammatory indices were independently associated with overall anemia among U.S. children and adolescents in this NHANES analysis. These findings support the potential value of these readily available hematologic markers for population-level risk stratification, while further studies are needed to clarify their relevance across specific anemia subtypes.
