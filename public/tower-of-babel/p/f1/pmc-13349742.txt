Effect of post-weaning diet on hibernation occurrence in Syrian hamsters
Authors: Akari Yamauchi, Sachiyo Enju, Kanako Sone, Nanaka Matsuoka, Yutaka Tamura, Yoshifumi Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mammalian hibernation is an adaptive strategy for surviving harsh cold seasons and food scarcity by suppressing the metabolic rate and thermogenesis. The Syrian hamster (Mesocricetus auratus) (hereafter termed as hamsters), a small mammalian hibernator, hibernates upon exposure to winter-like, short-photoperiod and cold (SP-Cold) conditions throughout a year in a laboratory, and therefore is a useful animal model obtained from a commercial breeder to study mechanisms of hibernation. However, the rates of hibernation occurrence often vary among experiments and facilities, the reason for which is largely unclear. Here we examined the effects of diet given to hamsters during post-weaning stages on hibernation occurrence. Hamsters fed either a high-nutrient (H) diet suitable for reproduction or a standard (STD) diet during the post-weaning period (from 3 or 4 weeks of age to sexual maturation) were exposed to winter-like conditions, and the occurrence of hibernation was assessed. Females showed a higher hibernation induction rate (over 80%) regardless of the post-weaning diet type than males. Approximately half (52%) of the males fed the STD diet from 3 weeks hibernated, whereas males fed the STD diet from 4 weeks or the H diet showed markedly lower hibernation induction rates (≤33%). These results suggest that female Syrian hamsters are more prone to hibernation than males and that the hibernation induction rate in male hamsters is influenced by diet during a critical developmental window between 3 and 4 weeks of age.

Introduction

Hibernation allows animals to survive harsh cold seasons and food deprivation with a lowered metabolic rate and body temperature (Tb) [1,2,3]. Mammalian hibernators show a range of remarkable physiological characteristics, such as seasonal body remodeling [4,5,6], cold resistance [7,8,9,10,11,12,13], resistance to disuse-induced muscle atrophy [14,15,16,17] and protection from ischemia–reperfusion injury [18, 19]. However, mechanisms regulating hibernation remain poorly understood. The Syrian hamster (Mesocricetus auratus) (hereafter termed hamster) possesses several advantages as an animal model for hibernation research. First, they hibernate upon exposure to winter-like short photoperiodic and cold conditions (SP-Cold) throughout the year in a laboratory. This allows researchers to examine hibernating animals at any time of the year, which is not possible in obligate or strongly seasonal hibernators that hibernate only once in subjective winter timing according to their internal circannual rhythm [3, 20,21,22,23,24]. Second, hamsters are easy to obtain from outbred colonies maintained by commercial breeders. This is in striking contrast to other hibernators that should be obtained from the field. Finally, although hamster oocytes are vulnerable to light [25], genetic manipulation of hamsters has become possible owing to recent advances in developmental engineering and reproductive biology [26,27,28,29,30,31,32]. These features now shed light on this species as a classic but emerging useful model animal for studying the mechanisms of hibernation.

Hamsters reproduce under summer-like, long photoperiod, and warm (LP-Warm) conditions. If exposed to winter-like SP-Cold conditions, they begin to hibernate after several months of pre-hibernation period (pre-HIBP) [6, 33,34,35,36,37]. During hibernation period (HIBP), two distinct states, deep torpor (DT) and periodic arousal (PA), are repeatedly cycled in many small hibernators. DT is characterized by a very low metabolic rate, inactivity, and core Tb below 10°C, and lasts for several days to weeks [38, 39]. PA occurs intermittently, interrupting DT, during which rewarming and arousal are achieved through non-shivering thermogenesis in the brown adipose tissue and shivering thermogenesis in the skeletal muscles. In the case of hamsters, DT continues for 4–5 days at ambient temperature 5°C, with half a day or a day of euthermic PA. During PA, hamsters eat stored food and drink water, although certain hibernators do not. After many cycles of DT-PA, the HIBP ends spontaneously. Notably, it has long been known that some hamsters do not hibernate despite long-term exposure to winter-like conditions [37, 40]. We designated these hamsters as “unresponsiveness to hibernation induction (unHIB).” In addition, the rate of hibernation occurrence varies across experiments and facilities. However, the conditions affecting the occurrence of unHIB remain unclear.

Accumulating evidence suggests that nutritional conditions early in life can influence metabolism and physiological function later in life. The developmental origins of health and disease (DOHaD) hypothesis proposes that early life nutrition determines susceptibility to diseases in adulthood [41,42,43]. Low-birth-weight offspring resulting from maternal undernutrition or stress during pregnancy are known to be more susceptible to developing hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and kidney disease in adulthood [44,45,46,47,48]. For instance, both low and high protein intakes during the fetal period have been suggested to increase the risk of developing disease and metabolic dysfunction later in life [42, 49]. Male rats fed a high-fat diet from the post-weaning period (3 weeks of age) were more prone to developing metabolic syndrome–like conditions in adulthood, including higher fasting glucose, dyslipidemia, and obesity, compared with those exposed to the diet from 8 weeks of age [50]. In male Syrian hamsters, exposure to a high-fat diet (HFD) from the post-weaning period (3 weeks of age) leads to increased visceral fat accumulation, microvascular dysfunction, and enhanced insulin resistance later in life [51].

Metabolic programming established during development may affect the occurrence of seasonal hibernation, which is accompanied by extensive remodeling of lipid metabolism, endocrine regulation, and energy homeostasis. Previous studies on some hibernators, including ground squirrels, arctic ground squirrels, garden dormice, marmots, and Syrian hamsters, have examined the effects of diet during the pre-HIBP and HIBP on the initiation and depth of hibernation [52,53,54,55,56,57,58]. However, it remains largely unclear whether an early life diet in the active or summer season affects later hibernation occurrence in the winter season. In this study, we aimed to determine whether the post-weaning diet in summer-like conditions affects the occurrence of hibernation in winter-like conditions in hamsters. Hamsters supplied by commercial breeders in Japan are typically reared on a high-nutrient (H) diet suitable for reproduction. Our previous study demonstrated that female hamsters fed a standard (STD) diet from 3 weeks of age under summer-like conditions to adults showed a high hibernation induction rate in winter-like conditions [36]. Materials and Methods

Animals and housing

Syrian hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus) from a closed outbred colony maintained on the H diet (Nihon Nosan, Kanagawa, Japan) were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). Three to five animals were housed per cage with ad libitum access to either the MR standard (STD) diet or the H diet (Nihon Nosan, Kanagawa, Japan) (Supplementary Table 1) and water under LP-Warm conditions (light:dark=14:10 h and ambient temperature=22–25°C). The body mass and food consumption of the hamsters was measured weekly. As previously described [37], one to three weeks before the transfer to the SP-Cold condition (light:dark=8:16 h and ambient temperature=4–5°C), Tb loggers (iButton, #DS1925L-F5, Maxim Integrated, Mouser, Chandler, AZ, USA) coated with rubber (Plasti Dip, Performix Coatings, Blaine, MN, USA) were surgically implanted into the abdominal cavity of animals under inhalation anesthesia with 4% isoflurane (DS Pharma Animal Health, Osaka, Japan). After one-three weeks recovery, animals were transferred to SP-Cold condition for hibernation induction. Under SP-Cold conditions, animals were housed individually in polypropylene cages and provided ad libitum access to water and the STD diet. The onset of hibernation was confirmed immediately by the characteristic postures of the animals (rolled into a ball), reduced activity, and food consumption when cages were changed weekly. The modified ‘saw-dust method’ was also used to confirm that animals successfully hibernated; wood chips placed onto the back of hibernating individuals remained in place until the animals experienced PA [33]. Hibernation was further validated using Tb recordings from the implanted iButton loggers. Cage replacement and body mass measurement of animals in the DT were skipped to avoid disturbing the DT.

Criteria for hibernation onset

The occurrence of hibernation in hamsters was determined by visual inspection and finally by the core Tb recorded by a temperature data logger. Individuals whose Tb remained below 10°C for more than 24 h were classified as hibernating individual [59]. A Python-based Tb analysis program [60] was used as a supportive tool to aid in the identification of hibernating individuals. For hibernating individuals in which body temperature recording failed, the onset of hibernation was defined as the day on which the characteristic hibernation posture (rolled into a ball) was first observed during weekly cage-change records. For hibernating individuals, the time from the start of cold exposure to the onset of the first DT was defined as the pre-HIBP. Female animals that did not show a Tb decrease meeting the above criterion within 7 months (210 days) under SP-Cold conditions were classified as unresponsiveness to hibernation induction (unHIB). In the case of male hamsters, testicular regression normally occurs prior to the onset of hibernation [34], and all the hamsters we examined so far in our laboratory hibernated within four months of pre-HIBP. Therefore, we classified male individuals that did not exhibit hibernating features, that is, a Tb decrease meeting the above criterion, food consumption reduction, and testicular regression, as unHIB for subsequent analyses. One female hamster died during the pre-HIBP and was excluded from subsequent analyses.

Tb recording with data loggers

Tb loggers were used to determine the exact onset of hibernation. Tb loggers (iButton, #DS1925L-F5, Maxim Integrated, Chandler, AZ, USA) coated with rubber (total mass of approximately 3.5 g; Plasti Dip, Performix Coatings, Blaine, MN, USA) were surgically implanted into the abdominal cavity under inhalation anesthesia with 4% isoflurane (DS Pharma Animal Health, Osaka, Japan). The iButtons calibrated in the range (−10 to +65°C) by the manufacturer set a resolution of 0.0625°C and used to accurately determine the Tb ±0.5°C. All iButtons were set to record the Tb every 10 min.

Experiment 1

Four-week-old male hamsters (n=80) were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. Animals were raised under LP-Warm conditions until 9–10 weeks of age and were fed either a standard (STD) diet (STD_male_4w group, n=43) or a high-nutrient (H) diet (H_male_4w group, n=37) to examine whether differences in nutritional quality during early life affect hibernation occurrence. Tb loggers were surgically implanted 1–2 weeks before transfer to SP-Cold conditions. After surgery, the animals were individually housed in polypropylene cages with ad libitum access to food and water. After the animals were transferred to SP-Cold conditions, they were individually caged and given ad libitum access to water and food. All animals were fed the STD diet throughout the SP-Cold conditions. Core Tb was recorded every 10 min by the implanted loggers. Tb loggers were retrieved from animals after they had spent four to seven months under SP-Cold conditions. Tb data from one hibernating animal (H_male_4w hamster) were not recovered due to a failure in recording.

Experiment 2

The sex and age of the hamsters when the two diets were administered were different from those in Experiment 1. Three-week-old male hamsters (n=41) and three-week-old female hamsters (n=36) were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. Animals were fed either an STD diet (male: STD_male_3w, n=21; female: STD_female_3w, n=20) or an H diet (male: H_male_3w, n=20; female: H_female_3w, n=16) under LP-Warm conditions until they were 12 weeks old. Tb loggers were surgically implanted 2–3 weeks before the transfer to SP-Cold conditions. After surgery, animals recovered individually in polypropylene cages with ad libitum access to food and water. After animals transferring to SP-Cold conditions, animals caged individually given with ad libitum access to water and food. All animals were fed the STD diet throughout the SP-Cold conditions. Tb was recorded every 10 min with the logger after the implantation. Tb loggers were retrieved from animals after they had spent four to seven months under SP-Cold conditions. Tb data from two hibernating animals (one STD_female_3w hamster and one of H_female_3w hamster) were not recovered due to failure in recording. One STD_female_3w hamster died during the pre-HIBP and was excluded from subsequent analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software. For the hibernation induction rate, statistical comparisons between the STD and H diet groups were conducted within each cohort (male_4w, male_3w, and female_3w) using Fisher’s exact test. The time to hibernation onset was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, with group differences tested using the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards model was applied to compare the hazard of entering hibernation among groups, using H_male_3w (the lowest hibernation induction rate) as a reference. A Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test with Holm correction, was used to compare the pre-HIBP values among the groups. For the time-course analyses of body mass and food consumption, each cohort consisted of four groups defined by diet (STD or H) and hibernation outcome (HIB or unHIB). These longitudinal data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) that included the interaction between the week and group (diet × hibernation occurrence). After fitting the LMMs, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference test was performed to identify pairwise group differences. Pairwise comparisons between HIB and unHIB individuals at each week were tested using Welch’s t-tests, with Holm correction applied when multiple comparisons were performed. The relationships between pre-HIBP and body mass variables (maximum body mass during the pre-HIBP, body mass at SP-Cold transfer, and body mass one week before the onset of HIB) were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation tests.

To help interpret the results from the two experiments, we represented the data using the cumulative probability distribution of hibernating individuals (Fig. 2A) and found a significant difference among the six experimental groups (log‐rank test, P<0.0001). Using H_male_3w as the reference (the group with the lowest hibernation induction rate), the Cox proportional hazards model showed that STD_male_3w had a significantly higher hazard of entering hibernation (HR=4.42, P=0.023), indicating a greater likelihood of hibernation. No significant differences were detected for STD_male_4w or H_male_4w when compared with the reference. In contrast, both female groups displayed a markedly higher probability of hibernation (STD_female_3w: HR=10.58; H_female_3w: HR=7.78) than the male group. However, the pre-HIBP did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig. 2B).

Together, these results suggest that male hamsters became reluctant to hibernate if they were fed a certain type of diet during the post-weaning period from 3 to 4 weeks of age, while female hamsters were more prone to hibernation than males, irrespective of the post-weaning diet.

The difference in hibernation occurrence by diet in male hamsters was independent of growth rate and body mass during the post-weaning stage

To evaluate whether post-weaning early life nutritional conditions affect hibernation occurrence via early growth or body mass trajectories, we compared body mass and food consumption between HIB and unHIB individuals throughout the entire LP-Warm rearing period in each diet group (Supplementary Fig. 1). Linear mixed model (LMM) analyses throughout the LP-Warm rearing period detected no differences in body mass or food consumption among the male groups (Supplementary Figs. 1A, B, D and E). In females, food consumption did not differ among the groups, whereas body mass differed significantly (P=0.009; Supplementary Figs. 1C and F); unHIB females fed with H diet exhibited higher growth after 9 weeks of age (P=0.04 at 9–11w). These results indicate that the post-weaning diet differences in this study did not alter growth or food consumption trajectories in males, while divergence in female body mass could emerge later in development under LP-Warm rearing conditions.

Different colored lines represent linear regression for each group: STD_male_4w (green), H_male_4w (yellow), STD_male_3w (blue), H_male_3w (cyan), STD_female_3w (red), and H_female_3w (magenta). The relationship between pre-HIBP and body mass at each time point was evaluated using Pearson’s correlation test.

In contrast, in males, pre-HIBP was not correlated with maximum body mass during pre-HIBP in either the weaning age or the diet group (Fig. 4A), and similarly showed no correlation with body mass at the SP-Cold transfer (Fig. 4C) or body mass one week before the hibernation onset (Fig. 4E). Together, these results suggest that body mass during the pre-HIBP under SP-Cold conditions is related to the timing of hibernation onset in females but not in males.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that post-weaning conditions shape subsequent hibernation occurrence in Syrian hamsters in a sex- and timing-dependent manner. In males, the hibernation induction rate was highest in animals switched to the standard (STD) diet at 3 weeks of age (STD_male_3w; 52%), whereas groups exposed for a longer period to the high-nutrient (H) diet showed the lowest (15%) or at best approximately 30% induction rate (Fig. 2, Table 1). Thus, post-weaning diet may markedly affect hibernation induction later in life, with less effect on growth rates and food consumption under LP-Warm conditions in our experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast to males, females exhibited consistently high hibernation induction rates (>80%) regardless of the post-weaning diet, indicating that post-weaning nutritional conditions primarily modulate hibernation propensity in males.

The dietary effect in males strongly depended on the timing of dietary exposure. STD_male_3w showed a higher induction rate than STD_male_4w (52% vs. 33%; Table 1). Because hamsters from the vendor are routinely fed the H diet (Fig. 1) and weaned at 3 weeks of age, animals assigned to the 4w group consumed the H diet during the critical 3–4-week window, potentially blunting the later effect of STD feeding. After cold exposure, HIB males in the STD-fed groups showed a significant reduction in food consumption (Fig. 3). Early STD feeding may program later-life regulation of energy metabolic pathways in response to chronic cold exposure via unknown epigenetic mechanisms, thereby promoting hibernation.

Our findings are consistent with broader evidence that nutritional environments influence later hibernation strategies. Nutrient-rich diets can reduce hibernation expression in golden-mantled ground squirrels (Spermophilus lateralis), arctic ground squirrels, and European hamsters, including associations with lipid intake, PUFA balance, and access to high-calorie foods [53, 56, 61, 62]. Based on our previous preliminary observations, hamsters fed the H diet during pre-HIBP showed reduced hibernation occurrence. Therefore, prolonged exposure to the H diet, which is nutritionally favorable for reproduction, may induce metabolic or body composition states that reduce the reliance on energy-saving hibernation in Syrian hamsters.

We also found prominent sex differences in the relationship between hibernation and body mass. Consistent with our previous report in females [36], we observed a positive correlation between the pre-HIBP and maximum body mass (Fig. 4). However, such a correlation was not found in males, and hibernation occurrence was not associated with body mass at the SP-Cold transfer, maximum body mass during the pre-HIBP, or body mass one week before hibernation onset (Fig. 4). Although in the male_4w group with a low hibernation induction rate, unHIB individuals had higher body mass than HIB individuals, such body mass differences were not observed in the male_3w groups despite large differences in the hibernation induction rate between the H and STD groups. These observations suggest that body mass is not a critical determinant of hibernation occurrence in male hamsters.

Previous studies have suggested sex differences in vulnerability to early life nutritional conditions in rats, mice, hamsters, and humans [63,64,65,66,67]. Male rats and mice are reported to be more prone to metabolic dysregulation, such as hypertension, following early life nutritional perturbations [63, 64]. In hamsters, gestational food restriction induced an increase in body mass more prominently in male offspring [66], implying that environmental sensitivity is higher in males than in females. Overall, our sex-specific dietary effects align with the literature, showing that males are more vulnerable to early life nutritional perturbations and long-term metabolic dysregulation.

Within the framework of the DOHaD hypothesis, early-life nutritional conditions have been shown to affect later disease susceptibility. In male hamsters, exposure to a HFD from 3 weeks of age increased susceptibility to metabolic disorders in adulthood [51]. Our findings suggest that the DOHaD framework may also apply to adaptive seasonal physiological phenotypes, such as hibernation, in addition to disease susceptibility. In hamsters, parental food restriction during mid-gestation (E5.0–E14.0) has been reported to increase later growth in male offspring [66]. In garden dormice, birth timing affects hibernation frequency and duration, potentially through lasting differences in body composition despite compensatory growth [68]. These findings imply that nutritional conditions during earlier developmental stages, including gestation or lactation, may potentially influence hibernation occurrence in offspring. Further investigation into the developmental regulation of hibernation occurrence may help extend the DOHaD framework to encompass seasonal physiological adaptations.

The limitations of this study should be noted. First, the hamsters used in this study were supplied from an outbred colony of the breeder, not excluding the possibility that subtle genetic differences among animals may affect hibernation occurrence. Second, the age at SP-Cold transfer differed between the male_3w (12 weeks) and male_4w (9–10 weeks) groups. A previous study reported that aged hamsters are more prone to hibernation under natural photoperiodic and ambient temperature conditions [69]. Although it is unclear whether 2–3 weeks of age differences affect the hibernation induction rate and pre-HIBP, we cannot exclude the possibility that age at SP-Cold transfer may influence the observed differences in hibernation induction rate. Third, the STD and H diets are not chemically defined and differ in multiple components, including protein content and fatty acid composition. Thus, it is currently unclear whether our findings can be applied to studies using other diets. Despite these limitations, our results identified post-weaning dietary conditions that enhance hibernation induction in male hamsters and provide new insights into the early life nutritional regulation of hibernation.

In summary, this study demonstrated that male hamsters become more likely to hibernate when weaned earlier and fed the STD diet, and suggested that the post-weaning period between 3 and 4 weeks of age is critical for shaping later hibernation occurrence. This study will help establish reliable conditions for hibernation induction in this species, which is essential for advancing basic and applied research to understand the fascinating and remarkable physiology of hibernation.
