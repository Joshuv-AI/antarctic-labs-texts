Yeo’s Index for Mitral Valve Assessment during Percutaneous Transvenous Mitral Commissurotomy
Authors: Jamal Yusuf, Ankit Bansal, Dixit Goyal, Arima Nigam, Vimal Mehta, Ankur Gautam, Mohit D. Gupta, Gaurav Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Rheumatic mitral stenosis (MS) severity assessment by planimetry, pressure half-time (PHT), and continuity equation (CE) has limitations. Yeo’s index, the product of mitral leaflet separation index and dimensionless index, combining anatomic and hemodynamic severity, offers a promising alternative.Objective:The objective of this study was to evaluate the Yeo’s index accuracy in MS before and after percutaneous transvenous mitral commissurotomy (PTMC) and its correlation with conventional methods and mitral valve area (MVA) by three-dimensional (3D) transesophageal echocardiography (TEE).Materials and Methods:A prospective, single-center interventional study of 100 patients with severe symptomatic MS undergoing PTMC. Transthoracic echocardiography and 3D TEE were performed pre-PTMC and 48 h post-PTMC.Results:The mean age was 35.94 years, with 68% of females and 34% in atrial fibrillation (AF). The mean Yeo’s index before PTMC was 0.16 ± 0.08 cm, and after PTMC, it increased to 0.31 ± 0.12 cm. Pre-PTMC, the Yeo’s index showed moderate-to-strong positive correlations with MVA by PHT (r = 0.584), planimetry (r = 0.483), 3D TEE (r = 0.592), and CE (r = 0.665); P < 0.0001. Post-PTMC, correlations remained strong with CE (r = 0.665) and 3D TEE (r = 0.643); P < 0.0001. Receiver operating curve analysis confirmed its strong predictive value, with a pre-PTMC cutoff of < 0.147 cm identifying very severe MS (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.974; sensitivity: 94.74% and specificity: 100%) in non-AF and AF patients (AUC = 0.95; sensitivity: 90% and specificity: 100%). Severe MS was defined with a cutoff of 0.147 − 0.26 cm (AUC = 0.964 in non-AF and 0.95 in AF).Conclusion:The Yeo’s index is a reliable, noninvasive tool for assessing MS severity both pre-and post-PTMC, demonstrating strong correlations and accuracy even in AF.

INTRODUCTION

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is an acquired chronic valvular heart disease affecting mainly the younger population, especially in developing countries.[1] It is the leading cause of mitral stenosis (MS) worldwide, which results from thickening of mitral leaflets, commissural fusion, and shortening and fusion of chordae tendineae as a sequela to rheumatic fever.[2] Echocardiography remains the cornerstone investigation for diagnosing and assessing the severity of MS and evaluating valve morphology for potential interventions. Recently, three dimensional (3D) transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) has gained popularity and is increasingly used as a gold standard to calculate mitral valve area (MVA) accurately.[34] Anatomical and hemodynamic methods are commonly used for MVA assessment. The former includes the planimetry method, and the latter includes the pressure half-time (PHT) and continuity equation (CE) methods. However, there are certain limitations bound to all these methods. Planimetry depends on direct visualization of MV orifice, which is operator dependent and can be technically challenging with suboptimal images. PHT determines the functional valve area from the pressure decay between the left atrium (LA) and left ventricle (LV). Hence, its results can be changed by any factors affecting the atrial or ventricular compliance or LV diastolic pressure, such as LV hypertrophy, LA fibrosis, or significant aortic regurgitation (AR). CE method is limited by multiple measurements and the assumption that the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) is circular. Its results are also inaccurate in the presence of significant mitral regurgitation (MR) or AR.[5]

The mitral leaflet separation index (MLSI), measuring the anatomical separation of the MV leaflet tips in diastole, has also been described earlier as an accurate method to assess the severity of MS.[6] Recently, a new novel index, i.e. Yeo’s index, has emerged, which is a product of MLSI and MV dimensionless index (DI). DI is calculated by dividing LVOT pulse wave (PW) velocity–time integral (VTI) with CW VTI of the mitral valve. It is not affected by the previously mentioned limitations and has shown a good correlation against MVA by planimetry and PHT.[7] However, its validation is lacking in the prospective interventional study of percutaneous transvenous mitral commissurotomy (PTMC), both in pre- and postprocedure correlation with MVA calculated with 3D TEE, which is considered the gold standard. Hence, in the present study, we evaluated the accuracy of the Yeo’s index in patients of rheumatic MS before and after PTMC, compared to conventional methods with TTE and MVA with 3D TEE, for the first time in the Indian subcontinent.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Research site

The study was conducted in the Department of Cardiology, Govind Ballabh Pant Institute of Post Graduate Medical Education and Research (GIPMER), New Delhi, after approval of the Institutional Ethics Committee on Clinical Investigation (approval no. F.1/IEC/MAMC/111/04/2024/No. 487).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

It was a single-center, prospective interventional study and included 100 patients with severe MS, whether in normal sinus rhythm or atrial fibrillation (AF), who were eligible for PTMC according to ACC/AHA guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease.[8] Following patients were excluded: suboptimal images or severe mitral valvular calcification that hinders accurate MLSI calculation, any other significant lesions such as moderate to severe MR, or significant aortic stenosis (AS) or AR, valvular morphology incompatible with PTMC (including significant commissural calcium or extensive subvalvular disease), Left atrial/left atrial appendage (LA/LAA) clot preventing PTMC, or contraindications for transesophageal echocardiography.

Two-dimensional echocardiography

Each patient underwent transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) using a 5-MHz phased array probe and transesophageal echocardiography using an X7-2t probe, both performed with a standard echocardiographic system (EPIQ 7, Philips), before and 48 h after PTMC. MVA was calculated using planimetry, PHT, and CE according to the American Society of Echocardiography 2023 guidelines.[5] For measuring MVA by planimetry, the short-axis imaging plane was positioned at the tip of the valve to avoid overestimation. The transmitral pressure gradient was measured from the apical four-chamber window using continuous wave Doppler (CWD) aligned coaxially with mitral inflow. The CWD spectral display of mitral inflow provided the VTI of transmitral flow, the mean transmitral pressure gradient derived from the VTI, and PHT. PHT was defined as the time taken for the instantaneous pressure gradient to decrease by half from its peak value and was calculated as MVA (cm2) =220/PHT. MVA by the CE was calculated as follows: MVA (cm2) = Cross sectional area (CSA) LVOT × VTI LVOT/VTI mitral. The mean of three readings for patients in sinus rhythm and five readings for patients in AF of MVA by planimetry, PHT, and CE were taken. Yeo’s index was the product of MLSI and DI. MLSI was measured from inner edge to inner edge in diastole in both the parasternal long-axis and apical four-chamber views. The gain was adjusted to accurately identify the inner edge and compensate for the effect of attenuation. DI was calculated by dividing LVOT VTI (measured by PW Doppler in the apical five-chamber view) by mitral valve VTI (measured by CWD in the apical five-chamber view). The mean of three readings for patients in sinus rhythm and five readings for patients in AF of MLSI, LVOT VTI, and mitral valve VTI were taken. Yeo’s INDEX was calculated as the product of MLSI and DI (Yeo’s index = MLSI × DI).

Three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography

To acquire 3D TEE images, a 2D image of the mitral valve in the bicommissural view was taken. Real-time 3D “en face” views of the mitral valve were then obtained using the 3D zoom mode, or full volume if good 3D zoom views were not achievable. By centering the mitral valve on the screen, the sector settings were adjusted to optimize image and color resolution. Multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) measurements of the MVA were performed offline using the dedicated Philips 3DQ advanced software. First, the maximum early diastolic opening of the mitral valve was determined. MPR of the mitral valve orifice was then conducted to locate two orthogonal planes crossing the tips of the mitral valve, followed by a third cropping plane at the level of the mitral valve where the orifice was smallest [Figure 1]. Planimetry was performed “en face” at the cross-sectional plane during early to mid-diastole, when the diastolic opening was greatest, to obtain the MVA [Figure 2]. The MVA obtained by the 3D TEE was correlated with Yeo’s index before and after PTMC. All measurements were taken by two operators who were blinded to the clinical and echocardiographic data.

Planimetry to measure mitral valve area

The primary endpoint was to correlate the Yeo’s index with MVA on 3D TEE before and after PTMC, while the secondary endpoint was to correlate the Yeo’s index with MVA measured by planimetry, PHT, and CE before and after PTMC on TTE and to determine a cutoff value for the severity of rheumatic MS.

The categorical variables were presented in the form of numbers and percentages (%). On the other hand, the quantitative data were presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD) and median with 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range). The data entry was done in the Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and the final analysis was done with the use of the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, IBM manufacturer, Chicago, USA, version 25.0. For statistical significance, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals (CIs) were presented where relevant. The 3D MVA of >1.5 cm2, ≤1.5 cm2, and ≤1.0 cm2 was considered as moderate, severe, and very severe MS, respectively.[8] According to a previous study, the Yeo’s index cutoff values of <0.147 cm and 0.147–0.26 cm were taken to predict very severe MS and severe MS, respectively.[7] Intra- and interobserver variability of Yeo’s index was assessed by intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% CI using SPSS reliability analyses. No artificial intelligence-assisted technologies (such as large language models, chatbots, or image creators) were used in the production of submitted work.

Receiver operating characteristic curve of pre-PTMC Yeo’s index in predicting very severe mitral stenosis

In addition, the predictive value of the Yeo’s index for identifying very severe and severe MS was evaluated. The ROC curve revealed that the pre-PTMC Yeo’s index exhibited excellent performance, with a cutoff point of <0.147 cm, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.974 (AUC = 0.974; 95% CI: 0.922–1) for accurately predicting very severe MS in patients without AF [Figure 5a]. In patients with AF, the AUC was 0.95 (AUC = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.852–1) [Figure 5b]. The Yeo’s index demonstrated a sensitivity of 94.74% and specificity of 100% in patients without AF and retained strong predictive value in patients with AF, with a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 100%.

DISCUSSION

RHD remains a significant burden in the developing countries, with India often referred to as the capital of RHD. India contributes nearly 40% of the global RHD burden, with about 13.2 million of the 33 million affected worldwide. It remains the leading cause of MS.[9]

Whenever suitable, PTMC has always been considered the treatment of choice for moderate-to-severe MS. Echocardiography has been the cornerstone investigation for diagnosing MS, assessing its severity, and evaluating the feasibility of PTMC. MVA is primarily used to determine the severity of mitral valve stenosis, with planimetry, PHT, and CE being the main methods employed for a very long time. However, all of these traditional methods have well-recognized limitations.[56] Studies showed a good correlation between the MLSI and invasively obtained MVA by Gorlin’s formula and MVA by planimetry and PHT.[1011] The application of MLSI before and after PTMC has also been validated in previous studies.[121314] Further research demonstrated a strong correlation between MLSI and MVA as assessed by 3D TEE.[1516] However, MLSI is purely based on anatomy with its own limitations, particularly as it becomes difficult to calculate in the presence of heavily calcified leaflets.

Yeo’s index provides both anatomical and hemodynamic assessment of the severity of mitral valve stenosis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study which has assessed and shown the accuracy of Yeo’s index in predicting MVA both pre- and post-PTMC and also showed its strong positive correlation with MVA calculated by 3D TEE. Yeo’s index also showed a moderate-to-strong positive correlation with pre-PTMC and post-PTMC MVA measurements obtained by PHT, planimetry, and CE. 3D TEE has emerged as the gold standard for MVA assessment because it provides en face visualization of the mitral valve orifice, allows precise planimetry, and is less influenced by loading conditions compared to PHT or CE. Prior studies have shown strong agreement between 3D TEE and catheterization-derived MVA, supporting its role as the reference standard.[57] For this reason, we used 3D TEE as the comparator for validating Yeo’s index in our study.

Yeo’s index accurately predicted very severe MS (MVA ≤1 cm2), with an excellent AUC of 0.974 (95% CI: 0.922–1). A Yeo’s index value of <0.147 cm identified very severe MS with a sensitivity of 94.74% and a specificity of 100%. Notably, Yeo’s index maintained a strong predictive performance even in patients with AF, achieving a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 100%.

Yeo’s index also demonstrated excellent accuracy in predicting severe MS (MVA ≤1.5 cm2), with an AUC of 0.964 (95% CI: 0.894–1). A Yeo’s index value between 0.147 and 0.26 cm identified severe MS with a sensitivity of 92.86% and a specificity of 100%. Importantly, the presence of AF did not impact its diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.852–1) even in the presence of AF.

Similarly, studies conducted by Leow et al. also demonstrated the strong discriminative power of Yeo’s index in assessing the severity of MS in isolated lesions as well as mixed valvular heart disease, even in the presence of AF. These studies were mainly retrospective studies, and the study population comprises mainly older age group. Their findings also highlighted that Yeo’s index performs well in evaluating the severity of rheumatic MS, showing a strong positive correlation with the gold standard of 3D TEE-derived MVA, consistent with the results of our study.[717] Our study is a prospective study which comprises mainly a younger age population who were eligible for PTMC in comparison to the older age population, who are generally not fit for PTMC and hence are subjected for mitral valve replacement as the results of PTMC are not very conducive in this age group. Hence, it will not be easy to validate the findings of Yeo’s index in the older age population undergoing PTMC, as the required number of cases will be too small to draw any conclusion. Future validation studies in older patients and those with degenerative MS are warranted to establish the broader applicability of Yeo’s index in these cohorts.

For the first time, we evaluated the predictive value of Yeo’s index in post-PTMC patients. Our study demonstrated that a Yeo’s index >0.254 cm (AUC = 0.978) in patients without AF and >0.2844 cm (AUC = 0.819) in those with AF accurately predicted moderate MS (MVA >1.5 cm²). Remarkably, the index achieved a sensitivity of 100% in both AF and non-AF groups, with a specificity of 94.4% in non-AF patients and 77.8% in those with AF.

Yeo’s index is a promising tool for predicting the severity of MS in both pre- and post-PTMC settings, demonstrating moderate-to-strong positive correlation with MVA measurements obtained through planimetry, PHT, CE, and 3D TEE. AF affects nearly one-third of patients with rheumatic MS.[18] It is noteworthy that Yeo’s index retains its accuracy even in the presence of AF, overcoming limitations associated with other assessment methods. Moreover, it requires no additional equipment, is easy to apply, and offers a time-efficient approach for evaluating MS severity. It is flow independent, as changes in flow would have a similar directional effect on the mitral velocity time integral (VTI) and LVOT VTI. Yeo’s index may serve as a valuable adjunct to existing diagnostic measures, particularly in cases, where discrepancies arise among conventional MVA assessment techniques.

Limitations

A major limitation of the study is the small sample size. Since this study involves the assessment in both the pre- and post-PTMC periods, a large sample size was difficult to achieve. The second limitation of our study is that we did not correlate Yeo’s index with cardiac catheterization data, but nowadays, MVA by 3D TEE is also considered the gold standard for MVA assessment. Another limitation of our study is that we included only patients with isolated rheumatic MS, excluding those with any other significant associated valvular lesions. This selection was necessary because our study involved PTMC, a procedure that is feasible only in the absence of any other major valvular abnormalities. Our study was conducted in a single center with a relatively young rheumatic MS population, and therefore, the findings may not be directly generalizable to elderly patients or those with degenerative MS.

CONCLUSION

This prospective study validates Yeo’s index as a reliable, simple, and reproducible tool for assessing MS severity before and after PTMC. It demonstrated strong correlation with conventional echocardiographic parameters and excellent agreement with 3D TEE, the current gold standard, while maintaining accuracy even in patients with AF. By integrating both anatomic and hemodynamic information, Yeo’s index overcomes several limitations of existing methods and can serve as a practical adjunct in routine evaluation.
