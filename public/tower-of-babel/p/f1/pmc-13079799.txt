NOX1/4 drives hepatic iron and lipid dysregulation, redox imbalance, and inflammation in ethanol-fed mice
Authors: Linna Yu, Tian He, Pengli Zhang, Xueru Zhao, Linting Xun, Ruochang Li, Ping Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) is pathologically characterized by iron overload, oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and inflammation. However, the central regulatory mechanisms orchestrating these processes remain elusive. This study aimed to explore the role of NADPH oxidases 1 and 4 (NOX1/4) in ethanol-induced liver injury and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of the NOX1/4 inhibitor, Setanaxib. Mice were randomly divided into three groups: pair-fed control, ethanol-fed, and ethanol-fed + Setanaxib treatment. On day 16, the mice were euthanized, and liver tissue and blood samples were collected for comprehensive analyses to explore the involvement and potential mechanisms of NOX1/4 in ethanol-induced liver injury. Hepatic NOX1/4 expression was significantly upregulated in ethanol-fed mice, implicating their role in ALD pathogenesis. Setanaxib treatment markedly attenuated multiple facets of this injury, including liver injury, iron overload, hepatic steatosis, and oxidative stress. Mechanistically, Setanaxib may restore iron homeostasis by suppressing hepcidin/FPN1 signaling and downregulating TFR1 expression, activate antioxidant defenses via the Nrf2/HO-1/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis, and mitigate inflammation by reducing macrophage infiltration and pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Our findings suggest that NOX1/4 may play a central role in coordinating the interconnected pathogenic processes that drive ALD progression, including iron overload, lipid dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Our findings establish Setanaxib, a novel NOX1/4 inhibitor, as a promising multi-target therapeutic strategy for ALD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42716-4.

Introduction

Alcoholic liver disease (ALD) represents a major global health burden, contributing to over 50% of cirrhosis-related deaths and 20–30% of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cases worldwide1. This disease encompasses a spectrum of pathological conditions ranging from simple steatosis to steatohepatitis, progressive fibrosis, cirrhosis, and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma2. The pathogenesis of ALD is multifactorial and involves complex interplay between ethanol hepatotoxicity, metabolic dysregulation, oxidative stress, inflammatory cascades, and gut-liver axis dysfunction3,4. Despite decades of research, current therapeutic strategies remain primarily limited to alcohol abstinence and supportive management5, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapies targeting underlying pathological mechanisms.

Chronic alcohol consumption disrupts intestinal barrier integrity and induces dysbiosis, facilitating the translocation of microbial products, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), into the portal circulation, thereby amplifying hepatic inflammation6. Additionally, alcohol metabolism generates excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) through multiple sources, including the cytochrome P450 system (particularly CYP2E1), mitochondrial dysfunction, and activated NADPH oxidase (NOX) enzymes7,8. This oxidative burden triggers hepatocyte death via diverse pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis. Ferroptosis is a recently recognized form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation9. Simultaneously, infiltrating immune cells and activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) exacerbate liver damage and promote fibrosis10, highlighting the multicellular nature of alcoholic liver disease progression.

Iron overload is emerging as a critical but often overlooked factor in the pathogenesis of ALD. Chronic alcohol consumption disrupts systemic iron homeostasis, leading to pathological hepatic iron accumulation11. This excess iron serves as a potent catalyst for oxidative stress by catalyzing the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the Fenton reaction12, which in turn which exacerbates lipid peroxidation, promotes hepatic steatosis, and impairs mitochondrial function13,14. The resultant oxidative burden amplifies iron-dependent cellular injury, potentially through the induction of regulated cell death pathways such as ferroptosis15. Concurrently, oxidative stress stimulates macrophage infiltration into the liver and these activated immune cells not only perpetuate inflammation but also contribute to further hepatic steatosis and iron retention16. This interplay establishes a self-amplifying vicious cycle involving iron overload, lipid dysregulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation, thereby significantly accelerating the progression of ALD.

Hepcidin, a peptide hormone synthesized by hepatocytes, serves as the master regulator of systemic iron homeostasis17. It functions by binding to ferroportin (FPN), the sole identified mammalian iron exporter18, thereby triggering FPN internalization and degradation19,20. This interaction, known as the “hepcidin-FPN axis,” constitutes the major checkpoint for iron transport from hepatocytes, macrophages, and enterocytes. Paradoxically, hepcidin overexpression can cause serum iron deficiency alongside liver iron overload21,22. Conversely, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) is the primary receptor for cellular iron uptake. Its overexpression promotes hepatic iron accumulation by increasing the internalization of transferrin-bound iron23. The concurrent dysregulation of iron efflux (via hepcidin) and iron influx (via TFR1) thus establishes a molecular foundation for progressive hepatic iron overload.

NADPH oxidase 1 and 4 (NOX1/4) are key intracellular ROS-generating enzymes24,25. However, their potential role as upstream regulators linking to key pathological processes in ALD, including oxidative stress, inflammation, and iron/lipid dysregulation, remains insufficiently explored26. Given that NOX enzymes are major sources of ROS in cells, they could be a missing link connecting ethanol-induced oxidative stress to dysregulated iron and lipid metabolism. Setanaxib, a clinical-stage NOX1/4 inhibitor, demonstrated a favorable safety profile in patients with primary biliary cholangitis27. Preclinical studies in a nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) model indicated that Setanaxib attenuates hepatic fibrosis by suppressing collagen deposition and macrophage infiltration28,29. Despite these advances, the therapeutic potential of Setanaxib for ALD remains unexplored, particularly its capacity to simultaneously target iron overload, lipid peroxidation, and inflammation.

Accordingly, we hypothesized that NOX1/4 upregulation plays a critical role in mediating ethanol-induced liver injury and dysfunction. Using a mouse model of chronic-plus-binge ethanol feeding, we investigated the effect of ethanol exposure on hepatic NOX1/4 expression and the therapeutic efficacy of the selective NOX1/4 inhibitor Setanaxib against ethanol-induced hepatocellular injury, iron overload, steatosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation. Our findings provide new insights into ALD pathogenesis and highlight NOX1/4 as a promising therapeutic target.

Animals and Experimental Design

The study procedures were reviewed and formally approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Yunnan Labreal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Approval No. PZ20240902) on September 10, 2024. All experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and were conducted in strict adherence to the institution’s guidelines for the welfare and use of laboratory animals.

Eighteen specific pathogen-free (SPF) male C57BL/6J mice (20 ± 2 g), 6–8 weeks old, were acquired from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Hunan, China), given free access to food and water at a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C, and kept under a 12 h dark/light cycle.

After 1 week of conventional adaptive feeding, the mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (n = 6): pair-fed control (Co) group, ethanol-fed (EtOH) group, and ethanol-fed + Setanaxib (ESET) group. All mice were fed a Lieber-DeCarli control diet for 5 days. From day 6 to day 15, mice in the Co group were fed a control diet, while those in the EtOH and ESET groups were fed a Lieber-DeCarli liquid diet containing 5% ethanol. Meanwhile, mice in the Co and EtOH groups were given normal saline by gavage, while mice in the ESET group were given Setanaxib (60 mg/kg) by gavage30,31. The day following the last feeding, mice in the Co group were given maltose dextrin (5 g/kg) by gavage, and mice in the EtOH and ESET groups were given a large dose of ethanol (5 g/kg) by gavage. The compositions of the Lieber–DeCarli and control liquid diets are presented in Table 1. All the animals had free access to an appropriate diet during the experiment. All treatments were performed at the same time each day.

All the mice were weighed, observed, and recorded daily for 16 days. On the sixteenth day, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with an overdose of 1% pentobarbital sodium (20 µl/g) and then euthanized by cervical dislocation. Serum and liver samples were collected. After weighing the liver, a portion was used to prepare a single-cell suspension for flow cytometry. Another portion of the liver was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and the serum and remaining liver were immediately frozen at − 80 °C for future studies.

HE staining

Liver tissue was fixed with paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. After dewaxing with xylene and ethanol, the slices were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions32. Images were obtained using Holographic Scanning Imaging Equipment (3DHISTECH, Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary) to observe and analyze the pathological changes in liver tissue.

Prussian blue staining

After deparaffinization, 5 μm-thick liver tissue sections were stained with Prussian blue according to the manufacturer’s instructions32. The images were obtained using Holographic Scanning Imaging Equipment (3DHISTECH, Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary) and were used for iron deposition analysis.

Oil Red O Staining

Liver samples were embedded in OCT compound, and frozen tissue sections of 10 μm thickness were prepared. The frozen sections were first placed in a constant-temperature oven at 37 °C for at least 30 min, then soaked in distilled water for 20 min, followed by immersion in 60% isopropanol. Subsequently, they were stained with Oil Red O for 10 min, differentiated in 60% isopropanol, and finally washed with pure water. Images were captured using a Holographic Scanning Imaging Equipment (3DHISTECH, Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and image acquisition

Liver tissues were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene, dehydrated in ethanol, microwaved in sodium citrate buffer, and washed with PBS. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min. Each sample was blocked with 5% goat serum for 60 min and then stained with anti-CD163 antibody (1:60), anti-F4/80 antibody (1:80), and anti-CD68 antibody (1:80) at 4 °C overnight. The next day, after washing three times with PBS, each sample was treated with goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200) for 60 min and washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, the samples were stained with 3–3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and hematoxylin. After dehydration and drying, sections were mounted with neutral gum. Images were obtained using Holographic Scanning Imaging Equipment (3DHISTECH, Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary).

Serum and Hepatic Parameter Measurements

Serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, hepcidin, and hepatic 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), were measured using ELISA kits. Serum levels of ALT, AST, TG, FFA, iron, and the levels of hepatic of TG, FFA, GSH, GSSG, MDA, ferrous (Fe2+), ferric (Fe3+), and total Fe were determined using biochemical kits. All parameters were determined according to the manufacturers’ instructions32.

Flow cytometry detection of liver ROS

Fresh mouse liver tissue was collected, washed once with physiological saline containing 2% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S), and then thoroughly ground. The tissue suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 500 g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with physiological saline and centrifuged at 250 g for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 0.1% DCFH-DA at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min. After washing three times in PBS, the ROS content in the cells was detected using an ACEA Novocyte 2060R flow cytometer32.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from liver tissues using RIPA lysis buffer and quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: NOX1 (1:1000), NOX4 (1:1000), Fpn1 (1:1000), SLC7A11 (1:1000), GPX4 (1:1000), TFR1 (1:1000), HO-1 (1:1000), MCP-1 (1:1000), FTH (1:1000), Prx2 (1:1000), or Nrf2 (1:1000) at 4 °C overnight. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h. After washing with TBST 3 times, the enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) method was used to visualize the protein bands, and images were obtained using an Imaging System (Tanon, Shanghai, China). ImageJ software was used to analyze the grayscale values for each group, with β-actin and Lamin B1 as the internal reference proteins.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR for Liver Tissue

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue using the TRIzol Reagent (Cat# 15596-018; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). cDNA was then synthesized using a FastKing RT Kit (Cat# KR116-02; TIANGEN, Beijing, China) based on the manufacturer’s instructions, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat# Q712-02; Vazyme, Nanjing, China)32. The expression levels of each target gene were normalized to the internal reference gene β-actin using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA; www.graphpad.com) and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. All analyses were conducted with a one-way ANOVA followed by a post hoc Tukey’s test (p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant).

Results

Setanaxib ameliorates ethanol-induced liver injury by inhibiting NOX1/4 activation

To investigate the role of NOX1/4 in ALD and to evaluate the therapeutic potential of its specific inhibitor, Setanaxib, we employed an ethanol-fed mouse model, a well-established system for studying alcohol-induced liver injury33. The results showed that both the mRNA and protein levels of NOX1/4 in the liver of ethanol-fed mice were significantly upregulated (Fig. 1A-F; NOX1/4 mRNA: P < 0.0001; NOX1/4 protein: P < 0.001). This simultaneous increase suggests that ethanol may activate a common upstream signaling pathway that coordinately regulates the expression of both NOX isoforms, such as pathways involving oxidative stress or inflammatory mediators. Concomitantly, mice in the EtOH group exhibited significant liver injury, as evidenced by elevated serum ALT and AST levels (Fig. 1G and H; ALT: P < 0.0001; AST: P < 0.0001) and showed marked hepatocyte ballooning and necrosis (Fig. 1I). However, Setanaxib significantly inhibited ethanol-induced NOX1/4 mRNA and protein expression, reduced the ethanol-induced elevation in serum AST and ALT levels, and attenuated hepatocyte ballooning and necrosis (Fig. 1A-I; NOX1/4 mRNA: P < 0.0001; NOX1 protein: P < 0.05; NOX4 protein: P < 0.0001; ALT: P < 0.0001; AST: P < 0.001). These findings collectively position NOX1/4 activation as a key contributor to ethanol-induced liver injury, while Setanaxib ameliorates such injury by inhibiting NOX1/4 activity and improving histopathological outcomes. Our results corroborate and extend prior evidence implicating NOX1/4 activation in ALD pathogenesis34, thereby solidifying its role as a central therapeutic target.

NOX1/4 inhibition attenuates ethanol-induced iron dysregulation

Iron dysregulation has long been implicated in the progression of liver diseases, particularly in alcohol-induced liver injury11. Our results showed that, compared with the Co group, mice in the EtOH group exhibited significant hepatic iron accumulation, as evidenced by increased Fe2+, Fe3+, and total iron content (Fig. 2A-C; Fe2+: P < 0.001; Fe3+: P < 0.001; total iron: P < 0.001), as well as marked iron deposition in histological analyses (Fig. 2D-E; P < 0.0001), accompanied by upregulation of FTH, a key iron storage protein (Fig. 2F-G; P < 0.0001). Conversely, treatment with Setanaxib, a specific NOX1/4 inhibitor, significantly attenuated these alterations. Setanaxib reduced hepatic Fe2+, Fe3+, and total iron content (Fig. 2A-C; Fe2+: P < 0.001; Fe3+: P < 0.001; total iron: P < 0.001), alleviated iron deposition (Fig. 2D-E; P < 0.0001), and downregulated FTH expression (Fig. 2F-G; P < 0.01). These results demonstrate that NOX1/4 inhibition can ameliorate ethanol-induced hepatic iron overload and counteract iron-mediated hepatotoxicity, potentially by suppressing ferroptosis.

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the ameliorative effect of Setanaxib on ethanol-induced hepatic iron overload, we examined key iron metabolism factors, including hepcidin, FPN, and TFR1. Serum hepcidin levels and hepatic hepcidin gene expression were significantly increased in ethanol-fed mice (Fig. 3A-B; serum hepcidin: P < 0.0001; hepcidin gene: P < 0.0001), accompanied by downregulation of the sole iron exporter, ferroportin (FPN1), at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3C-E; FPN1 mRNA: P < 0.0001; FPN1 protein: P < 0.001). Concurrently, TFR1, which mediates cellular iron uptake, was markedly upregulated in the livers of ethanol-fed mice (Fig. 3F-G; P < 0.001), accompanied by elevated serum iron levels (Fig. 3H; P < 0.0001). However, Setanaxib significantly reversed all the above effects (Fig. 3A-H; serum hepcidin: P < 0.0001; hepcidin gene: P < 0.0001; FPN1 mRNA: P < 0.01; FPN1 protein: P < 0.05; TFR1 protein: P < 0.01; serum iron: P < 0.0001). These results suggest that Setanaxib may restore iron homeostasis by modulating the hepcidin-FPN1 axis and regulating TFR1 expression.

NOX1/4 inhibition counteracts ethanol-induced lipid dysregulation

Next, we evaluated whether Setanaxib ameliorated ethanol-induced lipid dysregulation. Oil Red O staining revealed that, compared with the pair-fed control mice, ethanol-fed mice exhibited increased lipid droplet deposition in the liver, an effect that was significantly attenuated by Setanaxib (Fig. 4A-B; Co vs. EtOH: P < 0.0001; EtOH vs. ESET: P < 0.0001). We further demonstrated that Setanaxib reduced ethanol-mediated upregulation of hepatic TG and FFA levels (Fig. 4C-D; TG: P < 0.0001; FFA: P < 0.0001). Additionally, Setanaxib decreased ethanol-mediated upregulation of serum TG and FFA levels (Fig. 4E-F; TG: P < 0.0001; FFA: P < 0.0001). These results highlight that NOX1/4 plays a critical role in alcohol-related hepatic steatosis and that Setanaxib holds therapeutic potential for restoring lipid homeostasis in alcohol-induced liver injury.

NOX1/4 inhibition alleviates ethanol-induced redox imbalance

To investigate the role of Setanaxib in mitigating oxidative stress, we evaluated its effects on ROS levels, the GSH system, and markers of oxidative damage in ethanol-fed mice. As shown in Fig. 5A, ethanol-fed mice had significantly higher hepatic ROS levels compared with pair-fed control mice (P < 0.0001). Setanaxib treatment inhibited this ethanol-induced ROS production (P < 0.001) and reversed ethanol-induced changes in the glutathione system: it increased GSH levels, decreased GSSG, and restored the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 5B-D; GSH: P < 0.0001; GSSG: P < 0.01; GSH/GSSG: P < 0.05), as well as reduced the levels of oxidative damage markers MDA and 4-HNE in the liver (Fig. 5E-F; MDA: P < 0.0001; 4-HNE: P < 0.0001). These data indicate that NOX1/4 inhibition effectively alleviates ethanol-induced oxidative damage by inhibiting ROS production and restoring the glutathione system.

We also investigated whether Setanaxib could restore the hepatic antioxidant defense system in ethanol-fed mice. This system is critical for mitigating oxidative stress-induced liver injury. Our data demonstrated that ethanol decreased Nrf2 levels and nuclear translocation (Fig. 6A-D; Cytoplasm Nrf2: P < 0.0001; Nucleus Nrf2: P < 0.0001), thereby impairing the activation of antioxidant response genes. Furthermore, ethanol downregulated hepatic HO-1, SLC7A11, and GPX4 protein levels in mice (Fig. 6E-I; HO-1: P < 0.0001; SLC7A11: P < 0.001; GPX4: P < 0.0001). In contrast, the protein level of Prx2, a marker of oxidative stress, was significantly increased in the liver of ethanol-fed mice (Fig. 6J-K; P < 0.0001). However, Setanaxib promoted Nrf2 nuclear translocation and restored the expression of Nrf2, HO-1, SLC7A11, and GPX4, while suppressing Prx2 expression in the liver of ethanol-fed mice (Fig. 6A-K; Cytoplasm Nrf2: P < 0.05; Nucleus Nrf2: P < 0.05; HO-1: P < 0.05; SLC7A11: P < 0.05; GPX4: P < 0.01; Prx2: P < 0.05). These results show that ethanol significantly suppressed the Nrf2 pathway and its downstream antioxidant proteins, effects that were reversed by Setanaxib treatment.

NOX1/4 inhibition suppresses ethanol-induced inflammation

To investigate the role of Setanaxib in suppressing inflammation, we examined its effects on inflammatory markers and macrophage infiltration in ethanol-induced liver injury. We found that ethanol significantly upregulated monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) expression in the liver of mice, and this upregulation was attenuated by Setanaxib (Fig. 7A-B; Co vs. EtOH: P < 0.001; EtOH vs. ESET: P < 0.01). Moreover, large numbers of macrophages, including F4/80⁺, CD163⁺, and CD68⁺ macrophages, infiltrated the damaged liver tissue, an infiltration that was markedly suppressed by Setanaxib (Fig. 7C-E; Co vs. EtOH: F4/80⁺: P < 0.0001, CD163⁺: P < 0.0001, CD68: P < 0.01; EtOH vs. ESET: F4/80⁺: P < 0.0001, CD163⁺: P < 0.0001, CD68: P < 0.05). The ethanol-induced liver injury was accompanied by increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6, which were both significantly reduced by Setanaxib (Fig. 7F-G; Co vs. EtOH: IL-1β: P < 0.0001, IL-6: P < 0.0001; EtOH vs. ESET: IL-1β: P < 0.05, IL-6: P < 0.0001). Serum IL-1β and IL-6 levels were similarly elevated by ethanol and reduced by Setanaxib (Fig. 7H-I; Co vs. EtOH: IL-1β: P < 0.0001, IL-6: P < 0.0001; EtOH vs. ESET: IL-1β: P < 0.0001, IL-6: P < 0.0001). These results demonstrate that Setanaxib effectively suppresses ethanol-induced inflammation by attenuating macrophage infiltration and inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that NOX1/4 signaling could drive hepatic inflammatory responses in ALD.

Discussion

In this study, we found that hepatic NOX1/4 were upregulated in ethanol-fed mice and that pharmacological inhibition with Setanaxib conferred protection against multiple pathological features of ALD, including hepatocellular injury, iron overload, hepatic steatosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation. These observations suggest that NOX1/4 may serve as a central hub that integrates the iron-lipid-oxidative-inflammatory networks that drive ALD progression, thereby highlighting Setanaxib as a promising multidimensional therapeutic candidate.

Setanaxib-mediated attenuation of hepatic iron overload and the concurrent reversal of hepcidin, FPN1, and TFR1 expression raise the possibility that these effects are causally linked to the pharmacological inhibition of NOX1/4. These findings suggest that NOX1/4 may act as an integrative hub in iron dysregulation, coordinately driving both the hepcidin-mediated intracellular retention and the TFR1-mediated cellular iron uptake. This dual mechanism of inhibiting iron export and promoting iron uptake forms a self-reinforcing cycle of iron toxicity, as iron-catalyzed Fenton reactions in turn amplify ethanol-induced ROS generation. The efficacy of Setanaxib in restoring serum and hepatic iron levels, including Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting NOX1/4 to break this cycle. While our data strongly support the existence of this NOX-ROS-hepcidin-FPN1/TFR1 axis, the precise molecular intermediaries (e.g., IL-6/STAT3) remain to be elucidated.

The marked reduction in hepatic steatosis following Setanaxib treatment suggests that NOX1/4-derived oxidative stress may be a key driver of lipid accumulation in ALD. Recent studies have established ROS as a key upstream activator of c-jun N-terminal kinase (JNK). Upon activation, JNK phosphorylates and enhances the transcriptional activity of the lipogenic transcription factor SREBP-1c 35, while concurrently inhibiting PPARα (a key regulator of fatty acid oxidation)36, thereby promoting lipid accumulation37. We therefore propose that the lipid-lowering effect of Setanaxib is attributable, at least in part, to the inhibition of this NOX1/4-driven JNK activation, which warrants direct validation by measuring JNK phosphorylation in future studies. However, it remains unclear whether NOX1/4 physically interacts with these transcription factors or modulates their post-translational modifications (e.g., acetylation and ubiquitination). Future studies employing chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) in NOX1/4-deficient hepatocytes could provide mechanistic insights into these potential interactions.

Nuclear factor E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a master regulator of cellular antioxidant defense, exerting its protective effects through downstream targets, including HO-1, SLC7A11, and GPX438. This pathway is particularly critical in oxidative stress-related diseases such as ferroptosis, as SLC7A11 functions as a cystine/glutamate antiporter whose downregulation directly impairs glutathione synthesis, whereas GPX4 inactivation triggers lipid peroxidation cascades39. Setanaxib appears to exert its protective effects by restoring the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response, suggesting a role for NOX1/4 in promoting lipid peroxidation and potentially ferroptosis in ALD. Notably, lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) represents a key cellular event triggered by various stressors, leading to increased oxidative stress and initiation of multiple programmed cell death pathways beyond ferroptosis, including apoptosis and pyroptosis40. Emerging evidence demonstrates that NOX4, a homolog of NOX1 localized to mitochondria, directly contributes to mitochondrial oxidative stress by inhibiting complex I activity and promoting ROS-induced ROS release41. Given the established crosstalk between lysosomes and mitochondria in amplifying cellular oxidative damage, it is plausible that NOX1/4-derived oxidative stress may contribute to both lysosomal destabilization and mitochondrial dysfunction. Future work could therefore explore whether NOX1/4 inhibition confers protection by stabilizing lysosomal integrity and attenuating mitochondria-derived oxidative stress, thereby breaking this organelle-driven pathogenic cycle.

Monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1/CCL2), a member of the CC chemokine subfamily, mediates the recruitment of monocytes, memory T cells, and dendritic cells to sites of inflammation or injury by binding to its receptor CCR2. These infiltrating cells subsequently differentiate into macrophages42, which can be identified by canonical markers including F4/80, CD68, and CD16343–45. The observed inhibition of MCP-1 expression and macrophage infiltration by Setanaxib highlights the role of NOX1/4 in shaping the hepatic immune microenvironment in ALD. A recent study reported that cyclic helix B peptide alleviates liver fibrosis by attenuating ROS-mediated pyroptosis46. Furthermore, another recent study demonstrated that SIK2-driven mitochondrial stress and subsequent inflammasome activation contribute to tissue injury in spinal cord injury47. These findings collectively suggest that NOX1/4-derived oxidative stress may contribute, at least in part, to inflammasome activation (e.g., NLRP3 or AIM2 inflammasome activation), thereby amplifying inflammatory cascades in ALD. Notably, infiltrating macrophages secrete TNF-α, which inhibits PPARα (via JNK phosphorylation) and stimulates SREBP-1c (via ER stress), thereby exacerbating lipid accumulation48. This interplay suggests the existence of an “inflammation-lipid” axis, wherein macrophage-derived cytokines exacerbate steatosis and enhance oxidative stress49,50, forming a self-perpetuating pathogenic loop.

ALD and alcoholic pancreatic disease (APD) share key pathophysiological features, particularly the involvement of stellate cells in disease progression51. This parallelism prompts consideration of whether Setanaxib’s protective effects in our model derive primarily from reduced hepatocellular injury, from modulation of hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, or from both. Our data support a protective effect on hepatocytes (reduced ALT/AST). Concurrently, evidence from pancreatic stellate cells demonstrates that ethanol augments NOX activity to drive proliferation52, suggesting that HSCs may be targets of NOX1/4 inhibition. We therefore propose that Setanaxib confers protection through alleviating hepatocellular injury while suppressing HSC-mediated fibrogenesis, a premise warranting genetic validation (e.g., cell-type-specific NOX1/4 knockout).

Notably, recent evidence in liver fibrosis models demonstrates that NOX4 deficiency or inhibition not only attenuates liver injury but also restores intestinal microbial diversity and composition53,54. This suggests that NOX4-derived oxidative stress might exacerbate gut dysbiosis, which in turn fuels hepatic inflammation. Indeed, supplementation with Akkermansia muciniphila has been shown to prevent alcoholic liver disease by repairing the intestinal barrier55,56. A similar “gut-first” strategy is recognized in neurological disorders57. While this study focused on hepatic NOX1/4, it is tempting to speculate that Setanaxib may confer additional therapeutic benefits by indirectly modulating the gut-liver axis, potentially by reducing the oxidative burden on the intestinal epithelium or by altering the inflammatory signaling that influences microbial communities. Investigating the effect of Setanaxib on the gut microbiome in ALD models represents a promising avenue for future research.

Limitations and Future Perspectives

This study provides evidence that Setanaxib may exert therapeutic benefits in ALD by targeting NOX1/4-driven oxidative stress, metabolic dysregulation, and inflammation, but several limitations must be considered. The experimental model employed, although well-established, fails to capture the full spectrum of ALD, and our mechanistic dissection of iron metabolism relied primarily on pharmacological inhibitors rather than genetic validation (e.g., cell-type-specific NOX1/4 knockout), which limits mechanistic precision. Additionally, while Setanaxib showed a favorable safety profile in our studies, long-term toxicity and off-target effects in the context of chronic alcohol consumption remain to be evaluated.

Despite these limitations, our findings implicate NOX1/4 as an attractive therapeutic target in ALD, warranting future exploration into combination therapy strategies (e.g., iron chelators or pentoxifylline). The emerging role of ferroptosis also underscores the need to identify reliable biomarkers (e.g., circulating lipid peroxidation products) to stratify patients and develop targeted therapies. Finally, given the overlapping pathogenic mechanisms of ALD with other liver diseases, the established safety profile of Setanaxib provides a rationale for its evaluation in ALD patients with evidence of NOX activation and ferroptotic stress.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our data suggest that NOX1/4 may serve as effective therapeutic targets in ethanol-induced liver injury and support the multifaceted protective effects of Setanaxib, including concurrent amelioration of hepatic steatosis, iron overload, oxidative stress, and inflammation. This pleiotropic action highlights a potential advantage of Setanaxib over single-pathway interventions (e.g., N-acetylcysteine for glutathione replenishment alone), positioning it as a promising multidimensional therapeutic agent for ALD. These findings provide a rationale for further evaluation of Setanaxib in preclinical models and, ultimately, for consideration in future clinical research in patients with ALD.
