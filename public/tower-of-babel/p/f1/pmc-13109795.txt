End-Stage Renal Disease Treatment Choices Model and Use of Home Dialysis and Kidney Transplant
Authors: Meehir N. Dixit, Kalli G. Koukounas, Kelsey M. Drewry, Adam S. Wilk, Yoojin Lee, Rachel E. Patzer, Rajnish Mehrotra, Maricruz Rivera-Hernandez, David J. Meyers, Daeho Kim, Ankur D. Shah, Christopher H. Schmid, Amal N. Trivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study assesses the impact of the End-Stage Renal Disease Treatment Choices model on home dialysis, kidney transplant, and transplant waitlist, as well as measures the rate of financial penalties.

Introduction

Kidney failure, or end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), is a costly, life-threatening condition that affects more than 800 000 individuals in the US.1 Kidney transplant is the preferred treatment and is associated with improved clinical outcomes, yet persistent access barriers have kept transplant rates in the US below those of other high-income countries.1 Without transplant, most patients receive thrice-weekly in-center hemodialysis, a demanding regimen that frequently limits quality of life and life participation due to time burdens and postdialysis symptoms.2 Home dialysis can offer greater flexibility, independence, and shorter recovery time, with similar morbidity and mortality, and is preferred by some patients and clinicians.3,4,5,6,7

As part of the Advancing American Kidney Health initiative aimed at expanding access to home dialysis and kidney transplant,8 the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) launched the End-Stage Renal Disease Treatment Choices (ETC) model on January 1, 2021.4 The ETC model is one of the largest randomized tests of pay-for-performance incentives ever implemented in the US. In a randomly selected 30% of hospital referral regions (HRRs), all dialysis facilities and nephrologists became subject to payment bonuses and penalties based on their patients’ use of home dialysis, transplant, or transplant waitlist. Dialysis facilities and nephrologists exceeding performance benchmarks earned bonuses (up to 6% in 2024), while those below benchmarks faced penalties (up to −7% in 2024).9

The ETC model incorporates several features that distinguish it from prior pay-for-performance initiatives. First, the model’s incentives are substantial, scheduled to reach 8% bonuses and 10% penalties by the model’s end, and apply to all dialysis payments within the traditional Medicare program, a major payer for patients with ESKD in the US.10 Second, participation is mandatory for all dialysis facilities and nephrologists in randomly selected regions.11 Third, facilities are evaluated against both peer and their own historic performance.9 Finally, in 2022, CMS added a health equity incentive to address concerns that pay-for-performance programs disproportionately penalize health care professionals caring for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.11 This incentive adjusts scoring based on a facility’s proportion of patients dually enrolled in Medicare and Medicaid or receiving low-income subsidies.9

Early evaluations of the ETC model found no meaningful improvement in clinical outcomes, though 1 study reported an increase in home dialysis initiation among incident patients in the first year.12,13,14 Our prior work reported that dialysis facilities serving greater proportions of patients with high social risk experienced substantially higher penalty rates in year 1,11 a gradient that diminished after the health equity incentive was introduced.15 Published evaluations have been limited to the model’s first 2 years, leaving uncertain whether the ETC’s financial incentives have influenced home dialysis and transplant use with longer implementation.

In this cross-sectional study, we evaluated the ETC model nearly 4 years after its implementation. We examined its associations with home dialysis, kidney transplant, and transplant waitlist, and assessed the rate of financial penalties, particularly after the introduction of health equity adjustments.

Methods

Study Design/Policy Timeline

We conducted a difference-in-differences (DiD) study evaluating changes in outcomes for traditional Medicare beneficiaries with kidney failure treated in dialysis facilities randomly assigned to the ETC model compared with concurrent changes among patients treated in control facilities. The study used Medicare claims and enrollment data from January 1, 2017 (4 years before model implementation), to September 30, 2024 (3.75 years postimplementation) linked to concurrent transplant data from the United Network for Organ Sharing. We also used CMS-published data on ETC facilities’ performance from 2021 to 2023. Additional details on study methods and the ETC model are in the eAppendix in Supplement 1.

The institutional review board at Brown University approved this study and waived the need for informed consent because the research involved no more than minimal risk to participants. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines were followed.

Study Population and Exposure

We identified all traditional Medicare beneficiaries with kidney failure who received maintenance dialysis at any time during the study period.16,17 Following CMS’s published methodology, we excluded person-months in which beneficiaries lacked both Parts A and B coverage; were enrolled in Medicare Advantage; lived outside of the 50 US states and Washington, DC; were younger than 18 years; received dialysis care in hospice or nursing home settings; had Alzheimer disease or dementia; or received dialysis exclusively for acute kidney injury (excluded patient proportions are shown in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).18,19 These exclusion criteria are intended to provide a best approximation of the population of patients with ESKD for whom home dialysis and kidney transplant are clinically feasible.

The key exposure was receiving dialysis in a facility located in 1 of the 95 HRRs randomly selected for ETC participation20; patients receiving dialysis in facilities located in other HRRs were the control group. For patients visiting multiple dialysis centers within a month, we assigned the HRR of their most frequently used facility, excluding those with a missing HRR.

Measures

The primary outcomes of interest were patient-level home dialysis utilization (both peritoneal and home hemodialysis), kidney transplant (living or deceased donor), and transplant waitlist (active and inactive status), as well as facility-level financial penalization (among ETC-participating facilities). To mirror CMS methods, transplant waitlist and home dialysis utilization were measured for each month of eligibility. Receipt of a kidney transplant was measured only in the month of occurrence (or the month prior to transplant, depending on when dialysis treatment ceased). Stratification using patient characteristics included age, sex, race and ethnicity, and dual eligibility with Medicaid. Stratification using the characteristics of a patient’s facility included prepolicy utilization rate for a given outcome (grouped into terciles) and status as a large-dialysis organization (LDO; defined as any organization with ≥200 dialysis facilities nationwide, generated from annual, publicly available CMS Provider of Services Files).21

A baseline composite social risk score was generated using CMS Medical Evidence Form 2728 data, which includes self-reported demographic data for all patients initiating dialysis from January 1, 2017, to June 30, 2020, to identify facilities in the highest quintile of the proportion of incident patients who were Hispanic or non-Hispanic Black (included as identifiers of socially disadvantaged groups in the US, as well as proxies for exposure to systemic racism), living in a highly disadvantaged neighborhood, or either uninsured or covered by Medicaid at dialysis initiation.19 A facility-level composite social risk score was assigned representing those in the highest quintile of 0, 1, or at least 2 of these characteristics, and was used to stratify ETC-participating facilities when evaluating financial penalization rates.

Secondary patient-level outcomes included 3-month mortality, hospital admissions, home dialysis training, and first occurrence of waitlist. Three-month mortality was calculated excluding the last 3 months of the study period (July to September 2024) to account for data censoring. All outcome measures were calculated at the patient-month level.

Statistical Analysis

We constructed generalized linear models with an identity link function at the patient-month level for each study outcome that included an interaction between the key exposures of treatment in an ETC facility and the postpolicy period. Despite random assignment to model participation, baseline differences in treatment utilization and patient population in the preperiod support our use of DiD methods and further covariate adjustment.22 The models adjusted for age, sex, race and ethnicity, dual Medicare and Medicaid enrollment (hereafter, dual status), reason for Medicare entitlement, zip code–level poverty, college completion, and COVID-19 death rate, and further included month (1-12) and census region fixed effects to reflect seasonal variations in dialysis treatment and the level at which model randomization was stratified. Standard errors were clustered at the level of treatment randomization (HRR; eMethods in Supplement 1).

To identify time-varying effects of the model, we conducted year-by-year DiD evaluations comparing each year’s data separately to the entire prepolicy period. We also conducted event-study evaluations by interacting ETC status and quarter. Additional DiD analyses (eAppendix in Supplement 1) stratified the sample by the following sociodemographic and facility-level characteristics: age (<65 years vs ≥65 years), sex, race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic White), dual status (dual vs nondual), LDO status, and facility’s prepolicy proportion of treatment utilization. Stratification by race and ethnicity was limited to Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic White patients due to the limited sample sizes of other racial cohorts in the available data. Finally, we evaluated the proportion of facilities that received a financial penalty, stratified by social risk score, from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023.

In sensitivity analyses, we assessed preimplementation parallel trends between ETC and control regions,23 changed the policy implementation date to reflect the ETC model announcement (July 2019) and date of HRR assignment (September 2020), and restricted to incident patients, evaluating the use of home dialysis or training in the 3-month period following entry into the Medicare ESRD program (a proxy for dialysis initiation). All analyses were conducted in Stata, version 18 (StataCorp), with a 2-sided P < .05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Primary Outcomes

The proportion of home dialysis utilization increased from 12.8% to 16.7% of attributed patient-months in ETC regions (change of 3.9 percentage points [pp]) and from 13.7% to 17.3% in control regions (change of 3.7 pp), yielding an adjusted DiD of −0.1 pp (95% CI, −0.6 to 0.5 pp). The number of kidney transplants per 1000 patient-months increased from 3.3 to 4.5 in ETC regions (change of 1.2) and from 3.4 to 4.4 in control regions (change of 1.0), resulting in an adjusted DiD of 0.2 pp (95% CI, −0.1 to 0.4 pp). The percentage of patients per month on the transplant waitlist decreased from 16.1% to 15.5% in ETC regions (change of −0.5 pp) and from 17.7% to 16.7% in control regions (change of −1.0 pp). The adjusted DiD for transplant waitlist was 0.6 pp (95% CI, −0.3 to 1.6 pp), though it should be noted that the preperiod trends for this outcome were not parallel (Figure 1, Table 2, and eFigure 2 in Supplement 1).

The proportion of ETC-participating facilities that were penalized at least once between 2021 and 2023 was 43.1%. Facilities with a composite social risk score of 2 or higher (those with highest social risk) were more likely to be penalized in 2021 than those with a score of 0 (18.5% vs 11.5%), a pattern that was reversed in 2022 after the health equity incentive was applied (16.0% vs 20.9%) (Figure 2). However, by 2023, the penalty rates among high social risk facilities were once again on par with other cohorts, at 26.2% compared to 25.4% in the lowest risk category. Across all social risk strata, the proportion of facilities receiving financial penalties increased from 13.8% (2021) to 25.1% (2023).

Secondary Outcomes and Stratified and Sensitivity Analyses

For all secondary outcomes examined (3-month mortality, hospital admissions, billed home dialysis training, and first waitlist entry), changes over time were similar in ETC and control regions, with adjusted DiD estimates that were small and not statistically significant (eTable 3 and eFigure 3 in Supplement 1). Stratified analyses by year and by dialysis facility characteristics, including LDO status and baseline use of home dialysis, also showed no consistent ETC effects (Table 2 and eFigures 4-7 and eTables 4 and 5 in Supplement 1).

Among patient subgroups, dual beneficiaries exhibited a small but meaningful increase in home dialysis use in ETC regions relative to control regions by 2024 (0.9 pp; 95% CI, 0.1-1.7 pp), and incident patients (eTable 6 in Supplement 1) had an increase in home dialysis initiation (adjusted DiD, 1.1 pp; 95% CI, 0.1-2.1 pp). Other subgroup effects, including those observed in Hispanic beneficiaries in 2021, were not sustained or not statistically significant (Figure 3 and eTables 7-9 in Supplement 1).

Event study plots (eFigures 2, 5, and 7-9 in Supplement 1) supported the preperiod parallel trends assumption across most outcomes and subgroups, except for transplant waitlist, which showed that rates increased at a greater rate in ETC areas compared to control areas prior to the implementation of the model. The main study results were robust to an adjusted prepolicy end date of either model announcement in July 2019 or HRR randomization in September 2020 (eTable 10 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

After nearly 4 years of implementation, the ETC model was not associated with increases in the home dialysis use, kidney transplant, or transplant waitlist, even as an increasing proportion of ETC facilities incurred financial penalties. Secondary and subgroup analyses, including stratifications by patient characteristics, facility attributes, and prepolicy treatment patterns, were consistent with the main findings. We observed an increase in home dialysis use among dual Medicare-Medicaid beneficiaries in the final year of the model and among incident patients, but these effects were modest and should be interpreted with caution due to multiple subgroup analyses.

The share of ETC facilities receiving penalties increased steadily over the study period, likely reflecting the model’s escalating performance thresholds.16 Prior work has shown that facilities serving patients with the highest social risk were disproportionately penalized in the model’s first year. Although the health equity incentive introduced in 2022 initially reversed this gap,15 penalty rates converged across facility cohorts by 2023. Over the entire study period, more than 40% of facilities were penalized at least once.

This study extends earlier ETC evaluations in several important ways. First, it examines nearly the full duration of the model’s shortened time frame (January 2021 to December 2025). Notably, in the first month following the study period (October 2024), Hurricane Helene disrupted the production and distribution of peritoneal dialysis supplies, which is likely to complicate future analyses of home dialysis trends and assessments of the ETC model.24 Second, the study includes a broad set of facility-level and patient-level subgroups. Third, by evaluating both clinical outcomes and financial penalties, we offer a comprehensive assessment of model performance. Together, these findings reinforce that the ETC model did not have a meaningful effect on its targeted outcomes even after prolonged implementation, while imposing widespread and increasing financial penalties.

Several factors may explain the model’s limited effectiveness. Patient-level and structural barriers to home dialysis, such as out-of-pocket costs for utilities and home modifications, limited space for equipment, inadequate caregiver support, and limited availability of training services and personnel, are unlikely to be overcome with payment incentives directed at dialysis facilities and nephrologists.25,26 National increases in home dialysis and transplant use during the study period likely reflect preexisting momentum rather than differential effects of the ETC model. The kidney transplant process remains complex and lengthy, and access is constrained by the availability of organs. These structural factors that contribute to the length of the kidney transplant process are unlikely to shift rapidly in response to dialysis facility incentives, particularly as transplant centers themselves received no direct incentives in the ETC model.27,28 The highly consolidated dialysis market may also blunt the impact of regionally targeted interventions if LDOs adopt uniform (cross-regional) national strategies,29,30,31 though comparisons of outcomes across LDO and non-LDO facilities in this study did not show statistically significant difference in model performance. Concurrent changes, including the introduction of alternative payment models in kidney disease, like the Comprehensive Kidney Care Contracting model, the expansion of Medicare Advantage enrollment among patients with ESKD, changes in the organ procurement organization and the kidney allocation system, and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, may have also diluted the ETC model’s impact.32 Finally, the ETC’s escalating penalties may have further strained facilities financially, reducing their ability to improve model performance.

Although the ETC model was originally set to run through 2027, CMS announced its early termination in March 2025, with an end date of December 31, 2025.33 Key design features of the ETC, including randomization, mandatory participation, incentives for both absolute performance and improvement, and equity adjustments, reflect strategies that have been adopted or proposed in other value-based payment programs. Although the ETC model did not appear to achieve its intended effects, its randomized design enabled rigorous evaluation. The absence of measurable improvements, despite substantial and increasing financial penalties, aligns with prior pay-for-performance evaluations that have reported limited or no gains in quality of care.34,35,36 It remains uncertain whether these findings reflect limitations of pay-for-performance incentives themselves, or features of the design and implementation of these models.34,35,36 As CMS launches new kidney-focused payment models, including the Increasing Organ Transplant Access model, these findings underscore the importance of pairing financial incentives with structural and system-level supports to facilitate access to care for persons with ESKD.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, we could only observe outcomes among beneficiaries in traditional Medicare, limiting our ability to assess potential spillover effects to those with other sources of insurance coverage. Second, despite the large national sample, we are not powered to observe effects in certain patient subcohorts. Third, the concurrent implementation of the 21st Century Cures Act in 2021 substantially increased Medicare Advantage enrollment among patients with ESKD and therefore reduced the population eligible for the ETC model. Of note, enrollment in Medicare Advantage has not been found to differ in ETC and control areas.14 Finally, this study relied on administrative claims and transplant registry data, which are collected for payment and reporting rather than research. However, these data sources are the standard approach for measuring home dialysis, transplant, and waitlist, and any misclassification is unlikely to differ systematically between ETC and control regions.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study with DiD analysis of nearly 800 000 traditional Medicare beneficiaries with kidney failure, treatment at facilities randomly assigned to pay-for-performance incentives was not associated with increased use of home dialysis, kidney transplant, or transplant waitlist, while financial penalties became increasingly common. Future value-based payment models may need to move beyond narrowly targeted financial incentives to address the broader structural and patient-level barriers that influence access to complex specialty care.
