How do I implement a whole blood program with low blood wastage?
Authors: Denise Bäckström, Malin Jonsson Fagerlund, Stella Larsson, Aseel Alshamari, Ragnar Henningsson, Karin Holst, Anna‐Maria Harstad, Norbert Lubenow, José‐Gabriel Sato Folatre, Pia Remneberg Carlbom, Agneta Wikman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of low‐titer O whole blood (LTOWB) is requested in the treatment of major bleeding, initially used in military medicine but now increasingly utilized in civilian prehospital care. The advantage is the administration of a balanced transfusion, red blood cells, coagulation factors, and platelets, in one bag. The challenges are the availability of LTOWB and difficulties in predicting the need in major bleeding, leading to the risk of wastage.MethodsThis study describes different logistical strategies when implementing whole blood in the Swedish civilian healthcare. The five transfusion centers producing whole blood in Sweden participated, providing experience of the production line, usage, and wastage.ResultsIn Sweden, LTOWB is used prehospital in helicopter emergency medical service (HEMS), in one physician‐manned rapid response vehicle, and inhospital in three University Hospitals. The logistical strategies to reduce wastage vary but involve the rotation of LTOWB not used prehospital to inhospital use in two centers and the preparation of red blood cell (RBC) units from 1 to 2 weeks old LTOWB in three centers. The number of transfused LTOWB units varies between the centers, and wastage was 0%–13% in 4/5 centers and higher in one center, 34%.ConclusionIt is difficult to predict the need of LTOWB, requested in prehospital emergencies. Aiming for low wastage requires different logistical chains, depending on the local prerequisites. In Sweden, LTOWB is either rotated for use in major bleeding in hospital or prepared to RBC units after 1 week prehospital.Keywords: blood center operations, blood component preparations, blood management

BACKGROUND

The revival of the use of whole blood in the treatment of major bleeding originates from military medicine driven by the critical needs in battlefield conditions. Many studies, initially from military settings but more recently also from civilian health care, have suggested advantages of whole blood (WB) over traditional resuscitation with blood components for bleeding patients in both survival and need for blood products. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 The advantages appear evident in prehospital settings. Norway has been at the forefront among Western countries in the integration of whole blood into routine operations in civilian health care, prehospital in rescue helicopters as well as in hospital in massive bleeding. 5 , 6 A contributing factor to this success has been the close collaboration between civilian and military healthcare systems, which has facilitated the sharing of best practices and innovations in blood transfusion protocols. The adoption of whole blood transfusion practices has been systematically evaluated and implemented to improve outcomes in severely injured patients, where rapid blood replacement is critical. 5 , 6

The adoption of whole blood transfusion in civilian healthcare has expanded to many countries with implementation of this practice into their emergency medical services. 3 , 7 , 8 , 9 This trend reflects a growing recognition of the benefits whole blood offers in managing severe trauma and hemorrhagic shock.

There are obvious logistical advantages in urgent bleeding situations with whole blood compared with blood components, offering an “all in one” option. The concentration of red cells, platelets and coagulation factors are higher than in separated blood components diluted in additive solutions and it is also the only possibility to provide platelets prehospital. 10 But whole blood results in challenges for the blood bank, adding a new production line with the requirement of low‐titer blood group O whole blood (LTOWB) and risk of wastage. 11 , 12 , 13

The purpose of this article is to describe and discuss the various methods used in hospitals in Sweden to provide whole blood pre‐ and in‐hospital aiming for low wastage.

METHODS

Ethics

Ethical approval is not required for describing methods and standard operating procedures. Individual patient records have not been utilized.

This article provides a description of the different strategies in Sweden for the production and logistics of whole blood, used prehospital and inhospital. It also includes the indications for transfusion of whole blood and the number of units produced, administered, and wasted. The five transfusion centers producing whole blood today in Sweden participated.

Demography

Sweden is the largest country in Northern Europe, covering an area of more than 450,000 square kilometers and housing a population of 10.5 million, with large sparsely populated areas in the north. The healthcare system is divided into 21 autonomous regions, based on national regulations, featuring ~45 emergency hospitals. The availability of blood products is limited in prehospital settings and is primarily sourced in physician‐staffed prehospital units. There are about nine physician‐staffed prehospital units, the majority of which utilize helicopters and operate across larger areas that span multiple healthcare regions. All physician‐staffed units have access to blood products, either as LTOWB or red blood cell (RBC) units and freeze‐dried plasma (FDP) or thawed fresh frozen plasma (FFP). Out of the nine physician‐staffed prehospital units, four carry LTOWB. Prehospital physicians at these units are primarily specialists in anesthesiology and intensive care, and they also work in hospitals.

Transfusion Medicine, including donation centers, is part of Laboratory Medicine in the hospitals, in the 21 regions. The regions are autonomous and have different organizations, equipment, and procedures. LTOWB and blood components are prepared in the university and regional hospitals.

Five regions in Sweden produce and utilize LTOWB. This includes the two largest regions, which support populations of 2.4 million and 1.7 million, respectively, along with three medium‐sized regions. The introduction of LTOWB varied across these regions, the first in 2019 and the most recent in 2023, with applications in prehospital environments, inhospital, or both, as detailed in Table 1. Prehospital use involves physician‐staffed teams, including three HEMS teams 14 and one rapid response vehicle unit. 15 Inhospital, LTOWB is administered at four university hospitals.

Production

In all five regions, LTOWB is collected from male blood group O donors. Two regions utilize, in addition, female donors screened negative for HLA antibodies. One region exclusively uses RhD negative blood donors, while the other four regions employ both RhD positive and RhD negative blood. All centers only use donors negative for the Kell antigen. Titer tests are conducted using the Ortho Vision platform (Quidel Ortho Corp, San Diego, CA) in three centers and manually with the BioRad gel technique (BioRad Corp, CA) in two centers. The threshold for low titer IgM anti‐A and anti‐B is set at <250–256 across all centers, with one center also requiring a titer of IgG anti‐A and anti‐B <500. The platelet‐sparing, leukocyte‐depleting filter IMUFLEX® (Terumo Blood and Cell Technologies, Lakewood, CO) is utilized in all centers (Table 2).

Logistics and follow‐up

In Region 1, introducing LTOWB at the HEMS from 2019, the screened donors are called on a regular schedule to ensure availability. A total of six units of LTOWB are produced each week, and four units are collected and transported to the HEMS base. Two units are stored at the blood bank as back‐up.

The base units are split in two cooling boxes (Crēdo Cube™, Peli Biothermal EMEA, UK) with a temperature monitoring unit (Boomerang, ICU Scandinavia AB, SE); one box goes in the helicopter, and the other one is stored in a temperature‐controlled refrigerator (BioCompact II 210, Gram Scientific ApS, DK) at +2°C to +6°C, remotely controlled by the blood bank. The units are rotated every 12 h to guarantee that blood units are available on all missions and to ensure sufficient cooling. If a unit surpasses +14°C, the unit is discarded upon return to the blood bank, and ≥+10°C shortens its shelf life.

The unused base units are returned to the blood bank after 7 days and prepared into RBC units that are reinstated into the hospitals blood stock. The plasma is discarded.

In Region 2, which started in 2019, four units of LTOWB are produced each week and the helicopter carries two units, which are available for prehospital transfusion. If not used, these are changed regularly with the other two units stored temperature‐controlled in Crēdo ProMed portable at the helicopter base. The quality of the LTOWB is checked at production and control for hemolysis at return after 1 week, according to quality criteria (EDQM) (17). Red blood cell units are prepared and plasma is discarded.

In Region 3, with start 2022, 3–6 units of LTOWB are produced each week. One unit of LTOWB is stored prehospital in a Crēdo ProMed portable thermoregulated bag validated for 72 h, which is exchanged on fixed days, four times a week. A temperature monitoring logger transported in a box is always read before the blood is accepted for return. Once the blood has been utilized, it is replaced with a new unit of LTOWB at the transfusion center, regardless of the time of day. If unused, the unit is returned after 1 week and used as emergency blood at the trauma center. One unit is always stored at the blood bank for immediate replacement if the prehospital unit is transfused and 2–4 units are stored in the satellite fridge at the trauma center, where LTOWB can be used for up to 21 days. Quality controls are done at production according to requirements of hemoglobin, platelet, and leukocyte counts and hemolysis measurement. 16 Expired non‐transfused units returned are analyzed for the same parameters. From start, a validation which included levels of potassium, glucose, pH, lactate, and platelet function was done.

In Region 4, which started in 2023, 4 units of LTOWB are produced and delivered to the ambulance helicopter weekly and returned to the university hospital blood bank after 1 week, where they can be used in hospital in major bleeding. A temperature monitoring logger transported in the container is always read before the blood is accepted for return. In case of use in the helicopter, these are replaced by LTOWB units produced at the earliest possible opportunity, typically the following working day. The units can be stored for 21 days (first week for prehospital use, last 2 weeks for inhospital use at the university hospital). Parameters in the random sampling quality control program are hemoglobin, platelet count, leukocyte count, and hemolysis at the end of storage life (17).

Finally, in Region 5 which started in 2023, LTOWB is stored in a fridge in the emergency department for a maximum duration of 2 weeks. One RhD positive and two RhD negative units of LTOWB are produced every 14 days. During this period, they remain available for urgent transfusion needs. If the whole blood is not utilized within this timeframe, it is returned to the blood center. At the blood center, the whole blood is processed into RBC units, which can then be used for up to 35 days from the date of collection. The plasma is discarded.

Quality control for whole blood includes a volume of 400–500 mL, Hb >43 g/unit, platelets >50 × 109/unit, leukocytes <1 × 106/unit, and hemolysis control, according to quality criteria. 16 In addition, the following patient tests are done in Region 5 as follow‐up day 0 and 1: hemolysis (LD, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocytes), blood counts, fibrinogen, antithrombin, potassium, Na, Ca, creatinine, and direct antiglobulin test.

INDICATION FOR TRANSFUSION OF WHOLE BLOOD

In the prehospital setting, a restrictive approach to blood transfusions is applied, administered only to patients with life‐threatening bleeding. Only patients assessed as having life‐threatening bleeding are transfused prehospitally. The indications for blood transfusions are consistent with those in hospital; a physician prescribes a transfusion if deemed necessary. Specific blood pressure or pulse thresholds are not applied as criteria for transfusion. To avoid delaying transport to the hospital, transfusions are primarily performed during transport. In cases of traumatic cardiac arrest, blood is not the initial fluid used due to its longer preparation and transfusion time compared with clear fluids. This is because blood warmers are required for LTOWB, clear fluids are stored warm; hence, they do not need warmers, and blood has a higher viscosity than clear fluids. The MEQU blood warmer is always used. Tranexamic acid and calcium gluconate are given as adjuncts to the transfusion of LTOWB.

The indication for transfusing LTOWB at the trauma unit or emergency operation theater mirrors that for using O‐negative emergency RBCs, that is, major ongoing hemorrhage or suspected hemorrhagic shock requiring urgent transfusion when there is no time to wait for blood typing and erythrocyte antibody screening. While specific vital parameters or laboratory values are not strictly required for the decision, they are considered alongside the overall clinical picture. If the prehospital trauma alert signals major hemorrhage, blood units are transferred from the refrigerator to a temperature‐controlled carry‐on bag and brought to the trauma room in preparation for a massive transfusion. The Belmont or Fluido blood warmer is utilized. In three regions prehospital O RhD positive LTOWB can be administered to all patients in emergencies, including young females.

STRATEGIES

There are primarily two different logistics used: preparing RBC units, from the LTOWB after 1–2 weeks or rotating LTOWB from prehospital locations with low usage to inhospital departments with high usage. The former strategy is applied by three of the centers and results in very low wastage of the LTOWB units, ranging from 0% to 10%. The quality criteria for the converted RBCs are reported as acceptable according to quality criteria. The rotation of whole blood to inhospital users requires monitoring and is effective in high‐volume settings such as trauma centers, in region 3 with wastage at 13%. However, it is more challenging in the context of use in perioperative major bleeding applied in region 4, where wastage is 34% (Table 3).

WASTAGE

The most common reason for the disposal of LTOWB was expiry (n = 45, 1.7%). The second most common cause was improper handling (n = 10, 0.4%), followed by hemolysis/lipemic (n = 9, 0.3%), technical issues with bags or instruments (n = 8, 0.3%), and unknown reason for disposal (n = 12, 0.5%).

TRANSFUSION REACTIONS

This paper did not include an in‐depth analysis of transfusion reactions. One reported transfusion complication involved a patient where elevated potassium levels were recorded following the transfusion of two units of whole blood; no further analysis was made for this paper regarding this report.

DISCUSSION

There is an increasing demand for whole blood in the management of bleeding, but due to difficulties to predict the need and the risk of wastage, there is a certain resistance in the blood banks. In this article, we aimed to describe different strategies to implement LTOWB in the blood supply chain. We report various approaches designed to minimize wastage, even with relatively low utilization rates of whole blood. In large hospitals with trauma and thoracic departments with many major bleedings, LTOWB may be used inhospital; in smaller hospitals, this might not be an option. The possibility of preparing RBCs from the LTOWB, with preserved quality, reduced wastage to a minimum.

Despite Sweden's modest size, the diversity in its healthcare infrastructure allows for different strategies of blood management practices to adapt to the specific needs of different hospitals and regions. The diversity is mainly due to the uneven population distribution across the country, with most people concentrated in major urban areas. A similar pattern can be observed in other countries as well. This ensures that each region can implement tailored solutions that optimize the use of LTOWB, according to their respective prehospital and inhospital requirements.

In Sweden, primarily two different systems are applied for managing LTOWB: rotating LTOWB units from facilities prehospital with lower usage to those inhospital with higher demand, or converting whole blood into PRBC.

The availability blood of blood type O RhD negative is always limited; in Sweden, 6% of the blood donors have blood type O RhD negative. 17 In Sweden, the transfusion of RhD positive blood to men and post‐fertile women in life‐threatening bleeding is supported but restricted for women of childbearing age. This has resulted in different policies between the regions regarding the transfusion of RhD positive LTOWB in life‐threatening situations. In some regions, RhD positive blood is not given to women; in others, it is not given to women of childbearing age, and in some regions, it is given to both men and women without any restrictions.

Sweden has a decentralized healthcare management system where the 21 different regions possess significant autonomy. This can be seen as an advantage when examining the various methods developed for managing LTOWB in Sweden. However, there are disadvantages; in this case, the lack of national guidelines results in diverse practices associated with the use of LTOWB.

The primary reason for discarding LTOWB was units' expiration, an occurrence that may be reduced in a mature system despite the difficulties to predict the need in urgent bleeding situations. The second most common reason for discarding was improper handling, which can always occur but should be reduced through proper education and training. Other reasons for discarding, such as hemolysis and technical issues with bags and instruments, must be considered as normal deviations in production and transfusion operations.

With LTOWB, an early balanced transfusion including platelets can be administered, but there are only a few centers providing LTOWB in Sweden. The main challenges are difficulties predicting the need and risk of wastage. There is also limited availability of prehospital blood components such as RBCs and plasma. Many studies today support prehospital administration of blood products in major bleeding, especially with long transport time. 4 , 10

On‐going conflicts have put focus on critical preparedness in the health care, notably in the context of ensuring the availability of vital medical supplies such as blood products. The supply of LTOWB is requested from military forces, due to both logistical and medical advantages. One blood unit contains all blood cells and coagulation factors. Enhancing the capabilities of blood centers to produce and manage LTOWB effectively, with low wastage, would be important both for civilian and military emergency care.

CONCLUSIONS

Despite the low production and use of LTOWB, a permissive attitude towards different logistic strategies can increase the availability and minimize the wastage of LTOWB. Information and education are likely to reduce the most common causes of discarding, such as expiry and improper handling, ensuring that routine practices are well understood and optimally performed.
