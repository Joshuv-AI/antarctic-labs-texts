Diagnostic utility of cardiac magnetic resonance-derived myocardial strain and fractal analysis in differentiating left ventricular noncompaction from dilated cardiomyopathy
Authors: Yanan Jin, Yong Zhang, Jingliang Cheng, Xiaoning Shao, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic value of combining cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) myocardial strain analysis with fractal analysis in differentiating left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC) from dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM).MethodsRetrospective CMR data were evaluated for 25 patients diagnosed with DCM, 27 patients diagnosed with LVNC, and 31 healthy control participants. Myocardial strain parameters were obtained using CVI42 software, while trabeculae of the left ventricle was analyzed via fractal analysis using FracAnalyse V1.2. Additionally, standard left ventricular functional parameters were calculated. Differences among the three groups were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with pairwise comparisons conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. Diagnostic efficacy of the parameters was assessed utilizing receiver operating characteristic curve analysis (MedCalc version 15.2.2).ResultsGPRS, GPCS, and GPLS exhibited statistically significant differences among the LVNC, DCM, and control groups (p < 0.05). GPRS, the absolute value of GPCS, and GPLS of DCM were significantly lower than LVNC group. The global FD, apical mean FD, apical maximum FD, basal mean FD, and basal maximum FD were significantly different among the LVNC, DCM, and control groups (p < 0.05). The global FD, apical mean FD, apical maximum FD, basal mean FD and basal maximum FD of LVNC were significantly higher than DCM group (p < 0.05). GPLS, GPCS, Global FD and apical maximum FD demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy in differentiating LVNC from DCM, with areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) of 0.928, 0.896, 0.901 and 0.893, respectively. The AUC showed that the diagnostic efficacy of GPLS combinate Global FD was higher than that of Global FD, but there was no statistically significant difference compared to GPLS and the combination of GPLS and apical maximum FD.ConclusionThe integration of myocardial strain assessment with fractal analysis of trabecular morphology using CMR demonstrates significant utility in differentiating LVNC from DCM, offering improved diagnostic precision through evaluation of both myocardial deformation and trabecular complexity.

Background

Left ventricular noncompaction (LVNC) and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) are two commonly encountered cardiomyopathies associated with increased trabeculation within the myocardium. The clinical manifestations and demographics of both conditions are nonspecific, with disease progression advancing from asymptomatic stages to overt heart failure. These disorders may manifest across a wide age spectrum, from childhood through to advanced age. LVNC is characterized by the presence of a spongiform myocardial structure resulting from an arrest in the compaction process during embryonic development, leading to prominent trabeculae and deep intertrabecular recesses that communicate with the ventricular cavity [1]. Conversely, DCM is defined as a congestive cardiomyopathy associated with systolic and diastolic dysfunction of the left ventricle. Diagnosis requires the exclusion of myocardial dysfunction secondary due to abnormal load conditions or ischemic etiologies, including hypertension and valvular heart disease [2].

In advanced stages, LVNC may exhibit myocardial dysfunction and left ventricular dilation, which can mimic the clinical and imaging features of DCM, thus complicating differential diagnosis. The present study aimed to characterize myocardial deformation in patients diagnosed with LVNC and DCM using a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based myocardial tissue tracking technique utilizing steady-state free precession sequences. Additionally, trabecular complexity was quantitatively assessed through fractal analysis to provide supplementary diagnostic insights and refine the differentiation between these two cardiomyopathies.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

All participants with DCM and LVNC were enrolled who underwent 3.0 T MRI from January 2023 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria for LVNC were as follows: (1) presence of a two-layered myocardium composed of a thin compacted epicardial layer and a thickened non-compacted endocardial layer with prominent trabeculations and deep intertrabecular recesses; (2) communication of interventricular recesses with the ventricular cavity; and (3) a non-compacted to compacted myocardium ratio ≥ 2.3 measured at end-diastole on short-axis cine MRI images of the left ventricle [3].

The inclusion criteria for DCM were: (1) left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 45%; (2) left ventricular end-diastolic diameter > 55 mm (males) or > 50 mm (females); and (3) presence of myocardial hypertrabeculation.

Exclusion criteria applicable to all groups included: (1) presence of concomitant cardiac conditions, including other forms of cardiomyopathy, significant coronary artery disease (> 50% stenosis on coronary angiography or computed tomography angiography), and valvular disease; (2) severe arrhythmias that precluded MRI examination or compromised image quality; and (3) the presence of MRI-incompatible implants or devices, such as cardiac pacemakers or other metallic foreign bodies. The clinical indications for the CMR examination in the patient groups (LVNC and DCM) included the assessment of suspected cardiomyopathy based on symptoms (e.g., heart failure, arrhythmia, chest pain, or dyspnea), abnormal findings on prior tests such as echocardiography (e.g., ventricular dilation, or systolic dysfunction), or family history of cardiomyopathy.

A total of 64 participants with DCM and LVNC underwent 3.0T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). 4 cases were excluded due to poor image quality caused by arrhythmia, 3 cases were excluded because claustrophobia prevented the completion of the examination, and 5 cases were excluded due to combined with ischemic heart disease. Finally, this study included 27 patients diagnosed with LVNC, and 25 patients diagnosed with DCM. A control group comprising 31 age and sex matched healthy volunteers were also enrolled for comparative analysis. The flowchart of the study was shown in the Fig. 1.

The study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024-KY-2314-001), written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study.

Instruments and methods

Cardiac MRI was conducted using a Siemens Skyra 3.0T scanner equipped with an 18-channel surface phased-array coil. Electrocardiographic gating was employed for scan synchronization. All cine images were acquired during end-expiratory breath-hold using a standardized balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) sequence, without the use of compressed sensing or other undersampling techniques. The CMR protocol was identical for all participants and included the following sequences: Cine images were acquired utilizing balanced steady-state free precession sequences across multiple standard views, including four-chamber, left ventricular two-chamber, left ventricular three-chamber, as well as contiguous short-axis views of the left ventricle. The imaging parameters were as follows: TR 35.5 to 42.4 ms, TE 1.1 to 1.4 ms, flip angle 45° to 55°, FOV 290 mm × 290 mm to 350 mm × 350 mm, and slice thickness 6–8 mm, pixel size 1.8 mm× 1.8 mm. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images of LV two-chamber, three-chamber, four-chamber and short-axis were acquired after the intravenous administration of Gd-DTPA contrast agent (Magnevist; Kang Chen Pharmaceutical, Guangzhou, China) at a dose of 0.2 mmol/kg of body weight and at a rate of 2.0 ml/s using a phase sensitive inversion recovery sequence (PSIR) with the following parameters: TR 5.2ms, TE 1.9ms, flip angle 55°, FOV 360 mm × 360 mm; slice thickness 8 mm; pixel size 1.5 mm× 1.5 mm. Native T1 mapping or other parametric mapping sequences were not part of the study protocol.

MRI image post-processing

All images were exported in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine format and analyzed using CVI42 software (version 5.14.2).

Left Ventricular Functional Assessment: Endocardial and epicardial contours of the left ventricle at end-diastole and end-systole were delineated in short-axis cine images using automated or semi-automated techniques. In accordance with SCMR guidelines and to ensure reproducibility, papillary muscles and trabeculae were excluded from the blood pool and included as part of the left ventricular myocardial mass for all volumetric and functional analyses. The software generated standard left ventricular functional metrics, including ejection fraction (EF), end-diastolic volume (EDV), end-systolic volume (ESV), stroke volume (SV), and cardiac output (CO).

Using cine images in both short- and long-axis orientations, endocardial and epicardial boundaries at end-diastole and end-systole were identified and outlined via the software’s artificial intelligence algorithms and subsequently refined manually on a slice-by-slice basis. The same contouring principle (exclusion of papillary muscles and trabeculae from the cavity) was applied for consistency across all analyses. The software then automatically calculated myocardial strain parameters, including global peak radial strain (GPRS), global peak circumferential strain (GPCS), and global peak longitudinal strain (GPLS). The thickness of non-compacted myocardium and compacted myocardium was measured at end-diastole in the short-axis cine sequence, followed by further calculation of the NC/C ratio.

At end-diastole, left ventricular short-axis cine images were analyzed using FracAnalyse v1.2 which was accomplished using the Matlab software(MathWorks, Natick, Mass) with free online customized codes. The software operates by applying a box-counting algorithm to the binarized trabecular structure of the left ventricle, thereby quantifying the fractal dimension (FD) as a measure of trabecular complexity. Manual delineation of the endocardial and epicardial contours was performed, again following the same principle of excluding papillary muscles from the cavity, after which the software automatically computed the fractal dimension (FD). Global FD was defined as the average FD from the base to the apex of the left ventricle (Fig. 2). The left ventricle was segmented into basal and apical regions, and for each region, the maximum and mean FD values were automatically calculated across all cine sequences. This methodological approach for fractal analysis has been previously validated and applied in cardiovascular imaging studies, including those published in Radiology [4].

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 21.0). The Kruskal-Wallis test was applied to assess differences in parameter values among the control group, LVNC group, and DCM group, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. For subsequent pairwise comparisons between groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was utilized and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using MedCalc software (version 15.2.2) to evaluate the diagnostic performance of each parameter in distinguishing LVNC from DCM. Using the Delong test to compare the ROC area under the curve for each parameter and composite parameters. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were used to assess the intra- an inter-observer reproducibility of LV global strains and FD. ICCs were defined as excellent (ICC ≥ 0.75), good (ICC = 0.60–0.74), moderate (ICC = 0.40–0.59), or poor (ICC ≤ 0.39).

Diagnostic efficacy of myocardial strain and fractal analysis parameters in differentiating LVNC

GPLS, GPCS, Global FD and apical maximum FD demonstrated high diagnostic efficacy in differentiating LVNC from DCM, with areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) of 0.928, 0.896, 0.901 and 0.893, respectively. Notably, the combination of GPLS and Global FD yielded an AUC of 0.966, with a sensitivity of 96.3% and a specificity of 84%. The combination of GPLS and apical maximun FD yielded an AUC of 0.950, with a sensitivity of 85.19% and a specificity of 96.00%. These findings are summarized in Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 8. Comparing the ROC areas under the curve showed that the diagnostic efficacy of GPLS combinate Global FD was higher than that of Global FD, but there was no statistically significant difference compared to GPLS and the combination of GPLS and apical maximum FD.

Discussion

LVNC is characterized by the presence of a distinct two-layered myocardial structure, consisting of a noncompacted inner layer and a compacted outer layer. The noncompacted myocardium typically exhibits a honeycomb-like pattern, and as the disease progresses, left ventricular function may become increasingly impaired. In contrast, DCM primarily results from myocardial fibrosis secondary to myocardial injury. This pathological process leads to junctional dysfunction and the transformation of myocardial cells into a trabecula-like reticular formation. This remodeling increases the endocardial surface area in an effort to compensate for declining cardiac output [5].

Although the morphological features of non-compacted myocardium LVNC and hypertrabeculation in DCM share similarities, important differences exist. In the present study, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)-derived myocardial strain analysis was employed to quantitatively assess the deformation characteristics of both conditions. Additionally, fractal analysis was conducted to evaluate trabecular complexity, providing further quantitative information to differentiate between the two conditions.

Myocardial strain analysis utilizing CMR feature tracking allows for the quantitative evaluation of global and regional myocardial motion and deformation from long- and short-axis cine sequences [6]. The three principal components of myocardial strain—radial, circumferential, and longitudinal—represent deformation in specific orientations: radial strain reflects myocardial thickening during systole; circumferential strain describes rotational shortening; and longitudinal strain reflects base-to-apex elongation and shortening [7].

In this study, the GPRS, GPCS and GPLS in patients with LVNC was significantly reduced compared to controls. Radial strain measures the degree of myocardial thickening between end-diastole and end-systole. In LVNC, the thinning of the compacted myocardial layer leads to impaired end-systolic thickening and a decrease in radial strain. These findings are consistent with previous study results. For instance, Zheng et al. concluded that GPRS, GPCS, and GPLS in LVNC were significantly lower than those in the control group [8]. In a study by Ochs et al., a strong correlation was found between GPCS, GPLS, and LVEF [9]. The relatively lower LVEF in the LVNC group in the current study may explain the lower absolute value of GPCS and GPLS.

In patients with DCM, GPRS, GPCS and GPLS were all significantly lower compared to the control group. DCM is characterized by left ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction, which manifest as myocardial thinning, impaired systolic thickening, and reduced myocardial segmental shortening. These structural and functional changes contribute to the marked reductions in all myocardial strain components, reflecting global systolic and diastolic dysfunction. Notably, GPRS, GPCS, and GPLS were significantly lower in the DCM group compared to the LVNC group.

In this study, cardiac function was significantly impaired in the DCM group than in the LVNC group, consistent with the more advanced myocardial impairment observed in DCM. These findings support the interpretation that reduced myocardial strain correlates with the severity of cardiac dysfunction. Additionally, in this study, the proportion of patients with delayed enhancement positivity in DCM was significantly higher than that in LVNC, suggesting that myocardial fibrosis is also one of the causes leading to reduced left ventricular myocardial strain.

The trabecular structure of the heart encodes crucial morphological and functional information, which may be influenced by genetic background pathological conditions associated with cardiac stress [10]. FD, derived from fractal analysis, provides a quantitative measure of trabecular complexity and endocardial surface irregularity [11]. The FD value is a non-integer value ranging from 1 to 2, with higher values indicating greater trabecular density and structural complexity. Global mean FD represents the average FD across all short-axis slices of the left ventricle, while apical and basal FD values refer to the average and maximum FD measured specifically at the apical and basal segments. In the present study, global FD, apical mean FD, apical maximum FD, basal mean FD, and basal maximum FD were significantly higher in the LVNC and DCM group than in the control group.

Distinguishing between excessive trabeculation in LVNC and that observed in DCM has remained a diagnostic challenge. FD-based quantification enabled objective assessment of trabecular complexity in both conditions. In this study, global FD, mean apical FD, maximum apical FD, mean basal FD and basal maximum FD were significantly higher in the LVNC group than in the DCM group, to some extent consistent with the findings reported by Zheng et al. [8]

Gati et al. demonstrated that chronic hemodynamic overload, such as sickle cell anemia, can lead to increased left ventricular trabeculation, particularly in response to sustained elevations in cardiac preload and output [12]. Captur et al. concluded that hypertensive cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or other forms of myocardial hypertrophy due to increased afterload could also result in increased trabeculae, indicative of myocardial hypertrabeculation [13]. These observations suggest that subendocardial trabecular proliferation may represent a compensatory response in various pathological states and may correlate with disease severity. In DCM, trabecular proliferation is typically a secondary phenomenon that evolves as the disease progresses, often localizing to the lateral wall of the left ventricle while sparing the apex. This pattern results in less pronounced apical trabeculation compared to LVNC, which is reflected in the significantly lower FD values observed in the DCM group in this study. The pathogenesis of LVNC is thought to involve embryonic arrest of the myocardial compaction process, potentially due to gene mutations affecting embryonic development. This leads to persistent postnatal noncompaction and a characteristic spongiform myocardial structure. Additionally, congenital heart defects, such as aortic stenosis, which occur during embryonic development may exacerbate left ventricular pressure load. This increased load directly affects the trabeculae and hinders the closure of myocardial sinusoids, thereby promoting myocardial noncompaction [14].

Myocardial compaction progresses in a defined spatiotemporal pattern—from the base to the apex, from the epicardium to the endocardium, and from the septum to the lateral wall—with the left ventricle typically undergoing earlier compaction than the right [15]. Therefore, LVNC involves both ventricles, but most characteristically affects the apical and lateral regions of the left ventricle. This regional predilection accounts for the significantly elevated apical FD values observed in patients with LVNC compared to those with DCM.

ROC curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of GPRS, GPCS, GPLS, global FD, apical mean FD, apical maximum FD, basal mean FD and basal maximum FD in differentiating LVNC from DCM. Among the myocardial strain parameters, GPLS demonstrated the highest diagnostic efficacy. Within the fractal analysis parameters, global FD and apical maximum FD yielded the higher AUC. Additionally, the combined application of GPLS and global FD, GPLS and apical maximum FD produced higher AUC of 0.966, 0.950, which were higher than that of Global FD, but no statistically significant than that of GPLS. The present study underscores the additive diagnostic value of combining functional assessment (myocardial strain) with advanced morphological analysis. While the NC/C ratio is a foundational diagnostic criterion for LVNC, its utility in differentiating LVNC from DCM with hypertrabeculation can be limited, as it primarily reflects myocardial thickness rather than architectural complexity. Our findings demonstrate that FD captures a distinct aspect of trabecular morphology, namely, its spatial complexity and heterogeneity, which we hypothesize is more reflective of the embryogenic arrest characteristic of LVNC compared to the adaptive hypertrabeculation in DCM. This is supported by the superior diagnostic performance of FD parameters alone (e.g., Global FD AUC = 0.901) and in combination with strain (GPLS + Global FD AUC = 0.966). It should be noted that while LGE imaging was performed, its presence and patterns were variable and non-specific in our cohort, consistent with the known heterogeneity of these diseases. Therefore, this study focused on the diagnostic performance of parameters derived solely from non-contrast cine CMR.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the LVNC group included patients at varying stages of disease progression, resulting in a broad range of EF values. This may have influenced both myocardial strain and fractal analysis measurements. Future studies should consider stratifying participants based on disease stage to improve parameter consistency. Second, LVNC can be classified into isolated (solitary) and non-isolated forms, with the latter often occurring in association with other congenital cardiac anomalies. This classification was not applied in the present study, and its potential impact on the results warrants further investigation. Third, it is important to note that among the myocardial strain parameters, GPRS is known to have lower reproducibility compared to GPCS and GPLS in CMR feature-tracking, largely due to its greater susceptibility to through-plane motion. Although GPRS showed significant differences in our cohort, the findings for GPLS and GPCS are considered more robust. Fourth, the overall sample size was relatively limited, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Larger multi-center studies are recommended to validate and expand upon these results. Furthermore, the relationship between trabecular complexity and underlying myocardial tissue composition, such as diffuse fibrosis quantified by T1 mapping and ECV, was not explored in this study and warrants future investigation. Additionally, although LGE data were acquired, the prevalence of LGE positivity was relatively low in our cohort (particularly in the LVNC group), and its patterns were heterogeneous. Therefore, a formal statistical analysis examining the association between LGE presence/patterns and the primary parameters of interest (myocardial strain and FD) was not performed, as it would likely be underpowered and potentially misleading. This represents another aspect of tissue characterization that merits exploration in larger, dedicated studies. In addition, it is important to acknowledge the technical limitations inherent to CMR feature-tracking itself. Although this technique benefits from high tissue contrast and is not susceptible to acoustic window limitations or anterior chest wall deformity—common challenges in speckle tracking echocardiography (STE) [16, 17], its reproducibility can be influenced by other factors. These include image resolution, the specific cine sequence parameters used (e.g., temporal resolution), and the proprietary algorithms of different post-processing software vendors [18]. In this study, all CMR examinations were performed on a single vendor’s scanner (Siemens) and analyzed with one software (CVI42) to ensure internal consistency. However, the generalizability of our strain values to setups from other CMR vendors or using different software solutions may be limited and warrants further investigation. Additionally, our study reported the compacted left ventricular mass. While the independent quantification of non-compacted trabecular mass could provide further pathophysiological insights, it is associated with substantial technical challenges regarding segmentation accuracy and reproducibility, and its clinical utility is yet to be determined; this represents a promising focus for future technical developments. The diagnostic efficacy of strain and FD parameter was analyzed using the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. It should be noted that without internal validation, this analysis may be subject to overfitting, particularly given the number of parameters evaluated relative to the sample size. The reported AUC values should therefore be interpreted as indicative of promising diagnostic performance that requires confirmation in larger, independent cohorts.

Conclusion

This study comprehensively evaluated cardiac function, myocardial strain, and trabecular complexity in patients with LVNC and DCM. The parameters GPRS, GPCS, GPLS, global FD, apical mean FD, apical maximum FD, basal mean FD and basal maximum FD demonstrated significant diagnostic utility in distinguishing between LVNC and DCM. These findings suggest that myocardial strain analysis and fractal dimension measurement are effective in characterizing myocardial deformation and assessing trabecular microarchitecture, thereby providing valuable quantitative tools for the differential diagnosis of LVNC and DCM.
