Persistent and emerging cancer risks after migration: Evidence from North and South Korean cohorts
Authors: Junshik Hong, Kyeong Jin Kim, Jimi Choi, Jung A. Kim, Kyoung Jin Kim, Yo Han Lee, Sang Min Park, Sung In Jang, Jung Myun Lee, Ju Hyang Lee, Mun Jeong Choi, Young‐Hoon Kim, Sin Gon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNorth Korean defectors in South Korea offer a rare natural model to trace cancer risk evolution after rapid environmental transition, given shared genetics but markedly contrasting early life exposures with South Korean residents.MethodsUsing the Korean National Health Insurance database, we constructed a nationwide matched cohort of 25,798 North Korean defectors and 1,276,601 South Korean residents (1:50 frequency matching by sex, age, and index year). We compared overall and site‐specific cancer risks and examined time‐varying hazard ratios (HRs) since resettlement.ResultsOverall cancer risk was higher among defectors (HR 1.13; 95% confidence interval, 1.07–1.18), particularly in men (HR 1.31). Liver (HR 2.53), uterine cervical (HR 2.10), and lung cancer (HR 1.69) were markedly elevated, reflecting infection‐ and deprivation‐related legacies. In contrast, cancers more prevalent in developed countries, notably breast (HR 0.48) and colorectal (HR 0.71), were initially lower. However, HRs began to rise over time, especially for breast cancer, indicating a trend toward convergence with host‐population patterns.ConclusionThe cancer profile of North Korean defectors demonstrates a dual burden: persistent risks from infection‐related cancers rooted in pre‐defection exposures and growing vulnerability to lifestyle‐related cancers after resettlement. These findings highlight the importance of dual‐track prevention strategies that address both legacy and emerging cancer risks in rapidly transitioning populations.

Introduction

Migration plays a significant role in shaping global disease patterns through a complex interplay of genetic background, environmental exposures, and healthcare systems. When people move from one geographic and socio‐environmental region to another, substantial shifts in population health may occur. Such changes are evident not only in cardiovascular, respiratory, and metabolic diseases but also in the epidemiology of cancer. Several studies in Europe [1, 2] and the United States [3, 4] have shown that cancer incidence and subtype distribution can shift when populations with different racial and genetic backgrounds migrate. These shifts arise not from migration itself, but from environmental and lifestyle factors in the host society. For example, colorectal cancer increased among Japanese immigrants to Hawaii [5], but not in those to São Paulo, Brazil [6], highlighting the role of environment and behavior in cancer risk.

North and South Koreans are widely regarded as genetically homogeneous, sharing common ancestry, language, and ethnicity, yet they have lived under radically different social, economic, and environmental conditions since the division of the Korean peninsula in 1948. The Korean War (1950–1953) deepened this division and catalyzed the development of two distinct socio‐environmental pathways. South Korea underwent rapid economic growth and democratization, whereas North Korea initially established a centralized public health infrastructure but later faced significant deterioration of public health capacity due to repeated natural disasters and growing isolation driven by international sanctions related to its nuclear program [7].

Since 1998, more than 34,000 North Korean defectors have resettled in South Korea. Despite extensive governmental and civic supports, North Korean defectors remain socioeconomically vulnerable [8]. For such populations, a cancer diagnosis—which demands intensive and prolonged treatment—can pose a particularly heavy burden. Cancer risk is largely driven by modifiable factors, including carcinogen exposure, infections, and lifestyle habits such as smoking, alcohol use, and physical activity [9]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 30%–50% of cancers are preventable through risk factor reduction [10].

Most adult defectors were born and raised in North Korea and thus experienced early life exposures and a healthcare system markedly different from those of native South Koreans. Consequently, longitudinal comparisons between defectors and native residents offer a unique opportunity to understand how socio‐environmental transitions influence disease phenotypes, particularly noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), without the confounding effect of genetic variation. Recognizing these differences is essential for developing cancer prevention and treatment strategies for North Korean defectors, which may also help advance health equity and reduce disparities in this population.

This study aims to compare overall and site‐specific cancer incidences between North Korean defectors and native South Koreans and to examine how risk evolves after resettlement. By doing so, it seeks to provide novel insight into how rapid socio‐environmental transitions reshape cancer risk and to inform more effective and tailored strategies for cancer prevention and care in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Data source

Enrollment in the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) is mandatory for all residents and healthcare providers in South Korea. As the sole insurer, the NHIS covers nearly the entire population, with about 3% of low‐income individuals enrolled in the Medical Aid program, which is also managed by the NHIS [11]. The NHIS operates the National Health Information Database (NHID), containing demographic and comprehensive medical data from claims and the National Health Screening Program. This has enabled numerous high‐quality nationwide epidemiological studies [12, 13], including in oncology [14, 15].

Population identification and matching

First, we obtained data on 52,932,270 individuals insured through the NHIS‐NHID from January 2002 to December 2022. Among these, 41,936 were identified as North Korean defectors (please see the additional explanations for Fig. S1). The NHIS‐NHID includes a specific eligibility code (code 14) exclusively for North Korean defectors. Upon resettlement, all defectors are initially enrolled in the Medical Aid program (code 7), but their eligibility may later shift to NHIS depending on employment, income, and assets. Thus, those with both codes (codes 14 and 7) can be reliably identified as North Korean defectors. Next, 2,096,800 native South Korean residents were selected through 50‐fold frequency matching to the 41,936 North Korean defectors with the distribution of sex, birth year, and index year of the North Korean defectors used as matching strata. We then excluded individuals under the age of 20, those enrolled before 2003 or after 2020 (washout period), those with less than 2 years of follow‐up, and those with a prior cancer history. The final analysis included 25,798 North Korean defectors and 1,276,601 South Korean residents (Fig. S1 and its additional explanations).

Data acquisition

We collected data from the NHIS‐NHID on sex, age at enrollment, disability status (based on baseline disability grade), Medical Aid enrollment, and income quartile (ranging from 1 to 20, with higher quartiles indicating higher income). Medical Aid enrollment and income quartile were used as proxies for socioeconomic status (SES). We also obtained data on major chronic diseases, medications, and the most recent health check‐up results near enrollment from a subset of patients with available records in both groups.

New cancer diagnoses were identified from 2 years after enrollment until the end of follow‐up or death, based on the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision codes plus the cancer‐specific code (V193) in the NHIS database (to exclude preexisting cancers within 2 years after enrollment). Codes are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequency and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. Group differences were assessed using absolute standardized difference (ASD), with an ASD < 0.1 indicating negligible imbalance [16]. The incidence rates of cancer were calculated as the number of events per 1,000,000 person‐years. Cumulative incidence was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves.

Cancer risk was compared using stratified Cox models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence interval (CI). To appropriately account for the frequency‐matching cohort design, the models were stratified by sex, age, and year of enrolment, allowing stratum‐specific baseline hazards. When effect modification was of specific interest, interaction terms were included in the models, and corresponding p‐values for interaction are presented. The proportional hazard assumption was evaluated using the Grambsch and Therneau test based on Schoenfeld residuals [17].

With the exception of Medical Aid enrollment and income quartile used as proxies for SES, all covariates included in the adjusted models were assessed during the 2‐year run‐in period prior to cohort entry.

For overall cancer risk, two multivariate models were constructed. Model A adjusted for the presence of disability, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease), and use of antithrombotic agents. Model B additionally included body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and physical activity. Variables such as BMI and metabolic comorbidities were not included in the matching process, as they may be influenced by post‐migration environmental and lifestyle changes and could lead to overadjustment if controlled at baseline. The HRs for each cancer type risk were estimated without any adjustment variable. To examine whether the association between group and cancer risk differed by sex, an interaction term was included in the Cox models. To examine changes over time in cancer risk among North Korean defectors compared with South Korean residents, time‐varying HRs were estimated for the following time intervals since baseline: 0–6, 6–10, 10–13, and >13 years. Nonlinear changes in HRs over time were assessed and visualized using restricted cubic spline functions with 95% confidence bands. All p‐values were two‐sided, and statistical significance was considered p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using the SAS Enterprise Guide version 7.1 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R software version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

North Korean defectors had a mean age of 38.7 ± 11.5 years, with 76.3% female, reflecting the predominance of younger women among defectors [18]. The South Korean residents had nearly identical age and sex distribution to North Korean defectors due to the matching. As for comorbidities, South Korean males had higher rates of diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia compared to male defectors. At baseline (i.e., within 2 years of enrollment), 82.5% of North Korean defectors received Medical Aid, and NHIS beneficiaries were concentrated in lower income quartiles. In contrast, only 1.9% of the South Korean residents received Medical Aid, with a more even income distribution. By the end of the study, Medical Aid use among defectors dropped to 38.1%, suggesting economic adaptation, though their SES remained lower throughout (Table 1).

In health screening data available for a subset of participants, smoking and alcohol use were more prevalent among North Korean male defectors than South Korean males, whereas the opposite pattern was observed in females. Female BMI was similar between the two groups, but South Korean males had higher BMI (Table 1 and Table S2).

Overall cancer incidence and relative risk between two groups

The median follow‐up (from 2 years after the enrollment to the end of follow‐up or death) was 10.01 years (IQR, 6.01 to 13.02) for each group (Table S3).

Table 2 presents age‐ and sex‐stratified cancer risk. Among 25,798 North Korean defectors, 1723 developed cancers, compared to 76,461 cases among 1,276,601 South Korean residents. The standardized incidence rates per 1,000,000 person‐years were 63.05 in defectors and 55.51 in South Koreans. The HR for cancer incidence was 1.13 (95% CI, 1.07–1.18; p < 0.001). Among males, the age‐standardized cancer incidence rate (ASR) was substantially higher in North Korean defectors (62.92) than in South Korean residents (48.22) with a 1.31‐fold increased risk after resettlement (p < 0.001). Among females, the risk was also elevated but less pronounced, with an HR of 1.08 (p = 0.005) and ASRs of 63.09 versus 57.88, respectively.

When we exploratorily adjusted for baseline NCD risk factors using comorbidity and medication data from the NHIS‐NHID alone (model A) or in combination with health screening indicators among those who underwent check‐ups (model B), the observed trend remained significant, indicating a modest but significantly higher risk of cancer after resettlement in defectors (Table 2).

Site‐specific cancer risk

As shown in Table 3 and Fig. S2, North Korean defectors had significantly higher risk of liver (HR 2.53; 95% CI, 2.28–2.82), lung (HR 1.42; 95% CI, 1.18–1.71), uterine cervix (HR 2.10; 95% CI, 1.75–2.53), and bladder cancer (HR 1.61; 95% CI, 1.21–2.14; all p < 0.001). Stomach cancer showed a nonsignificant trend toward increased risk (HR 1.18; p = 0.081). In contrast, defectors had lower risk of female breast (HR 0.48; 95% CI, 0.41–0.58), colon (HR 0.71; 95% CI, 0.59–0.87), and prostate cancer (HR 0.57; 95% CI, 0.41–0.81).

Changes in risk over time after resettlement

To assess changes in the trajectory of HRs over time, HR was analyzed by follow‐up duration quartiles: 0–6, 6–10, 10–13, and 13–18 years (Fig. 1 and Table S4). In male defectors, lung cancer risk remained consistently elevated across all follow‐up periods, suggesting sustained exposure or residual effect related to smoking acquired before migration. In contrast, the initially high HR for liver cancer gradually declined in both sexes, consistent with improved viral control and better access to screening and treatment after resettlement. Similar decreasing but nonsignificant trends were observed for stomach cancer in men and uterine cervical cancer in women, possibly reflecting partial convergence toward host‐population risk levels.

Conversely, HRs for cancer types commonly reported to increase with socioeconomic development, including prostate, colon, and breast, showed upward trajectories. Notably, breast cancer risk among female defectors increased early after resettlement and continued to rise over follow‐up, although estimates in later periods were less precise and did not consistently reach statistical significance. This pattern implies that lifestyle changes after migration may rapidly modify underlying cancer susceptibility, underscoring the dynamic nature of cancer epidemiology in transitioning populations.

Discussion

This study examines the distinct cancer epidemiology of North Korean defectors resettled in South Korea. By comparing genetically similar North and South Koreans who experienced different early life conditions, we describe differences in cancer risk patterns. Defectors exhibited higher risks of infection‐related cancers (e.g., liver and cervix) and lower risks of breast, colon, and prostate cancers, reflecting patterns seen in low human development index countries [19, 20, 21]. Over time, their cancer profile changed, suggesting rapid adaptation to South Korean society. These findings are consistent with changes observed during rapid socio‐environmental transitions and may offer indirect insight into future cancer trends in North Korea in the event of future opening and development. A similar shift occurred in East Germans after reunification, marked by a rapid rise in cardiometabolic diseases as living conditions and lifestyles changed [22, 23].

A notable finding of this study is the elevated risk of lung cancer risk among North Korean male defectors, a pattern not observed in women. This likely reflects higher smoking prevalence among North Korean men. Although official data report only modest rates [24], defector interviews suggest widespread tobacco use, reinforced by long military service where smoking is common [25]. In contrast, smoking is socially discouraged among North Korean women. The WHO estimated smoking rate in 2019 at 37.7% for North Korean men and 0% for women [24], which may explain the similar lung cancer incidence between female defectors and South Korean women. The rising HRs for lung cancer in men over time (Fig. 1) may suggest continued smoking after resettlement, driven by ingrained habits and stress of adaptation. Although women comprise the majority of the defector population and receive considerable public health attention [26], these findings highlight the need for focused cancer prevention in men, especially smokers.

Liver, stomach, and uterine cervical cancer showed significantly higher incidence among North Korean defectors compared to South Korean residents. These cancers are associated with modifiable risk factors—chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection and alcohol use for liver cancer, high salt intake and Helicobacter pylori for stomach cancer, and human papilloma virus (HPV) infection for cervical cancer. Although the incidence of these cancers has declined in South Korea due to improved sanitation, diet, vaccination, and screening, they remain a concern for populations previously lacking such access [27]. A report from Hanawon, the national resettlement facility, found that 10.9% of defectors (669 of 6087) tested positive for HBV during entry screening, more than twice the prevalence in the general South Korean population (∼4%), suggesting suboptimal hepatitis B control in North Korea [28]. Although HPV prevalence has not been directly compared, cervical cancer screening remains substantially lower among defectors (42% vs. 70%) [29].

Unlike lung cancer—where smoking cessation and risk control are difficult to achieve after migration—liver and cervical cancers represent areas where post‐resettlement interventions such as vaccination, antiviral therapy, and organized screening can yield substantial benefits. These improvements are likely to contribute to the gradual decline in the relative risk of infection‐related cancers observed among defectors over time (Fig. 1).

Breast and colon cancers, often regarded as cancers more prevalent in high‐income countries, showed lower incidence among North Korean defectors. The difference was more pronounced for breast cancer, whereas lower colon cancer risk was mainly seen in women. Notably, although the HR for breast cancer in defectors remained below 1.0, it showed a progressive and significant upward trend over time (Fig. 1). In South Korea, breast cancer typically peaks in the late 40s to early 50s, about a decade earlier than in many Western countries [30]—reflecting shifts in reproductive behavior and lifestyle, such as delayed childbirth, fewer children, reduced breastfeeding, and dietary changes [30]. The relatively rapid convergence of breast cancer risk between defectors and South Korean women suggests that nongenetic factors may contribute to young‐onset breast cancer patterns in Korea, although causal attribution cannot be made in the current study.

Based on these findings, cancer control efforts for North Korean defectors should adopt a dual‐track approach addressing both legacy and emerging risks. Immediate priorities include modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly smoking and alcohol use among male defectors, and strengthening early detection and risk factor control for infection‐related cancers such as liver, stomach, and uterine cervical cancer. In parallel, growing attention is needed for cancer types commonly reported to increase with socioeconomic development as resettlement progresses, with particular vigilance for rising breast cancer risk among younger female defectors. This strategy offers a framework not only for improving health outcomes among defectors but also for anticipating future cancer burdens in the broader North Korean population.

This study should be interpreted with several limitations. First, despite the substantial sample size, the number of cancer cases for some subtypes was small, limiting the precision of risk estimates. Second, the study population—mostly younger women from North Korea's border regions with China—may not fully represent the broader North Korean population, particularly older adults, men, or residents of urban centers such as Pyongyang. Third, surveillance bias, especially increased detection shortly after resettlement, may have contributed to early incidence. Of note, because individual‐ or community‐level environmental exposures were not directly measured, any interpretations related to environmental factors should be regarded as associative rather than causal. The study also raises intriguing questions for long‐term follow‐up. Although male North Korean defectors face high lung cancer risk, they have lower BMI and a lower prevalence of metabolic diseases than South Korean men. Observing how these differences affect cancer incidence over 10 or 20 years would be of great interest.

In conclusion, the cancer profile of North Korean defectors reflects the dual impact of early life exposures in a resource‐limited setting and rapid adaptation to a new environment. These findings call for targeted strategies to address both current vulnerabilities and emerging risks. While grounded in the unique context of the Korean peninsula, this study may also inform our understanding of how cancer patterns evolve in other populations undergoing similar rapid social and environmental change.
