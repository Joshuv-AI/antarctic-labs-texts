Selective sparing of sartorius and biceps femoris muscles on MRI in post-polio syndrome: a case report of two cases
Authors: Guangrui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-polio syndrome (PPS) is characterized by progressive muscle weakness and atrophy decades after acute poliomyelitis. While muscle involvement is typically asymmetric and patchy, specific patterns of selective muscle sparing have not been well characterized.Case PresentationWe report two male patients (ages 36 and 69 years) with a remote history of paralytic poliomyelitis who presented with progressive lower limb weakness and atrophy, fulfilling the March of Dimes diagnostic criteria for PPS. Detailed clinical examination revealed asymmetric motor deficits with right-sided predominance. Comprehensive electrophysiological studies demonstrated chronic neurogenic changes without evidence of active denervation or upper motor neuron involvement. Muscle MRI of the thighs revealed a progressive pattern of selective muscle sparing: Patient 1 demonstrated unilateral preservation of the sartorius and biceps femoris in the newly affected left thigh, with end-stage fatty obliteration of these muscles in the previously affected right thigh; Patient 2 demonstrated bilateral preservation of these muscles, representing an earlier stage of the same pathophysiological process.ConclusionsThis observation suggests that post-polio muscular degeneration follows a hierarchic and progressive pattern. The sartorius and biceps femoris exhibit relative resilience—delayed rather than absolute resistance to degeneration—possibly reflecting unique anatomical, developmental, or pathophysiological protective factors. Introduction

Post-polio syndrome (PPS) affects 20–85% of survivors of acute paralytic poliomyelitis, typically emerging 30–40 years after the initial infection [1]. Characterized by progressive muscle weakness, atrophy, pain, and fatigue, PPS represents a complex neuromuscular disorder with pathophysiology thought to involve degeneration of enlarged post-polio motor units [1]. Diagnosis relies on the March of Dimes criteria: prior paralytic poliomyelitis with residual motor neuron loss, period of neurological stability ≥ 15 years, gradual onset of new weakness or fatigue, and exclusion of alternative causes [1]. While clinical features are well-documented, imaging patterns of muscle involvement remain underinvestigated. Advanced muscle MRI has emerged as a powerful tool for characterizing patterns of muscle degeneration in inherited and acquired neuromuscular diseases [2]. Recent studies have explored paraspinal muscle involvement in PPS [3] and neurogenic motor unit changes on MRI [4]. We identified a distinct and consistent pattern of selective muscle preservation in two PPS patients that may provide insights into disease pathophysiology and aid in differential diagnosis.

Case presentation

Patient 1

A 36-year-old man presented with progressive left lower limb weakness for 3 years. He had a history of paralytic poliomyelitis in childhood (age 2 years) with residual right lower limb weakness, followed by 30 years of neurological stability. Three years prior to admission, he developed progressive weakness in the left leg without sensory symptoms. He also had a history of autoimmune thyroiditis, currently well-controlled on levothyroxine.

Patient perspective

The patient reported significant functional impact, noting difficulty with ambulation and frequent falls, which substantially affected his quality of life and ability to work. He sought definitive diagnosis to guide rehabilitation strategies.

Clinical Examination: Vital signs were stable. Neurological examination revealed normal cranial nerves and upper extremity strength (5/5). Lower limb examination demonstrated asymmetric weakness: right hip flexion 0/5, left hip flexion 3/5; right knee extension 0/5, left knee extension 4/5; right knee flexion 3/5, left knee flexion 4/5; right ankle dorsiflexion 2/5, left ankle dorsiflexion 4/5; right ankle plantarflexion 3/5, left ankle plantarflexion 4/5. Muscle bulk was markedly reduced bilaterally, particularly on the right. Deep tendon reflexes were diminished (1+). Sensory examination and pathological reflexes were normal. Gait was antalgic with circumduction of the right leg.

Electrophysiology

Nerve conduction studies showed normal motor and sensory conduction velocities. Notably, right tibial nerve compound motor action potential (CMAP) amplitude was reduced (2.1 mV) compared to the left (24.3 mV). Needle electromyography revealed chronic neurogenic changes in left tibialis anterior, quadriceps, and right lower limb muscles, characterized by increased motor unit potential duration (up to 24.8 ms in quadriceps), high amplitude (up to 970 µV), and reduced recruitment pattern.

Laboratory Findings: Creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at 244.8 U/L (normal 50–310 U/L). Thyroid function tests were normal. Thyroid autoantibodies were elevated (TPOAb > 1044 IU/ml), consistent with autoimmune thyroiditis. Autoimmune serology (ANA, anti-dsDNA, ENA panel) was negative. Inflammatory markers and coagulation profiles were normal.

Imaging

MRI of both thighs (3.0 Tesla, axial T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and STIR sequences) demonstrated extensive fatty infiltration and volume loss in quadriceps (particularly vastus lateralis, medialis, and intermedius) and adductor magnus muscles. Signal intensity was increased on T1-weighted images and suppressed on fat-saturated sequences. In the left thigh, the sartorius and biceps femoris (particularly the long head) showed marked relative preservation with minimal fatty infiltration and maintained muscle bulk. In the right thigh, these muscles were not distinctly visualized due to end-stage fatty replacement. Similar but less severe changes were noted in the calves (Fig. 1A-C).

Patient 2

A 69-year-old man presented with progressive muscle weakness and atrophy predominantly affecting the right lower limb over 5 years. He had a documented remote history of paralytic poliomyelitis at age 5 years, followed by 59 years of neurological stability. He denied sensory deficits, dyspnea, or dysphagia, and had no family history of neuromuscular disorders.

Patient perspective

The patient described progressive limitation in climbing stairs and gait instability leading to social isolation. He expressed concern about further deterioration.

Clinical Examination: Vital signs were stable. General examination revealed bilateral lower limb muscle wasting, more pronounced on the right. Upper extremity strength was normal (5/5). Lower limb examination revealed asymmetric weakness: right hip flexion 0/5, left hip flexion 4/5; right knee extension 0/5, left knee extension 4/5; right knee flexion 3/5, left knee flexion 4/5; right ankle dorsiflexion 3/5, left ankle dorsiflexion 4/5; right ankle plantarflexion 4/5, left ankle plantarflexion 4/5. Deep tendon reflexes were absent on the right and diminished (1+) on the left. Sensory examination was normal. Pathological reflexes were negative. Gait analysis revealed a right-sided Trendelenburg pattern with foot drop.

Electrophysiological studies

Comprehensive nerve conduction studies and needle electromyography were performed. Motor nerve conduction studies showed normal distal latencies and conduction velocities in bilateral peroneal and tibial nerves, but significantly reduced compound motor action potential amplitudes in the right peroneal nerve (2.8 mV, normal > 4 mV) and tibial nerve (3.2 mV, normal > 5 mV), consistent with chronic axonal loss. Sensory nerve conduction studies were normal in bilateral sural and superficial peroneal nerves. Needle electromyography revealed chronic neurogenic changes characterized by increased motor unit potential duration (up to 26.3 ms), high amplitude (up to 1120 µV), and reduced recruitment pattern in the right quadriceps, tibialis anterior, and gastrocnemius muscles. Fasciculation potentials were occasionally observed at rest.

Laboratory Investigations: CK was normal (70.1 U/L). Inflammatory markers were minimally elevated (erythrocyte sedimentation rate 19 mm/h, reference 0–15). Autoimmune serology (ANA, anti-dsDNA, ENA profile) was negative. Thyroid function, fasting glucose, renal function, and electrolytes were unremarkable.

Imaging Findings

Muscle MRI

Bilateral thigh MRI at the mid-femoral level demonstrated extensive fatty replacement and volume loss involving the quadriceps musculature (vastus lateralis, medialis, and intermedius) and adductor magnus, with characteristic hyperintensity on T1-weighted sequences and signal suppression on fat-saturated STIR sequences. The sartorius and biceps femoris (particularly the long head) exhibited striking relative preservation with maintained muscle bulk and minimal fatty infiltration. The pattern was remarkably concordant with Patient 1’s imaging. Bilateral calf MRI showed similar with the right-side predominance. Subcutaneous soft tissue edema was noted in a reticular pattern (Fig. 1D-F).

Discussion

We report a progressive pattern of selective muscle sparing in PPS that encompasses two stages: unilateral sparing with contralateral end-stage disease (Patient 1) and bilateral sparing (Patient 2). This pattern was observed across two patients with divergent ages and disease durations, suggesting a dynamic, time-dependent pathophysiological process rather than a static distribution [2, 5].

Differential Diagnosis

PPS is fundamentally a diagnosis of exclusion. Although genetic testing and muscle biopsy were not performed, we systematically excluded major alternative diagnoses through clinical, electrophysiological, and radiological evaluation.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) was excluded by the absence of upper motor neuron signs (no spasticity, hyperreflexia, or pathological reflexes), the lack of active denervation or widespread fasciculations on EMG, and the clinical course of slow progression over decades following prolonged stable periods (30–59 years), contrasting sharply with the rapid progression of ALS.

Hereditary motor neuronopathies (e.g., adult-onset spinal muscular atrophy) typically present with symmetric weakness and often a positive family history, unlike the markedly asymmetric distribution and absent family history in our patients. The antecedent history of paralytic poliomyelitis provides a compelling alternative etiology.

Muscular dystrophies and genetic myopathies usually manifest in childhood or early adulthood with progressive symmetric or diffuse involvement, whereas our patients showed asymmetric, patchy fatty replacement with chronic neurogenic EMG features (large-amplitude, long-duration motor unit potentials), inconsistent with a myopathic process.

Inflammatory myopathies were excluded by normal or only mildly elevated CK, negative autoimmune serology, and the absence of edematous changes on MRI (STIR hyperintensity); the observed T1 hyperintensity with STIR suppression is characteristic of fatty infiltration rather than inflammation.

Inclusion body myositis (IBM) typically affects patients > 50 years with symmetric involvement and characteristic finger flexor weakness, demonstrates myopathic EMG features, and shows rimmed vacuoles on biopsy—features absent in our patients [6].

Diabetic amyotrophy presents with acute or subacute painful asymmetric thigh weakness in the context of diabetes mellitus, shows inconsistent anatomical patterns, and lacks the chronic selective sparing observed here. Both patients had normal glucose metabolism.

Structural and vascular etiologies were excluded by normal cervical spine MRI (Patient 2, showing disc bulging without stenosis or cord signal change) and normal lower extremity arterial ultrasound (Patient 2). Patient 1 had no clinical evidence of myelopathy.

Specificity of the observed mri pattern

Selective muscle preservation has been described in other neuromuscular conditions, requiring careful comparison. In spinal muscular atrophy, diffuse symmetric involvement is typical [7], contrasting with our asymmetric pattern. In inclusion body myositis, quadriceps involvement with hamstring sparing is common but accompanied by finger flexor weakness and myopathic EMG features [6]. The pattern observed here—sartorius and biceps femoris sparing amid severe quadriceps involvement—appears distinctive to PPS, possibly reflecting differential developmental innervation timing, motor unit remodeling capacity, and unique anatomical architecture [8, 9].

Recent MRI studies in PPS have characterized paraspinal muscle involvement [3] and neurogenic motor unit changes [4], supporting the utility of muscle MRI in this population. Kriss and Jenkins’s systematic review of muscle MRI in motor neuron diseases [10] provides context for interpreting these patterns, while Sakamoto et al.‘s report of unusual MRI findings in a polio survivor [11] highlights the heterogeneous imaging manifestations in this condition.

Pathophysiological implications

This hierarchic vulnerability appears to be progressive rather than absolute. In Patient 1, the right thigh—previously affected by the initial polio infection and subsequently subjected to decades of compensatory overuse—has undergone complete fatty degeneration of all compartments, including the sartorius and biceps femoris. In contrast, the newly affected left thigh demonstrates the early selective sparing pattern, with relative preservation of these muscles amid severe quadriceps and adductor involvement. Patient 2, with a more recent onset of PPS symptoms, demonstrates bilateral sparing, consistent with an earlier stage of the same process.

This temporal progression suggests that the relative resilience of the sartorius and biceps femoris is time-limited. Potential protective mechanisms—differential developmental innervation timing, efficient motor unit remodeling, superior vascular supply, or lower metabolic demands—may delay but not ultimately prevent degeneration in the face of sustained disease activity. This hypothesis aligns with the “exhaustion of motor unit reserve” model of PPS pathophysiology, where initially enlarged post-polio motor units gradually degenerate over decades.

Clinical implications

While this pattern requires validation in larger cohorts, it may serve as a dynamic imaging clue to support PPS diagnosis and stage assessment in the appropriate clinical context. The relatively preserved sartorius and biceps femoris may be leveraged for functional compensation strategies during the window of relative resilience, while protecting the vulnerable quadriceps from overuse injury.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, the two-patient cohort requires validation in larger studies to confirm the generalizability of the observed MRI pattern. Second, genetic testing and muscle biopsy were not performed in either patient. While the clinical diagnosis of PPS was secure based on established March of Dimes criteria and comprehensive non-invasive evaluation, the absence of histopathological confirmation and genetic exclusion of hereditary motor neuronopathies represents a limitation. We explicitly acknowledge that in atypical presentations or for definitive mechanistic studies, these investigations would be valuable. Third, Patient 1 did not undergo cervical spine MRI; although no clinical evidence of myelopathy existed, structural lesions were not completely excluded by imaging. Fourth, the cross-sectional design precludes longitudinal assessment of muscle sparing sustainability. Fifth, the progressive nature of selective muscle sparing proposed herein is based on cross-sectional comparison between two patients at different disease stages. Longitudinal imaging studies are required to confirm that the unilateral sparing pattern (Patient 1) indeed progresses to the bilateral end-stage pattern observed in the contralateral limb. Without such longitudinal data, the temporal sequence remains hypothetical. Finally, the absence of quantitative MRI analysis (such as Dixon fat fraction measurement) limits the precision of muscle fat infiltration assessment.

Conclusion

This consistent finding highlights a progressive pattern of selective muscle sparing in chronic poliomyelitis. The relative resilience of the sartorius and biceps femoris appears to be time-limited, with eventual fatty degeneration in the setting of sustained disease activity. This observation underscores that post-polio muscular degeneration is not uniform but follows a discernible hierarchy and temporal progression. Advanced muscle MRI may facilitate disease staging by identifying the selective sparing pattern before its complete obliteration, potentially informing rehabilitation strategies that leverage the preserved muscles during the window of relative resilience.
