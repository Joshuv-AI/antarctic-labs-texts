Prevalence and predictors of anxiety and depressive symptoms among medical students of Ebonyi State University Abakaliki, Nigeria
Authors: Edmund N Ossai, Ann N Nwuzor, Nicodemus O Obayi, Miracle Onyebara, Henry Nduka Ekechi, Irene I Eze, Monday N Igwe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms among medical students are higher than those of other students in other faculties. This study was designed to determine the prevalence and predictors of anxiety and depressive symptoms among medical students of Ebonyi State University Abakaliki, Nigeria.MethodsA cross-sectional study involving all medical students of the university was conducted. Information was obtained using a self-administered semi structured questionnaire. Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9), Generalized Anxiety Disorder − 7 (GAD-7) and Oslo Social Support Scale were used in the study. Chi square test and multivariate analysis using binary logistic regression were used in the analysis and level of statistical significance was determined by p value of < 0.05.ResultsA total of 535 medical students participated in the study, (response rate, 82.3%). Mean age of the students was 23.5 ± 3.6 years and majority, 55.7% were male. Prevalence of depressive symptoms was 54.6% and anxiety symptoms, 49.3%. Higher odds of depressive symptoms included being in preliminary class (the first year of medical school) (AOR = 2.7; 95%CI: 1.5–5.1), having poor social support, (AOR = 3.0; 95%CI: 1.8–5.1) while lower odds included perceiving academic performance as good, (AOR = 0.3; 95%CI: 0.2–0.5) and willingness to study Medicine if given another opportunity, (AOR = 0.6; 95%CI: 0.4–0.9). Higher odds of anxiety symptoms included being in preliminary class, (AOR = 2.2; 95%CI:1.2–4.1), and having poor social support, (AOR = 4.5; 95%CI: 2.7–7.7) while lower odds was perceiving academic performance as good (AOR = 0.3; 95%CI:0.2–0.5).ConclusionsA high proportion of students had anxiety and depressive symptoms. Given the association between being in preliminary class and higher odds of anxiety and depression, providing newly admitted students with orientation on the medical curriculum including stress management and mentorship pairing between students and faculty members by authorities of the University may be beneficial. The orientation should also provide specific psycho-education about the “transition shock” between the Faculty of Science and the College of Medicine. Because perceiving academic performance as good was associated with lower odds of anxiety and depression, prioritizing academic guidance for students could potentially contribute to improved mental well-being. The students require strong social support from families, friends, members of faculty and their colleagues. The high burden of depressive symptoms necessitates that the university should put in place measures for early detection and prompt management of cases of depression among the students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44192-026-00496-3.Keywords: Anxiety symptom, Depressive symptom, Social support, Medical students, Medical school, Nigeria

Background

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines mental health as a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn well and work well and also contribute to their community [1]. Thus, mental health disorders are associated with impairment in important areas of functioning. It has been postulated that this group of diseases will constitute the highest burden of disease globally by the year 2030 [2]. Anxiety and depressive disorders are regarded as the most common mental disorders [3]. There is evidence that the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms among medical students are higher than that of students in other faculties [4, 5]. For instance, a study among medical students in India revealed that 60.3% of the students had mental health problems [6]. Another study in Morocco revealed that 62.3% of the respondents had anxiety symptoms and 74.6% had depressive symptoms [7].

It has been found that medical students have a high level of academic stress [8, 9] and high stress perceptions lead to mental health impairments. Suffice it to say that in the training of students in the health professions there is always an exposure to stress [10]. This observation has prompted the suggestion that measures should be taken to alleviate the stress associated with medical education on the students [11]. Another approach is the need to put in place measures that will encourage early detection and prompt management of cases of depression among medical students [12]. These preventive measures are very essential as the mental health of medical students tend to become worse as they commence training in medical school [13]. Moreover, the students are less likely to identify symptoms of depression or access treatment when necessary than the general population even though they could be classified as having good access to healthcare [14].

The overall effect of mental health conditions could be said to be enormous. For example, mental health disorders adversely affect empathy [15] and quality of life [16]. It could also lead to burnout which is capable of affecting the professional conduct of a physician [17]. Mental health disorders also beget school dropout, drug abuse and suicidal ideations [18]. The need to pay attention to the mental health of medical students cannot be over-emphasized bearing in mind they are the physicians of tomorrow with huge responsibilities. Thus, a good understanding of the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms among medical students is of essence as that will help initiate preventive services and also enhance the process of rehabilitation [19]. This study was designed to determine the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms and associated factors among medical students of Ebonyi State University Abakaliki, Nigeria.

Methods

Study setting

The medical school of Ebonyi State University Abakaliki, Nigeria was established the same year as the university in 1999. The university admits an average of 100 students each year based on the recommendations of the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria which is one of the agencies of Government that regulates medical education in the country. As at the time of the study, the university had a total of 650 medical students. A minimum of six years is required to obtain the Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery degree in Nigerian universities. The first year of study also referred to as 100 level is regarded as the preliminary year and academic activities during this period take place at the Faculty of Science of the university. The second and third years of study are recognized as the pre-clinical period of training while the fourth to the sixth year of study is referred to as the clinical training period. The medical students of the university receive their clinical training at Alex- Ekwueme Federal University Teaching Hospital Abakaliki, Nigeria.

Study design and study population

A cross-sectional study design was adopted involving a total population sampling approach. The students who were absent on the days of data collection were excluded from the study based on logistics. Data collection for the study lasted for two weeks between January and February 2022.

Sample size determination

This was a total population study of all medical students in Ebonyi State University from the first year to final year. A total of five hundred and thirty-five medical students took part in the study representing a response rate of 82.3%.

Study instrument

The study instrument used for the assessment of depression and anxiety among the medical students was the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD7). Oslo Social Support Scale (OSS-3) was also used in the study. There was also a section of the questionnaire that focused on the socio-demographic characteristics of the students including the age of the respondents, gender, year of study, educational attainment of father and mother, perceived academic performance and other variables.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). This is a validated questionnaire that is used for screening, diagnosing, monitoring and measuring the severity of depressive symptoms [20]. The questionnaire has nine variables and responses are in a Likert scale format with scores ranging from zero to three. The assessment of each of the variables was made with reference to the past two weeks. The minimum score that could be obtained for each respondent was zero while the maximum score could be 27. The total score for each respondent was obtained by adding the score for each of the nine variables. The total score was then categorized as follows, score of zero to 4 indicating ‘No depressive symptoms,’ score of 5 to 9 representing mild depression, score of 10 to 14 for moderate depression, score of 15 to 19 ascribed as moderately severe depression and score of 20 to 27 for severe depression. The depressive symptom of the respondents was further categorized into two for the purpose of data analysis, No depressive symptoms for scores of zero to four while scores of five and above was used to define presence of anxiety symptoms.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) is a short screening measure used in medical and community settings to assess anxiety severity [21]. The questionnaire has seven variables and responses are in a Likert scale format with scores ranging from zero to three. The minimum score that could be obtained for each respondent was zero while the maximum score could be 21. The total score for each respondent was obtained by adding the score for each of the seven variables. The assessment of each of the variables was made with reference to experiences in the past two weeks. The total score was then categorized as follows, score of zero to 4 meaning No anxiety, score of 5 to 9 representing mild anxiety, score of 10 to 14 for moderate anxiety, score of 15 and above ascribed as severe anxiety. The anxiety symptom of the respondents was further categorized into two for the purpose of data analysis, No anxiety symptoms for scores of zero to four while scores of five and above was used to define presence of anxiety symptoms.

Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3) is a 3-item self-reported measure of the level of social support [22]. It is made of three variables that enquire about the number of close confidents, the sense of concern from other people and the relationship with neighbours with the focus being on accessibility to practical help. The total score of the three variables ranges from 3 to 14. The higher the score for each respondent the higher the level of social support. The total score of each respondent was then categorized into three; a score of 3–8 indicate poor social support, 9–11 for moderate support while a score of 12–14 represent strong social support.

Method of data collection

Information was obtained from the students using a paper based, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire was distributed to the students after the class for the day and the instruction was that the questionnaire should be completed and submitted immediately. However a few of the students went home with the questionnaire and returned them after two days. These questionnaires were accepted based on the fact this was a total population study however this approach could introduce social desirability bias or affect the choice of responses to questions by the students. Data collection was done for each class at a time and this was to ensure that no student returned more than one questionnaire after completion. No member of the ‘Faculty’ was involved in the data collection process as this was done by the medical students.

Data management

Data entry and analysis were done using IBM-SPSS statistical package, version 26. Categorical variables were represented using frequencies and proportions while continuous variables were summarized using mean and standard deviation. Chi square test and multivariate analysis using binary logistic regression were used in the analysis and the level of statistical significance was determined by a p value of < 0.05.

Outcome measures of the study included the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms among the students and the influencing factors. In determining the factors associated with depressive and anxiety symptoms among the students, the socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents and other variables that follow a logical sequence were cross-tabulated with the outcome variables. Variables that had a p value of < 0.2 after bivariate analysis were entered into the logistic regression model to determine the predictors of depressive and anxiety symptoms among the respondents. The results of the binary logistic regression analysis were presented using adjusted odds ratio and 95% confidence interval and the level of statistical significance was determined using a p value of < 0.05.

In categorizing the independent variables, the mean age of the respondents was used to categorize the age of the respondents into two groups, (< 25 years and 25 years and above). This was because the majority of the students, 53.2% were in the age group, 20–24 years. The academic level of the respondents was designated as ‘Stage of training’. The students in 100 level were designated as being in the ‘Preliminary class’ those in 200–300 level were denoted as being in the ‘Pre-clinical stage’ while those in 400–600 level were regarded as being in the ‘Clinical stage’ of training. The educational attainment of the parents of the students was categorized into two, ‘Tertiary education’ and ‘Secondary education and below.’ This was because the majority of the parents of the students attained tertiary education. The perceived academic performance of the students was dichotomized into ‘Good’ and ‘Poor.’

Discussion

From the results of our study, a high proportion of the students, 54.6% had depressive symptoms. This proportion of medical students that had depressive symptoms were found to be lower than that obtained in other studies [7, 23, 24]. These results attest to the high burden of depressive disorders among medical students. However, in a nation-wide study in Portugal among medical students, 46.9% of the students had depressive symptoms [25]. In a study among French university students, only 12.6% of the students had depressive symptoms [26]. The prevalence of ‘severe depression’ among the respondents was 3.7%. This proportion may appear small but in a population of 535 students, this represents an approximate 20 individuals who are potentially at high risk for self-harm. This finding may necessitate that the university management should put in place measures for early detection and prompt management of cases of depression among the students.

Less than half of the respondents in this study, 49.3% had anxiety symptoms. This proportion that had anxiety symptoms is lower than that obtained from some studies [7, 23, 24]. Also, in a study among medical students in Saudi Arabia, 53% of the students had anxiety symptoms [27]. Again, as in the case with depressive symptoms, the study in France that involved undergraduate students had a lower proportion of respondents that had anxiety symptoms, 7.6% [26]. This difference in the proportion of the students that had depressive and anxiety symptoms between the results of this study and that from the medical school in France could be attributed to the differences in economic stability, tuition pressure, and future employment prospects, which are significantly more precarious for Nigerian medical students than their Western counterparts.

The results of the study revealed that the second-year students had the highest proportion of students that had both depressive 25.7% and anxiety symptoms 25.4%. In both cases the final year students had the least proportion of those with depressive and anxiety symptoms. In a previous study in the institution, students in the examination year, the third year had the highest proportion of students with depressive symptoms [12]. As at the time of this present study the third-year medical students had completed their first professional examination in the medical school and this could be responsible for the second-year students who are the next in line for the professional examination having the highest proportion of those that had depressive and anxiety symptoms. It could then be said that it is the enormous work that need to be done by the students to be able to pass the first professional examination that is partly responsible for the class facing the examination to have the highest proportion of students with anxiety and depressive symptoms. Another study among medical students of the University of Birmingham, United Kingdom identified the first year of medical study (which is actually the second year in Nigeria) as a critical transition period during which the students adapt to being a university student, medical student and a doctor [28]. Even though a six-group typology of students were identified in that study, it was found that the students struggled between one or more of these transitions [28].

However, it is important to note that the challenges in medical school extend beyond the professional examinations. For instance, a study in China revealed that 12.1% of medical students had mental health problems during the postgraduate entrance examination and this proportion decreased significantly to 4.4% six months after the examination [29]. This could be a pointer that the period of professional medical examinations in the medical school is a trying moment for the students.

The first-year medical students had the second highest proportion of students that had both depressive, 21.5% and anxiety symptoms, 20.5%. This observation among newly admitted students is fast spreading. For example, in a study among medical students enrolled in a medical college in India, 32% of the respondents had depressive symptoms while 40% had anxiety symptoms [30]. In another study in a northern Thailand university, the second and fourth year medical students were high risk groups for depression [31]. However, in a study among medical students in the first three years of study, the second-year students had the highest proportion of those with depressive symptoms [32].

From the results of our study, three variables significantly predicted both depressive and anxiety symptoms among the respondents. They included being in the preliminary class and having poor social support which increased the odds of depressive and anxiety symptoms and perceiving academic performance as good which decreased the odds of depressive and anxiety symptoms. The respondents who were in the preliminary class were more likely to have depressive or anxiety symptoms when compared with the students in the clinical stage of training. It is likely that as medical students move from the preclinical to the clinical stage of their training, they are more reassured of realizing their dreams of becoming medical doctors. For example, the final year students in this study had the least proportion of those who had depressive or anxiety symptoms. This is similar to what have been observed in other climes. For example, the pre-clinical class has been identified as a risk factor for depression [7] and mental disorders [33]. Another study found higher levels of mental distress among medical students in the pre-clinical years when compared with those in the clinical years [26].

Some studies went further to focus on first year medical students. For example, a study in Peru affirmed that being in the first year of medical training was associated with mental disorders [24]. Another study that involved Mexican and Columbian medical students identified the early years of medical training as being associated with an increase in depressive and anxiety symptoms among the students [34]. A study in Turkey concluded that the mental health of students was negatively affected in the first year of training and suggested the need to raise awareness of mental illness among the students and plan appropriate interventions for those at risk [35].

It is important to point out that in some settings, it is the second-year medical students that are actually referred to as the first year in medical school. In Nigeria, the first year of study in the medical school is referred to as the preliminary year since lectures during this period are delivered at the Faculty of Science thus this stage of training is referred to as the preliminary class. This observation of a high proportion of first year students in Medicine having depressive symptoms in Nigeria prompted a suggestion that university authorities should provide proper orientation for newly admitted medical students on the medical curriculum [12]. This orientation exercise is expected to prepare the students adequately for the task ahead of them in the medical school rather than the students receiving such information from other students who may only relay their own experiences or frustrations to the newly admitted students. Other contributory factors for this observation, could be the fact that the students may be adapting to new methods of learning being their first year in the university. Also, for some of the students that period could be the first time they are moving away from home with the possibility of taking care of themselves and making new friends. All these could contribute to the relatively high proportion of the students in the preliminary class that have depressive and anxiety symptoms. Contrary to these findings, a study among medical students in Cameroon revealed that being a student in the clinical level of training was associated with depression [36]. Similarly, a study in Saudi Arabia reported that the least proportion of students with depressive symptoms, 8.9% were first year students [37].

The respondents who perceived their academic performance in the medical school as good were three times less likely to have depressive or anxiety symptoms. Having good academic performance is like an assurance that the dream of becoming a medical doctor could be realized. Thus, a study in India revealed that poor academic performance was associated with emotional distress [38]. Another study in Brazil concluded that being dissatisfied with one’s academic performance was associated with common mental disorders [39]. From the results of a study in Saudi Arabia, it was found that having regret studying Medicine is a predictor of depressive symptoms [37]. A study in Nigeria revealed that being afraid that one may not graduate from medical school was associated with having depressive symptoms [12]. Based on this finding, it is important that institutions should prioritize academic advising and guidance like use of staff advisers as a way of ensuring that the students meet their academic goals. This also makes it necessary that the ‘Faculty members’ in the various medical schools should be more patient and compassionate with the students while providing guidance. For example, a study among medical students in Calabar, Nigeria, revealed that a recent experience of mistreatment by trainers and colleagues was significantly associated with poor mental health among the students [40]. From anecdotal evidence, peer tutoring is routine among the medical students of the institution.

This study also revealed that the respondents who had moderate and poor social support were more likely to have depressive and anxiety symptoms when compared with those who had strong social support. This finding is consistent with that of several studies. For example, poor social support has been found to be associated with depressive symptoms [30] and mental distress [41]. Also, it has been identified that strong relationship with friends is protective against emotional distress [38] and satisfaction with the social support received has been found to be protective for mental distress [25]. There is also a greater risk of depression when a medical student is faced with inadequate support from family, friends and fellow medical students [42]. The observation among the medical students of this institution is that they show strong social support to any set of students who are preparing or taking any of the professional examinations in the medical school. Also, peer tutoring and group discussions are very prevalent among the medical students in the university. This observation is the same for members of their respective families especially those students who have explained the details of the medical trainings and examinations to their families.

The respondents who were single were four times more likely to have anxiety symptoms when compared with those who were married, Even though the result is statistically significant, the wide confidence interval suggests that the estimate is imprecise and should be interpreted with extreme caution. This is because the finding could be due to the small number of married students who accounted for 3.7% of the total sample size. Thus, this finding may require validation in studies with a larger, more balanced sample of married and single students. Similarly, the respondents whose mothers attained tertiary education were about twice more likely to have anxiety symptoms when compared with those whose mothers had secondary education and below. From the results of this study, it could be said that the students whose mothers are highly educated could have a higher tendency to be anxious based on what is expected from them academically. For example, a study in Anambra state, Nigeria revealed that maternal educational attainments significantly influence the academic achievements of adolescents [43]. Another study in Lagos state, Nigeria showed that highly educated mothers motivate their children using every available technique so as to ensure that their children excel in their academic pursuits [44]. Thus, it could be said that this high investment in the education of the child by the mothers could serve as a form of parental pressure which could eventually lead to anxiety on the part of the children. This may however be a direction for future research.

This study took place after the COVID-19 pandemic which was postulated to have affected the mental health of medical students [45, 46]. This may have had an influence on the results obtained from this study. In any case, previous studies have revealed that the prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms are higher among medical students when compared with that of students in other faculties [4, 5]. Also, responses to the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 questionnaires were based on experiences of the respondents in the last two weeks and there could be recall bias on the part of the students. Moreover, the study design being cross-sectional did not permit conclusions about causality. Suffice it to say that there was no assessment of the symptoms of anxiety and depression among the students prior to their entry into the university. Also, even though this was a total population study, a minor proportion of the students, 17.7% did not take part in the study. This proportion though small and the fact that this study involved a single medical school in Nigeria could affect the generalization of study findings. Another weakness of this study was allowing some students to go home with the questionnaire and return them after a maximum of two days. Even though only a minor proportion of the students were involved, this approach could introduce social desirability bias or permit peer influence in choice of responses to questions as some students could have discussed their responses to the variables in the questionnaire.

Conclusions

A high proportion of students had anxiety and depressive symptoms. Given the association between being in preliminary class and higher odds of anxiety and depression, providing newly admitted students with orientation on the medical curriculum including stress management and mentorship pairing by authorities of the University may be beneficial. The orientation should also provide specific psycho-education about the “transition shock” between the Faculty of Science and the College of Medicine. Because perceiving academic performance as good was associated with lower odds of anxiety and depression, prioritizing academic guidance for students could potentially contribute to improved mental well-being. The students require strong social support from families, friends, members of faculty and their colleagues. The high burden of depressive symptoms necessitates that the university should put in place measures for early detection and prompt management of cases of depression among the students.
