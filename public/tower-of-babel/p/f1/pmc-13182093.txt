Colostomy in patients with anorectal malformation with rectoperineal fistula or rectovestibular fistula: a single-center experience
Authors: Xianming Xiao, Wei Feng, Zhili Wang, Pengfei Chen, Jinping Hou, Yi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study summarizes the clinical characteristics of colostomy in children with anorectal malformation (ARM) with rectoperineal fistula (RPF) or rectovestibular fistula (RVF) to inform surgical decision-making.MethodsMedical records of children with RPF or RVF undergoing colostomy were retrospectively reviewed to analyze indications.ResultsAmong 761 rectoperineal fistula and 295 rectovestibular fistula patients, 124 received colostomy: 16 (2.1%) with RPF and 108 (36.6%) with RVF. Indications for colostomy in sixteen RPF were: nonvisible fistula with high Pouch-Perineum (P-P) distance (n = 5), prior colostomy elsewhere (n = 4), penile or scrotal fistula with high P-P distance (n = 2), high P-P distance alone (n = 2), perineal body fistula in a female (n = 1), failed intraoperative pouch identification (n = 1), and necrotizing enterocolitis (n = 1).For rectovestibular fistula (n = 108), indications included: treatment pre-2018 (n = 49, 45.4%), poor general condition or severe anomalies (n = 14, 12.9%), small fistula with persistent distension despite dilation (n = 13, 12.0%), failed prior repair on colostomy (n = 9, 8.3%), neonatal abdominal distension or defecation difficulty (n = 4, 3.7%), fistula near posterior commissure (n = 4, 3.7%), and tethered cord syndrome, reduction of neonatal wound complications, nonvisible fistula with high P-P distance, staged repair choice, or secondary megacolon (each 2.8%, n = 3).ConclusionsFor children with fistula at the penis or scrotum and high Pouch-Perineum distance, primary repair carries risks of rectal pouch misidentification and urethral injury. In rectoperineal fistula cases without a visible fistula and with a high Pouch-Perineum distance, colostomy is often unavoidable. A staged surgical procedure is recommended for patients with rectovestibular fistula in cases of poor general condition, severe concomitant congenital malformations, unresolved defecation difficulties following anal dilatation, or a fistula near the posterior commissure.

Background

Anorectal malformation is the most common gastrointestinal malformation in children, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 5,000 live births [1]. The presence of a visible perineal fistula usually indicates a low-type ARM, for which further imaging is often unnecessary, and definitive anoplasty can be performed soon after birth [2]. In contrast, the absence of a perineal fistula or the passage of meconium via the urethra or vagina typically suggests an intermediate or high-type ARM. Reliance on perineal examination alone to determine ARM type can be challenging, as up to 10–20% of male and 10% of female infants may lack an obvious fistula [3]. In some cases, the external fistula becomes apparent only 12–24 h postpartum as meconium advances distally. For newborns without a visible fistula at birth or 24 h thereafter, assessment of the Pouch–Perineum distance via invertography or transperineal sonography is crucial [4]. When the Pouch-Perineum (P-P) distance exceeds 1 cm, colostomy remains the widely accepted practice worldwide.

ARM with rectoperineal fistula or rectovestibular fistula is generally classified as a low-type defect. RPF can usually undergo single-stage repair. For RVF, some centers routinely perform a diverting colostomy in all cases, whereas others advocate primary repair, reporting favorable outcomes [5–10]. The optimal approach—primary repair versus staged repair with initial colostomy remains a matter of debate. To our knowledge, few studies have analyzed the indications for colostomy in patients with RPF or RVF. Therefore, this study aims to describe the clinical characteristics and indications for colostomy in this patient cohort, and to provide surgical decision-making support for surgeons managing low-type ARM patients regarding the necessity of stoma formation.

Patients and methods

We retrospectively reviewed all patients diagnosed with anorectal malformation with rectoperineal fistula or rectovestibular fistula at our hospital between January 2015 and January 2025, identified at the time of colostomy or definitive anorectoplasty. A total of 1,056 patients were diagnosed (761 RPF and 295 RVF). The inclusion criteria were defined as patients who have undergone enterostomy. Among them, 124 underwent colostomy, including 16 with RPF and 108 with RVF. Among the 124 patients, 13 were male (all with rectoperineal fistula), and 111 were female (3 with rectoperineal fistula and 108 with rectovestibular fistula). Prior to 2018, our hospital practice for RVF was staged repair to minimize postoperative complications such as wound infection and dehiscence. Since 2018, single-stage posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP) has been performed in RVF patients who met the criteria for primary repair.

In our center, for children with rectoperineal fistula undergoing a single-stage procedure, screening of the sacrococcygeal region and spine is not routinely performed prior to the anorectal operation. In contrast, for those undergoing a staged procedure, such screening is routinely conducted before the definitive anorectal surgery. The diagnosis of tethered spinal cord syndrome primarily relies on MRI and is made by a professional neurosurgeon.

In this study, colostomy indications were classified according to the predominant clinical issue at presentation, with final confirmation by the operating surgeon. The P–P distance has been defined by Teele et al. [11] and Haber et al. [3], and Yuan et al. [12]. The P–P distance refers to the distance between the distal rectal pouch and the perineum.

A high P–P distance was defined as > 1 cm, as measured by invertography, transperineal sonography, or probing [4, 13, 14]. Special fistula location refers to openings on the scrotum or penis in RPF, or in proximity to the posterior commissure in RVF, as shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Anorectal malformation with rectoperineal fistula

Among 761 patients with ARM with rectoperineal fistula, 16 patients (13 males, 3 females) underwent enterostomy, representing an enterostomy incidence of 2.1%. None were premature or of low birth weight. Before enterostomy, 7 cases had visible fistula in the perineal region, 8 cases had no obvious fistula opening (including 3 with colostomy performed in outside hospitals), and 1 case was unknown (colostomy performed in an outside hospital). Of the seven patients with a visible fistula (five males, two females), three underwent invertography > 24 h after birth, and five underwent transperineal sonography > 24 h after birth. In the two female cases, one underwent colostomy because sonography revealed a high pouch–perineum distance, and the other because the fistula opened at the perineal body. Among the five male cases, two exhibited meconium discharge from the penile shaft or scrotal root before colostomy and had high P-P distance on imaging; one underwent colostomy for high P-P distance; one required colostomy after intraoperative exploration identified an extremely narrow tract extending into the pelvis without locating the distal rectal pouch; and one required ileostomy due to concurrent neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis and bowel obstruction.

In the five male patients without a visible fistula opening, both invertography and transperineal sonography demonstrated a high P-P distance; four were assessed within 24 h of birth, and one after 24 h. The mean time to colostomy was 35.2 h. One underwent colostomy within 24 h of birth, while the remaining four underwent surgery after 24 h, three of whom had significant preoperative abdominal distension. Two patients exhibited meconium discharge from the fistula site after colostomy (one perineal, one scrotal root). Intraoperative exploration localized the fistula to the perineum and penile root adjacent to the urethra in two cases, and to the perineum in two cases; one had anorectoplasty at another institution.

Four patients (three males, one female) had undergone colostomy at outside hospitals before referral for definitive anorectoplasty at our center, with the original indications unknown. In three of these, no fistula opening was visible preoperatively, but intraoperative findings confirmed high RPF; one patient had anal canal stenosis.

Anorectal malformation with rectovestibular fistula

Among 295 patients with ARM with rectovestibular fistula, 108 children (36.6%) underwent colostomy. Of those who underwent colostomy, 49 cases (45.4%) had prior surgery before 2018, 14 (12.9%) had poor general conditions or severe congenital malformations, 13 (12.0%) had small fistula calibration with persistent abdominal distension or defecation difficulties even after fistula dilation, 5 (4.6%) were referred from other hospitals after colostomy, 4 (3.7%) had failed anorectoplasty at external hospitals, 4 (3.7%) newborns experienced abdominal distension, defecation issues, and/or vomiting, 4 (3.7%) had fistulas located near the posterior commissure, 3 (2.8%) had tethered spinal cord, 3 (2.8%) had colostomies to prevent postoperative wound infections or dehiscence, 3 (2.8%) had no visible fistula but high P-P distance, and 3 (2.8%) were selected for staged repair by their parents, and 3 (2.8%) were complicated by secondary megacolon. Discussion

Rectoperineal fistula represents the most common subtype of anorectal malformations, accounting for approximately 40–41% of all cases [1, 15]. In females with ARM, rectovestibular fistula is the most common defect [16]. Single-stage repair is generally feasible for rectoperineal fistula without the need for colostomy; no uniform consensus exists regarding whether rectovestibular fistulas should undergo primary repair or colostomy. However, children undergoing colostomy are also exposed to certain risks. In addition to the initial creation and subsequent closure of the colostomy, parents face significant financial burdens and psychological distress while managing their child’s stoma. Moreover, the condition carries a risk of various complications, such as prolapse, retraction, skin excoriation, parastomal hernia, and adhesive intestinal obstruction [5, 17, 18]. Therefore, this study aims to describe the clinical characteristics and indications for colostomy in this patient cohort, and to provide surgical decision-making support for surgeons managing low-type ARM patients regarding the necessity of stoma formation.

In this study, we reported a colostomy rate of 2.1% (16 per 761) among patients with ARM with rectoperineal fistula. In rectoperineal fistula, a careful perineal examination within the first 24 h of life typically identifies the fistula orifice, enabling single-stage repair. In some medical centers, cutaneous puncture may substitute for cross-table lateral X-ray and/or ultrasound to assess whether the distance between the rectum and overlying skin is within 1 cm [4]. In this cohort, 8 patients had no visible fistula prior to colostomy; in all of them, invertography or transperineal sonography revealed a pouch–perineum distance > 1 cm. Consequently, in newborns with no identifiable fistula orifice after birth or at 24 h and a high P–P distance, colostomy is often unavoidable. Furthermore, intraoperative exploration revealed that 3 patients had perineal fistulas and 4 had fistulas opening onto the penis or scrotum, suggesting that preoperative observation for meconium discharge from these sites could potentially obviate the need for colostomy. In patients with visible fistula, invertography and transperineal sonography often yield P–P distances that differ markedly from those measured via distal colostography or intraoperatively, raising questions about their accuracy in assessing the pouch–perineum distance [19]. For fistulas opening on the penis or scrotum, a high P–P distance may indicate an increased likelihood of unsuccessful distal rectal pouch identification during primary repair.

No consensus exists on whether colostomy is necessary for rectovestibular fistula. Karakus et al. reported no statistically significant differences in postoperative wound infection or dehiscence between single-stage and staged repair [5]. Other reports have documented satisfactory short-term outcomes and long-term bowel function following primary repair [6–10]. Nonetheless, severe complications, including wound infection, wound dehiscence, and rectal mucosal retraction, have been observed in some patients after primary repair. This study retrospectively reviewed the reasons for colostomy in rectovestibular fistula patients managed at our hospital over the past decade. Before 2018, all patients underwent initial colostomy to mitigate the risk of postoperative wound infection and dehiscence. Since 2018, we have adopted a selective approach, performing primary repair in some cases, while others received colostomy. In patients with poor general condition, severe congenital malformations, or a narrow fistula calibration accompanied by defecatory difficulty, colostomy is the treatment of choice. When the fistula lies near the posterior commissure, primary repair necessitates reconstruction of the perineal body, which increases the substantial risk of postoperative wound infection or dehiscence; in such female cases, initial colostomy is advisable. In neonates presenting with abdominal distension, vomiting, or defecatory difficulty, initial colostomy is recommended. In the absence of obvious abdominal signs, it is also recommended that surgeons in primary hospitals perform colostomy or fistula dilation first, followed by delayed surgery. In primary hospitals, if the surgeon is not confident in performing single-stage repair, colostomy should be performed first to avoid irreversible damage to the patient. For patients whose fistula location cannot be clearly identified before surgery, careful physical examination or initial colostomy is recommended.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis, incomplete clinical records for some patients may have influenced the categorization of colostomy indications. Second, as a single-center study, the categorization of indications for colostomy may not be fully representative. Additionally, the clinical experience summarized here may not be directly generalizable to all regions or settings. Decision-making should be adapted to local clinical contexts, and future multicenter research is warranted to confirm and refine these findings.

Conclusions

Colostomy is uncommonly performed in ARM patients with rectoperineal fistula. For male infants with visible fistulas, if the fistula is located on the penis or scrotum and the P-P distance is high, primary repair carries the risk of failed exploration of the rectal pouch and potential urethral injury, so preliminary colostomy should be considered. For male infants without visible fistulas at birth or 24 h after birth, colostomy is unavoidable if the P-P distance is high. A staged surgical procedure is recommended for patients with rectovestibular fistula in cases of poor general condition, severe concomitant congenital malformations, unresolved defecation difficulties following anal dilatation, or a fistula near the posterior commissure.
