Robust phase unwrapping for Dixon magnetic resonance imaging multiresolution pixel clustering and local surface fitting
Authors: Shuyu Cheng, Piaoran Li, Xufeng Zheng, Junying Cheng, Songsong Sun, Jianhua Ma, Yanqiu Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWater-fat separation plays a crucial role in clinical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations. However, the Dixon technique is encumbered by the challenge of water-fat swapping due to factors such as magnetic field inhomogeneity and low image signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). This study aimed to develop a multiresolution pixel clustering and local surface fitting (MrCLOSE) phase-unwrapping method to improve the robustness of water-fat separation in two-point Dixon MRI.MethodsMrCLOSE first constructs a multiresolution pyramid via acquired in-phase and out-of-phase complex MRI. It then performs hierarchical phase unwrapping with pixel clustering and local surface fitting (CLOSE) within a coarse-to-fine multiresolution framework. The performance of MrCLOSE was systematically evaluated and compared with those of B0 mapping with magnitude-based correction for bipolar two-point Dixon (B0-NICEbd), rapid opensource minimum spanning tree algorithm (ROMEO), Graph Cut, and the original CLOSE algorithm with both simulation and in vivo datasets.ResultsIn the simulation data, MrCLOSE achieved robust phase unwrapping under different thresholds, varying SNRs, disconnected regions, and phase changes from 4π to 10π. In the threshold-dependence simulation, CLOSE showed high error ratios of 43.71% and 94.74% at thresholds of π/10 and π/5, whereas MrCLOSE reduced these values to 0.67% and 0.11%, respectively. In additional simulations involving varying SNRs, disconnected regions, and increasing phase changes, MrCLOSE consistently maintained low error ratios and outperformed ROMEO and Graph Cut. In the 355 in vivo MR images acquired from the ankle, lumbar spine, pelvis, and knee, the overall water-fat swap ratio was 3.38% for MrCLOSE, while it was 17.75% for B0-NICEbd, 96.34% for ROMEO, 67.61% for Graph Cut, and 16.34% for CLOSE.ConclusionsBy innovatively integrating multiresolution strategies into CLOSE, MrCLOSE achieves high robustness and accuracy in phase unwrapping and provides a promising solution for phase-related MRI applications, particularly for water-fat separation in clinical scenarios.

Introduction

In magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), the phase map reflects variations in the main magnetic field (B0) inhomogeneity and is widely employed in applications such as water-fat separation (1-7), phase-contrast angiography (8-10), susceptibility-weighted imaging (11-14), and therapeutic temperature monitoring (15-18). Theoretically, phase values are calculated via the arctangent function, whose range is (−π, π). When the true phase exceeds this range, the calculated value corresponds to the true phase plus or minus an integer multiple of 2π, resulting in the wrapping of the true phase into the interval (−π, π). This manifests as discontinuities or abrupt phase jumps in the calculated phase map. Therefore, phase unwrapping is typically required to recover the true phase for phase-dependent MRI applications (19-21).

Under the assumption that the true phase is spatially continuous or smooth, phase unwrapping is performed through the addition or subtraction of integer multiples of 2π to each pixel to restore the true phase. In MRI applications, phase unwrapping involves limitations such as severe noise, rapidly varying phases, and disconnected regions (22). To address these issues, various methods have been proposed, including region-growing (23) and graph-cut approaches (24). However, region-growing methods entail difficulties in the selection of reliable initial seed pixels, and their performance in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) regions is unstable, often resulting in error accumulation during propagation (23). The graph-cut method formulates phase unwrapping as an energy minimization problem and mitigates the limitations of local optimization by identifying a global minimum through graph-cut optimization. However, this strategy lacks robustness when applied to data with severe noise, as noise obscures the true/false minima of the residual function and undermines the effectiveness of spatial smoothness constraints (25).

Block-based phase unwrapping methods (22,26-30) first partition phase maps into subregions, perform intrablock unwrapping, and subsequently integrate these blocks via region growing. Cheng et al. (22) developed a phase-unwrapping algorithm based on pixel clustering and local surface fitting (CLOSE). This method initially classifies pixels into easy-to-unwrap blocks and difficult-to-unwrap residual pixels by threshold. It then sequentially performs intrablock, interblock, and residual-pixel phase unwrapping using a region-growing local polynomial surface fitting (LPSF) method. By prioritizing the unwrapping of smooth regions, this strategy reduces error propagation. CLOSE demonstrated robust performance even in scenarios involving severe noise, rapid phase variations, and disconnected regions. However, the performance of CLOSE is sensitive to the threshold used for clustering pixels into blocks. A high threshold may produce excessively large blocks, increasing the likelihood of intrablock phase-unwrapping failure, whereas a small threshold may result in greater separation between (sub)blocks and a higher risk of inter-(sub)block unwrapping failure. In practice, this threshold typically requires manual adjustment for different applications (22). Additionally, identifying an optimal threshold is often challenging in scenarios involving complex anatomical structures and/or severe B0 inhomogeneity.

Incorporating multiresolution strategies into phase unwrapping and water-fat separation has been shown to improve performance (7,24,31). In these approaches, initial field maps are embedded within a pyramid structure and processed with a coarse-to-fine progressive approximation, in which low-resolution results serve as references to guide higher-resolution computations. Although multiresolution strategies do not guarantee a global optimal solution, they significantly enhance robustness against local optima, implicitly preserve spatial continuity, and offer notable advantages in the processing of images affected by severe noise or disconnected regions.

The original CLOSE algorithm was developed for phase unwrapping in two- or three-point Dixon MRI. Therefore, our study focused on two-point T2-weighted fast-spin-echo Dixon imaging, in which robust phase unwrapping remains critical for reliable water-fat separation. We propose integrating a multiresolution strategy with the CLOSE phase-unwrapping method. The resulting method, multiresolution pixel clustering and local surface fitting (MrCLOSE), first constructs a multiresolution pyramid via the in-phase and out-of-phase complex MR images and subsequently implements coarse-to-fine phase unwrapping using CLOSE at progressively higher resolutions. The performance of the proposed MrCLOSE method was evaluated and compared with the original CLOSE algorithm and other phase-unwrapping approaches through both simulation and in vivo experiments. We present this article in accordance with the CLEAR reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-1-0106/rc).

Hierarchical phase-unwrapping based on CLOSE

Phase unwrapping is performed progressively using the CLOSE method, starting from the lowest-resolution layer (Level k−1) and proceeding to the highest-resolution layer (Level 1). The unwrapped phase from Level k−1 is first upsampled to the size of Level k and then used as an initialization reference for phase unwrapping at the current level. Specifically, the wrapped phase at Level k is adjusted via the addition or subtraction of an integer multiple of 2π under the guidance of the upsampled lower-resolution phase so as to obtain an initial phase-unwrapping result for the current level. In this sense, the lower-resolution result guides the higher-resolution unwrapping by providing a coarse but more stable estimate of the phase distribution and helping determine the appropriate 2π offset for the wrapped phase at the current level.

Notably, the interpolation involved in the downsampling and upsampling operations slightly alters the original data and may introduce local mismatch, especially near tissue boundaries or in regions with rapid spatial phase variation. Therefore, the upsampled phase cannot be directly used as the final phase estimate at the current level. CLOSE first classifies pixels into easy-to-unwrap blocks and difficult-to-unwrap residual pixels based on a threshold applied to the local variations of the true phase. Small blocks containing fewer pixels than a predefined threshold are also classified as residual pixels. In the experiments, the minimum block size of CLOSE was set to 50 (22). As image resolution decreases across layers, the minimum block size should be adjusted accordingly for each layer. For MrCLOSE, the minimum block size was scaled proportionally with decreasing resolution across layers: 50 for the highest-resolution layer, 25 for the middle-resolution layer, and 12 for the lowest-resolution layer. Phase unwrapping was then performed sequentially on intrablock, interblock, and residual pixels via a region-growing LPSF method. Additional implementation details of CLOSE can be found in Cheng et al. (22).

In vivo data acquisition

The ankle, lumbar spine, pelvis, and knee data used in this study were retrospectively collected from Yuyao Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Ningbo, China) and were acquired on the ScintCare BroadScan 1.5T MR scanner (Xingaoyi Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Ningbo, China) under a research protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of Yuyao Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Ningbo, China (No. 2025-11-017). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the analysis. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

A two-point fast-spin-echo Dixon sequence was used to obtained images of the sagittal ankle [repetition time/first echo time/ second echo time (TR/TE1/TE2) =2,060/45.6/47.9 ms, slice thickness =3.0 mm, field of view (FOV) =190×190 mm2, acquisition matrix size =280×210, echo train length =7, bandwidth =80 kHz], the sagittal lumbar spine (TR/TE1/TE2 =2,180/91.2/93.5 ms, slice thickness =4.0 mm, FOV =280×280 mm2, acquisition matrix size =280×180, echo train length =15, bandwidth =80 kHz), the axial pelvis (TR/TE1/TE2 =3,300/51/53.3 ms, slice thickness =4.0 mm, FOV =340×340 mm2, acquisition matrix size =320×238, echo train length =9, bandwidth = 100 kHz, and the axial knee (TR/TE1/TE2 =1,900/43.8/46.1 ms, slice thickness =4.0 mm, FOV =180×180 mm², acquisition matrix size =280×170, echo train length =5, bandwidth =80 kHz).

The lumbar spine and pelvis were scanned with a 16-channel body coil, the knee was scanned with an 8-channel knee coil, and the ankle was scanned with an 8-channel foot coil. All coils were manufactured by Xingaoyi Medical Equipment Co., Ltd.

Implementation

All datasets used in this study were acquired as two-dimensional image series, and all phase-unwrapping methods were applied on a slice-by-slice basis. The same two-dimensional slice-wise processing framework was used for MrCLOSE, CLOSE, B0 mapping with magnitude-based correction for bipolar two-point Dixon (B0-NICEbd), rapid opensource minimum spanning tree algorithm (ROMEO), and Graph Cut to ensure a fair comparison. All postprocessing was implemented in MATLAB (R2023a; MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Both MrCLOSE and CLOSE excluded background pixels to improve computational efficiency, with masks generated via the edge detection function in MATLAB (32). Runtime evaluation was performed on a computer (ASUS, Taipei) equipped with a Ryzen 9 7940HX CPU (AMD, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and 16 GB of RAM. For in vivo data, a threshold value of π/4 was applied for pixel clustering to distinguish subblocks from residual pixels. The minimum block size in CLOSE was set to 50 pixels, whereas in MrCLOSE, it was set to 12 pixels for the lowest-resolution layer, 25 pixels for the middle-resolution layer, and 50 pixels for the highest-resolution layer. For comparative evaluation, B0-NICEbd (33), ROMEO (34), and Graph Cut (35-37) were also applied with their default parameters. Notably, ROMEO provides an option tailored for datasets with disconnected regions. For subsequent simulation experiments involving datasets with such disconnected regions, we enabled the “correct-regions” parameter in the corresponding MATLAB function as part of the experimental workflow.

In the simulation, the error ratio was calculated as the proportion of wrapped pixels relative to the total number of pixels within the region of interest, providing a quantitative evaluation of phase-unwrapping performance. For in vivo data, residual errors in the unwrapped phase images can lead to water-fat swaps. The water-fat separation results for each slice in every dataset were evaluated by an expert radiologist using a three-point scale, which included (I) many swaps; (II) few swaps; and (III) no swaps. Many swaps indicated extensive or multiple visually apparent swap regions within a slice, whereas few swaps indicated limited or focal swap regions. For quantitative comparison, the error ratio was defined via a binary criterion and calculated as the percentage of slices exhibiting any swap artifact, with instances of both many swaps and few swaps counted as swap slices. The same water-fat separation equations and reconstruction procedure were applied to all investigated phase-unwrapping methods after phase unwrapping.

Results

Simulation

Figure 2 presents the unwrapped results of the simulated data for CLOSE and MrCLOSE under different threshold values. At thresholds of π/10 and π/5, CLOSE produced inaccurate unwrapping, with error ratios of 43.71% and 94.74%, respectively. In contrast, MrCLOSE yielded results with negligible residual phase wrapping, with error ratios of 0.67% and 0.11%, respectively. At higher thresholds of π/4 and π/3, both CLOSE and MrCLOSE produced phase maps without any obvious residual wrapping, with error ratio of 0.05% and 0.02%, respectively.

As shown in Figure 3, MrCLOSE, with minimum block sizes (12, 25, and 50) set for the low-resolution to high-resolution layers, yielded superior unwrapped results across various phase variations. Unwrapping errors were primarily observed in low-SNR regions. For the configuration of 3, 12, and 50, the error ratios were 0.04%, 0.07%, 0.08%, and 0.11% under maximum phase changes of 4π, 6π, 8π, and 10π, respectively; meanwhile, for the configuration of 12, 25, and 50, the corresponding error ratios were 0.02%, 0.03%, 0.01%, and 0.01%, confirming that the latter configuration provided better unwrapping performance. Comparisons of MrCLOSE with Graph Cut, ROMEO, and CLOSE under different phase-change levels, discontinuous regions, and varying SNRs are presented in Figures S2,S3. In the presence of discontinuous regions and varying SNRs, ROMEO and Graph Cut produced error ratios of 29.67% and 24.79%, respectively, whereas CLOSE and MrCLOSE achieved much lower error ratios of 0.02% and 0.01%, respectively. Under phase-change levels of 4π, 6π, 8π, and 10π, MrCLOSE consistently yielded low error ratios of 0.02%, 0.03%, 0.01%, and 0.01%, respectively, which were comparable to or slightly better than those of CLOSE and markedly lower than those of ROMEO and Graph Cut. These findings further demonstrate the robustness of MrCLOSE under challenging simulation conditions.

Additional examples of the phase-unwrapping process are shown in Figures S4,S5. Figure S4 presents a representative failure case of CLOSE on a sagittal lumbar spine slice. In CLOSE, pixels are clustered into smooth blocks and residual pixels according to the local difference of true phase (LDTP) or local difference of wrapped phase (LDWP). When the clustering threshold is low, numerous small blocks may be generated, which increases interblock distances and may lead to interblock unwrapping failure owing to insufficient or excessively distant pixels for surface fitting. As shown in Figure S4, interblock unwrapping begins from the largest block and sequentially unwraps its nearest neighboring blocks via region-growing LPSF. In this figure, the block indicated by the white arrow is excessively distant from the previously unwrapped blocks, resulting in an insufficient number of valid fitting points for LPSF and subsequent unwrapping failure. These errors then propagate to neighboring blocks and eventually produce widespread unwrapping errors.

By contrast, Figure S5 shows the phase-unwrapping process of MrCLOSE on the same sagittal lumbar spine slice. In MrCLOSE, errors may still occur during interblock phase unwrapping at a lower-resolution level because of the excessive distances between blocks, as indicated by the white arrow in Level 1. However, after upsampling and refinement at the next level, LDTP clustering generates larger unwrapped blocks with shorter interblock distances at the same location, as indicated by the red rectangles in Levels 1 and 2 of Figure S5. Consequently, errors present at Level 1 are corrected during phase unwrapping at Level 2. These examples further illustrate the advantage of the coarse-to-fine framework in improving phase-unwrapping robustness.

Discussion

This study developed a novel phase-unwrapping method by integrating a multiresolution image pyramid into the CLOSE algorithm. The proposed MrCLOSE method first constructs a multiresolution pyramid from in-phase and out-of-phase MRI complex images and then performs hierarchical coarse-to-fine phase unwrapping using CLOSE. Simulation experiments demonstrated that MrCLOSE outperforms the original CLOSE algorithm, producing more accurate and robust phase-unwrapping results across different clustering thresholds. In vivo experiments further demonstrated that MrCLOSE provides more robust performance than does CLOSE in water-fat separation, producing water and fat images with substantially reduced swap ratios.

The improved performance of MrCLOSE compared with the original CLOSE method can be attributed to the incorporation of the multiresolution pyramid structure. In the original CLOSE algorithm, excessively high clustering thresholds may increase the risk of intrablock unwrapping failure, whereas excessively low thresholds may produce many small blocks and large interblock distances, thereby increasing the likelihood of fitting failure during interblock unwrapping. By contrast, the multiresolution framework in MrCLOSE provides smoother low-resolution representations and allowed for the phase unwrapping to be progressively refined from low to high resolution. This coarse-to-fine strategy improves robustness against block separation and local fitting failure, particularly in challenging regions with complex anatomy, severe field inhomogeneity, or disconnected structures. Although advanced multiecho Dixon techniques (38-40), such as six-point Dixon methods, can reduce phase swaps and improve the robustness of water-fat separation, the purpose of this study was not to compare different Dixon acquisition schemes. Instead, it aimed to improve the robustness and reduce the threshold dependence of the original CLOSE phase-unwrapping method for two-point T2-weighted fast-spin-echo Dixon MRI. The datasets used in this study were acquired with a T2-weighted fast-spin-echo Dixon sequence. One advantage of this sequence is that, in addition to the separated water and fat images, the acquired in-phase and out-of-phase images also provide useful tissue T2 contrast, allowing four sets of images to be obtained in a single scan. In contrast, the advanced multiecho Dixon techniques currently used in clinical practice are often implemented with three-dimensional spoiled gradient-echo-based sequences, which are well suited for multiecho acquisition but do not provide the same T2-weighted tissue contrast as does the fast-spin-echo Dixon sequence. Therefore, two-point T2-weighted fast-spin-echo Dixon and multiecho gradient-echo Dixon techniques have distinct advantages and clinical roles. For this reason, advanced acquisition strategies were not evaluated in the present study.

On a representative in vivo lumbar spine dataset consisting of 11 slices with a matrix size of 315×280, the average runtime of MrCLOSE was 3.45 s per slice on an ASUS computer equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 7940HX CPU and 16 GB of RAM. Limitations

The principal limitation of MrCLOSE is the number of pyramid layers. Using too few layers reduces the noise-suppression capability of the low-resolution layers, which may lead to residual phase wrapping in the unwrapped outputs. Conversely, an excessive number of layers significantly increases computational overhead, limiting clinical applicability. Additionally, our study focused on two-point T2-weighted fast-spin-echo Dixon MRI, and the performance of MrCLOSE was not evaluated with advanced multiecho Dixon acquisition schemes. Future work will focus on the adaptive selection of the number of pyramid layers and broader application scenarios, including anatomically challenging regions such as the neck and breast, and will further investigate the applicability of MrCLOSE to other Dixon acquisition schemes, including multiecho approaches.

Conclusions

In this study, a robust phase unwrapping method for Dixon MRI was developed through the incorporation of a multiresolution framework into pixel CLOSE. Compared with the original CLOSE method, MrCLOSE is less sensitive to the selection of the clustering threshold. Results from both simulation and in vivo experiments demonstrate that MrCLOSE can achieve high robustness and accuracy in phase unwrapping and water-fat separation. These findings suggest that MrCLOSE offers a promising solution for phase-related MRI applications.
