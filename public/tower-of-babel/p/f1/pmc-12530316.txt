Sweet relief or lasting impacts? Review of neonatal pain and sucrose in developing rodents
Authors: Fermin S. Hoq, Colette Newby, Sophie Tremblay, Manon Ranger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This review examines translational rodent models of repeated neonatal pain and sucrose exposure, highlighting neurodevelopmental effects and their relevance to clinical care in preterm infants.

1. Introduction

Preterm birth is the most significant perinatal challenge facing developed countries,28 with 10% of infants globally being born too early at 23 to 36 weeks' gestational age (GA).60 In recent decades, the survival rates of preterm infants have increased dramatically, but unfortunately complication-free survival has not.85 Preterm infants are usually cared for in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for weeks to months. During their NICU stay, when their brains are developing most rapidly, neonates typically experience 7 to 17 clinically required but painful and stressful procedures daily.25 These procedures can range from daily routine stressful events such as adhesive removals and diaper changes to more substantial painful interventions, including wound treatments, chest tube insertions, and lumbar punctures.93 Two of the most frequent skin-breaking painful procedures experienced by preterm neonates in the NICU are heel lances and venipunctures for blood collection.80,94 A recent review reported that preterm infants experience daily heel lances and venipuncture at rates of 100% and 97%, respectively.42 Given that these infants have immature biological systems and fragile brains unprepared to process the unexpected stimulation of the critical care environment, there is concern about the developmental consequences of such exposure during a period of critical neural development. This is particularly important because of their early experiences with pain/stress and their related treatments. Worldwide, oral sucrose is the standard treatment for minor procedural pain in the NICU95 because it yields observable calming effects on infant pain behaviors. Yet, there is a growing concern about the long-term effects of repetitive sucrose exposure, especially in the preterm population, and there is a need for mechanistic research to understand how sucrose interacts with pain to affect brain development.24,35,40,83

This review examines the impacts of early-life exposure to pain and sucrose on brain development and behavior in developing rodents. Research in rodents is especially relevant because rats and mice are born neurologically immature. Their first week of life serves as a good model for immature central nervous system development ex utero, being physiologically similar to preterm human neonates in the late second and early third trimesters.43

1.1. Developmental aspects of pain

Pain can be considered a primitive experience in neonates. In preterm infants, nociceptive activity is processed at lower processing levels of the central nervous system in regions of the brainstem and spinal cord.18,29 It has been established through electroencephalography (EEG) that preterm infants can perceive noxious stimuli beginning at 28 to 30 weeks of GA,45 with some components of this immature response being present only transiently while others appearing only near term-age.73 The afferent neurons that detect noxious stimuli and innervate the spinal cord are present as early as embryonic day 14 in rodents, and between the 8 and 19th week of gestation in humans.3,37 Importantly, functional thalamocortical connections are required for fetal awareness of noxious stimuli.18,29 Although thalamocortical connections begin around 24 weeks' gestation, these connections are functionally immature and remain in the subplate zone until 24 to 26 weeks' GA, when thalamocortical axons begin entering the cortical plate and creating synapses with layer IV cortical neurons. By 36 weeks, there is a dramatic increase in associative connections commencing with the cortex. As the nervous system develops, nociceptive signals reach higher-order processing and can be discriminated from other types of sensory inputs by around 35 weeks' GA in infants,27 and postnatal day (P) 14 in rats.18,22 Before this critical developmental milestone, preterm infants will have difficulties discerning between various types of sensory input, making them far more vulnerable to developmentally unexpected sensory stimulation of any kind. Together, this developmental timeline highlights how vulnerable the preterm infant is to pain because their nervous system is still maturing and not yet equipped to properly process or distinguish different types of sensory input.

1.2. Effects of procedural pain in human neonates

Early-life pain experiences have been linked to several adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm infants including diminished white matter tract integrity at term-equivalent and school age,19,90 reduced cerebellum, amygdala, and thalamic volumes at school age,23,69 reduced cortical thickness in the frontal and parietal lobes at 8 years,65 and altered brain connectivity in infancy.44,76 Early pain-related stress exposure in preterm infants has also been associated with downregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis activity33 and poorer cognition and motor function at 8 and 18 months of age.34 Furthermore, behavioral effects including reduced effortful control temperament in toddlers,31 greater internalizing (anxiety or depressive) behaviors,48,49 and difficulties with sensory processing at preschool age26 have been observed in those born preterm who experienced greater neonatal pain-related stress or pain frequency. Indeed, it has been substantiated through extensive and corroborating research that early-life pain exposure has negative outcomes on neurodevelopmental processes in preterm infants, especially those born extremely (<28 weeks' GA) or very prematurely (<32 weeks' GA). For more fulsome and extensive reviews, please see Refs. 16, 66, 75, 89. Given the susceptibility of this immature and highly vulnerable population to the detrimental consequences of recurring pain exposure, especially with their heightened sensitivity to pain,29 it is crucial to mitigate the experience of pain during hospitalization.

1.3. Sucrose as a procedural pain intervention in the neonatal intensive care unit

The optimal method of pain management is to reduce the total number of painful procedures experienced by neonates, but this may not be feasible for preterm infants in the NICU who receive medically required but painful procedures.93 To manage procedural pain, oral sucrose is considered the standard of care worldwide and is used as a nonpharmacological analgesic for minor procedural pain relief in the NICU (reviewed in Ref. 95). Newborns provided sucrose for painful procedures display lower mean pain scores than those provided with a placebo.86 Preterm infants given oral sucrose before a heel lance or venipuncture display significant reductions in crying time and in pain behaviors (pain scores) compared with infants provided no intervention or placebo. For a more fulsome review, see Yamada et al.95

In early studies investigating suckling- and sucrose-induced analgesia in human newborns, it has been suggested that sucrose mediates the effects of pain through endogenous opioid pathways, which are activated by sweet tasting solutions like sucrose.14 The belief of sucrose's effects by opioid mediation are based on taste-induced analgesia in humans and animal models. Unlike the transient calming effect of suckling, which ends once the pacifier is removed, sucrose provides a lasting calming effect that persists beyond ingestion.14,82 Newborn infants who were exposed to methadone during gestation failed to exhibit taste-induced analgesia (ie, a reduced behavioral pain response) and were not calmed by oral sucrose.11

Although oral sucrose administration in humans is suggested to dampen an infant's behavioral response to pain, the cerebral nociceptive response shows no such evidence.81 Evoked potentials assessed by electroencephalography (EEG) in newborn infants did not show a dampened nociceptive-specific regional response when oral sucrose was administered for a heel lance procedure.81 Therefore, despite reducing behavioral pain scores, it is possible that the calming effects of sucrose are related to its sedative properties rather than analgesic.81,95 Physiological stability and reduced cortisol levels fail to be achieved by using oral sucrose to treat procedural pain in the first week of life.17,95 Most significantly, oral sucrose administration for procedural pain in preterm infants was shown to increase plasma adenosine triphosphate oxidative stress markers, which may contribute to cellular injury.6 In more recent studies, 2 independent groups reported on the effects of cumulative sucrose exposure for pain mitigation on neurodevelopment in very preterm neonates (<32 weeks' GA) at 18-month corrected age.20,48 Both studies showed that cumulative sucrose exposure in very preterm neonates was not associated with neurodevelopmental delays or greater internalizing behaviors. Yet, sucrose administration in the context of procedural pain was not neuroprotective at 18-month corrected age. These findings were further supported by a recently published multisite study, which demonstrated that early-life cumulative sucrose exposure in very preterm infants failed to ameliorate the adverse effects of pain on internalizing behaviors at 18 months and showed no association with child behavior.50 More notably, a single dose of sucrose had a 4-fold negative effect on internalizing behaviors compared with that of one painful procedure. In a separate study, Johnston et al. found that in infants born between 25 and 31 weeks postconceptional age, a greater number of sucrose doses (>10 doses) predicted reduced alertness and orientation at 36 weeks of postnatal age (PNA) and poorer motor development and vigor at 36 and 40 weeks of PNA.40 More recently, greater exposure to glucose, another oral sweet-tasting solution, in very preterm neonates (<28 weeks GA) was associated with poorer mental and psychomotor development at 18-month corrected age.74 Collectively, these findings suggest that sucrose administration primarily decreases behavioral rather than physiological and cortical pain responses. Cumulative exposure to sucrose provides little benefit for pain mitigation and may even carry consequential risks to neurodevelopmental trajectories in preterm infants. Although sucrose remains a widely used intervention for procedural pain in neonates, its analgesic efficacy, long-term safety, and underlying mechanisms remain partly understood and complex, and merit ongoing investigation in both clinical and preclinical contexts.

The following section presents a comprehensive overview of preclinical rodent models investigating neonatal pain and sucrose, and their evolution over time. It highlights key advancements in these models, delineates the progression of their translational use in neonatal research, and identifies critical gaps that warrant ongoing investigation.

2. Translational animal models

Rodent models have been instrumental in advancing our understanding of the short-term and long-term effects of early-life pain, sucrose administration for pain relief, and their combined impact on the developing nervous system. Despite inherent species differences, the substantial similarities in pain processing and neurodevelopment between rodents and humans9,32,78 support the translational relevance of these models. Alongside the development of neonatal pain models, rodent studies have also explored sucrose as a nonpharmacological analgesic to assess its potential benefits and risks in neonatal care. This section outlines the evolution of these models, particularly those examining sucrose administration for procedural pain since the late 1990s. Key findings are presented in Sections 2.3 and 2.4.

Preclinical evidence suggests that sucrose's analgesic effects are mediated, at least in part, by activation of the endogenous opioid system. Blockade of sucrose-induced analgesia by naloxone and naloxonazine implicates opioid receptor involvement, particularly μ-opioid receptors.4,10,30,70 However, sucrose may primarily dampen the acute behavioral expression of pain without engaging broader neuroprotective pathways that prevent long-term structural and functional consequences. This raises the possibility that its effects are limited to short-term nociceptive modulation rather than influencing developmental brain trajectories. These mechanisms and their implications are further discussed in Sections 2.3 and 2.4.

2.1. Animal models of procedural pain exposure

When rats and mice are born, they are neurologically immature and completely dependent on maternal care for survival.9 The first week of brain development in rodents (P0–P7) corresponds to preterm cerebellar, striatum, limbic, and glial development in the human neonate from 24 to 32 weeks of GA,9,78 and very similar to full-term infants in myelination stage and developmental cell death in mice.43 Researchers agree that in their first week of life, rat pups correspond to premature human neonates, whereas those aged 2 weeks correspond to mature human neonates.79 The neurodevelopmental trajectory and maternal dependency of rodents in their first week of life mimic development in preterm infants, making them a reliable preclinical model to study conditions in human preterm neonates.88 The parallel neurodevelopmental trajectory between mice and preterm infants posits the use of rodents as animal models to explore the consequences of early-life adversity on the developing brain.

Animal models of early-life adversity have been created to reproduce early-life stress experienced by human neonates. Several such rodent models exist, including prenatal stress exposure,46 chronic early-life stress,36 limited bedding and nesting,91 prenatal alcohol exposure,15,63 and chronic or periodic maternal separation.51,55 Importantly, exposure to neonatal procedural pain in rodent pups provides a translational paradigm that mimics procedural pain experienced by critically ill infants in the NICU, specifically with heel lances.53 Here we provide an overview of the development of neonatal pain models in rodents, detailing their adaptation into a translational preterm neonatal model for procedural pain exposure.

The first group to pioneer research on neonatal pain in rodents were Ren et al.71 Their rodent model of pain induced inflammation and hyperalgesia through the use of complete Freund adjuvant (CFA) in the dorsal forepaw or hind paw. Although not aimed at investigating common neonatal painful procedures such as heel-lance and venipuncture, their model served to reproduce the persistent response to tissue injury as a consequence of surgical intervention in human infants. Anand et al. continued this initial work and advanced the field of neonatal pain; it is often cited as the preclinical neonatal pain model.1 Unlike Ren et al.,71 research by Anand's group aimed to more closely mimic a preterm neonate's experience to heel lance in the NICU rather than inflammation-induced surgical intervention and traumatic tissue injury in this population. Using a neonatal rat model, they hypothesized that repetitive neonatal pain exposure results in permanent or long-term alterations in the somatosensory cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala because of the developmental plasticity of the immature brain. Neonatal Sprague-Dawley rat pups were administered pain using a 25-gauge (G) needle inserted rapidly through the paw pads 1, 2, or 4 times per day from birth to postnatal day (P)7. Pain thresholds were assessed using a hot-plate test on P16, P22, and P65. In a subsequent study, Long-Evans hooded rat pups were subjected to single or repetitive subcutaneous formalin injections as an inflammation-inducing event while receiving ketamine.2 Rat pups received 5 μL of 4% formalin injections in their right forepaw on P1, P7, or P14 or hourly injections into each paw from P1 to P4, once per paw. Pups assigned to the ketamine group received interscapular ketamine (2.5 mg/kg × 2) alone or 5 minutes before formalin injections. Building on these studies, Nuseir et al. conducted further investigations, in which Wistar rat pups had their paws either stroked with a cotton-tipped swab or punctured with a 25-G needle 4 times per day at hourly intervals for 8 weeks starting at birth.57 The authors aimed to investigate the effects of chronic pain exposure from birth to adolescence on memory and molecular brain alterations.

Mooney-Leber et al. modified previous neonatal pain models to better mimic NICU experiences.54 Here, Sprague-Dawley rat pups were exposed to repetitive needle (26 G) paw insertions 4 times per day from P1 to P4. Compared with earlier pain models where painful events were limited to 4 per day, this group modified the frequency of painful procedures administered over the course of the study, with 8 painful events on P1, 6 on P2, 4 on P3, and 4 on P4.54,87 As postnatal ages increased, the number of painful procedures experienced were reduced to reproduce the decreasing rate of painful events experienced by neonates during the course of their NICU stay.

Similar to Mooney-Leber et al.,54 Grunau and Goldowitz's groups68,88 constructed a translational model of neonatal pain to accurately reflect the mode and frequency of pain exposure experienced by infants in the NICU born prematurely, this time in C57BL/6J mice. Mouse pups were exposed to 24% oral sucrose 2 minutes before needle-prick (pain) exposure on paw pads from P1 to P6. Parallel to Anand's model of neonatal pain exposure,1 the model focused on early-life pain exposure and its effects on long-term brain volumes and functional outcomes by exposing rodent pups to pain during developmental periods associated with preterm human development. One unique aspect of the model was the number of painful interventions experienced. Unlike Anand et al.,1 and subsequent neonatal preclinical pain exposure studies described above that exposed rat pups to a maximum of 4 needle-pricks per day, Grunau and Goldowitz's groups exposed mouse pups to 10 needle-pricks daily from P1 to P6. This increased pain exposure frequency more accurately depicts the frequency of painful procedures because it better reflects the 7 to 17 painful procedures experienced by preterm infants in the NICU.25 In addition, the timeline in which the painful procedures were administered more accurately represents the neurodevelopmental period in which premature infants experience heel lance and other blood-drawing procedures, as P0–P7 in rodent models is developmentally parallel to neurodevelopment in the human preterm neonate from 24 to 32 weeks of GA.9,78 Another important difference relates to the far more invasive painful stimulus, where a 25- or 26-G needle was inserted through the paw1,54,57,58,87 in comparison with a more superficial prick with a 30-G needle.68,88 The use of a superficial injury more accurately reflects the experience of neonates with heel lance procedures in the NICU.

2.2. Animal models of neonatal sucrose exposure in the neonatal intensive care unit

To better understand the mechanisms and consequences of sucrose-mediated pain relief in neonates, several rodent models have been developed; this section outlines the evolution of these models and their contributions to the field.

Blass et al. were the first research group to examine sucrose administration in Sprague-Dawley rat pups.10 Early work with Blass' group aimed to investigate an association between sucrose and opioid-mediated pathways for early-life pain and comforting distress during isolation. They particularly aimed to establish an opioid-based functional connection between sucrose, pain thresholds, and distress vocalizations. P10 Sprague-Dawley rat pups were provided with intraoral infusions of one of 4 substances: 3.5% sucrose, 7.5% sucrose, 11.5% sucrose, or distilled water (control) before paw lift latency testing on a hot plate. Intraoral infusions were provided through the insertion of 2 cannulae: one placed in the rat's lower jaw and the other inserted beneath the animal's tongue and out through the ventral surface of the jaw. In a subsequent study, Blass and Shide continued examining the effects of sucrose and other sweet solutions on pain thresholds and ultrasonic vocalizations (distress vocalizations) in P10 Sprague-Dawley rat pups.12 Using the same intraoral infusion methodology as previously described, 1% of the rat pup's body weight was used to determine the volume of sucrose, fructose, glucose, or lactose solution provided (concentrations ranging from 0.22 to 0.66 M). Later, Ren et al. used a similar sucrose administration procedure in rat pups, where 7.5%, 0.22 M sucrose solution was delivered using an infusion pump at a flow rate of 0.01 to 0.06 mL/minute.71

Anseloni et al. continued developing a translational model of sucrose administration for minor pain to investigate the critical period in which rats may experience its analgesic effects.5 In this model, Sprague-Dawley rat pups were assigned to 1 of 5 groups: controls (no cannulation), cannulated with no infusion, distilled water infusion, 0.1 M ammonium chloride infusion, or 7.5% sucrose infusion delivered at 0.03 mL/minute. Similar to previous research,10,11,71 2 cannulae were implanted to provide the assigned solution. Testing of mechanical and thermal withdrawal responses took place from P0 to P14 and P17 to P21 shortly before and after solution infusions. In a subsequent study, authors aimed to better understand the neural circuitry underlying sucrose-induced analgesia in human and rodent neonates and used the same methodology of intraoral infusion of sucrose.4 Thus far, sucrose administration using a cannula was the most common method for testing intraoral sweet solutions in rodent models.

Although the described studies provide evidence for sucrose-induced analgesia for pain in rat pups (see below), it is challenging to discern through which pathway the effect is generated. Is sucrose providing its analgesic effect through developmentally mature antinociceptive brain structures (ie, midbrain periaqueductal gray and the rostroventromedial medullary region),4 or simply through the gustatory pathway which dampens behavioral pain responses? A P10 rodent model, as previously studied by Blass et al.10,11 and Anseloni et al.,4,5 will have bypassed the developmental trajectory of a preterm neonate.9,78 Thus, sucrose activation of these antinociceptive regions from a human preterm-equivalent standpoint (ie, P0–P7) remains unclear. Furthermore, to accurately replicate sucrose administration in the NICU setting, it should be noted that intraoral infusions using cannulae do not best depict the typical oral sucrose delivery method used in hospitalized preterm infants for minor painful procedures. In clinical practice, oral sucrose is administered using a syringe, dropper, or sucrose-dipped pacifier to provide 0.1 to 1 mL of sweet solution (reviewed in Ref. 95). Therefore, the described intraoral infusion methods may be considerably more invasive compared with standard NICU practice.

More recent work by Nuseir et al. replicate more closely the NICU reality.56–59 Wistar rat pups were orally administered 0.2 mL of 25% sucrose solution before receiving needle-pricks 4 times per day at hourly intervals from P0 to 8 weeks57 or P0 to P14.58 In a subsequent study, experimental protocol was initiated on P5 where the first group of animals received a saline injection, whereas rats in groups 2, 3, and 4 were injected with 20 μL of CFA into both hind paws.56 Five minutes after CFA injection, group 2 received no pain treatment, group 3 received ibuprofen, and group 4 received 0.5 mL of intraoral 25% sucrose. In their most recent work, Nuseir et al. used the same experimental protocol, but in Sprague-Dawley rat pups with a shorter exposure, lasting only 2 weeks (P0 to P14).59

Although models described thus far have used various strains of neonatal rats, in the last years, there has been a pain and sucrose model developed in neonatal mice. The first pertinent model of sucrose administration in the NICU setting was introduced by Grunau and Goldowitz's group.68,88 In this neonatal mouse model, sucrose dosages were calibrated to match those administered to preterm infants in the NICU. At the time these studies were designed and conducted, the standard guideline for very preterm infants (24–32 weeks GA) recommended 0.5 mL per dose, corresponding to 0.08–0.2 g of sucrose per kg body weight (Reviewed in 84). C57BL/6 J mouse pups were assigned to receive either sterile water or 24% sucrose (0.1–0.2 g/kg) orally (with micropipette) 10 times daily over a 10-hour period from P1 to P6. Two minutes after sucrose or water treatment, mice were exposed to one of 3 interventions: paw needle-prick with a 30 G needle without going through the paw, tactile stimulation with a cotton-tipped swab, or handling.68,88

2.3. Effects of early life pain and sucrose on brain development

Preclinical research has consistently shown that early-life pain has diverse immediate and lasting effects on neurobiological development. Early life pain has been shown to disrupt the stress response.1,52,54 It has been previously shown that rats exposed to early-life pain had slightly elevated baseline corticosterone levels on P4 but by adulthood, exhibited normal HPA axis responses to an air-puff startle.1 Furthermore, a more pronounced effect of neonatal pain was found in rats at P4, with significantly increased levels of serum corticosterone, which were heightened when coupled with maternal separation.54 Subsequent work indicated that these alterations in the stress system could persist into adulthood, evidenced by impaired HPA axis recovery to acute stressors in female rats between P79 and P126.52

Alongside changes in the stress response, early-life pain in rats has been shown to affect glutamate signaling, where pups exposed to neonatal pain showed decreased levels of glutamate in the frontal cortex at P4, an effect exacerbated by maternal separation.54 Furthermore, only rat pups subjected to neonatal pain displayed reduced glutamate in the hippocampus. These findings suggest that early-life pain can lead to significant neurochemical changes, potentially affecting cognitive and emotional functions later in life.

Early-life pain has also been found to alter both brain structure and function.1,2,7,57,58,87 Anand et al. reported rats subjected to early pain showed significantly lower expression of nucleus Fos in the somatosensory cortex at adulthood (P65) when exposed to a hot plate, suggesting altered sensory processing.1 Furthermore, Bhutta and Anand highlighted that neonatal pain exposure on P1 and P7, but not on P1, significantly increased the sprouting of peripheral nerves, hyperexcitability of dorsal horn neurons, and neuronal cell death in cortical areas such as the piriform, temporal, and occipital cortex.7 The authors proposed that N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors mediate excitotoxicity and apoptosis triggered by inflammatory cytokines or mitochondrial injury.7 This interference with spinal transmission of pain can lead to long-term effects such as hyperalgesia, allodynia, and central sensitization.7 Later, Anand et al. showed that repetitive neonatal pain increased neuronal excitation and cell death in various cortical and subcortical regions, including the amygdala and hippocampus, in rat pups at P1 and P4.2 Subsequent research by Nuseir's group explored more long-term effects into adulthood (∼P60) and found decreased levels of brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus in response to chronic early-life pain.57,58 Timmerman et al.87 investigated long-term alterations (between P104 and P126) in brain function by examining reelin, an extracellular protein essential for neuronal migration, dendritic growth, and synapse formation.41 In their study, sex-specific effects were found, where both neonatal pain and maternal isolation individually elevated reelin levels in the dentate gyrus of adult female rats.87 These results emphasize the importance of early interventions to prevent the adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes associated with early life pain.

Further studies have provided insights into pain modulation mechanisms, revealing that in inflamed P10 rats (induced by forepaw or hind paw CFA injections), nonnutritive suckling combined with intraoral sucrose resulted in a significant reduction in Fos-positive nuclei in the cervical dorsal horn of forepaw-inflamed rats; indicating that endogenous pain-modulatory effects are, at least in part, mediated at the spinal level.71 By contrast, Fos expression in the lumbar dorsal horn after hind paw inflammation was not significantly reduced by this treatment, and Fos expression on the contralateral spinal cord was unaffected.71 These findings underscore a differential rostrocaudal maturation of pain-modulating pathways, suggesting sucrose has a localized rather than systemic effect. Similarly, it has been demonstrated that sucrose-induced analgesia, marked by a reduction in mechanical withdrawal responses, is mediated by brainstem circuits and does not require the forebrain.4 Specifically, in P10 rats, sucrose activated neurons in the rostroventrolateral medulla, locus coeruleus, periaqueductal gray (PAG), and the nucleus raphe magnus—key sites involved in descending pain modulation. Moreover, sucrose-induced analgesia persisted even after a midcollicular transection, which separates the brainstem from the forebrain, suggesting that brainstem structures alone are sufficient for mediating sucrose-induced pain relief. This analgesic effect was shown to be opioid receptor-dependent, as naloxone inhibited the analgesic effects of sucrose.4

Expanding on these localized and central effects, sucrose administration before painful procedures in rat pups led to increased serum levels of β-endorphin and enkephalin (endogenous opioids) and prevented reductions in hippocampal BDNF in early adulthood.57 A follow-up study revealed that both sucrose or naltrexone administered from P0 to P14 could mitigate reductions in BDNF and increase serum levels of β-endorphin measured until P56.58 The somewhat surprising efficacy of naltrexone, a known opioid antagonist, in mitigating pain-related changes in the brain is suggested to be because of biphasic opioid responses in rats, with excitatory opioid responses during immaturity and inhibitory opioid responses in adulthood.58 By contrast, others have found long-term detrimental effects of sucrose exposure before painful procedures in a neonatal mouse model.88 Specifically, adult mice (P85–P95) exposed to neonatal sucrose had significantly smaller brain volumes in several white matter structures, irrespective of being exposed to pain (needle prick) or not. Thus, although sucrose demonstrates significant neurochemical pain-modulatory effects at both spinal and brainstem levels and promotes endogenous opioid production, its long-term structural impact on the brain necessitates careful consideration.

Together, these findings demonstrate the pervasive short-term and long-term detrimental effects of early-life pain on neurobiological development, stressing the importance of early and effective pain management, with consideration for possible side effects of sucrose on brain development. 2.4. Effects of pain and sucrose on behavior

Studies examining early-life pain alone have found both adaptive and maladaptive effects on rodent behavior. Although one study found that early-life pain enhanced spatial learning in adulthood, as shown in the Morris water maze (MWM) between P79 and P126,52 most studies report detrimental effects.1,8,72 For instance, P4 rats subjected to repetitive neonatal pain showed increased vigilance (social discrimination test) and anxiety (defensive withdrawal test) behaviors.1 Adult rats exposed to neonatal pain also display hyper-responsiveness to painful stimuli, such as a pinch and formalin injection, and reduced locomotor activity.8,72 Findings on alcohol preference remain inconsistent because neonatal pain has been associated with both an increase1 and a decrease in alcohol preference.8 Collectively, these findings suggest that although early-life pain may enhance certain cognitive functions, it more commonly leads to detrimental long-term sensory and behavioral alterations.

Numerous studies have investigated the analgesic effects of sucrose, where researchers generally agree that early-life pain decreases withdrawal latency in adulthood58 and sucrose administered before pain procedures reduces this effect.4,56–58,71 One popular method for measuring pain sensitivity is paw withdrawal latency to thermal plate or mechanical stimulation, where increased latency corresponds to decreased sensitivity to pain. In particular, it has been found that both 3.5% and 11.5% sucrose infusions increased paw withdrawal latencies as well as substantially reduced distress vocalizations in isolated P10 rats compared with controls, with naltrexone administration inhibiting the effects of sucrose.10 Similarly, in the tail-flick test, young and adult rats treated with oral sucrose showed reduced behavioral pain responses, an effect attenuated by naloxone and the μ1-opioid receptor antagonist naloxonazine, supporting the involvement of the endogenous opioid system in sucrose-induced analgesia.30,70 Subsequent investigations into the effects of different types of sugars on P10 rats found that sucrose, fructose, and glucose reduced ultrasonic vocalizations in rat pups to 50% of baseline levels and increased foot-withdrawal latency in response to the hot plate, while lactose had no effect.12 Interestingly, the dose-response functions for these effective sugars were flat,12 which aligns with the results seen in Ref. 10. This also parallels results in human neonates, where a total of 0.5 mL of 0.51 M sucrose, fructose, or glucose were effective calming agents for spontaneous crying in 1- to 3-day old human infants,13 indicating a minimal impact of dose on pain reducing effects.

In inflamed neonatal rats (CFA), the combination of nonnutritive suckling and oral sucrose markedly increased forepaw, but not hind limb, withdrawal latencies at P10.71 In addition, it has been found that there are age-dependent results, where inflamed neonatal rats given short-term intraoral sucrose demonstrated sucrose-induced analgesia for thermal stimuli at P3, peaking at P7 to P10 and declining until effects ceased by P17.5 When examining the same animals in response to mechanical stimuli, sucrose-induced analgesia was delayed until P13 in the hind paw compared with the forepaw, but analgesic effects were absent by P17 for both forepaw and hind paw mechanical stimulation.

Although some have found age-dependent effects of sucrose in pain modulation, others have found sucrose treatment before early-life pain modulates pain sensitivity well into adulthood.56–58 Specifically, neonatal rats exposed to chronic early-life pain exhibited decreased paw withdrawal latency at adulthood (P56), where the effect was significantly reduced in rats treated with sucrose or paracetamol57 and naltrexone and sucrose treatment alone or together,58 before pain procedures. A similar study found that sucrose was more effective than ibuprofen in preventing heightened sensitivity to pain (both hyperalgesia and allodynia) at P6 and P14, with analgesic effects lasting into adolescence and adulthood, at P28 and P56.56

Although early-life pain consistently heightens pain sensitivity, a recent study suggests that sucrose may not modulate pain sensitivity equally across different models and rodent species. It is noteworthy that the rat model used in the study by Nuseir et al. shifted from Wistar rats56–58 to Sprague-Dawley rats.59 This shift in rat strains may explain the new finding where heightened pain sensitivity in adult rats (P28 and P56) related to early-life pain was not mitigated by sucrose treatment in their latest study in Sprague-Dawley rats.59

Research further indicates that early-life pain can lead to short-term and long-term behavioral changes distinct from pain sensitivity, whereas sucrose treatment does not steadily mitigate this effect, and in some cases may be related to poor cognitive outcomes. In adult rats, early-life pain has been linked with short-term memory impairment, where sucrose has been shown to either worsen59 or improve short-term memory performance;57 and in others, sucrose combined with naltrexone prevented deficits.58 In addition, adult rats exposed to early-life pain exhibited heightened anxiety, which was not mitigated by sucrose treatment.59 Studies in adult mice have found that repetitive neonatal sucrose before needle-pricks did not protect against memory performance deficits, and instead, sucrose exposure alone led to poorer short-term memory and reduced sugar preference in adulthood.68 The effects of neonatal pain on long-term memory remain unclear. Long-term memory impairment in rats exposed to neonatal pain has been observed, with sucrose or naltrexone preventing impairment.58,59 However, other studies did not find neonatal pain-related long-term memory impairments, as indicated in rats using the Radial Arm Maze and Barnes test56,57 and in mice using the MWM.68

These findings collectively highlight the complex interactions between early-life pain, behavioral outcomes, and the potential role of sucrose in modulating these effects, demonstrating both therapeutic potential and limitations. 3. Clinical significance and future directions

The most common blood-drawing procedures encountered daily by preterm infants in the NICU are heel lances and venipuncture.42 On average, infants born preterm can experience 8 blood draws per day within the first week of life alone in comparison with full-term neonates, who experience an average of 0.7 blood draws daily.47,62 In the NICU, blood draws are used to monitor, diagnose, and manage potential illnesses, such as sepsis.61,80 These tests provide valuable information on the health of critically ill neonates and offer insight regarding necessary treatments if results deviate from within normal limits, making these painful procedures clinically required to provide adequate care for premature infants.

The greater frequency of painful procedures administered to preterm neonates contributes to a considerable burden of aversive stimuli in early life. Research has demonstrated that exposure to such procedures in these vulnerable infants is associated with altered basal nociceptive processing in infancy up to early adulthood.64,92 In human cohort studies from Grunau, Stevens, et al. found short- and long-term adverse outcomes in preterm infant brain development and behavior with early-life pain exposure, including slower maturation of neonatal structural connectivity, reduced regional brain volumes, and modified internalizing behaviors.19,23,48,65,67,69,76 Notably, parallel outcomes involving reduced regional brain volumes have also been reported in rodent models2 alongside various behavioral effects.1,52,68 Pain mitigation is a priority in this immature and vulnerable population, and its management is crucial to improve physiological stability and neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Oral sucrose is widely regarded as the standard of care for managing procedural pain in neonates and is routinely administered in the NICU for alleviating pain during minor procedures (reviewed in Ref. 95). However, to the best of our knowledge, preclinical and clinical research have yet to demonstrate that sucrose provides neuroprotective effects for procedural pain treatment. Recent work from 2 independent Canadian groups showed that cumulative sucrose exposure in very preterm infants was not neuroprotective at 18-month corrected age20,48,77 and even exacerbated the damaging effect of early pain exposure on neurodevelopmental outcomes.77 Several rodent studies on early-life pain and sucrose administration echo these findings.68,71,88 At present, randomized controlled clinical trials of sucrose for minor procedural pain management in preterm neonates in the NICU are unfeasible, as sucrose is considered the standard of care in NICUs worldwide.95 Yet, there is a growing concern about the long-term effects of repetitive sucrose exposure and clinicians must exercise caution with repeated oral sucrose administration as a nonpharmacological analgesic for neonatal procedural pain. There is a need for mechanistic research in understanding the interaction between sucrose and pain on brain development, especially in the preterm population.24,35,83 Despite these concerns, research in this area remains largely unexplored but is crucial in providing effective and neuroprotective treatments in this critically ill and fragile population. It remains essential to continue improving translational preclinical models to investigate both the short- and long-term effects of sucrose when used for minor pain management.

As clinical research in this field is limited, translational animal models are used to seek out parallel outcomes and better understand mechanistic pathways under circumstances presented to preterm infants in the NICU. Although preclinical models allow for the investigation of molecular alterations that cannot be as readily studied in humans, the lack of standardization between preclinical experimental designs in this research area makes comparisons between studies challenging. As described in this review, the few translational models of preterm infant pain and sucrose administration vary between research groups. Significant evidence on the use of oral sucrose in rodent models of neonatal pain adds to the growing concerns within the pediatric community regarding cumulative sucrose exposure for minor procedural pain treatment during early development. Results in rodent models of pain in combination with sucrose administration align with adverse clinical findings observed in human neonates.

It is crucial to acknowledge the inherent limitations of translational research. Despite the use of specifically developed paradigms designed to bridge preclinical and clinical investigations, direct extrapolation of findings using animal models to human clinical practice remains challenging. Differences in neurodevelopmental trajectories and higher-order processes, species-specific pain processing mechanisms, and environmental factors may contribute to discrepancies between preclinical and clinical outcomes. In addition to species differences, variables such as dosing regimens, the timing of sucrose exposure, and the presence of concurrent stressors in the NICU (eg, varied time away from the maternal figure) further complicate direct comparisons. Although rodent models provide valuable mechanistic insights on potential short- and long-term outcomes, further research integrating human-based studies is necessary to fully elucidate the implications of sucrose administration in neonatal pain management.

Effective pain management is essential for neonates, and clinical practice must continuously evolve to offer the most optimal care, especially for preterm neonates who experience statistically greater frequencies of pain. Investigating the use of oral sucrose for procedural pain, and its potential impacts on both immediate and long-term developmental outcomes, is critical. Longitudinal clinical studies are urgently needed to assess the effects of cumulative sucrose exposure for procedural pain in human infants. Future research in this area will provide valuable insights into the potential benefits, limitations, and lifelong implications of sucrose use for pain management in NICUs, ultimately guiding safer, evidence-based practices for preterm infants and their families in Canada and worldwide. Alternatively, mother-centred, touch-based behavioral treatments are powerful mediators of neonatal procedural pain,21 with skin-to-skin care considered the most effective.38,39 Clinicians may opt to use touch-based interventions for procedural pain in neonates, and future research could examine how combining sucrose with those human-centred therapies might enhance analgesic effects, optimize neurodevelopmental outcomes, and support parental involvement in neonatal care.

By examining the mechanistic pathways associated with outcomes observed in both human preterm infants and translational rodent models, research can build on existing knowledge and enhance our understanding of neurodevelopmental trajectories associated with early pain management interventions. Over time, these findings will drive improvements in NICU care, inform healthcare policy, and elevate the standard of care, ultimately benefiting millions of critically ill infants who require intensive care globally each year.
