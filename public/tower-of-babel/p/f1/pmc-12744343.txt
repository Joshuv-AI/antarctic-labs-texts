The Black Soldier Fly Hermetia illucens Larva Presents an Antimicrobial Activity in Response to Clostridioides difficile Exposure

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) have been categorized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the three greatest public health threats of the 21st century, mainly due to the increased rates of ARB infections alongside limited treatment options (O’Neill, 2016) . While most ARB were first identified in healthcare facilities, they are currently also prevalent in the community. ARB infections are associated with a great economic burden. According to a recent study, the associated costs range from US$627 to US$768 billion (median value of US$693 billion) (Naylor et al., 2025).

(O’Neill, 2016). Antibiotic resistance considerably challenges the efficacy of infectious disease treatment. Despite the urgent clinical need, development of new antimicrobial agents has dwindled in recent years due to the low costs of current antibiotic treatment, exhaustion of the antibiotic potential, and high development costs of new treatments versus the anticipated low sales margins. Therefore, new cost-effective and low-resistance antimicrobial drugs are a priority (Hirsch et al., 2019).

One new strategy to combat resistant pathogens uses antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), oligopeptides with five to over 100 amino acids that can be found in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes (Wu et al., 2018). Various mechanisms mediate AMPs antibacterial activity, including membrane disruption, interference with bacterial metabolism and targeting of cytoplasmic components (Wu et al., 2018) . These peptides are expected to provide a versatile, cost-effective, and low-resistance solution for ARBs (Xia et al., 2021.

Insects are one of the major AMP-producing organisms, as AMPs provide them the first line of defense against various pathogens (Wu et al., 2018). Some of the insect AMPs are generated in an organ called the fat body and are secreted to the hemolymph. These peptides most likely function as part of the immune system response to infections (Alvarez et al., 2019). To date, the black soldier fly Hermetia illucens (BSF) has the most diverse and rich immune system among insects, with an unprecedented high number of predicted AMP genes (Zhan et al., 2020). The BSF larvae (BSFL) consume decomposing organic material, which is typically contains various microorganisms. This exposure to pathogens may be the reason for the remarkable AMPS production by BSF (Müller et al., 2017).

Non-immunized BSF lysates contain AMPs effective against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, bacterial biofilms and fungi (Müller et al., 2017) . Several studies have shown the feasibility of BSF AMPs in treating several human pathogens (Li et al., 2017; Park and Yoe, 2017a; Alvarez et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2021; Qosimah et al., 2023; Franco et al., 2024). For example, the BSF cecropin-like peptide 1 (CLP1) exhibited bactericidal activity against the Gram-negative bacteria Escherichia coli E. coli, Enterobacter aerogenes and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Park and Yoe, 2017a).

Nowadays, BSF is used to recycle various types of organic waste and for biodiesel production (Müller et al., 2017). BSF is the leader of the nascent insect protein industry relying on circular economy model of production using sustainable sources (Odongo et al., 2024). Combination of low mass-production costs alongside wide repertoire of naturally occurring antimicrobials, may position BSF as a main and sustainable source of novel antibiotics in the next decades.

To the best of our knowledge, the BSFL hemolymph and AMPs activities against Clostridioides difficile C. difficile, a common ARB and the primary cause of nosocomial diarrhea (Al-Zahrani, 2023), have never been reported. C. difficile is a spore-forming bacterium. Its highly durable spores survive on surfaces for long periods and are highly transmissible from person to person via the fecal-oral route. Once colonized in the host large intestine, the spores germinate to vegetative cells which produce toxins (Al-Zahrani, 2023). These toxins are glucosyltransferases, targeting Rho proteins, which are small GTPases, the master regulators of the actin cytoskeleton. Rho glycosylation by C. difficile's toxins results in the disappearance of actin filament, peripheral membrane ruffling, filopodial extensions, disorganization of focal complexes and ultimately to the complete loss of cell shape (reviewed at (Chandrasekaran and Lacy, 2017)).

This study aimed to investigate whether BSFL hemolymph has an antimicrobial effect against clinical C. difficile isolates, and to determine the effect of C. difficile exposure mode (feeding with infected food versus injection) on BSFL antimicrobial activity. Furthermore, it aimed to identify the specific AMPs that contribute to antibacterial activity against C. difficile.

Materials and methods

Insect growth

Fourth instar larvae were purchased from Entoprotech Ltd. and maintained under stable conditions at 28 ± 2 °C, photoperiod of 12 h per day, 70 % relative humidity. The larvae were fed with brewer's grains medium.Bacterial Growth for BSFL Exposure Clostridioides difficile BAA-1382 strain 630 (a toxin-producer strain) and BAA-1801 (a non- toxin-producer strain) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The strains were grown on Brucella blood agar (Hy Laboratories Ltd., Rehovot, Israel) at 37 °C, for 48 h, in a Bactron EZ 300 anaerobic chamber (Sheldon Manufacturing, Cornelius, USA). Thereafter, bacterial colonies were suspended in brain-heart infusion broth supplemented with yeast extract and 0.1 % l-cysteine (BHIS) (Hy Laboratories Ltd.) and incubated overnight, at 37 °C, in an anaerobic chamber to reach the logarithmic phase. The bacterial suspension was diluted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity.

BSFL exposure to C. difficile

The BSFL were exposed to C. difficile by injection ("Injection group") or feeding with C. difficile-infected food ("Feeding group") (Figure S1). For the Injection group, 10 µl of an overnight culture of BAA-1382 strain 630 (0.5 McFarland) was injected into the larva anus using a 100 µl needle (Hamilton, Energy Way Reno, NV, USA). The Feeding group were fed with C. difficile-infected brewer's grains medium (1 ml bacterial suspension at 0.5 McFarland per 1-gram larvae weight). One control group was treated with 10-µl BHIS medium and two other injected or fed with a non-toxic C. difficile strain (ATCC BAA-1801, 0.5 McFarland, 10 µl).

Following treatment, BSFL were incubated for an additional 24 h under the same conditions described above. Thereafter, hemolymph was extracted for further analysis.

Hemolymph extraction

Hemolymph was collected according to a previously reported protocol (Hetru and Bulet, 1997) (Fig. S2). In brief, BSFL were positioned on ice, and BSFL abdomens were sectioned with a scalpel, and transferred to a 1.5 ml perforated Eppendorf tube containing a working buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA pH 8, 115 mM sodium citrate, 100 mM citric acid, pH 4.5, 1 mg/ml aprotinin, 100 µg/ml phenylthiourea), which prevents hemolymph clotting, melanization and reactive oxygen species (ROS) reaction. The tubes were centrifuged inside a 5 ml tube at 1000 rpm, 4 °C, for 1 min and again at 4500 rpm for 5 min.

Hemolymph inhibition assay

Bacterial growth for the inhibition assay

Eighteen isolates (6 vancomycin-resistant isolates, 6 metronidazole-resistant isolates and 6 vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant C. difficile isolates) were taken from an isolates bank of the clinical microbiology laboratory, at the Tzafon Medical Center, Poriya, Israel. This bank includes isolates collected from stool samples of patients aged ≥18 years, who were diagnosed with CDI during hospitalization at the medical center, between January 2018 and April 2020, and were enrolled in a previous study that was approved by the Tzafon Medical Center Helsinki Committee (POR-0085–15).

The frozen bacterial isolates (as beads) were inoculated in selective Chromagar medium (ChromID™ C. difficile (CDIF) (BioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France), and then incubated at 37 °C, in an anaerobic chamber for 48 h. C. difficile colonies are black-colored and asymmetric, with an irregular border. Pure colonies were stored in porous beads at −80 °C, for future use. Before each subsequent experiment, bacteria were grown on Brucella blood agar (Hy Laboratories Ltd.) for 48 h, at 37 °C, in an anaerobic chamber.

Hemolymph antimicrobial activity testing

Antimicrobial activity of the extracted BSFL hemolymph against the 18 bacterial isolates was evaluated using the disk diffusion method. Bacterial colonies of each isolate (grown on Brucella agar, as described above) were suspended in BHIS until 0.5 McFarland turbidity was achieved. Then, the bacterial suspension was spread on Brucella blood agar (Hy Laboratories Ltd.) using an inoculation loop. Blank paper disks (Becton Dickinson) were soaked in the extracted hemolymph until the hemolymph diffused throughout the disk, and then laid on the agar surface. The plates were incubated in an anaerobic chamber, at 37 °C, for 48 h. The inhibition zone was visualized under a magnifying glass and measured using a ruler.

AMPs gene expression

The expression levels of five AMP genes (DLP4, DLP3, attacin B-like, cecropin-like, stomoxyn, which were selected based on literature review) (Elhag et al., 2017; Li et al., 2017; Park and Yoe, 2017a, 2017b; Shin and Park, 2019; Ma et al., 2021) were measured by real-time RT PCR following exposure of BSFL to C. difficile and compared to their expression levels without exposure. Treatments included feeding of BSFL with C. difficile, C. difficile injection into BSFL, injection of native toxin A and B into BSFL, injection of BHIS into BSFL (Control 1) or BSFL without any treatment (Control 2).

Native toxin A (ab123999) and B (ab124001) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) were diluted in double distilled water to a final concentration of 200 ng/ml, each. Injection of toxins was performed as described above.

Following the different treatments, BSFL were fast-frozen in liquid nitrogen to maintain the larvae tissues' vitality. BSFL were then fixed to a Styrofoam using thin needles and the first and the last metamere of each larva was cut. Using a scissor, the cuticle on the left (or right) side of the body was cut from the anterior to the posterior end and the body was opened. Then, the fat body was collected using a thin sterile tweezer and transferred into a sterile Petri dish. The process was carried out on dry ice, and the collected fat body was stored at −80 °C, until further use.

For gene expression measurements, RNA was extracted from the fat body using the TRIzol Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts,USA) and then DNase-treated. The cDNA was synthesized and amplified using theqPCRBIO cDNA Synthesis Kit (PCRbiosystems, Pennsylvania, USA). The genes wereamplified by PCR with the SYBR™ Green PCR Master Mix (Roche Diagnostics, Laval,Quebec, Canada) using the CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio Rad, California, USA), under the following conditions: 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 15 s and 72 °C for 1 min, 40 cycles. All kits were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The BSFactin gene served as a housekeeping gene. The experiments were performed three times (with 2 technical repeats per an isolate). The primers of each targetgene are presented in Table S1 (adapted from references Bruno et al., 2021; Elhag et al., 2017; Gao et al., 2019). GenBank accession numbers: DLP4 - KF805350.1. The inhibitory effect of five synthesized AMPs on C. difficile growth was evaluated. Lyophilized peptides (DLP4- W5U4 × 3 · W5U4 × 3_HERIL, DLP3- W5U5D4 · W5U5D4_HERIL, attacin B-like, cecropin-like 1- L7VIN3 · CLP1_HERIL, stomoxyn- Q8T9R8 · STMX_STOCA) purchased from GL Biochem, Shanghai, China, were dissolved in Double-destiled water (DDW) (stoke solution of peptides- 2 mg/ml concentration). The former 18 isolates were incubated for 16 h in BHIS medium at 37 °C, and then diluted to 1 × 105 CFU/ml in fresh BHIS medium. The bacterial suspension was spread on Brucella blood agar (Hy Laboratories Ltd.). Blank paper disks (Becton Dickinson) were soaked in each peptide (at concentration of 2 mg/ml) until the peptide diffused throughout the disk, and then laid on the agar surface. DDW served as anegative control. The plates were incubated in an anaerobic chamber, at 37 °C, for 48 h. The inhibition zone was visualized under a magnifying glass and measured using a ruler.

The minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) of DLP4 was measured using broth dilution assay. The 18 C. difficile isolates were incubated in BHIS medium for 16 h at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions, and then diluted to 1 × 105 CFU/ml in fresh BHIS medium. Blanked disks were soaked in DLP4 at concentrations of 8– 2000 µg/ml and laid on the agar. Plates were incubated in an anaerobic chamber, at 37 °C, for 48 h., MIC was determined as the lowest concentration of the peptide at which bacterial growth was completely inhibited.

Difficile colony-forming units counting

All 18 isolates were incubated for 16 h in BHIS medium at 37 °C, and then diluted to 1 × 105 CFU/ml in fresh BHIS medium. Then, 100 μl of each bacterial suspension were transferred along with 100 μl DLP4 (at the MIC) or with DDW (as control) to 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes. The tubes were incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C, 150 RPM for 2, 4 and 24 h under anaerobic conditions. Afterwards, the bacterial suspensions were diluted (1:100 at fresh BHIS medium) and inoculated in a selective Chromagar medium (ChromID™ C. difficile (CDIF) (BioMérieux), which was incubated for 48 h. Colony-forming units (CFU) were counted under magnifying glass.

Cell viability levels measurement

The human colon adenocarcinoma cell line (Caco-2, HBT37) was used to assess the cytotoxicity of DLP4. Cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (IMBH, Beit Haemek, Israel) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, supplemented with 1 % penicillin and streptomycin (IMBH), at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and 90 % relative humidity. Cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h at the same conditions described above. Then, media was removed and 50 µl DLP4 (16–500 µg/ml, diluted in 100 µl DMEM) was added to each well. Untreated cells served as a negative control. Following 2 and 24 hours' incubation, media was removed, and 100 µl DMEM and 50 µl of 1 mg/ml sodium 3′-[1-(phenylaminocarbonyl)-3,4- tetrazolium]-bis (4‑methoxy-6-nitro) benzene sulfonic acid hydrate (XTT) solution (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) were added into each well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, and 90 % relative humidity for 2 h. Then absorbance was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) at a wavelength of 630 nm (OD630) and reference wavelength of 450 nm (OD450). The viability of Caco-2 cells was calculated as percentage of control, as follows: (OD of DLP4-treated cells/OD of control cells) *100.

Assessment of morphological alterations in bacterial membrane

To assess alteration in C. difficile's membrane following treatment with DLP4, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used. An overnight culture of C. difficile strain 630 was diluted to 0.5 McFarland turbidity and 100 μl of bacteria was transferred along with 100 μl DLP4 (125 µg/ml) or DDW (as control) to 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes. The tubes were incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C, 150 RPM for 1 and 2 h under anaerobic conditions. A sample from each tube was transferred into a 24-well plate. The specimens were then fixed at room temperature, for 20 min, in Carnovsky's fixative combined of 2 % paraformaldehyde, 2 % glutaraldehyde, 80 mM 1, 4-Piperazinediethanesulfonic acid (PIPES) buffer, pH 7.4, 50 mM sucrose, and 1 mM MgCl2. After the primary fixation, specimens were washed two times for 3 min in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate (pH 7.4) and post-fixed with 1 % osmium tetroxide in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) for 10 min, at room temperature. Specimens were then washed twice with ultrapure water for 3 min and dehydrated using a graded ethanol series (3, 7.5, 15, 30, 50, 70, 90, 95, and 100 %; 2 × 3 min for each step). Critical point drying was performed from high-purity liquid CO2 on a K850 CPD apparatus (Quorum Technologies, Lewes Road,

Laughton, East Sussex, UK) and cover slips with cells were mounted on aluminum stubs (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA) using adhesive carbon tape. In order to increase conductivity of the specimens, the upper surfaces of the cover glasses were bridged with stubs using conductive silver paint (Ted Pella). The specimens were further dried for 30 min under a gentle flow of argon and then coated with a ∼1-nm-thick layer of iridium using a Q150T turbo-pumped sputter coater (Quorum Technologies, Lewes Road, Laughton, East Sussex, UK). Images were acquired with a “Merlin” scanning electron microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) using in-lens secondary electron detector.

Statistical analysis

The Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to assess differences in inhibition zone values between the different isolates categorized according to their antibiotic susceptibility status (i.e., metronidazole-resistant, vancomycin-resistant, metronidazole- and vancomycin-resistant). Dunn's Multiple Comparison Test was used as a post-hoc test. The Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's Multiple Comparison Test as a post-hoc test were also used to analyze differences in AMP gene expression, inhibitory effects of synthetic peptides, and MIC distribution between the different treatments.

The Mann–Whitney test was used to analyze differences in bacterial viability between the different treatments and the effect of DLP4 on C. difficile growth.

Assays were performed with three technical repeats. A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0 (322) (GraphPad company, San Diego, USA).

Hemolymph extracted from injection BSFL imparts a greater inhibitory effect compared to hemolymph extracted from feeding BSFL

The inhibitory effect of Injection group hemolymph on the growth of vancomycin-resistant and of vancomycin and metronidazole-resistant C. difficile isolates (1.35 ± 0.13 cm and 1.31 ± 0.03 cm, respectively) was greater than the effect of Feeding group hemolymph (0.7 ± 0.05 cm and 0.8 ± 0.06 cm, respectively), but the differences were statistically insignificant (Fig. 2A, 2C, respectively). AMP gene expression levels were altered following C. difficile exposure

The expression levels of five AMP genes (DLP4, DLP3, attacin B-like, cecropin-like, stomoxyn) were measured in the fat body in non-immunized BSFL and in BSFL following exposure to C. difficile strain 630 (Fig. 3). Feeding BSFL with C. difficile resulted in a 4.3-fold increase in DLP4 gene expression levels compared to the Control group (p = 0.252) (Fig. 3A). Stomoxyn (STX) expression was 2.2-fold higher than in Control Group. DLP3, AttB and CLP1 gene expression levels were not significantly different in C. difficile-exposed as compared to Control BSFL.

AMP gene expression levels as response to injection of C. difficile or a specific bacterial product

In order to understand whether there was a response to C. difficile toxins, BSFL were injected with a toxin-producing C. difficile strain, a non-toxigenic strain, native toxin A or B, or BHIS (as Control) (Fig. S3). Following injection of toxin-producing strain 630, the gene expression levels of all five AMPs were increased, as compared to Control (Figure S3A-E(. Statistically significant increases were detected in expression levels of DLP4, DLP3 and CLP1 compared to the Control group (8.7-fold increase, p = 0.0127, 40.3-fold increase, p = 0.013 and 45.8-fold increase, p = 0.0293, respectively (Fig. S3A, S3B, S3C). Although gene expression levels of STX and AttB were higher compared to the Control group (13.2-fold and 11.6-fold increase, respectively), these differences were statistically insignificant (Fig. S3D, S3E, respectively). In each of the treatments, no statistical significant differences were seen in gene expression levels between the different genes.

Following injection of a non-toxigenic C. difficile strain, no significant increases in AMP gene expression levels were observed. CLP1 and STX gene expression levels increased by 3.7-fold and 2.6-fold, respectively (Fig. S3C, S3E). The increase of other AMPs was even smaller (Fig. S3A, S3B, S3D). Injection of native C. difficile toxin A resulted in upregulation of DLP4 (3.1-fold increase) (Fig. S3A), CLP1 (6.9-fold increase) (Fig. S3C), and AttB (4-fold increase) (Fig. S3D). Similarly, injection of native C. difficile toxin B resulted in increased expression levels of DLP4 (3.9-fold increase) (Fig. S3A), CLP1 . (3.9-fold increase) (Fig. S3C) and of AttB (2.5-fold increase) (Fig. S3D) The effect of toxin exposure on the gene expression levels of DLP3 and STX was minor (Fig. S3B, S3E).

Inhibitory effects of synthetic peptides on C. difficile growth

C. difficile isolates were exposed to synthetic peptides of the 5 AMPs mentioned earlier. DLP3, CLP1, AttB and STX synthetic peptides had no inhibitory effect on C. difficile isolate growth (Figure S4A). In contrast, the inhibition zone diameters of all isolates exposed to DLP4 (average diameter of 1.97 ± 0.2 cm) were larger compared to those exposed to control treatment (p < 0.001) (Figure S4A). The mean inhibition zone diameter of vancomycin-resistant isolates following exposure to DLP4 was higher (2.2 ± 0.12 cm) compared to metronidazole-resistant and vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant isolates (1.86 ± 0.16 cm and 1.83 ± 0.13 cm, respectively) (Fig. S4B).

DLP4 minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC)

Since DLP4 was the only synthetic peptide with a substantial inhibitory effect against C. difficile isolates, its antimicrobial activity was studied in further detail. A range of DLP4 concentrations was tested against C. difficile isolates and the MIC was determined for each isolate (Fig. 4). Higher DLP4 MIC was found for metronidazole-resistant isolates (166.7 ± 60.6 µg/ml) as compared to the MIC of vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant isolates (145.8 ± 48 µg/ml), although this difference was statistically insignificant. The DLP4 MIC of metronidazole-resistant isolates was also higher than the MIC of vancomycin -resistant isolates (104.7 ± 30 µg/ml) (p = 0.0158) (Fig. 4). Similarly, the mean DLP4 MIC for vancomycin- andmetronidazole-resistant isolates was significantly higher than the mean MIC for vancomycin-resistant isolates (p = 0.0005).

The effect of DLP4 on C. difficile growth

To assess the impact of DLP4 on C. difficile growth, the number of colony-forming units (CFUs) of C. difficile isolates was counted following their exposure to DLP4 in for 2, 4 and 24 h. A decrease in the number of CFUs was already observed after 2 h of exposure to DLP4 (Fig. 5A). Specifically, a mean 3 000 ± 5 060 CFU/ml of vancomycin-resistant isolates was observed following exposure to DLP4, while a mean of 118 000 ± 9 716 CFU/ml was observed in the same without DLP4 exposure (97.5 % decrease, p < 0.01). Counts of metronidazole-resistant isolate CFUs were 126 000 ± 33 782 CFU/ml without DLP4 and 4 000 ± 3 800 CFU/ml after exposure to DLP4 (96.8 % decrease, p < 0.01). The mean CFU counts for vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant isolates were 129 000 ± 23 169 CFU/ml and 1 000 ± 750 CFU/ml in the absence or presence of DLP4, respectively (99.2 % decrease, p < 0.01). Four hours after exposure to DLP4, the decrease in CFU count was more pronounced (Fig. 5B); the CFU numbers of isolates decreased (vancomycin-resistant isolates- 99.4 % decrease, p < 0.01, metronidazole-resistant isolates- a 99.6 % decrease, p < 0.01 and vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant isolates- a 99.2 % decrease, p < 0.01). After exposure to DLP4 for 24 h (Fig. 5C), the mean CFU/ml of vancomycin-resistant isolates decreased by 99.9 % (p < 0.01), of metronidazole-resistant isolates by 99.9 % (p < 0.01) and of vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant isolates by 99.85 % (p < 0.01).

DLP4 cytotoxic effect on caco-2 cells

In order to examine the effect of DLP4 on human cell viability, XTT assay was performed on Caco-2 cells with different concentrations of DLP4. Exposure of Caco-2 cells to 500 µg/ml DLP4 for 2 h or 24 h, resulted in a 20 % and 25 % reduction in cell viability, respectively, compared to untreated cells (Fig. S5A, S5B, respectively). At lower concentrations, the effect of DLP4 on cell viability was smaller. All reductions measured were statistically insignificant.

Morphological alterations in C. difficile bacteria following DLP4 exposure

SEM imaging was used to screen for morphological changes in C. difficile following exposure to DLP4 at its MIC for the tested strain (125 µg/ml) (Fig. 6). Untreated C. difficile strain 630 cells had a rough textured cell wall (Fig. 6A). Following a 1 h incubation with DLP4, pores were observed in the outer bacterial layer and the texture became smoother (Fig. 6B). After a 2 h incubation with DLP4, the outer layer was damaged and deeper pores were observed (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

The current study assessed the activity of BSF-derived AMPs against C. difficile, the etiological agent of one of the most common nosocomial infections. The in vitro antimicrobial activity of BSF hemolymph against various Gram-negative bacteria and Gram-positive bacteria has been previously demonstrated (Li et al., 2017; Park and Yoe, 2017a; Alvarez et al., 2019; Xia et al., 2021; Qosimah et al., 2024). To best of our knowledge, no previous study has investigated its effects on C. difficile.

Antimicrobial activity of BSFL hemolymph

Exposure of BSFL to non-toxigenic C. difficile by either direct injection or feeding did not result in hemolymph with antimicrobial properties. This result is compatible with those of a previous study (Patrnogic et al., 2018) which found that pre-exposure of Drosophila melanogaster to non-pathogenic bacteria does not later protect them against the same bacteria. In contrast, exposure of BSFL to pathogenic C. difficile (strain 630) resulted in hemolymph with notable antimicrobial activity. A study that investigated the effect of BSFL feeding with bacteria on the growth of E. coli, M Micrococcus luteus M. luteus, Pseudomonas fluorescens P. fluorescens and Bacillus subtilis B. subtilis (Vogel et al., 2018) demonstrated that diet during the larval rearing stage can have a significant impact on the antimicrobial activities of the resulting larval extracts.

Moreover, in the current study, the antimicrobial activity of the hemolymph was more pronounced following proactive exposure (Injection group) compared to natural exposure (Feeding group). This was the first study to compare the effect of bacterial feeding vs. injection on hemolymph properties. In their exploration of the immune response of BSFL to injection of E. coli and M. luteus mixture, Bruno et al. found that the inhibitory effect of the subsequent hemolymph against the infecting bacteria increased 14 h after bacterial exposure (Bruno et al., 2021). These findings are consistent with our results and can be explained by the higher percentage of bacterial exposure at Injection group (100 %) as compared to the Feeding group (unknown percentage of exposure). Additionally, the midgut epithelium produces immune effectors, which may eliciting a stronger localized response in the gut. Another explanation for the difference in antimicrobial effect of hemolymph from feeding and injection groups is the AMPs doses to which the BSFL were exposed in each group; it is possibile that these doses were not similar. Furthermore, it is known that the bacteria are eliminated in the digestive tract of larvae, most likely due to the enzymes, antibacterial peptides and different pH levels (Mumcuoglu et al., 2001).

It should be noted that hemolymph extracted from Control BSFL had low antimicrobial activity, which may have been the result of natural exposure to pathogens under lab conditions. The antimicrobial activity of BSFL hemolymph after bacterial exposure was lower in metronidazole-resistant isolates compared to the other antibiotics-resistant isolates, and was lower in vancomycin- and metronidazole-resistant isolates compared to vancomycin-resistant isolates. This difference could be due to the differences in the resistance mechanisms of each subgroup of isolates, and should be further investigated.

AMP gene expression

We investigated the changes in some AMPs genes expression levels following exposure to C. difficile. We chose BSFL peptides that were proved to have antimicrobial activity against gram-positive bacteria. We found that gene expression of all 5 AMPs (DLP4, DLP3, CLP1, AttB, STX) was upregulated in response to C. difficile exposure. More pronounced increases in gene expression of some of these AMPs were measured in the Injection group as compared to Feeding group. Previous studies have demonstrated an increased gene expression of AMPs following injection of pathogens (Freitak et al., 2007; Li et al., 2017; Park and Yoe, 2017a; Shin and Park, 2019; Buonocore et al., 2021), such as E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus S. aureus. For example, Freitak et al. (Freitak et al., 2007) which examined the immune response of Trichoplusia ni larvae to E.coli, reported on increases in AMPs expression when bacteria were injected to larvae. Additionally, increases in 8 AMPs expression were also observed following feeding of larvae with infected food as compared to hemolymph of larvae with bacteria-free diets. Further studies will be needed to further characterize the immune response of larvae to bacterial injection vs. bacterial feeding.

In the current study, injection of C. difficile resulted in the pronounced upregulation of DLP4 expression in BSF hemolymph. Increased insect defensin levels in response to bacterial feeding or injection has already been reported by Telleria et al. (Telleria et al., 2013); Lutzomyia longipalpis (sandfly) defensin levels increased following their exposure to E.coli (injection and feeding). Moreover, defensin expression levels were higher after injection as compared to feeding, as in the current analysis. Similarly, in the present work, DLP4 was the only AMP upregulated after bacterial feeding of BSFL, and likely mediated the inhibitory effect of the associated hemolymph on C. difficile isolates. Further investigations should be performed to prove this assumption.

Of note, although bacterial toxin injection upregulated expression of some AMP genes, the most notable increase was observed following injection of whole bacteria. On the other hand, the inhibitory response following injection of non-toxic bacteria was minor. Therefore, it is assumed that while the immune response was seemingly not directed against the bacterial toxins, their presence may have amplified the response. Another possibility is that toxin-producing strains bear an additional virulence factor other than the toxins that stimulates the immune response of BSFL.

Inhibitory effects of synthetic peptides on C. difficile growth

Among the five AMPs, synthetic DLP4 was the only one to have an inhibitory effect on C. difficile. In general, insect defensins are active against Gram–negative bacteria such as E. coli, but mainly against Gram-positive bacteria, such as S. aureus, and M. luteus (Hetru et al., 2003). Although no previous studies investigated the effect of insect defensins on C. difficile, there are some reports on the antimicrobial effect of human α-defensins against C. difficile (Giesemann et al., 2008; Furci et al., 2015; Fischer et al., 2020; Korbmacher et al., 2020).

Interestingly, although DLP4 differs from DLP3 in six amino acids only, all located near DLP4′ s loop structure, the antibacterial activities of the two defensins against C. difficile were different. Four of the six amino acids are lysine in DLP4 instead of Gly-10, Arg-25, Asp-32 and Arg-40 in DLP3. It is possible that this 6-amino acid sequence confers the antimicrobial activity of DLP4 (Park and Yoe, 2017b). For example, a previous study found that increasing the positive charge of an AMP by adding lysine to the peptide sequence could increase its antibacterial activity (Findaly ey al., 2010). The increased charge raises electrostatic interactions between AMPs and the negatively charged bacterial membranes, without affecting its interactions with the lipids found in mammalian membranes.

DLP4 inhibition of C. difficile was more pronounced against vancomycin-resistant isolates than against metronidazole-resistant and metronidazole- and vancomycin-resistant isolates. This pattern is similar to the inhibition pattern observed for hemolymph of immunized BSFL. This may be explained by differences in the resistance mechanisms of the isolates of each antibiotic resistance subgroup. Peng et al. showed that metronidazole resistance is likely driven by several alterations in metabolic pathways, such as those involving the activity of nitroreductases, iron uptake and DNA repair (Peng et al., 2017). In contrast, vancomycin resistance might be due to amino acid changes in peptidoglycan biosynthesis-associated proteins (Leeds et al., 2014). It is possible that structural changes associated with resistance to antibiotic, enhance or reduce DLP4 binding to the bacteria. Further studies are necessary to fully characterize the influence of antibiotic resistance mechanisms on the efficacy of DLP4.

The minimum inhibition concentration (MIC) of DLP4

Previous studies on DLP4 MIC against Gram-positive bacteria determined a MIC of 4 µg/ml against Staphylococcus hyicus S. hyicus (Ma et al., 2021), 32 µg/ml against Streptococcus pneumoniae and 64 µg/ml against Staphylococcus epidermidis S. epidermidis (Li et al., 2020). The current research measured a DLP4 MIC range of 64–250 µg/ml for clinical C. difficile isolates. The lowest MIC was needed for vancomycin-resistant isolates, i.e., very low DLP4 doses were necessary to inhibit these isolates. This finding may explain why vancomycin-resistant isolates had the largest diameter of growth inhibition, among all isolates, when exposed to 2 mg/ml DLP4.

DLP4 effect on C. difficile CFU count

The current study demonstrated that DLP4 could effectively inhibit C. difficile growth. CFU counts of all clinical isolates decreased after 2 h of treatment with the mean MIC of DLP4. Following 4 h of treatment with DLP4, significant decreases in the CFU counts of both vancomycin-resistant and metronidazole-resistant isolates were noted. These results point to a bacteriocidial effect of DLP4 on C. difficile isolates.

In a previous study, a 1-hour treatment of S. aureus with DLP4 resulted in a reduction of 2.7 log10 in bacterial CFU/mL (Li et al., 2020). In another study, S. hyicus counts decreased dramatically after treatment with DLP4 (Ma et al., 2021). Bacteria were completely inhibited after treatment with 4-fold MIC of DLP4. In another study, DLP4 exhibited broad-spectrum and high-efficiency bactericidal activity against Gram-positive bacteria, including S. aureus, S. epidermidis and Streptococcus suis S. suis (Li et al., 2017). Further research will be needed to determine whether higher doses of the peptide can confer a bactericidal effect against antibiotic-resistant C. difficile.

AMPS effect on human cell viability

One of the main concerns of treating pathogens with antimicrobial peptides is the possible cytotoxic effect of the peptide on eukaryotic cells. In the present work, DLP4 exhibited a strong antimicrobial effect on various C. difficile isolates and a low cytotoxic effect against human epithelial cells (Caco-2). A previous study found that the cationic DLP4 had a low cytotoxic effect on mammalian macrophage cells (RAW264.7) (Li et al., 2017), which is consistent with our results. In contrast, analysis of the toxic effects of highly active cationic AMPs (such as cecropin A, melittin) on human cells measured low therapeutic indexes for some peptides (Bacalum and Radu, 2015). Generally, defensins interact with lipopolysaccharides and polysaccharides of Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, respectively, which may help DLP4, distinguish between bacterial and human membranes (Bruggeman et al., 2019).

DLP4-Induced bacterial membrane damage

The surface of C. difficile strain 630 was visibly damaged after 1–2 h of treatment with DLP4, an observation that aligned the disruptive effect of AMPs on bacterial membranes. A previous study that examined the surface of S. aureus following exposure to synthetic AMP, reported on outer-layer rupture and microspore formation (Luo et al., 2022). Another study identified pores in the bacterial peptidoglycan layer (cell wall) and release of membrane vesicles following incubation on S. hyicus with DLP4 (Ma et al., 2021). This can be explained by the hydrophobic and cationic characteristics of DLP4, which result in its electrostatic attraction to the bacterial cell membrane, and to an accumulation of hydrophobic residues. This leads to swelling of the outer layer of the cell wall and stenosis, eventually resulting in pore formation (Wang et al., 2021). Additional studies will be required to determine the mechanisms underlying the observed membrane damage.

Study limitations

The study suffers from several limitations. First, the study had focused on 5 AMPs; as BSF had a remarkable AMPs repertoire, further investigation should be performed in order to search for additional AMPs that may mediate the antimicrobial effect against C. difficile. Second, DLP4 effects were tested against 18 isolates. Future study should include a larger number of isolates, with different resistance profiles. Third, ther is still a significant challenge regarding the feasibility of systemic or oral use of AMPs, as these peptides might be destroyed either by proteolytic enzymes or bu the acidic pH of the stomach (Hancock and Sahl, 2006; Yi et al., 2014) . Nevertheless, there are some solutions to this challenge such as chemical modifications and encapsulation of the AMPs (Fjell et al., 2012; Mahlapuu et al., 2016).

In conclusion, hemolymph of C. difficile-immunized BSFL exhibited antimicrobial activity against antibiotics-resistant C. difficile isolates. This antibacterial activity can be partially explained by the inhibitory effects of DLP4, an antimicrobial peptide in the BSFL hemolymph. As DLP4 showed potent activity against various isolates on the one hand, and low cytotoxicity against human cells, on the other hand, further studies should investigate its potential as a candidate for treatment of CDI. Future study is required to assess the effect of higher DLP4 doses on bacterial growth. Additionally, the synergistic effect of peptide combination should also be investigated. Finally, as AMPs may be degraded inside the human body, studies should focus on systemic administration profile, and on in-vivo assessment of efficacy, pharmacokinetic and safety.
