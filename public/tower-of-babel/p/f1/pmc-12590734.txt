Financial burden of systemic lupus erythematosus in India: prevalence and predictors of catastrophic health expenditure in a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Vineeta Shobha, Kriti Kishor, Chanchal Gera, V. Nayana, Smruti Ramteke, Sunitha Kayidhi, Anuj Shukla, Namisha Patel, Ramaswamy Subramanian, Mamadapur Mahabaleshwar, Avinash Jain, Aradhana Singh, Anshul Goel, Subramanian Nallasivan, Sahana Baliga, Sourabh Malviya, Sumithra Selvam, Amita Aggarwal

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe estimated the frequency of catastrophic healthcare expenditure(CHE), and their determinants in Indian patients with systemic lupus erythematosus(SLE).MethodsThis was a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based survey conducted by the Lupus Special Interest Group of the Indian Rheumatology Association across 14 centers. Patients with SLE diagnosed as per SLICC-2012 on follow-up for at least 1-year were interviewed regarding annual disease-related expenditures including direct (medical and non-medical) and indirect costs. CHE was defined as > 20% of the annual income. Results are presented in Indian currency (INR), wherein 100 INR = 1.19 USD = 1.1 EURO.ResultsWe included 655 patients with SLE [92.7% women], with a mean age of 32.9 ± 11.6 years. The median direct annual expenditure was INR 52400(30810,96300), largest component being cost of medications [INR 24000(12000,40000) and hospitalizations [INR 35000(14400,90000)] One-third of patients(n = 237,36.2%) suffered CHE; they were older [AOR1.01(0.99,1.03)], had lower level of education [AOR1.95(1.01,3.81)], belonged to lower socio-economic-strata[AOR 9.63(5.66,16.4)], had renal and/or neuropsychiatric lupus [AOR1.42(0.99,2.06)] and higher damage(SDI) [AOR1.84(1.22,2.77)]. The median annual indirect cost was INR16416(5016,52896). Three-fourths(73.7%) of the participants incurred out-of-pocket expenses for their healthcare. The employed population was low(n = 187;28.3%), and the absenteeism rate was 24%.ConclusionHospitalization and medication costs are major factors driving exponentially high out-of-pocket expenses, resulting in CHE in one-third of patients with SLE in India.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41927-025-00583-y.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic autoimmune disease associated with significant healthcare costs, especially during hospitalisation and flares [1, 2]. The chronic nature of the disease and its impact on multiple organ systems create a considerable economic burden for patients, families, society, and healthcare systems. Disease-related organ damage further exacerbates the financial strain, pushing families closer to poverty, particularly in regions where there is limited national healthcare support [1]. As it primarily affects younger individuals, the repercussions on productivity and quality of life are considerable [2].

The ‘Cost of illness’ studies encompass an evaluation of three financial elements: direct costs, indirect costs, and intangible costs [3]. Direct medical costs refer to expenses that are directly associated with treatment, while indirect costs pertain to the loss of productivity and intangible costs, which relate to physical disability, mental distress, and quality of life. Catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) is defined as out-of-pocket expenses that surpass 40% of nonfood consumption or 10% of total consumption or income. SLE patients frequently suffer from CHE, particularly in low and middle-income countries (LMIC) [4, 5]. The heightened risk of infection, the accumulation of damage, and comorbidities further contribute to increased costs over time [6].

The economic burden studies in SLE are largely from the USA, the UK, Canada, and Germany. These studies have reported a high cost of treatment; in the Western population, indirect costs have been identified as a major component [7–14]. Drivers of high expenditure include renal disease, higher damage, and poor social support, amongst several others [1, 13, 15]. Studies from Southeast Asian countries reveal overall comparable expenditures, but direct costs predominates here [16, 17].

India’s population is expanding, and it is currently estimated to have more than 50,000 SLE patients who utilize both public and private healthcare facilities [18]. At present, SLE is not included in the National health care Schemes in India. The majority of private insurance companies also do not provide coverage for SLE, for either inpatient or the outpatient services. This translates into predominantly out-of-pocket spending for most patients. A systematic literature review from India identified only four observational studies (one prospective and three retrospective) comprising a total sample size of 1,628 patients [19]. Notably, none of these studies employed model-based comprehensive economic evaluations, thereby underscoring critical paucity of data on economic burden of SLE in the Indian context.

We conducted a cost-of-illness analysis in Indian patients with SLE, capturing all treatment-related expenses to evaluate the economic burden of the disease and its association with both disease and non-disease variables. Additionally, we assessed the prevalence and determinants of catastrophic healthcare expenditure.

Methods

This is a cross-sectional questionnaire-based survey carried out by the members of the Lupus Special Interest Group (SIG) of the Indian Rheumatology Association (IRA). SLE patients attending outpatient departments for their routine care diagnosed as per SLICC-2012 classification criteria, and on follow-up for at least one year at respective centers were invited to participate [20]. Subjects of undifferentiated connective tissue diseases, mixed connective tissue disorder, overlap syndrome, and those who refused to provide their financial or socioeconomic status were excluded. This study was carried out between July 2023 and March 2024. All participants or their legal representatives provided written consent for participation and all centers received ethics approval. (IEC Ref No:121/2023) The study follows the STROBE guidelines for reporting and the cost of illness checklist for economic studies [21].

A detailed questionnaire was collectively developed for this including a pilot run to understand the economic impact of SLE on patients and their families. Multiple meetings were held to discuss and finetune the case record form (CRF)[Supplementary 1]. Once consensus was reached, each center translated the CRF into their local language as per regional requirements. The questionnaire was administered by trained research staff in their local language which took approximately 20–30 min. Patients’ personal and hospital medical records were used to confirm clinical and laboratory information, current disease activity, damage, and comorbidities. The health care delivery set-ups were categorized into academic (Government and private medical college), corporate hospital and stand-alone rheumatology clinics.

The socio-demographic profile of the family head was recorded as per the modified Kuppuswamy 2022 scale (SES) [22]. This information was captured as informed by the patient or their caregiver and was not formally verified. SES was categorized as low (low and upper lower, middle and high (upper and upper middle) strata based on their monthly family income, education and occupation of head of family. Additionally, the patient’s education level was also captured as per SES categories. Patient and caregiver were enquired about expenditures (previous 3 months recall), which included medical and non-medical costs. Direct medical costs incorporated all medical costs such as consultation and cross-consultation fees, laboratory and all medications costs including that of topical therapy and supplements. Similarly, if a patient was hospitalized or made emergency visits in the previous year, that cost was also recorded. Direct non-medical costs included travel, food expenses, and any other expenses. Expenses incurred in the prior 3 months were multiplied by 4 to calculate the annual cost. The source of funding for treatment was recorded. Expenditure was captured in Indian currency (100 INR = 1.19USD = 1.1EURO). We defined catastrophic healthcare expenditure (CHE) as ≥ 20% of annual family income and analyzed for various sociodemographic and clinical variables [23].

Disease-related parameters records included their clinical manifestations as ever or never, current disease activity and damage were assessed using SLEDAI 2 K and SLICC damage index (SDI) respectively [24, 25]. Current prescription medications were ratified using the recent prescriptions and hospital records. Patient and Physician global assessment was recorded using a 1-100 numeric scale; wherein 0 is considered excellent health while higher values denote poor health status. Multimorbidity was defined as the presence of two or more comorbid illnesses.

The productivity and absenteeism were assessed using iMTA Productivity Cost Questionnaire (PCQ), with the developer’s consent [26]. Indirect cost was calculated using productivity losses and was measured in domains of productivity losses at paid work (due to absenteeism and presenteeism at paid work). Absenteeism was computed by multiplying the number of workdays missed by number of work hours per day while presenteeism was computed by capturing the number of workdays with reduced participant performance. All the responses for work were recorded over the past 4 weeks [26].

Sample size was computed for the estimation of CHE. Considering the proportion of CHE as 15% with 22% relative precision and 95% confidence interval, the required number of patients was 412. Accounting for the multicenter clustering effect using a design effect of 1.5, the total number of patients required was 618.

Statistical analysis

Normality of the data checked by Q-Q plot. Non-normal data were log-transformed and considered for data analysis. Descriptive statistics were reported as mean with standard deviation for the normally distributed continuous variables else median with interquartile range. All the costs were reported as median with an interquartile range. Direct cost was computed by adding the medical and non-medical costs. Factors associated with direct cost were analyzed using simple and multiple linear regression analysis, and a beta coefficient with 95% C.I. was calculated. Significant variables from simple linear regression were considered for multiple linear regression analysis. Unadjusted and adjusted analyses were performed using logistic regression analysis to assess the factors associated with CHE > 20%. In the adjusted analysis, variables significant in the univariate analysis were considered for multivariable analysis. Adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% C.I. were reported. Indirect costs, as assessed by productive losses were reported. P value less than 5% was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed using STATA version 15.0 and SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corp. Released 2023. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0.2.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp).

Results

We included 655 SLE patients, mean age of 32.9 ± 11.6 years, 92.7% women, and 6.4% children from 14 rheumatology centers across India. Missing data was < 3% and all the available data was analyzed. The rheumatology centers included medical teaching Institutes (Govt = 3, private = 4), stand-alone rheumatology clinics (n = 5) and the rest were corporate hospitals (Fig. 1). The mean duration of follow-up with an individual center was 41.3 ± 53.5 months. Renal involvement was noted in 274(41.8%), while the nervous system was affected in 122(18.6%). Current prescriptions included hydroxychloroquine in 587/655(89.6%), and glucocorticoids in 457(69.8%) patients. Cyclophosphamide was administered to 53 (8.1%), while rituximab prescription was noted in 37 (5.6%) patients. Hospitalisation was required in 187 (28.5%) during the previous one year. Socio-demographic details, disease-related variables, medications prescribed, and comorbidities are depicted in supplementary Table 2.

Multimorbidity was documented in 100 (15.2%) patients and the most frequent comorbidities were hypertension in 122 (18.6%) and thyroid illness in 112 (17.1%) patients. The mean SLEDAI and SDI were 4.9 ± 7.7 and 0.51 ± 1.02 respectively. About 64.3% and 35.7% of the patients had SLEDAI scores ≤ 4 and > 4 respectively.

The median direct cost of annual expenditure including all-encompassing medical and non-medical expenses was INR 52,400 (30810,96300), whereas the cost of medications was INR 24,000 (12000,40000). In the past year, 187 patients (28.5%) were hospitalized, the median cost of hospitalisation per patient being INR 35,000(14400,90000). Direct medical expenditure was largely driven by medications (40%), followed by hospitalisation (16%) and laboratory investigations expenses (15%). (Fig. 2) The median annual indirect cost was INR 16,416 (5016, 52896).

More than half of our cohort, 55.6% (n = 364) utilized more than10% of their annual income for health-related expenditure, while about one-third of patients 36.2% (n = 237) incurred CHE at > 20%. Among the total direct costs, expenditure for laboratory investigations (15%), the cost of hospitalisation (16%), and costs of medications towards treatment (52%) (SLE, intercurrent infections, comorbidities, supplements) significantly contributed towards CHE (p < 0.001). On multivariable analysis, patients with CHE were younger [AOR 0.97 (0.96, 0.99)], had a lower level of education [AOR 1.95 (1.01,3.81)], and belonged to lower SES [AOR for middle-class SES 2.67(1.71,4.17) and lower-class SES 9.63(5.66,16.4)]. Amongst SLE-related variables, CHE was directly associated with renal and/or neuropsychiatric lupus [AOR 1.42(0.99,2.06)] and higher SDI [AOR 1.84(1.22,2.77)]. This information is represented in Table 2; Fig. 3. Results of association between CHE at > 10% with study variables is presented in Supplementary Table 1, 3. We observed that SES and SDI scores had similar significant association with CHE (> 10%) as CHE > 20% except for age.

Patients with SDI ≥ 1 (n = 191) were older, had longer duration of illness, were more likely to have had renal or NPSLE, and had multimorbidity. This group of patients had significantly higher annual expenditure by more than 50% than those with SDI score of 0. (Fig. 3)

Health costs were largely out-of-pocket in 73.7% (n = 483); medical insurance and Government health schemes supported only < 7% of SLE patient’s direct health care. The distribution of the expenditure in various healthcare systems (Government versus others, teaching institutes versus others is presented in Fig. 3. In our cohort, 28.6% of patients were currently gainfully employed. Approximately one-fourth (24%) reported absenteeism for a mean duration of 6.7 ± 9.7 days.

Discussion

We report the economic impact of SLE in Indian patients. About one-third of our cohort spent 20% or more of their annual income on medical care resulting in CHE. Patients with lower education levels, lower socioeconomic status, rural residency, and those enrolled in the BPL (below-poverty-line) schemes were more likely to have incurred CHE. These findings highlight the disproportionate economic impact on the marginalized and economically vulnerable groups underscoring an urgent need for inclusive health financing mechanisms and expanded coverage through public health care schemes.

Approximately 75% of our patients paid out-of-their pocket for medical treatment and could not access health insurance or government healthcare schemes. Consistent with our findings, recent studies from India have found that two-thirds of the population incur out-of-pocket expenses for chronic diseases, placing India among the countries with highest household healthcare spending globally [27, 28]. These reported that medium and large households, household members with increased duration of stay in the hospital, utilization of private health facility and the presence of chronic illness had higher odds of impoverishment due to out-of-pocket health expenditures.

In a retrospective cohort study involving 73 hospitalized patients with SLE from a private medical academic institute in India, the median cost of hospitalisation was INR 135,768(94,053–223,954) contributing substantially to overall direct medical expenditure [29]. The hospitalisation rates as well as cost of hospitalisation were comparatively lower in our cohort. This difference may be attributed to the inclusion of patients under chronic care for at least one year, with approximately two-thirds having low disease activity (SLEDAI < 4), reflecting a more stable clinical state and thus lower associated costs.

Pharmacoeconomic studies from several developed countries have shown that factors such as longer disease duration, higher disease activity, greater organ damage, and compromised physical and mental health are associated with increased healthcare expenditures. Notably, in these studies, Indirect costs are often at least twice as high as direct medical costs [1, 7, 8, 9, 10, 30]. In contrast, the cost of medications constituted the largest component of medical expenditure in our study while the indirect costs were a minor component possibly due to relatively lower employment rates and differences in health care financing models. Interestingly, two retrospective studies from Hong Kong also reported that the direct cost comprised a major part of the total healthcare cost deviating from trends observed in western literature.[15,16 ]These studies identified similar key drivers for higher healthcare costs such as disease duration, disease damage, lupus flares, and neuropsychiatric events. A recent Japanese study revealed that the patients with renal involvement or those with NPSLE had the highest rate of hospitalisations and patients with severe SLE had the highest expenses regardless of the type of cost [31]. Data from the CSTAR registry in China similarly reported that direct medical costs accounted for a substantial proportion (86%) of total healthcare expenditure [32]. Factors contributing significantly to increased direct costs included moderate to severe disease activity, use of biologic therapies, treatment with moderate to high doses of glucocorticoids, hospitalization, and involvement of the peripheral vascular, cardiovascular, and/or renal systems. In alignment with these international findings, we also observed higher healthcare costs among patients with NPSLE or with renal disease. Older age, lower education, hospitalisation, higher disease activity, organ damage, presence of multimorbidity also exhibited higher expenditures. These observations reinforce the multifactorial nature of cost burden in SLE highlighting the complex interplay between disease-related, sociodemographic, and healthcare system-level factors.

The type of healthcare facilities varies across different regions of the world. India enjoys diverse healthcare provider mechanisms ranging from government-run public healthcare facilities to corporate multispeciality hospitals. As expected, the current study revealed lower annual expenditure in the Govt. medical academic institutes and stand-alone rheumatology clinics in comparison to the corporate set-ups and private medical academic institutes. ​​This is expected as the overhead costs of running a corporate setup are high. In a recent analysis by Yadav et al. exploring the health care utilization and expenditure inequities in India to strengthen the public healthcare system to ensure accessibility and quality of care [33].

In a study from the UK, higher educational level was reported to be associated with higher direct and indirect healthcare-related expenditure [9]. The authors postulated that it may be related to better follow-up and medication compliance amongst this group of patients. We found lower CHE in families where the patient and head of family had higher levels of education. This could mean that with higher education they had higher capacity to pay and thus did not incur CHE.

A report from Malaysia indicated that more than two-thirds of patients experienced some degree of impairment in work productivity and activities of daily living as a result of disease [34]. In our study, only about one-fourth were gainfully employed, among whom nearly 25% reported work absenteeism reflecting substantial socioeconomic impact. Prevalence based cost-of-illness models from Malaysia, Taiwan and United Arab Emirates over a five-year period have demonstrated high annual per-patient costs associated with SLE, from both payer and society perspectives [35, 36, 37].

This is the first of its kind cost-of-illness analysis in a real-world setting of SLE patients during their routine outpatient visits, from rheumatology setups across all regions of India. This report is strengthened by a structured assessment identifying parameters which lead to catastrophic expenditure. We believe that this data can be generalized to the entire Indian SLE population during their chronic care in India as we have captured information from all categories of healthcare delivery systems across our country. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged such as self-declared information about education, occupation, family income and expenditure. The annual expenditure estimates were derived by extrapolating expenses reported in the preceding 3 months, however, this approach is subject to recall bias and may not accurately capture long-term spending trends. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of study limited the ability to assess fluctuations in healthcare-related expenditure. Moreover, we included patients who had at least one year follow up, this meant exclusion of expenditure at the start of illness where expenditure is usually high.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights of healthcare expenditure associated with SLE. Approximately one-third of patients with SLE incurred catastrophic health expenditure, and it was more frequent among younger individuals, those with lower educational attainment, and individuals from lower socioeconomic strata. Further, hospitalisation, active disease, higher damage, multimorbidity, and type of treating health facilities had significant associations with higher direct medical expenditure. Notably, nearly three-fourth of patients reported out-of-pocket payments for medical care, with limited access to health insurance coverage or government-sponsored healthcare schemes.
