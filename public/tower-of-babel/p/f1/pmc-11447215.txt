First fluoride ceramics lasing at 605 nm at ambient temperature fabricated from chemically synthesized powders
Authors: Yiyu Li, Hiroyuki Yada, Yuki Ichikawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaser ceramics have emerged as promising candidates of solid-state laser gain media. However, most laser ceramics are currently focused on lasing in the infrared wavelength range. The very limited options of host materials and the increased optical scattering losses at shorter wavelengths within ceramics have greatly limited the development of visible laser ceramics. Here we report visible laser ceramics with a composition of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2, in which a visible (orange) laser oscillation at 605 nm has been successfully achieved at room temperature pumped by InGaN blue laser diodes with a maximum slope efficiency of 8.1%. The ceramics were fabricated by vacuum hot pressing (VHP) of wet-chemistry synthesized 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 powders, followed by hot isostatic pressing (HIP). It was found that the visible laser ceramics exhibited a lower optical scattering loss along the processing pressure direction of the VHP process, indicating a uniaxial anisotropy in optical transmission within the ceramics. To our best knowledge, the current work is the first report on visible laser ceramics lasing in the orange region at ambient temperature, fabricated from chemically synthesized powders, which could pave the way for future scientific research on and industrial applications of more sophisticated and cost-effective visible laser ceramics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73172-7.Subject terms: Lasers, LEDs and light sources; Optical materials and structures; Materials for optics

Main

The rapid development of ceramic technology has enabled the emergence of highly transparent, laser ceramics as promising materials for solid-state laser gain media. Compared with the conventional solid-state laser gain media including single crystals and glasses, ceramics have the merits of excellent thermo-mechanical properties, capability of cost-effective and large-size productions, and good quality control of laser-active dopants, which are essential for reliable laser performance in industrial applications1–3. Since the 1960s when the infrared lasing in transparent Dy2+:CaF2 ceramics was first realized4, extensive efforts have been made to develop laser ceramics (with successful lasing demonstrations) based on various oxide and non-oxide materials hosts, such as Nd3+:ThO2-Y2O35, Nd3+:Y3Al5O12 (YAG)6, Yb3+:YAG7, Er3+:YAG8, Nd3+:Lu3Al5O12 (LuAG)9, Yb3+:LuAG10, Yb3+:Lu2O311, Yb3+:Y2O312, Yb3+:Sc2O313, Yb3+:Ca10(PO4)6F2 (FAP)14, F2−:LiF15, Yb3+:CaF2-SrF216, Er3+:CaF217, Yb3+:CaF218–21, Nd3+:SrF222, Nd3+,Y3+:SrF223, Cr2+:ZnSe24, and Fe2+:ZnSe25.

However, most laser ceramics that have been researched so far are mainly focused on lasing in the infrared wavelength range, rather than in the visible wavelength range. Since the excited state absorption needs to be avoided or suppressed in host materials for the realization of laser oscillation in gain media26, fluoride-based materials are almost the only option of host materials for visible solid-state lasers operating at ambient temperature. For visible laser ceramics, the option is further limited to those with the cubic crystal structure, to achieve high transparency by minimizing the optical scattering or birefringence in ceramics resulting from the difference in refractive index between crystal axes1. Moreover, due to Rayleigh scattering, the optical scattering losses that result mainly from residual pores and impurities within ceramics1 are more pronounced in the visible wavelength range, which makes the high densification and purity of ceramics more demanding for high transparency, and thus makes it more technically challenging to achieve visible laser oscillation in ceramics.

As far as visible laser ceramics are concerned, there are only two published reports. In 2011, Basiev et al.27 achieved lasing at 639 nm in 0.3%Pr3+:SrF2 ceramics under optical pumping at 444 nm by InGaN blue LDs in continuous-wave (CW) mode. The Pr3+:SrF2 polycrystalline ceramics were fabricated by a hot pressing/forming method, in which the single crystal was fabricated first, and the single crystal was deformed via hot pressing/forming to form polycrystalline ceramics. However, the involvement of single crystal growth in ceramic fabrication process could make the process complicated, expensive, and time-consuming. In 2020, Fujita et al.28 reported a laser oscillation at 616 nm in Pr3+:YAG ceramics (fabricated by Konoshima, Inc.) at 40 K. However, the laser operation was only realized at 40 K, as this cryogenic temperature was required to suppress the unfavorable excited state absorption in YAG. Currently, the development of visible laser ceramics that can enable laser operation at ambient temperature and can be fabricated using more cost-effective and time-saving processes is still needed.

Indeed, following the first demonstration of visible lasing in Pr3+:SrF2 ceramics, several studies have been performed recently on the development of Pr3+-doped alkaline-earth metal fluoride (Pr3+:AF2; A = Ca, Sr, or Ba) based transparent ceramics fabricated using alternative fabrication methods for potential visible laser applications29–36. On the one hand, due to the various transitions in the blue, green, orange, and red spectral range (see Supplementary S1 for the detailed illustration) that can be excited by InGaN-based blue LDs with a wavelength of approximately 440–450 nm, Pr3+-doped fluoride single crystals have been extensively researched for solid-state visible laser applications37–44. On the other hand, these alkaline-earth metal fluorides not only meet the stringent requirements for host materials of potential visible laser ceramics (see Supplementary S2 for the illustration of crystal structure of AF2), but also have wide transmission wavelength ranges, low phonon energies, short UV-Vis cut-off wavelengths, and good thermal conductivities, which are of benefit to potential visible laser applications45,46. In these recent studies29–36, rather than using hot pressing/forming of single crystal precursors, vacuum hot pressing of chemically synthesized fluoride powders was generally used as the alternative and more cost-effective method to fabricate the Pr3+:AF2 based transparent ceramics. It was further revealed that the co-doping non-radiative rare-earth ions (as the buffer ion) in the Pr3+:AF2, such as Gd3+ and La3+, led to enhancements in the Pr3+-induced visible photoluminescence emission33–36. However, none of these Pr3+:AF2 ceramics were reported to succeed in visible lasing. This could be attributed to either the relatively low optical transmittance or the insufficient photoluminescence emission in the ceramics.

In the present study, we have also adopted Pr3+:SrF2 as the primary material for visible laser ceramics. Based on a previous study reporting the co-doping effects of the non-radiative ion, Y3+, on Pr3+:SrF2 single crystals47, we have co-doped Y3+ into the Pr3+:SrF2 ceramic system to enhance the Pr3+-induced absorption and visible photoluminescence emission. The highly transparent ceramics with a composition of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 were fabricated via VHP of wet-chemistry synthesized powders, followed by HIP. Visible lasing in the orange wavelength range at 605 nm was successfully demonstrated in the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics at ambient temperature by pulsed pumping using InGaN blue LDs. This marks the development of the first visible laser ceramics fabricated from chemically synthesized powders that realized lasing in the orange region at ambient temperature.

Results

Characterizations of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics

Highly transparent 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics have been fabricated by VHP of chemically synthesized powders, followed by HIP (see Methods). The resulting 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics are primarily composed of the fluorite phase (see Supplementary Sect. 2 for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of ceramics). The synthesized 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramic powders are composed of nanoparticle agglomerations, and the resulting ceramics are well densified (see Supplementary Sect. 3 for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis of synthesized powders and fabricated ceramics). Figure 1a displays a photo of a polished, cylinder-shaped 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramic sample. During the VHP process, a cylinder die was used to fabricate the ceramics, where a uniaxial pressure was applied on the vertical direction of the cylinder-shaped ceramics, as shown in Fig. 1c. This vertical axis is defined as a axis. The polished, cylinder-shaped ceramic sample is highly transparent along the a axis, as the words underneath the ceramic are clearly observed. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, the cylinder-shaped ceramic sample was further cut and polished to be a cuboid-shaped ceramic sample shown in Fig. 1b.

Figure 2b shows the absorption spectra of the cuboid-shaped ceramic sample along a, b, and c axes (see Supplementary Sect. 4 for the calculation of absorption coefficient). The absorption in the spectra is a combined effect of optical scattering and Pr3+-activated absorption in the ceramics. In the spectral range where there are no dopant-activated absorptions, the absorption coefficient quantitatively provides a direct insight into the optical scattering or transmission of the ceramics, regardless of the thickness/length of the measured geometric optical path. The spectra show that αa, the absorption coefficient along the a axis, is generally smaller than αb or αc, the absorption coefficient along the b or c axis, at the same wavelength. This indicates that the optical scattering loss along the a axis is smaller than that along the b or c axis (see Supplementary Sect. 5 for detailed comparisons). Along the axes (b and c axes) that are perpendicular to the uniaxial pressure direction during the VHP process (a axis), the absorption coefficients are similar. For example, at 605 nm, αa (0.126 cm− 1) is much less than half of αb (0.335 cm− 1) and αc (0.336 cm− 1). In contrast, an isotropy in Pr3+-activated absorption is confirmed with the similar Pr3+-activated absorptions at 442 and 467 nm (see Supplementary Sect. 5 for the definition and estimation) along the three axes. These results further quantitatively verify the uniaxial high transmission (low optical scattering) in the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics, which is likely correlated with the uniaxial pressure applied during the VHP process.

Figure 2c shows the images of a green laser light source going through the cuboid-shaped ceramic sample along the a, b, and c axes, which further visualizes this uniaxial anisotropy in transmission or optical scattering. Figure 2c (i) displays the case where the a axis is parallel to the propagation direction of the green laser. It is shown that the optical scattering sources inside the sample uniformly modify the laser spot. In contrast, when the b axis (Fig. 2c(ii)) or the c axis (Fig. 2c(iii)) is parallel to the laser’s propagation direction, a degradation in transparency and a characteristic stripe-like pattern are observed, indicative of a stripe-like structure within the ceramics along the b-c plane. The stripes are oriented parallel to the b-c plane (perpendicular to the a axis), with an estimated interval of a few tens of µm, based on the scale in Fig. 2c. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. S6, the light scatterings along the a, b, and c axes clearly display this uniaxial anisotropy in transmission of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics, as demonstrated in comparative images of the laser spot on the screen after the laser goes through the cuboid-shaped ceramic sample (see Supplementary Sect. 6).

The photoluminescence behaviors of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics have also been characterized (see Supplementary Sect. 7). The highest emission cross section is 20.8 × 10− 20 cm2 at 605 nm (in the orange wavelength range), corresponding to the3P0→6H3 transition in Pr3+. The lifetime of the orange photoluminescence is determined to be 26.8 µs. It is also noted that the thermal conductivity of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics was measured to be 3.3 W/mK, which is about 2.5 times lower than that of pure SrF2 ceramics (8.4 W/mK) fabricated using a similar method (see Supplementary Sect. 3). This is due to the phonon scattering induced by doping Pr3+ and Y3+ into the SrF2 host48.

The pulses with 100 µs duration from InGaN blue LDs in overdriven mode (wavelengths of 442 nm and 467 nm) were used as the excitation source as shown in Fig. 3c (see Supplementary Sect. 9 for details of the laser experimental setup) to avoid the possible thermal damage in the ceramics due to the aforementioned relatively low thermal conductivity. The wavelength of 442 nm and 467 nm of these commercially available LDs were employed corresponding to the absorptions/excitations at 442 nm (3H4→3P2) and 467 nm (3H4→1I6+3P1) of the 0.5%Pr3+, 5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics. The ceramic sample was placed inside the spherical cavity at the Brewster angle. The cavity had a high reflectivity in the spectral range of 515–640 nm for visible lasing in this wavelength range. As demonstrated in the photograph in Fig. 3d, these components facilitated the oscillation of the pulsed laser at an orange wavelength. The spot image is displayed in the inset of Fig. 3d. The laser’s spectrum shows a peak wavelength of 605 nm and a full width at half maximum of about 0.8 nm. To our best knowledge, this is the first demonstration of visible laser ceramics working at ambient temperature that are fabricated using chemically synthesized powders.

Figure 4a shows the relationship between the peak power of the laser output and the absorbed peak power of the pump pulse (see Supplementary Sect. 10 for the estimation of the absorbed peak power). The slope efficiency is 8.1%, and the threshold (absorbed) pump (peak) power is 3.3 W. The repeatability of the ceramic fabrication method has been verified with four other independently fabricated ceramics that also exhibited similar laser oscillations (see Supplementary Sect. 11). Despite the batch-to-batch difference, all of the ceramic samples have succeeded in lasing. The measured slope efficiencies vary from 1.4 to 8.1%, and the threshold pump powers are in the range from 2.1 to 7.1 W. Figure 4b describes the results of M2 measurements of the laser. The caustic curves for both of the x and y directions exhibit clear quadrature behaviors, and the fitting yields values of M2x = 1.075 and M2y = 1.080. These good values are consistent with the laser beam profile image (inset of Fig. 4b), which has a Gaussian-like circular shape. The high quality of the beam mode can be attributed to the isotropic characteristic along the a axis of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics.

Discussion

Although it is determined by XRD analysis that 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics are mainly composed of the isotropic, cubic fluorite phase, a uniaxial anisotropy in transmission (optical scattering) in the ceramics is observed (as shown in Fig. 2c and Fig. S4), and further quantitatively verified by the comparison of absorption coefficient along different axes in a cuboid shaped sample. In addition, as aforementioned, this uniaxial anisotropy is correlated with the uniaxial pressure employed during the VHP process, as the observed stripes (as shown in Fig. 2c) are oriented perpendicular to the uniaxial pressure direction. Based on the phase diagram and thermal behaviors of SrF2-YF3 reported by Sobolev et al.49,50, apart from the primary cubic fluorite phase, a slight amount of an anisotropic, tetragonal phase derived from the distortion of the cubic fluorite structure may be present in the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics, which could lead to birefringence in the ceramics. We assume that the uniaxial pressure applied during the VHP process may not only facilitate the distortion of the cubic fluorite phase, but also have some aligning or preferred orientation effects on the ceramic grains with the tetragonal phase51, making the optic axis of the tetragonal phase, possessing a very similar refractive index to that of the cubic fluorite, be aligned along the uniaxial pressure direction. As a result, the birefringence only leads to the non-uniform refractive index distribution along the b and c axes within the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics, which is shown as the stripe-like pattern in Fig. 2c. Thus, the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics exhibit the uniaxial anisotropy in transmission (optical scattering) along the a axis that is along the uniaxial pressure direction. Microscopically, the stripe-like structure or texture in the ceramics may be correlated with the aligning or preferred orientation effects. It should be noted that the XRD pattern of the tetragonal phase, which is derived from the distortion of the cubic fluorite structure, has a very similar XRD pattern to that of the cubic fluorite, making it very difficult to distinguish the two phases in our XRD analysis50. Further studies on XRD refinement and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) are suggested to characterize the tetragonal phase qualitatively and quantitatively, as well as to determine the assumed distortion and aligning effect of the uniaxial pressure.

As demonstrated in Figs. 3 and 4, pulsed lasing at 605 nm in the orange wavelength range has been achieved in 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics while red lasing at 639 nm in both Pr3+:SrF2 ceramics27 and single crystals44 has been reported in previous studies. The co-doping of Y3+ in Pr3+:SrF2 leads to the change in relative intensity of the visible emission peaks in the photoluminescence emission spectrum, which contributes to the difference in lasing wavelength. Moreover, the addition of Y3+ (a non-radiative ion that does not show luminescence) reduces the number of [Pr3+-Pr3+] clusters that lead to luminescence quenching, by forming luminescent [Y3+-Pr3+] clusters42,43, which is beneficial enhancing the Pr3+-activated absorption and photoluminescence. When laser-active trivalent lanthanide ions are doped into the alkaline-earth metal fluoride hosts, as the doping concentration increases, the lanthanide dopants tend to aggregate easily and form clusters. The interatomic interaction in the clusters can decrease the quantum efficiency and emission intensity52,53. Previous reports have demonstrated that the co-doping of another non-radiative rare earth ion, Gd3+, into Pr3+:SrF2 or Pr3+:CaF2 single crystals led to lasing at 605.98 nm42 and 642 nm43, respectively, by reducing the quenched [Pr3+-Pr3+] clusters and forming the [Gd3+-Pr3+] clusters. Similarly, in our study, the co-doping in Pr3+:SrF2 leads to the lasing in the orange spectral range. The variation in the photoluminescence emission spectra is attributed to the difference in local environment or symmetry of Pr3+, resulting from the difference in the co-dopant, material type, and host material.

Detailed comparisons are performed in terms of the laser operation characteristics between our study and the previous studies on visible laser ceramics. The Pr3+:YAG ceramics exhibited slope efficiencies of 0.6–0.8% and threshold pump power of < 0.5 W at 616 nm at 40 K28. The higher threshold pump power in the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics in our study can be due to the higher optical losses. The absence of the excited state absorption in the fluoride system can lead to the higher slope efficiency in the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics in our study at room temperature compared with Pr3+:YAG ceramics in ref28 at 40 K. The 0.3%Pr3+:SrF2 ceramics fabricated by hot pressing/forming of single crystals exhibited a slope efficiency of 9.1% and a threshold pump power of < 150 mW at 639 nm27. Although the direct comparison is not reasonable due to the difference in the laser wavelength, the higher threshold pump power (≥ 2.0 W) in our ceramics can be due to the optical losses in our ceramics, and the comparable slope efficiency can be related to a suppression of the thermal effect in our pulse pumping setup.

Outlook

To the best of our knowledge, this visible laser oscillation using 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics as the laser gain medium is the first successful demonstration of a visible laser at ambient temperature using ceramics fabricated from chemically synthesized powders. This development opens possibilities for the future of visible ceramic laser technology, including the potential for lasing at red, orange, green, and blue wavelengths. In addition, the ceramic fabrication method in this study, starting from chemically synthesized powders without the use of expensive single crystals, contributes to more cost-effective developments of future visible laser ceramics. The chemical synthesis of the ingredients should also facilitate the tuning of the composition for improved laser efficiency. For instance, in the ceramic system, alternative alkaline-earth metal fluorides (such as CaF2, BaF2, or their solid-solutions) can be adopted as host materials, and other non-radiative rare-earth ions (e.g. Lu3+, Gd3+, and La3+) can be co-doped with Pr3+. The primary goal of future research is to improve transparency, emission intensity, and thermal conductivity of the ceramics, aiming at higher slope efficiencies and lower threshold pump powers. Further study may be also required to elucidate the uniaxial anisotropy in transmission or optical scattering of the fabricated 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics. The above-mentioned potential improvements in optical properties and cost-effectiveness of the fabrication method in this study, combined with other advantages of laser ceramics, such as the capability of mass productivity, good thermo-mechanical property, and feasibility of gradient doping, will lead to more affordable and flexible developments of fluoride visible laser ceramics with tunable laser performances. With the foreseeable advancement of high-power blue and shorter-wavelength LD technologies, these fluoride visible laser ceramics can serve as a viable platform for future sophisticated visible lasers or amplifiers used in scientific research and industrial applications.

Methods

Powder synthesis of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2

The 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 powders were synthesized via a wet-chemistry precipitation method. First, commercial Sr(NO3)2 (Thermo Scientific Chemicals), Y(NO3)3·6H2O (Thermo Scientific Chemicals), and Pr(NO3)3·6H2O (Thermo Scientific Chemicals) were added into deionized (DI) water, strictly based on the stoichiometry of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2. After stirring and dissolving, the aqueous solution of Sr(NO3)2, Y(NO3)3, and Pr(NO3)3 (Solution 1) was prepared. Meanwhile, the aqueous solution of excessive HF acid (Solution 2) was prepared by adding commercially available HF acid (48%; Thermo Scientific Chemicals) into DI water. Second, Solution 1 was added into Solution 2 directly, and milky precipitates were immediately formed through the wet-chemistry precipitation. After an aging process of 24 h, the precipitates were then washed through a washing cycle that included repeated centrifuging and dispersing via ultrasonicating (repeated for 3 times). Lastly, the washed precipitates were dried by freeze drying for 72 h to obtain the synthesized ceramic powders.

Fabrication of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics

The 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics were fabricated via VHP of synthesized ceramic powders, followed by HIP. The dried powders were loaded into a cylinder-shaped graphite die with a diameter of 15 mm, and then sintered via VHP at 660 °C for 2 h under a uniaxial pressure of 40 MPa, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. The heating rate was 5 °C/min. After the dwelling process, the furnace was cooled down to room temperature naturally, and the uniaxial pressure was immediately released. After the VHP process, the sintered, cylinder-shaped 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics were further post heat-treated by HIP at 750 °C for 2 h under 200 MPa at a heating rate of 5 °C/min in argon gas. After the dwelling process, the furnace was cooled down to room temperature naturally, and the isotropic pressure was gradually released. Thus, highly transparent, cylinder-shaped 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics were fabricated.

Cutting and polishing of 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics for laser tests

The cylinder-shaped, highly transparent 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics were first cut and mirror-polished to cuboid-shaped ceramics. The cuboid-shaped ceramics were further Brewster-angle cut (see Supplementary Sect. 8) using in-house developed polishing jigs. Then, the ceramics were finely polished through optical-grade polishing using the combination of polishing films and slurries on a polishing machine, with a sequence of 30 μm alumina film, 6 μm diamond film, 1 μm alumina film, and 0.05 μm alumina film with a silica polishing slurry.

Characterizations of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics

The phase composition of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics was measured via XRD (MiniFlex 600, Rigaku), equipped with a CuKα radiation (40 kV, 15 mA). SEM (JSM-6060, JEOL) was employed to observe the microstructure of the surface of the ceramics. The in-line transmittance of the ceramics was measured using UV-Vis spectroscopy (UV-3600, Shimadzu). The photoluminescence emission was measured using a spectrofluorometer (FluoroMax-4, Horiba Scientific) at an excitation wavelength of 442 nm. The lifetime of the photoluminescence emission was determined by the pulsed pumping setup (see Supplementary Sect. 7). The thermal conductivity of the ceramics was tested using the laser flash measurement based on ASTM E1461.

Laser test of the 0.5%Pr3+,5%Y3+:SrF2 ceramics

As shown in Fig. 3c, the spherical cavity for the laser test uses two concave mirrors with the same radius of 75 mm. The pump mirror has a high reflectivity (HR: > 99%) in the range of 515 to 640 nm and, the output coupler (OC) has a transmission efficiency of 2%. This spherical cavity facilitated the Brewster angle arrangement of the ceramics shown in Fig. 3b. To avoid thermal damage to the ceramics, pulsed pumping was applied. Since the lifetime of the emission was estimated to be 26.8 µs (see Supplementary Sect. 7), the pulse duration was selected to be 100 µs, which is long enough to obtain the saturation of the stored energy. By using four LDs, a peak power as high as 33.4 W was realized. The pump arrangement is detailed in Supplementary Sect. 9.
