Peritumoural adipose tissue promotes ferroptosis resistance by 3-hydroxykynurenine-mediated suppression of ferritinophagy
Authors: Yan-Yu Zhang, Yi Han, Yue-Tao Tan, Yun-Xin Lu, Meng-Yao Ma, Yong-Qiang Zheng, Hao-Jie Chen, Hai-Yan Fu, Hai-Yu Mo, Qi-Nian Wu, Xiao-Jing Luo, Kun Liao, Wen-Qi Chen, Zhao-Lei Zeng, Hai-Long Piao, Hong-Li Du, Jun-Zhong Lin, Tian Tian, Rui-Hua Xu, Huai-Qiang Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The peritumoural adipose tissue (PAT) is a key contributor to cancer therapy resistance, yet its role in regulating ferroptosis remains unclear. Here we demonstrate that PAT confers ferroptosis resistance to cancer cells by upregulating ferritin (FTH1/FTL) and sequestering intracellular iron. PAT-derived kynurenine (KYN) was identified as the principal mediator. KYN is taken up by cancer cells and metabolized to 3-hydroxykynurenine, which directly binds to nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4). This interaction inhibits NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, preventing ferritin degradation and limiting the free iron pool required for ferroptosis. In murine models, pharmacological inhibition of the KYN pathway synergized with PD-1 blockade to overcome ferroptosis resistance and suppress tumour progression. These findings reveal a PAT–KYN–ferritinophagy axis that promotes ferroptosis resistance, highlighting the potential of targeting adipose–tumour cross-talk to enhance immunotherapy in PAT-associated tumours.

Main

The tumour microenvironment (TME) is dynamically shaped by adjacent stromal components1. Among these, peritumoural adipose tissue (PAT) is anatomically associated with many malignancies, including breast cancer, renal cell carcinoma and abdominal tumours such as gastric cancer (GC), colorectal cancer (CRC) and ovarian cancer2. Clinically, PAT correlates with poor prognosis in various cancers3 and serves as an independent predictor of recurrence-free survival in CRC4. Adipocytes promote tumour proliferation through metabolic coupling, primarily by supplying free fatty acids and lactate to fuel tumour cell metabolism5–7. In parallel, adipocytes also upregulate CD36 in cancer cells to enhance free fatty acid uptake and secrete adipokines to facilitate invasion and migration8,9. Despite these advances, the precise mechanisms by which PAT drives tumour progression and therapeutic resistance remain incompletely understood.

Ferroptosis is a regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, making intracellular iron pivotal for its sensitivity10. Ferritin, composed of ferritin heavy chain (FTH1) and ferritin light chain (FTL) subunits, maintains iron homeostasis by sequestering or releasing Fe2+ (ref. 11). This process is linked to ferroptosis through ferritinophagy, in which nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) binds ferritin to degrade it and release stored iron, promoting lipid peroxidation12. Previous studies revealed that activation and diffusion of lysosomal iron initiates membrane lipid oxidation and ferroptosis13,14, and evidence links ferritinophagy to radiation-induced ferroptosis15, thus highlighting its critical role in regulating ferroptosis. Conversely, suppression of ferritinophagy impairs iron release and confers ferroptosis resistance16,17. Pharmacologically disrupting the NCOA4–FTH1 interaction reduces ferrous iron levels and ferroptosis susceptibility18. Emerging evidence has established ferroptosis as a distinct form of immunogenic cell death that critically regulates CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumour immunity and immunotherapy19,20. Our previous work also demonstrated that interleukin-1β within the TME protects tumour cells from ferroptosis by sustaining iron homeostasis21. However, the role of PAT in modulating ferroptosis remains unknown.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the role of PAT in modulating ferroptosis susceptibility in cancer cells. We found that PAT elevates 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-HK) in cancer cells, which directly binds NCOA4 to suppress ferritinophagy and confer ferroptosis resistance. Furthermore, inhibiting the kynurenine (KYN) pathway synergized with anti-PD-1 therapy to achieve robust tumour eradication in adipose-rich tumour models. This study reveals a PAT-driven metabolic mechanism of ferroptosis resistance and proposes a dual-targeted strategy combining KYN pathway inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) to enhance immunotherapy in PAT-associated tumours.

Results

PAT confers tumour ferroptosis resistance and reduces the immunotherapy efficacy

In advanced CRC and GC, tumours frequently invade through the serosa and directly contact visceral adipose tissue (Fig. 1a). To investigate whether PAT affects ferroptosis, we established two in vivo models by implanting CRC tumours either into the mid-dorsal subcutaneous region (a non-adipose site) or into the inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT, an adipose-associated site), as described in a previous report22 (Fig. 1b). First, using doxycycline-inducible knockdown of the key ferroptosis suppressor glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4-iKD)23, we found iWAT-implanted DLD1 xenografts were more resistant to ferroptosis than subcutaneous tumours (Fig. 1c,d and Extended Data Fig. 1a–c). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) confirmed lower lipid peroxidation (4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA)) in iWAT-associated tumours after GPX4 silencing (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 1d). To validate the generality of PAT-mediated ferroptosis resistance, we then assessed the tumour response under pharmacological and immunological stress. In C57BL/6J mice with MC38 tumours, iWAT-implanted tumours showed markedly reduced sensitivity to the ferroptosis inducer imidazole ketone erastin (IKE), with tumour growth inhibition declining from 82.5% in subcutaneous implants to 0.24% in PAT-associated tumours (Fig. 1f,g and Extended Data Fig. 1i). This IKE effect was confirmed as ferroptosis dependent, as it was rescued by Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1) in subcutaneous tumours (Extended Data Fig. 1h). Accordingly, IHC revealed diminished lipid peroxidation in IKE-treated iWAT tumours (Fig. 1h and Extended Data Fig. 1j). Interestingly, PAT promoted tumour growth only in immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice, not in immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice (Fig. 1c,f). This pro-tumour effect was abolished upon CD8+ T cell depletion (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). Consistently, in Rag1− / − mice, tumour growth was similar at non-adipose and iWAT sites, but adoptive transfer of CD8+ T cells selectively inhibited tumours at the non-adipose site (Extended Data Fig. 2c,d). Thus, PAT protects tumours from both exogenous ferroptosis inducers and endogenous CD8+ T cell-mediated ferroptotic stress.

Given that CD8+ T cell-derived interferon-γ sensitizes tumour cells to ferroptosis during ICB, we additionally compared the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy between different implantation sites. In line with our previous observations, iWAT-implanted CRC tumours exhibited reduced response to anti-PD-1 treatment compared with subcutaneous tumours (Fig. 1i,j and Extended Data Fig. 2e), accompanied by diminished lipid peroxidation (Fig. 1k and Extended Data Fig. 2f). Consistent with the adipose-embedded status of serosa-invading tumours, lipid peroxidation signals (4-HNE/MDA) were significantly attenuated in stage III–IV tumours compared with stage I tumours that remain anatomically segregated from adipose compartments (Fig. 1l,m and Extended Data Fig. 2g,h) Collectively, these results demonstrate that PAT confers resistance to ferroptosis in cancer cells, thereby impairing antitumour immunity and reducing the efficacy of immune checkpoint therapy.

PAT confers tumour ferroptosis resistance via ferritin-mediated iron sequestration

Because PAT mediates ferroptosis resistance in tumours, we speculated that PAT secretes protective factors. To test this hypothesis, we established an adipose-derived conditioned media (adipose-CM) assay for in vitro validation (Fig. 2a). Adipose-CM significantly attenuated RAS-selective lethal 3 (RSL-3)-induced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in human CRC cells (Fig. 2b,c and Extended Data Fig. 3a) and murine MC38 cells (Extended Data Fig. 3b,c). Although antioxidants such as vitamin E and extra-mitochondrial coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) can suppress ferroptosis25,26, their levels were unchanged in adipose-associated tumours (Extended Data Fig. 3d,e). To systematically elucidate the mechanism by which PAT contributes to ferroptosis resistance, we conducted quantitative proteomic analysis and found that tumour cells cultured with adipose-CM exhibited distinct proteomic signatures compared with controls (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 1). Notably, both FTH1 and FTL were markedly upregulated in adipose-CM-treated cells (Fig. 2e), while other core ferroptosis regulators remained unchanged (Extended Data Fig. 3f). Ferritin is typically upregulated by iron supplementation. However, FerroOrange staining showed reduced intracellular Fe2+ in adipose-CM-treated cells (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 3h), indicating the ferritin increase was not due to iron accumulation. Dual knockdown of FTH1 and FTL attenuated this Fe2+ depletion and abolished the ferroptosis protective effect of adipose-CM against RSL-3 in DLD1 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 2h–j and Extended Data Fig. 3i–l), indicating that ferritin is essential for adipose-CM-mediated cytoprotection.

To validate this in vivo, we established an mCherry-labelled MC38 tumour model and performed subsequent analyses (Extended Data Fig. 3m). MC38 tumour cells from adipose-rich inguinal sites showed higher FTH1/FTL expression, lower intracellular Fe2+, reduced lipid peroxidation and greater ferroptosis resistance compared with subcutaneous tumours (Fig. 2k–n). The peritoneum is an adipose-rich environment and a common site for gastrointestinal metastasis27,28. We therefore established a peritoneal tumour model by intraperitoneally injecting MC38 cells into C57BL/6J mice. These tumours showed higher FTH1/FTL expression than subcutaneous tumours (Fig. 2o). Notably, FTH1/FTL knockdown inhibited peritoneal tumour progression, as indicated by decreased tumour mass and burden (Fig. 2p,q), confirming the pathophysiological relevance of this axis in promoting peritoneal metastasis. Collectively, these results demonstrate that PAT confers ferroptosis resistance by upregulating ferritin to sequester intracellular iron.

PAT establishes a KYN-rich TME to elevate ferritin in cancer cells

Building on evidence that PAT-derived factors induce ferritin-mediated iron sequestration, we further characterized the components responsible for this effect. The activity of adipose-CM to upregulate FTH1/FTL was heat-stable and remained after 3-kDa filtration (Fig. 3a), pointing to low-molecular-weight metabolites as the active factor. Ferritin subunits decrease with adipose-CM dilution, confirming this induction by concentrated metabolites (Extended Data Fig. 3n). Metabolomic profiling identified KYN as the only metabolite consistently elevated in both adipose-CM and iWAT-implanted MC38 tumours (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, conditioned medium from PAT of patients with CRC and MC38 tumour-bearing mice contained 124 μM and 97 μM KYN, respectively (Supplementary Table 3). KYN, a tryptophan (TRP) metabolite whose production is limited by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1)/tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase 2 (TDO2)29, led us to hypothesize that PAT creates a KYN-rich microenvironment to promote ferroptosis resistance. Targeted metabolomics of tumour interstitial fluid (TIF) confirmed this, showing elevated KYN (up to 50 µM) and depleted TRP in TIF from PAT-associated tumours versus subcutaneous tumours (Fig. 3g–i). In vitro, adipose-CM also increased intracellular KYN (Fig. 3j,k). We next evaluated potential transporters (SLC7A5, SLC7A8, SLC7A11 and SLC1A5)30–32 using small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown with KYN-FITC uptake assays in DLD1 cells. Knockdown of either SLC7A5 or SLC7A11 led to a modest reduction in KYN uptake, suggesting cooperative involvement of multiple transporters rather than one dominant pathway (Extended Data Fig. 3o,p). Accordingly, PAT from MC38 iWAT tumours expressed higher levels of IDO1 and TDO2 than the tumour tissue itself (Fig. 3l). Conditioned medium from PAT of mice treated with IDO1/TDO2 inhibitors (referred as adipose-CM + IDO/TDOi) failed to upregulate FTH1/FTL, an effect rescued by exogenous KYN (Fig. 3m).

To determine whether KYN secretion is unique to PAT or adipose tissue in general, we compared PAT from MC38 tumour-bearing mice with contralateral iWAT (normal white adipose tissue (WAT)), analysing both tissue samples and their corresponding conditioned media. KYN levels were significantly higher in both PAT tissue and its conditioned medium (Fig. 3n,o), confirming enhanced production specifically in the tumour-adjacent niche. To identify the cellular sources, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on PAT from MC38 iWAT tumours. This revealed that Ido1 was highly expressed in adipocytes, while Tdo2 was high in pre-adipocytes (pACs) and adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs) (Fig. 3p,q and Supplementary Table 4). Because adipocytes, pACs and ASCs collectively constitute 54.8% of PAT cellularity (36%, 14.8% and 4%, respectively), we concluded they are the primary contributors to KYN production. These findings demonstrate that PAT establishes a KYN-rich TME to drive ferritin upregulation, iron sequestration and ferroptosis resistance. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PAT confers ferroptosis resistance to tumour cells by establishing a KYN-rich TME, which promotes iron sequestration and suppresses ferroptosis through ferritin upregulation.

KYN-derived 3-HK is responsible for PAT-mediated tumour ferroptosis resistance

KYN is taken up by cancer cells and metabolized into downstream products33 (Extended Data Fig. 4a). To determine whether KYN or its metabolites directly regulates ferritin, we systematically tested intermediates of the TRP–KYN pathway. Among them, KYN, 3-HK and 3-hydroxyanthranilic acid (3-HAA) were found to confer ferroptosis resistance to cancer cells (Extended Data Fig. 4b), and notably, these are sequential products of two enzymatic steps. To further reveal their roles, we genetically inhibited these metabolic steps. In KYN-supplemented cultures, knockdown of KYN monooxygenase (KMO), which converts KYN to 3-HK, reduced FTH1/FTL, increased Fe2+ level, enhanced lipid peroxidation and sensitized cells to ferroptosis (Fig. 4a–d and Extended Data Fig. 4c–f). Conversely, silencing kynureninase (KYNU), which converts 3-HK to 3-HAA, potentiated KYN’s effects, further elevating FTH1/FTL, reducing Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation, and protecting against cell death (Fig. 4e–h and Extended Data Fig. 4g–j). Consistently, direct supplementation with 3-HK induced a dose-dependent upregulation of FTH1/FTL, decreasing labile iron, suppressing lipid peroxidation and enhancing ferroptosis resistance (Fig. 4i–l and Extended Data Fig. 4k–n). Next, we wondered by which mechanism 3-HK regulates ferritin. As neither 3-HK nor KYN affected FTH1/FTL mRNA (Extended Data Fig. 5a), we investigated whether this is regulated by post-translational regulation. Pulse-chase assays revealed that 3-HK delayed FTH1 degradation, indicating it stabilizes ferritin by inhibiting its breakdown (Extended Data Fig. 5b). As ferritin is degraded by NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy34, we hypothesized that 3-HK stabilizes ferritin by inhibiting this process. Indeed, 3-HK treatment decreased lysosomal Fe2+ in cancer cells (Fig. 4m). Moreover, in NCOA4-knockout (KO) cells, both 3-HK and KYN failed to upregulate ferritin and lower intracellular Fe2+ (Fig. 4n,o and Extended Data Fig. 5c,d). The protective effect of 3-HK against RSL-3 was also attenuated in NCOA4-KO cells compared with controls (Fig. 4p,q), confirming that NCOA4 is essential for the function of 3-HK. Of note, pretreatment with low-dose 3-HK (2.5 μM) conferred significant ferroptosis protection in MC38 and DLD1 control cells. By contrast, in NCOA4-KO MC38 cells and FTH1‑KO DLD1 cells, low concentrations of 3‑HK failed to confer protection, and partial rescue was observed only at higher concentrations (≥20 μM) (Extended Data Fig. 5e,f). This partial rescue at high concentrations might be attributed to the radical-trapping antioxidant (RTA) activity of 3-HK. Notably, even at high concentrations, the protective effect of 3-HK against ferroptosis remained stronger in control cells than in NCOA4-KO or FTH1-KO cells (Extended Data Fig. 5e,f). Consistently, both KYN and 3-HK exhibited only mild RTA activity at low concentrations (Extended Data Fig. 5h). These findings indicate that, at low concentrations, the anti‑ferroptotic effect of 3‑HK critically depends on ferritin‑mediated iron metabolism modulation, whereas at higher concentrations, the anti-ferroptotic effect of 3-HK results from a combination of ferritinophagy suppression and RTA activity.

We further examined the involvement of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), a primary target of TRP metabolites, and similarly found that AHR KO did not impair 3-HK-mediated FTH1/FTL upregulation (Extended Data Fig. 5i), thereby excluding AHR involvement. Previous studies have reported that metabolites of KYN pathway can protect against ferroptosis by activating the NRF2/SLC7A11 antioxidant axis or through direct radical scavenging at high concentrations (50–200 μM)31,35. However, we found that low-dose 3-HK (5 μM) upregulates FTH1/FTL protein levels without altering SLC7A11 expression and maintained its ability to induce FTH1/FTL even in NRF2- and SLC7A11-KO cells (Extended Data Fig. 5j–l), suggesting a mechanism independent of the canonical antioxidant pathway. Furthermore, 3-HK-mediated upregulation of FTH1/FTL was independent of CD44-mediated iron uptake (Extended Data Fig. 5m,n). Taken together, these findings delineate a metabolite-driven axis whereby PAT-derived KYN is converted to 3-HK within tumour cells, leading to suppression of NCOA4-mediated ferritin degradation, reduced iron bioavailability and enhanced ferroptosis resistance.

To further elucidate how 3-HK modulates ferritinophagy, we investigated its impact on the interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1, an essential step initiating ferritinophagy12. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis revealed that treatment with 3-HK or KYN markedly reduced the colocalization of FTH1 and NCOA4 in tumour cells (Fig. 5a and Extended Data Fig. 6a). Consistently, immunoprecipitation (IP) assays demonstrated that 3-HK weakened the binding between FTH1 and NCOA4 (Fig. 5b and Extended Data Fig. 6b). Notably, KYNU knockdown similarly reduced the binding between FTH1 and NCOA4, whereas knockdown of KMO exerted the opposite effect (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, we purified full-length NCOA4 (NCOA4-fl) and ferritin-binding domain (termed NCOA4383–522) to conduct in vitro pull-down experiments36. The results showed that 3-HK specifically inhibited the direct binding between FTH1 and NCOA4, whereas neither KYN nor the downstream metabolite 3-HAA exhibited this effect (Fig. 5d,e). As expected, IF analysis revealed a colocalization of biotinylated 3-HK and NCOA4 in tumour cells (Extended Data Fig. 6c). Targeting KYN biosynthesis sensitizes PAT-associated tumours to ferroptosis and immunotherapy

Accordingly, a treatment with IDO/TDO inhibitors (epacadostat and LM10) significantly lowered KYN in PAT and 3-HK in tumours (Extended Data Fig. 7a,b). Immunoblotting confirmed corresponding downregulation of FTH1, FTL and NCOA4, consistent with enhanced ferritinophagy (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Thus, inhibiting the KYN pathway in vivo effectively disrupts the ferritin accumulation axis in tumours.

Discussion

Adipose tissue is abundant in the human body and distributed across multiple sites38. Clinically, PAT is frequently observed surrounding malignancies including CRC, GC, renal cell carcinoma and intra-abdominal metastatic tumours22,27,39, independent of obesity, which is now recognized as a state of low-grade chronic inflammation linked to metabolic disorders. Adipocytes are known to support tumours by supplying fatty acids, coupling metabolically and secreting adipokines22,40,41. This study demonstrates that PAT suppresses tumour ferroptosis and confers immune resistance by inhibiting ferritinophagy. We identify 3‑HK as the metabolite linking KYN metabolism to iron homeostasis and show that dual inhibition of IDO1 and TDO2 restores ferroptosis sensitivity and improves checkpoint blockade efficacy in PAT‑associated tumours.

The mechanism by which PAT regulates ferroptosis has been unclear, with prior studies showing conflicting effects of adipocytes28,42. Here, we find that PAT elevates L-KYN levels in the TME, leading to intracellular increase of its downstream metabolite 3-HK. Beyond known antioxidant effects, we demonstrate that 3-HK directly suppresses ferritinophagy. It upregulates ferritin and depletes labile iron independently of NRF2 or AHR31. While 3-HK displayed only weak RTA activity at concentrations up to 50 μM (ref. 35), we found that as little as 5 μM of 3-HK was sufficient to upregulate ferritin levels and inhibit ferroptosis. This protection was lost in FTH1- or NCOA4-deficient cells, confirming ferritinophagy inhibition as the core mechanism. Given the established role of ferritinophagy in releasing lysosomal iron to trigger ferroptosis13, 3-HK-mediated inhibition of this process represents a pivotal mechanism by which PAT confers ferroptosis resistance.

Emerging evidence indicates that metabolites function not only as biological substrates but also as pivotal signalling molecules, modulating cellular processes43. For instance, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate and leucine regulate AMPK and mTORC1 signalling, the two central pathways governing metabolic homeostasis, by directly binding their regulators44,45. More recently, ω-6 linoleic acid was reported to modulate mTORC1 activity46. Similarly, several TRP metabolites are recognized as ligands of the AHR47,48. As an extension, our study demonstrates that 3-HK functions as an endogenous regulator of ferritinophagy and iron homeostasis. Notably, cellular iron availability influences TRP and KYN metabolism through haem biosynthesis, because haem is an essential cofactor for IDO1 and TDO249. This suggests a bidirectional regulatory circuit between iron homeostasis and TRP metabolism, potentially mediated by 3-HK.

Cancer immunotherapy has revolutionized cancer treatment. However, a considerable proportion of patients, especially those with CRC and GC with peritoneal metastases, respond poorly to ICB50,51. Our prior work showed that modulating the TME can boost immunotherapy52–54. IDO1 was a promising target55, but its inhibition combined with anti-PD-1 failed in a phase III trial56, indicating other resistance mechanisms. Here, we found that both IDO1 and TDO2 are highly expressed in PAT, driving KYN accumulation and conferring ferroptosis resistance. Because ferroptosis is immunogenic, our findings suggest that dual inhibition of IDO1 and TDO2 may synergize with immunotherapy to improve outcomes.

In conclusion, our study uncovers a mechanism by which PAT protects tumour cells from ferroptosis and impairs immunotherapy by elevating 3-HK and suppressing ferritinophagy. These findings deepen our understanding of the mechanistic link between PAT and antitumour immunity and highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting 3-HK-mediated iron regulation.

Ethical approval

Patient samples were obtained from the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC). The collection of these samples was conducted with written informed consent and in accordance with the regulations established by the Medical Ethics Committee of SYSUCC. The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of SYSUCC (B2024-759-01). The characteristics of participants in these experiments are described in Supplementary Table 5. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University (no. L025504202205013) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Animals meeting humane endpoints were euthanized promptly, and all survivors were humanely euthanized at study completion.

Cell culture

Cell lines, including 293T, DLD1, HCT116, A549 and CT26, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection. The MC38 (mouse colon adenocarcinoma) cell line was purchased from iCell Bioscience. Dialysed foetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used in metabolite-treatment experiments. For routine culture, cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 or Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Wisent) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cells were negatively tested for mycoplasma contamination and authenticated on the basis of short tandem repeat polymerase chain reaction (PCR) DNA profiling before use.

siRNA transfection and stable cell line generation

Sequences of oligonucleotides, including primers, RNA interference and CRISPR, are provided in Supplementary Table 7. siRNAs targeting human and mouse KMO and KYNU were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China). For siRNA transfection experiments, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentiviral particles were produced by cotransfecting HEK293T cells cultured in T25 flasks with packaging plasmid psPAX2 (2.8 μg), envelope vector pMD2.G (1.56 μg) and target plasmid (2 μg, including small guide (sg) NCOA4, shSLC7A11, shGPX4 and sgAHR) using the X-tremeGENE HP transfection reagent (Roche), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 60 h, the supernatant containing the lentivirus was collected, centrifuged at 1,800g for 10 min at 4 °C and filtered through a 0.45-µm membrane. Target cells at approximately 70% confluency in six-well plates were transduced with the viral supernatant in the presence of polybrene (8 µg ml−1). After 24 h, the viral medium was replaced, and puromycin selection was initiated. For clonal selection, cells were plated at a density of one cell per well in 96-well plates and cultured in a medium containing 20% FBS for 10 days. Clones derived from single cells were expanded and confirmed by immunoblotting.

Adipose-CM preparation

For preparation of human CRC peritumoural adipose-CM, freshly resected surgical specimens containing adjacent adipose tissue were promptly transferred to sterile tubes containing 5 ml of serum-free RPMI 1640 medium. The tissues were washed five times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual blood. The adipose tissue was carefully dissected using sterile surgical blades, followed by three additional PBS washes. Isolated adipose samples were incubated in T25 flasks containing serum-free RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Following incubation, the supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 400g for 10 min at 4 °C and filtered through 0.22-μm membranes to remove cellular debris and potential microbial contaminants. Control medium was prepared under identical conditions but without adipose tissue and served as a baseline reference.

For murine peritumoural adipose-CM, PAT was collected from the iWAT region of C57BL/6J mice bearing subcutaneous MC38 tumours. The procedures for tissue dissection, culture conditions and subsequent dilution for use in cell experiments were identical to those described for human adipose tissue. For subsequent cell treatment, adipose-CM or control media were diluted 1:3 with routine culture media (1640 or DMEM, each containing 10% FBS) before experimental use.

For IDO1/TDO inhibitor-treated adipose-CM, C57BL/6J mice bearing subcutaneous MC38 tumours were treated with epacadostat and LM10 for 7 days. PAT was collected from the iWAT region of corresponding mice. The procedures for tissue dissection and subsequent dilution for use in cell experiments were identical to those described for human adipose tissue, except for the supplementation of epacadostat and LM10 in the culture conditions.

DPPH assay

The 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl radical (DPPH) assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, DPPH was dissolved in absolute ethanol to prepare a 0.1 mM solution. KYN or 3-HK at various concentrations was then added to the DPPH solution, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The reduction in absorbance at 517 nm resulting from radical scavenging was calculated relative to the PBS control. Trolox (20 μg ml−1) served as the positive control.

Cytosol CoQ10 assay

Cytosol fractions of cells were obtained using a mitochondria isolation kit (Thermo 89874). In brief, 3 × 107 cells were suspended in mitochondria isolation reagent and homogenized by Dounce tissue grinder on ice, followed by centrifuging at 700g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, and the cytosol fraction was separated by centrifuging at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit. CoQ10 was detected using a CoQ10 ELISA kit (Cusabio, CSB E14081h), according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Quantitative proteomics analysis

Quantitative proteomics analysis was conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology. In brief, MC38 cells treated with either control medium or adipose-CM were lysed using lysis buffer composed of 8 M urea, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride and 2 mM EDTA. Cell lysates were subjected to ultrasonic disruption on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris. The resulting supernatant was collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Subsequently, the collected protein samples were used for proteolytic desalting and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis. Raw data-independent acquisition (DIA)-MS data were processed using DIA-NN (version 1.8.1) in library-free mode. Spectral library generation and peptide identification were performed using deep learning algorithms incorporated into DIA-NN. The UniProtKB mouse proteome database (uniprotkb_proteome_UP000000589_mouse_54910_20240528.fasta), containing 54,910 sequences, was used for target matching. The match between runs (MBR) function was enabled to construct a spectral library directly from DIA data and improve identification consistency across runs. The false discovery rate was controlled at <1% for both protein- and precursor-level identifications. Quantification was based on the remaining high-confidence identifications.

Lipid peroxidation, ferrous iron and GSH measurement

For lipid peroxidation analysis, the cells were seeded in six-well plates one day before treatment. After exposure to the indicated compounds for the specified durations, cells were collected via trypsinization and resuspended in 200 μl of PBS containing 5 μM C11-BODIPY 581/591 (Invitrogen). Following a 30-min incubation at 37 °C, lipid peroxidation was measured using a Beckman flow cytometer with excitation at 488/510 nm, and fluorescence was detected using the FITC channel. Data were analysed using CytExpert 2.4 software.

Ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels were quantified using an iron assay kit (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Alternatively, intracellular Fe2+ levels were assessed using FerroOrange dye (Dojindo) and visualized by confocal microscopy (LSM880 with Fast Airyscan, Carl Zeiss) or analysed using a Beckman flow cytometer. In addition, intracellular GSH content was measured using the GSH-Glo Glutathione Assay Kit (Promega), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS; Promega). Cells were seeded in 96-well clear-bottom plates at a density of 2,000 cells per well in media with or without the indicated metabolites. On the following day, cells were treated with ferroptosis inducers, including RSL-3 and erastin, as well as cell death inhibitors, such as liproxstatin. After 48 h of treatment, 20 μl of MTS reagent was added to each well (containing 200 μl of medium), followed by incubation for 1 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a Synergy Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek), and cell viability was expressed relative to the untreated control group.

Immunoblotting, IP, IHC and IF analysis

Immunoblotting and IP analysis were performed according to the standard procedure as previously reported21,52,53. The proteins were extracted from cultured cells or tissues using either RIPA or NP-40 lysis buffer, and the protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), followed by immunoblotting using corresponding antibodies against GPX4 (1:1,000, ab125066, Abcam), FTH1 (1:1,000, 4393S, Cell Signaling Technology), FTL (1:1,000, ab109373, Abcam), SLC7A11 (1:1,000, 12691S, Cell Signaling Technology), TFRC (1:1,000, ab214039, Abcam), FPN (1:1,000, 26601-1-AP, Proteintech), IRP2 (1:1,000, 29916-1-AP, Proteintech), tubulin (1:1,000, 2125S, Cell Signaling Technology), NCOA4 (1:1,000, ab314553, Abcam), KYNU (1:1,000, ab320833, Abcam), KMO (1:500, MAB8050, R&D Systems), AHR (1:1,000, ab309491, Abcam), NRF2 (1:2,500, ab137550, Abcam) and β-actin (1:1,000, 4970S, Cell Signaling Technology).

For IP analyses, cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 1% Triton X-100) containing protease inhibitors on ice for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and incubated with primary antibodies (NCOA4, 1:100, ab314553, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by an additional 4-h incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads at the same temperature. The beads were then collected using a magnetic stand, and the supernatant was removed and discarded. After adding 1 ml of wash buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 0.05% Tween-20), the beads were thoroughly resuspended, then collected again with the magnetic stand, and the supernatant was discarded. This washing step was repeated twice. Finally, the beads were boiled in SDS–PAGE sample buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 6.8, 20 mM dithiothreitol, 1% SDS, 10% glycerol and 0.1% bromophenol blue).

IHC was performed as previously described21,53. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was carried out in citrate buffer (pH 6) at 121 °C for 10 min. After retrieval, the slides were cooled at room temperature for 1 h, followed by treatment with 3% H2O2 for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, the slides were blocked with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer (Beyotime) for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-4-HNE antibody (Abcam, cat. no. ab46545) diluted 1:100 in Antibody Diluent (Dako). After five washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with Dako REAL EnVision/HRP, Rabbit/Mouse for 45 min at room temperature, and finally visualized using Dako REAL DAB. Other primary antibodies used include FTH1 (4393S, Cell Signaling Technology), FTL (ab109373, Abcam), MDA (1:100, JAIMMD030, AdipoGen Life Sciences). Two independent pathologists evaluated the stained sections. A composite histological score was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positively stained tumour cells (0–100%) by the staining intensity (graded 0–3). IF was performed as previously reported21.

For the IF analysis, MC38 and DLD1 cells were fixed and stained according to standard protocols using primary antibodies against KYN (IS003, ImmuSmol), NCOA4 (ab314553, Abcam) and FTH1 (ab75973, abcam), followed by incubation with fluorescence dye-conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstaining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Images were acquired using an LSM880 microscope equipped with Fast Airyscan (Carl Zeiss).

Duolink in situ PLA

PLA was performed using a Duolink in situ PLA kit (Sigma-Aldrich). In brief, DLD1 and MC38 cells were fixed and incubated with primary antibodies (at 1:100 dilution), secondary PLA probes and DAPI according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Positive reactions were detected with a 594-nm fluorescent label detection kit. Hybridization between the two PLA-plus and PLA-minus probes leading to a fluorescent signal occurs only when the distance between the two antigens is less than 40 nm. Images of the cells were obtained using an LSM880 with Fast Airyscan (ZEISS).

RNA isolation and quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of RNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix Kit (Takara). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the GoTaq qPCR Master Mix (Promega). Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin, and relative quantification was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

LC–MS-based metabolomics analysis

Comprehensive untargeted metabolites were analysed according to the previously described method, with modifications52,57. In brief, samples stored at −80 °C were thawed on ice and vortexed for 10 s. Extraction of hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds was performed using extraction solvent A (acetonitrile:methanol = 1:4, v/v) or extraction solvent B (methyl tert-butyl ether:methanol = 3:1, v/v), each containing an internal standard (IS). The sample extracts were analysed using an LC–electrospray ionization (ESI)–MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD; MS, QTRAP6500+ System). Linear ion trap (LIT) and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans were acquired on a QQQ–LIT mass spectrometer (QTRAP), QTRAP LC–MS/MS system, equipped with an ESI Turbo IonSpray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). A specific set of multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions was monitored for each period, based on the metabolites eluting within that period.

TIF was obtained through centrifugation, following the methodology outlined in a previous study58. In brief, tumour-bearing mice were euthanized, and tumours were rapidly dissected within 2 min. The excised tumours were weighed, briefly rinsed with saline and blotted on filter paper. The entire preprocessing procedure was completed within approximately 2 min. The tumours were then placed onto 20-mm nylon mesh (Merck Millipore) mounted on 50-ml conical tubes and centrifuged at 106g for 10 min at 4 °C. TIF collected in the conical tubes was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Then, 50 μl sample was extracted with 250 μl of methanol, and 10 μl IS mixed solution (250 ng ml−1) was added into the extract as IS for the quantication. The extract was vortexed for 3 min, then kept at −20 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 12,000 revolutions min−1 for 10 min at 4 °C, and 150 μl of the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was again centrifuged at 12,000 revolutions min−1 for 5 min at 4 °C, and 100 μl of the supernatant was transferred and analysed using an LC–ESI–MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD; MS, QTRAP 6500+ System). LIT and QQQ scans were acquired on a QQQ–LIT mass spectrometer (QTRAP), QTRAP 6500 + LC–MS/MS system, equipped with an ESI Turbo IonSpray interface, operating in both positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). TRP and its metabolites were analysed using scheduled MRM. Data acquisitions were performed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). Multiquant 3.0.3 software (Sciex) was used to quantify all metabolites. Mass spectrometer parameters, including the declustering potentials (DP) and collision energies (CE) for individual MRM transitions, were set following further DP and CE optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions was monitored for each period, based on the metabolites eluting within that period. All metabolomics analysis were conducted by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology.

Pulse-chase assay using HPG

HEK293T and MC38 cells at 80% confluence were washed twice with prewarmed PBS and then methionine-starved in DMEM supplemented with 10% dialysed FBS for 1 h. After starvation, 2 mM L-homopropargylglycine (L-HPG) was added to the culture medium for a 4-h pulse labelling. Cells were subsequently washed twice with prewarmed PBS and replaced with DMEM containing 10% FBS. Cells were collected at indicated timepoints (0, 2, 4, 6, 12 and 24 h) and lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 1% Triton X-100) containing protease inhibitors on ice for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and subjected to IP using an anti-FTH1 antibody coupled with Protein A/G magnetic beads. HPG-labelled FTH1 proteins were then biotinylated via a copper-catalysed click reaction with biotin alkyne according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Click-&-Go Click Chemistry Reaction Buffer Kit). The beads were washed three times with wash buffer (25 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl and 0.05% Tween-20) and boiled in SDS–PAGE sample buffer. Biotinylated FTH1 proteins were detected by western blot analysis using an HRP-conjugated anti-biotin antibody.

In vitro pull-down assay

Purified Flag-NCOA4-fl or His-NCOA4383–522 protein was pre-incubated with the indicated metabolites (10 μM), followed by incubation with purified ferritin overnight at 4 °C in the presence of either anti-Flag magnetic beads or Ni-NTA agarose beads. Beads were washed six times with wash buffer, and bound proteins were eluted using SDS sample loading buffer. Eluted samples were analysed by immunoblotting.

SPR assay

The binding affinity between NCOA4 proteins and metabolites (3-HK, L-KYN or 3-HAA) was assessed using a Biacore T200 system equipped with CM7 sensor chips (GE Healthcare) at 25 °C. All measurements were performed in triplicate. Data were analysed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software (version 2.0) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The CM7 sensor chip was activated with a mixture of 200 µM 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide and 50 µM N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) at a flow rate of 10 µl min−1 for 10 min. Subsequently, 30 µl of purified NCOA4 protein (as described above), pre-adjusted to pH 4.5 by mixing with 150 µl of 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5), was immobilized at a flow rate of 10 µl min−1 for 5 min over three cycles. Blocking was performed using 1 M ethanolamine at 10 µl min−1 for 10 min. The reference channel was prepared with an identical activation and blocking protocol, except that PBS (pH 4.0) was injected in place of protein solution. Both flow cells were equilibrated with PBS. Stock solutions of 3-HK, L-KYN and 3-HAA (1 mM each) were serially diluted in PBS to concentrations ranging from 0 to 20 µM and injected at 30 µl min−1 for 180 s. Sensorgrams were recorded using Biacore T200 Control Software (version 2.0), and response values from the reference cell were subtracted from the experimental channel. Binding kinetics and affinity parameters (association and dissociation constants) were derived by globally fitting the data to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

BLI

NCOA4 full-length (NCOA4-fl) protein was biotinylated using an EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotinylation Kit (Thermo) and subsequently exchanged into PBS using Zeba Spin Desalting Columns. BLI was performed on an Octet Red 96 system (ForteBio) under standard conditions (30 °C with 1,000 rpm shaking) at a signal collection frequency of 2.0 Hz. After a 10-min equilibration, Ni-NTA and Super Streptavidin biosensors (Sartorius) were used to immobilize His-NCOA4383–522 or biotinylated NCOA4-fl protein, respectively, onto the sensor tips. A series of 3-HK concentrations diluted in PBS was then introduced to associate with the immobilized proteins. All raw sensorgrams were baseline-aligned, and kinetic parameters were calculated by fitting the data to a 1:1 binding model. Data were processed and curves were generated using Octet Analysis Studio 11.0 software.

Protein expression, purification and verification

The plasmid encoding His-tagged NCOA4 (pET28a-His-NCOA4383–522) was transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells. Protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside at 30 °C for 24 h. Cells were collected and lysed by sonication, and His-tagged proteins were purified using Ni-NTA resin (GenScript) at 4 °C for 4 h. The resin was washed with buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl and 20 mM imidazole until the absorbance returned to baseline. Elution was performed using an imidazole gradient (0–250 mM) in the same buffer. Proteins were further purified using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column on an ÄKTA FPLC system (Cytiva), followed by concentration and desalting in PBS using Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filter units (Millipore). Protein concentration was determined with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

For Flag-tagged protein expression, HEK293F cells were transfected with the pcDNA3.1-Flag-NCOA4 plasmid. After 48 h, the cells were lysed, and Flag-NCOA4 proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag magnetic beads. Beads were washed six times with wash buffer containing 136.89 mM NaCl, 2.67 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.76 mM KH2PO4 and 0.5% Tween -20. Bound proteins were eluted with elution buffer composed of 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl and 300 μg ml−1 Flag peptide. Flag peptides were subsequently removed, and the protein was concentrated using Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter units (Millipore). Protein concentration was again determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit.

Thermal shift assay

The cells were collected and lysed by three cycles of freeze–thaw using liquid nitrogen and water at room temperature. Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000g for 15 min to obtain the supernatant. The resulting lysates were incubated with either PBS or 3-HK (5 µM) for 60 min at room temperature. Protein thermal stability was evaluated by exposing the samples to a series of increasing temperatures for 5 min. Following cooling to room temperature, samples were subjected to western blot analysis to assess protein stability changes upon ligand binding.

Antibody-mediated CD8+ T cell depletion

For CD8+ T cell depletion in the iWAT model, C57BL/6J mice were administered six intraperitoneal injections of anti-CD8a (BioXCell) or isotype control (BioXCell). The treatment regimen included an initial dose of 300 μg on day 1, followed by five additional doses of 200 μg on days 4, 8, 12 and 16 after tumour implantation. To assess the efficacy of CD8+ T cell depletion, blood samples were collected via retro-orbital puncture on days 3, 10 and 18, and analysed by flow cytometry using antibodies targeting non-overlapping CD8 epitopes (BioLegend).

Acquisition of OT-1 CD8+ T cells and adoptive transfer model

OT-1 CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of OT-1 mice by mechanical dissociation using 40-μm cell strainers in 6-cm culture dishes, followed by two washes with magnetic-activated cell sorting buffer. After centrifugation, the cell pellets were resuspended in red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated for 5 min to lyse red blood cells. The purified OT-1 CD8+ T cells were then maintained in DMEM-based culture medium supplemented with IL-2 (10 ng ml−1, 212-12, PeproTech), T-Select H-2Kᵇ OVA Tetramer (Medical & Biological Laboratories) and β-mercaptoethanol (100 mM, M3148, Sigma-Aldrich) and cultured for 3–5 days. For adoptive transfer experiments in the iWAT tumour model, Rag2−/− mice were inoculated with 1 × 106 MC38-OVA tumour cells. Seven days after tumour engraftment, these mice received a systemic administration of 1 × 106 OT-1 CD8+ T cells via tail vein injection.

Mouse PAT sample preparation and Chromium Single Cell Gene Expression Flex

MC38 tumours were implanted adjacent to the iWAT of C57BL/6 mice and allowed to grow until reaching approximately 300–400 mm3. PAT samples were then collected from four mice and processed using the Chromium Single Cell Gene Expression Flex platform (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. This service was provided by Guangzhou Yuanxin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. In brief, after tissue dissociation and nuclei isolation, nuclei were fixed following the 10x Genomics Demonstrated Protocol for Cell & Nuclei Fixation (CG000478). Nuclei concentration and integrity were assessed intermittently using a LUNA dual fluorescence cell counter (Logos Biosystems). Following probe hybridization, uniquely barcoded samples were quantified and pooled in equal nuclei numbers according to the 10x Genomics Chromium Fixed RNA Profiling Multiplexed Samples Pooling Workbook (Rev B, CG000565). Pooled nuclei were washed, and approximately 20,000 nuclei per sample were targeted for partitioning into gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs) using Chip Q. Subsequent steps included GEM barcoding, GEM recovery, cDNA pre-amplification and library construction, as outlined in the user guide (CG000527). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeqX platform with paired-end dual-indexing, achieving a depth of ~300 million reads per sample with 2 × 150 read length. Demultiplexing of sequencing libraries was performed using bcl2fastq (Illumina), and FASTQ files were processed with Cell Ranger v7.1.0 multi-pipeline (10x Genomics) aligned to the GRCh38-2020-A reference genome.

PDX model

The PDX model was established as described previously with slight modifications59. Freshly resected human CRC specimens were dissected into 1–2-mm3 fragments under sterile conditions. Each PDX tumour fragment was surgically implanted into the iWAT depot of NOD-scid IL2rgamma (NSG) mice. Once the tumours reached a volume of 50–100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to one of four treatment groups: vehicle, IKE (30 mg kg−1, intraperitoneally, once daily), LM10 (200 mg kg−1, per os (p.o.), once daily), epacadostat (300 mg kg−1, p.o., once daily) or a combination of treatments as indicated. Tumour growth was continuously monitored using digital calipers, and tumour volume progression was tracked over time.

Experimental animals

Female OT-1, C57BL/6J and BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old), NSG mice and Rag1 −/− mice (5–6 weeks old) were obtained from GemPharmaTech. BALB/c nude mice were sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in a temperature-controlled facility, maintained on a 12-h light/dark cycle. All experimental procedures using these mice were performed in accordance with a research protocol approved by the Institutional Laboratory Animal Care and Use Committee of SYUCC (L025504202205013).

Mouse tumour models

For the non-adipose and iWAT tumour models, MC38 cells (2 × 106), DLD1 cells (2 × 106) or CT26 cells (1 × 106) in 100 μl of PBS were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flank of C57BL/6J, BALB/c nude or BALB/c mice to generate the first generation of cell-derived xenograft tumours. Tumours were collected when they reached 300–400 mm3 in volume, at which point they were minced into 1–2-mm3 fragments. These cell-derived xenograft tumour fragments were then subcutaneously inoculated into the middle dorsal or inguinal WAT region of the indicated mice under anaesthesia. Once the tumours reached 50–100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to different treatment groups for the indicated treatments. Tumour size was routinely measured using a caliper, and tumour volume was calculated using the following formula: volume (mm3) = 0.5 × (longer diameter × shorter diameter2).

In the in vivo ferroptosis-inducing model, DLD1 cell-derived tumours with shNC (negative control) or shGPX4 (doxycycline-inducible) were implanted into the middle dorsal or iWAT region of BALB/c nude mice. Three days after implantation, mice were randomly assigned to different groups, and doxycycline (1 mg ml−1, HY-N0565, MedChemExpress) was administered in drinking water. Similarly, MC38 cell-derived tumours were implanted in the middle dorsal or iWAT region of C57BL/6J mice. When the tumours reached 50–100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups receiving IKE (30 mg kg−1, intraperitoneally, daily, S7242, Selleckchem) or anti-PD-1 (100 μg per mouse, BE0146, BioXCell). For IKE preparation, a 60 mg ml−1 stock solution was first made in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted in a 1:1:8 ratio of DMSO:castor oil (ethoxylated):saline to obtain the working solution. The control group received only the vehicle solution. Anti-PD-1 or IgG (100 μg per mouse) was administered intraperitoneally every 3 days for a total of three doses. Tumour growth was continuously monitored using digital calipers, and tumour volume progression was tracked over time.

For the iWAT model with IDO/TDO inhibitor administration, the TDO2 inhibitor LM10 (200 mg kg−1, p.o., HY-33298, MedChemExpress) and the IDO1 inhibitor epacadostat (300 mg kg−1, p.o., HY-15689, MedChemExpress) were suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose and administered daily via oral gavage. Lip-1 (10 mg kg−1, once daily, HY-12726, MedChemExpress) was administered intraperitoneally starting on day 0. The administration protocols for IKE or anti-PD-1 followed the same procedures as described above.

In peritoneal metastasis models, MC38 cells (5 × 105) were injected intraperitoneally into mice. Lip-1 (10 mg kg−1, once daily) was administered intraperitoneally starting on day 0 after cell injection. Epacadostat (300 mg kg−1, once daily) and LM10 (200 mg kg−1, once daily) were administered via oral gavage starting on day 3 after cell injection. Anti-PD-1 (100 μg per mouse) was delivered intraperitoneally every 3 days starting on day 7, for a total of three doses. Tumour burden was assessed using in vivo imaging.

Statistics and reproducibility

No formal power analysis was performed to predetermine sample size. Our sample sizes were chosen on the basis of those used in previous studies21. During experiments and outcome assessment, the investigators were not blinded to group allocation. In animal studies, mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups unless stated otherwise. No data were excluded from the analyses. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.). Three independent biological replicates have been performed for each experiment. Numbers of experimental replicates, P values and the tests can be found in the figures or figure legends. Comparisons of two groups were performed with a two-sided unpaired Student’s t-test or two-sided paired Student’s t-test. Pairwise comparisons among ≥3 independent groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. For tumour growth curve groups, pairwise comparisons were conducted with two-way ANOVA plus Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Survival functions were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, and the log-rank test was used to compare the survival distributions of difference groups. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad version 10.0. A P value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance. All statistical tests were two-sided. All experiments were repeated at least three times (unless otherwise specified) and yielded consistent results.
