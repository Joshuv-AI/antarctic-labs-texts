A conserved Sas10/C1D domain protein, CaLrp1, is a critical regulator of hyphal development and pathogenicity in Candida albicans
Authors: Yang Yang, Dongxu Song, Mingjiao Huang, Chaoqin Sun, Juan He, Ruxia Cai, Weiwei Jin, Na Zhao, Guo Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCandida albicans represents the predominant opportunistic fungal pathogen in clinical contexts, with its virulence contingent upon multilayered regulatory networks. CaLrp1 (orf19.5067) encodes a conserved Sas10/C1D domain-containing protein, though its precise biological role has remained uncharacterized.ResultsCaLrp1 deleted and complemented strains were constructed to systematically investigate its function in C. albicans. Abrogation of CaLrp1 resulted in delayed hyphal induction, a loss of rugose colony morphology, and a pronounced reduction in invasive growth on solid media. Furthermore, the mutant demonstrated impaired epithelial translocation, diminished capacity to damage Caco-2 cells, and enhanced susceptibility to macrophage phagocytosis in vitro. In murine models of gastrointestinal colonization and systemic infection, CaLrp1-deficient strains displayed restricted proliferative capacity, reduced tissue pathology, and significantly attenuated virulence. Transcriptomic profiling via RNA-seq revealed that CaLrp1 deletion induced extensive transcriptional reprogramming, marked by the downregulation of genes implicated in ribosome biogenesis, rRNA processing, energy metabolism, and multiple virulence-associated pathways. Notably, key hyphal development and adhesion genes—including Als1, Ece1, and Hwp1—were significantly suppressed, a finding consistent with the phenotypic defects observed on solid substrates.ConclusionsOur results establish CaLrp1 as a critical regulator of C. albicans morphogenesis and pathogenicity, likely operating via the maintenance of cellular RNA-processing and protein-biosynthetic fidelity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05111-7.

Background

Candida albicans is the leading etiological agent of invasive candidiasis in humans and asymptomatically colonizes approximately 90% of the population as a commensal [1]. It typically resides on mucosal surfaces, including the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, and reproductive tract, as well as the skin [2, 3]. Disruption of host homeostasis—through events such as antibiotic-mediated dysbiosis, iatrogenic immunosuppression, or clinical procedures that compromise mucosal integrity or immune function—can precipitate its transition from commensalism to pathogenicity. This shift results in a diverse disease spectrum, ranging from superficial mucosal infections (e.g., oropharyngeal and vulvovaginal candidiasis) to life-threatening disseminated systemic disease and candidemia [4]. Among high-risk cohorts, including critically ill patients, transplant recipients, and individuals receiving chemotherapy, the incidence of C. albicans infection rises sharply. Notably, this fungus is responsible for nearly 70% of fungal infections in such settings, with associated mortality rates exceeding 40% for systemic disease [5]. Consequently, C. albicans represents not only the most prevalent opportunistic fungal pathogen but also a significant public health burden, underscoring the critical need to delineate its pathogenic mechanisms.

The virulence of C. albicans is governed by a complex and dynamic regulatory network [6]. This polymorphic fungus undergoes reversible morphological transitions between yeast and hyphal forms, and its pathogenicity is further modulated by coordinated programs mediating host adhesion, epithelial invasion, biofilm formation, metabolic adaptation, nutrient acquisition, and immune evasion [5]. These integrated processes enable adaptation to fluctuating host microenvironments and support persistent infection [7]. Disruption of central regulatory nodes within these virulence programs frequently diminishes host colonization, reduces tissue damage, and attenuates overall virulence.

Despite considerable progress in defining the regulators of morphology and virulence in C. albicans, a large subset of its protein-coding genes remains functionally uncharacterized. In a prior study examining the antifungal peptide AMP-17, we noted significant alteration in the expression of orf19.5067 following AMP-17 treatment in C. albicans [8]. Based on this differential expression and its inferred biological relevance, we selected orf19.5067 for functional characterization. Sequence analysis indicated homology to Lrp1 in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, an RNA-binding protein in RNA processing and RNA surveillance [9], leading us to designate the gene CaLrp1. However, its biological role in pathogenic fungi has not been established.

Here, we systematically investigated the function of CaLrp1 in C. albicans. Deletion of CaLrp1 did not affect proliferation under standard growth conditions but severely impaired hyphal induction, solid-medium invasion, and morphological plasticity. In murine models of gastrointestinal colonization and systemic infection, the CaLrp1-null strain showed reduced fungal burden, limited tissue invasion, and significantly attenuated virulence. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that CaLrp1 loss triggers extensive transcriptional reprogramming, with differentially expressed genes enriched in rRNA processing, ribosome biogenesis, energy metabolism, and multiple virulence-associated pathways. Taken together, our results demonstrate that CaLrp1 is a critical regulator of C. albicans pathogenicity, operating primarily through the control of hyphal development and the maintenance of RNA homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

Candida albicans strains were routinely cultured in YPD medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% glucose) with shaking at 30 °C. Solid media included Spider agar (1% nutrient broth, 1% mannitol, 0.2% K₂HPO₄, and 1.5% agar; pH 7.2) and YPD agar supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), which were used for inducing hyphal formation and assessing invasive growth, respectively. All solid media were incubated at 37 °C.

Gene disruption and complementation

A unified CRISPR–Cas9-based genome-editing platform was applied to construct CaLrp1 deletion, complementation, GFP-tagged, and subcellular localization strains in C. albicans. All genetic manipulations were performed in the wild-type SC5314 background or its isogenic derivatives: the homozygous deletion mutant (CaLrp1-/-) and the complemented strain (CaLrp1C). The system utilized plasmid pADH100 for guide RNA (gRNA) expression and plasmid pADH99 for Cas9 expression. Donor DNA fragments were generated by overlap-PCR using primers containing the ADD-TAG sequence and were linearized prior to transformation to serve as homology-directed repair templates.

Strain construction was designed as follows: for gene deletion, the donor DNA contained 5′ and 3′ homology arms flanking the CaLrp1 open reading frame (approximately 500 bp each), which were amplified using primers containing the ADD-TAG sequence to facilitate subsequent donor assembly. For C-terminal GFP tagging, a donor fragment was constructed containing homology arms targeting the NEUT5L locus, the NEUT5L coding sequence, and a GFP sequence. To assess subcellular localization, a complementation strain expressing a C-terminal GFP fusion of CaLrp1 was generated in the CaLrp1-/- background.

Transformations were conducted using a lithium acetate/polyethylene glycol (LiAc/PEG) protocol, co-transforming recipient strains with plasmids pADH99 and pADH100 alongside the relevant linearized donor DNA. For each transformation, 2 µg plasmid DNA and 5 µg donor DNA were used. Primary transformants were selected on YPD agar supplemented with 200 µg/mL nourseothricin and incubated at 30 °C for 48–72 h. Putative GFP-tagged strains were initially identified via fluorescence microscopy, and all candidate strains were subsequently validated by PCR analysis to confirm precise genomic integration. Verified strains were then utilized for downstream phenotypic and cell biological analyses. The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Germ tube induction and morphological observation

Following overnight growth in YPD, cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended at 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL in Spider medium or YPD supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Subsequently, the cell suspensions were incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 200 rpm. Aliquots were collected at 2 and 4 h. Morphological observations were performed using confocal microscopy, and representative images were captured. Germ tube formation was quantified by counting 100 cells per field in five randomly selected fields. The germ tube formation rate (%) was calculated as (number of cells forming germ tubes / total number of cells counted) × 100%.

Assessment and quantification of invasive growth on solid media

To evaluate hyphal invasion, 5 µL of log-phase cultures (1 × 10⁵ cells/spot) were inoculated onto Spider agar or YPD agar supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Strains were incubated for 5 days. Colonies were photographed, and the underlying agar was then vertically bisected using a sterile blade to microscopically assess the depth of invasion. The horizontal expansion width (W) and vertical invasion depth (D) on solid media were measured. Measurements were performed using ImageJ on sections from at least three independent biological replicates.

Phospholipase and aspartyl protease activity assays

Phospholipase activity was assessed using egg yolk agar plates. SDA medium was supplemented with 0.5 M NaCl, 0.005 M CaCl₂, 1% fresh egg yolk emulsion, and 1.5% agar. Aspartyl protease activity was assessed using a bovine serum albumin (BSA) agar degradation assay. The medium was prepared with 1% BSA, 0.01 M KH₂PO₄ (pH 4.5), and 1.5% agar. Log-phase cultures of SC5314 (wild-type), CaLrp1-/-(deletion), and CaLrp1C (complemented) were centrifuged, washed with PBS, and adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL. A 5 µL aliquot of each strain was spotted at the center of the plate. Epithelial cell damage assay (LDH Release)

Epithelial cell damage was evaluated by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release from infected epithelial monolayers. Caco-2 cells (ATCC number: HTB-37) were seeded into 24-well plates and allowed to form confluent monolayers. Cells were then infected with C. albicans strains SC5314, CaLrp1-/-, or CaLrp1C at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 for 3 h or 6 h. Supernatants were collected, and LDH levels were quantified using a commercial LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm, and the percentage of LDH release was calculated to assess epithelial cell damage.

Transwell translocation assay

The ability of C albicans to penetrate the intestinal epithelial barrier was assessed using a Transwell system with 0.8 μm pore-size inserts. Caco-2 cells were seeded into the upper chamber and cultured until a tight, confluent monolayer was formed. Subsequently, C. albicans strains (SC5314, CaLrp1-/-, or CaLrp1C) were added to the upper chamber at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h or 24 h. Culture medium from the lower chamber was collected, serially diluted, and plated on YPD agar to enumerate colony-forming units (CFUs) representing translocated fungal cells.

Macrophage phagocytosis assay and calculation of the phagocytic index

Macrophage phagocytosis was assessed using GFP-labeled C. albicans strains (SC5314 and CaLrp1-/-) to infect RAW264.7 murine macrophages (ATCC number: TIB-71). After 1.5 h of infection, cells were washed with PBS to remove non-phagocytosed fungal cells, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 and stained with DAPI to label nuclei and TRITC-phalloidin to visualize the actin cytoskeleton. Confocal microscopy was used to acquire three-channel images (blue: DAPI; red: phalloidin; green: GFP) to evaluate fungal uptake and intracellular localization. Confocal images were also collected to qualitatively assess macrophage morphology and fungal distribution post-infection.

Immunosuppressed mouse model and gastrointestinal infection

Female SPF-grade BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the institutional animal facility. To disrupt the native gut microbiota, mice received drinking water supplemented with penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (5 mg/mL) for three consecutive days prior to infection. One day before fungal challenge, mice were intraperitoneally injected with cyclophosphamide (CTX, 150 mg/kg) to induce immunosuppression. For gastrointestinal infection, mice were orally gavaged with 200 µL of PBS containing 1 × 10⁸ CFU of C. albicans; mice receiving sterile PBS served as negative controls. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No. 2302013) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. At the indicated time points, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel, and tissues were collected for subsequent analyses.

Survival curve analysis of mouse infection models

To evaluate mortality associated with systemic or gastrointestinal infection, mice were monitored daily after inoculation, and the time of death for each animal was recorded. The observation period extended up to 21 days. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism, and statistical differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

Histopathological analysis

At designated time points post-infection, mice were euthanized and intestinal or renal tissues were collected. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with Periodic acid Schiff (PAS). Histological features were examined under a light microscope, including inflammatory cell infiltration, integrity of the intestinal epithelium, and the extent of renal tissue damage.

Systemic candidiasis model

A systemic candidiasis model was established by intravenous injection. BALB/c mice were inoculated via the tail vein with 2 × 10⁶ CFU of C. albicans (SC5314, CaLrp1-/-, or CaLrp1C) and monitored daily for survival and clinical symptoms. Mice were euthanized on days 2, 5, and 10 post-infection, and kidneys were collected for fungal burden quantification.

RNA-seq analysis

Log-phase cells of C albicans wild-type SC5314 and the CaLrp1-/- mutant were harvested, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen). RNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and samples with RIN ≥ 7.0 were submitted to BGI (Shenzhen, China) for library preparation and sequencing. Clean reads were mapped to the C. albicans SC5314 reference genome using HISAT2. Gene expression levels were quantified as FPKM values, and differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 with the criteria |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler, with adjusted p < 0.05 considered significant.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons post hoc test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, different lowercase letters (e.g., a, b, c) indicate statistically significant differences among groups under the same experimental condition or at the same time point (P < 0.05), whereas groups sharing the same lowercase letter are not significantly different. Error bars in all bar graphs represent standard deviation (SD).

Results

CaLrp1 harbors a conserved Sas10/Utp3/C1D domain and shows dual nuclear–cytoplasmic localization

Sequence analysis identified a canonical Sas10/Utp3/C1D conserved domain (Pfam: PF04000) within CaLrp1 (Supplementary Fig. 1), a domain widely conserved across eukaryotic lineages (Fig. 1A). Phylogenetic analysis further revealed CaLrp1 within the fungal Lrp1 protein subfamily and indicated a close evolutionary relationship with Saccharomyces cerevisiae Lrp1 (Fig. 1B). A homozygous deletion mutant (CaLrp1-/-) and a corresponding complemented strain were generated via a CRISPR–Cas9 strategy, with genotypes confirmed by PCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2). To determine its subcellular localization, a functional C-terminal EGFP-tagged fusion of CaLrp1 was constructed. In yeast-form cells, CaLrp1-EGFP exhibited a nucleocytoplasmic distribution, with diffuse cytoplasmic fluorescence and partial co-localization with DAPI-stained nuclei. Following induction of hyphal growth, the fluorescent signal became organized along the hyphal axis and showed pronounced enrichment at the hyphal apex (Fig. 1C). These findings delineate the dynamic localization of CaLrp1, which shifts in conjunction with morphogenetic transition.

Loss of CaLrp1 restricts hyphal growth in C. albicans

The growth kinetics analysis showed SC5314, CaLrp1-/- and CaLrp1C comparable growth kinetics, with similar proliferation rates and final cell densities in liquid YPD medium (Fig. 2A). In contrast, distinct phenotypic impairments were observed for CaLrp1-/- mutant on solid hypha-inducing media. On both Spider agar and YPD supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), the mutant formed smooth, non-filamentous colonies, while the wild-type and complemented strains produced characteristically wrinkled, filamentous colonies with radiating peripheries (Fig. 2B). Cross-sectional analysis further revealed that the CaLrp1-/- was primarily confined to surface growth, whereas SC5314 and CaLrp1C exhibited substantial agar penetration (Fig. 2C). Quantification of invasive growth parameters—colony invasion width and penetration depth—confirmed a significant reduction in invasiveness for the mutant under both inducing conditions, with the complemented strain largely restoring these phenotypes to wild-type levels (Fig. 2D).

Under liquid hypha-inducing conditions, SC5314 and CaLrp1C initiated germ tube formation within 2 h and developed elongated hyphae by 4 h. In contrast, the CaLrp1-/- displayed delayed germ tube emergence and attenuated hyphal elongation (Fig. 2E). Quantitative assessment corroborated a significantly reduced germ tube formation rate in the mutant relative to the wild-type and complemented strains (Supplementary Fig. 3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while CaLrp1 deletion does not affect vegetative growth under nutrient-replete conditions, it results in marked deficiencies in hyphal morphogenesis, invasive growth, and germ tube formation in C. albicans.

Loss of CaLrp1 attenuates virulence of C. albicans in mouse infection models

The role of CaLrp1 in host infection was assessed by both gastrointestinal colonization and systemic infection models. In the systemic model, mice infected with the CaLrp1-/- mutant showed a significant survival advantage, indicating attenuated virulence. In contrast, infection with wild-type SC5314 and CaLrp1C resulted in rapid lethality (Fig. 3A, B). Histopathological analysis corroborated these findings. Periodic acid–Schiff staining revealed that infection with CaLrp1-/- was associated with preserved tissue architecture and only mild inflammatory infiltrates. Conversely, wild-type SC5314 infection caused extensive fungal invasion, severe tissue damage, and pronounced inflammation, with CaLrp1C infection yielding an intermediate phenotype (Fig. 3C).

To quantify fungal burden in the gastrointestinal model, colony-forming units (CFUs) were quantified in kidney, intestinal tissue, and fecal samples. Mice infected with CaLrp1-/- exhibited significantly lower fungal loads in both kidney and intestinal tissues compared to wild-type-infected mice (Fig. 3D). Notably, fecal samples from CaLrp1-/- -infected mice contained significantly higher fungal counts than those from mice infected with either SC5314 or CaLrp1C, suggesting altered intestinal colonization dynamics despite reduced tissue invasion (Fig. 3D). In the systemic model, a time-course analysis demonstrated that renal fungal burdens in CaLrp1-/- -infected mice remained significantly lower than in wild-type-infected mice at days 2, 5, and 10 post-infection, with the complemented strain again displaying intermediate levels (Fig. 3E). Taken together, these results demonstrate that CaLrp1 deletion attenuates C. albicans virulence in vivo, as reflected by improved host survival, reduced tissue fungal burden, and diminished invasive capability.

Loss of CaLrp1 reduces epithelial cell damage, invasion capacity, and secreted virulence factor activity

To further delineate the function of CaLrp1 in host–pathogen interactions, a systematic evaluation of its contribution to epithelial invasion, host cell damage, immune evasion, and secreted hydrolase activity was performed. The CaLrp1-/- mutant demonstrated a marked impairment in translocation across the epithelial barrier by using a Caco-2 cell monolayer transwell model. Significantly fewer colonies were recovered from the lower chamber at both 12- and 24-hours post-infection compared to the wild-type SC5314 and CaLrp1C (Fig. 4A, B). Assessment of epithelial cytotoxicity via lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release revealed that infection with CaLrp1-/- resulted in significantly lower LDH levels at 24 h, indicating a reduced capacity to induce host cell membrane damage. In contrast, wild-type SC5314 and CaLrp1C infection elicited substantial LDH release (Fig. 4C).

Macrophage phagocytosis assays demonstrated altered host–fungus interactions. Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed that the CaLrp1-/- mutant was more readily internalized by macrophages and frequently formed intracellular clusters, whereas SC5314 and CaLrp1C exhibited minimal intracellular accumulation (Fig. 4D). Quantification of the phagocytic index confirmed a significantly higher rate of phagocytosis for the CaLrp1-/- compared to the wild type (Fig. 4E). Finally, the activity of key secreted virulence-associated enzymes was assessed. The CaLrp1-/- mutant exhibited significantly reduced extracellular phospholipase activity, evidenced by a smaller precipitation zone compared to SC5314, with partial restoration in the complemented strain (Fig. 4F). Secreted aspartyl protease activity was also significantly attenuated in the CaLrp1-/- mutant, while both SC5314 and CaLrp1C maintained robust enzymatic activity (Fig. 4G).

Loss of CaLrp1 triggers extensive transcriptomic reprogramming and perturbs ribosome- and virulence-associated pathways

To delineate the global regulatory role of CaLrp1, we conducted RNA-sequencing analysis comparing the isogenic CaLrp1-/- mutant and the wild-type SC5314 strain. Deletion of CaLrp1 precipitated comprehensive transcriptional remodeling, resulting in 3,402 differentially expressed genes (DEGs; |log₂FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05), comprising 1,565 upregulated and 1,837 downregulated transcripts (Fig. 5A). Functional enrichment analysis across Gene Ontology (GO) categories revealed widespread alterations (Fig. 5B). Within Biological Processes, DEGs were significantly associated with rRNA processing, translation, fungal-type cell wall organization, response to drugs, nutrient deprivation, hyphal growth, and pathogenesis. Molecular Function enrichment highlighted terms including RNA binding, DNA binding, ATPase activity, transcription factor binding, metal ion binding, and oxidoreductase activity. Cellular Component analysis showed predominant localization of DEGs to the nucleus, nucleolus, plasma membrane, mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum membrane, and cell wall, with pronounced nucleolar enrichment underscoring a potential role in ribosome biogenesis and RNA metabolism. KEGG pathway analysis further indicated that DEGs were most significantly enriched in the ribosome, proteasome, RNA degradation, oxidative phosphorylation, and protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum pathways (Fig. 5C), suggesting that CaLrp1 centrally coordinates ribosome assembly, energy metabolism, and proteostatic regulation.

Examination of virulence-associated gene expression revealed marked downregulation of key factors (Fig. 5D), including the adhesin Als1, the hyphal regulator Ece1, multiple ergosterol biosynthesis genes (Erg4, Erg6, Erg10, Erg12, Erg25, Erg26, Erg27), the chaperone Hsp70, and cell wall/protein modification genes (Msb3, Plb5, Pmt6, Mnn13, Mon2, Gda1). Transcriptional regulators such as Rob1, Rbt5, and Hap31 were also suppressed. These expression patterns correlate with the observed mutant phenotypes of delayed filamentation, reduced invasion, and cell wall alterations, while select upregulated genes (e.g., Iff4) may reflect compensatory regulatory responses. To substantiate the enrichment findings, we assessed expression of representative genes involved in ribosome biogenesis and the RNA exosome complex (Fig. 5E). Notable downregulation was observed for core exosome components (Rrp6, Rrp4, Rrp40, Csl4), rRNA processing factors (Nop58, Cbf5, Dbp3, Enp1), and multiple ribosomal protein genes (Rpl11, Rpl7, Rpl9, Rpl10, Rpl13, Rpl20, Rps1, Rps2, Rps5, Rps7, Rps18). This coordinated suppression underscores a profound disruption in ribosome biogenesis and RNA processing pathways upon CaLrp1 deletion.

Discussion

Candida albicans is a leading cause of invasive fungal disease globally, posing a significant threat to immunocompromised populations where it is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [1, 10]. In this study we identified a Sas10/C1D family member CaLrp1 (orf19.5067), Genetic ablation of CaLrp1 resulted in a pronounced virulence defect, characterized by impaired filamentation, compromised cell wall integrity, diminished host interaction capacity, and significantly attenuated virulence in murine models. Moreover, spot dilution assays revealed that on YPD medium, the CaLrp1-/- strain showed growth limitations at high cell densities compared to SC5314, but this difference was not observed at lower cell densities. Under stress conditions, however, the growth disparity between the CaLrp1-/- strain and the SC5314/complemented strain persisted and became more pronounced at lower cell densities (Supplementary Fig. 4). Importantly, across all conditions tested, the CaLrp1-/- strain maintained growth levels comparable to those of SC5314, indicating that loss of CaLrp1 does not cause severe or complete growth defects in C. albicans. Global transcriptomic profiling revealed that CaLrp1 deletion triggers extensive transcriptional reprogramming, with differentially expressed genes enriched in ribosome biogenesis, RNA metabolism, metabolic adaptation, and virulence-associated pathways [11]. These findings collectively establish CaLrp1 as a central coordinator of cellular homeostasis and pathogenic programs in C. albicans.

Hyphal morphogenesis is a cornerstone of C. albicans virulence, directly influencing tissue invasion, colonization, biofilm development, and immune evasion [12]. This process is governed by a well-defined transcriptional circuitry, including key regulators such as Efg1, Ume6, Brg1, and Tec1, whose deletion consistently impairs filamentation and attenuates virulence [13–15]. While the direct physical interactions between CaLrp1 and these core regulators remain to be fully characterized, our transcriptomic data show a significant coordination between CaLrp1 and the established EFG1-mediated transcriptional circuitry. Specifically, the downregulation of hypha-specific genes such as ALS1 and ECE1 suggests that CaLrp1 may function as a key post-transcriptional modulator that ensures the robust execution of these canonical hyphal programs. In this study, the CaLrp1-/- mutant exhibited delayed hyphal initiation and impaired elongation under multiple inducing conditions. This morphological defect aligns with our transcriptomic data, which demonstrated significant downregulation of critical hypha-associated genes, including Als1 and Ece1. Given its homology to RNA exosome-associated factors and the observed broad dysregulation of ribosome biogenesis and RNA metabolic pathways, we propose that CaLrp1 represents a novel class of regulatory factor. It likely supports hyphal development indirectly, by maintaining post-transcriptional RNA homeostasis and ensuring efficient protein synthesis, thereby enabling the execution of morphogenetic programs [16].

Animal infection models represent the gold standard for the direct assessment of fungal virulence [17, 18]. In C. albicans, pathogenicity within the host is known to depend on the coordinated function of multiple critical determinants. These include hyphal morphogenesis, adhesins (e.g., the Als family), and effector peptides such as candidalysin (encoded by Ece1) [5, 19, 20], The disruption of any key virulence factor typically attenuates the pathogen’s capacity for tissue proliferation and damage. A central aspect of this process is epithelial invasion, which requires the integrated activity of adhesins, invasions, and secreted hydrolytic enzymes [21]. Among these, secreted aspartyl proteases (SAPs) are particularly pivotal for breaching host barriers and facilitating tissue invasion [22, 23]. In this study, CaLrp1-/- mutant exhibited significantly reduced extracellular phospholipase and aspartyl protease activities. SAP family members facilitate tissue penetration and immune modulation via proteolytic degradation of host extracellular matrix and immune proteins, while phospholipases primarily exert cytotoxic effects by hydrolyzing host membrane phospholipids [22, 24–26]. Notably, while ECE1 (the precursor of candidalysin) was significantly downregulated in our transcriptomic data, this was not reflected in our in vitro hemolytic assays. This suggests that the impact of ECE1 regulation may be more prominent during actual host infection, contributing to the attenuated virulence observed in our murine models. Macrophage recognition and phagocytic efficiency are governed by the surface exposure of fungal cell wall components [27]. Consequently, the enhanced phagocytosis susceptibility of the CaLrp1 mutant may be attributed to alterations in cell wall architecture or the surface availability of specific molecular patterns, thereby promoting pattern recognition receptor (PRR)-mediated immune detection.

Transcriptomic profiling indicated that deletion of CaLrp1 was associated with differential expression of genes implicated in metabolic reprogramming (including the glyoxylate cycle and fatty acid β-oxidation), antioxidant defense (e.g., superoxide dismutases and catalase), and cell wall integrity. These pathways are established as critical for C. albicans survival under the nutrient-limited, oxidative, and acidic conditions characteristic of the phagosomal environment [28]. The pathogenicity of C. albicans is orchestrated by a multifactorial network, wherein no single determinant is solely responsible for virulence [29]. RNA exosome constitutes an essential complex for maintaining cellular RNA homeostasis and facilitating ribosome biogenesis. It functions in the selective degradation and processing of various RNA species, thereby exerting broad post-transcriptional control [30, 31]. Transcriptomic data in this study reveal altered expression of multiple components of RNA exosome machinery, suggesting that CaLrp1 modulates the morphogenesis and pathogenicity of C. albicans, likely mediated by its influence on RNA processing and the fidelity of protein biosynthesis. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the exosome and its associated cofactors are indispensable for pre-rRNA processing, non-coding RNA turnover, and nuclear RNA surveillance. Dysfunction of exosome activity leads to the accumulation of aberrant transcripts and pre-rRNA intermediates, causing widespread transcriptomic dysregulation and highlighting the integral role of the exosome in ribosomal maturation [32].

Conclusions

This study characterizes the unannotated Sas10/Utp3/C1D family protein CaLrp1 in C. albicans. Deletion of CaLrp1was found to have no effect on basal fungal growth under nutrient-replete conditions but resulted in significant impairments in hyphal induction, substrate invasion, and morphogenetic switching. Phenotypic analysis revealed that the CaLrp1-deficient strain displayed diminished capacity for epithelial barrier traversal, attenuated host cell damage, and increased susceptibility to phagocytosis in vitro. Correspondingly, in murine models of both intestinal colonization and systemic infection, the mutant strain exhibited restricted proliferation, reduced tissue damage, and a marked attenuation in virulence—defects that were partially rescued in the genetically complemented strain. Transcriptomic profiling indicated that loss of CaLrp1 triggers extensive transcriptional reprogramming, characterized by the coordinated downregulation of hypha-associated and virulence-related genes, thereby aligning with the observed impairments in morphogenesis, invasion, and pathogenicity. Collectively, these findings establish CaLrp1 as a significant regulator of C. albicans morphogenesis and virulence, offering novel mechanistic insight into the regulation of fungal pathogenicity.
