Genome-wide identification and expression analysis of the PYL gene family in response to salt, drought and cold stresses in Solanum melongena L.
Authors: Fangyi Gong, Yanhong Lan, Tian Zhang, Chun Li, Yifan Li, Feng Xia, Xiaojun Liu, Duchen Liu, Genyun Liang, Chao Fang, Peng Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEggplant (Solanum melongena L.) is a globally important vegetable crop, yet its production faces challenges due to various abiotic stresses. PYL proteins, which act as crucial abscisic acid (ABA) receptors, play a pivotal role in the plant's response to abiotic stresses. However, a genome-wide analysis of the PYL gene family in eggplant has not been conducted to date, hindering the study of their functions in plant development and their roles in abiotic stress tolerance.ResultsThis study presents the first genome-wide identification of the PYL gene family in eggplant (Solanum melongena) (Sm-PYL), revealing 24 Sm-PYL genes (Sm-PYL1-24) encoding proteins with lengths (92–722 aa), molecular weights (10,256.59–83,087.82 Da), and isoelectric points (4.45–9.77), classified into three subfamilies (I-III). Introduction

Eggplant (Solanum melongena L.), a globally significant vegetable crop and member of the Solanaceae family, originated in ancient India and has since been widely cultivated across Asia, Africa, the Mediterranean, and the Americas through extensive domestication and artificial selection [1]. Despite its agricultural importance, eggplant production is frequently constrained by abiotic stresses, including salinity, drought, and low-temperature stress, which significantly impair growth and yield [2–4]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to excavate genes associated with abiotic stress resistance in eggplant.

Abscisic acid (ABA), a pivotal phytohormone, regulates key physiological processes such as seed dormancy, germination, fruit ripening, and leaf senescence [5–9]. Under abiotic stress conditions, ABA levels rapidly increase, triggering adaptive responses that enhance plant survival [10, 11], particularly during drought, salinity, and cold stresses. The ABA signaling pathway is mediated by three core components: PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors, PP2C phosphatases, and SnRK2 kinases [12–14]. Among these, PYL proteins belong to the START (Steroidogenic acute regulatory protein-related lipid transfer) superfamily of ligand-binding proteins [15]. Structural studies reveal that PYL proteins contain four conserved loops (CL1-CL4). Notably, the CL2 and CL3 loops form a unique Gate-Latch mechanism that enables specific ABA perception and binding [15]. PYL proteins function at the first step of the ABA signal pathway and activate downstream ABA signaling cascade. Upon ABA binding, PYLs inhibit PP2C activity, allowing SnRK2 kinases to become activated through autophosphorylation or RAF-mediated phosphorylation [16, 17].

As core regulators in ABA signaling, PYL genes had been identified that play diverse roles in plant growth, development and the regulation abiotic stress resistances, especially in drought, salinity and cold stresses in many species [9, 18–20]. In Arabidopsis, AtPYL1-5 and AtPYL8 promote seed germination and stomatal closure [21, 22], while AtPYL5 enhances tolerance to drought, salt, and cold stresses and the overexpression of AtPYL4/AtPYL9 was highly sensitive to ABA and can enhance water use efficiency [23]. In rice, overexpression of OsPYL3/5/7 increased sensitivity to changes in ABA content while simultaneously improving drought resistance [24, 25], whereas OsPYL9 knockout boosted grain yield under both normal and drought conditions [26]. Similarly, tomato SlPYL4 is strongly induced by drought stress, and its silencing compromised drought tolerance [27], while SlPYL9 modulates flower abscission and fruit ripening through regulation of ABA signaling components [19]. In potato, overexpression of StPYL8, 16 and 20 significantly enhances tolerance to drought in transgenic plants, respectively [28–30]. Functional characterization of these genes has uncovered their diverse roles in plant biology.

Genome-wide analyses have revealed the PYL gene family in numerous plant species, with varying numbers of members identified: 20 in tomato (SlPYLs) [31], 17 in potato (StPYLs) [32], 14 in cucumber (CsPYLs) [33], and 13 in both rice (OsPYLs) [34] and maize (ZmPYLs) [35], 10 in barley (HvPYLs) [36] and 19 in sunflower (HaPYLs) [37]. Despite the well-characterized roles of PYL genes in other crops, this gene family remains unexplored in eggplant. In this study, we identified 24 members of the PYL gene family (Sm-PYL) in the eggplant genome. We analyzed the gene structure, conserved domains, and phylogenetic relationships of these 24 Sm-PYL genes. Meanwhile, we also investigated the expression patterns of Sm-PYL genes in different tissues (roots, shoots and leaves) and under cold, drought and salt stress conditions. These findings provide crucial insights into the potential roles of Sm-PYL genes in eggplant stress responses and establish a foundation for future functional studies aimed at improving eggplant resilience through molecular breeding.

Results

Identification and chromosome localization analysis of PYL in eggplant

To identify PYL proteins in eggplant, the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile of the PYL domain (PF10604) was used to identify PYL genes from the eggplant genome using HMMER 3.0. In addition, 13 PYL protein sequences in Arabidopsis were obtained from previous research and were used to blast against the eggplant genome using BLASTP to predict the presence of PYL candidate genes. A total of 24 PYL genes (Sm-PYL) were identified in the eggplant genome were designated as Sm-PYL1-24. Sequence analysis showed that these Sm-PYL proteins ranged in length from 92 (Sm-PYL6) to 722 (Sm-PYL22) amino acids, with molecular weights ranging from 10,256.59 Da (Sm-PYL6) to 83,087.82 Da (Sm-PYL22) and the isoelectric points ranged from 4.45 (Sm-PYL6) to 9.77 (Sm-PYL3) (Table 1). 24 Sm-PYL genes were mapped onto 10 chromosomes (Chr1-10), with Chr8 having the majority of Sm-PYL genes (5), whereas the Chr3, Chr7 and Chr9 had only one gene, respectively (Table 1).

Pylogenetic analysis of PYL in eggplant

To elucidate the evolutionary relationship of PYL genes between Solanum melongena and other species, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using 51 PYL proteins, including 14 A. thaliana PYL (AtPYR1, AtPYL1-13), 13 O. sativa PYL (OsPYL1-13), and 24 Sm-PYL proteins. It showed that eggplant Sm-PYL gene family members could be divided into three subfamilies (I-III). Specifically, 26 PYL genes, including 7 OsPYLs, 4 AtPYLs, and 15 SmPYLs, were clustered to form clade I; 14 PYL genes, consisting of 3 OsPYLs, 6 AtPYLs, and 5 SmPYLs, were grouped into clade II; and 6 PYL genes, with 3 OsPYLs, 4 AtPYLs, and 4 SmPYLs, were clustered into clade III (Fig. 1).

Gene structure and conserved motifs analysis of Sm-PYL genes

The results of gene structure analysis showed that the number of exons of Sm-PYL genes varied between 1 (Sm-PYL14, Sm-PYL10, Sm-PYL17, Sm-PYL18, Sm-PYL19, Sm-PYL21, Sm-PYL23, Sm-PYL24) and 11 (Sm-PYL22). All Sm-PYL protein sequences were analyzed using the MEME tool, resulting in the identification of a total of 15 conserved motifs. These motifs varied in length, ranging from 7 amino acids (Motif 13) to 50 amino acids (Motifs 1, 3, and 5). Members of subfamily III all contain six motifs (motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 11), with motif 6 being exclusively present in subfamily III members. Members of subfamily II all contain motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 8, and Sm-PYL12 and Sm-PYL16 also contains motif 11. Among these, motif 8 is uniquely found in subfamily II members, except for Sm-PYL8 from subfamily I, which also contains motif 8 (Fig. 2).

Gene structure and conserved motifs analysis of Sm-PYL genes

To investigate segmental duplication events in eggplant, we performed collinearity analysis using TBtools II-MCScanX, identifying six pairs of syntenic Sm-PYL genes. Among these, five pairs resulted from interchromosomal segmental duplications (Sm-PYL1/Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL1/Sm-PYL20, Sm-PYL7/Sm-PYL20, Sm-PYL10/Sm-PYL13, and Sm-PYL11/Sm-PYL20), while one pair (Sm-PYL23/Sm-PYL24) arose from intrachromosomal duplication. Notably, all gene pairs exhibited Ka/Ks ratios < 0.22, indicating strong purifying selection (Fig. 3A, Table S1). Further, our analysis of the PYL gene family within the eggplant genome, revealed a marked heterogeneity in their expansion patterns. we demonstrated that this family has expanded through multiple mechanisms: 10 Sm-PYL genes (41.7%) belonged to dispersed duplications, 8 Sm-PYL genes (33.3%) originate from whole-genome duplications (WGD) or segmental duplications, 2 Sm-PYL genes were singleton, 1 Sm-PYL genes belonged to tandem duplication clusters, and an additional 3 Sm-PYL genes arise through proximal duplications.

To investigate the evolutionary origins and assess the functional conservation of the Sm-PYL gene family, we performed a collinearity analysis comparing the eggplant genome with those of tomato, potato, and pepper. Our findings revealed that 20, 20, and 18 Sm-PYL genes exhibited homology with genes in the tomato, potato, and pepper genomes, respectively. However, four genes (Sm-PYL2, Sm-PYL5, Sm-PYL18, and Sm-PYL22) lacked homologous counterparts in the tomato genome; similarly, four genes (Sm-PYL2, Sm-PYL4, Sm-PYL5, and Sm-PYL18) and six genes (Sm-PYL5, Sm-PYL13, Sm-PYL17, Sm-PYL18, Sm-PYL19, and Sm-PYL21) showed no homologous matches in the potato and pepper genomes, respectively (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, we identified a total of 31, 34, and 21 syntenic PYL gene pairs between eggplant and tomato, eggplant and potato, and eggplant and pepper, respectively, all with Ka/Ks ratios below 0.50 (Table S1). These results provide further evidence of strong functional conservation and purifying selection acting on the eggplant PYL genes.

Promoter element analysis

We analyzed the promoter sequences (2 kb upstream of 5'UTR) of 24 Sm-PYL genes using the PlantCARE database and identified 20 cis-regulatory elements in which the number of light response cis-element (Box 4, ACE, MRE, G-Box, ATCT-motif and ACA-motif) was highest among the promoter sequences of 24 Sm-PYL genes. We categorized these elements into three functional groups related to: (1) abiotic and biotic stress response, (2) hormone responsiveness, and (3) growth and development. The hormone responsiveness cis-elements (5) mainly included gibberellin (GARE-motif, TATC-box and P-box), auxin (AuxRR-core and TGA-element), salicylic acid (TCA-element and SARE), MeJA (CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif) and abscisic acid (ABRE). The abiotic and biotic stress responsive group had 7 elements involving drought (MBS), light, low-temperature (LTR and DRE), anaerobic (ARE) and salt stresses (DRE). The growth and development group had 9 elements containing seed-specific regulation (RY-element), endosperm expression (GCN4_motif and AACA_motif) and so on (Fig. 4, Table S2).

MicroRNA targeting and protein interaction network analysis of Sm-PYL genes

Extensive research has demonstrated that miRNAs play a crucial role in plant stress responses by modulating the expression of stress-related genes. To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of miRNAs in controlling Sm-PYL genes, we predicted 33 potential miRNAs targeting 14 Sm-PYL genes and constructed an interaction network using Cytoscape software (Expectation ≤ 3). Network analysis revealed that Sm-PYL21 and Sm-PYL14 were the most frequently targeted genes. Specifically, Sm-PYL21 was regulated by seven members of the miR444 family and two members of the miR5143 family, while Sm-PYL14 was targeted by six members of the miR395 family. Furthermore, multiple miRNAs were found to interact with Sm-PYL2, Sm-PYL4, Sm-PYL5, Sm-PYL6, Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL9, Sm-PYL11, Sm-PYL12, Sm-PYL16, Sm-PYL18, Sm-PYL21, Sm-PYL22, and Sm-PYL24, indicating a complex regulatory network (Fig. 5).

To further investigate the functions of Sm-PYL proteins, we constructed a protein interaction network using Arabidopsis as a reference and STRING software version 11.5. A total of 9 potential interacting proteins of Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL12, Sm-PYL19, Sm-PYL20 and Sm-PYL24 were identified, including ABI1, ABI2, AHG1, AIP1, HAB1, HAB2, PP2CA, SAG113.

Expression patterns of Sm-PYL genes in different tissues

To investigate the spatiotemporal patterns of the Sm-PYL genes in different tissues, we retrieved expression profiling data of 24 Sm-PYL genes in different tissues sampled at different time points from publicly available RNA-seq data of eggplant. We found Sm-PYL genes showed four major expression patterns as follows: 3 Sm-PYL genes, namely Sm-PYL4, Sm-PYL6 and Sm-PYL18 were either not expressed (Fragments Per Kilobase per million Mapped reads, FPKM < 1) or lowly expressed (1 ≤ FPKM < 5) in different tissues sampled at different time points. In addition, 6 Sm-PYL genes only were highly expressed at specific developmental stages of specific tissues; Sm-PYL8, Sm-PYL19 and Sm-PYL23 only highly expressed at root and Sm-PYL12 and Sm-PYL22 only highly expressed at leaf and Sm-PYL5 only highly expressed at fruit. 8 Sm-PYL genes (Sm-PYL1, Sm-PYL2, Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL9, Sm-PYL11, Sm-PYL14, Sm-PYL17, Sm-PYL20) were highly expressed (5 ≤ FPKM) in all tissues sampled. The remaining 7 genes highly expressed in different tissues. For example, Sm-PYL3 only highly expressed in flower and fruit; Sm-PYL10 only highly expressed in root and fruit (Fig. 6A).

Expression response of Sm-PYL genes to cold stress

To investigate the expression responses of the Sm-PYL genes to cold stress, we retrieved expression profiling data from our laboratory. The data included the expression levels of 24 Sm-PYL genes in leaves after 0, 1, 2, 4, and 7 days of cold stress. Six genes (Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL9, Sm-PYL10, Sm-PYL13, Sm-PYL23, Sm-PYL24) showed an initial increase followed by decrease in expression. Notably, Sm-PYL16 demonstrated significant upregulation, while 11 genes were downregulated compared to the control. Two genes (Sm-PYL8, Sm-PYL18) and four genes (Sm-PYL4, Sm-PYL16, Sm-PYL19, Sm-PYL21) exhibited no or low expression at different time points (Fig. 6B).

Expression response of Sm-PYL genes to salt and drought stresses

To elucidate the role of Sm-PYL genes in salt and drought stress responses, we analyzed the expression patterns of 13 Sm-PYL genes (grouped into subfamilies I-III) across multiple tissues (roots, shoots, and leaves) under normal, salt-tolerant, and drought-tolerant conditions. The analysis revealed that 13 Sm-PYL genes exhibited varying expression levels in different organs.

Under salt stress, Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL12 and Sm-PYL23 exhibited significant up-regulation in roots, shoots, and leaves compared to normal conditions. In contrast, six genes (Sm-PYL10, Sm-PYL13, Sm-PYL16, Sm-PYL19 and Sm-PYL22) were significantly down-regulated across all tissues. Some genes showed tissue-specific responses. For example, Sm-PYL1, Sm-PYL15 and Sm-PYL24 were significantly up-regulated in roots but down-regulated in shoots and leaves. Sm-PYL8 showed down-regulation in roots but up-regulation in shoots and leaves (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The PYL protein family serves as crucial ABA receptors in the core PYLs-PP2Cs-SnRK2 signaling pathway, playing pivotal roles in regulating plant growth, development, and abiotic stress responses [5–12]. Multiple studies have identified and functionally characterized PYL genes in species such as tomato, potato, cucumber, maize, and rice [31–34]. Eggplant (Solanum melongena L.), a significant vegetable crop globally, faces challenges in its production process due to various abiotic stresses, including salt, low-temperature, and drought stresses [1–4]. However, a genome-wide analysis of the PYL gene family in eggplant has not yet been reported, which hampers the study of their developmental functions and roles in abiotic stress tolerance. Our comprehensive analysis identified 24 Sm-PYL genes in eggplant, which were phylogenetically classified into three distinct subfamilies (I-III), consistent with the evolutionary patterns observed in other plant species [31, 34]. Notably, each subfamily exhibited unique motif compositions: Subfamily III uniquely contained motif 6 among its six conserved motifs, while Subfamily II was characterized by motif 8 (except for Sm-PYL8 from subfamily I), with Sm-PYL12/16 additionally possessing motif 11. These results suggested Sm-PYL genes of the same subfamily have similar unique/specialized biological functions.

Synteny analysis revealed that the expansion of the Sm-PYL gene family in eggplant has been driven by diverse duplication mechanisms, including segmental, dispersed, whole-genome duplication (WGD), tandem, and proximal duplications. However, six gene pairs within eggplant exhibited Ka/Ks ratios < 0.22 and all 86 orthologous pairs between eggplant and tomato, eggplant and potato, and eggplant and pepper exhibited Ka/Ks ratios < 0.50. These results suggest that the Sm-PYL family in eggplant has evolved through multiple duplication events, contributing to functional diversification while also undergoing strong purifying selection during evolution to maintain essential functions, likely related to ABA signaling and stress responses. Cis-regulatory elements present in the promoter region significantly contribute to the regulation of plant control systems and genes that respond to different types of stimuli [38, 39]. In this study, we identified 20 distinct cis-elements associated with stress adaptation, hormone signaling, and fruit development in the promoters of 24 Sm-PYL genes. This finding implies that Sm-PYL genes are subject to multifaceted transcriptional control, which may underlie their functional versatility in eggplant.

Abiotic stresses represent significant environmental constraints that limit plant productivity. Extensive studies have demonstrated that overexpression of PYL genes can substantially enhance plant tolerance to various abiotic stresses [19, 21–27]. Overexpression of the ABA receptor PYL9 in both Arabidopsis and rice significantly improved drought resistance by inducing ethylene-independent leaf senescence through the SnRK2-ABFs/RAV1 pathway, thereby creating an osmotic potential gradient that redirects water to developing tissues [9]. Overexpression of GhPYL10/12/26 in Arabidopsis enhanced drought tolerance by regulating root growth [30]. PePYL4 enhances drought tolerance by modulating water-use efficiency and ROS scavenging in Populus [18]. The transient and stable expression of StPYL8-like in tobacco enhanced drought resistance, resulting in increased plant height, leaf number, and fresh weight, along with an improved root system architecture [30]. Similarly, overexpression of StPYL16 and StPYL20 in transgenic plants significantly elevated drought tolerance in independent studies [28, 29]. In eggplant, we found six Sm-PYL genes showed tissue specific expression: root-preferential (Sm-PYL8/19/23), leaf-preferential (Sm-PYL12/22), and fruit-specific (Sm-PYL5) expression patterns. Under drought and salt stress conditions, Sm-PYL1, 12, 14, 15 and 23 were significantly up-regulated in roots or leaves compared to normal conditions, suggesting their potential importance in drought and salt stress response regulation through modulation of root and leaf development. In Triticum monococcum, the genes TbPYL2, TbPYL5, and TbPYL12 exhibited responsive expression under cold stress conditions [40]. Overexpression of VaPYL9 had been shown to enhance cold tolerance in tomato by modulating key genes involved in hormone signaling and antioxidant enzyme pathways [41]. In this study, we identified seven cold-responsive Sm-PYL genes in eggplant (Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL9, Sm-PYL10, Sm-PYL13, Sm-PYL16, Sm-PYL23 and Sm-PYL24). The result indicated that these seven genes might participate in regulating eggplant cold stress resistance.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play crucial roles in abiotic stress response mechanisms, particularly in relation to oxidative stress and DNA effects in various plant species [42]. For instance, miR395 has been shown to respond to cold stress in both cucumber and grapevine [43, 44]. In rice, miR444 has been validated to regulate root development by targeting OsMADS27 that could promote salt tolerance [45, 46]. In arabidopsis, ABA-induced miR5628 cleaves PYL6 mRNA for degradation, fine-tuning ABA receptor levels [47]. We predicted that miR444 and miR395 target Sm-PYL14 and Sm-PYL21, respectively. In addition, nine predicted interactors were found to interact with Sm-PYL7, Sm-PYL9, Sm-PYL12, Sm-PYL20, and Sm-PYL24. Proteins such as ABI1, ABI2, AHG1, AIP1, HAB1, HAB2, and PP2CA were well-known key components of the ABA signaling pathway [12–14]. Additionally, Overexpression of TaNAC29 in wheat represses SAG113, reduces water loss, and enhances drought tolerance [48]. Based on these results, we propose that a miRNA395/444-Sm-PYL-Protein (ABA signaling pathway) regulatory network regulates abiotic stress tolerance in eggplant. With the advent of gene editing technology [49], precise modifications can be made to these genes, potentially leading to enhanced stress tolerance in eggplant and offering a promising avenue for agricultural improvement.

Conclusions

This study provides the first comprehensive genome-wide analysis of the PYL gene family in eggplant (Solanum melongena), identifying 24 Sm-PYL genes. The expansion of Sm-PYL genes was driven by multiple duplication events, with strong purifying selection maintaining their essential roles in ABA signaling and stress responses. Expression profiling demonstrated that Sm-PYL1/12/14/15/23 in salt/drought responses and Sm-PYL7/9/10/13/16/23/24 in cold adaptation. Furthermore, we propose that a miRNA395/444-Sm-PYL-Protein (ABA signaling pathway) regulatory network regulates abiotic stress tolerance in eggplant. Our results provide valuable insights into the functional diversification of Sm-PYL genes and aimed at improving abiotic stress tolerance in eggplant through genetic engineering or molecular breeding strategies targeting key Sm-PYL regulators.

Materials and methods

Identification of PYL Genes in Solanum melongena

The eggplant (Solanum melongena L.) genome sequences, protein sequences, coding sequences and annotation profiles were obtained from the Solanaceae Genomics Network (https://solgenomics.net/, last accessed May 19, 2025) and EnsemblPlants (https://plants.ensembl.org/info/data/ftp/index.html, last accessed May 19, 2025). The Hidden Merkov Model (HMM) profile of the Polyketide_cyc2 (PYL) domain (PF10604) [50] was downloaded from the Pfam protein family database (http://pfam.xfam.org/, last accessed May 19, 2025) and was used to identify PYL genes from eggplant genomes using HMMER3.0 search toll with E-value ≤ 10–5. Thirteen Arabidopsis PYL protein sequences from prior research [20] were BLASTP-blasted against the eggplant genome, with E-values of 10^−5 and > 200-bit scores as cut-offs to identify putative PYL genes, and the results were tabulated. The protein sequences were further checked using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI)-Conserved Domain Database (CDD) search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi, last accessed May 19, 2025) to identify the conserved protein domain and reject some candidate genes that were outside the PYL domain [51].

Gene structure and conserved domain analysis

Conserved protein motifs were identified by using the MEME Suite web server v5.5.7 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/index.html, last accessed May 19, 2025), with the maximum number of motifs specified as 15 and the optimum width of motif sets specified as 5 to 200 amino acids. Additionally, the ExPASy online tool (https://www.expasy.org/, last accessed May 19, 2025) was employed to predict the amino acid length, molecular weights, and theoretical isoelectric points (pI) of Sm-PYL proteins. The exon–intron structure diagram of Sm-PYL gene family members was analyzed using TBtools-II software v2.225 [52].

Chromosome distribution and promoter element analysis

To determine the chromosomal mapping of Sm-PYL genes, TBtools-II (v2.225) software was employed to analyze and locate the positions of all Sm-PYL genes on chromosomes. The putative promoter region, defined as the 2000 bp sequence upstream of the starting codon, was extracted from the eggplant genome. Subsequently, these sequences were analyzed using PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/, last accessed May 19, 2025) for cis-element prediction [53].

Phylogenetic and syntenic analysis

PYL protein sequences from rice (OsPYL), and Arabidopsis (AtPYL) were downloaded from Ensembl Plant. The amino acid sequences of all PYL genes were aligned using MEGAX-clustalW software and the resulting alignments were used to construct a phylogenetic tree using the Neighbor Joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replications and MEGAX software and iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/, last accessed May 19, 2025). The collinearity analysis of PYL genes between eggplant and tomato was performed by TBtools II-One Step MCScanX and Multiple Synteny Plot and the synonymous (Ks) and nonsynonymous (Ka) mutation rates were computed using TBtools II-simple Ka/Ks Calculator (NG) (v2.225) [52].

MicroRNA targeting and protein interaction network analysis

The prediction of miRNAs targeting PYL genes in eggplant was carried out by employing psRNATarget (https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/#, last accessed May 19, 2025), with all mature miRNAs as the reference. Subsequently, the protein interaction network of PYL protein was examined utilizing the online tool STRING version 12.0 (https://cn.string-db.org/, last accessed May 19, 2025), and the resulting network was visualized using Cytoscape.

Plant materials and tolerance assay

The eggplant seeds were germinated on sterile culture dishes lined with filter paper for 14 days. Subsequently, all seedlings were cultivated in a greenhouse at a controlled temperature of 26 ± 1 ℃ under a 16/8-h light/dark cycle at the agricultural science institute of sichuan province (Chengdu, China) prior to the experiment. To induce drought or salt stress, seedlings with uniform growth were randomly selected and transferred to Hoagland nutrient solution supplemented with 30% PEG6000 or 200 mM NaCl for the treatment groups, respectively, while the control group was maintained in standard Hoagland nutrient solution. For cold treatment, eggplant seedlings with 4–5 leaves were moved to a growth chamber set to a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle and a temperature of 5 ℃ during the day and 10 ℃ at night. Shoots, leaves, and roots from individual plants were collected at 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h after treatment, with three biological replicates for each time point [32]. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃ for subsequent analysis.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from root, shoot and leaf samples using TRIzolTM reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Tokyo, Japan). First-strand cDNA was synthesized using the TaKaRa PrimeScriptTM RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Gene-specific primers were designed using Primer3 Plus (http://www.primer3plus.com/, last accessed May 19, 2025) and Primer-BLAST(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/index.cgi?LINK_LOC=BlastHome, last accessed May 19, 2025) (Table S3). qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in a 10 uL reaction mixture comprising 2 ng of cDNA, 5 uL of 2 × SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa), and 0.5 uL of each primer. Gene expression levels were quantified relative to SmActin controls using the 2−ΔΔCT method [54]. Each data point was derived from three biological replicates.
