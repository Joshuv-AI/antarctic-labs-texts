Electronic nose for detecting abnormal glucose metabolism in heart transplant recipients
Authors: Nynke Wijbenga, Annelot J Muntinga, Marleen M Goedendorp-Sluimer, Britt C J van Dijk, Stefan Roest, Jasper J Brugts, Kadir Caliskan, Alina A Constantinescu, Adrienne A M Zandbergen, Daniel Bos, Merel E Hellemons, Olivier C Manintveld
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPost-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) affects up to 34% of heart transplant recipients (HTR) within five years, increasing the risk of adverse outcomes. Although the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) is the most sensitive method for detecting prediabetes and diabetes, it is burdensome. This study evaluates the diagnostic accuracy of exhaled breath analysis using an electronic nose (eNose) as a non-invasive alternative for detecting prediabetes or PTDM.MethodsHTR > one year post-transplant undergoing OGTT were included, along with fasting HTR with known PTDM as a control group. Exhaled breath was analyzed before glucose loading using the SpiroNose. Diagnostic performance of eNose parameters alone and combined with clinical variables was assessed using multivariate logistic regression.ResultsSeventy-six HTR were included (29% female, median age 56 years, median 7.8 years post-transplant). Of these, 18 had normal glucose tolerance, 33 had prediabetes, and 25 had PTDM (including 8 newly diagnosed). Overall, 76% had prediabetes or PTDM. eNose alone differentiated normal from abnormal glucose tolerance (prediabetes or PTDM) with an AUROC of 0.70 (95% CI 0.57–0.83), 68% accuracy, 69% sensitivity, 67% specificity, 40% negative predictive value (NPV), and 87% positive predictive value (PPV). Combining eNose with clinical parameters improved diagnostic performance (AUROC 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96), achieving 78% accuracy, 71% sensitivity, 100% specificity, 51% NPV, and 100% PPV.ConclusionsPrediabetes or PTDM is common in HTR. eNose technology, especially when combined with clinical data, shows promise as a non-invasive screening tool with high specificity and PPV. This approach may reduce OGTT burden, pending further confirmation.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Heart transplantation (HTx) remains the treatment of choice for selected patients with end-stage heart failure. Median survival post-HTx is approximately 15 years [1, 2]. Both prediabetes and diabetes mellitus are frequently observed post-HTx, with an incidence of 34% within 5-year post-transplant for diabetes mellitus [1, 3]. Prediabetes is an intermediate metabolic state between normal glucose tolerance and diabetes mellitus [4]. Patients with prediabetes have an increased risk (up to 70%) of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus; leading to an increased risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [4–6]. Post-transplant diabetes mellitus (PTDM) is associated with increased risk for progressive renal dysfunction, re-transplantation, and death [5, 7]. Early identification of prediabetes and PTDM enables timely interventions, such as lifestyle adjustments, glycemic control, and review of immunosuppression. These intervention can reduce the risk of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [7, 8].

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) has added value to fasting glucose levels and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) for identifying patients with (pre)diabetes [9–11]. Previously we have shown that in HTx recipients (HTR) OGTT is the preferred test to screen for prediabetes and PTDM, facilitating timely interventions [12]. However, OGTT is a burdensome and time-consuming process because it requires fasting, consuming a high-sugar glucose drink, multiple blood draws, and remaining at a medical facility for at least two hours. Therefore, an alternative, less burdensome screenings tool is preferred.

An emerging non-invasive (diagnostic) technique is the application of exhaled breath as decision support tool, using an electronic nose (eNose). Exhaled breath contains an extensive mixture of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that are representative of either normal physiology or pathophysiological inflammatory and oxidative activity. An eNose can be used to grasp the pattern of the mixture of VOCs present in exhaled air by reacting to cross-reactive gas sensors, producing a distinctive sensor response pattern known as a breathprint. Utilizing pattern recognition algorithms, these breathprints can be classified into disease categories. While most studies on diabetes monitoring via exhaled breath have focused on measuring exhaled acetone, the value of acetone as a single biomarker remains uncertain. A more promising approach may be to identify clusters of VOC biomarkers allowing a broader, pattern-based diagnostic perspective [13]. As such, eNose technology holds potential as an additional diagnostic modality in clinical practice.

The aim of this study was to assess whether eNose technology could discriminate normal from abnormal glucose tolerance (prediabetes or PTDM) in HTR, and potentially serve to replace OGTT as a screening tool. Additionally, we investigated the discrimination between normal glucose tolerance and prediabetes, normal glucose tolerance and PTDM, and prediabetes and PTDM. Finally, we investigated whether adding clinical parameters improved diagnostic performance.

Methods

Setting

We performed a prospective patient cohort study at the Erasmus Medical Center, Rotterdam the Netherlands. This study was approved by the medical ethics committee (MEC-2019-0497). All patients provided written informed consent prior to the eNose measurements.

At our outpatient clinic, HTR with PTDM are monitored annually through HbA1c and glucose level assessments. In addition, we screened all stable adult HTR without diabetes, who are more than one year post-transplant with an OGTT. OGTT screening is conducted every three years in patients with normal glucose tolerance and annually for those with prediabetes. HTR with known PTDM, active infection, active rejection treatment, who are treated for malignancy, and HTR unwilling or unable to undergo an OGTT are excluded from OGTT screening.

The OGTTs are performed according to American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [9]. Fasting glucose level and HbA1c are measured at the outpatient clinic. Subsequently, 75 g of glucose in a 200 mL solution is administered. After 2 h, a second glucose level measurement is performed. Glucose level measurements are performed using a finger prick (Accu-Chek Inform II, Roche).

HTR who underwent OGTT screening were classified according to ADA criteria (Fig. 1) into normal glucose tolerance, prediabetes, or PTDM. Additionally, HTR with prediabetes or PTDM were combined in one group.

Definition of (pre)diabetes according to the ADA. IFG: impaired fasting glucose; 2-h PG 75-g OGTT: 2h plasma glucose value during a 75-g OGTT

Study procedures—patient selection

All HTR undergoing scheduled OGTT between July 2020 and October 2024, were asked to participate in eNose measurements, prior to glucose intake. We included all patients without diabetes that underwent OGTT screening while fasting. Furthermore, we included several known patients with PTDM with available fasting glucose levels and fasting eNose measurements as a control group to balance groups.

eNose—exhaled breath measurements

Exhaled breath samples were obtained using the cloud-connected SpiroNose (Breathomix®, Leiden, the Netherlands). This eNose comprises seven different types of cross-reactive metal-oxide semiconductor sensors, each present in duplicate inside and outside of the SpiroNose. This configuration enables the SpiroNose to account for ambient air and provide real-time measurements of exhaled breath.

A detailed description of the eNose set up and methods has been previously described by de Vries et al. [14]. In essence, a SpiroNose measurement is performed in duplicate and consists of five tidal breaths, followed by an inspiratory capacity maneuver to total lung capacity, a five second breath hold, and lastly a slow expiration to residual volume. Sensor responses were sent directly to the online data analysis platform, BreathBase, a secured online database. This database has the highest European Certificate for data safety (NEN7510 and ISO27001).

Statistical analysis

For the patient characteristics, normality of distribution was tested using the Shapiro-Wilks test. Continuous variables were expressed with a mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed and compared using one-way ANOVA. Not normally distributed data were presented with a median with the 25th–75th percentile (Interquartile range, IQR) and compared using a Kruskal Wallis test. Categorical variables were demonstrated as numbers with percentages (%) and compared using Fisher’s exact test.

All eNose sensor responses were processed and corrected for ambient air, as previously described [14]. For each sensor, peak values were normalized to the most stable sensor and ratios between the sensor peaks and breath hold point were calculated. Both the normalized sensor peaks and the ratios between the sensor peaks and breath hold points were used for data analysis.

To assess the potential of eNose technology as a screening tool for prediabetes and PTDM in HTR, first dimensionality reduction of the eNose sensor data was performed by applying partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), a supervised machine learning approach to investigate the discriminative ability of the eNose sensors to distinguish between normal and abnormal (prediabetes or PTDM) glucose tolerance. Second, we investigated the discriminative ability of the eNose sensors to distinguish between normal glucose tolerance and prediabetes, between normal glucose tolerance and PTDM, and between prediabetes and PTDM.

In addition, to assess the added diagnostic value of the eNose, sensor data were integrated with predefined clinical parameters known to be predictive of PTDM (time after HTx (in years), fasting glucose level (mmol/L), HbA1c (mmol/mol), steroid use, weight (kg)) in multivariable logistic regression models. Finally, we compared the diagnostic performance of the eNose-based models to the OGTT.

Model performances were evaluated by computing the area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUROC) and the corresponding model sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, negative predictive value (NPV), positive predictive value (PPV). Additionally, the 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated.

The data were analyzed using RStudio (version 1.4) with R software (version 4.1.2) and supported with the following packages: mixOmics, fpc, tableone, pROC, ggplot2.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 76 HTR were included, of whom 17 were known with PTDM. Median age was 56 [19–77] years, median time after HTx was 7.8 [1.5–26.5] years, and 29% were female. Of these HTR, 18 were classified as normal and 58 as abnormal glucose tolerance. Of the HTR classified as abnormal glucose tolerance, 33 were diagnosed with prediabetes and 25 with PTDM. Primary outcome

Normal glucose tolerance versus either prediabetes or PTDM

A PLS-DA model was created to distinguish between patients with normal and abnormal glucose tolerance. The results of this model are shown in Fig. 2. Without addition of clinical parameters, the eNose could discriminate between both groups with an AUROC of 0.70 (95% CI 0.57–0.83), an accuracy of 68%, sensitivity of 69%, specificity of 67%, NPV of 40%, and PPV of 87%. After the addition of the predictive clinical parameters (time after HTx, fasting glucose level, HbA1c, steroid use, weight), the model could accurately discriminate between normal and abnormal glucose tolerance with an AUROC of 0.94 (95% CI 0.89–1.00), an accuracy of 89%, sensitivity of 86%, specificity of 100%, NPV of 66%, and PPV of 100%.

The eNose-based model correctly detected abnormal glucose tolerance in 67% of cases. This implies that eNose could potentially replace the OGTT in two-thirds of cases.

Subanalyses

Normal glucose tolerance versus prediabetes

When comparing HTR with normal glucose tolerance and with prediabetes, eNose technology was able to adequately discriminate between both with an AUROC of 0.74 (95% CI 0.61–0.88), an accuracy of 69%, sensitivity of 55%, specificity of 94%, NPV of 53%, and an PPV of 95% (Table 2, Fig. 3). After addition of clinically relevant parameters, this improved to an AUROC of 0.93 (95% CI 0.87–1.00), an accuracy of 87%, sensitivity of 81%, specificity of 100%, NPV of 70%, and an PPV of 100% (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this study, we found that exhaled breath analysis using an eNose has the ability to reliably distinguish between a normal and abnormal glucose tolerance in fasting HTR. Diagnostic accuracy was highest when eNose data were combined with clinically relevant parameters such as weight and steroid use. The combined model predicted abnormal glucose tolerance with 77% accuracy, and achieved 79% accuracy for distinguishing normal glucose tolerance from prediabetes, 95% for normal glucose tolerance versus PTDM, and 92% for prediabetes and PTDM. Across these comparisons, PPVs were consistently high, indicating that a positive test result is highly reliable.

However the NPV was lower, suggesting that a negative eNose result does not definitively exclude abnormal glucose tolerance. Specifically, the model’s detection rate of 67% to distinguish abnormal glucose tolerance implies that in our dataset the eNose could potentially have replaced the OGTT in two-thirds of cases. For the remaining 33%, follow-up OGTT remains necessary to prevent false-negative results. Therefore, a stepwise diagnostic strategy is proposed: patients with a positive eNose result could be confidently diagnosed, while those with a negative result should undergo confirmatory testing with an OGTT to rule out false negatives. This approach ensures that the OGTT is reserved for cases where it is most needed, reducing patient burden while maintaining diagnostic safety.

Importantly, the 100% positive predictive value indicates that a positive eNose result reliably confirms abnormal glucose tolerance, making it a powerful diagnostic tool in this context. In addition, eNose technology is non-invasive and suitable for point-of-care application, supporting its feasibility for integration into routine clinical screening strategies. Further larger, multicenter validation studies are needed to confirm our findings. Following validation, implementation studies are required to determine its role and impact in clinical practice as well as its cost-effectiveness from the perspective of healthcare providers and insurers.

Approximately a third of all HTR will develop PTDM at 5-years post-HTx, mostly because of treatment with corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors [1, 5]. HTR with prediabetes are at increased risk of developing PTDM and require intensive metabolic monitoring, including glucose levels and other cardiometabolic risk factors, as PTDM is associated with reduced graft longevity and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. Early detection of prediabetes facilitates timely interventions. In patients with prediabetes, lifestyle intervention may decrease the risk of prediabetes progressing to diabetes for as long as 10 years [4, 8], nearly the median survival post-HTx.

Improving patient experience, by simplifying diagnostic procedures or reducing burden, can increase adherence to medication and care regimens and decrease the risk of prediabetes progressing to PTDM [8, 15]. Although current international guidelines do not specifically recommend the use of SGLT2 inhibitors and/or GLP-1 receptor agonists in transplant recipients, emerging evidence suggests potential cardiorenal benefits in HTR [16, 17]. Given the known cardio metabolic effects, there is an increasing interest in the use of these drugs after HTx [17–19]. A prospective randomized controlled trial in HTR to validate this is ongoing [20, 21].

Diagnosing prediabetes, not only diabetes, is already known to have important prognostic implications but has to be actively pursued. Roest et al. have shown that OGTT identifies significantly more patients with prediabetes and PTDM than (fasting) glucose and HbA1c levels alone [12].

This finding is consistent with the results of the present study, in which two patients with normal HbA1c levels were diagnosed with PTDM, and 25 patients with normal HbA1c levels were identified as having glucose levels in the prediabetic range based on OGTT results. In patients with prediabetes the advice was given to adapt their lifestyle with the advice to increase physical activity, change their diet in order to achieve weight loss.

Inclusion of clinically relevant parameters enhanced the models' ability to differentiate between all groups, with the most substantial contribution originating from fasting plasma glucose levels. This outcome was expected, as the diagnosis of PTDM can also be established solely based on fasting blood plasma glucose [9]. However, for the diagnosis of prediabetes, fasting blood plasma glucose alone is not sufficient and an OGTT is needed [9, 10]. While fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c are routinely measured, implementing routine OGTT testing is burdensome for patients, which can discourage patients from this screening procedure [22]. Additionally, OGTT screening brings considerable workload to the hospital. Given its rapid and non-invasive application, eNose technology offers an alternative as initial screening tool to reduce the need for OGTT. In cases where eNose results are inconclusive, conventional OGTT can be employed as a follow-up, enabling a stepwise diagnostic approach that balances patient convenience with clinical accuracy. This strategy may facilitate earlier risk stratification and allow for more personalized metabolic surveillance in HTR.

A limitation of this study is the relatively small dataset. As such, we could not perform internal validation with the dataset, which poses a risk for overfitting of the model. To prevent overfitting, one would like to have a validation cohort with unseen data for the model. Therefore, further validation in a larger prospective cohort is warranted. On the other hand, a major strength of this study is that the model is built with a combination of the eNose variables with clinically relevant parameters. By combining both in the model, the model is enhanced and able to identify patients at risk. This allows for performing only one fasting measurement with the eNose, instead of a two hour long OGTT. Furthermore, as eNose technology can be used as a fast point-of-care test, it is still possible to perform a subsequent OGTT if the results from the eNose measurements are unclear.

In conclusion, this is the first time that exhaled breath analysis analyzed with eNose technology combined with clinical parameters was demonstrated to be a promising diagnostic decision support tool in distinguishing between a normal and abnormal glucose tolerance. Furthermore, this point-of-care diagnostic modality holds promise as a less time-consuming screening tool. This can possibly encourage more patients to undergo this screenings procedure which can be applied to all transplant patients who are at risk for PTDM. Identifying and initiating treatment at an earlier stage may help prevent progression to overt diabetes and has the potential to improve long-term cardiovascular outcomes and graft survival.
