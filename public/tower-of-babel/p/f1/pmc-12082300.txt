A microbiome-dependent gut-bone axis determines skeletal benefits from mechanical loading
Authors: X. Edward Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A recent study published in Cell Metabolism entitled “Gut microbial alterations in arginine metabolism determine bone mechanical adaptation” demonstrated that administration of L-arginine enhanced bone mechanical adaptation by activating a nitric oxide-calcium feedback loop in osteocytes. The findings revealed that mechanical regulation of bone adaptation is associated with gut microbiota. The underlying cause of heterogeneity of bone mechanoresponsiveness was the significant difference in the composition of the gut microbiota, in which the family Lachnospiraceae contributed to the inter-individual high variability in bone mechanical adaptation. Additionally, administration of Lachnospiraceae exhibited increased expression levels of L-citrulline and L-arginine and enhanced bone mechanoresponsiveness in recipients. Collectively, this study provides mechanistic insights into inter-individual variability of the gut microbial, which is related to the heterogeneity of bone mechanical adaptation and provides a novel preventive and therapeutic strategy to anti-osteoporotic for maximizing bone mechanoresponsiveness via the microbiota-metabolite axis.

Osteoporosis is a serious global public health problem that afflicts more than 200 million people worldwide, with high morbidity and mortality due to significant increases in bone fragility and fracture risk.1 Bone is a highly adaptable system that responds to mechanical loading.2 Mechanical loading plays a crucial role in maintaining bone health and combating osteoporosis by maintaining bone integrity. During mechanical loading, osteocytes in the bone matrix regulate the biological activities of osteoclasts and osteoblasts on the bone surface through complex mechanical transduction mechanisms.

Exercise is a primary non-pharmacologic preventive measure to maintain the function of skeletal muscle, respiratory, cardiovascular, nervous, endocrine, and many other systems.3 The research also found that exercise-induced changes in cardiopulmonary parameters, glucose metabolism, and other functions are highly heterogeneous among individuals, a large number of clinical and animal experimental studies have also revealed that the benefits of bone exercise also vary significantly among inter-individuals.4, 5, 6 The high heterogeneity of mechanosensitivity is more obvious in postmenopausal and aging osteoporosis groups.7,8 However, safe and effective intervention strategies for osteoporosis prevention and treatment to improve bone mechanical adaptability are limited, and further research is necessitated to explore therapeutic strategies.

Gut microbiota plays an important role in health and disease in humans, which is described as the “microbial organ” of our body. A large number of studies have found that the occurrence of a variety of chronic diseases is related to the changes in the composition of gut microbiota. Studies have also found that compared to the 99.9% human genomic similarity among individuals, the composition and function of microbial composition similarity among populations is only 10%–20%.9 The high heterogeneity of gut microbiota mediates interindividual differences in susceptibility to diseases. Accumulating evidence has also revealed an association between gut microbiota and bone homeostasis, and the medication efficacy of certain osteoporosis-regulating drugs and nutrients is dependent on the activity of gut microbiota.10

Previous studies have shown that normal mice exhibit a strong bone response to mechanical loads, while mice with microbial dysbiosis or an imbalance of harmful microorganisms tend to have suppressed bone mass.11 To investigate the link between the variability in bone adaptation and the diversity of the gut microbial population, researchers conducted a series of experiments. A recent article, titled “Gut microbial alterations in arginine metabolism determine bone mechanical adaptation,” published in Cell Metabolism,12 presented interesting findings. The study focused on two mechanical stimulation models-treadmill exercise and uniaxial tibial loading and demonstrated that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in influencing the variability of bone adaptation to exercise-induced loading. This variability in mechanosensitivity was found to be linked to individual differences in the microbial production of L-citrulline and its conversion into L-arginine. Further investigations uncovered that L-arginine enhances the bone benefits of mechanical loading by triggering a nitric oxide (NO) -calcium positive feedback loop in osteocytes.

L-arginine, a semi-essential amino acid, can be sourced from poultry, fish, peanuts, and walnuts. It plays a crucial role as an intermediate in the urea cycle and serves as a precursor for the biosynthesis of various important compounds such as proteins, NO, creatine, polyamines, and L-glutamate. Of particular significance, L-arginine is a substrate for nitric oxide synthase (NOS) enzyme, which catalyzes the production of NO.13 NO, known as a key signaling molecule in mammals, regulates a wide range of physiological processes, including blood pressure regulation, prevention of atherosclerosis, and management of heart failure.14 Additionally, animal studies have demonstrated that L-arginine supplementation can enhance fracture healing.15 Notably, NO acts as a critical second messenger in cells, transducing external environmental stimuli into intracellular signaling events that govern various cellular processes.

In mice treated with antibiotics (Abx), the reduction of gut microbiota leads to a decrease in bone responsiveness to mechanical loading, impacting the bone's ability to adapt to mechanical loading. To explore whether the variations in bone mechanical adaptation across individuals are influenced by the diversity of gut microbiota, researchers conducted a treadmill running experiment to observe the bone adaptation process. They discovered a significant variability among individuals in changes to bone architecture, with species from the Lachnospiraceae family identified as key microbial taxa mediating these differences in bone mechanosensitivity. Subsequently, researchers administered 23 strains of Lachnospiraceae and observed an improvement in bone mechanoresponsiveness in the recipients. Further investigations unveiled that the microbial production of L-citrulline and its conversion into L-arginine play crucial roles in regulating bone mechanoadaptation. Treating Abx mice with these metabolites resulted in enhanced bone mechanoresponsiveness. Moreover, studies have shown that elderly individuals and post-menopausal women often exhibit reduced and highly variable bone mechanoresponsiveness. Treatment with L-arginine has been found to enhance bone gains mediated by treadmill exercise in this population. Specifically, L-arginine improves the calcium response of osteocytes to mechanical stimuli, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic agent for enhancing bone health and adaptation to mechanical loading.

The findings suggest that L-arginine plays a pivotal role in regulating the diverse skeletal response to bone-loading exercise. L-arginine has been shown to enhance the increase in intracellular NO levels induced by fluid shear stress, thereby activating Ca2+ signaling in osteocytes and influencing the bone's reaction to mechanical stimuli. The amplification of bone benefits from mechanical loading by L-arginine can be primarily attributed to the establishment of a complex NO–Ca2+ positive feedback loop in osteocytes (refer to Fig. 1). These discoveries offer a promising strategy for combating osteoporosis, particularly in elderly and postmenopausal individuals. By understanding and leveraging the role of L-arginine in promoting bone health and mechanoresponsiveness, novel therapeutic approaches for the management of osteoporosis in these populations can be developed. This study focused only on the effect of gut microbial on heterogeneity of bone mechanoresponsiveness. However, there are many factors affecting osteoporosis, such as genetic background, age, endocrine environment and lifestyle. Therefore, future studies should comprehensively explore the role of gut microbiota in the pathogenesis of osteoporosis and explore other therapeutic approaches. Additionally, although this study demonstrated that L-citrulline and L-arginine could improve the mechanoresponsiveness of bones in osteoporotic mice, future large-scale animal and clinical studies are needed to confirm the safety and effectiveness of this strategy.
