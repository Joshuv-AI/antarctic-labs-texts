Sialic acid-anchored haemagglutinin stalk neutralizing antibody M-SiaB enhances protection against highly pathogenic influenza H5N1/Texas/2024
Authors: Ruina Jin, Runhong Zhou, Aruzhan Aldungarova, Pui Wang, Dongyan Zhou, Kyungmin Kim, Li Liu, Na Liu, Yuting Chen, Yelim Lee, Vincent Kwok-Man Poon, Chris Chung-Sing Chan, Lucetta Joselyn, Honglin Chen, Kwok-Yung Yuen, Jasper Fuk-Woo Chan, Zhiwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The recently emerged cattle H5N1/Texas/2024 strain highlights the need for effective prophylactic and therapeutic drug interventions, yet most existing neutralizing antibodies (NAbs) have limited efficacy against genetically divergent pathogenic influenza viruses. Here we engineer a sialic acid-anchored tandem M-SiaB by fusing a hemagglutinin (HA) stalk-specific monoclonal NAb with a sialic acid-receptor-binding domain (SiaB). M-SiaB shows 4- to 20-fold greater neutralizing potency against diverse authentic influenza viruses compared to the parental NAb and suppresses multiple stages of the viral life cycle, including viral attachment, entry and release. Importantly, intranasal M-SiaB confers markedly enhanced protection against nasal challenges with the pathogenic H1N1/PR8 and H5N1/Texas/2024 strains. Notably, a single dose of M‑SiaB maintains survival after a highly lethal H5N1/Texas/2024 challenge for up to 21 days. These findings demonstrate that simultaneously targeting HA stalk and sialic acid-receptor is a promising strategy to enhance the potency and breadth of NAbs against genetically divergent pathogenic influenza viruses.

Introduction

Influenza virus infection caused by newly emerging strains with pandemic potential remains a major public concern. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the global influenza burden accounts for the annual morbidity of about 1 billion people1. While seasonal influenza epidemics are associated with various degrees of clinical complications in both general and immunocompromised populations, the threat from highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) virus is potentially greater, contingent upon its potential for human-to-human transmission2–4. In recent years, the clade 2.3.4.4b HPAI H5N1 has been particularly worrisome because the overall fatality rate of H5N1 is ~52% in humans to date5,6. Although HPAI H5N1 viruses have not resulted in a major pandemic, the outbreak of HPAI H5N1/Texas/2024 virus, belonging to the clade 2.3.4.4b, has been reported in cattle herds and subsequently linked to 70 human cases, including one fatality, since the spring of 20247–9. A study has demonstrated that the clade 2.3.4.4b has caused more infections and transmissions in mammals10. Due to the risk of transmission from mammals to humans, effective prophylactic and therapeutic drug interventions including broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) are needed.

Vaccination remains one of the most effective modalities for protection. However, antigenic drift and shift frequently reduce vaccines effectiveness11. As an alternative strategy, passive immunization using bnAbs is useful not only for prophylaxis and therapy but also for novel vaccine discovery12. BnAb epitopes are predominantly located in the conserved stalk (HA2) region of HA, whereas antibodies targeting the head (HA1) region are generally strain-specific or only partially cross-reactive13. On the one hand, cross-reactive bnAbs C05 and F045-092 target the head region14,15. These bnAbs typically exhibit high neutralizing potency but narrow breadth due to the highly variable nature of the head region. On the other hand, cross-reactive bnAbs MEDI8852, FI6v3 and CR9114 target the more conserved stalk region16–18. These bnAbs exhibit broader coverage of influenza viruses in both group 1 and group 2, but some mutations resulting from antigenic drift and host selection pressure can affect their potency19. For instance, a mutation at the HA2-I45 site has been reported that significantly decreases the efficacy of FI6v3 and CR9114 against both H1N1 and H3N2 strains20. The A388T mutation within the HA trimer also conferred escape from some stalk bnAbs21,22. To improve the potency and breadth of the bnAbs, we sought to engineer bispecific bnAbs.

A bispecific antibody targeting at two different HA stalk epitopes has been previously reported for preventing viral escape23. However, its antiviral activity was comparable to that of the parental mAbs. Recently, several bispecific antibodies have been developed to block both influenza HA and neuraminidase (NA) proteins24,25. Since these bispecific antibodies exhibited only comparable or marginally enhanced antiviral activities compared to their parental mAbs, they might not be able to simultaneously bind to the desired two viral epitopes due to steric hindrance. To date, no studies have explored the simultaneous blockade of influenza HA and its sialic acid receptor. In this study, we engineered a panel of bispecific bnAbs by fusing HA stalk-specific bnAbs with a receptor sialic acid-binding domain (SiaB). These efforts led to the discovery of a potent sialic acid-anchoring bispecific bnAb, M-SiaB. Herein, we describe the characterization of its mode of action, potency in vitro and efficacy in in vivo infection models.

Results

Design and preparation of bispecific bnAbs

We first assessed the neutralizing activity of six existing bnAbs (F045-092, CR6261, CT149, MEDI8852, 1G01, and Z2B3) against diverse H1N1/PR8, H2N2/JAPAN, H3N2/AICHI, H5N1/QingHai, H5N1/Turkey, H7N9/ShangHai pseudoviruses, and two authentic strains (H1N1/WSN and H3N2/HK68) (Fig. S1A–C and Table S1). Among these candidates, MEDI8852 demonstrated the broadest neutralizing spectrum, neutralizing all viral strains tested (Fig. S1D). To construct sialic acid-anchored bnAbs, we engineered a panel of recombinant bispecific antibodies by fusing HA stalk-specific bnAbs MEDI8852 or NA-specific bnAb 1G01 with the SiaB that was derived from Vibrio cholerae NanH sialidase without the catalytic region (Fig. 1a and S2A and Table S2)26. Among these engineered antibodies, MEDI8852-based M-SiaB and M-SiaB2 were shortlisted for further analysis because the 1G01-SiaB did not show improved neutralizing activity compared to the parental 1G01 (Fig. S2B). As monomeric controls, Fc-SiaB and Fc-SiaB2 were constructed and produced for comparison (Fig. 1a). After the Protein-A affinity chromatography, these purified proteins were analyzed by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) (Fig. 1b). As expected, the elution peak of M-SiaB appeared earlier than that of MEDI8852 while both products exhibited a single monodisperse peak. The elution peak of Fc-SiaB also aligned with the predicted size, with minor aggregation observed. M-SiaB2 and Fc-SiaB2, however, showed two peaks (Fig. 1b), suggesting that their two tandem SiaB domains might induce some protein instability. The purity of these proteins was also measured by SDS-PAGE (Fig. S3). Binding of M-SiaB to HA and Fc Receptors

To evaluate the binding properties of the purified products, we first assessed their binding activities with HA proteins derived from various influenza strains by ELISA. These strains included H1N1/Victoria/2022, H3N2/Darwin/2021, H5N1/HK/483/97, and H7N9/Shanghai/2013 (Fig. 1c). We found that M-SiaB exhibited EC50 values comparable to those of the parental antibody MEDI8852 (Fig. 1d), suggesting that the addition of the SiaB domain(s) did not affect the specific HA binding of M-SiaB. In contrast, the binding of M-SiaB2 was reduced relative to M-SiaB and MEDI8852, probably due to protein misfolding and aggregation (Fig. 1b and Fig. S3). As expected, Fc-SiaB and Fc-SiaB2 had no binding to the HA proteins. Subsequently, we analyzed the binding kinetics of MEDI8852 and M-SiaB with HA proteins by surface plasmon resonance (SPR). MEDI8852 and M-SiaB were immobilized onto CM5 sensor chips and exposed to various dilutions of HA proteins. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) values were determined using a 1:1 binding model. Consistent with the ELISA results, M-SiaB exhibited KD values like those of the parental antibody for binding to HA proteins (Fig. S4). These results indicated that M-SiaB is a lead candidate for further development, given its unshifted binding affinity.

We then compared the binding affinities of M-SiaB to human FcRn and FcγRIIIa, which are related to the antibody half-life and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) functions27, respectively. ELISA results demonstrated that M-SiaB exhibited similar binding affinities to FcRn and FcγRIIIa compared to MEDI8852 (Fig. 1e, f). Binding affinities to Fc receptors were also analyzed by SPR (Fig. 1g, h). MEDI8852 and M-SiaB showed similar binding affinities to human FcRn (KD = 0.107 µM for MEDI8852 and 0.114 µM for M-SiaB) and to human FcγRIIIa (KD = 0.79 µM for MEDI8852 and 0.32 µM for M-SiaB). These results demonstrate that the SiaB domain in M-SiaB does not interfere with Fc receptor binding of the MEDI8852 mAb.

M-SiaB binds the sialic acid receptor and inhibits HA binding to host cells

We evaluated M-SiaB binding to sialic acid receptors expressed on the surface of host cells. The binding affinities of M-SiaB, Fc-SiaB, and MEDI8852 to α2,3- and α2,6-linked sialic acid receptors were first determined by biolayer interferometry (BLI). As expected, MEDI8852 exhibited no detectable binding to either sialylated receptor. In contrast, both M-SiaB and Fc-SiaB demonstrated comparable binding affinities for α2,3-sialic acid (KD = 14.95 nM for Fc-SiaB and 6.219 nM for M-SiaB) and α2,6-sialic acid (KD = 7.814 nM for Fc-SiaB and 3.396 nM for M-SiaB) (Fig. S5). To further confirm that sialic acid is the binding receptor for M-SiaB on the MDCK cell surface, we treated MDCK cells with sialidase to remove sialic acids as a control. The expression of α2,3- and α2,6-linked sialic acids, detected by the maackia amurensis II (MAL-II) and sambucus nigra lectin (SNA) staining, was significantly decreased after the sialidase treatment (Fig. 2a). Different concentrations of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB were incubated with MDCK cells, followed by staining with APC-conjugated anti-human IgG antibody for flow cytometry. The histogram graphs and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) were shown in Fig. 2b, c. While MEDI8852 did not bind to the cell surface, M-SiaB bound to the MDCK cell surface with the potency like the control Fc-SiaB. In contrast, the binding abilities of M-SiaB and Fc-SiaB were decreased dose-dependently after the sialidase treatment. Consistently, live imaging studies demonstrated that M-SiaB binds specifically to the MDCK cell surface via sialic acids because this binding was largely abrogated after the pretreatment with sialidase (Fig. S6A). We investigated whether the SiaB domain on Fc-SiaB blocks viral HA binding to MDCK cells. The cells were either preincubated with 1 μM of Fc-SiaB or left untreated, after which the binding of various HA proteins was assessed by flow cytometry. We found that all HA proteins tested (H1, H3, H5, and H7) bound effectively to MDCK cells without Fc-SiaB pretreatment. In contrast, pretreatment with Fc-SiaB reduced HA binding significantly (Fig. 2d and Fig. S6B). Notably, the pretreatment with Fc-SiaB did not block HA binding completely, likely due to the abundant expression of sialic acids on the MDCK cell surface. These findings demonstrate that the SiaB domain interferes with HA binding to sialic acid receptors

M-SiaB exhibits enhanced neutralizing potency against authentic influenza viruses

To determine the activity of M-SiaB on influenza viral neutralization, we conducted microneutralization assays comparing the efficacy of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, Fc-SiaB, and the combination of MEDI8852 with Fc-SiaB against a panel of diverse authentic virus strains. These strains covered five subtypes of human influenza viruses including H1N1/PR8, H1N1/Guangdong/2019, H1N1/WSN, H3N2/HK/2019, and H3N2/HK68 (Fig. 3a–e). We found that the neutralizing half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of M-SiaB were about four- to ten-fold lower than that of the parental MEDI8852 (Fig. 3g), indicating enhanced potency in vitro. As a control, Fc-SiaB did not exhibit neutralizing activities at the highest concentration of 30 μg/mL against all strains tested. The combination of MEDI8852 and Fc-SiaB exhibited reduced or comparable neutralizing efficacy compared to MEDI8852 mAb alone. Notably, we tested the neutralizing potency of M-SiaB against the recently emerged cattle H5N1/Texas/2024 influenza virus that uses both α2,3- and α2,6-linked sialic acid receptors28. The neutralizing potency of M-SiaB (IC50 = 0.19 μg/mL) was about 20-fold greater than the parental MEDI8852 (IC50 = 3.86 μg/mL) and the combination of MEDI8852 with Fc-SiaB (IC50 = 3.64 μg/mL) (Fig. 3f, g). M-SiaB blocks viral attachment, entry and progeny virion release

To understand the mode of M-SiaB action, we tested it during the lifecycle of influenza infection, including viral attachment, entry and progeny virion release. For anti-attachment experiments29, H1N1/PR8 and H3N2/HK68 viruses were preincubated with varying concentrations of M-SiaB compared against MEDI8852 or Fc-SiaB alone, as well as a combination of MEDI8852 with Fc-SiaB. The culture mixtures were subsequently used to infect MDCK cells at 4 °C for 1.5 h. The 4 °C incubation allows viral attachment but prevents viral entry into MDCK cells. After the removal of unbound virus, the cells were cultured in fresh medium for 24 h. Viral infection was then assessed by measuring NP expression in the cells using an ELISA assay. We found that M-SiaB enhanced the inhibition activity of attachment with the half-maximal attachment inhibition (AI50) values of 0.09 μg/mL for H1N1/PR8 and 0.8 μg/mL for H3N2/HK68, reflecting a 21-fold and 27-fold reduction, because MEDI8852 exhibited higher AI50 values of 1.93 μg/mL for H1N1/PR8 and 21.4 μg/mL for H3N2/HK68, respectively. In contrast, Fc-SiaB was inactive. Moreover, the combination of MEDI8852 with Fc-SiaB resulted in reduced activity against H1N1/PR8 (AI50 = 8.59 μg/mL) and activity comparable to MEDI8852 alone against H3N2/HK68 (AI50 = 20.94 μg/mL) (Fig. 3h, k). Next, for anti-entry experiments29, MDCK cells were incubated with H1N1/PR8 and H3N2/HK68 viruses at 4 °C, allowing for viral attachment first. After unbound viruses were removed and washed, M-SiaB, MEDI8852, Fc-SiaB or the combination of MEDI8852 and Fc-SiaB was added to the cells during the subsequent viral entry period. Viral infection was then assessed after 24 h incubation by measuring NP expression in the cells using an ELISA assay. We found that M-SiaB significantly inhibited viral entry, with IC50 values of 0.69 μg/mL for H1N1/PR8 and 11.79 μg/mL for H3N2/HK68. In contrast, neither MEDI8852 nor the combination of MEDI8852 with Fc-SiaB exhibited significant inhibitory activity. Fc-SiaB alone showed no detectable antiviral entry activity (Fig. 3i, k). These results indicated that M-SiaB possesses a distinct advantage in blocking viral entry following viral attachment. To assess the ability of M-SiaB to inhibit virion release25, MDCK cells were infected with 0.1 MOI of H1N1/PR8 and H3N2/HK68 strains for 24 h, respectively. After washing, cells were treated with serial concentrations of M-SiaB or control compounds in serum-free medium supplemented with 2 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin for 1 h. The released progeny virus was then used to infect fresh MDCK cells, and infection rates were quantified by measuring HA expression via flow cytometry. We observed that M-SiaB exhibited substantially enhanced potency in inhibiting virion release. Compared to MEDI8852, M-SiaB showed a 5.9-fold lower IC50 (0.23 µg/mL vs. 1.35 µg/mL) against H1N1/PR8 and a 76.1-fold lower IC50 (0.16 µg/mL vs. 12.19 µg/mL) against H3N2/HK68. However, the combination of MEDI8852 and Fc-SiaB displayed comparable or reduced inhibitory activity relative to MEDI8852 alone (Fig. 3j, k). Collectively, these results demonstrated that M-SiaB blocks influenza infection at multiple stages of the viral lifecycle with enhanced potency.

M-SiaB shows improved in vivo efficacy against nasal challenge with influenza H1N1/PR8 virus

We next investigated the efficacy of M-SiaB against the H1N1/PR8 virus in BALB/c mice. Groups of mice were intranasally administered with either a high dose (3 mg/kg, n = 5) or a low dose (0.3 mg/kg, n = 4) of M-SiaB or MEDI8852. A separate control group received the placebo PBS (n = 5). Twelve hours post-administration, each mouse was challenged intranasally with 1000 PFU H1N1/PR8 influenza virus. Body weight was monitored daily post-challenge, and lung and nasal turbinate tissues were collected 3 days post-infection for analysis (Fig. 4a). We found that no significant body weight loss was observed in any animal in the MEDI8852 or M-SiaB groups (Fig. 4b). In contrast, viral infection resulted in approximately a 16% body weight loss in the PBS control group. Moreover, viral loads in the lung and nasal turbinate tissues revealed that high-dose (3 mg/kg) pretreatment with either MEDI8852 or M-SiaB conferred substantial protection against lung infection, resulting in a 3-log reduction (p < 0.001 or p < 0.01) or undetectable PFU (p < 0.0001 or p < 0.001) in most mice. Notably, 0.3 mg/kg pretreatment with M-SiaB reduced lung infection by 1 log PFU (p < 0.01), whereas MEDI8852 at the same dose did not provide significant protection (Fig. 4d, left). Viral RNA results were consistent with plaque assay findings (Fig. 4c, left). For protection in nasal turbinates, M-SiaB demonstrated superior efficacy compared to MEDI8852 because 80% of mice treated with 3 mg/kg M-SiaB exhibited undetectable PFU in nasal tissues (Fig. 4d, right, p < 0.001). In contrast, only one mouse showed undetectable PFU in 3 mg/kg MEDI8852 group, whereas other mice reduced the average PFU by one log (p < 0.05). In low-dose (0.3 mg/kg) groups, neither M-SiaB nor MEDI8852 significantly decreased PFU (Fig. 4d, right). More sensitive RT-PCR analysis, however, revealed that the low 0.3 mg/kg dose of M-SiaB treatment further reduced viral RNA by approximately one log, comparable to the 3 mg/kg MEDI8852 group (Fig. 4c, right). Notably, the 0.3 mg/kg MEDI8852 did not show any reduction in viral loads. Mice were administered 10 mg/kg of M-SiaB at 7, 3, or 1 day(s) prior to challenge with 20 PFU of H5N1/Texas/2024 on day 0 (Fig. 7c). Distribution and repeated use of M-SiaB in mice

To determine whether the SiaB domain improves the long-term anchoring of M-SiaB in the nasal cavity, thereby enhancing its in vivo efficacy against nasal challenge with influenza viruses, we assessed the tissue distribution of M-SiaB following intranasal administration. Nasal turbinates and lung tissues were collected at 1 and 7 days post-treatment from mice that received PBS, 100 μg of MEDI8852, or 100 μg of M-SiaB intranasally. Antibody presence in tissue sections was detected using Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human IgG antibodies. M-SiaB was more abundant than MEDI8852 within the nasal cavity, with the majority of the signal observed in epithelial cells at 1 day post-treatment. Notably, MEDI8852 was no longer detectable in nasal epithelial cells while M-SiaB binding remained evident in the epithelial layer at 7 days post-treatment (Fig. S11A). In lung tissue, both MEDI8852 and M-SiaB showed comparable fluorescence intensities, primarily localized to the epithelial cells of the alveoli 1 day after administration. By day 7, M-SiaB persisted in the alveolar epithelial cells, whereas MEDI8852 was absent (Fig. S11B). These results indicated that M-SiaB predominantly accumulated in the respiratory tract, particularly within the nasal cavity, through its interaction with sialic acid receptors, while MEDI8852 was transiently retained before diffusing into the bloodstream.

To determine whether the immunogenic potential of SiaB could affect the repeated use of M-SiaB for protection, mice were initially immunized with a high dose (10 mg/kg) of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, Fc-SiaB or PBS (Fig. S12A). Thirty days after the primary immunization, all groups received a preventive dose of 3 mg/kg M-SiaB one day prior to challenge with H5N1/Texas/2024 influenza virus. Remarkably, all treatment groups, regardless of the initial immunization (PBS, MEDI8852, M-SiaB, or Fc-SiaB), showed complete protection, with titers BLQ in both the lungs (Fig. S12B) and nasal turbinates (Fig. S12C). These findings demonstrated that initial administration of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, or Fc-SiaB does not interfere with the subsequent antiviral efficacy of M-SiaB.

Discussion

In this study, we describe an engineered sialic acid-anchored bnAb M-SiaB made by fusing a HA stalk-specific monoclonal bnAb with a receptor sialic acid-binding domain SiaB. Although the SiaB domain alone does not neutralize influenza viruses effectively, it binds to the cellular sialic acid receptor and therefore anchors the bnAb M-SiaB at the portal of viral entry, resulting in 4- to 20-fold greater neutralizing potency against genetically divergent authentic influenza viruses as compared with the parental bnAb. Moreover, the anchoring of M-SiaB to the sialic acid receptor uniquely leads to three-stage suppression of the influenza viral lifecycle by blocking viral attachment, entry and progeny virion release with increased potency. Importantly, intranasal administration of M-SiaB conferred markedly enhanced protection against nasal challenges using the highly pathogenic H1N1/PR8 and cattle H5N1/Texas/2024 strains, respectively, alleviating severe lung injuries during the acute phase of infection. Notably, the elimination of viral infection in the nasal turbinate was achieved by 3 mg/kg M-SiaB but not by the same dose of parental antibody. Our findings on M-SiaB, therefore, have implications not only for enhanced therapeutic efficacy in alleviating severe lung injuries to save lives but also for improved prophylactic use by blocking the nasal infection of genetically divergent highly pathogenic influenza viruses.

The sialic acid-anchoring strategy enhances the antiviral potency of the HA stalk-specific bnAb through dual targeting of viral HA and host receptors, while also increasing its local retention within the respiratory tract. Currently, the number of cross-reactive bnAbs remains limited to fight diverse pathogenic influenza viruses. Although some bnAbs have been found targeting either the HA head (e.g., C05 and F045-092)14,15 or the HA stalk (e.g., MEDI8852, FI6v3, and CR9114)16–18, their limited potency and/or breadth have affected their clinical development. To overcome these limitations, some bispecific antibodies have been developed by targeting two different HA stalk epitopes23 or both HA and NA proteins24,25. Due to the spatial restriction of two distinct viral epitopes, potent synergistic neutralization is often difficult to achieve. Considering the evolutionarily conserved nature of host sialic acid receptors, we hypothesized that the strategy of sialic acid-anchored bnAb may enhance the antiviral activity at the site of viral transmission against genetically divergent influenza strains. To date, no studies have explored the concurrent blockade of influenza HA and its sialic acid receptor although some host-targeted agents have been explored31–34. We, therefore, engineered the bispecific M-SiaB. Our results showed that the engagement of the sialic acid receptor by M-SiaB did not enhance authentic influenza viral infection. We found that M-SiaB exhibits good stability for scale-up production and maintains binding affinity to HA stalk and Fc receptors equivalent to that of the parental antibody. In addition, M-SiaB retained measurable and similar binding to both α2,3- and α2,6-linked sialic acid receptors compared with Fc-SiaB. However, it displayed slightly reduced binding to cell-surface sialic acid receptors, likely due to partial steric occlusion35. The SiaB domain partially masks the HA binding to the sialic acid receptor but shows no potent viral neutralization because exceedingly high Fc-SiaB concentrations are needed to effectively block the abundant sialic acid receptors on mammalian cells36,37. Notably, enhanced antiviral activity was observed only in the M-SiaB bispecific format, but not in the combination of the two individual components of MEDI8852 and Fc-SiaB. This result suggests that the augmented activity of M-SiaB arises from the specific presentation of the two domains within the bispecific construct. In this configuration, the SiaB domain likely enriches the concentration of M-SiaB at the portal of viral entry, ambushing incoming authentic viruses as demonstrated by enhanced antiviral potency in vitro and efficacy in vivo38,39. These results are like previous findings on bispecific bnAbs against HIV-1 gp120 and host receptor CD4 with demonstrated in vivo safety and enhanced efficacy40–42. The host receptor sialic acid-anchoring strategy, therefore, is useful to further improve bnAbs targeting at various conserved epitopes of the HA stalk and head regions43–45.

MEDI8852 primarily inhibits viral entry by stabilizing the prefusion conformation of HA and preventing the low pH-induced conformational rearrangements required for membrane fusion, thereby blocking viral membrane fusion16. M-SiaB exhibits enhanced in vitro antiviral activity at three critical stages of the influenza viral lifecycle: attachment, entry and virion release. M-SiaB reduced the AI50 values by 21- to 27-fold in the viral attachment inhibition assay of authentic viruses. Strikingly, M-SiaB also demonstrates dose-dependent inhibitory activity against viral entry after viral attachment, whereas the parental antibody MEDI8852 shows only minimal activity. Furthermore, considering that HA stalk-binding antibodies can inhibit virion release by sterically hindering neighboring NA cleavage of sialic acid receptors46–48, we tested this mode of action on M-SiaB. While MEDI8852 inhibits virion release as well, M-SiaB showed further enhanced potency, probably due to better steric interference and/or masking of the sialic acid NA substrate. It is, therefore, conceivable that M-SiaB simultaneously engages viral HA stalk and host receptor, establishing a previously unrecognized barrier to impede viral attachment, entry and virion release49.

M-SiaB demonstrates enhanced in vivo efficacy against intranasal challenges with influenza H1N1/PR8 and cattle H5N1/Texas/2024 strains. The comparable efficacy of M-SiaB and MEDI8852 in reducing lung viral burden demonstrated their related mechanism of action. Notably, M-SiaB exhibited better protection than MEDI8852 against both influenza strains in the nasal cavity, conferring a unique advantage for broad-spectrum protection in the upper respiratory tract against genetically divergent highly pathogenic influenza viruses. It is known that systemic administration of neutralizing antibody often results in insufficient drug concentration in the nasal cavity for viral protection50. Consistently, M-SiaB exhibited a greater initial localization and prolonged retention in the airway mucosa, particularly in the nasal epithelium, possibly explaining the improved nasal protection observed against both influenza strains. This superior efficacy is likely attributable to the sialic acid binding domain following intranasal administration. Despite relatively low systemic exposure in plasma after intranasal delivery, M-SiaB effectively prevented viral infection in peripheral organs, including the liver, spleen, and brain, suggesting that early viral control in the respiratory tract may limit subsequent systemic dissemination. Thus far, the pandemic threat remains from emerging cattle H5N1/Texas/2024 strains, which exhibit more efficient replication and higher pathogenicity than historical H5N1 viruses51,52. Importantly, HPAI H5N1 strains are characterized by enhanced replication efficiency in lungs with worse pathological damage due to their multi-basic cleavage sites in HA53–55. A recent study has demonstrated the feasibility of intranasal delivery of a broadly neutralizing influenza antibody, supporting the clinical potential of this approach for prophylaxis against influenza56. Besides enhanced nasal protection, the advantage of M-SiaB for inhibiting influenza replication at all three stages tested, including blocking viral entry after the virus-receptor attachment, may have contributed to better protection against lung injury caused by cattle H5N1/Texas/2024. Our results may warrant the clinical development of M-SiaB for both prophylactic and therapeutic applications against genetically divergent highly pathogenic influenza viral infection.

There are some limitations in this study. First, the sialic acid-anchored domain SiaB did not exhibit potent antiviral activity. Structure- and AI-based optimization is needed to improve its antiviral potency so that the synergistic neutralization would be explored, which may also increase the barrier to prevent M-SiaB resistance. Second, although M-SiaB showed reduced prophylactic efficacy when administered 3 and 7 days before challenge in the highly pathogenic and neurotropic H5N1/Texas/2024 model, delayed body weight loss and disease progression were still observed, indicating sustained antiviral activity after intranasal delivery. Whether M-SiaB can provide more durable protection against less virulent viruses that primarily target the upper respiratory tract remains to be investigated. Third, the immunogenicity of M-SiaB remains unclear, although protective activity at 3 mg/kg was observed following 10 mg/kg pretreatment. Future PK and PK/PD studies of M-SiaB and MEDI8852 are needed to understand the antibody concentrations in the nasal and lung cavity for protection and to evaluate the immunogenicity of M-SiaB for potential human use. To avoid these limitations, future optimization of M-SiaB should consider the use of the minimal sialic acid-anchoring epitope, peptide, or antibody, which will allow its repeated therapeutic doses to be administered clinically without inducing anti-drug antibody. In addition, since the release of progeny viruses is mediated by NA, which cleaves sialic acid residues from glycoproteins and glycolipids57, how M-SiaB would block or mask the NA sialic acid cleavage site remains to be investigated. Also, the M-SiaB-mediated differential blockade for α2,6-linked sialic acid receptor in humans and α2,3-linked sialic acid receptor in avians will be studied58,59. Lastly, viral escape from M-SiaB would more plausibly involve mutations that diminish MEDI8852 binding while preserving functional receptor engagement. Systematic viral escape assays comparing M-SiaB and MEDI8852 will be an important future direction for quantitatively assessing resistance development.

Methods

Cells

MDCK cells (CCL34) and HEK293T cells (CRL-3216) were obtained from ATCC. Expi293F™ cells (A14527) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. MDCK cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s minimal essential medium (DMEM), supplemented with identical quantities of serum and antibiotics. Both MDCK and HEK293T cells were incubated at 37 °C in an 8% CO2 atmosphere. Expi293F™ cells were maintained in Expi293™ Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere on an orbital shaker platform at 125 rpm.

Authentic influenza viruses

Authentic influenza viruses (Table S1) used in this study were rescued by reverse genetics and propagated in MDCK cells, titered, and used to evaluate neutralizing activity. For in vivo challenge experiments, authentic H1N1/PR8 (A/PR8/1934 H1N1) and H5N1/Texas/2024 (A/dairy cattle/Texas/24-008749-003-original/2024 virus) (GISAID number: EPI_ISL_19014386) strains were used as previously described5.

Mice

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from the Center for Comparative Medicine Research (CCMR) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU). All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Committee on the Use of Live Animals in Teaching and Research of HKU (CULATR: 24-274). All animal experiments related to the influenza virus were performed in a biosafety level 3 laboratory at HKU. Animals were housed and bred under an AAALAC International accredited program at the Center for Comparative Medicine Research (CCMR), The University of Hong Kong. Mice were maintained in individually ventilated cages under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle in environmentally controlled rooms. Room temperature was maintained at 70 ± 2 °F, and humidity levels were maintained between 30 and 70%.

Construction of plasmids expressing bnAbs and fusion proteins

The sequences of the variable regions of MEDI8852, 1G01 and SiaB were determined using the NCBI BLAST tool and synthesized by BGI. Expression plasmids for mAbs were constructed by subcloning the variable regions of the heavy and light chains into the mammalian expression vector Abvec, which contains the conserved regions of human IgG1 and human κ chain, respectively. To construct M-SiaB, M-SiaB2, 1G01-SiaB, Fc-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB2, one or two tandem SiaB fragments (residues 25–216 from Vibrio cholerae NanH sialidase, wing 1) were linked to the C-terminus of the mAb heavy chain or human IgG1 Fc region using a (GGGGS)3 linker and subsequently cloned into the Abvec vector. Amino acid sequences of antibody constructs used in this study are provided in Table S2. Fc-SiaB and Fc-SiaB2 were expressed by transfecting Expi293F™ cells with the respective expression plasmids, while mAbs and SiaB fused antibodies (M-SiaB, M-SiaB2, and 1G01-SiaB) were expressed by co-transfecting Expi293F™ cells with plasmids encoding the heavy and light chains. Cell supernatants were collected after 5–7 days of culture and subjected to affinity purification using protein-A resin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The purified antibodies and Fc fusion proteins were then concentrated and buffer-exchanged with PBS using centrifugal ultrafiltration.

ELISA for measuring HA binding

High-binding 96-well EIA/RIA plates were coated overnight with 1 μg/mL of HA proteins (ACROBiosystems, SinoBiological) in PBS buffer (pH 7.4). The plates were then washed three times with a wash buffer consisting of PBS and 0.05% Tween-20 (pH 7.4). To block nonspecific binding, 150 μL of 1% BSA in PBS was added to each well, followed by a 1-h incubation at 37 °C. The plates were then washed twice with wash buffer. Serial dilutions of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, M-SiaB2, Fc-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB2 in 1% BSA-PBS buffer were added to the wells. The plates were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, 100 μL of a 1:4000 dilution of HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to wells after three washes. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and then washed five times with wash buffer. To develop the colorimetric reaction, 30 μL of T4444 substrate was added to each well, and the reaction was stopped by adding 10 μL of 0.5 M H2SO4 when a blue color developed. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

ELISA for measuring Fc receptor binding

Ninety-six-well EIA/RIA high-binding plates were coated with 1 μg/mL of either MEDI8852 or M-SiaB in PBS (pH 7.4) buffer and incubated overnight. The plates were then washed three times with wash buffer. To block the plates, 150 μL of 1% BSA in PBS was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following the blocking step, 100 μL of serial dilutions of His-conjugated hFcγRIII protein (ACROBiosystems, prepared in pH 7.4, 1% BSA buffer) and hFcRn protein (ACROBiosystems, prepared in pH 6.0, 1% BSA buffer) were added to the respective wells after two washes with pH 7.4 and pH 6.0 buffers. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, a secondary HRP-anti-His antibody (Abcam), diluted 1:2000, was added at 100 μL per well following three washes with pH 7.4 and pH 6.0 buffers. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and washed five times with pH 7.4 and pH 6.0 buffers. Finally, 30 μL of T4444 substrate was added to each well, and the reaction was stopped by adding 10 μL of 0.5 M H2SO4 when a blue color developed. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

The binding affinities of MEDI8852 and M-SiaB with HA and Fc receptor proteins were assessed using a Biacore T200 instrument via SPR. MEDI8852 or M-SiaB were immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip through the amine coupling method, achieving final capture surface densities of ~1000 resonance units (RU). Two-fold serial dilutions of HA and FcγRIIIa proteins were prepared in PBS at pH 7.4, while FcRn proteins were prepared in PBS at pH 6.0. These protein solutions were sequentially injected at a flow rate of 30 µL/min for 180 s, followed by a dissociation phase lasting 300 s. The association and dissociation curves were recorded, and nonspecific binding was subtracted from the data. The equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) values were determined from the binding data using the BIAcore 3000 evaluation software, employing a 1:1 binding model.

Biolayer interferometry (BLI)

The binding affinities of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB to α2,3- and α2,6-linked sialic acid receptors were measured by BLI using the Octet R2 system (ForteBio). Biotinylated 3′SLN-C3-PAA and 6′SLN-C3-PAA (GlycoNZ) were immobilized onto pre-hydrated streptavidin (SA) biosensors at 10 μg/mL. Two-fold serial dilutions of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB were prepared in PBS at pH 7.4, starting from 320 nM. Kinetic measurements were performed with sequential baseline (60 s), ligand loading (40 s), equilibration (60 s), association (180 s), and dissociation (240 s) steps, followed by a final baseline (60 s). The equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) values were calculated based on a 1:1 binding model following the analysis of generated sensorgrams using Octet Analysis Studio.

Cell surface binding of the host sialic acid receptor

MDCK cells were digested and washed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h with 1 U/mL of Neuraminidase (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. N6514-1UN) in PBS with 2% FBS. The expression of α2,3 and α2,6-sialic acids was detected by staining the cells with 1 μg/mL biotinylated MAL-II and 1 μg/mL FITC-conjugated SNA for 1 h. APC-streptavidin was added to label the fluorescence of MAL-II. To assess the sialic acid receptors binding, various concentrations of MEDI8851, M-SiaB and Fc-SiaB were prepared in PBS with 2% FBS and incubated with either neuraminidase-treated or untreated MDCK cells for 1 h. After two washes with PBS, cells were incubated with APC-conjugated anti-human IgG antibody (BioLegend) for 30 min. The expression of sialic acids on MDCK cells and the binding affinities of antibodies were detected by flow cytometry.

For the imaging study of cell surface binding, MDCK cells were seeded into a 96-well black/clear bottom plate at a density of 30,000 cells per well and cultured overnight. Neuraminidase (0.04 U) was added to the cells in 100 μL of serum-free medium (SFM). After a 1-h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, 10 μg/mL biotinylated MAL-II, 10 μg/mL FITC-conjugated SNA, 1 μM MEDI8852, and 1 μM M-SiaB in SFM were respectively added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, FITC-conjugated streptavidin was added to label MAL-II, and PE-conjugated anti-human IgG was added to label MEDI8852 and M-SiaB. After a 30-min staining period and subsequent washing, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was added to stain the cell nuclei. Finally, cell fluorescence was observed with a Carl Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscope and analyzed using ZEN 3.3 software (Blue edition).

Fc-SiaB blocking of HA protein binding to cells

MDCK cells were digested and washed with PBS. The cells were either preincubated with 1 μM Fc-SiaB in PBS containing 2% FBS for 2 h, or left untreated, and subsequently washed with PBS. Following this, 10 μg/mL of HA proteins with a His tag were added to the cells and incubated for 1 h. After two washes with PBS, APC-conjugated anti-His antibodies were used as a secondary antibody to label the HA proteins. The HA binding ability was determined by measuring APC fluorescence of the cells using flow cytometry. The fluorescence intensity of the HA binding signal was calculated by multiplying the percentage of positive cells by the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Microneutralization assay against authentic influenza viruses

MDCK cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 15,000 cells per well and cultured overnight. MEDI8852, M-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB or a combination of MEDI8852 + Fc-SiaB (both at the same concentration as the individual treatments) were serially diluted in serum-free MEM and mixed with 100 TCID50 of authentic influenza viruses at a 1:1 volume ratio. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently added to MDCK cells. After a 1-h incubation at 37 °C, the mixtures were removed. Cells were then cultured at 37 °C for an additional 20 h in 100 μL of serum-free MEM containing 2 μg/mL TPCK-Trypsin, along with the same concentration of the corresponding antibodies. Viral infection was assessed by detecting influenza nucleoprotein (NP) expression using ELISA. Briefly, Cells were first fixed with 80% acetone for 10 min and subsequently washed with a wash buffer containing 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS. A mouse anti-influenza NP antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific), diluted 1:1000 in 5% skim milk in PBS, was added to the cells and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Following three washes, a HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific), diluted 1:20,000 in 5% skim milk in PBS, was added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, 30 μL of T4444 substrate was added to each well after five washes, and the reaction was stopped by adding 10 μL of 0.5 M H2SO4 when a blue color developed. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Microneutralization assay against pseudotyped strains

Influenza pseudoviruses were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with the Env-deficient, HIV-1-based PNL4.3-luciferase backbone plasmid, together with separate pVAX1 plasmids expressing the influenza HA and NA proteins. Pseudovirus-containing supernatants were collected 48 h after transfection and treated with 2 μg/mL TPCK-Trypsin at 37 °C for 1 h to cleave HA0 into HA1 and HA2 (except for H5N1 strains, which can be processed without TPCK-Trypsin treatment). The complete viral strain designations of the pseudoviruses used in this study were shown in Table S1. For the microneutralization assay, MDCK cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 15,000 cells per well in 100 μL cell culture medium and grew overnight. bnAbs were serially diluted in cell culture medium and then mixed with the pseudovirus at a 1:1 ratio. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by the addition of 50 μL mixture to each well of MDCK cells. After a 48-h incubation, the supernatants were removed. Cells were lysed with 50 μL of lysis buffer, and 30 μL of the cell lysate was transferred to a white 96-well plate containing 30 μL of luciferase substrate (Promega). Viral infection was quantified by measuring luciferase activity using a plate reader.

Viral attachment inhibition assay

The viral attachment and entry inhibition assays were conducted following published procedures29. MEDI8852, M-SiaB, Fc-SiaB, or a combination of MEDI8852 + Fc-SiaB (both at the same concentration as the individual treatments) were serially diluted threefold in MEM medium, with initial concentrations of 300 μg/mL. MEDI8852 and M-SiaB were mixed in a 1:1 ratio with H1N1/PR8 or H3N2/HK68 viruses at an MOI of 0.1, and the mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Fc-SiaB dilutions were preincubated with MDCK cells for 1 h. Subsequently, the MDCK cells and antibody-virus mixtures were chilled at 4 °C for 30 min. The antibody-virus mixtures or viruses alone were then added to chilled MDCK cells on ice and incubated for 1.5 h at 4 °C. After two washes with PBS, the cells were replenished with fresh complete medium containing FBS and cultured for an additional 24 h. Intracellular viral NP expressions were detected by ELISA in the same manner as in the microneutralization assay against authentic influenza viruses.

Viral entry inhibition assay

MDCK cells were chilled at 4 °C for 30 min, then infected with H1N1/PR8 or H3N2/HK68 viruses at an MOI of 0.5 on ice. The cells were incubated at 4 °C for 1.5 h to facilitate virus attachment. Unbound viruses were removed by washing three times with PBS. MEDI8852, M-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB, or a combination of MEDI8852 + Fc-SiaB (both at the same concentration as the individual treatments) were serially diluted (threefold) in serum-free MEM containing 2 μg/mL TPCK-treated trypsin and added to the infected cells. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow viral entry and fusion with the cell membrane. After two washes with PBS, the cells were replaced with fresh complete medium containing FBS and cultured for an additional 24 h. Intracellular viral NP expressions were detected by ELISA in the same manner as in the microneutralization assay against authentic influenza viruses.

Virion release inhibition assay

The ability of antibodies to inhibit NA activity was assessed by measuring the inhibition of progeny virus release as previously described25. MDCK cells were seeded into 96-well plates and infected with 0.1 MOI of H1N1/PR8 or H3N2/HK68 viruses for 24 hours. Following infection, the cells were washed with PBS. MEDI8852, M-SiaB, and Fc-SiaB, or a combination of MEDI8852 + Fc-SiaB (both at the same concentration as the individual treatments), were serially diluted in serum-free MEM supplemented with 2 μg/mL TPCK-Trypsin, and 100 μL of the diluted solutions were added to the cells. After a 1-h incubation at 37 °C, the cells were washed with PBS, and the medium was replaced with 100 μL of MEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 1 h to inhibit viral replication. The cells were then replenished with serum-free MEM and subsequently cultured for 48 h. The supernatants, containing newly budded viruses, were collected and added to MDCK cells seeded in 24-well plates for a 1-h incubation. The cells were then washed and cultured in serum-free MEM for 24 h. The proportion of infected cells was determined by measuring HA expression. Cells were detached and incubated with 1 μg/mL MEDI8852 mAb in PBS with 2% FBS for 40 min, followed by staining with an APC-conjugated goat anti-human IgG secondary antibody (BioLegend) for 40 min. HA expression was analyzed using flow cytometry.

Mouse viral challenge experiment

For viral load analysis, mice were randomly assigned to receive intranasal administration of either MEDI8852 or M-SiaB at doses of 3 or 0.3 mg/kg. The same volume of PBS administration was used as a control. The MLD50 of H1N1/PR8 and H5N1 virus A/dairy cattle/Texas/24-008749-003-original/2024 was 200 and 1 PFU, respectively, as described previously5,60. Twelve hours post-administration, the mice were intranasally challenged with 1000 PFU of the H1N1/PR8 virus or 500 PFU of the H5N1 virus A/dairy cattle/Texas/24-008749-003-original/2024. Body weights were monitored daily following infection. The mice were sacrificed three days post-infection, and their lungs, nasal turbinates, brains, spleens, and livers were harvested for analysis.

For the animal survival study, mice were intranasally administered 10 mg/kg of M-SiaB on days −7, −3, and −1 prior to viral challenge. PBS was administered on day −1 as a control. On day 0, the animals were inoculated with 20 PFU of A/dairy cattle/Texas/24-008749-003-original/2024 virus. Body weights were monitored daily following infection. Survival was monitored for up to 21 dpi.

Viral load assay

Lungs and nasal turbinates were homogenized and subjected to viral load determination by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) and plaque assay as previously described61. Briefly, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), followed by reverse transcription with the PrimeScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara). The real-time RT-PCR assay for Influenza non-structural protein 1 (NS1) or nucleocapsid protein (NP) was performed using the One-Step TB Green PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit II (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse β-actin was used for normalization. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S3 as previously described62. For the viral load PFU determination, a standard plaque assay was performed. Tenfold dilutions of tissue homogenate supernatants were prepared and inoculated into MDCK cell in 12-well plates for 1 h at 37 °C. Following removal of inoculum, cells were overlaid with 0.75% agarose-MEM containing 2 μg/mL TPCK-Trypsin and incubated for 72 h. Plaques were counted after formalin fixation and crystal violet staining.

Histopathology and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Lung and nasal turbinate tissues were fixed in zinc formalin and subsequently processed into paraffin-embedded blocks. Afterward, the tissues were dehydrated in alcohol, embedded in paraffin wax, sliced into 5-μm sections, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Histopathological changes in the lungs were observed under a light microscope at a low (40×) and high (400×) magnification. Histopathological changes in the nasal epithelium were observed under a light microscope with a magnification of 400×. Lung pathological lesions were independently assessed and graded in a blinded manner using the Smith scoring system63. This method evaluates four domains: alveolar and interstitial inflammation; edema; alveolar and interstitial hemorrhage; and alveolar septal thickening. Each domain was scored on a scale from 0 to 4, with 0 indicating no injury and 4 indicating severe injury. Specifically, scores were assigned as follows: 0 = no injury; 1 = injury involving less than 25%; 2 = injury involving 25 to 50%; 3 = injury involving 50 to 75%; and 4 = injury involving more than 75%. The total lung injury score was calculated as the sum of these four domain scores. For identification and localization of Influenza NP in organ tissues, immunofluorescence (IF) staining was performed on deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections using a rabbit anti-Influenza NP antibody (1:1000), followed by staining with an AF488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000) and DAPI. Images were captured with a Carl Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscope and analyzed using ZEN 3.3 software (Blue edition).

Antibody distribution assay

Eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were administered 100 μg of either MEDI8852 or M-SiaB, with 50 μL of PBS as a control. Lung and nasal turbinate samples were collected at 1 and 7 days post-administration. The tissues were fixed in zinc formalin, processed into paraffin-embedded blocks, and sectioned at 5 μm. The sections were then dehydrated in graded alcohols, embedded in paraffin, and stained with AF488-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (1:500) and DAPI for cell nucleus visualization. Images were acquired using an Operetta CLS imaging system.

Immunogenicity assay

Mice were initially immunized intranasally with 10 mg/kg of MEDI8852, M-SiaB, Fc-SiaB, or PBS. Thirty days after the primary immunization, all groups received a preventive dose of M-SiaB (3 mg/kg) 12 h prior to viral challenge, whereas the non-immunized mice received PBS as naïve controls. Mice were then intranasally infected with 500 PFU of H5N1/Texas/2024 virus. Body weights were monitored daily following infection. The mice were sacrificed 3 days post-infection, and viral titers in the lungs and nasal turbinates were assessed by plaque assay.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0, with continuous data presented as mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, we employed unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests when data met assumptions of normality and equal variance. When these assumptions were violated, the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U-test was used instead. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to normally distributed data, while the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc analysis was used for nonparametric data. Significant differences between the two groups were performed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test. In all analyses, a p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
