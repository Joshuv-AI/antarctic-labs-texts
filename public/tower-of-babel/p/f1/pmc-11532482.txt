Whole-transcriptome analysis reveals the effect of retinoic acid on small intestinal mucosal injury in cage-stressed young laying ducks
Authors: Xiaolong He, Wenzhuo Wei, Jia Liu, Zhenhua Liang, Yan Wu, Jingbo Liu, Jinsong Pi, Hao Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetinoic acid (RA) is an active derivative of vitamin A and is involved in a variety of physiological processes, including cell growth, antioxidant, and inflammation. However, the role of RA in intestinal oxidative stress injury in caged-stressed laying ducks is unknown. In this study, we analyzed the effect and underlying mechanism of RA supplementation on intestinal damage in cage-stressed young laying ducks. One hundred and sixty laying ducks were divided into 5 treatment groups, including a control group (CR) and 4 treatment groups exposed to different RA concentrations (2,500, 5,000, 7,500 and 10,000 IU/kg, TG1 to TG4). The experimental period comprised a 7-d prefeeding period and a 10-d experimental feeding period, for a total of 17 d. Phenotypic analysis revealed that compared with the control group, RA addition increased the intestinal villus height and the villus-to-crypt ratio; decreased the crypt depth (P < 0.01); decreased the serum diamine oxidase and D-lactate concentrations (P < 0.05); increased the serum antioxidant capacity and intestinal antioxidant gene expression levels (P < 0.05); and increased the expression levels of tight junction-related genes, with the greatest effect observed in TG2 group. Our further whole-transcriptome analysis of duodenum tissues from CR and TG2 ducks revealed 706 differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs), 357 differentially expressed lncRNAs (DElncRNAs), 14 differentially expressed circRNAs (DEcircRNAs), and 4 differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs). These DEGs are involved in calcium signaling, NOD-like receptor signaling, pyruvate metabolism, Jak-STAT signaling, Wnt signaling, riboflavin metabolism, and the adherens junction and tight junction pathways. The results of omics and marker gene expression analysis suggested that RA treatment may play a role in endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and apoptosis. In conclusion, the addition of RA to the diet improved intestinal injury by improving the redox homeostasis of intestinal cells associated with ERS, enhancing the intestinal tight junction structure and alleviating the apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells; moreover, 5,000 IU/kg RA was determined to be the most appropriate concentration for supplementation.Key words: retinoic acid, intestinal barrier damage, endoplasmic reticulum stress, apoptosis, whole-transcriptome analysis

INTRODUCTION

China has the largest duck industry worldwide, accounting for approximately 70% of global duck raising (Zeng et al., 2018). With the increasing attention on environmental protections and the development of intensive industrial raising of laying ducks, the original free-range model has shifted to floor-raising, net-raising, cage raising and other new raising models. Cage raising of laying ducks not only solves the pollution problems associated with traditional raising but also improves production efficiency. Studies have reported that cage raising of ducks can prolong both the overall laying period and peak laying period and increase the egg production rate (Guo, 2022). Although intensive cage raising can enhance production efficiency, it restricts the behavioral activities of ducks, potentially leading to maladaptation to stressors and inducing stress responses, particularly during the early stages of cage rearing (Ninomiya, 2014). Our previous research indicates that transitioning ducks from floor rearing to cage rearing generally induces stress responses, particularly in prelaying ducks, resulting in growth retardation, oxidative damage, and impaired intestinal barriers (Zheng et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Other studies have also demonstrated that transitioning laying ducks to cage rearing results in liver and duodenal tissue damage, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses during the initial stages, thereby affecting production performance and economic efficiency (Zhang et al., 2019; Gu et al., 2020). Additionally, nontargeted metabolomics studies have revealed that transitioning laying ducks from floor rearing to cage rearing significantly reduces plasma endogenous retinoic acid (RA) levels due to cage stress (Zheng et al., 2022). This suggests that RA may have an important role in the repair of stress damage in laying ducks.

Retinoic acid is the active metabolite of vitamin A and has various pharmacological effects, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects; RA is also involved in cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis (Elias et al., 2008; Tekade et al., 2008). RA plays an important regulatory role in the host immune response by regulating lymphocyte differentiation and B cell proliferation and differentiation (Ertesvåg et al., 2009; Iwata, 2009). Studies have shown that prehatching injections of RA can increase the production of innate immune cytokines and enhance the antigen–antibody response, thereby improving the immunity of newly hatched chicks (Alizadeh et al., 2022). RA also plays an important role in maintaining the intestinal epithelial barrier, and the bioavailability of cellular RA has been shown to determine the integrity of the intestinal epithelium, acting as a key regulatory factor in protecting the barrier during intestinal mucosal injury (Osanai et al., 2007). A previous study revealed that RA protects intestinal barrier function by increasing the levels of tight junction proteins (zonula occludens-1) and TEER, reducing the paracellular permeability of intestinal epithelial cells, and maintains epithelial integrity (Yamada and Kanda, 2019). Additionally, RA plays a key role in regulating intestinal innate immunity by promoting the differentiation and maturation of epithelial cells as well as the formation of the epithelial layer (Czarnewski et al., 2017). Supplementation with RA alleviates intestinal inflammatory damage by increasing the number and function of dendritic cells and regulatory T cells (Collins et al., 2011). RA treatment regulates the expression of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) in intestinal epithelial cells through the RARβ signaling pathway, thereby promoting the expression of zonula occluden-2 and improving intestinal epithelial barrier function (Li et al., 2017). Studies have also shown that RA can alleviate stress-induced increases in lipopolysaccharide (LPS) levels (Austenaa et al., 2009; Khafaga and El-Sayed, 2018). Lipopolysaccharide is the key indicator of intestinal barrier injury, suggesting that RA can alleviate the intestinal barrier injury caused by stress. In addition, RA can reduce the levels of oxidative stress-related markers in serum, increase the body's antioxidant capacity, and reduce oxidative damage (Li et al., 2022a). Studies have reported that the addition of RA enhances the expression of tight junction (TJ) protein markers (zonula occludens-1, occludin, and claudin-1) in duck intestinal epithelial cells, reduces intestinal permeability, and alleviates H2O2-induced barrier dysfunction in the duck intestinal epithelium (Zhang et al., 2023). These studies suggest that RA plays a key role in protecting the intestinal barrier and mitigating intestinal damage.

RNA-seq-based transcriptome analysis provides data to support the study of cell state changes at the molecular level and reveal intrinsic cell biological regulatory mechanisms (Haas and Zody, 2010; Zhai et al., 2020). With the rapid development of second-generation sequencing, many noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), including long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), circular RNAs (circRNAs), and microRNAs (miRNAs), have been found to be widely involved in and regulate various biological processes in the body (Dhanoa et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2018; Dhuppar et al., 2022). ncRNAs play important roles in the gut by participating in immune cell activation and the activation of related signaling pathways, epithelial barrier integrity and inflammatory factor production (Lashgarian et al., 2020; Fitzgerald and Shmuel-Galia, 2024; Kazmi et al., 2024). Whole-transcriptome sequencing enables systematic analysis of both coding and noncoding RNAs to quantify gene expression in vivo and better explain phenotypes at the molecular level (Jiang et al., 2015). However, the appropriate level of RA in the diet of cage-stressed laying ducks and the role and mechanism of RA in the intestinal barrier dysfunction of these ducks are still unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the optimal level of RA supplementation in the diet through exogenous gradient supplementation with RA, observe the effects of RA on intestinal barrier dysfunction in laying ducks under stress, and analyze the mechanism by which RA affects cage-stress-related intestinal damage in laying ducks.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics Statement

All animal experiments were conducted according to the guidelines established by the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (Order No.676, State Council, China, 2017.3.1).

Animals and Experimental Design

In this study, the selected ducks were raised in a floor environment before the beginning of the experimental period. Afterwards, the ducks were changed from floor rearing to cage rearing for rearing. A total of 160 female mallard ducks (10 wk old) with similar body weights (mean initial body weight was 1±0.1 kg) were then randomly divided into 5 groups: the control group (CR, basal diet), treatment group 1 (TG1, basal diet + RA 2,500 IU/kg), treatment group 2 (TG2, basal diet + RA 5,000 IU/kg), treatment group 3 (TG3, basal diet + RA 7,500 IU/kg) and treatment group 4 (TG4, basal diet + RA 10,000 IU/kg). This duck breed, using local Chinese breeds such as Jingjiang, Shaoxing, and Jinding duck as materials, is a new commercial egg-laying breed with mayu and green-shelled (developed in collaboration with Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences and Hubei Lihu Poultry & Egg Co., Ltd.). The tested RA supplementation concentration range was established according to a combination of previous cell experiments and nutritional standards for ducks (Zhang et al., 2023). The RA used was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Product Number: R2625, St. Louis, MO). Each group had 4 replicates, and each replicate included 8 ducks. The test period lasted for 17 d in total and was divided into a 7-d prefeeding period and a 10-d experimental feeding period. During the prefeeding period, all the experimental ducks were raised under floor conditions. During the experimental period, all the experimental ducks were reared in individual cages, with a cage size of 28 × 40 × 40 cm for each duck. During the experiment, all test ducks had free access to drinking water and were fed twice daily at 7:00 am and 17:00 pm. All ducks were raised at room temperature(25-27°C) with appropriate ventilation and humidity (50-70%), and ducks were housed under standard lighting conditions (10-h light/14-h dark cycle) during the test period (Hubei Academy of Agricultural Sciences Poultry Experimental Farm, Wuhan, China). Sample and Data Collection

The animal weights were taken at the beginning and end of the test period to calculate the average daily gain (ADG) during the test period, the daily feed intake was recorded, and the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated. Sample collection was carried out on the morning of the 18th d. In our previous study, approximately 10 d after caging was the critical period of transition from the stress-sensitive period to the stress-adapted period. Therefore, we collected samples on the 10th d of cage rearing (Zheng et al., 2022). One duck was randomly selected from each replicate in each group for blood sampling under the wings, and the separated serum was placed at -80°C for later use. The ducks were subsequently anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (intraperitoneal injection: 150 mg/kg) and sacrificed. Samples were collected from the duodenum of each duck and placed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent intestinal morphology analysis or liquid nitrogen for storage at -80°C. TRIzol (Invitrogen, CA), was used to extract total RNA from all duodenal tissues, which was used for reverse transcription reactions. The rationale for selecting the duodenum is based on our previous findings, which consistently indicated that the duodenum is particularly susceptible to stress-induced damage (Zhang et al., 2023,2024).

Intestinal Morphology Analysis

The duodenal tissue fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution was gradually dehydrated in ethanol and embedded in paraffin, and the wax blocks were sliced at a thickness of 5 μm via a microtome, after which they were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The sections were analyzed under a Nikon light microscope (Nikon ECLIPSE E100, Tokyo, Japan) to evaluate damage to the small intestinal villi. The villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured for nine well-preserved villi and crypts on each slide via image processing software (ImageJ 1.8.0, National Institute of Health, Bethesda, MD), and the average values were calculated. The VH/CD ratio was then determined.

Detection of Serum Antioxidant Indicators and Intestinal Permeability Indicators

The serum superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were analyzed via ELISA kits. The endotoxin (ET), diamine oxidase (DAO) and D-lactic acid (D-LA) levels in the serum were measured via commercial kits. The experiments were performed according to the manufacturer's protocols. The kits used were provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Jiancheng Institute of Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

RNA Extraction, Library Construction, and Sequencing

Samples from the CR and the TG2 groups were used, with 3 samples analyzed for each group. Total RNA was extracted from the duodenal tissue with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA). The integrity of the RNA samples was analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA); RNA concentration and purity were detected via a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (IMPLEN, CA); and samples that passed quality testing were used for subsequent library construction and sequencing. For mRNA, lncRNA, and circRNA sequencing, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) removal was performed according to the manufacturer's recommendations, and 1μg of total RNA per sample was used to generate sequencing libraries via the NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, Ipswich MA). Sequencing libraries with an insert size of approximately 250 to 300 bp were then diluted to 1 ng/μL. Finally, sequencing was performed via the Illumina NovaSeq 6,000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. For small RNA libraries, a total of 3 μg of total RNA per sample was used to generate sequencing libraries via the Illumina NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB) as recommended by the manufacturer. Briefly, the total RNA was ligated to the 5′ and 3′ adapters via the 5′ phosphate group and 3′ hydroxyl group structures of small RNAs, and the fragments were then amplified via reverse transcription and PCR and purified using 8% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) to recover DNA fragments of approximately 140 to 160 bp in size. The collected fragments were used to generate cDNA libraries, which were then sequenced via the Illumina HiSeq 2,500/2,000 platform to generate 50 bp single-end reads.

Data Quality Control

The high-throughput sequencing data were transformed into reads, and clean reads were obtained by removing the adapter, poly-N and low-quality reads from the raw data. The Q20, Q30 and GC contents of the clean reads were also calculated. Subsequent data analysis was performed using the clean reads.

Identification and Analysis of mRNAs and lncRNAs

The duck reference genome (GCF_015476345.1_ZJU1.0) and annotation files were downloaded from NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The reference genome indices were constructed via HISAT2 (v.2.0.5), and the clean reads were compared with the reference genome (Kim et al., 2015). Stringtie (v.1.3.3) was used to calculate the number of reads mapped to each gene, and the reads were spliced into transcripts based on the results of the comparison with the reference genome (Pertea et al., 2015). Transcripts were merged and compared with reference databases via Cuffmerge software (v.2.2.1) to annotate known and new transcripts. Then, transcripts with ≥2 exons and lengths >200 bp were selected as candidates. The coding potential of the candidates was predicted via CPC2, Pfam, and CNCI, and transcripts with nonprotein coding potential according to the results of these software programs were examined to identify new lncRNAs. The expression levels of the identified mRNAs and lncRNAs were quantified via Stringtie (v.1.3.3) software, and the expression levels of the genes in each sample were quantified via the fragments per kilobase per million reads (FPKM) method. Based on the quantification results, DESeq2 software (R-2.15.3) was subsequently used to analyze the significance of differences in the expression of mRNAs and lncRNAs between groups (Love et al., 2014); absolute fold changes ≥ 1 and P < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. The cis- and trans- target genes of lncRNAs were predicted according to the location relationships and expression correlations of lncRNAs with protein coding genes. Finally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed for GO and KEGG pathway enrichment via clusterProfiler (v.3.8.1) software, and pathways with P < 0.05 were defined as significantly enriched.

Identification and Analysis of circRNAs

CircRNAs were detected and identified using find_circ and CIRI to obtain the reverse shear site and features of each circRNA, and candidate circRNAs with ≥ 2 read counts in the integrated results of the 2 tools were used for further analysis. To quantify circRNA levels, circRNA expression levels were normalized to transcripts per million reads (TPM) (Zhou et al., 2010). Differentially expressed circRNAs (DEcircRNAs) were identified via DESeq2 (R-2.15.3) (Love et al., 2014), with a P < 0.05 and a |log2(foldchange)| >1 used as threshold screening conditions. The host genes of the DEcircRNAs were subsequently subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

Identification and Analysis of miRNAs

For the small RNA data, 18 to 35 nt clean reads were mapped to the duck reference genome (GCF_015476345.1_ZJU1.0) via Bowtie to analyze their expression and distribution in the reference sequence, and sequences that mapped to exons, introns or repeat sequence tags were removed. Known miRNAs were subsequently identified by searching for precursor miRNAs of different species in the miRBase database (v.22.0), while miRDeep2 software was used to predict derived novel 3p and 5p miRNAs (Friedländer et al., 2012). Finally, the expression levels of known and novel miRNAs were normalized across samples via TPM (Zhou et al., 2010). The miRNAs with P < 0.05 and | log2(fold change) | > 1, as determined via the DESeq2 R package (Love et al., 2014) were defined as differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs). The miRNA target genes were predicted via miRanda software (Enright et al., 2003) and RNAhybrid software (Krüger and Rehmsmeier, 2006). To understand the miRNA target gene functions, the predicted miRNA candidate target genes were subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses.

Real-time Quantitative PCR Analysis of Intestinal Gene Expression

Total RNA was extracted from the duodenal tissue of test ducks with TRIzol according to the method provided by the manufacturer (Invitrogen, CA) and then treated with RQ1 DNase (Promega, Madison, WI). A Beckman Coulter DU800 spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA) was used to determine the total RNA concentration. RNA integrity was assessed via the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA). cDNA was synthesized in a volume of 40 µL via the Thermo® RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo, K1662). The mRNA expression levels of glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPX1), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2), catalase (CAT), zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin (OCLN), claudin-1 (CLDN-1), CCAAT/enhance-binding protein homologous protein (CHOP), glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78), heat shock protein A8 (HSPA8), heat shock protein 90 B1 (HSP90B1), inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R), B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2), and caspase 3 (CASP3) were determined via real-time quantitative PCR (Roche, Life Science, Munich, Germany) with a SYBR Prescripts RT-PCR kit (Roche, Switzerland). The primers for the relevant genes were designed with Premier 5, as shown in Table 2. In addition, the RNA samples used for high-throughput sequencing were also used for qPCR to validate the sequencing data, and the sequencing validation primers used are described in Table S1. Relative mRNA expression was calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCT method. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene.

Statistical Analysis

The SAS 9.4 software (Cary, NC) was used to perform one-way ANOVA on the experimental data, with Tukey's test used to determine the significance of the mean differences. Polynomial contrasts for linear and quadratic responses were used to assess the effects of different levels of RA supplementation in the diet. All differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. The data are expressed as the mean and standard errors of the mean. Data visualization was performed via GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 software.

Effect of RA on the Duodenal Morphology of Laying Ducks

The effects of different treatments on the structure of the duodenal villi in ducks are shown in Figure 2. Effect of RA on Serum Antioxidant and Intestinal Permeability Indices in Laying Ducks

The serum concentrations of intestinal barrier damage indicators (D-LA, DAO, and ET) are shown in Figure 3A. Dietary supplementation with RA linearly decreased the serum D-LA concentration (P = 0.021), and the D-LA concentration in TG2 were significantly lower than those of CR (P < 0.05). The serum DAO concentrations of TG1 and TG2 were significantly lower than those of CR (P < 0.05). The addition of RA had no significant effect on the serum ET concentration (P > 0.05). Moreover, the concentrations of antioxidant indices (SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, T-AOC and MDA) in the serum are shown in Figure 3B. The serum SOD concentrations of TG1, TG2 and TG3 were significantly greater than those of CR (P < 0.05). The serum GSH-Px and CAT concentrations of TG2 were significantly greater than those of CR (P < 0.05). Dietary RA supplementation quadratically increased the serum T-AOC concentration (P = 0.004). However, the serum MDA concentrations of TG1, TG2 and TG3 were significantly lower than those of CR (P < 0.05).

Effect of RA on mRNA Expression in the Duck Duodenum

To investigate the key genes involved in mucosal repair during RA amelioration of intestinal mucosal injury, we performed whole-transcriptome analysis of intestinal samples from TG2 (the most effective dose) and CR. The CR and TG2 libraries generated 278,574,016 and 266,901,732 raw reads, respectively. After removing adapter reads and low-quality reads, we obtained 274,021,278 CR small intestine clean reads and 259,869,922 TG2 small intestine clean reads. The Q30 values of the clean reads ranged between 91.80% and 92.85%, whereas the GC content values ranged between 47.47% and 48.70%. More than 86.70% of the clean reads could be mapped to the duck reference genome. Among the mapped reads, more than 5.53% and 80.45% of the samples were multiple mapped reads and uniquely mapped reads, respectively (Table S2).

On the basis of the sequencing results, a total of 14,895 mRNAs were identified in CR and TG2. Ultimately, 706 DEmRNAs were identified between CR and TG2, of which 383 were upregulated and 323 were downregulated (Figure 5A, Table S3). The DEGs between the two groups were plotted into a clustered heatmap, and the results are shown in Figure 5B. Most of the genes that were upregulated in the TG group were mainly downregulated in CR. In contrast, genes that were predominantly downregulated in TG2 and predominantly upregulated in CR were significantly different between the two groups. GO enrichment analysis revealed that DEmRNAs were significantly enriched in 156 pathways. Among the biological processe terms, DEmRNAs were enriched mainly in fatty acid metabolic process, oxidation-reduction process, steroid biosynthetic process, acute inflammatory response pathways, and so on; among the cellular component terms, DEmRNAs were enriched mainly in the preribosome, small-subunit processome and membranes pathways; and among the molecular function terms, DEmRNAs were enriched mainly in calcium ion binding, oxidoreductase activity related-pathways (Figure 5C, Table S4). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed eight pathways enriched between the two groups, including pyruvate metabolism, calcium signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, and other pathways (Figure 5D, Table S5).

DISCUSSION

The villi of the small intestine are the first line of defense for intestinal barrier function. Damage to the structure of the villi of the small intestine inevitably leads to nutrient absorption and weakening of intestinal barrier function. The VH, CD, and VH/CD ratios are the key indicators of the reaction to intestinal absorption (Pelicano et al., 2005). Small intestinal villi increase the contact area between food and the intestine, which in turn increases the rate of nutrient absorption by the organism. Studies have shown that vitamin A deficiency can cause changes in the morphology and function of the small intestinal mucosa and downregulate the expression of brush border enzymes (Zaiger et al., 2004). Adding vitamin A to the diet can increase the villus height in the small intestine of broilers (Li et al., 2022b). RA, the main active metabolite of vitamin A, is crucial for maintaining intestinal health. In the present study, we observed disruption of the intestinal villus structure in laying ducks exposed to cage stress and its amelioration by the addition of RA. Moreover, the intestinal VH and VH/CD ratios increased, and the CD decreased significantly with increasing RA concentration. This finding is consistent with previous research showing that in SD rats fed all-trans RA, the duodenal CD tends to decrease linearly, and the VH/CD tends to increase (Mou, 2022). It has also been shown that rotavirus infection in piglets can lead to significant damage to the intestinal villus structure and that RA treatment can ameliorate this damage by increasing the VH and VH/CD ratios (Lai et al., 2024). These findings suggest that the addition of RA at an appropriate concentration can improve the negative effects of cage stress on the intestinal morphology and structure of laying ducks. Although RA significantly improved intestinal morphology and structure, this study revealed that RA did not significantly improve final body weight. We speculate that although RA plays an important role in maintaining intestinal health, its effect on overall growth performance may take longer to manifest, which may be due to the relatively short duration of this study, leading to the absence of significant phenotypic changes in growth.

Cage stress can alter the balance of the oxidative and antioxidant systems in animals, and oxidative damage can affect tissues by decreasing SOD and GSH-Px activities and increasing MDA and ROS levels (Sahin and Gümüşlü, 2004; Maheshwari and Dubey, 2009; Huang et al., 2015). RA is an active derivative of vitamin A with antioxidant function. Studies have shown that RA can reduce the level of intestinal MDA, increase the activity of SOD and GSH-Px, and reduce oxidative damage in the mouse intestinal tract (Ozdemir et al., 2013). Consistent with previous studies, dietary supplementation with RA increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, and T-AOC) in laying duck serum and decreased the serum MDA concentration in this study. In addition, a previous study showed that RA treatment can increase the expression level of antioxidant genes, regulate redox homeostasis, and reduce oxidative damage (Lai et al., 2024). These finding are consistent with our findings that dietary supplementation with RA increased the expression levels of GPX1, CAT, SOD1, and SOD2. These findings indicate that adding an appropriate amount of RA to the diet can effectively enhance the antioxidant capacity of laying ducks under cage stress and alleviate oxidative damage. Among the treatments tested, 5,000 IU/kg RA had the best treatment effect. However, with increasing addition of RA, the antioxidant capacity did not significantly improve. Similar results were reported in previous studies, where excessive supplementation with vitamin A had a negative effect on antioxidant enzyme activity to a certain extent (Huang et al., 2022). Excessive RA may produce excessive oxygen free radicals during metabolism, triggering a compensatory response from the antioxidant system to the excess RA (Vašková et al., 2014).

The integrity of the intestinal barrier plays a crucial role in the normal growth and development of organisms. However, the large amounts of ROS produced by oxidative stress can damage the intestinal barrier and cause intestinal barrier dysfunction (Lauridsen, 2019). Serum D-LA, DAO and ET are key indicators of intestinal barrier dysfunction. Studies have shown that intestinal barrier dysfunction leads to an increase in the serum concentrations of D-LA and DAO (Li et al., 2015; Du et al., 2020). In this study, dietary supplementation with 2,500 IU/kg RA and 5,000 IU/kg RA reduced the serum DAO and D-LA levels, indicating an improvement in intestinal barrier permeability. TJ proteins between intestinal epithelial cells are important components of the intestinal barrier. Oxidative stress can reduce the expression of TJ proteins between intestinal epithelial cells, increase intestinal permeability, and lead to decreased intestinal barrier function (Hashimoto et al., 2008; Bai et al., 2019; He et al., 2019). A previous study revealed that RA treatment enhances intestinal epithelial barrier integrity by increasing the expression of tight junction proteins (CLDN-1, OCLN, and ZO-1) and reducing paracellular permeability (Xiao et al., 2018; Yamada and Kanda, 2019). Another report also indicated that supplementation with RA in piglets can enhance the expression of intestinal barrier function-related proteins ZO-1 and CLDN-1, improving rotavirus-induced intestinal damage (Lai et al., 2024). For poultry, a published paper reported that supplementation with β-carotene (a provitamin A carotenoid) can increase the expression of RA receptor α (RARα) and intestinal barrier-related genes in the ileum of Hyline Brown cockerels, promoting intestinal barrier development (Hui et al., 2020). Consistent with these data, the results of the present study indicate that dietary supplementation with RA can increase the expression of intestinal tight junction-related genes and reduce intestinal permeability, alleviating intestinal barrier damage, with the best effect observed at a supplementation level of 5,000 IU/kg.

To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which RA promotes intestinal barrier repair, we performed RNA-seq on samples from the CR and TG2 groups. The sequencing results were screened to obtain 357 DEmRNAs, 706 DElncRNAs, 14 DEcircRNAs, and 4 miRNAs. We further investigated DEmRNAs and the source genes of DElncRNAs. KEGG analysis revealed that most of the DEGs were enriched in pyruvate metabolism, calcium signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, butanoate metabolism, Jak-STAT signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, riboflavin metabolism and protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) pathway. A previous study revealed that pyruvate metabolism plays an important role in maintaining antioxidant capacity and mitigating oxidative stress (Yiew et al., 2023). Another study indicated that RA signaling is involved in pyruvate metabolism and that the inhibition of RA signaling enhances mitochondrial function, increasing oxidative metabolism (Fernandes-Silva et al., 2024). In view of these findings, the enrichment of the pyruvate metabolism pathway after RA supplementation in cage-stressed laying ducks may indicate a protective mechanism. Ca2+ is an important cell signaling molecule involved in transcription factor activation, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and protein modification, and the mitochondria and ER are the main sites of Ca2+ release (Groenendyk and Michalak, 2023). Oxidative stress can cause elevated ROS levels, disrupting the structure and function of the ER and mitochondria and inducing ERS and mitochondrial dysfunction (Li et al., 2024). Ca2+ signaling has been previously reported to play an important role in the response to ERS (Groenendyk and Michalak, 2023). A previous study showed that RA treatment can increase the steady-state Ca²⁺ content in the ER and that Ca²⁺ signaling participates in RA function by activating the RA response element, thereby promoting cell proliferation (Demczuk et al., 2016). In this study, RA may exert its protective effects on intestinal cells by regulating Ca²⁺ signaling, but further research is needed to clarify this mechanism. Additionally, the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway was also significantly enriched among the DEGs. A previous study showed that NOD-like receptor signaling is involved in innate immunity and regulates inflammatory responses and apoptosis (Kersse et al., 2011; Wen et al., 2013). Various stressors that induce ER stress, leading to increased ROS production, are considered common pathways for activating NLR family members (e.g., NLRP3) (Song et al., 2021; Lu et al., 2024). One study reported that carbachol can increase the expression of the NLRP3 inflammasome (NLRP3, IL-1β, and IL-18) and activate NF-κB, whereas RA treatment can mitigate NF-κB-mediated activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome (Purbasari et al., 2022). These findings suggest that RA may play a mitigating role by modulating the inflammatory response. In addition, the Jak-STAT and Wnt signaling pathways were also significantly enriched among the DEGs. These pathways are involved in biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis and immunomodulation. This result is consistent with those from studies on Clostridium perfringens-induced enteritis in piglets and chickens, in which DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to these immunomodulatory pathways (Truong et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019). As a precursor of flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) coenzymes, riboflavin has been shown to be involved in the body's antioxidant system, maintaining redox balance (Tang et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2020). Riboflavin plays an important role in promoting the formation of disulfide bonds in newly synthesized proteins within the ER and in maintaining the ER redox balance (Tu et al., 2000; Zhang et al., 2022). FAD metabolism in mitochondria is also believed to control ER redox homeostasis (Simmen et al., 2010). Therefore, the DEGs identified via high-throughput sequencing analyses were significantly enriched in calcium and ER-related pathways, suggesting that RA may play a role in ameliorating ER oxidative stress and apoptosis to alleviate intestinal barrier dysfunction.

The intestinal epithelial barrier plays an important role in intestinal health. This barrier consists of complexes of connectors such as TJs, AJs, and desmosomes, the disruption of which is thought to be a key mechanism in the development of intestinal barrier disorders (Lechuga and Ivanov, 2017). KEGG enrichment analyses of the target genes of the DEmiRNAs in this study revealed that they were significantly enriched in AJ and TJ pathways. These findings suggest that RA treatment affects miRNA expression and further posttranscriptional regulation of target genes maintains intestinal barrier function by regulating the integrity of TJs and AJs. Moreover, quantitative analysis revealed that the expression levels of TJ-related genes were elevated after RA treatment.

The RNA-seq results suggested that RA treatment affected calcium signaling pathway and ER-related pathways. We further analyzed the expression of the ERS marker genes CHOP, GRP78, HSPA8, and HSP90B1 and the apoptosis marker genes IP3R, BCL-2, and CASP3 in both groups. The results showed that RA supplementation reduced endoplasmic reticulum stress marker genes and enhanced anti-apoptotic gene expression. HSP90B1 and HSPA8 are key proteins involved in ERS and are central regulators of ER homeostasis and antiapoptotic mechanisms. It has been reported that oxidative stress leads to elevated expression of HSP90B1 and HSP70 (Finka et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2016; Fu et al., 2019). Moreover, studies have demonstrated the role of GRP78 in the unfolded protein response (UPR) and that a significant increase in CHOP occurs during ERS (Liu et al., 2016). ERS is triggered when unfolded proteins accumulate in the lumen of the ER, resulting in chaperone proteins binding to the unfolded proteins and detaching them from the ER membrane, which activates the ER membrane protein GRP78, exacerbating the UPR, which in turn activates the downstream gene CHOP (Ron and Walter, 2007). CHOP is an important signaling molecule in the ERS-related apoptosis pathway, and CHOP can lead to ER peroxidation by inducing the upregulation of endoplasmic reticulum oxidase 1α (ERO1α) (Han et al., 2013). IP3R regulates Ca2+ release from the ER, whereas CHOP-induced ER peroxidation disrupts the interaction between IP3R and endoplasmic reticulum protein 44, which leads to an imbalance in IP3R regulation, an increase in Ca2+ release, and the induction of apoptosis (Li et al., 2009; Zhou et al., 2018). Bcl-2 is an oncogene that significantly inhibits cell apoptosis. Bcl-2 can inhibit the release of cytochrome C, thereby inhibiting the generation of superoxide anions, indirectly achieving antioxidant effects and thereby inhibiting cell apoptosis (Cai and Jones, 1998). It has been reported that oxidative stress can lead to a decrease in Bcl-2, whereas the expression of Bcl-2 is significantly increased after the addition of antioxidants (Zhao et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018), which is consistent with the results of this experiment. Our findings suggest that cage stress triggers oxidative stress in the ER and induces apoptosis in the intestinal epithelial cells of laying ducks, whereas the addition of RA alleviates cage stress-induced intestinal mucosal damage by increasing the antioxidant capacity of the organism and enhancing intestinal barrier integrity.

CONCLUSIONS

Challenges associated with cage stress adversely affect the antioxidant capacity, intestinal permeability and intestinal barrier integrity of laying ducks, leading to damage to the intestinal mucosa. This damage could be reversed by dietary supplementation with RA, with the best results obtained with the addition of 5,000 IU/kg RA. Whole-transcriptome sequencing revealed 706 DEmRNAs, 357 DElncRNAs, 14 DEcircRNAs, and 4 DEmiRNAs in the optimal RA-addition group compared with those in the control group. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the DEGs and DEncRNA target genes revealed that RA addition affects redox, stress response and other biological processes of intestinal mucosal cells and affects the calcium signaling pathway, NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, Jak-STAT signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and other pathways. Thus, the present study investigated the possible biological mechanism by which dietary RA protects against intestinal mucosal injury. However, the limitation of the small sample size of the study indicates that further validation and in-depth research are needed to elucidate the exact molecular mechanisms underlying these biological phenomena.
