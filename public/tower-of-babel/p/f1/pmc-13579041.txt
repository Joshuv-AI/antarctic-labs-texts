Why the current evidence does not support classifying e-cigarettes as likely carcinogenic to humans
Authors: Christopher Junker
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

I read with interest the paper by Stewart et al. [1] and have serious concerns that its conclusions are not supported by the evidence presented. The review collapses evidentiary categories, and conflates exposure, intermediate biological perturbation, and mechanistic plausibility, and on that basis concludes that nicotine e-cigarettes are likely carcinogenic to humans, despite the acknowledged absence of epidemiological evidence in exclusive e-cigarette users. This departs from established carcinogenic risk assessment, in which underlying epidemiological evidence is primary and mechanistic or animal data are supportive.

That conclusion is further weakened by the authors’ stated approach. Their “qualitative risk assessment” ignores quantitative differences, even though dose and exposure conditions are central to any credible assessment of carcinogenic risk. Without addressing this, the review lacks the framework needed to interpret indirect evidence and cannot sustain a product-class conclusion.

The review also describes itself as “objectively definitive,” yet provides no transparent basis for that degree of certainty. It does not explain how the evidence base was delimited, how discordant findings were reconciled, or on what grounds indirect evidence was elevated to a conclusion as strong as “likely carcinogenic to humans.” Reliance on prior reviews does not confer objectivity, because reviews are not independent evidence streams and often reproduce the same underlying studies, limitations, and biases [2]. Restricting the evidence base to reviews published since 2017 is likewise a selection decision lacking a stated justification, not protection against bias.

The cited evidence derived from studies of humans does not support the authors’ broad conclusions. The review relies on case reports, tissue irritation, biomarker abnormalities, and molecular changes, none of which demonstrate that e-cigarette use causes cancer in humans. Cotinine, nitrosamine metabolites, volatile organic compounds, metals, and flavor-related compounds indicate exposure, not malignancy [3]. Oxidative stress, inflammation, methylation changes, altered transcription, and DNA-damage markers may be biologically relevant, but they do not establish cancer causation [4]. Even the cited case reports cannot bear the weight placed on them [5]. The lung cancer report is treated as part of the human evidence base, despite the patient's smoking history and the source's own acknowledgment that causality cannot be established [6].

The treatment of mechanistic and animal evidence is similarly problematic. The review turns the key characteristics of carcinogens into a de facto scoring system and on that basis describes the mechanistic evidence as “unequivocal.” This is not a validated system and cannot transform heterogeneous in vitro studies, animal experiments, and human biomarker findings into evidence of human carcinogenicity. The final conclusion rests heavily on one mouse inhalation study, then increases support with studies of tumor promotion, metastasis, adenoma formation, immunologic disruption, and assorted mechanistic assays. These findings may be hypothesis-generating, but they do not show that nicotine e-cigarettes cause primary oral or lung cancer in humans.

Equally concerning is the decision to exclude comparisons with cigarette smoking. This removes the context most relevant to public health—whether exposure associated with e-cigarettes differs materially from cigarettes. By setting aside comparative studies showing substantial reductions in carcinogen biomarkers among smokers who switch to e-cigarettes, the review treats any detectable biomarker signal as meaningful, even when the magnitude of exposure is lower than that seen in cigarette smokers. In doing so, it disregards the basic toxicological principle that level of exposure determines level of risk.

A further misconception in the review is the treatment of nicotine as though it were a carcinogen. Nicotine is not classified as carcinogenic by IARC or other recognized toxicological authorities [7]. Nevertheless, the manuscript repeatedly implies carcinogenicity and draws on findings from chronic, extreme-dose animal exposure models that are not representative of human e-cigarette use. Results obtained under such non-physiological conditions cannot support reliable inferences about human cancer risk.

Taken together, these flaws leave the review claiming the authority of a carcinogenicity judgment without the evidentiary rigor needed to support it. Independent experts have already cautioned that the article does not meet methodological standards for evidence synthesis and cannot be relied on as a rigorous assessment [8]. I respectfully recommend that the authors reconsider both the weighting of the evidence and the conclusions derived from it.
