Live-Cell SICM Imaging: An Introductory Guide for New Users
Authors: Martina Papa, François Treussart, Jean-Pierre Mothet, Aleix G. Güell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-resolution minimally invasive imaging of live cells is essential for investigating cellular morphology and its dynamic changes. Scanning ion conductance microscopy (SICM) offers a unique combination of precise topographic imaging down to the nanoscale with experimental conditions that preserve realistic live-cell behavior. This practical guide walks readers through critical aspects including instrumentation, probe characterization, cell preparation and morphometric data extraction and presentation, equipping them to implement SICM with confidence and begin exploring live-cell structure and dynamics.

Introduction

In 1989, Paul Hansma et al. introduced Scanning Ion Conductance Microscopy (SICM), a scanning probe microscopy (SPM) technique that utilizes a glass pipette as probe and the ion conductance current driven by a potential bias between two electrodes as feedback signal. It possesses two appealing characteristics particularly well-suited for applications in biological sciences: it operates in liquid environments, and the tip-to-sample distance control is based on the ion current variations, phenomena occurring before any physical contact between probe and sample, hence making it truly contact-free SPM. More than 30 years after its initial development, the technique shows a huge potential in several fields, especially relevant in biology, enabling the study of living cells and other biological structures with minimal perturbation of their native physiological environment, hence attracting strong interest among biologists. These years served also for SICM to evolve technologically and meet some of the specific needs associated with the study of biological systems: faster scans to follow the dynamics of biological processes; − high-resolution imaging for subcellular imaging; − multifunctional probes for local analysis; − and integration with complementary techniques to enable a multimodal microscopy approach.

Even in its basic configuration, SICM has the inherent capability of acquiring topography of surfacesor interfacesat high spatial resolution. When applied to biology, basic SICM is a powerful tool to study live-cell behavior and related phenomena, such as membrane integrity and repair, in near physiological conditions. For example, Ida’s group monitored the dynamic response of cell membrane structure upon external stimuli, triggered by cell-penetrating peptides and epithelial–mesenchymal transition inducers, presenting time-lapse SICM topographic images. Lazenby’s group studied the effects of anticancer drugs on A549 lung cancer cells by tracking morphological changes at the single-cell level, , adapting their time-lapse experiments to the slower dynamics of apoptosis, in experiments that implied continuous imaging of individual cells for a duration of up to 24 h. Their work is an example of the SICM large dynamic range in topographic measurements and its ability to operate at different spatial scales, assessing cell volume changes as well as detecting subcellular features as protruding bulges on the membrane, all with the same setup and the same scanning probe. Indeed, SICM owes part of its outstanding attributes to its particular probe, a high-aspect ratio pipette that permits imaging steep cell edges but also to carry out intracellular experiments with minimal invasiveness. This unique ability is emphasized in the work of Taira et al. where endothelial cells were studied as part of a microvascular model. Upon probe insertion, they acquire topographic images of the cells at the lower part of the microvessel lumen, maintaining both tip and membranes structural integrity.

A number of extended reviews − covers these three decades of research, highlighting applications, revising the evolution of different imaging modes and multifunctionalities, , and providing a broad overview of the current state of the art. Herein, we wanted to approach SICM and its application for live-cell studies from the perspective of a new user: introduce the basics of the instruments main parts and assembly; guide with key considerations for probe characterization; propose model cells and suitable imaging environmental conditions; and unify the measurements of the relevant structural parameters for cell morphology analysis. While not exhaustive due to the extensive literature, already covered in the general reviews, , this document aims to helpand encouragenew users to approach SICM with confidence for the stimulating application of live-cell imaging.

Experimental Setup

SICM research groups are frequently motivated to build their own customized system, with capabilities spanning from basic topographic mapping to advanced time-resolved − , or large scale imaging, yet with the key elements in common: a nanopipette made in-house from glass or quartz capillaries; coarse and fine positioners; a low-noise current amplifier; and electronics for data collection and piezo control (Figure a). In the particular case of live-cell imagingbut not limited to itSICM is frequently mounted on an inverted optical microscope. This is a suitable and polyvalent platform for sample allocation and observation, that can be easily upgraded with adapted accessories (i.e., stage top incubators), and port for coupling with other optical techniques, toward an appealing multimicroscopy approach. ,,

The system should accommodate large samples (i.e., Petri dish) and facilitate the loading of the scanning probe. To this end, coarse positioners with centimeter-scale travel lengths are installed on both the sample stage and the probe standholder. These positioners, which can be operated either manually or remotely via stepper motor actuators, enable sample manipulation and selection of the region of interest and facilitate the subsequent rapid positioning of the scanning probe to the proximities of the sample surface.

For high-resolution probe positioning, SPM systems commonly utilize piezoelectric actuatorselectromechanical devices based on lead zirconate titanate (PZT) ceramicenabling precise control of probe and sample relative position with subnanometer resolution over travel ranges up to several hundred micrometers. Although PZT strictly denotes the ceramic material, this abbreviation is broadly adopted in the SPM community to refer to the actuator device itself, a convention followed hereafter. The mechanical displacement of a PZT, whether expansion or retraction, is achieved by a high-voltage driver that applies a voltage across the piezoelectric terminals. Since PZT behave as a capacitor (at low frequencies), the current to be supplied (or sunk) by the controller to induce PZT displacement at certain speed (i.e., change in accumulated charge, dQ/dt) depends on PZT capacitance following the equation: 1 I=dQdt=C·dVdt where I is the driver current, Q is the charge accumulated on the PZT capacitor, C is the capacitance, and V the voltage across the PZT. High-capacitance PZT, usually the ones that offer long-range displacements (i.e., large piezo tubes or many multiple stacking layers) would require special instrumentation (i.e., high-current high-voltage amplifiers with sufficient slew rate) to operate at high speed (i.e., fast feedback control, fast-speed SICM setups, AC dynamic mode). Furthermore, in addition to the electrical working restrictions, there is also a mechanical operation frequency limit set by the PZT resonant frequency, which depends on geometry (i.e., length), stiffness and, particularly relevant toward experimental setup design, the load applied (i.e., probe holder or sample accessories). Working at frequencies 30% below the resonance frequency will ensure stable positioning and avoid the so-called ringing. Therefore, when selecting PZT, attention must be paid to the values of capacitance (usually in the few μF range) and resonant frequency (commonly between a few hundreds of Hz and a few kHz for commercial sets) as these parameters influence the operation settings and, as a consequence, they impact the design. Indeed, it is common to decouple the z-axis from the xy scanning with the use of two different types of PZT. This separation permits the adaptation and optimization of each element to its main function: for xy displacement it prevails large scanning range (up to 200 μm) and features such as an optical access, over precision or translation speed; for z-axis, low-capacitance high-resonant-frequency PZT are aimed to increase resolution (subnanometric), response time, speed and minimize overshooting. ,,

Cells are challenging samples with high aspect ratio, steep edges and sensitivity to mechanical stress. This topographic complexity is overcome by employing the widely used backstep scanning protocol (approach/retract scanning, ARS) or hopping mode, , with the area of interest being mapped through a series of individual approaches and withdrawals. We illustrate in Figure d this scanning process that begins with the probe vertically approaching the sample. With the ion conductance current continuously monitored, the probe is advanced toward the sample until a drop in current is detected (Figure c, approach curve), indicating the proximity of the surface to the probe aperture. The approach is then stopped when a predesignated current value (setpoint) is reached. Setpoint values are commonly reported as the percentage decrease in the ion current relative to the bulk value (i.e., away from the surface), due to the variability across experiments (i.e. applied bias, electrolyte solution) and probes (i.e., pipette geometry, aperture size). Typical setpoint values range between 0.3% and 1%, ensuring reliable landings (i.e., avoid false engages) with no probe or cell damage.

Upon landing, coordinates (x-, y-, and z-axis piezo extensions) are recorded and the probe withdrawn to the starting position, so the tip is at a safe distance before moving laterally (translation speed less critical to the overall operation). This scanning protocol avoids any potential contactand damagebetween probe and cells when moved laterally, however it increases significantly the time for image acquisition due to longer traveling distances. Fast approaches (and withdrawals) is an appropriate strategy to reduce the time necessary for a completion of a scanning map, minimizing the time spent between pixels. However, the approach cannot be arbitrarily fast, as the feedback mechanism must have sufficient time to detect the decrease on the ion current and stop the approach before tip makes contact with the surface. The reaction time is therefore determined by the sensing distance (i.e., tip-to-surface separation at which the ion current begins to drop measurably, Figure c, which scales with the pipette diameter) and approach translation speed. Thus, when utilizing large aperture probes, the current begins to decay at larger tip-to-sample distances, providing more time to feedback loop to respond; small probes require faster reaction time (i.e., set by amplifier, filters and PZT specifications) to avoid overshooting. ,, Approach translation speed thus must be adapted to probe size. In standard hopping mode scanning approach (and certainly withdrawal) translation rates up to 100 μm/s are reported, resulting in map acquisition times of the order of tens of minutes (depending on number of pixels and approach/retract distances). While this time scale is suitable for many, , efforts to speed up image acquisition have been invested.

In adaptive scanning protocols, − ,,, the strategy is to minimize the overall probe traveling distances, by optimizing the pixel density only on the areas of interest, , and by continuously adjusting the approach/withdrawing distances to surface topography, setting larger withdrawing distances when taller topographic features are encountered. Alternatively, in high-speed SICM, the approach is to operate with probes moving at up to 1200 μm/s. When applied to live-cell imaging, with average cell heights of 20 μm, high-speed SICM imaging is particularly challenging as it requires fast high-resonance-frequency PZT that are, as previously discussed, rare with large translational ranges. Despite the difficulties, time-resolved SICMs are being developed, , involving customized PZT, fast electronics and damping mechanisms to avoid uncontrolled overshooting that could lead to potential mechanical stress to the cell. − ,

A stable, low-noise ion conductance baseline is essential in SICM imaging, as the current changes used to define the setpoint (stop approach) can be as small as 0.3%. Typically, current amplifiers for electrophysiology applications (e.g., Molecular Devices, HEKA) meet the requirements of current range, bandwidth, and low noise, and they are therefore commonly utilized in custom-built SICM systems. In our experimental setup, we have chosen a FEMTO DLPCA-200 current amplifier, commonly employed in photodiodes or photomultipliers and also widely used in SICM setups, for being an affordable alternative with excellent specifications.

Current amplifier specifications also impose operational limits that must be considered to ensure stable and reliable SICM imaging. Given that ion currents during imaging (i.e., probe size, electrolyte, bias) typically range from hundreds of pA to a few nA, a gain of 109 V/A is commonly usedas for the case of the DLPCA-200, where this gain setting corresponds to a bandwidth of 1.1 kHz. The amplifier bandwidth defines the range of signal frequencies the amplifier can reliably detect. Thus, when low-frequency signals are of interest it might be appropriate to add low-pass filters to set new (lower) cutoff frequencies to reduce the electrical noise and improve the signal-to-noise ratio. However, reducing the bandwidth can attenuate high-frequency signal components, potentially slowing the amplifier response time, with the consequent increase of the risk of unintended probe–sample contact. Low-pass filters are sometimes identified by their time constant τ = 1/(2πBW) instead of their bandwidth (BW).

Minimizing sources of external noise, both vibrational and electrical, is the key to ensuring stable and reliable SICM maps. Preferably installed in a dedicated room, the imaging platform should be mounted on vibration isolation tables with active damping systems and away from digital instrumentation such as computers, screens, or major laboratory equipment that is power demanding, such as incubators or hot plates. A large Faraday cage, without unnecessary holes and with covered gaps or apertures, is a common approach to isolating the system from electromagnetic pollution and electric fields. The inner walls could also be covered with sound absorption panels (see Supporting Information, Figure S1). The current amplifier can be placed inside the Faraday cage if its size allows it, in which case it is recommended that it be securely fixed and grounded to the cage. In the case of utilizing other current amplifiers of larger dimensions (e.g., HEKA, Molecular Devices), those are equipped with a headstage that can be introduced in the cage and thus be placed next to the SICM cell. The instrument(s) outside the cage should also be grounded to the same ground point as the cage, however, utilizing different earth cables (common earth or star earthing). ,

Electrical noise is highly dependent on the experimental setup and varies between systems, so mitigation relies on testing and optimizing different configuration rather than a universal solution. For its assessment, we emulated an SICM experiment utilizing a 10 MΩ resistor, yielding an approximately 10 nA current when a constant bias of 0.1 V is applied. The chosen current amplifier (DLPCA-200) enables the application of a constant bias to the electrodes (or resistor) through its internal power supply, with a significant reduction of the noise level. Furthermore, this compact low-noise current amplifier allowed us to evaluate the impact of its location within the setup, and the qualityand lengthof the coaxial cables used (vide infra). As shown in Figure a, in addition to the primary Faraday cage that shields the entire instrument, our optimal configuration involves placing the amplifier next to the measuring cell, and the incorporation of a smaller secondary Faraday cage, covering the positioners, electrodes, cell culture dish, and the amplifier. With this configuration, the minor defects of the primary cage were compensated by the secondary, as well as shielding the potential noise from the inverted microscope.

We also assessed different combinations for the connections between the SICM-cell electrodes and the amplifier (Figure e and Supporting Information, Figure S2). We observed a remarkable improvement when utilizing low-noise coaxial cable (compared to regular coaxial cables) and when minimizing the length of any exposed or unshielded wiring (Supporting Information), using the center conductor for the pipette electrode and the braided shield conductor for the bath electrode.

Noise reduction can be approached at different stages: at the grounding level, at the hardware level (i.e., limiting the bandwidth of the amplifier by introducing low-pass filter), or at the data acquisition level. A moving average applied to the recorded signal (i.e., averaging over a window of N consecutive points) improves signal-to-noise ratio as high-frequency fluctuations are attenuated, hence serving as a low-pass filter. However, its effective cutoff frequency decreases as the averaging window grows (i.e., larger average will filter out fast features of the signal). Indeed, fast acquisition rates or sampling at high frequency is desirable when precise timing of events is requiredsuch as resolving the drop in ion conductance current during approachbut it also collects high-frequency signal that results in more noise. Averagingor filteringis a valid approach for signal clarity, at the expenses of temporal resolution, as previously discussed. In our experimental settings, we usually operate at 100 kHz acquisition rate, with N = 256 moving average.

Although several SPM manufacturers offer commercial instruments with SICM capabilities, demonstrating the growing impact of SICM in key research fields such as biomedicine, materials, and electrochemistry, the commercial availability of key elements and the access to open source LabVIEW-based code for real-time data acquisition and SPM control are strong reasons that motivate building your own SICM. The instrument utilized in this work was operated via the FPGA-based data acquisition system interfaced through LabVIEW, kindly provided by Warwick Electrochemistry and Interfaces Group (WEIG) from The University of Warwick, accessible through a publicly available academic license (https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/sci/chemistry/research/electrochemistry/wec-spm/).

Pipette Fabrication and Characterization

SICM probes are fabricated effortlessly from commercially available capillaries through a controlled-pulling process. Laser pullers provide a high control over the different steps of the pulling process and a remarkable reproducibility through a set of pulling parameters (we refer to the puller instructions manual and Supporting Information, Table S1, for more details on parameters and their function). In conjunction with an extended assortment of capillaries of different materials (i.e., quartz or borosilicate) and wall thicknesses (i.e., inner and outer diameter, ID and OD), it is possible to fabricate nanopipettes with specific shape and tip opening dimensions. The work of Takahashi et al. is an example of combining preheating pulling settings and specific ID/OD ratios to achieve sub-20-nm aperture borosilicate nanopipettes. It is therefore common to find in the literature the pulling settings for particular nanopipette sizes, although those should be considered only as a starting point for fabricating nanopipettes of similar dimensions. Indeed, fine-tuning a laser puller can be a tedious procedure and requires practice, even on the tightness of the capillary pulling clamps. We provide our pulling settings for different capillaries, materials, and dimensions in the Supporting Information, as well as other references with their own settings.

In live-cell SICM imaging, the common pipette size employed ranges between 50 and 100 nm in inner radius. Given the spatial resolution is found to be 3 times the inner opening tip radius r i , , this tip dimension provides the suitable spatial resolution to reveal subcellular features and are still feasible to fabricate and characterize. It is a common situation in SPM techniques to have a large difference in size between probe tipor pipette inner opening sizeand pixel size. Despite the associated error, the use of small tip apexes prevails due to their advantage in providing higher vertical resolution, simplifying subsequent zooming for subcellular features observation and analysis that require higher lateral resolution (vide infra).

Before use, the overall shape of the probe should be examined using an optical microscope, which is convenient for spotting contamination or broken tips. For higher resolution characterization, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is well adapted, with the ability to acquire images from different views (lateral or top, Figure ), which is key to obtaining structural parameters such as pore size or semicone angle (vide infra). A thin metallic coating is sometimes applied to the pipette to reduce sample charging and obtain more defined images, although care must be taken to avoid an excessive thickness that would provide misleading dimensions. The challenge arises for sub-50-nm aperture probes and for determining precisely inner cone angles. Working with SEM at higher acceleration voltages improves the image resolution, however, we observed that this can deform, sometimes done intentionally, , the probe apex, leading to incorrect pipette aperture dimensions (Figure b). Alternatively, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) possesses the high resolution needed, as well as the ability to observe through the thin glass walls of the pipette end and measure the actual inner cone angle. This is, however, a technique that requires a skilled user and adapted or customized sample holders able to host pipettes of just millimeters in length. ,

A good estimation of pipette pore size can be obtained by measuring the resistance induced by the pipette when assembled as part of a conductivity cell. This method is broadly used within SICM community as it is simple, fast, utilizes the same experimental setup as SICM and covers a broad range of pipettes pore sizes, down to 10 nm aperture sizes. It is based on the Nitz et al. model that interprets the SICM setup as a circuit with two resistances in series, Figure b, one associated with the probe geometry (pipette resistance, R p ) and the other the gap between the probe and the surface/interface (access resistance, R a ). Assuming the pipette has a geometry of a simple truncated cone, its resistance is modeled as 2 Rp=1σπritan⁡α comprising geometrical parameters as the inner opening tip radius, r i , and semicone angle α, and experimental conditions as the conductivity of the electrolyte solution of the experiment σ. This characterization is sometimes carried out in the physiological media used for the SICM imaging of live cells, as it allows the probe to be used afterward. Alternatively, we utilized high concentrated electrolyte solutions as it minimizes the rectifying behavior of the response, especially when working with very small and tapered probes. ,

Figure c presents the response of a conductivity cell experiment employing a 55 nm pore nanopipette (dimension from SEM), 1 M KCl as electrolyte solution and chlorinated silver electrodes as quasi-reference counter electrodes (QRCEs). The probe resistance can be obtained either using Ohm’s law for a specific current–voltage (I–V) pair, or extracted from the slope of an V(I) curve when sweeping the potential between, for example, from −500 mV to 500 mV.

The value of the semicone angle plays a key role in the pipette resistance. ,,− However, there is a lack of an unified criterion in the scale of the measurement, from a few hundreds of nanometers from tip end, to microns, up to tens of microns of pipette length. There is an inherent variation of semicone angle along the pipette length, in some cases with variation from 3° to up to 12°, with an impact in SICM imaging, as revealed by finite element method models. ,,, For the calculation of the inner radius utilizing eq , the common approach is to approximate the semicone angle to the most frequently reported value of 3°, ,, unless experimentally obtained, usually by means of SEM. However, as highlighted in Figure a, this measurement might provide different values of semicone angle in function of the length of pipette assessed or considering the inner (or external) walls of the pipette. In the example provided in Figure a, this variation ranges from 4° to 9°. When comparing the calculated pore aperture (using eq ) with the experimentally observed (by SEM) aperture dimensions, we obtain a better agreement when using the semicone angle measured over a length of microns (i.e., α = 4°).

Nanopipettes are backfilled with the electrolyte solution utilizing flexible microsyringes. Pipettes with small pore apertures and long tapers are particularly difficult to fill because of air bubbles being trapped at the shunt. To facilitate the filling and increase success rate, methods to reduce the surface tension, such as prefilling the pipette with ethanol and subsequently exchanging it for the electrolyte solution, have been proposed. Recently, high-throughput thermally driven methods have been described to fill sub-20-nm pipettes, either using a hot plate to create a thermal gradient within the pipette or pulsed microwave radiation to remove trapped bubbles. We found the use of pipettes with an integrated internal glass filament (Figure c) as a simple and effective approach to ensuring a complete filling of the pipettes of 50 nm aperture with a high success rate when backfilling the working electrolyte solution at room temperature.

Silver chlorinated wires are the most common electrodes used in SICM owing to their stable potential, robustness, and simple preparation. They are usually fabricated in-house from silver wire of a diameter that fits in the scanning capillary. The chlorinated layer can be created either by anodizing the silver wire in a KCl-saturated solution, utilizing a power supply or a potentiostat, or through chemical reaction by immersion in hypochlorite solution (bleach) for 10–20 min, or in FeCl3 (50 mM) solution for 1 min. We assessed electrochemical and chemical methods, both of which successfully led to the formation of a chlorinated layer on a previously sand-polished silver wire (the experimental details and stability results are provided in the Supporting Information, Figures S3 and S4). We recommend the chemical preparation method, for the simplicity of the experimental setup (i.e., small beaker) and the robustness of the formed layer that facilitates a posterior thorough cleaning with soft tissues soaked in the working electrolyte, even after one month of its preparation. In comparison, when the chlorinate layer was created via electrochemical means (in a 2-electrode or 3-electrode electrochemical cell), the formed layer was easily detachable when faintly wiped with a soft cleaning paper tissue (i.e., Kimwipes, Kimtech Science). In addition to requiring a more complex experimental setup, the electrochemically grown silver chloride layer exhibited weaker adhesion and could eventually detach or peel off, especially during insertion into the capillary and contact with sharp edges, potentially leading to clogging of the capillary aperture and contamination of the bath solution. In some live-cell experiments, where the presence of silver ions can be toxic for cells, the use of a salt bridge might be considered to prevent any potential leakage of silver ions from the electrode into the bath. It is also common to work with aged chlorinated electrodes to stabilize the potential, although for the experiments presented herein, we worked with electrodes freshly prepared before each experiment for consistency.

Live-Cell Imaging: Substrate, Electrolyte, and Cell Type

In this section, we propose 3 different cell types considering their adhesion, morphology, and robustness, in order to progressively cover the different key aspects during scanning, such as the complexity of cell morphology to be imaged and the specific environmental requirements needed to preserve cell viability.

Cells are typically cultured on glass-bottomed dishes, compatible with optical microscopy to allow simultaneous visualization with SICM. To promote adhesion, glass surfaces are coated with biological molecules, such as poly-l-lysine, collagen, or fibronectin, promoting cell attachment and spreading (Table ). We found the use of commercialized surface-treated 35 mm dishes, with specialized proprietary polymer coating, as a suitable starting point, avoiding frustrating tests of cell adhesion on different substrates. Those substrates are featured with grids which are convenient to facilitate the localization of the cell(s) under observation in case of multimicroscopy experiments or long imaging sessions where tip exchange might be needed. We confirmed by atomic force microscopy that the polymer coating forms a fairly homogeneous layer on the substrate with features below 4 nm, which will have a negligible impact on the overall cellular topography (Supporting Information, Figure S5). For neuronal cells (vide infra), substrates with specialized coatings such aslaminin or Geltrex, which are rich in extracellular matrix proteins, provide cell adhesion and neurite outgrowth, facilitating survival and preserving cells in their native morphology.

Experimental Conditions for Live-Cell SICM Imaging

Cell detachment is a recurring problem that affects the quality of the sample as well as disturbing the ionic current readings, with partial or complete blocking pipette opening. We suggest carrying out cell seeding the day before imaging and adding an extra rising step with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) solution at the moment of replacing the culture medium with the medium for imaging. This step removes cellular debris and floating cells from routine handling and passaging. Note that any unnecessary mechanical disturbances should be avoided, for example, during this medium exchange or mounting the sample on the SICM stage. Moreover, we worked with cell densities ranging between 500 and 10000 cells/cm2, depending on the cell type (details in the Materials Section), which yielded a suitable density to find cells in groups and avoid clumping or aggregates.

The choice of the electrolyte depends on the nature of cells to image and the specific experiment. In Table we listed a few examples of live-cells imaged in SICM, substrates employed and nanopipette size, all grouped by the electrolyte utilized. Cell culture media such Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) and Leibovitz L-15 medium (L-15) are characterized by a complex formulation including a variety of ions, amino acids, vitamins, and sugars. To improve pH stability under ambient CO2 conditions for extended periods, we supplemented DMEM imaging medium with 20 mM HEPES buffer, or alternatively use L-15 as self-buffering medium. Cell media are commonly supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), however its presence in the SICM imaging electrolyte must be avoided, as FBS compromises measurements quality (vide infra). We assessed the ionic current stability of different electrolyte media utilizing probes with an aperture diameter of approximately 50 nm, under conditions suitable for live-cell imaging over at least 1 h. Figure a summarizes these results, with L-15, PBS and DMEM all exhibiting stable steady-state ion current values, and reasonable current drifts of about 2% per hour. We paid special attention to the impact of FBS on current stability, as it may be found as a residue from the cell culture media utilized in previous stages (i.e., DMEM + 10% FBS). As shown in Figure b, ion current transients recorded in DMEM containing FBS revealed that the initially stable current (RMS noise ≈ 0.2%) became abruptly unstable, in this particular case, a sudden drop in current consistent with a partial blockage of the pipette aperture, accompanied by a significant increase in current fluctuations (RMS noise ≈ 3.5%). Complete blocking of the pipette was also observed on several occasions (Supporting Information, Figure S6). Interestingly, this blocking proved reversible: sweeping the potential bias over a few cycles (i.e., from 0.5 V to −0.5 V) was sufficient to unblock the aperture and restore current levels comparable to those prior to the blockage, though repeated blocking events were typically observed thereafter suggesting a persistent interaction between FBS components and the pipette aperture. The complex composition of FBS makes it difficult to identify the precise cause of these fluctuations and blockages (details of the current transients, potential sweeping and following measurements can be found in the Supporting Information, Figure S6). We recommend a thorough medium exchange (at least twice) to be carried out with serum-free media to ensure successful SICM imaging conditions.

Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells are an appropriate starting model as they are widely used in cell biology to explore basic cellular mechanisms like organelles trafficking between the apical and basolateral sides, or the impact of viral infection on cellular functions. As epithelial cells, they are highly adherent and generally remain more stably attached throughout scanning, enabling reliable long-term imaging (Figure a). Additionally, their uniform shape and low mobility-cell dynamics facilitate the acquisition of consecutive images, simplifying the process of understanding and optimizing scanning parameters such as the retracting distance and approach/withdrawal rates, and their impact in acquisition time and resolution. A higher level of complexity in live-cell imaging can be achieved with human podocytes (vide infra, section Image Analysis and Presentation), with larger and more irregularly shaped cells, occasionally with the development of small lateral protrusions, yet with strong surface adhesion. This type of cell, with significant changes in thickness of the cell body is appropriate to adapt different retracting distances based on the area of interest, improving spatial resolution and reducing time-per-frame, as for the existing automatized algorithms for adaptive scanning. − ,,

SICM 2D topographic maps of live cells: a) MDCK cells; b) human neurons derived from iPSCs.

More complex cell systems such as neuronal cell lines (Neuro2a cells, N2a) and neurons derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are the ultimate transition to more delicate and morphologically challenging models due to their highly branched processes, three-dimensional structure, and a high sensitivity to mechanical and environmental stress. Neurons in particular are characterized by a rich arborization and dynamic morphology that respond rapidly to subtle changes in their environment (Figure b). Imaging such cells demanded a higher level of system stability, scanning precision, and, especially, if long-term experiments are planned, constant environmental conditions of temperature, pH, and humidity. Depending on the experimental setup, customized mini-incubators provide an effective approach to control temperature, humidity, and CO2 levels. We made use of a commercial culture-dish microincubator directly assembled onto the microscope stage (see Materials Section for details and Supporting Information, Figure S7, for photographs of the setup) equipped with thermoresistive connectors linked to a temperature controller for precise temperature regulation. Moreover, perfusion inlets were connected to a CO2 and humidity control units. Note that when working with incubators, substantial changes in height due to thermal expansion of the structural elements should be expected during temperature stabilization and therefore taken into account prior to the experiments with live cells.

Image Analysis and Presentation

A semiquantitative analysis of morphological changes is frequently carried out to reinforce observations and trends from topographic maps of live cells. In this section, we revise the key parameters, such as width, height, or volume, among others, utilizing an SICM data set of a live N2a cell obtained at different pixel sizes (resolution) and without interpolation. Our aim in this section is to illustrate measurements, data treatment, and analysis as visually and instructively as possible. Although open-source tools, such as CellMAP, Gwyddion, or Fiji, facilitate this morphological analysis through integrated routines, a hands-on understanding of each step remains essential for interpreting data critically and confidently. In Figure , we exemplify this data modification and analysis, and MATLAB code is provided in the Supporting Information.

Establishing the limit that defines the cell from the substrate is key for the subsequent measurements of height, width, or volume. A minimum height threshold is set, well above the noise level of the SICM image, to differentiate the pixels (or approaches) that belong to the cell from those that landed on the bare substrate, thereby delineating the cell contour. Note that this minimum threshold is applied once any potential substrate slope or offset has been previously removed, so that all of the pixels corresponding to the substrate have a value of zero height (within noise level).This process is flattening or plane fitting.

Raw Landing Points (Figure a)

Raw SICM data consists of individual landing points distributed in 3D space, defined by the piezo axis extensions. The spatial resolution of this sampling is set by two parameters: the hopping distance, Δx, and the raster scan line separation, Δy, which together define the area associated with each measurement point. Each landing point eventually becomes a pixel when the data is treated and plotted as an image.

Plane Fitting (Figure b)

Another presentation of raw data in a 3D space can be obtained by generating rectangular prisms defined by the pixel area and the height value (maximum z-piezo extension of the map − z-piezo extension) of each landing point. This 3D view reveals the inherent sample slope that we emphasized by plotting the pixels corresponding to the bare substrate in grayscale. A height threshold is applied to classify or assign pixels as substrate or cell, thereby defining the cell contour. Note that while the threshold is set based on the topographic variability on the flat areas (outside cell), typically in the range of 100–150 nm (and up to 500 nm in some cases), ,, we use a deliberately large value (1 μm) throughout this tutorial to highlight the effect of this parameter on cell contour definition and make the boundary exclusion effect readily visible in the line profiles. The plane defined by the substrate is subsequently subtracted to the entire map and a new zero height being defined, leading to a new plane-fitted or flattened map (Figure c). ,, Note that the map of the isolated substrate shows no slope.

Volume (Figure c)

SPM techniques are particularly well-suited for high-resolution volumetric measurements, with SICM outperforming AFM for this purpose thanks to its high aspect ratio probe that minimizes tip convolution artifacts on tall and steep edges, and its well-established noncontact imaging feedback that preserves cell integrity. In this case, cell volume, V cell , is defined by the sum of the individual volumes of all the rectangular prisms within the cell boundaries: 3 Vcell=∑i=1n∑j=1mz(xi,yj)ΔxΔy where i and j are the indices identifying each rectangle pixel in the map, n and m are the total number of steps along the x-axis and y-axis, respectively, x i and y j are the coordinates of each measurement point on the cell surface, z(x i , y j ) is the height of the plane-fitted topography map at each raster-scan point, and Δx and Δy are the pixel dimensions along the x-axis and y-axis, respectively (Figure c). For didactic purposes, an offset has been applied to the data-points corresponding to the cell body to visually separate the cell volume from the substrate, facilitating the visualization of both cell volume and cell area. The cell adhesion or contact area is correspondingly defined as the sum of all individual pixel areas within the cell boundary, represented in Figure c as an assembly of black squares.

Height (Figure d)

Cell height in SICM is defined as the 95th percentile of all height values within cell boundaries, minimizing the impact of potential single-pixel spikes or protrusions. Alternatively, in some cases, it is more appropriate to utilize height values from a specific limited area of the cell, in order to best represent the morphological changes of the cell. This is clearly exemplified in the case of red blood cells (RBCs) that can exhibit significant differences in shape, from discoid to spheres and speculated echinocytes. In a study of the spiculation of RBCs, Zhu et al. selected only a few pixels within the center of the cell to obtain the average height.

Width (Figure f)

Live cells can exhibit irregular shapes due to processes or protrusions such as lamellipodia or filopodia, which complicates a unified definition of cell width. Although several methods or definitions are proposed for optical microscopies, such as fitting geometrical shapes to cell outlines (i.e., bounding box, ellipse fitting), we focus on methods employing SICM as an imaging technique. The Baker group, in their study on RBCs, utilized as cell width the average value from 10 line profiles across the cell center. Alternatively, Muhammed et al. demonstrated that utilizing the shortest distance across the cell body, and therefore giving special focus to the cell body, is a valid approach to highlight significant differences in cell width in their study of the effect of cisplatin in A549 cells. Note that the use of width as the full width at half-maximum height (fwhm) is more suitable for subcellular features (vide infra).

As previously mentioned, the threshold or minimum height value defines cell contour and, in consequence, has an impact on the cell projected area, volume and width. To exemplify this, in Figures e and f we present two line profiles in which some pixels at the cell boundary are being excluded from the cell body due to setting a large minimum height value (1 μm). We encourage users to consider alternative criteria to set cells contour or limits, such as the slope value of the cell map.

Slope (Figure g)

Described as how steeply the surface height changes at each point of the topographic map, flat surfaces have a low slope and edges a high slope. It is computed by comparing the height of each pixel with the height of its neighbor pixels in x and y direction. The local slope S corresponds to the magnitude of the surface gradient: 4 S=(∂z∂x)2+(∂z∂y)2

Rheinlaender et al. showed in their multifunctional SICM study of liquid–liquid interfaces how slope map outlines the steep contour of droplets. Applied to cell imaging, slope maps can be utilized to outline cell boundary independently of the absolute height value (Figure g), as well as reveal differences in local contact angle along the cells contour, leading to a potential correlation to cell adhesion or migration, as well as facilitating the detection of subcellular features.

Curvature (Figure h)

Quantifying how much the shape deviates from being flat at a location, local curvature measures changes of the slope across the surface, describing the tendency of a surface to bend inward or outward at a point. Mathematically, mean curvature H is obtained from the second spatial derivatives of the height map in the x and y directions: 5 H=∂2z∂x2[1+(∂z∂y)2]+∂2z∂y2[1+(∂z∂x)2]−2·∂2z∂x∂y·∂z∂x·∂z∂y2[1+(∂z∂x)2+(∂z∂y)2]3/2

With curvature playing a key role in cellular behavior (i.e., adhesion, differentiation), , the measurement of cell mean curvature can be a powerful tool to visualize and quantify dynamic processes that imply changes in cell overall shape, such as apoptosis or RBC spiculation, in addition toor instead ofassessing individual changes in height, width and volume. In Figure h, the mean curvature map at lower resolution shows a fairly homogeneous value of approximately −100 mm–1 (Supporting Information, Figure S8). We also present maps of local slope and mean curvature at higher magnification to highlight subcellular features, potentially revealing the appearance of protruding elements such as blebs or bulbs (Figures g and h).

Subcellular Features

The exceptional noncontact nature of SICM allows the observation of microvilli dynamics, formation of blebs, bulges, budding or ruffles, with minimal perturbation. In these cases, although the lateral resolution of SICM is capable to reveal the formation or movement of these structures, the number of informative pixels is significantly reduced, and thus the morphometrical parameters are measured differently. For example, the width of individual membrane protrusions or buds is measured as the fwhm and the height as the average of heights of the relevant pixels. Certain subcellular changes, such as the formation of microvilli or folds, might be better assessed as a whole rather than at the individual feature level; in such cases, measuring surface roughness  defined as the root mean square of cell height deviations from the mean surface  is appropriate. , An interesting approach by Ida’s group , is the application of two-dimensional Fourier transform (2D-FFT) filter to isolate specific surface structures from the imaging data, facilitating their subsequent analysis (i.e., time-lapse, volume) on isolated images. This 2D-FFT filtering is based on the different spatial frequency signals, from high frequencies for noise and steep edges, to low frequencies for mildly curved structures (i.e., cell body). In their work, they transform SICM images with ruffle-featured cells into separated topography maps containing uniquely the subcellular features, facilitating the visualization of ruffle formation (time-lapse), and simplifying the subsequent data analysis of their overall ruffle volume.

Color

The choice of colormap can strongly influence the interpretation of topographic features. The commonly used jet colormap is not perceptually equal, meaning equal changes in height does not correspond to equal visual changes in color, and the sudden transitions between colors might lead to artificial boundaries. − In Figure we present two SICM topographic maps of a human podocyte utilizing turbo and jet, to emphasize how a perceptually uniform colormap (i.e., turbo), with a smooth, monotonic mapping from height to color, preserves fine topographic details without creating false contrasts, enhancing visibility of gradual slopes, protrusions, and depressions across the full height range. This information is lostor not as evidentwhen jet colormap is used.

Conclusion and Perspectives

With first-time users in mind, we have outlined the key steps for successful SICM imaging of live cells, from ensuring a low-noise stable experimental setup and imaging conditions to proposing examples of cell types for building confidence and expertise progressively. We also examined commonly overlooked experimental issues among which the selection of the optimal imaging medium, illustrated the main morphometric parameters for a subsequent thorough analysis of cell images, and provided relevant literature to support further analysis and deeper insight. We hope to be a practical starting point for those beginning to explore SICM for live-cell applications, aligning with other recent pipette-based SPM tutorials, exemplified by the work of Anderson and Edwards on Scanning Electrochemical Cell Microscopy (SECCM) for (nano)­electrochemistry imaging.

The customization and promising future of SICM are built upon its unpretentious probea pulled capillarywhich confers to this technique its multifunctional potential, unmatched by other SPM techniques that have limited flexibility for tip modification. Indeed, multibarreled probes have been key to SICM’s evolution into a multifunctional SPMs, enabling modes like potentiometric SICM for surface charge imaging, or simultaneous topographic and sensing mapping with dual SICM-SECM probes. While the in situ topographic imaging of live cells is per se a powerful tool, the vast possibilities of nanopipettes in analytical applications, as highlighted in Ando’s group’s review, pave the way to the concept of an SPM lab-on-a-tip, offering virtually unlimited possibilities and applications.

Materials Section

Pipette

A laser puller P-2000 (Sutter Instruments Co., Novato, CA) was used to fabricate nanopipettes from capillaries of diverse suppliers, sizes, and materials (glass and quartz capillaries, with and without filament, suppliers WPI, Harvard Apparatus, Intracel, and Dimmoch & Fock). Further details together with pulling settings are given in the Supporting Information. The pipettes were characterized by SEM (Hitachi S-4800, Japan).

SICM

A nanopipette perpendicular to the surface of interest is mounted on a 3-axis 100 μm piezoelectric actuator (PZT) for precise scanning (P-616.3C, PI, Germany) controlled through the analog port of a digital/analog piezo controller (E-727.3CDA, PI, Germany). For coarse positioning, up to 25 mm, a set of three 1-axis micrometer-driven linear stages (M-112.1DG, PI, Germany) controlled by DC motor controller (C-863, PI, Germany) were used to move the set pipette-holder-PZT in the x, y and z directions. A microscope xy stage for manual operation (M-545-2 ML, PI, Germany) was used to hold and move manually the sample, contained in a 35 mm diameter dish (μ-Dish, Grid-500, IbiTreat 80156, Ibidi). The whole SICM setup was mounted on an inverted microscope (DMi8, Leica, Germany) equipped with a digital camera (MC170 HD, Leica, Germany). Conductivity current was measured with a low-noise amplifier (DLPCA-200, FEMTO, Germany). Two Faraday cages were custom-made, one to enclose the entire instrument (including the inverted microscope) and a second one to shield sample, amplifier and positioners. The entire setup was assembled on a vibration isolation table (Smart Table UT2-48-8, Newport, US) with damping active legs (S-2000A, Newport, US). Data acquisition and control was carried out with a FPGA card (PCIe-7852R, National Instruments, US), with a LabVIEW-based software interface kindly provided by the Warwick Electrochemistry and Interfaces Group, The University of Warwick, UK. MATLAB (R2024a, Mathworks, US) was utilized to analyze data and plot maps without interpolation, except in Figure , where a spikes filter has been applied. For imaging living neurons, a microculture chamber (DH-40iL, Warner Instruments) was utilized, equipped with a temperature controller (TC-344C, Warner Instruments) and perfusion inlets to allow CO2 and humidity control (controller 0506.000, PeCon GmbH).

Culture of MDCK

The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment within a humidified incubator and passaged when they reached confluence. The culture medium was replaced every 48 h.

Conditionally immortalized human podocytes (CiPodocytes), originally obtained from M. Saleem, were kindly provided by the LOB Laboratory. CiPodocytes are cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 5% CO2 and 33 °C.

N2a cells (ATCC-CCL-131) were purchased from LGC Standards (Strasbourg, France) and were cultured in EMEM (ATCC-30-2003) supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment within a humidified incubator and passaged when they reached confluence. The medium was changed twice per week.

Culture of Neurons

Human neurons were derived from a neural progenitor (NPC) wild-type line named WT7889SA, originally obtained from the New York Stem Cell Foundation (ID: NYSCF-CO0002-01-SV-003) by Dr. Marco Antonio Mendoza (Genoscope, Evry, France). NPC cells were cultured and differentiated into neurons on Geltrex (ref. A1413302, ThermoFisher Scientific)-coated 18 mm coverslips in media from ThermoFisher Scientific: Neurobasal A medium (ref. 10378016), supplemented with 1% GlutaMAX (ref. 35050061), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (ref. 10378016), 0.2% β-mercaptoethanol (ref. 31350010), 2% B-27 (ref. 12587010), complemented with StemCell Technologies products: 10 ng/mL GDNF (ref. 78058), 10 ng/mL BDNF (ref. 78005), 100 μM AMPc (ref. 73884) and 1 μL/mL laminin (ref. L2020, SigmaAldrich). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment within a humidified incubator during 14 days (medium added every 3 days) before being imaged.

Sample Preparation for SICM Imaging

For MDCK, and podocytes, cells were seeded onto 35 mm diameter Petri dishes at a density of 10.000 cells/cm2, 1 day before imaging experiments. Similar procedure was followed for seeding N2a cells that were plated at a density of 500 cells/cm2, at least 3 days before the SICM experiment, to allow the observation of single cells with elongated lateral processes. One hour before SICM experiments, cells were thoroughly washed with PBS and immersed in 800 μL of DMEM culture medium supplemented with 20 mM HEPES for pH control. The same solution was used to fill the pipette for imaging. SICM imaging was performed at room temperature. For neurons, samples were transported to the imaging laboratory the day before the measurement. During imaging, neurons were immersed in their culture medium, while the probe channel was filled with PBS solution. Measurements were performed at a temperature of 32 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity.
