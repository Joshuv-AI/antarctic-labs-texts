Effect of liposomal bupivacaine for the suprascapular nerve combined with axillary nerve block on postoperative analgesia in patients undergoing shoulder surgery: a randomized, observer-blinded trial
Authors: Mengting Xu, Huiqing Gao, Renji Zheng, Hong Wu, Kaiming Yuan, Jun Li, Qinsai Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study was conducted to explore whether liposomal bupivacaine for the suprascapular nerve combined with axillary nerve block can provide noninferior postoperative analgesia and reduced hemidiaphragmatic paralysis (HDP) versus traditional interscalene brachial plexus block in patients undergoing arthroscopic shoulder surgery.MethodsPatients were divided into a suprascapular nerve and axillary nerve block group (SSANB group) and an interspinal brachial plexus block group (IBB group) via a random number table. The local anesthetic used was a mixture of 1.33% liposomal bupivacaine and 0.5% levobupivacaine hydrochloride injected in equal volume. The primary outcome measures included resting numerical rating scale (NRS) scores at 72 h after surgery and the incidence of HDP 30 min after block.ResultsFinally, 48 patients were included. The incidence rates of HDP in the SA and IB groups were 4% (1/25) and 78.3% (18/23), respectively, with statistical significance (P < 0.001). The motor block duration in group IB was significantly longer than that in group SA (30.57 ± 17.13 vs. 0.88 ± 4.40 h, P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in the NRS score at rest after surgery, opioid consumption, satisfaction with analgesia, sensory block duration, perioperative adverse effects, or neurological complications between the two groups (all P > 0.05).ConclusionsLiposomal bupivacaine for the suprascapular nerve combined with axillary nerve block can provide long-term postoperative analgesia that is noninferior to traditional interscalene brachial plexus block and results in fewer HDP occurrences in patients undergoing arthroscopic shoulder surgery.Trial registrationThis study was registered at www.chictr.org.cn on June 28, 2024 (registration number: ChiCTR2400086331).Keywords: Liposomal bupivacaine, Arthroscopic shoulder surgery, Suprascapular nerve block, Axillary nerve, Postoperative analgesia, Hemidiaphragmatic paralysis

Introduction

Interscalene brachial plexus block (IBB) remains the most commonly used method for anesthesia and analgesia in shoulder surgeries. However, its primary side effect is ipsilateral phrenic nerve block, which leads to hemidiaphragmatic paralysis (HDP). HDP leads to a reduction in respiratory function, this is easily compensated for by otherwise healthy individuals at rest. The situation is completely different for respiratory-compromised patients. Here, HDP should be strictly avoided.The commonly used drugs bupivacaine or ropivacaine can provide effective analgesia for only 6–12 h, which is insufficient to meet the postoperative analgesic needs for shoulder surgery. With the aid of ultrasound visualization technology, the selective blockade of brachial plexus branch nerves can be achieved. The suprascapular nerve originates from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus formed by the convergence of C5 and C6, providing 60%–70% of the nerve supply to the shoulder joint area. The axillary nerve, a branch of the posterior cord of the brachial plexus, provides 25%–30% of the nerve supply to the shoulder joint area [1]. In 2011, Nam et al. [2] first delineated the anatomical basis of combined suprascapular nerve and axillary nerve block (SSANB). In 2016, Waleed et al. [3] demonstrated for the first time that SSANB provided postoperative analgesic efficacy that was noninferior to that of IBB in arthroscopic shoulder surgery. In 2020, Abdallah et al. [4] demonstrated that suprascapular nerve block using 15 mL of a 0.5% ropivacaine‒adrenaline mixture provided noninferior postoperative analgesia compared with ISB, although diaphragmatic function was not evaluated. A 2021 study by Naraporn et al. [5] further confirmed that phrenic-sparing anterior suprascapular nerve block achieved a 90% maximum effective volume (MEV) of only 4.2 mL.The study by Ferré F et al. [6], utilizing a larger volume (10 ml) for the anterior suprascapular nerve block, was associated with a high hemidiaphragmatic paralysis (HDP) incidence of up to 40%. Data on lower volumes are lacking in the literature. Therefore, this study employed a lower volume (5 ml) to reduce the HDP incidence. Liposomal bupivacaine injection, including IBB was approved for domestic marketing at the end of 2022 and is indicated for regional analgesia in adults. This study hypothesizes that SSANB can provide effective analgesia for shoulder surgery with a lower incidence of HDP compared with that observed with IBB.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University, Wenzhou, China, on May 8, 2024 (approval number: 2024-K-063-02), and was registered at www.chictr.org.cn on June 28, 2024 (registration number: ChiCTR2400086331). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. We followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines.

Sample size calculation

Sample size determination was performed using PASS software (Version 21.0, NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA, ncss.com/software/pass.). This randomized controlled trial sought to evaluate the composite hypothesis that ultrasound-guided anterior suprascapular nerve block results in noninferior postoperative resting pain scores compared with the gold-standard IBB at 72 h postoperatively while concurrently reducing the incidence of HDP. Pain intensity was quantified via the validated 11-point numerical rating scale (NRS), with a prespecified noninferiority margin of 1.0 points established according to recent systematic reviews [7, 8]. In the pilot study, the postoperative 72-hour resting NRS scores for 20 patients (10 per group) undergoing the SSANB and IBB groups were 1.0 (0, 2.0) and 1.0 (1.0, 1.0), respectively. With a significance level of α = 0.05 (one-sided) and a power of 1 – β = 0.8, the calculated sample size was 40 cases. On the basis of preliminary trial data showing HDP incidence rates of 10% in the SSANB group vs. 70% in the IBB group, a sample size calculation was conducted using a one-sided α of 0.025 and 90% power (β = 0.1). Employing Fisher’s exact test via PASS software (Version 21.0, NCSS LLC), the minimum required sample size was determined to be 18 patients per group. To enhance clinical applicability and account for potential 20% attrition, we initially targeted 40 patients and ultimately enrolled 50 participants (25 per group) to ensure statistical robustness.

Study population

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 18–80 years; (2) patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade I–II; and (3) patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair surgery. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) inability to provide informed consent; (2) communication impairment; (3) severe obstructive or restrictive lung disease (per Global initiative for chronic obstructive lung disease (GOLD) [9] criteria); (4) body mass index (BMI) >35 kg/m2; (5) coagulopathy; (6) hepatic or renal failure; (7) pregnancy; (8) history of local anesthetic allergy; (9) chronic opioid use; (10) previous surgery in the ipsilateral neck or supraclavicular region; (11) preexisting sensory/motor deficits in the surgical upper limb; and (12) indications for arthroscopic lavage/debridement only.

Randomization and blinding

Fifty patients who underwent arthroscopic rotator cuff repair at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between October and December 2024 were allocated to either the IBB group or SSANB group via a random number table method.

Preoperative preparation and assessment

All participants preoperatively fasted (solid food: 8 h; clear fluids: 4 h). One hour before anesthesia induction, the participants were transferred to the preoperative holding area. A blinded assessor (unaware of group allocation) evaluated the following baseline parameters: (1) resting NRS score; (2) diaphragmatic excursion (ultrasonographic measurement); (3) arm motor function; (4) peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2); and (5) quality of recovery-15 (QoR-15) questionnaire. The assessor exited the area immediately after completing the evaluations to maintain the integrity of the blinding process throughout the subsequent procedures.

Nerve block

Standard peripheral venous access was established and the patient was monitored via electrocardiogram (ECG), noninvasive blood pressure (NBP), and peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2). The participant was placed in the supine position, with the head and operative side shoulder elevated. Propofol was administered intravenously at 0–0.5 mg/kg and/or midazolam at 0–20 µg/kg, and a Ramsay sedation score of 2–3 was assigned. Routine disinfection and draping were performed. A high-frequency linear array probe and a 5 cm nerve block needle (22G × 50 mm) were used to perform the block via a lateral-to-medial in-plane technique. In both groups, the local anesthetic mixture was an equal volume mixture of 1.33% liposomal bupivacaine injection and 0.5% levobupivacaine hydrochloride. In the IBB group, the anterior and middle scalene muscles and the interscalene groove of the brachial plexus were identified at the level of the cricoid cartilage. After the needle tip position was confirmed with saline, 15 ml of the mixture was slowly injected. In the SSANB group, the ultrasound probe was placed in the sagittal plane on the posterior aspect of the upper arm between the acromion and the axillary fold. The surface of the humeral neck, the posterior circumflex humeral artery, and the accompanying axillary nerve were identified. After confirming the correct position of the needle tip with normal saline, 5 ml of the mixed solution was slowly injected. Then, the ultrasound probe was placed obliquely in the sagittal plane over the supraclavicular fossa, parallel to the clavicle, to visualize the subclavian artery and brachial plexus. The probe was sloped posteriorly and superiorly to identify the suprascapular nerve, as it separates from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus and courses posterolaterally beneath the omohyoid muscle. The needle was advanced from posterior to anterior through the omohyoid muscle until the needle tip was visualized in the fascial plane adjacent to the nerve. The needle tip position was confirmed with saline, and then 5 ml of the mixture was slowly injected. Subsequently, supraclavicular nerve block was performed in both groups. The needle was advanced to the superficial layer of the prevertebral fascia overlying the middle scalene muscle. After confirming the correct needle tip position with normal saline, 5 ml of the mixed solution was slowly injected (Figs. 1 and 2). After nerve block, the puncture site was covered with opaque dressing. All blocks were performed by the same experienced anesthesiologist and nurse, who recorded any procedure-related adverse reactions. At 30 min after the block, assessments of the cutaneous dermatome, diaphragmatic mobility, arm motor function, pulse oximetry, and adverse reactions were performed by assessors who were blinded to the participant enrollment information. Following completion, the participants proceeded to the operating room for general anesthesia.

General anesthesia and intraoperative management

The anesthesiologist was blinded to the participants’ enrollment information. For induction, propofol (1.5–2 mg/kg), remifentanil (2–3 µg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg) were administered intravenously. A disposable double-lumen laryngeal mask or endotracheal tube was inserted. Lung-protective mechanical ventilation was used. For maintenance, sevoflurane inhalation (0.6–1 MAC) and remifentanil target-controlled infusion (1–3 ng/ml) were used, and the blood pressure was maintained within ± 20% of the baseline. Prior to skin incision, 50 mg of flurbiprofen axetil was administered intravenously. Thirty minutes before the end of surgery, 8 mg of ondansetron was administered intravenously for prophylactic antiemesis. At the conclusion of surgery, 2 mg of neostigmine and 1 mg of atropine were administered intravenously to reverse residual neuromuscular blockade. Following patient awakening and laryngeal mask airway (LMA) removal, the patient was transferred to the postanaesthesia care unit (PACU).

Postoperative analgesia and follow-up

In the PACU, the on-duty anesthesiologist assessed the participants’ resting NRS score when they were fully conscious. If the score exceeded 3, sufentanil (5–10 µg) was administered intravenously until the score was ≤ 3 and the Aldrete score was ≥ 9, at which point the participant returned to the ward. If the resting NRS score was > 3 or as needed, patients were instructed to press the intravenous sufentanil patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) pump (5 µg bolus dose with a 10-minute lockout interval and no basal rate) for pain relief. If the NRS score remained consistently > 3 and the PCIA pump failed to provide adequate analgesia, the surgeon administered a rescue dose of flurbiprofen at 50 mg intravenously. Resting and movement NRS scores were assessed in the ward at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h postoperatively by the assessor blinded to group assignment. Movement NRS was defined as the NRS score during shoulder movement. Telephone follow-ups for adverse events were conducted 1 week after surgery.

Diaphragm movement

Before the block and 30 min after, a low-frequency probe and M-mode ultrasound were used to assess diaphragm movement (Fig. 3). Three measurements were taken each time, and the results were averaged. The phrenic nerve block grading criteria were as follows: (1) Complete block: paradoxical movement of the blocked hemidiaphragm during the sniff test or diaphragmatic mobility of the blocked side decreased by ≥ 75% from baseline during deep inspiration. (2) Partial block: Diaphragmatic mobility of the blocked side decreased by 25%–75% from baseline during deep inspiration. (3) No block: Diaphragmatic mobility of the blocked side decreased by ≤ 25% from baseline during deep inspiration. Phrenic nerve block was defined as either complete or partial block, that was defined as diaphragmatic mobility of the blocked side decreased by > 25% from baseline during deep inspiration.

Assessment of sensory and motor blocks

Cutaneous sensation to ice was evaluated in the C4 (shoulder apex), C5 (lateral shoulder), and C6 (thumb) dermatomes. Sensory function was graded via a 4-point numerical scale:

0 = No cold sensation (complete block)

0 = No cold sensation (complete block)

1 = Marked hypoesthesia (cold perception impaired)

1 = Marked hypoesthesia (cold perception impaired)

2 = Mild hypoesthesia (delayed cold detection)

2 = Mild hypoesthesia (delayed cold detection)

3 = Normal cold sensation

3 = Normal cold sensation

A sensory block score of ≤ 2 in the C4–C6 dermatomes was defined as a successful block. Patients were asked to perform shoulder abduction, external rotation, elbow flexion, and supination. Motor function was assessed via a three-point numerical scale (0 = complete loss of strength; 1 = reduced strength; 2 = normal strength). A motor block score of ≤ 1 for muscles innervated by C4–C6 indicated a successful block.

Observation indicators

The primary observation indicators used were the resting NRS score at 72 h postoperation and the incidence of HDP at 30 min postblock. The secondary observation indicators were as follows: (1) NRS scores during the PACU stay and at 12, 24, 36, and 48 h postoperation; (2) opioid consumption and analgesic satisfaction scores (0–10) at 72 h postoperation; (3) SpO2 at 30 min postblock; (4) duration of sensory and motor blocks; (5) perioperative adverse reactions (e.g., vascular injury, Horner’s syndrome, hoarseness, local anesthetic toxicity, pneumothorax, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, itching, and abdominal distension) and neurological complications (persistent numbness, paresthesia, or weakness); and (6) quality of recovery (QoR-15) scores.

Statistical methods

Data analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data with a normal or approximately normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and they were compared between groups via the independent samples t test. Continuous data with skewed distributions are reported as medians (interquartile ranges, IQRs), and they were analyzed via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Categorical data are expressed as n (%), and they were compared between groups via Fisher’s exact test. The median difference in the NRS score at 72 h postoperation was evaluated via the Hodges–Lehmann estimation for noninferiority, with a Wilcoxon rank-sum test indicating the noninferiority P value. A one-sided P value of less than 0.025 was considered to indicate significance for the primary outcome, and a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate significance for the secondary outcome.

Results

In the IBB group, one patient underwent an intraoperative change in surgical method, and another was discharged early at 48 h postoperation and was thus excluded. A total of 48 patients were included in the study. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, BMI, ASA class, preoperative resting NRS score, preoperative movement NRS score, operative duration, preblock SpO2, mean quiet-breathing hemidiaphragmatic excursion, or mean maximum deep inspiration hemidiaphragmatic excursion (all P > 0.05). A significant difference was observed in the distribution of surgical laterality (left side vs. right side) between the two groups (P = 0.020). The detailed results are summarized in Table 1.

Discussion

IBB, which mainly blocks the C5–C7 nerve roots, is used for anesthesia and analgesia in shoulder, upper limb, and hand surgeries. Anatomically, the phrenic nerve is located near the brachial plexus at the level of the cricoid cartilage; thus, it is often blocked simultaneously during IBB. Traditional IBB with a volume of 20 mL or larger easily blocks the ipsilateral phrenic nerve (almost always when nerve stimulation techniques are used); thus, caution is needed in patients with lung diseases or contralateral phrenic nerve paralysis [10, 11]. The suprascapular nerve, a mixed sensory‒motor nerve from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus, innervates the supraspinatus, shoulder, acromioclavicular joints, and infraspinatus. The axillary nerve arises from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus in the subclavian fossa, innervating structures related to the anterior, inferior, lateral, and posterior aspects of the shoulder joint. It also provides motor innervation to the deltoid and teres minor muscles and cutaneous innervation to the shoulder region [2]. There is a lack of literature supporting the use of liposomal bupivacaine for comparing the SSANB and IBB. Previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have utilized the posterior approach to the suprascapular nerve block or did not specify the approach. Compared to the posterior approach, the anterior approach to the suprascapular nerve block offers higher success rates under ultrasound guidance and greater ease of adoption, primarily due to its anatomical landmarks, such as the omohyoid muscle. Furthermore, the study by Ferré F et al. [6], which compared the incidence of HDP between anterior and posterior suprascapular nerve blocks using a higher volume of 10 ml, reported an HDP incidence as high as 40% in the anterior approach group. We hypothesized that a low-volume combined anterior SSANB could precisely block sensory innervation of the shoulder joint while preserving phrenic nerve function. This hypothesis was rigorously tested through a randomized controlled trial, with results demonstrating a HDP incidence of 4%. The supraclavicular nerves originate from the cervical plexus (C3-C4) and primarily provide sensory innervation to the clavicular region, the superior aspect of the shoulder joint capsule, and the skin over the acromion. In shoulder surgery, blocking these nerves effectively covers the nociceptive transmission from the incision site, thereby preventing pain arising from their innervation territory from affecting pain scores and postoperative opioid consumption. This study provides important evidence for optimizing anesthetic strategies in shoulder arthroscopic surgery.

In this study, the number of patients with right-sided injuries was greater than those with left-sided injuries due to the increased likelihood of shoulder sleeve injury in the dominant hand. The results show that SSANB is noninferior to IBB for 72-hour post-op rest/movement NRS scores, with no difference in opioid use or satisfaction. Current evidence suggests that IBB provides superior analgesic efficacy compared with SSB exclusively during the PACU period, whereas the 24-hour postoperative analgesic difference lacks clinical significance [12]. Although suprascapular nerve block has advantages in preventing HDP, its analgesic efficacy is inferior to that of the IBB [13]. Zhao et al. [14] reported that, compared with isolated suprascapular nerve block, SSANB provided superior pain relief and analgesic satisfaction within 24 h postoperatively. In this study, the combined SSANB technique significantly reduced HDP, particularly benefiting patients with impaired respiratory function.Impaired respiratory function was defined by the presence of at least one abnormal objective criterion: (1) Abnormal pulmonary function tests (PFTs): Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) < 80% predicted, Forced vital capacity (FVC) < 80% predicted, or FEV₁/FVC < 0.7; (2) SpO₂ ≤ 94%; (3) Arterial blood gas values: PaO₂ < 80 mmHg or PaCO₂ >45 mmHg; and (4) Dyspnea severity: Modified medical research council (MMRC) grade ≥ 2 (requiring stopping for breath when walking at own pace on level ground). SSANB also reduces opioid-related adverse events, suggesting that it is safer than IBB [12, 15, 16]. Both groups exhibited comparable early postoperative recovery profiles, effectively maintaining patients’ independent ambulation and enhanced comfort levels [3, 4, 12].

Liposomal bupivacaine is a long-acting sustained-release formulation that utilizes DepoFoam technology to encapsulate bupivacaine within multivesicular liposomes, significantly prolonging bupivacaine release duration [17]. This study used the combination of liposomal bupivacaine and levobupivacaine hydrochloride to meet the demand for prolonged postoperative analgesia and rapidly establish a local block for surgery [18]. A controlled trial comparing conventional bupivacaine with dexamethasone versus liposomal bupivacaine alone for IBB demonstrated equivalent analgesic efficacy of liposomal bupivacaine in controlling postoperative pain scores within 72 h following shoulder surgery [19]. Another study showed that continuous IBB infusion via an indwelling catheter can facilitate postoperative analgesia. However, catheter placement may restrict patient mobility, potentially impair respiratory function, and carry risks of catheter dislodgment or infection, posing challenges to postoperative management [20].

This study revealed that the motor block duration in the SSANB group was significantly shorter than that in the IBB group. This difference may be related to the nerve distribution range, the spread of the local anesthetic, and the type of nerve fiber [21]. The suprascapular nerve primarily innervates the glenohumeral joint capsule, supraspinatus, and infraspinatus muscles. Owing to its anatomically restricted distribution, local anesthetic spread is limited. Furthermore, alpha (α) motor fibers within this nerve exhibit reduced sensitivity to local anesthetics, resulting in a shorter block duration [22]. The IBB primarily targets the C5‒C7 nerve roots, with the local anesthetic solution spreading cranially and caudally along the prevertebral fascia to anesthetize multiple muscle groups in the shoulder, upper arm, and forearm. This technique blocks both sensory (Aα fibers) and motor (Aδ and C fibers) nerves. Thinner Aα and C fibers demonstrate greater sensitivity to local anesthetics, resulting in prolonged block duration [23].

This study revealed that accurate SSANB targeting affects only the sensory area of the shoulder joint, significantly reducing the risk of HDP. Thus, this approach represents a new option for postoperative analgesia in shoulder arthroscopy. Furthermore, the SSANB procedure is technically straightforward with ultrasound guidance, enabling precise anatomical targeting, thereby significantly enhancing its clinical utility in perioperative practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations: (1) Diaphragmatic excursion was not reassessed at 24 h postoperatively [24, 25], leading to incomplete evaluation of respiratory depression severity. (2) Only sensory and motor block recovery times were included without assessing the maximum grip strength at various postoperative intervals. (3) Although this study demonstrated the feasibility of liposomal bupivacaine SSANB, this application currently constitutes off-label use.

Conclusions

SSANB offers postoperative analgesia for shoulder arthroscopy patients that is noninferior to traditional IBB, with a lower incidence of HDP. Furthermore, the application of liposomal bupivacaine provides prolonged postoperative analgesia while significantly reducing postoperative opioid consumption and the need for adjunct analgesics.
