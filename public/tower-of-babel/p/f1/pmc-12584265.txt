Background

Scorpion envenomation is a common toxicological issue in tropical and subtropical countries, with children constituting the majority of victims. While localized symptoms such as severe pain and a burning sensation at the sting site are the most frequent manifestations, systemic complications can also occur [1, 2].

In rare instances, patients may present with cerebrovascular events; however, fatal manifestations are usually associated with cardiac complications such as arrhythmias and hypotension [3].

Numerous case reports and studies have documented myocardial dysfunction and hemodynamic disturbances related to scorpion stings, predominantly in endemic regions. Nevertheless, no similar cases have been previously reported in Iran. This report underscores the need for thorough evaluation and documentation of cases involving toxic myocarditis in the context of scorpion envenomation.

This case report has been prepared according to the CARE (CAse REport) guidelines for case reports [4].

Case presentation

A 3.5-year-old girl with no prior medical history was brought to the emergency department of a hospital in Birjand, Iran, at 23:50 by ambulance following a scorpion sting by a Hemiscorpius lepturus scorpion (locally known as “Gadim”). The sting occurred on the dorsal surface of her right upper limb. The patient presented with decreased levels of consciousness, hypotension, and anaphylactic shock. She complained of severe pain at the sting site and had experienced one episode of non-projectile, bilious, and non-bloody vomiting.

At the sting site, which appeared as a visible dot adjacent to a vein, erythema, edema, and mild tenderness were observed. Neurovascular examination of the affected hand was reported as normal. Neurologically, the patient was neither conscious nor oriented, with closed eyes, no response to verbal stimuli, and only localization to painful stimuli. The attending nurse documented an initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 10. Spontaneous and normal breathing was noted, and the pupils were bilaterally equal and reactive to light.

The patient was promptly placed under cardiopulmonary monitoring. Upon admission, her vital signs were as follows: respiratory rate (RR) of 30 breaths/min, heart rate (HR) of 150 beats/min, temperature (T) of 34.7 °C, oxygen saturation (SpO2) of 89%, and blood pressure (BP) of 70/42 mmHg. An emergency medicine specialist evaluated the patient, who was instructed to remain NPO, and an intravenous line was established. At the time of admission, she received two intramuscular doses of epinephrine (0.1 mg/kg, 1:1000), and 5 mg of intravenous chlorpheniramine (equivalent to 0.5 cc from the 10 mg/ml ampoule available in the Iranian pharmacopeia), and three vials of scorpion antivenom, along with intravenous normal saline (approximately 1 L over one hour) as part of fluid resuscitation to correct severe hypotension (BP 70/42 mmHg) and anaphylactic shock. Initial laboratory tests, including CBC, Diff, BUN, Cr, Bs, Na, K, Mg, Troponin I, Creatine Phosphokinase (CPK), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and liver enzymes, were performed and sent for analysis. Based on clinical and paraclinical findings, no evidence of hemolysis or rhabdomyolysis was observed throughout the hospital stay. Chest X-ray (CXR) performed as part of the diagnostic evaluation, showing normal results (Fig. 1).

The chest X-ray appeared normal, which ruled out pulmonary edema or other acute thoracic complications at presentation

After stabilization, the patient was transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) and evaluated by a pediatric specialist. Due to persistent hypotension, an epinephrine drip at a rate of 0.05 µg/kg/min was initiated to manage her blood pressure. To alleviate nausea and vomiting, 2 mg/Kg of intravenous pantoprazole was administered every 12 h. Additionally, the patient received 200 mg of intravenous acetaminophen (Apotel) as needed (PRN) for pain and restlessness. Based on clinical judgment, antibiotic therapy was not indicated during the hospitalization period.

As per the physician’s orders, resuscitation equipment was kept at the patient’s bedside. Vital signs, along with fluid intake and output, were meticulously recorded hourly in the patient’s chart. The patient’s fluid balance remained normal, with urine output ranging from 1 to 4 cc/kg/h during the hospitalization.

Due to persistent hypotension, a cardiology consultation was conducted. On examination, cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were normal. An electrocardiogram (EKG) was performed, revealing sinus tachycardia on the 12-lead ECG and confirmed toxic myocarditis (Fig. 2). Dopamine was added at a dose of 10–15 micrograms per kilogram of body weight per minute. Regular monitoring of fluid balance and blood pressure was maintained throughout.

The 12-lead ECG demonstrated sinus tachycardia without ST and T changes. While this finding alone is nonspecific, in conjunction with elevated cardiac biomarkers and echocardiographic evidence of reduced ejection fraction, it supported the diagnosis of toxic myocarditis

The patient’s echocardiogram showed a reduced ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) of 45% and 22%, respectively, along with mild mitral insufficiency, indicating heart failure secondary to scorpion envenomation. Based on the continued management plan, additional cardiac tests were performed. The results revealed elevated troponin I (2.07 ng/mL; normal < 0.04 ng/mL), CPK (831 U/L; normal 24–170 U/L), D-dimer (2000 ng/mL; normal < 500 ng/mL), and NT-proBNP (8870 pg/mL; normal < 125 pg/mL), which confirmed the diagnosis of toxic myocarditis associated with scorpion envenomation [5].

At presentation, the patient exhibited reduced consciousness, characterized by confusion, lack of response to verbal questions, closed eyes, and only localization to painful stimuli. Although the brain CT scan was normal (Fig. 3), she experienced a generalized tonic-clonic seizure four hours after envenomation, which was successfully controlled with intravenous phenytoin at a loading dose of 15 mg/kg, followed by a maintenance dose of 5 mg/kg every 12 h. Her neurological status gradually improved, and by the time of discharge, she had fully recovered without any residual deficits.

Brain CT imaging was unremarkable, thereby excluding acute intracranial causes for the patient’s seizure and altered consciousness

After receiving supportive treatment for 10 days, the patient was discharged with an improvement in both EF and FS (55% and 27%, respectively). While EF had normalized, FS remained at the lower limit of normal, suggesting only partial recovery of systolic function at the time of discharge, and had achieved full recovery of consciousness with a good general condition. During the one-month follow-up, the patient’s EF (70%) and FS (40%) were within normal ranges, confirming complete recovery of systolic function, with resolution of the previously noted mitral valve insufficiency. Additionally, NT-proBNP levels decreased to 280 pg/mL, with a follow-up scheduled for two weeks later. Anticonvulsant medications were gradually tapered off over a 4-week period. At the six-month follow-up, echocardiographic parameters and laboratory markers remained within normal ranges; however, the patient did not attend her scheduled one-year visit, and long-term outcomes could not be assessed.

Discussion

The Center for Poisoning Control and Drug Monitoring (CAPM) records approximately 30,000 cases of scorpion envenomation annually, 25% of which affect children under the age of 15.2 years [1]. Scorpion envenomation incidents have been documented across various regions of Iran, often presenting with clinical symptoms lacking precise characterization [6].

Scorpion envenomation in Iran is highly significant, with an annual average of 50,000 cases and about 20 deaths, predominantly in rural and semi-tropical areas. Over 80% of these incidents occur in southern Iran [7]. The reported case of a H. lepturus scorpion (locally known as Gadim) sting in Birjand is unusual, as this region has not traditionally been associated with the presence of this species [1]. One possible explanation could be changes in scorpion distribution patterns, influenced by factors such as the transportation of goods across regions or broader climatic shifts [8]. Previous studies from southern Iran have suggested that scorpion sting frequency may be positively associated with higher temperatures and longer sunshine duration [9], whereas relative humidity has shown an inverse relationship [10]. Although no entomological or climatic data are currently available for Birjand, such ecological factors might hypothetically contribute to the presence of H. lepturus outside its typical range. These observations underline the importance of ecological monitoring and healthcare preparedness, particularly in areas not traditionally considered at risk.

If the scorpion is brought along with the patient after being killed, its identification by an entomologist can be beneficial [11]. In the present case, the scorpion species was identified by an entomologist and a relevant specialist based on clinical signs and epidemiological processes. The sting by the H. lepturus scorpion (Gadim) was confirmed.

Scorpion venom can cause a wide spectrum of clinical effects, ranging from localized pain to severe systemic complications and death [12]. The venom’s cardiovascular impacts include coronary artery spasms, platelet aggregation, thrombosis, and reduced Na-K-ATPase activity, along with elevated epinephrine, norepinephrine, and bradykinin levels, leading to anaphylaxis [13, 14]. Symptom severity depends on venom dose, influenced by factors like scorpion size, sting duration, and depth [15]. Common cardiac effects include hypertensive crises (30–77%), myocardial infarctions, myocarditis, heart failure with pulmonary edema (25%), and cardiogenic shock [16]. In this case, the patient presented with vomiting, hypotension, and anaphylactic shock after a H. lepturus sting. Clinical and laboratory findings confirmed toxic myocarditis, marked by elevated Troponin I (2.07 ng/mL, normal < 0.04 ng/mL) and NT-proBNP (8870 pg/mL, normal < 125 pg/mL), along with reduced ejection fraction (EF 45%), indicating severe cardiac damage.

Scorpion venom comprises a complex mixture of mucopolysaccharides, hyaluronidase, phospholipase, acetylcholinesterase, serotonin, histamine, protease inhibitors, histamine releasers, and neurotoxins [17]. Ismail et al. (1995) [18] conducted extensive studies on the venom’s distribution and pharmacokinetics in animals, showing a biphasic pharmacokinetic model. After intravenous injection, venom rapidly redistributes within tissues (alpha phase) over 4–7 min, followed by a terminal elimination half-life (beta phase) of 4.2 to 13.4 h. In this case, the sting occurred near a superficial vein in the upper limb, likely facilitating rapid venom absorption and systemic circulation. This rapid distribution, due to the venom’s complex composition and neurotoxic properties, explains the early onset and severity of toxic myocarditis observed. Clinical findings included significant reductions in EF and FS on echocardiography and elevated cardiac biomarkers, underscoring the critical role of sting location in the progression of severe cardiac complications.

Venom-induced myocarditis appears to result from a multifactorial interplay of direct cardiotoxicity, autonomic dysregulation, and immune-mediated injury. Certain venom components, such as phospholipases A₂, cytotoxins, and cardiotoxins, directly disrupt cardiomyocyte membranes and alter ion channel function, leading to cellular necrosis [19]. In addition, venoms rich in neurotoxins, such as those from scorpions, trigger an excessive catecholamine surge, which promotes myocardial stunning, ischemia, and inflammatory infiltration [20]. Complement activation and cytokine release (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) further amplify myocardial inflammation [21], while metalloproteinases contribute to endothelial damage and microvascular ischemia [22]. Moreover, oxidative stress induced by venom peptides exacerbates apoptosis and immune activation [23]. Collectively, these mechanisms establish a clear pathophysiological link between envenomation and myocarditis, underscoring the importance of early recognition and supportive cardiac management in affected patients.

Patients with severe symptoms should be closely monitored for cardiac effects. Scorpion venom directly impacts the myocardium by reducing Na-K-ATPase activity, leading to toxic myocarditis. It also stimulates the release of adrenaline and noradrenaline from the adrenal glands, ganglia, and neurons, resulting in adrenergic myocarditis. These effects include direct chronotropic and inotropic actions, which increase myocardial oxygen demand, particularly in already vulnerable myocardium [24]. In this case, the venom caused reduced EF, elevated troponin I levels, and worsened adrenergic myocarditis, increasing cardiac workload. Inotropic medications were required to support cardiac function and manage blood pressure. Close monitoring and proper management are crucial to prevent serious cardiac complications. In the present report, cardiac recovery was documented up to six months, during which both echocardiographic findings and laboratory markers remained normal. Nevertheless, long-term risks such as residual diastolic dysfunction or arrhythmias cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies and case reports should aim for extended follow-up to better characterize late cardiac sequelae in pediatric scorpion envenomation.

Neurological manifestations following scorpion envenomation vary widely, with seizures being among the most notable complications. Generalized tonic-clonic seizures and coma have been reported in severe cases, often associated with systemic toxicity and cardiovascular compromise [25–27]. Cytotoxic cerebral edema has also been described in this context [28, 29]. In pediatric populations, the incidence of seizures is higher than in adults, with one retrospective study reporting seizures in 14.7% of children and coma in 11% [30]. This increased susceptibility in children may be attributed to higher serum venom levels relative to body surface area and more intense inflammatory and catecholamine responses [31]. Neurological involvement, manifested as transient altered consciousness and a seizure, was observed in our patient. Although neuroimaging was unremarkable, these symptoms highlight the neurotoxic potential of scorpion venom, likely mediated through metabolic or toxic pathways rather than structural brain injury.

The use of antivenom for scorpion venom is a topic of controversy. Since the risk of death from scorpion venom is low and the risk of allergic reactions from antivenom administration is high, the benefits of using antivenom are unclear. Therefore, supportive care is likely as effective and safer than antivenom use. To be more effective, antivenom should be administered intravenously as soon as possible. The dose of antivenom depends on the severity of the envenomation, the time elapsed since the sting, the progression of symptoms, and the formulation and titer of the antivenom. The neutralizing effect of antivenom is seen only on circulating venom and tissues, not on receptors [32]. In this case, the patient was quickly treated with antivenom, receiving three vials of antivenom and normal saline. This treatment aimed to neutralize the circulating venom and prevent the progression of more severe complications. However, given the risk of allergic reactions and complications from the antivenom, supportive treatments and close monitoring of vital and cardiac signs were also implemented alongside antivenom administration. This combined approach can be more effective and safer, especially in cases with severe complications.

Antivenom use in scorpion envenomation is often debated; however, for H. lepturus in Iran, the calculus differs. This species is associated with cytotoxic and hemolytic effects (with risks of hemolysis, coagulopathy, Acute Kidney Injury (AKI), and cardiovascular toxicity) [33, 34], particularly in children [35]. Current local practice favors early intravenous antivenom in moderate–to–severe envenomation, as its benefit is time-dependent and limited to neutralizing circulating venom [36]; once tissue injury (e.g., hemolysis or myocardial damage) is established, antivenom cannot reverse it, and supportive care becomes decisive [35]. In our patient, antivenom was administered promptly alongside aggressive supportive measures (hemodynamic stabilization and seizure control). While causality cannot be inferred from a single case, the combined approach is consistent with species-specific management in Iran and likely contributed to stabilization. High-quality comparative studies are still needed to precisely quantify antivenom efficacy for H. lepturus, but early administration in severe pediatric cases remains prudent given the toxic profile of this species. Furthermore, the multivalent scorpion antivenom used in Iran is supplied in 5-mL ampoules as a pepsin-digested, refined, and concentrated preparation obtained from equine hyperimmune serum and stored at 2–8 °C. This product is manufactured by the Razi Institute of Vaccine and Serum Production (Karaj, Iran). Its efficacy against the major medically important species, including H. lepturus, has been documented in national studies, and it remains the standard of care in moderate-to-severe cases of envenomation [37].

In this case, several supportive interventions were critical to the patient’s survival. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring allowed for early recognition and management of persistent hypotension. The use of inotropes, including epinephrine and dopamine, was essential for maintaining adequate perfusion in the setting of toxic myocarditis. Furthermore, seizure control with intravenous phenytoin prevented further neurological compromise. These supportive measures, in combination with the timely administration of antivenom, were pivotal in stabilizing the patient and ensuring recovery.

While scorpion venom–induced myocarditis is rare in Iran, similar cardiac complications have been reported in other endemic regions. In Brazil, envenomation by Tityus serrulatus has been frequently associated with myocarditis, pulmonary edema, and arrhythmias [38–40]. Likewise, in India, Mesobuthus tamulus stings are well-documented causes of acute myocardial dysfunction and cardiogenic shock, particularly in children [41–43]. Reports from these countries highlight the global relevance of venom-induced cardiotoxicity, though the species differ in toxin composition and clinical presentation. Compared with these reports, our case involving H. lepturus is unique in that this species is primarily known for hemolytic [44], nephrotoxic [45], and some cytotoxic effects rather than cardiotoxicity. Moreover, in addition to toxic myocarditis, our patient also developed neurological complications (altered consciousness and seizure), which further broadens the recognized clinical spectrum of H. lepturus envenomation. Documenting such combined cardiac and neurological involvement expands current understanding and underscores the importance of vigilance for multi-organ complications, even in regions where these manifestations have not been typically described.

This case is unique because, unlike the majority of previous scorpion sting cases reported from this region, where patients typically presented with a good general condition and responded well to symptomatic management with analgesics, our patient developed severe complications requiring antivenom therapy, along with anaphylactic shock, neurological involvement, and transient cardiac dysfunction. Such severe outcomes have only recently been observed in this area. Furthermore, H. lepturus, the most lethal scorpion species in Iran, is endemic to Khuzestan in the southwest, not to Khorasan (Birjand) in the east [46–48], making the occurrence of this case in northeastern Iran highly unusual. These features highlight both the rarity of such severe presentations and the potential for underreporting or changing epidemiological patterns.

At the end, this case highlights the lethal potential of scorpion envenomation in children and underscores the importance of translational research to bridge current clinical gaps. Ultimately, further investigations with longer follow-up are required to refine evidence-based guidelines for managing this neglected tropical complication and to provide clearer recommendations regarding long-term monitoring strategies, including arrhythmia screening and diastolic function assessment.

Conclusion

This case emphasizes the importance of prompt and comprehensive diagnosis and treatment when dealing with scorpion stings and their severe complications, such as toxic myocarditis, anaphylactic reactions, and seizure. Effective management, including the rapid administration of antivenom, the use of supportive medications, and close monitoring of vital signs, facilitated improvement of cardiac function and overall recovery. Additionally, there is a need to increase awareness and preparedness for handling unusual stings in new areas, particularly in light of climate change. To strengthen future management strategies, extended follow-up beyond the first month is recommended. Such long-term monitoring would allow for early detection of possible late cardiac complications, including rhythm disturbances or residual dysfunction, and help refine clinical guidelines in pediatric scorpion envenomation.
