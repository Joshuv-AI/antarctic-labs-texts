Persistence of Weekly Injectable Semaglutide Use in Patients with Diabetes Mellitus and Obesity: A Retrospective Follow-Up in Colombia
Authors: Manuel Enrique Machado-Duque, Andrés Gaviria-Mendoza, Luis Fernando Valladales-Restrepo, Jorge Enrique Machado-Alba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSemaglutide is an effective antidiabetic agent that is injected weekly to induce weight reduction, and the impact on cardiovascular outcomes is favorable.ObjectiveThe aim was to evaluate the persistence of the use of weekly injectable semaglutide in Colombia for 12 months among new users.MethodsA retrospective longitudinal follow-up study of a cohort of patients with a new prescription of weekly injectable semaglutide between January and December 2022 and persistence for 365 days was evaluated. Kaplan‒Meier survival analyses were performed to evaluate the survival effect of semaglutide.ResultsIn total, 9356 new users of semaglutide were identified. The average age of the users was 61.6 ± 12.9 years, the main diagnoses were type 2 diabetes mellitus (43.8%) and obesity (41.8%), and 90.8% of them received the 0.25/0.5-mg dose. The mean duration of use was 93.7 ± 76.3 days, but only 13.8% and 0.2% of patients continued the treatment at 6 and 12 months of follow-up, respectively. In total, 35.4% had a prescription for a single month. The other concomitant antidiabetic drugs used were metformin (43.5%), sodium glucose-2 cotransporter inhibitors (38.3%) and insulin (18.2%).ConclusionsMost of the patients started semaglutide at the suggested doses to continue with staging; however, the persistence of use was low, which may be related to difficulties in access, education on application methods, drug tolerability, and lack of follow-up by the clinician.

Introduction

Obesity in Western and developing countries has led to a significant increase in the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which affects nearly 370 million people worldwide. The problem is of growing concern in South America and the Caribbean because the prevalence of T2DM among adults has reached 8%, indicating the addition of up to 24 million people to the affected population. As a contributor to this figure, Colombia ranks second in the region after Brazil, and it is estimated that T2DM affects more than 2 million Colombians, although only between 24 and 50% of cases are diagnosed [1–3].

The management of T2DM involves nonpharmacological strategies related to changes in lifestyle, which promote a healthy diet, aerobic exercise, and the avoidance of tobacco consumption [4, 5]. There are also currently multiple antidiabetic drugs, some for oral administration (biguanides, sulfonylureas, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors [iDPP4s], sodium glucose-2 cotransporter inhibitors [iSGLT2s], and glucagon-like peptide-1 [GLP-1] receptor analogs [GLP1-RAs]) and others administered by injection. Among the injectables are other GLP1-RAs (liraglutide, semaglutide, or dulaglutide) and short- and long-acting human or similar insulins [2–6]. In addition to adequate comprehensive management of cardiovascular risk, these drugs mitigate potential disease complications, improve quality of life, and decrease mortality [4–7].

GLP1-RAs have a novel mechanism of action via their effect on the incretin system. GLP1-RAs increase the synthesis and release of glucose-dependent insulin. In addition, they inhibit glucagon secretion and have other systemic effects, such as changes in intestinal transit and gastric emptying, as well as effects on the central nervous system, altering the sensation of satiety and appetite [8]. In the LEADER study, liraglutide proved to be effective in addition to metformin in reducing the probability of major cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with T2DM and high cardiovascular risk or a history of established cardiovascular disease [7]. In 2016, in the SUSTAIN-6 study, semaglutide significantly reduced the incidence of the main outcome, namely, MACE, and this was accompanied by a lower frequency of adverse effects than that in the placebo group but a higher probability of discontinuation of therapy due to gastrointestinal problems [6]. In a later study, the use of semaglutide at high doses in patients with obesity or overweight without T2DM was shown to be highly effective in achieving weight reduction compared with the placebo (−12.7 kg) after a follow-up of 68 weeks [9], although the probability of discontinuation of therapy was higher than that with the placebo due to gastrointestinal events (in up to 50% of users); this led to the current recommendation to start the medication at low weekly doses [9].

In a study by Uzoigwe et al. published in 2021 in the United States, with data from real-world studies, persistence of use by 67% of the participants was observed after 1 year of follow-up [10]. Adherence assessments are important for patients with T2DM since those who use the drug correctly and continuously have better glycemic control, a lower frequency of complications, and less frequent use of health system resources, with fewer acute hyperglycemic events, cardiovascular events, complications involving the kidney, and deaths [11]. Persistence and adherence can be affected by different factors, ranging from the form and ease of use of the drugs to access on a continuous basis and can even be affected by the expectations of the patient and the effectiveness and tolerability of the drug [11, 12].

The Colombian health system offers universal coverage through two affiliation regimes: one contributory or paid by workers and employers and the other subsidized by the state for those without a formal job or without the ability to pay, which covers most of the medical options approved for the treatment of T2DM, which are included in the Health Benefits Plan. Semaglutide is included for the management of patients with T2DM, especially those with high cardiovascular risk; however, it is a high-cost therapy that can create difficulties for the persistence of use, owing to problems related to access or barriers to its continuous delivery (it is covered by the system with a co-payment), as well as owing to a lack of tolerability. Pharmacoepidemiological studies that evaluate the persistence of use are very useful for decision-making in health, so the goal was to evaluate prescription patterns and the persistence of use for 12 months among new patients using weekly injectable semaglutide in Colombia in 2022.

Materials and Methods

This was a retrospective longitudinal study of a cohort of outpatients with weekly injectable semaglutide prescriptions treated in 2022 in Colombia. Data were collected from a population database of drugs dispensed that collects information from approximately 9.2 million people affiliated with the Colombian health system from four health insurance companies, corresponding to approximately 30.0% of the active affiliated population of the contributory (or payment) scheme and 6.0% of the state-subsidized scheme, which comprises 17.0% of the Colombian population.

Patients of any sex, aged greater than or equal to 18 years, were included and registered in a database of drug claims during the previous year, with a new prescription of weekly injectable semaglutide between January 1 and December 31, 2022. The index date was established as the time of the first weekly injectable semaglutide prescription, and a persistence follow-up was started for the next 365 days. The patients must not have received any GLP1-RAs in the previous 6 months. For the definition of persistent use, the continuous claim of the drug was considered as follows: (1) prescription date + 30 days of therapy duration and (2) gap or gap in the prescription of 60 days from the last prescription date (30 days duration + 30 days gap). Follow-up was carried out for the next 12 months until suspension of the therapy by medical order or for not attending to claim the drug, until there was a change in the indicated drug to another antidiabetic agent, or until the end of the follow-up period. Other exclusion criteria were not considered.

On the basis of the information on the drug consumption of the affiliated population systematically obtained by the dispensing company (Audifarma SA) [13], the information was extracted via a query in Oracle SAP. Subsequently, a dataset was created to include patient data across the following variable groups:

Sociodemographic: Age, sex, affiliation regime (contributory, subsidized), and dispensation city, which was categorized according to the regions of Colombia, taking into account the classification of the National Administrative Department of Statistics—DANE of Colombia, as follows: region Bogotá-Cundinamarca, Caribbean region, Central region, Eastern region, Pacific region, and Amazon-Orinoquía region.

Analysis Plan

The dataset built in Microsoft Excel was transformed into SPSS-v29 format (IBM for Windows), wherein the univariate analyses were performed. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions, and continuous variables are presented as the means and standard deviations or as medians and interquartile ranges, depending on whether or not there is parametric behavior evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Between-group comparisons of categorical variables were analyzed by means of the X2 test, and those of continuous variables were analyzed by means of Student's t test if there were parametric characteristics or differences in medians (Wilcoxon test) among nonparametric variables. Differences in characteristics are described between patients using semaglutide with a diagnosis of T2DM or obesity, with the goal of identifying possible differences in the persistence of use among users of semaglutide at a dose of 1 mg and those with a dose of 0.25/0.5 mg weekly. A Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was carried out to analyze the use of semaglutide by new users, and comparisons between those who started the two different treatments were performed by means of a log-rank test from an initial moment (t0) defined on the index date until follow-up censorship (tk) (the analysis was censored during follow-up if the gap in semaglutide dispensing was reached or the 365-day follow-up was completed). P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

The protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Technological University of Pereira in the category of "research without risk," according to Resolution No. 8430 of 1993 of the Ministry of Health of Colombia, which established scientific, technical, and administrative standards for health research. The ethical principles established by the Declaration of Helsinki (Endorsement Code: 25-180324) were respected.

Results

A total of 9356 new users of semaglutide were identified for the year 2022 in the drug claim database, with a mean age of 61.6 ± 12.9 years and a female predominance (63.4%). The Caribbean region had the highest number of new prescriptions (34.2%), followed by the Central region (29.6%) and the Bogotá-Cundinamarca region (17.0%).

The main diagnoses associated with the prescription of semaglutide were T2DM (43.8%), obesity (41.8%), and others not related to the above (14.4%). A total of 8496 patients (90.8%) started with the 0.25/0.5-mg dose, and the remaining 9.2% started with the 1-mg dose. A persistence of joint use was found for both preparations, with a mean of 93.7 ± 76.3 days during the 12 months of follow-up and a persistence ratio of 13.8% at 6 months and 0.2% at 12 months. Interestingly, 35.4% of all patients had a single prescription (only for 1 month), with this proportion being higher among patients with the 1-mg dose (42.2%, n = 363). The mean persistence for semaglutide 0.25/0.5 mg was greater than that for semaglutide 1 mg, which was significant according to the Student’s t test (94.2 ± 76.5 vs 89.0 ± 78.5 days; p < 0.001) (see Table 1). However, when these differences were compared via the Kaplan‒Meier logarithmic rank test, they were not significant (p = 0.127) (see Figs. 1, 2).

Discussion

It was possible to establish the persistence of the use of injectable semaglutide in a large group of patients affiliated with the Colombian social security system, and it was also possible to identify that, in approximately 10% of the cases, it started with high doses that are not recommended. In addition, the average duration of therapy was approximately 3 months, whereas only 0.2% continued receiving therapy without interruption at 12 months. This information can be useful for clinicians, service providers, and those responsible for ensuring that these patients make timely decisions.

The average age of the analyzed population was 61.6 years, with a female predominance, which is consistent with published studies of antidiabetic therapy in Colombia [15]. However, it differs in terms of the management of obesity with orlistat or liraglutide, since a higher proportion of women and a lower average age (47 years) have been reported [16]. The high proportion of patients whose diagnosis of obesity is registered without concomitant T2DM but who are receiving antidiabetic treatment is essentially an indicator of underreporting of the latter pathology, which surely points to the coexistence of the two conditions, added to the high cardiovascular risk, as indicated in current clinical practice guidelines [17]. However, in many patients, obesity is used as the main diagnosis for the justification of the prescription, and obesity was not yet approved in the country as a diagnosis for the management and control of obesity with semaglutide during the study period. However, it was approved for the treatment of obesity in Colombia in November 2024 [18].

Interestingly, the frequency of coprescription of iSGLT2, in more than a third of the cases, may be an indicator of high cardiovascular risk or kidney damage in this group of patients and represents the need for drugs with cardiovascular protection benefits [19, 20]. Notably, fewer than half of the patients received metformin, both in those with a primary diagnosis of T2DM and those with obesity, which may be consistent with current recommendations for first-line antidiabetic therapy in certain groups of patients [17]. The joint use of iDPP-4 with semaglutide, which was identified in 14.1% of the patients, is not recommended, because there may be some level of competition for the GLP-1 receptor, and the resulting metabolic control is not better than that achieved when one of these compounds is used in monotherapy; it does, however, increase the cost of treatment [14]. There is an opportunity to train physicians on the pharmacology of antidiabetics and the selection of appropriate drug combinations.

The finding of greatest interest was the mean persistence of only 93.7 days (approximately 3 months), considering that this is a long-term treatment for glycemic control, the prevention of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients with T2DM, and the effective reduction of weight. This short duration does not allow the achievement of the therapeutic objectives but is surely an economic and healthcare burden. This information should be of special interest to those responsible for the insurance and care of this group of patients and the implementation of multidisciplinary management strategies that require adherence to and persistence of antidiabetic treatment [21]. The persistence of use identified in this analysis contrasts with that reported by Mody et al. in the United States from retrospective information on claims from three IBM Marketscan databases; the report indicated that the average persistence was 220 days, and 42.3% of the patients continued to receive the drug after 1 year of follow-up [22]. It also differs from another real-life study, led by Cárdenas-Salas, that evaluated the switch to weekly subcutaneous semaglutide in patients with T2DM previously treated with another GLP1-RAs, finding solid treatment persistence, with a 12-month persistence of 77.8% and a low frequency (8.4%) of treatment discontinuation, mainly due to gastrointestinal adverse events (5.2%), leading to sustained improvements in glycemic control and weight [23]. A study carried out by Uzoigwe et al. with the Optum's Clinformatics database in the United States reported a persistence of 69.7% at 12 months among more than 3279 patients using semaglutide [10]. Notably, in the study by Mody et al., the allowed gap was 45 days. In the study by Uzoigwe et al., it was up to 60 days, while the analysis carried out in this study was 30 days after the delivery date. To some extent, this may explain the differences in persistence at 1 year, although gaps in the persistence of the continuous use of injectable semaglutide in Colombia are evident. In addition, it is noted that there have been difficulties with the supply of semaglutide, especially since 2024 [24].

Another relevant finding was related to the lower average persistence in users of semaglutide at 1 mg, which was 5.2 days less than that in those who started with 0.25/0.5 mg semaglutide, which was significant. However, there were no differences in persistence at 6 and 12 months. In addition, the group of patients who started the highest dose received a single prescription more frequently (42.2% vs 34.6%), which may be related to a lower gastrointestinal tolerability, which can be improved when doses are titrated appropriately. It is possible that patients who started on the 1-mg dose began with “clicks” (microdosing) [25]. The limited persistence of use can also be associated with access difficulties, delays in the approval of treatment by insurers, or ignorance, fear, or lack of training regarding the correct subcutaneous application of the drug [26, 27].

This study presents some limitations inherent to its observational nature and the origin of the information from a drug dispensing database and the lack of access to the clinical records of the patients to identify the precise indication for use and the possible causes of suspension or lack of adherence to treatment. There are also no data on metabolic control, the severity of T2DM, the body mass index of the patients with obesity, or the estimation of cardiovascular risk, which implies continuing with this line of research to establish other variables of interest. These include effectiveness, safety problems, a lack of persistence or adherence, and even clinical inertia. Furthermore, it is important to recognize that the findings correspond to patients with certain insurance characteristics and therefore cannot be extrapolated to the entire population. Finally, differences in persistence or characteristics of semaglutide users were not adjusted by multivariate analysis. Its strengths include the large number of new semaglutide users included in the analysis, as well as the strict and continuous follow-up of the dispensations in the following 12 months, on the basis of the robustness of the pharmacoepidemiological information source.

Conclusions

With the above findings, it can be concluded that patients who start injectable semaglutide for the first time in Colombia do so mostly at the doses suggested to start therapy and continue with staggering; however, the persistence of use is low, only 13.8% of users continue treatment after 6 months, and less than 1% are still continuing treatment at 1 year of follow-up. This may be related to difficulties with access or education in application techniques, tolerability problems with the drug, and a lack of follow-up, which requires a deeper understanding of the variables associated with the persistence of use of this drug and with adherence to its treatment. Therefore, there is a need to identify possible solutions that guarantee continuous use and the benefits offered by semaglutide treatment.
