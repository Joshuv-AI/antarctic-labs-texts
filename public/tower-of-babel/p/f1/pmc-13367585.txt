Pseudoptosis due to acute dacryoadenitis: An unusual presentation of Epstein–Barr virus infection in a child
Authors: Fahad Alotaibi, Zainah Alshehri, Tahir Hameed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpstein–Barr virus (EBV), a common human herpesvirus, usually causes a self-limited illness in children, manifested by fever, pharyngitis, and lymphadenopathy. However, patients may have atypical presentations including rare ocular manifestations like uveitis, dacryoadenitis, ptosis, or pseudoptosis. In this report, we describe EBV presenting as pseudoptosis in a previously healthy 7-year-old boy. The child was initially worked up as having possible myasthenia gravis with neurologic and ophthalmologic consultations. Ultimately, he was diagnosed with viral dacryoadenitis due to acute EBV infection causing pseudoptosis. We present this case to highlight rare ocular manifestations in EBV which are important to recognize to prevent misdiagnosis and unnecessary investigations.

Introduction

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a double-stranded DNA virus from Herpesviridae family. EBV infection is extremely common, and primary infection typically occurs during childhood. Approximately 90–95% of adults show serologic evidence of prior EBV exposure.[12] EBV infections have various manifestations ranging from asymptomatic infections to precursors to cancers and autoimmune conditions.[3] In adolescents and young adults, primary EBV infection often results in the infectious mononucleosis (IM) clinical syndrome with the classic triad of fever, pharyngitis, and lymphadenopathy. There may also be palatal petechiae, splenomegaly, and a rash.[12] Neurologic complications are generally rare, occurring in 1–5% of patients.[4] Periorbital and/or palpebral edema are thought to be common occurring in approximately one-third of patients, while other ocular manifestations such as conjunctivitis, keratitis, uveitis, and dacryoadenitis are considered rare and based on case reports.[56] Awareness of rare ocular manifestations are crucial to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary investigations. In this case report, we present a rare ocular presentation of an EBV infection in a child.

Case Report

A previously healthy 7-year-old Saudi boy presented to the emergency department (ED) with a history of drooping of both eyelids for 3 days. It was not associated with any visual complaint and slowly progressed over the 3-day period. There was associated fever at home (38°C) that responded to antipyretics, nasal congestion, sore throat, and a small swelling in right side of neck. On initial examination in the ED, the child was well-appearing except for notable drooping of the upper eyelids [Figure 1a]. Initially, he was afebrile and then spiked a fever of 39.3°C. Neurological examination revealed normal eye movements and pupillary reactions, normal power, deep tendon reflexes, and gait. ENT examination revealed enlarged, congested tonsils with follicular exudates. Ear examination was normal. There was a 3 × 3 cm right anterior cervical lymph node, mobile, mildly tender, without overlying skin changes. There was no hepatosplenomegaly. Other systemic examinations were unremarkable.

Both pediatric neurology and ophthalmology were consulted from the ED. Detailed neurologic examination revealed intact visual fields and full extraocular movements without signs of fatigue or diplopia. Other cranial nerve functions were normal, and there were no focal neurological deficits. There was no evidence of muscle fatigability or fatigable weakness. Though no signs of myasthenia were present, still ocular myasthenia gravis was considered. Ophthalmology’s assessment was that the child had puffy upper eyelids with follicular reaction and no significant ptosis. Both services recommended reassessment, and the child was admitted under the care of the general pediatric inpatient service.

During hospitalization, reassessment by ophthalmology revealed bilateral temporal swelling of the upper lids with swelling and redness of the lacrimal glands, and redness of the conjunctiva superiorly. The findings were consistent with dacryoadenitis secondary to a bacterial or viral infection, and he was started on prednisolone eye drops. The droopiness of the eyelids improved over his hospital stay, and he defervesced. He was discharged home on eye drops with follow-up with ophthalmology as well as general pediatrics (for follow-up of his liver function tests). After discharge, EBV serology which was ordered from the ED came back positive with EBV IgM >160 U/mL (positive; consistent with acute infection), EBV IgG 71 U/mL (positive), EBV EA 60.8 U/mL (positive), and EBNA IgG 134 U/mL (positive) [Table 1]. The final diagnosis was bilateral dacryoadenitis causing pseudoptosis of the eyes due to EBV infection. On outpatient follow-up, the child had complete resolution of his symptoms [Figure 1b].

Discussion

While EBV is a common viral infection in children, it is essential to recognize the atypical presentations including ocular signs and symptoms. To the best of our knowledge, we present the first case of EBV causing pseudoptosis due to dacryoadenitis in a Saudi child.

Ptosis in pediatric patients can be due to many causes, including congenital and acquired causes. Pseudoptosis, though resembling ptosis, is related to lack of physical support of the eyelids due to abnormality in orbital tissue. It can be due to many causes including congenital ocular malformations or eye infections.[7] Our patient had drooping of the eyelids due to dacryoadenitis. While eyelid edema has been reported as a common manifestation of EBV infections, acute dacryoadenitis has been rarely reported in children or adults. In a recent review of case reports of EBV-induced dacryoadenitis, 16 cases were reviewed, of which 5 were in children.[8] There have been very few published studies in Saudi Arabia on EBV infections in children, and ocular complications were not described.[9]

Dacryoadenitis refers to acute or chronic inflammation of the lacrimal gland due to an infectious, inflammatory, or idiopathic etiology.[10] Acute dacryoadenitis is considered a rare ocular manifestation of primary EBV infection. The exact incidence in EBV patients is not known but is estimated to be 1 in 300 patients.[6] Acute dacryoadenitis is often observed early in the course of the disease and may precede the typical symptoms of IM. As there are a small number of reported cases of EBV-associated dacryoadenitis, the treatment has not been well established.[11] In most cases, acute dacryoadenitis improves spontaneously, but symptoms may persist for several weeks.[6] While in general viral dacryoadenitis (including EBV-related) should resolve spontaneously, treatment with corticosteroids may reduce swelling of the lacrimal gland. Chronic EBV-related dacryoadenitis resistant to corticosteroids has been reported in an adult patient.[10]

Conclusions

Pseudoptosis due to dacryoadenitis is a rare manifestation of EBV in children. We report the first case of dacryoadenitis due to EBV in a Saudi child. Clinicians should consider EBV in the differential diagnosis of eyelid drooping, especially when accompanied by systemic signs of this infection, to avoid unnecessary investigations.
