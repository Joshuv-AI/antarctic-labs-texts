Identification of a unique tea germplasm exhibiting hyperaccumulation of trans-catechins and its molecular regulatory mechanism
Authors: Yazhen Zhang, Yueqi Wang, Canxiang Li, Xiangrui Kong, Xiaomei You, Zhihui Chen, Xinlei Li, Ruiyang Shan, Shiqin Zheng, Zhenghe Lin, Wenquan Yu, Kang Wei, Changsong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCatechins are the principal bioactive components in tea, and their trans-isomers offer superior benefits such as tea quality, and support human health when compared with their predominant cis-forms. This study aims to identify natural tea germplasm that is rich in trans-catechins and hypothesizes that such a unique catechin composition can broadly reshape the metabolism of other flavor compounds. Herein, a unique germplasm, Y5, was identified and comprehensively profiled. Metabolomic and quantitative analysis revealed that Y5 preferentially hyperaccumulates trans-catechins over cis-catechins, with gallocatechin gallate (GCG) reaching 70.1 ± 0.3 mg/g (over 50-fold higher than controls). Moreover, Y5 exhibited rare co-accumulation of theobromine and caffeine, along with increased levels of specific proanthocyanidins and anthocyanin derivatives. The amino acid profiles changed accordingly as well. Molecular investigation revealed a coordinated regulatory network that redirects biosynthetic flux toward the trans-catechins pathway. The redirection of metabolic flux was largely affected by the late biosynthetic genes and transporters rather than the early biosynthetic genes. Serine carboxypeptidase-like acyltransferases (SCPLs) and multidrug and toxic extrusion transporters (MATEs) were identified as the key genes that facilitate GCG hyperaccumulation. This study provides a valuable resource for catechin research and a foundation for the development of GCG-rich natural tea beverages.Keywords: Tea germplasm, Trans-catechins, GCG, Alkaloids, MATEs, SCPLs

Introduction

Tea, world's second most consumed beverage after water, is valued globally for its distinctive flavor and health-promoting properties. Such attributes are largely determined by the rich and complex profile of secondary metabolites present in tea leaves (Zhang, Cao, et al., 2020). Catechins, a secondary metabolite, stand out as the most characteristic and functionally considerable constituents. Catechins not only contribute to the bitterness and astringency of the tea infusion but also form the chemical basis for its potent antioxidant activity, which in turn mediates various health benefits, such as anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, anticancer, and cardiovascular protective effects (Khaleel et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2025).

Catechins belong to flavan-3-ols, which are characterized by a C6–C3–C6 skeleton comprising two benzene rings (A and B) and a dihydropyran heterocycle (C ring). Catechins are primarily distributed in the tender leaves of tea plants, accounting for 12%–24% of their dry weight and constituting 60%–80% of total polyphenols (Zeng et al., 2021). On the basis of the spatial configuration of the substituents on the C ring, catechins can be classified into cis-catechins (also known as epicatechins) and trans-catechins (nonepicatechins). The former primarily includes epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), epigallocatechin (EGC), epicatechin gallate (ECG), and epicatechin (EC), while the latter comprises catechin (C), gallocatechin (GC), and gallocatechin gallate (GCG).

Previous studies have demonstrated that trans-catechins exhibit superior physiological activities to their cis-isomers owing to their stereochemical configuration. Their spatial arrangement confers a more compact and stable molecular architecture, resulting in enhanced oxidative resistance and sustained bioactive performance (Shi et al., 2016). A relevant example is GCG, which demonstrates stronger antiallergic, antioxidant, and cholesterol-lowering activities than its cis-analog EGCG, although both share an identical molecular formula (Ikeda et al., 2004; Jovanovic et al., 1995). Regarding sensory quality, trans-catechins exhibit a more favorable taste profiles than their cis-counterparts. Under identical concentrations, compounds such as GCG impart less bitterness and astringency (Scharbert & Hofmann, 2005; Xu et al., 2018). Thermal processing facilitates the isomerization of cis- to trans-catechins (notably EGCG to GCG). Such a transformation is a key pathway for astringency reduction and mellower infusions production (Lu et al., 2023; Xue et al., 2023). However, the efficiency of such processing-induced conversion is limited. The identification of natural tea germplasm that is inherently rich in trans-catechins offers a more efficient alternative for quality enhancement.

In commonly cultivated tea plants (Camellia sinensis), cis-catechins predominate as the primary constituents and EGCG is the most abundant catechin, followed by ECG, EGC, and EC. The four cis-catechin monomers account for >95% of the total catechins. However, trans-catechins, especially GCG, are present at extremely low levels (<1 mg/g or undetectable), overall accounting for only ∼2% of total catechins (Jin et al., 2014; Luo et al., 2025). In some wild tea germplasms, such as Camellia ptilophylla (cocoa tea), trans-catechins have been identified as the predominantly accumulated form. Compared with conventional tea cultivars, unfermented green tea or lightly fermented white tea derived from cocoa tea exhibits enhanced antioxidant and free radical–scavenging capacities (Peng et al., 2011). Its water extract effectively inhibits tumor growth; it is a potential functional beverage that can prevent or treat colorectal cancer (Gao et al., 2020). However, owing to reproductive isolation and technical challenges in vegetative propagation, the practical application of such wild tea germplasm remains limited (Yang, Wei, et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2023).

Herein, a unique cultivated tea germplasm, Y5 (C. sinensis var. pabilimba Chang), characterized by exceptionally high trans-catechin contents, was identified. The metabolic profile of Y5 and its associated flavor components is analyzed using metabolomics, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Transcriptomics and gene co-expression network analysis further revealed the molecular regulatory mechanism underlying GCG hyperaccumulation. Ultimately, this study aims to provide superior raw material for enhanced tea beverages and insights into the biosynthetic pathway of trans-catechins.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

While selecting and breeding new tea cultivars, a series of promising new strains with desirable traits—designated as Y1, Y2, Y3, Y4, and Y5—were developed via natural hybridization. Subsequently, the strains were propagated through cuttings and planted in the experimental tea garden of the Tea Research Institute, Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences, in Fu'an (119°34′39′′E, 27°12′57′′N, Fujian, China). Plants were cultivated under uniform environmental conditions and received identical agronomic management. In April 2022, fresh samples (one bud and two leaves) were collected by the same researcher in the evening (3:00–4:00 PM) from a total of 10–12 individual plants per genotype (Fig. 1). For each of the five genotypes (Y1–Y5), three independent biological replicates were generated, with each replicate comprising pooled tissue from 3 to 4 different plants grown under identical conditions. Parts of materials were vacuum-dried using a vacuum dryer (Scientz-100F, Ningbo, China) and ground into powders using a globe mill (Retsch-MM 400, Haan, Germany) for biochemical component analysis. The remaining samples were subjected to liquid nitrogen grinding for RNA extraction.

Series of new tea strains and samples exhibiting one bud and two leaves.

Metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling analysis

The relative metabolome and transcriptome analysis were accomplished by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) described by Hao et al. (2023). Details are as follows:

For metabolomic analysis, 50-mg samples were weighed and dissolved in 1.2 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol. The resulting single extract was then processed through six cycles of vortexing: an initial 30-s vortex followed by five additional 30-s vertexing steps at 30-min intervals. The final supernatant was filtered through 0.22-μm filters (Millipore, Bradford, USA). Each sample was performed for three biological replicates and subjected to ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC, SHIMADZU Nexera X2, Tokyo, Japan) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS, Applied Biosystems 4500 QTRAP, SCIEX, Concord, Canada) for metabolite analysis. A chromatographic column C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) was used. Mobile phase consisted of solvent A (ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The reagents formic acid and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). A gradient elution program was applied as follows: 0–9 min, 5%–95% B; 9–10 min, maintained at 95% B; 10–11.1 min, 95%–5% B; and 11.1–14 min, maintained at 5% B for column re-equilibration. The flow rate was set to 0.35 mL/min, the column temperature was set at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 4 μL. The mass spectrometry data were then collected in positive/negative electrospray ionization (ESI) mode. The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: source temperature 550 °C; ion spray voltage 5500 V (positive ion mode)/−4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I, gas II, curtain gas was set at 345, 414, and 172 kPa, respectively; the collision-activated dissociation was set to high. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 μmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in triple quadrupole (QQQ) and linear ion trap (LIT) modes, respectively. QQQ scans were acquired as multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium. The declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) were optimized for each MRM ion pair. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period. To ensure instrument stability and data reliability, 2-chlorophenylalanine (1 ppm; purchased from J&K Scientific, Beijing, China) was used as an internal standard. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing all of the test sample extracts. Throughout the LC-MS/MS analytical run, QC samples were injected at regular intervals. The total ion chromatograms (TICs) of all QC injections were overlaid. The relative standard deviation (RSD) of all detected metabolites in QC samples was ≤30%, confirming satisfactory instrument stability and data reproducibility.

Metabolite identification was performed using the Metware Database (MWDB), a self-built library of authentic standards analyzed under identical LC-MS/MS conditions. Identification was based on matching accurate mass, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, isotopic distribution of MS/MS fragments, and retention time (RT) against the database, with MS and MS/MS tolerances set at 20 ppm and RT tolerance at 0.2 min. Confidence levels were assigned as follows: Level 1 (MS/MS spectrum + RT match score ≥ 0.7), Level 2 (MS/MS + RT match score 0.5–0.7), and Level 3 (Q1, Q3, RT, DP, and CE matched database entries without MS/MS confirmation). Quantification was performed using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. The dual filtering (precursor ion selection followed by characteristic fragment ion detection) inherently provides high specificity and excellent reproducibility. After data acquisition, peak areas of all metabolites were integrated using MultiQuant software (v3.0.2). For each metabolite, peak integration was corrected across different samples based on RT and peak shape information to ensure accurate comparative analysis. The data were then log2-transformed and mean-centered. To avoid overfitting, a permutation test with 200 iterations was conducted. The orthogonal partial squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using R package MetaboAnalystR, which generated score plots, permutation plots, and variable importance in projection (VIP) values. Differential accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were determined by VIP ≥1 and fold change ≥2 or ≤0.5. The DAMs were then annotated using Kyoto encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Pathways with significantly regulated metabolites mapped to were then fed into metabolite sets enrichment analysis, their significance was determined by hypergeometric test's p-values.

For transcriptomic analysis, RNA extraction was performed using the RNAprep pure Plant Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Each sample was performed for three independent replicates. RNA integrity was checked via agarose gel electrophoresis (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA); RNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Subsequently, extracted and qualified RNA samples were used for library construction and further sequenced on an Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA). The tea cultivar ‘Tieguanyin’ was used as a high-quality reference genome due to its completeness and annotation, despite genetic differences with Y-series strains (Zhang et al., 2021). Gene expression levels were normalized to FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million reads) values for visualization purpose. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 based on raw read counts. The p-values were further adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to derive the false discovery rate (FDR). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened by setting a threshold with |Log2FC| ≥ 1.0 and FDR < 0.05. The resulting DEGs were then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) annotation. Subsequently, hypergeometric tests were applied to identify GO terms that were significantly enriched compared to the whole genomic background.

Based on the specific metabolic and transcriptomic patterns observed in Y5, DAMs and DEGs derived from its pairwise comparisons (Y5 vs. other groups) were then selected for further analysis. A combined enrichment bubble chart (using ggplot 2 3.4.0.) and cluster heatmaps (using ComplexHeatmap 2.12.0) were performed on the online platform Metware Cloud (https://cloud.metware.cn, accessed July 15, 2025).

To verify the reliability of transcriptome data, 12 DEGs with known or putative functions in flavonoid metabolism were subjected to quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis. The selection of these genes was designed to cover a wide expression spectrum, as evidenced by their FPKM values ranging from 0.1 to 1484, thereby testing the sequencing accuracy across both lowly and highly expressed transcripts. The primers were designed on an online software Primer-BLAST +2.13.0 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/) (Table S1). cDNA synthesis and PCR experiments were conducted using the reverse transcription kit (R333) and SYBR qPCR master mix (Q712) purchased from Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The BIO-RAD Cycler CFX96 Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA) was used for qRT-PCR analysis. Each reaction was performed using three independent biological replicates, and each sample was measured in technical duplicate. GAPDH, a commonly used reference gene in tea plants, was employed for normalization in this study (Sun et al., 2010). The relative expression levels were determined using the 2−∆∆Ct method (Livaka & Schmittgen, 2001).

Determination of catechin and alkaloid contents

Catechin and alkaloid contents were measured using the HPLC system according to Zhang, Wang, et al. (2020). A 0.2-g tea sample was extracted using 70% methanol, centrifuged, and filtered through a 0.45-μm filters (Millipore, Bradford, USA) before injection. Component analysis was performed on a HPLC system (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) equipped with a C12 reverse-phase column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm, Phenomenex, Torrance, USA). The mobile phase consisted of 1% formic acid (A), acetonitrile (B), and ultrapure water (D) using the following linear gradient: 0–42 min, 4%–18.7% B; 42–43 min, 18.7%–4% B. The reagents formic acid and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). The ultrapure water was obtained from Milli-Q (Millipore, Bradford, USA). The flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min, and the detection was performed at 280 nm. Each sample was performed for three biological replicates. Seven monomeric catechins and two alkaloids were detected in this study. All of the standards were purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The standard calibration curves are presented in Table S2. The cis-catechin contents were calculated by adding the individual contents of EC, EGC, ECG, and EGCG. The trans-catechin contents were calculated by adding the individual contents of C, GC, and GCG. The alkaloid content was calculated by adding the individual contents of theobromine (TB) and caffeine (CAF).

Determination of proanthocyanidin and anthocyanin contents

Briefly, 50-mg samples were used and dissolved in a 500-μL extraction solvent (50% methanol in water containing 0.1% hydrochloric acid). Methanol was purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Hydrochloric acid was purchased from Xinyang Chemical Reagent (Changsha, China). All of the standards were purchased from isoReag (Shanghai, China). Following vertexing and ultrasonication each for 5 min, supernatant was collected by centrifuging the sample at 12,000 rpm for 3 min. The extraction procedure was repeated once more. The combined extraction solution was filtered through via 0.22-μm filters (Millipore, Bradford, USA) and subjected to LC-MS/MS analysis. Further measurement was accomplished by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., (Wuhan, China). The analytical conditions were as follows, UPLC: column, WatersACQUITY BEH C18 (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm, Waters Corporation, Milford, USA); solvent system, water (0.1% formic acid): methanol (0.1% formic acid); gradient program, 95:5 V/V at 0 min, 50:50 V/V at 6 min, 5:95 V/V at 12 min, hold for 2 min, 95:5 V/V at 14 min; hold for 2 min; flow rate, 0.35 mL/min; temperature, 40 °C; injection volume, 2 μL. The reagents formic acid and acetonitrile were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany).

LIT and QQQ scans were acquired on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform (SCIEX, Concord, Canada) System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: ion source, ESI+; source temperature 550 °C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V; curtain gas (CUR) was set at 35 psi, respectively. Anthocyanins were analyzed using scheduled MRM. Mass spectrometer parameters including the DP and CE for individual MRM transitions were done with further optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period. Quantification of target compounds was performed using a combination of absolute quantification (with authentic standards) and semi-relative quantification (with structural analogs). A series of standard solutions at different concentrations were prepared to generate calibration curves for each compound. Based on the retention time and peak shape information of authentic standards, the detected chromatographic peaks were integrated and corrected across samples. Data acquisitions were performed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (Sciex). Multiquant 3.0.3 software (Sciex) was used to quantify all metabolites. To ensure the reliability of quantification, the following validation parameters were determined for all authentic standards used in this study: recovery rates, limits of detection (LOD), limits of quantification (LOQ), calibration curve equations, and linear ranges. Detailed validation data for each compound are provided in Table S3. Three technical replicates were analyzed for each sample, and the final results are expressed as micrograms per gram of dry weight (μg/g DW).

Determination of amino acid contents

Freeze-dried powder of a 60-mg sample was used and dissolved in a 3-mL extraction solvent (20% ethanol in water containing 0.001-M hydrochloric acid). Ethanol was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Hydrochloric acid was purchased from Xinyang Chemical Reagent (Changsha, China). Subsequently, the sample was sonicated in an ice-water bath for 60 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. Supernatant was collected and filtered via 0.22-μm filters (Millipore, Bradford, USA) and subjected to HPLC–MS/MS (Agilent Technologies, Inc. Santa Clara, USA) analysis. Each sample was performed for three biological replicates.

HPLC analytical conditions were as follows: the chromatographic column used was Information-HILICZ (2.7 μm, 3.0 mm × 100 mm, Bonna-Agela Technologies Inc. Tianjin, China), solvent system comprised 75% acetonitrile (solvent A) and 0.1-mol/L sodium acetate (solvent B). Acetonitrile was purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). Sodium acetate was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). The linear elution gradient was set as 0–5 min (10% –50% A), 5–8 min (50% A), and 8–8.1 min (50%–10%A). Subsequently, 1-μL samples were eluted at 0.3 mL·min−1 at a 35 °C column temperature. The reagents were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany). All of the standards were purchased from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The mass spectrometry parameters were set as follows: ionization mode: electrospray ionization positive ion mode, scan type: multiple reaction monitoring, curtain gas: 103 kPa, spray voltage: +4000 V, nebulizer gas pressure: 448 kPa, auxiliary gas pressure: 483 kPa, and nebulizer temperature: 400 °C.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Expression levels of the tested genes in transcriptomic profiling were used for weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). To enhance the precision of network construction, gene filtering (via varFilter, threshold = 0.3) yielded a set of 21,282 genes, based on which the WGCNA parameters minModuleSize = 1000 and mergeCutHeight = 0.25 were applied to construct a network focused on large, robust co-expression modules. WGCNA was performed using R version 4.2.2 on the online platform Metware Cloud (http://cloud.metware.cn). A characteristic matrix for catechin metabolism was constructed using the abundance data of 15 key flavonoids identified in 2.3, 2.4. These metabolites encompass three major classes: catechins (EC, EGC, ECG, EGCG, C, GC, GCG), proanthocyanidins (B1, B2, B3, B4), and anthocyanins (Delphinidin-3-O-(6″-O-galloyl) glucoside, Cyanidin-3-O-galactoside, Delphinidin-3-O-galactoside, Cyanidin-3-O-(6″-O-coumaryl) galactoside). This matrix was subsequently employed in the gene significance–module membership (GS–MM) analysis. The significance of Pearson correlation coefficients was assessed with p-values adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR method. Gene nodes in the blue module were further filtered by setting a threshold with edge weights >0.3. Subsequently, gene co-expression networks were constructed and visualized on the Cytoscape 3.5.1 software.

Results

Comparative analysis of metabolomic profiling in five strains

To comprehensively understand the changes in the metabolites in new strains of tea plants, broadly targeted metabolomics using UPLC–MS/MS was conducted. Overall, 1147 metabolites that were divided into 11 classifications were detected. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the tested samples were clearly divided into five groups; three biological replicates were clustered closely, proving the credibility of metabolomic profiling. Notably Y5 was considerably distinct from other samples, indicating its particularity in metabolomic profiling (Fig. 2A).

Metabolomic profiling of the five tea strains. (A) Principal component analysis of metabolomic data. (B) Number of differentially accumulated metabolites identified in comparative analyses. (C) Top 20 co-enriched KEGG pathways using Y5 as the control.

To investigate detailed metabolic differences among the five strains, multiple comparative groups were generated via pairwise comparison. The number of DAMs identified in each sample is shown in Fig. 2B. The most DAMs were obtained using the Y5 as a control; notably, 172 DAMs were screened, which were noticeably higher than in other comparative groups. Of these 172 DAMs, 32 were co-upregulated and 115 were co-downregulated in Y5. The results reconfirmed the specificity of the metabolic mode in Y5.

To further clarify the specific metabolisms in Y5, DAMs obtained from each comparison were subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis. The top 20 enriched pathways of DAMs in Y5 are shown in Fig. 2C. Results demonstrated that flavonoid biosynthesis (ko00941) was the most considerably co-enriched pathway in all of the comparative groups. The upstream and downstream pathways were markedly co-enriched, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (ko00940) and flavone/flavonol biosynthesis (ko00944). In addition, some amino acid pathways were also co-enriched, such as phenylalanine (Phe; ko00360), arginine (Arg) and proline (ko00330).

Catechin and alkaloid contents in the five strains

The composition and contents of catechins were measured via HPLC. The catechins chromatograms of the tested samples are shown in Fig. 3A. The peak patterns of Y5 were clearly different from those of other samples, especially for the extremely higher peak of GCG. The sum of three trans-catechins (C, GC and GCG) in Y1–Y4 ranged from 0.6 ± 0.0 to 3.01 ± 0.1 mg/g. The contents of C, GC, and GCG in Y5 increased sharply, reaching 21.6 ± 0.1, 13.22 ± 0.0, and 70.1 ± 0.3 mg/g, respectively. Notably, GCG in Y5 was 50–121 fold higher than that in other samples (Fig. 3B). The contents of EC and EGC in Y5 were 4.24 ± 0.1 and 5.39 ± 0.4 mg/g, respectively, which were observed to markedly reduce at the level of p < 0.001 (Fig. 3C). In addition, the total catechin content was the highest in Y5 and reached statistical significance (Fig. 3D). The component and proportion of catechins dramatically changed in Y5 as well. The percentage of trans-catechins in Y5 reached 54.3% ± 0.1%, which was far higher than that in other samples with 3.0% ± 0.1%–4.3% ± 0.0% (Fig. 3E). In addition, Y5 exhibited the highest levels of TB, CAF, and total alkaloids (Fig. 3F). The CAF content in Y5 was 44.2 ± 0.2 mg/g, which was comparable to Y1. However, the TB content in Y5 was 16.6 ± 0.1 mg/g, considerably higher than that in other samples, which ranged from 1.6 ± 0.0 mg/g to 5.7 ± 0.2 mg/g.

Other related flavonoid contents in the five strains

Proanthocyanidins (PAs), known as condensed tannins, were formed by polymerizing monomeric catechins. The main adjacent subunits in tea plants were B-type PAs, namely B1 (EC-C), B2 (EC-EC), B3 (C—C), and B4 (C-EC) (Yu et al., 2022; Zhuang et al., 2020). The components and contents of the four PAs considerably varied across the five strains (Fig. 4A). Of the four PAs, B4 showed the highest level, ranging from 1536.5 ± 50.2 to 2054.7 ± 47.7 μg/g. In Y5, the contents of B1 and B3, which use trans-catechin (C) as basic extension units, were markedly increased. By contrast, the contents of B2 and B4, which use cis-catechin (EC) as basic extension units, showed a decreasing trend. Such an accumulation pattern of PAs in Y5 was highly consistent with their trans- and cis-catechin profiles. In addition, no notable changes in the total content of PAs were observed between Y5 and the other strains. However, the composition of PAs in Y5 remarkably altered, with an increased ratio of B1 and B3, and a decreased ratio of B2 and B4 (Fig. S1).

Contents of proanthocyanidins (A) and anthocyanins (B) in the five tea strains. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. p-values were obtained using Student's t-test.

Anthocyanins, an essential subclass of flavonoids in tea plants, were quantified and analyzed using targeted metabolomics in this study. Overall, 92 anthocyanins were detected and could be divided into six classes, namely 25 cyanidin, 26 delphinidin, 11 malvidin, 9 pelargonidin, 16 peonidin, and five petunidin (Table S4). From the tested samples with maximum value of ≥5 μg/g, four components were selected; their contents are shown in Fig. 4B. All four components showed the highest level in Y5. The most abundant component was delphinidin-3-O-(6″-O-galloyl) glucoside, followed by cyanidin-3-O-galactoside, delphinidin-3-O-galactoside, and cyanidin-3-O-(6″-O-coumaryl) galactoside. Notably, the content of delphinidin-3-O-(6″-O-galloyl) glucoside in Y1–Y4 was <0.2 μg/g, while its level in Y5 increased to 118.2 ± 2.6 μg/g. The results indicated that the content and composition of anthocyanidins changed dramatically in Y5 as well.

Amino acid contents in the five strains

To assess the changes in amino acid metabolism, the contents of 17 components were measured using the HPLC–MS system. The content of total amino acids (TAAs) in the in tested samples ranged from 19.8 ± 0.2 to 22.8 ± 0.2 mg/g. Theanine (Thea) was the dominant and characteristic amino acid component in tea leaves. TAA and Thea showed the lowest level and markedly decreased in Y5 (Fig. 5A). Contents of the other amino acid components are shown in Fig. 5B and C. Of these components, threonine (Thr), alanine (Ala), valine (Val), leucine (Leu), and Phe showed similar accumulation patterns and considerably decreased in Y5. Notably, Arg content in Y5 was markedly increased. Related metabolic pathway, Arg and proline (ko00330), was also co-enriched through KEGG analysis of DAMs (Fig. 2C). The results revealed that reduced TAA content in Y5 resulted from the declined levels of Thea, Thr, Ala, Val, Leu, and Phe.

Comparative analysis of transcriptomic profiling in five strains

To further reveal the molecular mechanism, transcriptomic analysis was performed. PCA revealed that the three replicates were closely clustered and can be clearly divided into five groups. Y5 was noticeably separate from other samples. Taking each sample as a control, 20 comparative groups were generated via pairwise comparison. The most DEGs were observed in Y5, with 1811 co-upregulated and 687 co-downregulated DEGs (Fig. S2). The results were consistent with the metabolic profiling analysis, strongly validating the specificity of metabolic and transcriptomic modes in Y5. Moreover, to verify the reliability of transcriptomic profiling, 12 candidate DEGs were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. As shown in Fig. S3, the expression patterns of these DEGs between transcriptome sequencing and qRT-PCR analysis were highly consistent. The results indicated that the transcriptomic data were reliable and could be used for further analysis.

To further clarify the functional classification of DEGs in Y5, GO enrichment analysis of DEGs obtained from each comparison was conducted. The top 20 GO terms of gene functions are shown in Fig. 6A. Results indicated that DEGs annotated as UDP-glucosyltransferase (UGT) activity (GO:0035251) were markedly and consistently enriched in the four comparisons. Subsequently, 23 candidate UGTs exhibiting a maximum FPKM ≥20 were screened in the tested samples; their expression heatmap is shown in Fig. 6B. These UGTs were divided into three subgroups, and 11 UGTs showed markedly higher expression levels in Y5.

Two FLS (TGY035030 and TGY081517) and one ANR (TGY074368) showed highly consistent expression patterns and were notably down-regulated in Y5. The results suggested the key roles of the abovementioned genes, namely UGTs, FLS, and ANR, in regulating flavonoid metabolism in the special tea germplasm Y5.

Identification of regulatory networks and hub genes involved in the catechin pathway

To further investigate the dramatic changes in catechin-related compounds present in Y5, WGCNA was performed to identify hub genes associated with their metabolic regulation. Overall, 21,282 genes were divided into 13 co-expression modules labeled using different colors. The module–trait relationships of the group samples were analyzed, and a blue module comprising 2341 genes was identified as the specific module in Y5 (Fig. 7A). These genes showed a significantly positive correlation with those the 15 metabolites, with a correlation coefficient of 0.98⁎⁎ (p < 0.01). GS–MM analysis further strongly confirmed the close connection between the blue module and relative catechin traits. Trans-Catechins, such as C, GC, and GCG, were observed to be significantly positively correlated with the genes in the blue module (Fig. 7B–D). Consistent results were observed in two PAs (B1 and B3) and four anthocyanins (Fig. S4). Furthermore, the correlation network in the blue module was analyzed using Cytoscape software. Hub genes were identified, including MATEs and SCPLs. The findings revealed the importance of the role played by the hub genes in dramatically changing catechin composition, as observed in Y5 (Fig. 7E).

Discussion

Y5 was identified as a unique tea germplasm exhibiting distinct catechin composition

Herein, metabolomic profiling revealed a markedly different accumulation profile of metabolites in Y5. Pairwise comparisons with Y5 and KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that DAMs were markedly co-enriched in flavonoid biosynthesis and its upstream pathway, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 2). Further HPLC analysis revealed the hyperaccumulation of trans-catechins in Y5, while cis-catechins, such as EC and EGC, were at markedly lower levels. Y5 showed distinctive accumulation patterns of catechin compositions, with trans-catechins (C, GC, and GCG) accounting for >50% of the total catechins. The content and proportion of GCG even exceeded those of EGCG (Fig. 3A–E). Moreover, the composition of polymeric catechins, such as PAs, showed consistent accumulated patterns with those of catechin monomers (Fig. 4A and Fig. S1). PAs with higher proportions of cis-catechins, such as B2 and B4, were perceived to be more astringent and bitter (Ma et al., 2014; Yu et al., 2023). Thus, the altered profiles of monomeric or polymeric catechins in Y5 contributed to an enhanced taste of tea infusion.

Similar catechin compositions were observed in several wild tea plants, such as cocoa tea (Jin et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025). A typical biochemical composition of cocoa tea could be characterized by high TB content (20–60 mg/g) and extremely low CAF content (<5 mg/g, or even undetectable) (Chen et al., 2024). However, Y5 contains abundant TB (16.6 ± 0.1 mg/g) and CAF (44.2 ± 0.2 mg/g) (Fig. 3F). Therefore, Y5 was identified as a unique tea germplasm exhibiting dramatically changed catechins and alkaloids, distinct from cultivated tea varieties (C. sinensis) and wild tea germplasms (C. ptilophylla Chang). Whether this coordinated accumulation results from direct pathway crosstalk, shared regulatory factors, or independent metabolic shifts remains to be determined. Elucidating the mechanisms linking catechin and alkaloid metabolism in Y5 warrants further investigation.

Y5 was a natural hybrid offspring of Lechangbaimaocha (C. sinensis var. pabilimba Chang); it showed pronounced advantages in plant morphology and was extensively cultivated through cutting propagation by our institute (Tea Research Institute, Fujian Academy of Agricultural Sciences). Based on its improved flavor metabolic profile, which was characterized by hyperaccumulation of trans-catechins (especially GCG), Y5 is suitable for producing white or green tea with lower bitterness and enhanced bioactivity. This tea beverages hold promise for the prevention or treatment of colorectal cancer (Peng et al., 2011; Xue et al., 2023). It can also be further processed into tea powders or purified extracts for use as functional ingredients in baked goods and dietary supplements (Luo et al., 2024). Taken together, the novel germplasm Y5 represents a valuable resource for the development and utilization of trans-catechins in functional tea products. Further research is needed to address key aspects of its practical application, including processing adaptability, metabolic stability, sensory properties, and bioactivity validation.

Profiles of anthocyanins and amino acids chang in Y5

Anthocyanins and catechins were derived from the flavonoid pathway and shared common precursors (Chen et al., 2023). Thus, the dramatic changes observed in catechin compositions were expected to markedly impact anthocyanin profiles. This was corroborated by our data, which showed a sharp increase in the accumulations of glycosylated anthocyanins in Y5 (Fig. 4B and Table S4). Further analysis of transcriptomic profiling helped identifying key regulatory genes (UGTs, FLS and ANR) at crucial branch points of flavonoid biosynthesis (Fig. 6). The upregulation of UGTs, which catalyzed the glycosylation of anthocyanins for vacuolar sequestration (Kumari et al., 2020), provided a direct mechanistic basis for the increased levels of glycosylated anthocyanins. Concomitantly, the coordinated downregulation of the two competing enzymes was observed: FLS, which channeled metabolic flux toward flavonol synthesis (Zhao et al., 2025) and ANR, which redirected shared anthocyanidin precursors into the cis-catechin branch (Xu et al., 2024). Such a coordinated transcriptional shift attenuated both the alternative pathways, thereby enhancing anthocyanin accumulation and reducing flavonol and cis-catechin biosynthesis in Y5.

The amino acid profile of Y5 displayed a decline in the abundance of several components (Fig. 5). This metabolic shift could be attributed to the similar metabolic crosstalk between amino acid and catechins biosynthesis, which was well documented in tea plants (Tai et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2020). Phe was a crucial metabolic node that connected the two pathways (Aksenova et al., 2024). Increased accumulation of total catechins coincided with decreased Phe levels in Y5 (Fig. 3 and Fig. 5C). The inverse correlation suggested that enhanced catechin biosynthesis competed for the Phe pool, leading to the observed reduction in amino acids.

MATEs and SCPLs as crucial contributors to the hyperaccumulation of trans-catechins in Y5

At present, studies on cultivated tea plants focus on cis-catechins EGCG and its biosynthesis is extensively elucidated, while the synthesis of its trans-isomer (GCG) remains poorly understood. MATEs and SCPLs were identified as the hub genes and were observed to be closely linked with the hyperaccumulation of trans-catechins in Y5 through WGCNA (Fig. 7). Recently, the key enzymes responsible for galloylated cis-catechins were identified, with CsSCPL4 acting as a catalytic acyltransferase and CsSCPL5 as a noncatalytic companion paralog. The co-expression of CsSCPL4 and CsSCPL5 necessarily led to the formation of ECG and EGCG when using EC and EGC as substrates, respectively. (Chen et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2022). Herein, the two genes that were homologous with CsSCPL4 and CsSCPL5 were identified; they exhibited strong positive correlation with the hyperaccumulation of the related derivatives of trans-catechins (Fig. 7). The correlation suggested that CsSCPL4 and CsSCPL5 could take C and GC as substrates to participate in the biosynthesis of GCG in Y5.

Beyond synthesis, the accumulation of secondary metabolites was governed by transport processes, which played a key role in their compartmentalization and storage (Zhao et al., 2020). CsMATE1 and CsMATE2 in Y5 showed sequence homology with CsTT12 (transparent testa 12), which functioned as cis-catechin transporter in tea plants (Wang et al., 2024). Numerous studies have demonstrated that TT12 homologous proteins in other plants exhibited similar transport activities (Chen et al., 2018; Debeaujon et al., 2001; Gao et al., 2016; Pal et al., 2023). In addition, TT12 orthologs were functionally diversified across species. In Medicago truncatula, MtMATE1 exhibited higher substrate affinity for cis-catechin derivatives, while MtMATE2 preferentially bound with and transported anthocyanins (Zhao & Dixon, 2009; Zhao et al., 2011). In Diospyros kaki, DkMATE1 functioned as a cis-catechin derivative transporter and was involved in PA accumulation (Yang, Jiang, et al., 2016), while DkMATE7 exhibited dual functions in transporting cis- and trans-catechins and displayed substrate preferences (Wu, 2021). The findings suggested that the substrate affinity of CsMATEs in Y5 extended to trans-catechins and related derivatives, highlighting the functional role of such transporters in facilitating GCG accumulation. The functions of the key candidate genes CsSCPLs and CsMATEs require further experimental validation through heterologous overexpression, gene silencing, and enzyme activity assays. Moreover, whether CsSCPLs and CsMATEs physically interact to coordinate GCG accumulation remains to be determined. Protein-protein interaction assays (e.g., yeast two-hybrid, co-immunoprecipitation, bimolecular fluorescence complementation) would help elucidate their potential cooperation in trans-catechin biosynthesis and sequestration.

Conclusion

In summary, a novel germplasm Y5, classified as cultivated tea plants (C. sinensis var. pabilimba Chang), exhibiting distinctive chemical compositions, was identified. Y5 preferentially accumulated ultra-high levels of trans-catechins (C, GC, and GCG) rather than cis-catechins. Moreover, Y5 contained abundant TB and CAF. The metabolic profiles of other flavor compounds were considerably altered, namely PAs, anthocyanins, and amino acids. The molecular regulatory mechanism of catechins and their derivatives in Y5 is shown in Fig. 8, which illustrates that the hyperaccumulation of trans-catechins is predominantly governed by downstream genes involved in their synthesis and transport. SCPLs and MATEs could jointly facilitate the accumulation of trans-catechins through coordinated regulation of their biosynthesis and transport. Taken together, a novel phytochemical foundation is established in this work through the identification of a unique tea germplasm Y5, which serves as both a functional model for studying catechin metabolism and a direct source for creating natural tea beverages with enhanced GCG content.
