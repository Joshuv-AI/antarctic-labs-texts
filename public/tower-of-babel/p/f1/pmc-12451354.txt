National trends in mortality associated with obstructive sleep apnea among adults with diabetes mellitus in the United States, 1999–2020
Authors: Ibrahim Nagmeldin Hassan, Siddig Yaqub, Muhsin Ibrahim, Nagmeldin Abuassa, Mohamed Ibrahim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and diabetes mellitus are two prevalent and interrelated chronic conditions that contribute significantly to cardiovascular and metabolic mortality. However, national trends in OSA-related mortality among adults with diabetes remain poorly characterized.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of U.S. death certificate data from 1999 to 2020 using the CDC WONDER platform. Adults aged ≥25 years with diabetes mellitus (ICD-10: E10–E14) as the underlying cause of death and OSA (G47.33) as a contributing cause were included. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated per 100,000 population, and temporal trends were assessed using Joinpoint regression.ResultsA total of 79,728 deaths were attributed to diabetes and OSA. The overall AAMR was 1.55 (95 % CI: 1.50 to 1.60), increasing significantly from 1999 to 2004 (APC: 19.42 %) and more gradually from 2005 to 2020 (APC: 6.86 %). Males had nearly double the mortality rate of females (2.13 vs. 1.07). The highest AAMRs were observed among American Indian or Alaska Native (2.16) and Black (1.98) individuals. Non-metropolitan areas had higher mortality than metropolitan areas (1.91 vs. 1.48), and the Midwest had the highest regional AAMR (1.95). Crude mortality increased with age, peaking at 5.42 in the 75–84 age group. State-level AAMRs varied nearly sixfold. Most deaths occurred at home (38.5 %), followed by inpatient facilities (32.8 %).ConclusionsMortality related to OSA in adults with diabetes has risen substantially across all demographics, underscoring the need for targeted interventions in high-risk groups.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) are two common and increasingly prevalent chronic conditions with major implications for global public health. Diabetes affects more than 460 million individuals worldwide and is projected to reach 700 million by 2045, with the United States bearing a significant share of this burden [1]. In parallel, OSA, a sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by recurrent upper airway obstruction, intermittent hypoxia, and fragmented sleep, is estimated to affect 9 %–38 % of adults in the general population, depending on diagnostic criteria and demographic factors [2,3]. Both disorders are independently associated with elevated cardiovascular and metabolic risk, but their coexistence represents a particularly dangerous convergence.

A growing body of evidence highlights a bidirectional and pathophysiologically interlinked relationship between diabetes and OSA. On one hand, OSA exacerbates insulin resistance, increases sympathetic activity, and promotes systemic inflammation, all of which contribute to worsening glycemic control [4]. On the other hand, diabetes—through autonomic neuropathy and alterations in ventilatory control—may increase the risk of developing OSA or worsen its severity [5]. When co-occurring, these conditions lead to compounded risks of hypertension, coronary artery disease, stroke, cognitive dysfunction, and premature death [6].

Despite growing awareness of the interplay between OSA and diabetes, national-level data quantifying long-term mortality trends in adults with diabetes mellitus remain limited. Prior research has often examined OSA or diabetes in isolation or focused primarily on cardiovascular complications, leaving a gap in understanding the direct mortality burden attributable to OSA in this population [7,8]. To address this, we analyzed U.S. death certificate data spanning 1999 to 2020 to evaluate trends in OSA-associated mortality among adults with diabetes mellitus. Our findings aim to illuminate evolving disease patterns, identify disparities, and guide integrated clinical and public health responses.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of national mortality data using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) platform [9]. This publicly accessible database compiles death certificate information for all U.S. residents. Our analysis covered the period from 1999 to 2020 and included deaths among adults aged 25 years and older in which diabetes mellitus (E10–E14) was listed as the underlying cause of death and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was listed as a contributing cause.

Deaths were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) [10]. The underlying cause of death was defined as diabetes mellitus, encompassing codes E10 (type 1 diabetes), E11 (type 2 diabetes), and other less common diabetes subtypes (E12–E14). Contributing cause of death was restricted to obstructive sleep apnea, identified using ICD-10 code G47.33. Only decedents with both conditions listed (diabetes as the underlying and OSA as a contributing cause) were included (Supplementary Table S1). In all included cases, diabetes mellitus (E10–E14) was listed in Part I of the death certificate as the underlying cause of death, while obstructive sleep apnea (G47.33) was recorded in Part II as a contributing cause.

As this study utilized publicly available and de-identified data, Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was not required. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [11] (Supplementary Table S2).

Data extraction

We extracted mortality data for the overall U.S. adult population and stratified results by sex, race/ethnicity, ten-year age groups (from 25 to 34 to ≥85 years), metropolitan status (metro vs. non-metro), U.S. Census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West), U.S. state of residence, and place of death.

Race and ethnicity classifications were based on death certificate information provided by funeral directors, typically based on next-of-kin reporting. Urbanization level was categorized using the 2013 National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Urban–Rural Classification Scheme, with metropolitan counties defined as having populations ≥50,000 and non-metropolitan counties <50,000 [12].

To preserve confidentiality and reduce statistical noise, CDC WONDER suppresses death counts fewer than 10. Accordingly, subgroup-specific analyses and trend modeling were limited to unsuppressed categories and time intervals. This was particularly relevant for younger age groups and smaller racial/ethnic populations.

Statistical analysis

Annual death counts and population estimates were obtained directly from CDC WONDER. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were calculated using the direct method and standardized to the 2000 U S. standard population. AAMRs were reported per 100,000 persons with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs), estimated assuming a Poisson distribution.

Temporal trends in OSA-related mortality were assessed using Joinpoint regression analysis. Log-linear segmented models were used to estimate Annual Percent Change (APC) for each trend segment, and Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) for the full study period. The number and location of joinpoints were determined empirically based on model fit, using a Monte Carlo permutation test. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Trend analyses were conducted using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2) developed by the U.S. National Cancer Institute [13]. Models began with a single segment and iteratively added joinpoints based on statistical significance [14]. Stratified Joinpoint models were generated for sex, race/ethnicity, age group, region, and urbanization status. APCs and 95 % CIs were reported for each segment.

Subgroup comparisons were made to evaluate differences in AAMRs and trend trajectories. Tests of parallelism were applied to determine whether trends differed significantly across demographic and geographic strata. All supplementary analyses and figure generation were performed using Python (version 3.10), employing the statsmodels and seaborn libraries.

Trends by sex

Between 1999 and 2020, females with diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) experienced a significant increase in mortality. The overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) among females was 1.07 (95 % CI: 1.02 to 1.13) per 100,000 population (Table 1)

Joinpoint regression identified two distinct trend segments: from 1999 to 2005, the mortality rate increased sharply, with an annual percent change (APC) of 21.35 % (95 % CI: 18.30 to 24.48; p < 0.0001). This was followed by a continued but slower rise from 2006 to 2020, with an APC of 5.73 % (95 % CI: 5.09 to 6.37; p < 0.0001). The average annual percent change (AAPC) for the overall period was 9.01 % (95 % CI: 7.68 to 10.36; p < 0.0001). Among males, the overall AAMR was nearly twice as high, at 2.13 (95 % CI: 2.05 to 2.22) per 100,000 population. From 1999 to 2004, the APC was 17.36 % (95 % CI: 15.78 to 18.96; p < 0.0001), followed by a slower but consistent increase from 2005 to 2020, with an APC of 7.64 % (95 % CI: 6.97 to 8.32; p < 0.0001). The AAPC across the entire study period was 10.03 % (95 % CI: 9.05 to 11.01; p < 0.0001) (Table 2; Fig. 1)

Trends by race/ethnicity

From 1999 to 2020, substantial racial and ethnic disparities were observed in mortality associated with diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). The highest overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) occurred among American Indian or Alaska Native individuals at 2.16 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 1.60 to 2.87), followed by Black or African American individuals at 1.98 (95 % CI: 1.81 to 2.16), White individuals at 1.55 (95 % CI: 1.49 to 1.60), Hispanic or Latino individuals at 1.02 (95 % CI: 0.88 to 1.16), and Asian or Pacific Islander individuals at 0.56 (95 % CI: 0.43 to 0.72) (Table 1)

Joinpoint regression revealed significant upward trends in all groups. Among White individuals, mortality rose sharply from 1999 to 2005 (APC: 20.12 %; 95 % CI: 18.34 to 21.93; p < 0.0001), then more gradually through 2020 (APC: 7.11 %; 95 % CI: 6.47 to 7.76; p < 0.0001), with an AAPC of 9.89 % (95 % CI: 8.77 to 11.03). For Black individuals, the APC was 16.42 % (1999–2004; 95 % CI: 11.57 to 21.48; p = 0.0022) and 5.24 % (2005–2020; 95 % CI: 4.18 to 6.31; p < 0.0001), yielding an AAPC of 8.00 % (95 % CI: 6.78 to 9.23). In Asian or Pacific Islanders, mortality rose from 1999 to 2011 (APC: 7.35 %; 95 % CI: 1.74 to 13.27; p = 0.0489), followed by a steeper increase through 2020 (APC: 12.00 %; 95 % CI: 8.33 to 15.80; p = 0.0003); AAPC: 7.70 % (95 % CI: 5.91 to 9.52). Among American Indian or Alaska Native individuals, mortality declined slightly from 1999 to 2010 (APC: 0.94 %; 95 % CI: 5.57 to 3.92; p = 0.7364), then rose significantly from 2011 to 2020 (APC: 7.43 %; 95 % CI: 4.54 to 10.39; p = 0.0009), with an overall AAPC of 7.80 % (95 % CI: 5.88 to 9.75). Hispanic or Latino individuals had an initial surge (1999–2004; APC: 20.50 %; 95 % CI: 10.65 to 31.21; p = 0.0016), followed by sustained growth (2005–2020; APC: 6.92 %; 95 % CI: 4.83 to 9.04; p < 0.0001), resulting in an AAPC of 9.29 % (95 % CI: 7.83 to 10.77) (Table 2; Fig. 2)Fig. 2Trends in OSA-associated mortality among adults with DM, stratified by race and ethnicity, United States, 1999–2020.Fig. 2

Trends by urbanization status

From 1999 to 2020, mortality rates associated with diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) were consistently higher in non-metropolitan than in metropolitan areas. The overall age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) was 1.91 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 1.77 to 2.04) in non-metro areas, compared to 1.48 (95 % CI: 1.42 to 1.53) in metro areas (Table 1).

In metropolitan areas, mortality increased significantly from 1999 to 2004 (APC: 18.31 %; 95 % CI: 16.84 to 19.80; p < 0.0001), followed by a slower rise from 2005 to 2020 (APC: 6.64 %; 95 % CI: 6.05 to 7.24; p < 0.0001). The overall AAPC was 9.25 % (95 % CI: 8.19 to 10.32; p < 0.0001). In non-metropolitan areas, the APC was 19.80 % from 1999 to 2005 (95 % CI: 16.78 to 22.89; p < 0.0001), then moderated to 8.11 % from 2006 to 2020 (95 % CI: 7.32 to 8.90; p < 0.0001). Trends by census region

From 1999 to 2020, age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) attributable to diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) varied notably across U.S. Census regions. The Midwest had the highest AAMR at 1.95 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 1.84 to 2.07), followed by the West (1.71; 95 % CI: 1.60 to 1.82), South (1.45; 95 % CI: 1.37 to 1.52), and Northeast (1.09; 95 % CI: 0.99 to 1.18) (Table 1).

Statistically significant increases were observed in all regions. Discussion

Over a 22-year period, we observed a sustained and statistically significant increase in mortality associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) among adults with diabetes mellitus in the United States. The overall age-adjusted mortality rate rose steadily, with particularly sharp increases between 1999 and 2004, followed by a more gradual yet persistent rise thereafter. Subgroup analyses revealed pronounced disparities by sex, race/ethnicity, geographic region, urbanization, and age. Males, American Indian or Alaska Native individuals, residents of non-metropolitan areas, and adults aged 75–84 years exhibited the highest mortality rates. These findings underscore the compounded risk posed by the intersection of OSA and diabetes and suggest the need for more targeted screening, diagnosis, and treatment strategies across vulnerable populations.

Our study identified a significant sex disparity in mortality associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) among adults with diabetes mellitus, with males exhibiting nearly twice the age-adjusted mortality rate compared to females. This aligns with previous findings that OSA is more prevalent and typically more severe in men than in women [15,16]. For example, the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort reported a higher incidence of sleep-disordered breathing in males and demonstrated that men are more likely to have apneas characterized by complete airway obstruction, contributing to increased cardiovascular risk [17,18].

However, growing evidence suggests that women with OSA may be underdiagnosed due to atypical symptom presentation. Instead of classic symptoms such as loud snoring and witnessed apneas, women more often report insomnia, fatigue, and depressive symptoms, leading to missed or delayed diagnoses [19,20]. This diagnostic gap may contribute to a falsely low mortality burden in women with OSA, particularly in those with coexisting diabetes. Hormonal influences have also been proposed to partially explain these sex-based differences. Estrogen and progesterone are thought to provide a protective effect on upper airway patency in premenopausal women, while postmenopausal hormonal decline increases the risk of OSA [21]. This has important implications, as undiagnosed OSA in older diabetic women may be associated with significant yet unrecognized health risks.

Beyond diagnostic disparity, sex-based variation in metabolic and cardiovascular consequences of OSA has been observed. Men with OSA often present with higher fasting glucose levels and impaired beta-cell function compared to women, suggesting that the metabolic burden of OSA may be more severe in males [22]. Conversely, studies also indicate that women with excessive daytime sleepiness—a common symptom of OSA—have a higher risk of all-cause mortality than men, possibly due to underrecognized cardiovascular or cerebrovascular complications [[23], [24], [25]].

Our study revealed marked racial and ethnic disparities in OSA-related mortality among adults with diabetes mellitus. American Indian or Alaska Native individuals had the highest age-adjusted mortality rates, followed by Black or African American individuals. These findings are consistent with prior research showing a disproportionate burden of OSA and related complications in minority populations. For example, a national mortality study from 1999 to 2019 found that OSA-related deaths remained stable overall but increased significantly among Black men—particularly in contrast to White individuals and Black women [26]. Multiple factors likely contribute to these disparities, including differences in access to care, diagnostic rates, and adherence to continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy. Studies suggest that Black adults with OSA are less likely to receive or adhere to CPAP treatment and are more likely to have comorbid cardiovascular disease, which may compound mortality risk [27,28].

Similarly, Hispanic or Latino individuals in our cohort experienced a notable increase in OSA-related mortality. This aligns with evidence that Hispanic adults in the U.S. are more likely to develop type 2 diabetes and to experience delayed diagnosis of OSA, possibly due to language barriers, healthcare access issues, and cultural factors affecting health-seeking behaviors [29]. The combination of these two conditions may explain the increasing mortality trends seen in our study. Conversely, Asian or Pacific Islander individuals had the lowest OSA-related mortality rates. However, prior studies emphasize that OSA in Asian populations may be underrecognized due to differences in craniofacial structure and symptomatology, despite similar or greater disease severity at lower body mass index (BMI) thresholds [30]. This could result in underreporting of OSA on death certificates or clinical records, and therefore an underestimation of actual disease burden.

Our analysis revealed that individuals with diabetes mellitus residing in non-metropolitan (rural) areas exhibited higher age-adjusted mortality rates associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) compared to their metropolitan (urban) counterparts. This finding aligns with existing literature indicating that rural populations often experience greater health disparities, including higher mortality rates from chronic conditions such as diabetes and OSA.

A study analyzing national mortality data from 1999 to 2019 found that the age-adjusted mortality rate of diabetes patients was consistently higher in rural areas across all age, sex, and ethnicity groups. Over the 20-year period, there was no statistically significant change in the mortality rate in rural areas, whereas urban areas saw a significant decrease. This led to a tripling of the urban-rural diabetes-related mortality gap in the USA, rising from 2.0 to 6.8 deaths per 100,000 population for diabetes as the underlying cause, and from 6.8 to 24.3 deaths per 100,000 population for the disease as a contributing factor [31]. Several factors may contribute to these disparities. Rural areas often face challenges such as limited access to healthcare services, fewer healthcare providers, and longer travel distances to medical facilities. These barriers can lead to delayed diagnoses and treatment of conditions like OSA and diabetes. Additionally, socioeconomic factors, including lower income and education levels, may impact health outcomes in rural populations [32].

Interestingly, a prospective cohort study examining treatment outcomes among rural and urban patients with OSA found no significant difference in continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) adherence between the two groups. However, the study noted that rural patients reported greater improvements in certain quality-of-life measures, suggesting that when access to treatment is available, rural patients can achieve comparable outcomes to their urban counterparts [33].

Our analysis revealed a clear age-related gradient in OSA-related mortality among individuals with diabetes mellitus, with the highest crude mortality rates observed in the 75–84 age group, followed by a slight decline in those aged 85 and older. This pattern aligns with existing literature indicating that the prevalence and severity of OSA increase with age, contributing to higher mortality risks in older populations [34].

Several studies have demonstrated that older adults with OSA are at an elevated risk of all-cause mortality. For instance, a longitudinal study found that individuals aged 70 years or older with OSA had a significantly higher risk of death compared to younger counterparts [35]. Additionally, research indicates that the combination of OSA and excessive daytime sleepiness in older adults further amplifies the risk of mortality, suggesting a synergistic effect of these conditions [36]. The slight decline in mortality rates among individuals aged 85 and older observed in our study may be attributed to a “survivor effect,” where individuals who live to advanced ages may possess protective factors that mitigate the impact of OSA. Alternatively, it could reflect underdiagnosis or misclassification of OSA in this age group due to overlapping symptoms with other age-related conditions [37]. Furthermore, the increased mortality risk associated with OSA in older adults may be compounded by the presence of comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes, which are more prevalent in this population [38]. The interplay between these conditions can exacerbate the adverse health outcomes associated with OSA, underscoring the importance of comprehensive management strategies in older adults.

Our study identified significant geographic variation in mortality associated with diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) across U.S. states from 1999 to 2020. The top five states with the highest age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) were Oregon, Montana, Minnesota, Washington, and Nebraska. In contrast, the bottom five states with the lowest AAMRs were New York, Massachusetts, Nevada, Georgia, and Louisiana.

These findings align with previous research indicating substantial state-level disparities in diabetes-related mortality. A study analyzing data from 2000 to 2019 found that age-standardized diabetes mortality rates decreased nationally but remained high in certain states, particularly in the South and Midwest [39]. The study highlighted that the highest mortality rates were concentrated in southeastern states, emphasizing the persistent geographic disparities in diabetes outcomes. Several factors may contribute to these state-level differences. Variations in healthcare access, socioeconomic status, prevalence of risk factors such as obesity and smoking, and public health initiatives can influence the burden of diabetes and OSA. For instance, states with higher rates of obesity and smoking often experience increased prevalence of diabetes and OSA, leading to higher mortality rates [40]. Additionally, differences in healthcare infrastructure and resources across states can impact the diagnosis and management of OSA. States with limited access to sleep medicine specialists and diagnostic facilities may have lower rates of OSA diagnosis and treatment, contributing to higher mortality rates. Conversely, states with robust healthcare systems and public health programs may achieve better outcomes through early detection and management of these conditions [41].

Our analysis revealed that among individuals with diabetes mellitus and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), the most common place of death was the decedent's home, accounting for 38.5 % of all deaths. This was followed by medical facility–inpatient settings (32.8 %), outpatient or emergency room settings (10.6 %), and other locations.

This distribution aligns with broader trends observed in end-of-life care, where a significant proportion of deaths occur outside hospital settings. A study by Cross et al. found that approximately 30 % of deaths in the United States occur at home, highlighting a preference for home-based end-of-life care among patients with chronic conditions [42]. The high percentage of home deaths among individuals with diabetes and OSA may reflect several factors. Firstly, the chronic nature of both conditions often leads to a gradual decline, allowing for end-of-life planning and the possibility of dying at home. Secondly, limited access to healthcare facilities, especially in rural or underserved areas, may result in patients remaining at home during critical health events [43]. Moreover, the sudden and unpredictable nature of OSA-related complications, such as nocturnal hypoxia or cardiac arrhythmias, can lead to unexpected deaths at home. A study by Gami et al. demonstrated that individuals with OSA have an increased risk of sudden cardiac death, particularly during sleep, which may contribute to the higher incidence of home deaths [44].

Strengths and limitations

This study offers the first comprehensive national analysis of long-term trends in mortality associated with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) among adults with diabetes mellitus in the United States, spanning over two decades. One of the major strengths lies in the use of nationally representative death certificate data from the CDC, which allowed for robust, population-level assessments across key demographic subgroups including sex, race/ethnicity, age, geographic region, and urbanization. The large sample size and extended time frame enhanced the precision and generalizability of trend estimates and enabled detection of subtle disparities and temporal shifts. Additionally, the use of Joinpoint regression modeling provided a statistically rigorous method to identify inflection points and quantify annual percent changes in mortality. Stratified analyses across multiple subpopulations offer new insights into vulnerable groups disproportionately affected by the intersection of OSA and diabetes—information that is essential for informing targeted public health responses.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the reliance on death certificate data may introduce misclassification bias. OSA is frequently underdiagnosed and often omitted from official cause-of-death records, especially in the absence of polysomnographic confirmation or formal diagnosis during life. This underreporting may lead to underestimation of the true burden of OSA-related mortality, particularly in marginalized or medically underserved populations. Second, death certificates do not capture clinical variables such as body mass index, glycemic control (e.g., HbA1c), OSA severity (e.g., apnea-hypopnea index), or treatment adherence (e.g., CPAP use), which could significantly influence outcomes. As such, we were unable to adjust for these potentially confounding factors. Third, while our stratified analyses offer valuable subgroup-specific insights, residual confounding related to social determinants of health, access to care, and lifestyle factors cannot be excluded. Fourth, the observed increases in OSA-related mortality over time may, in part, reflect evolving diagnostic and reporting practices rather than solely a true rise in disease burden. Over the study period, clinical awareness of OSA has increased substantially, aided by updated practice guidelines, broader recognition of its cardiometabolic consequences, and expanded access to diagnostic tools such as home sleep apnea testing. Concurrently, refinements in ICD-10 coding practices and increased emphasis on documenting comorbidities in mortality records may have improved the likelihood of OSA being recorded on death certificates, particularly as a contributing cause in Part II. These changes could have led to improved detection and reporting in later years, thereby influencing the temporal patterns observed in our analysis. While such factors do not diminish the public health significance of the rising trends, they should be considered when interpreting longitudinal changes. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of mortality surveillance data precludes causal inference. Nonetheless, the observed patterns remain clinically and epidemiologically important, particularly given the consistency and magnitude of trends across diverse populations.

Conclusion

In this nationwide analysis spanning over two decades, we identified a substantial and rising burden of mortality attributable to obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) among adults with diabetes mellitus in the United States. The increase was evident across sex, race and ethnicity, geographic region, urbanization level, and age groups, with particularly high mortality rates observed among males, American Indian or Alaska Native individuals, rural residents, and older adults.

These findings underscore the critical need for enhanced screening, diagnosis, and treatment of OSA within diabetic populations—especially among groups facing structural inequities or healthcare access barriers. Given the bidirectional relationship between OSA and diabetes and their combined impact on cardiovascular and metabolic health, integrated management approaches are essential. Our results also highlight the importance of public health strategies that are both population-specific and data-driven.
