Exploring clinician and patient perspectives on choice of general anaesthetic. A report of two U.S. surveys: an anesthesia clinician Multicenter Perioperative Outcomes Group survey and a patient Amazon Mechanical Turk survey
Authors: Katherine J Holzer, Rachel M Hurwitz, Bethany R Tellor Pennington, Mary C Politi, Michelle T Vaughn, Larry Chu, Amy Price, Melissa Hicks, Michael S Avidan, Sachin Kheterpal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInhalation anaesthesia or propofol-based TIVA are the two primary options for administering general anaesthesia. Shared decision-making between clinicians and patients is critical. This study explores the perspectives of both anesthesia clinicians and patients on general anaesthesia techniques, aiming to identify gaps in communication and decision-making.MethodsTwo surveys were conducted in December 2020: an 18-item clinician survey disseminated through the Multicenter Perioperative Outcomes Group to clinicians who administer general anesthesia and a 33-item patient survey distributed via Amazon Mechanical Turk to individuals who had undergone surgery using general anaesthesia. The clinician survey focused on preferences regarding use of general anaesthesia techniques, discussion practices, and perceived barriers to TIVA use. The patient survey assessed preoperative discussions and preferences for anaesthetic techniques. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics.ResultsOf the 1123 anesthesia clinician respondents [comprised mostly of anaesthesiology attendings (47.8%) or Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists (CRNAs, 34.6%)], when asked if they or a family member required general anaesthesia; 40% preferred TIVA, 13% preferred inhalation anesthesia and 33% indicated no preference. Nearly 80% of clinicians did not routinely discuss general anaesthetic technique options with patients. Among the 509 eligible patients (mean age: 38.1 yr), 65% reported that their anaesthesia team discussed general anaesthesia options, and 63% were offered a choice. Both anesthesia clinicians and patients identified intraoperative awareness risk and recovery quality as important factors influencing preferences.ConclusionsThis study highlights discrepancies between patient perceptions of shared decision-making and anesthesia clinicians’ reported practices for general anesthesia techniques in US hospitals. Further comparative studies are needed to address these communication gaps and support evidence-based shared decision-making in anaesthetic care.Keywords: clinician preference, inhaled anaesthetics, patient care, patient preference, total intravenous anaesthesia

Millions of patients undergo surgical procedures each year, many of which require general anaesthesia. The two most commonly used techniques of general anaesthesia are inhalation anaesthesia using volatile anaesthetics and intravenous anestheisa using propofol-based TIVA. There is limited evidence suggesting superiority of one technique in terms of clinical outcomes.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Beyond known, rare contraindications (e.g. malignant hyperthermia), there are few definitive situations where one method of general anaesthesia is indicated over another.7 Instead, an option that optimises the individual patient’s experience given their health, procedure, and personal preferences should be chosen. Clinicians may also factor in their familiarity with each method, the existing literature, and the known limitations of each approach, alongside the patient's unique clinical needs. Ultimately, shared decision-making between the clinician and the patient is crucial in guiding this decision.

When provided the opportunity to engage with anaesthetic care decisions, most patients prefer to play a collaborative or active role in decisions about their anaesthetic care.8,9 Yet, few studies have comprehensively explored patient preferences regarding the choice of anaesthesia.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Facilitating and encouraging active patient engagement in care-related decisions has been shown to increase patient satisfaction and promote a sense of respect from their clinicians.8 Addressing common patient concerns about general anaesthesia, such as fears of intraoperative awareness or postoperative nausea and vomiting, can help alleviate preoperative anxiety and mitigate postoperative pain.15, 16, 17 Furthermore, positive preoperative interactions and clear communication from anaesthesia clinicians can strengthen patients’ trust and perceptions of clinician competence.18

The risk of intraoperative awareness is a particularly important topic for the preoperative discussion. Although rare, occurring in approximately one in every 1000 patients, this phenomenon can lead to serious, long-term psychological consequences, including post-traumatic stress disorder.19,20 Notably, although this is an uncommon complication, it is what patients consider the most important to them when discussing their preferences on anaesthetic type.9 Educating patients about such risks as part of a preoperative conversation not only informs them but also supports their autonomy in decision-making.

There is also a notable gap in research exploring both the patients' and the clinicians' beliefs and preferences regarding TIVA and inhalation anaesthesia.1,4,14,21, 22, 23 Understanding the perspectives and experiences of both groups can provide valuable insights for designing trials comparing these techniques and for improving shared decision-making in anaesthetic care.24 To address this knowledge gap, we conducted two surveys, one distributed to clinicians who administer general anaesthesia, and another to former surgical patients who had received general anaesthesia. These surveys aimed to elucidate the factors influencing clinicians’ decision-making when selecting anaesthesia for patients and the values patients prioritise in their anaesthetic care. We hypothesised that most clinicians do not routinely discuss general anaesthesia options with their patients and that patients want to be included in the decision-making process.

Methods

To inform the background and significance of the Trajectories of Recovery after Intravenous Propofol vs inhaled VolatilE anaesthesia (THRIVE) study (NCT05991453), two distinct surveys were created to inform the background and significance of the research, one focused on clinician perspectives and the other on patient perspectives. The surveys were iteratively reviewed and refined based on feedback from the multidisciplinary THRIVE team, including researchers, clinicians, patient partners with previous general anaesthesia experiences, and experts in shared decision-making. Both surveys were created using an electronic web-based platform (Qualtrics International, Inc, Seattle, WA, USA). The Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys (CHERRIES) was used to report the data.25

Clinician survey

The clinician survey (Supplementary Appendix A) was distributed electronically via e-mail in December 2020 to leaders of active member institutions of the Multicenter Perioperative Outcomes Group (MPOG), a consortium of more than 40 community and academic institutions across USA.26 Leaders were asked to circulate the survey among their anaesthesiology clinicians, including faculty, residents, fellows, and nurse anaesthetists using institution-specific e-mail distribution groups. This survey was distributed in preparation for a grant proposal submission and did not require ethics board review. The University of Michigan institutional review board (IRB) determined that the analysis of these survey data did not meet the requirements for regulation (HUM00211890). The survey was open, voluntary, and anonymous and participants did not receive any compensation for responding. There was no planned limit to the number of participants, and individual survey links for each participant were not used, so it is not possible to establish the number of clinicians who received the survey.

The 18-item survey included questions about demographics and years of anaesthetic practice. Survey questions were neither randomised nor alternated. In some multiple-choice questions, more than one answer could be selected. Clinicians were asked about their preferred general anaesthesia technique, and their personal preferences if they or a family member required general anaesthesia, and whether or not they discuss general anaesthesia options with patients. Additional questions addressed practices to minimise risk of intraoperative awareness.

Patient survey

The patient survey was conducted in December 2020 via Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk), a platform that manages a pool of individuals who have signed up to participate in surveys and other online tasks.27, 28, 29 Eligibility included English-speaking adults (aged ≥18) residing in USA and had a history of at least one surgical procedure requiring general anaesthesia within the past 15 yr. If they met eligibility criteria and agreed to participate, the participants were then asked to complete the 33-question survey (Supplementary Appendix B). Survey questions were neither randomised nor alternated. In some multiple-choice questions, more than one answer could be selected. The patient survey was deemed exempt by the Washington University School of Medicine IRB owing to the ‘preparatory to research’ nature of the work (HRPO 202012047). The planned limit to number of participants with completed surveys was 500. Participants were provided $1 for their participation.

The survey collected demographic and anaesthesia-related information. Participants were provided definitions of surgery and general anaesthesia before completing the survey questions. Initial questions assessed whether anaesthesiologists had discussed different general anaesthesia techniques before surgery and whether patients were given a choice between these options. Additional questions explored patients’ awareness of propofol and volatile anaesthetic agents (e.g. isoflurane, sevoflurane, desflurane) and whether they had a strong positive or negative association with these agents. In order to understand participants’ knowledge or perceptions of each anaesthetic technique’s purported advantages and disadvantages, participants were presented with lists of known or possible advantages and disadvantages of each technique and asked which they ‘believe would be the advantages to have an anaesthetic done using this medication?’ and which they ‘believe would be the disadvantages…’. Patients could select multiple options, including ‘I don’t know’. Lastly, the survey included questions on patient preferences concerning the risk of intraoperative awareness to evaluate whether this risk influenced their perceptions and choices of anaesthetic technique.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using Stata Version 16.0 (StataCorp. 2019. Stata Statistical Software: Release 16. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).30 Descriptive statistics were generated for patient and clinician participants. Missing data are described in the tables.

Patient participant characteristics

Patients were also asked to describe their general health status. Responses included ‘excellent’ (n=108, 21.6%), ‘very good’ (n=203, 40.6%), ‘good’ (n=150, 30.0%), ‘fair’ (n=34, 6.8%), and ‘poor’ (n=4, 0.8%).

Clinician perspectives on anaesthetic type

Clinicians were asked which type of general anaesthetic they would choose for themselves or a family member if given a choice (Fig. 1). Approximately 40% (n=449) reported they would choose propofol TIVA, whereas 33% (n=371) indicated no preference. Only 13% (n=146) preferred inhalation anaesthesia. Despite these preferences, 79.3% (n=890) of clinicians reported that they do not currently or routinely discuss the option of propofol TIVA vs inhalation anaesthesia with their patients (Fig. 1). When asked about communication of intraoperative risks, 67.6% (n=759) believed anaesthesia clinicians should mention unintended intraoperative awareness as a possible, albeit rare, complication. Similarly, 69.8% (n=784) agreed that clinicians should inform patients about the steps taken to prevent unintended intraoperative awareness during surgery.

Clinicians were asked: (1) Do you currently (routinely) discuss propofol TIVA vs inhaled volatile as anaesthesia options with your patients?; and (2) If given a choice for themselves or a member of their family, which type of general anaesthetic they would choose?

Regarding patient experience and recovery from general anaesthesia, 2.4% (n=27) believed inhalation anaesthesia was superior to propofol TIVA. In contrast, 44.6% (n=501) considered propofol TIVA to be superior, and 16.7% (n=188) felt the experiences are equivalent. Notably, 28.0% (n=314) felt that the current evidence is insufficient to determine a meaningful difference, and 3.7% (n=41) cited limited personal experience with propofol TIVA.

When asked whether they would use propofol TIVA more often if evidence demonstrated that the risk of unintended intraoperative awareness was equivalent between TIVA and inhalation anaesthesia, 41.5% (n=466) responded ‘yes’, 31.9% (n=358) responded ‘no’, and 22.0% (n=247) were unsure.

Clinicians were asked whether they would adhere to randomisation in a clinical trial comparing propofol TIVA and inhalation anaesthesia, assuming no contraindications. The vast majority (n=968, 86.2%) responded ‘yes’, 3.6% (n=40) reported ‘no’, and 5.6% (n=63) were unsure.

Three additional questions addressed safety practices to prevent unintended intraoperative awareness. When asked whether anaesthesia clinicians should routinely verify that the propofol infusion pump is running correctly and the i.v. catheter is functioning during propofol TIVA administration, 93.7% (n=1052) responded ‘yes’. For inhalation anaesthesia, 77.9% (n=875) agreed that clinicians should routinely set an anaesthesia machine alert for low volatile agent concentrations. Finally, 56.2% (n=631) supported verifying that either propofol TIVA or inhalation anaesthesia is being administered during the pre-incision timeout, whereas 38.2% (n=429) did not.

Figure 2 provides additional details on clinician perspectives regarding the use of propofol TIVA vs inhalation anaesthesia. Clinician participants were presented with the following prompt: ‘The majority (80%) of general anaesthetics in the USA are based on inhaled volatile agents rather than propofol TIVA. In your opinion, please select all the reasons for this’. The most frequently selected responses were ‘set-up time affects choice’ (n=725, 64.5%), ‘risk of intraoperative awareness is higher with propofol TIVA’ (n=584, 52.0%), and ‘propofol TIVA sometimes not available due to drug shortages’ (n=565, 50.3%). Patient responses to anaesthetic-related questions.

A majority (n=416, 83.4%) had previously heard of the anaesthetic drug propofol. Those who reported familiarity were asked whether they had a strong positive or negative association with the drug based on their experience. Responses were mixed.

Detailed questions and responses regarding patients’ perceptions of possible advantages and disadvantages of propofol and inhalational anesthesia are reported in Table 3.

Participants were presented with the following prompt: ‘The risk of forming memories while under general anaesthesia is exceedingly rare (1/1000 patients)’. They were then asked: ‘If you could choose a type of anaesthesia that improved the chance of a faster and higher quality recovery, but might have a small increased risk of waking up during surgery, how likely would you be to choose it?’. In response, 19.2% (n=96) reported that they would be very likely to choose this option, 48.4% (n=242) somewhat likely, 19.2% (n=96) somewhat unlikely, and 13.0% (n=65) not at all likely.

Participants were then asked: ‘If you could choose a type of anaesthesia that improved the chance of a faster and higher quality recovery, but has no change in the risk of waking up during surgery, how likely would you be to choose it?’. In this scenario, 41.2% (n=206) reported being very likely to choose this type, 42.8% (n=214) somewhat likely, 11.8% (n=59) somewhat unlikely, and 4.0% (n=20) not at all likely.

Participants were also asked about their preferences regarding shared decision-making: ‘Using the following options, please choose your preferred role in making decisions about your medical treatment’. Responses varied: 27.6% (n=138) preferred to make the final decision themselves, 39.8% (n=199) preferred to make the final decision after seriously considering their doctor’s opinion, and 23.8% (n=119) preferred shared responsibility with their doctor. An additional 7.2% (n=36) preferred that their doctor make the final decision while seriously considering their opinion, and 1.6% (n=8) preferred to leave all decisions entirely to their doctor.

Finally, participants were asked ‘How often do you need to have someone help you when you read instructions, pamphlets, or other written material from your doctor or pharmacy?’. Responses included ‘never’ (n=127, 25.4%), ‘rarely’ (n=99, 19.8%), ‘sometimes’ (n=163, 32.6%), ‘often’ (n=62, 12.4%), and ‘always’ (n=49, 9.8%).

Discussion

This study provides insights into the perspectives of anesthesia clinicians and patients regarding general anaesthesia preferences and decision-making processes. Findings revealed inconsistencies between clinicians’ preferred general anaesthesia techniques for personal and family use and the reality of general anaesthesia administration in the USA.31 Additionally, clinicians’ and patients’ perceptions of patient involvement in the decision-making process regarding anaesthetic choice differed.

Although 13% of clinicians preferred inhalation anaesthesia for themselves or their family, more than 90% of general anaesthetics administered at MPOG participating hospitals during 2016–21 used inhalation anaesthesia.31 In our study, nearly half of clinicians believed that inhalation anaesthesia is used more commonly owing to a less time-intensive set-up, perceived lower risk of intraoperative awareness, predictability of patient response, and factors related to the drug supply and cost. These findings align with previous clinician surveys on TIVA, which have highlighted the burdensome set-up, lack of familiarity with clinical evidence supporting TIVA in specific settings, and inadequate education and training.1,21,23,32 Fewer than half of clinicians believe that propofol TIVA provides better patient recovery than inhalation anaesthesia, despite previous studies indicating superior recovery experiences with TIVA.1,21,33, 34, 35, 36 Importantly, the preferences clinicians express for personal or family use may reflect an idealised scenario rather than actual practice constraints. In real-world settings, clinicians likely default to techniques they are most familiar with, particularly in the absence of compelling evidence to support a change. In addition, the potential risk of intraoperative awareness and limited evidence may drive the variation between clinician preferences for their own care vs the care delivered to their patients. This reflects the importance of having strong evidence that is generalisable across various procedures and patient populations. A large comparative effectiveness trial rigorously evaluating postoperative outcomes could outweigh the factors currently influencing clinician choice of general anaesthesia technique.37

A notable discrepancy exists between anesthesia clinicians and patients in how they perceive engagement in the anaesthetic decision-making process. Clinicians’ perceptions, along with lack of evidence, likely influence the decision-making process and may hinder the ability of clinicians to initiate a comprehensive and informative discussion with patients. Although 65% of patients reported that their anaesthesia team discussed anaesthetic drug choices with them, fewer than 20% of clinicians reported discussing these options with patients. Prioritising shared decision-making, where clinicians present the risks and benefits of various anaesthetic options and invite patients to express their preferences, can improve communication, increase patient understanding, and promote decisions that align with the patient's goals and values, rather than leaving decisions solely to the clinician.16 Interestingly, patient preferences regarding shared decision-making varied: roughly 40% preferred to make the final decision after considering their clinician’s opinion, about 28% preferred to decide independently, and only 2% preferred that their clinician make the final decision. These findings suggest that patients desire an active role in both the anesthesia technique discussion and the final decision. Recent tools have been designed to facilitate these conversations in the perioperative setting, with preliminary results suggesting that they decrease decision conflict and demonstrate high acceptance by patients and clinicians.10,13 In addition to the need for studies comparing the safety and effectiveness of various general anaesthesia techniques, future research should investigate how clinicians engage patients in the decision-making process and assess their perceptions of this approach. Such studies could provide insights that enhance clinicians' confidence and motivation in discussing general anaesthesia options with patients.

Postoperative recovery and risks of intraoperative awareness are likely to influence a patient’s choice of anaesthetic. In our study, most patients were either somewhat likely or very likely to choose an anaesthetic type that improved the chance of a faster and higher quality recovery with either a small increased risk or no change in risk of experiencing intraoperative awareness.

Further research is needed to explore the reasons driving these disparities. Although both surveys provide insights into current general anaesthesia practice, preferences, and shared decision-making, several limitations should be noted. The clinician survey was disseminated via MPOG centres, preventing accurate calculation of response rates and limiting characterisation of clinician demographics. Additionally, the predominance of large academic centres among MPOG institutions may bias responses toward decisions for complex patients with higher illness severity. Because only US-based institutions were included, these findings may not reflect international perspectives on inhalation anaesthesia vs TIVA.36,37 Another unmeasured but likely influential factor is the type and complexity of surgery. For longer or more invasive procedures, modest differences in recovery or side-effect profiles between anaesthetic techniques may be less clinically meaningful, as postoperative recovery is influenced by multiple perioperative variables. This limitation highlights the need for future studies to stratify analyses by surgical type and complexity when comparing anaesthetic approaches. It is also possible that some patients were not offered anaesthetic choices because of institution-specific surgical requirements (e.g. TIVA for neuromonitoring). We could not determine the type of surgery, confirm whether patients received general anaesthesia, or differentiate between receipt of inhalation anaesthesia, TIVA, or mixed approaches. Although descriptions of general anaesthesia were provided, patients may not have fully understood the differences between general anaesthesia and other anaesthesia types. Additionally, because patients could reflect on operations that occurred up to 15 yr ago, recall bias is a concern as it may be difficult for respondents to accurately remember the details or context of their preoperative conversations with an anaesthesiologist.

The patient sample, recruited via MTurk, was younger, more educated, and less racially diverse than the general US surgical population, demonstrating the non-representative nature of our sample and further limiting generalisability. Although 20% of respondents identified as members of minority racial groups, systemic inequities faced by diverse populations may not be adequately represented.38,39 The average respondent age was 38 yr, with 82% holding a college degree, whereas the average age of surgical patients is increasing and less than 40% of Americans are college educated.22,36,37 It is unlikely that a meaningful proportion of the patient sample received care at an MPOG facility, potentially explaining the variation in responses.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, this study highlights an important discrepancy between anesthesia clinicians’ personal preferences, their actual anaesthetic practices, and a gap between clinician and patient perceptions of patients’ involvement in anaesthetic care decisions. However, both clinicians and patients agreed that intraoperative awareness and potential recovery time impact general anaesthesia preferences. For clinicians, the risk of intraoperative awareness is thought to be higher with propofol than with inhaled anaesthetic agents. For patients, even if there was a potential for a quicker and better recovery, the risk of intraoperative awareness appears to outweigh potential benefits. For this reason, research directly comparing these outcomes with other postoperative outcomes of inhalation anaesthesia and TIVA, such as the Feasibility Study for the Trajectories of Recovery After Intravenous Propofol Versus Inhaled VolatilE Anesthesia (THRIVE) Trial study24 is needed.35 Findings of future comparative effective trials can enhance shared decision-making in anaesthesiology by providing clinicians with evidence to inform patient discussions.
