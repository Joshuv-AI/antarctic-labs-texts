Open questions in elucidating neural mechanisms underlying sensory-guided motor control
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSensory feedback is needed to dynamically adjust motor behaviors as we interact with the world, and understanding sensory-guided motor control could be beneficial in guiding future therapeutic strategies for patients suffering from spinal cord injury or other neurological diseases. To explore some of the open questions in this topic, we spoke with several experts in the field: Professor Karl Friston (UCL); Dr. Andrew Pruszynski and Dr. Mehrdad Kashefi (Western University, Canada); Professor Fan Wang and Dr. Kyle Severson (MIT, USA); and Professor Anne Churchland and Dr. Felicia Davatolhagh (UCLA, USA). We discuss some of the main theories of sensory-motor integration, some of the challenges and open questions in this field, and exciting future directions, including neurorehabilitation.

Q: Is it possible to separate motor control from perception?

Karl Friston: No. Perceptual inference rests on an active sampling of the sensorium that is underwritten by motor control. All serious accounts of perception entail action. All serious accounts of action entail perception.

Fan Wang: The answer to this question depends on how one defines perception. If perception means conscious experience, then yes. The fact that athletes experience peak performance during flow state with subconscious automatic execution of actions is a clear indication that motor control can be separated from perception. Similarly, many sensory guided reflexes, such as nociceptive withdrawal reflexes, are executed before conscious awareness. Furthermore, there exist central pattern generator (CPG) circuits in the brainstem and the spinal cord that can generate movements independent of sensory inputs and perception.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: At the level of neural structures, it is very difficult to separate the two. For instance, when we record activity from the visual cortex of a mouse, we not only see neurons firing in response to what the mouse sees, but also a massive amount of neural activity in response to what the mouse is doing. Even when an animal is just sitting there, it is whisking, grooming, and making facial movements. These uninstructed movements create a tidal wave of activity that floods the sensory areas. Additionally, what we see is that the brain doesn’t just “perceive” and then “act”. Instead, the neural activity representing the movement starts almost immediately, often well before the decision is finalized. This poses a challenge for perturbation experiments, which might alter both the sensory signals and the movement planning, since both are reflected in neurons within a given area.

However, at the level of individual neurons, we absolutely can use models to disentangle sensory signals from movements (perception might be another story). For instance, we can build encoding models that include sensory inputs alongside instructed and uninstructed movements. Because sensations and movements occur at different times, we can decompose neural activity into components that are modulated by each. Moreover, because our models learn time-varying kernels that relate variables of interest to neural activity, we can look at the temporal dynamics to more deeply understand what a neuron is encoding. For instance, we have observed that neural activity in the barrel cortex is related to whisking movements. But because the neural activity peaks about 400 ms after the whisk, we can be confident that the neural activity reflects the sensory consequences of a movement rather than motor control (e.g., movement preparation or execution).

In summary, our observations suggest that that the brain doesn’t just “perceive” and then “act.” Instead, the neural activity representing the movement starts almost immediately, often well before the “decision” is finalized. The “perceptual” neurons are already whispering to the “motor” neurons from the very beginning.

Q: We currently have several theoretical accounts of sensorimotor integration/active sensing.

For example, active inference versus reinforcement learning; control theory (with continuous states) versus Markov decision process (in discrete states); forward-inverse models versus model predictive control. What are the best theoretical accounts if it is impossible to separate motor control from perception?

Karl Friston: The best theoretical account is offered by active inference, because it gracefully accommodates special cases; such as reinforcement learning and applications to continuous and discrete generative models of the world (e.g., model predictive control and partially observed Markov decision processes). Furthermore, the inversion of generative (i.e., forward) models replaces forward–inverse model formulations. In short, active inference—by construction—subsumes earlier accounts as special or specious cases.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: This is an interesting question– I am not sure I see many of these ideas as competing. Instead, they might just describe different things. For instance, the continuous nature of control theory is really helpful in understanding movements that unfold continuously (and with sensory feedback), like smooth pursuit eye movements or reaching. This is likely true of at least some cognitive processes, but others appear to come and go in a more discrete, state-like manner. For example, in Ashwood, Pillow, 2022, she explicitly tested the hypothesis that continuously changing states could better account for the data–and they didn’t. The cognitive processes her approach highlighted (which she called engaged and disengaged) appear to change rather abruptly.

Q: Humans and animals are capable of recombining existing motor primitives to enable new motor behaviors.

For example, an animal has learned action sequence as ABCD, then the same animal is able to learn a new action sequence such as ACDB by recombining existing action primitives.

The idea of neural composability is fascinating.

What are your thoughts on this?

Andrew Pruszynski and Mehrdad Kashefi: To navigate a complex world, the brain must control a large repertoire of complex behaviours, each shaped by context. Compositionality offers a parsimonious solution to this challenge: by building complex behaviours from a shared set of simple primitives, finite neural resources can flexibly generate many different outputs. Artificial neural networks now allow us to rigorously examine this well-established idea, as they naturally exhibit compositional strategies even when they are not directly enforced. The next frontier is defining the fundamental primitives of cognitive and motor abilities, their combinatorial syntax, and the mechanisms for synthesizing new primitives.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: I agree, super interesting and a hallmark of intelligence! I don’t have too much to say on the topic, however. I hope our future work can address this.

Q: What roles do you think sensory processing plays in neural decomposition and composition, in particular for action selection and motor planning?

Karl Friston: Sensory processing is essential for planning as (active) inference. And active inference is necessary for action selection. In hierarchical formulations, planning can be read as sequential policy optimization, where each policy can be regarded as a composition of actions. In evaluating the consequences of committing to a particular policy, it is necessary to infer the current state of the world—including the body—through sensory processing (i.e., perceptual inference). This speaks to the fundamental role of perception in action, as evinced by active vision, active inference, planning as inference, perceptual control theory, model predictive control, and so on. The notion of composition also speaks to deep (i.e., hierarchical) world models with separation of temporal scales that allow for compositions of compositions, and a deeply structured engagement with the sensed world.

Kyle Severson: I think sensory feedback is central to composability. First, sensory streams provide the state variables that render motor primitives portable across contexts. Proprioceptive, vestibular, tactile, and visual feedback continually updates the body’s current state—so that the execution of a motor primitive can be properly modulated and timed according to the inferred body state. Second, sensory feedback supports sequence control: it disambiguates the current state within an unfolding motor sequence and allows a smooth handoff between primitive transitions at the appropriate phase. Third, sensory prediction enables fluid composition. Forward models anticipate body physics, allowing the nervous system to smoothly adjust posture while leveraging fast error corrections when reality deviates from expectation.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: To decompose a behavior like ABCD, the brain needs to know exactly when A ends and when B begins. Sensory areas are able to contribute to these cues. For instance, the sound of the paw hitting the lever or the solenoid delivering reward can provide high-resolution information to the animal that allows the brain to segment a behavioral sequence. When an animal switches from ABCD to ACDB, it’s making a series of rapid action selections. For an animal to select “C” after “A” instead of “B”, it must weight incoming sensory evidence against its internal model of the task. Sensory processing provides the boundaries that allow for decomposition and the feedback then validates this new composition.

Q. How far away are we from achieving a unified theoretical framework and what are the critical knowledge gaps we still need to fill?

Karl Friston: Arguably, active inference already offers a unified theoretical framework. The critical knowledge gaps pertain to its application; particularly in relation to (i) scaling (or possibly scale-free) applications in neurorobotics (ii) and neurophysiological implementation in the neurosciences. The latter is particularly prescient in terms of translational neuroscience; for example, a mechanistic understanding of movement disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, and the role of underlying synaptopathies.

Fan Wang: I think we are still far from achieving a unified theoretical framework. We still lack a principled mechanistic understanding of how sensory information is transformed into motor commands in volitional behaviors. For example, when humans are asked to pick up a pencil, a smartphone, or a baseball placed in front of them, they effortlessly form object-specific hand preshapes before the hand even makes contact. This ability generalizes to a wide range of novel, unfamiliar objects, suggesting that it is not merely a set of object-specific learned associations. Despite extensive work on visuomotor pathways, the principles underlying the transformation from retinal input to the precise patterns of muscle activation that generate distinct grasp configurations remain unclear. Similar gaps exist for many behaviors guided by other sensory modalities.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: In my view, the unified theoretical framework will be more like a patchwork quilt where a collection of diverse models is stitched together but share common language of data and computation. A critical knowledge gap that we still need to fill includes standardization. Because tasks and data formats are different, we can’t tell if results between two studies complement each other. Additionally, reproducibility continues to be a concern. To address this, a cultural shift towards “horizontal” neuroscience is needed. For example, projects like the International Brain Laboratory (IBL) using the same task, standardization across labs, unified data format is one way we can move towards a unified framework.

Q: What kind of synaptic plasticity rules may be involved in governing sensory-guided motor control?

Karl Friston: Synaptic plasticity—under theoretical formulations—follows empirically verified associative plasticity rules and plays a key role in optimizing generative (i.e., forward or world) models necessary for intentional behavior (in both the motor and autonomic domain).

Kyle Severson: Sensory-guided motor control likely depends on multiple plasticity mechanisms rather than one simple rule. At baseline, Hebbian/STDP-like learning can strengthen sensorimotor pathways that consistently predict the sensory consequences of actions, supporting forward models and rapid correction, but natural behavior also requires learning across longer delays and distributed populations—making eligibility traces and three-factor rules especially plausible for temporal credit assignment. In circuits where movement is organized by smooth, phase aligned neural sequences across populations, calcium-dependent and dendrite plateau-gated mechanisms such as behavior timescale synaptic plasticity (BTSP) could stabilize reliable sequence transitions and consolidate learning around salient events (rewards, contacts, smooth action execution and transition).

Q. A related question, how would neuromodulators impact on sensorimotor integration?

Karl Friston: Neuromodulators play a key role that is distinct from learning per se. Neuromodulation equips neuronal circuits with a context-sensitivity that is essential to explain sensorimotor integration over short timescales. These can range from saccadic suppression during eye movements through to sensory attenuation necessary to realize intended or volitional acts.

The role of neuromodulators in gain control (e.g., excitation and inhibition balance) has also become a key cornerstone of computational psychiatry and neurology (in the form of controlling the precision or uncertainty afforded various prediction errors).

Kyle Severson: One hypothesis is that neuromodulatory systems contribute to movement vigor: their activity frequently covaries with locomotion and movement speed, suggesting they could act as a global gain or energy budget signal that scales the amplitude and tempo of motor programs. A key open question is causality, whether these signals drive vigor or primarily reflect state and ongoing movement. Temporally precise, state-contingent perturbations are an important next step to test this hypothesis.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: Studying neuromodulation in the context of sensorimotor integration is becoming increasingly important. Certainly, neuromodulation appears likely plays a key role in the discrete states that are identified by Markov models (see above). An intuitive example of this is the sympathetic and parasympathetic states. We sometimes transition from one to the other very rapidly (e.g., if a terrifying bear appears!), and norepinephrine is critical for this.

Q: Could you provide some insights into the application of basic neuroscience principles governing sensory-guided motor control in neurorehabilitation? Could such approaches contribute to translational applications?

Karl Friston: One obvious implication of mechanistic accounts based upon active inference or predictive processing is that new learning (necessary for rehabilitation) is driven by precision-weighted prediction errors. This speaks to paradigms that deliberately elicit precise prediction errors to enhance recovery. A complementary application—of theoretical principles—can be found in brain computer interfacing and how it rests upon the computational architectures of motor control in both the peripheral and central nervous systems.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: I am not sure about the potential of sensory-guided motor control to inform neurorehabilitation, although it certainly could. I see a different translational avenue, which is more deeply understanding psychiatric disorders. Abnormalities in sensory processing are present in autism spectrum disorders, schizophrenia and many other disorders. Although altered sensory processing is only one of a number of symptoms in these disorders, it nonetheless could be a tractable entry point for understanding how the genes implicated could alter neural circuits. Likewise, motor control might not be a fundamental problem in these disorders, but it is an incredibly powerful readout that likely will offer deep insights into neural circuits changes. Consider ADHD, for instance. The hyperactivity component is well established and is treatable with methylphenidate, but no one really knows why it is there. What do the movements of ADHD folks tell us about how their brains differ from typical brains? This remains a mystery.

Q. What are the most exciting advances/findings in the field of sensorimotor control?

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: I think the sensory motor loops are turning out to be quite different from how they were originally conceived. Sensory representations are sparse and sometimes mixed with other signals, and motor signals are encoded in fundamentally different ways (for example, they are dynamical systems).

Q. What are the challenges currently faced by the community?

Andrew Pruszynski and Mehrdad Kashefi: The field is navigating multiple views on the functional organization of biological motor control, each anchored in a different level of neural processing. On the one hand, cell-type studies, done mostly in mice, emphasize organization through molecular identity. On the other hand, population-dynamics studies, often done in primates, emphasize organization expressed in neural activity patterns. Both views are compelling and may be correct at their own level of abstraction. Despite initial efforts, what’s lacking is a unified account of whether and how circuit components are linked to neural dynamics. Are there links between particular cell types and specific dynamical motifs? Are such links conserved across brain regions, behaviors and species? Finding out where links exist (and where they do not) promises major advances in our understanding of biological motor control.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: Neuroscience is moving increasingly towards larger datasets, we need theorists who are also involved in experimental data and can offer their insight into these high-dimensional and complex datasets.

Q: What open scientific questions would you encourage the research community to pursue for this topic?

Karl Friston: It is difficult to anticipate where the most exciting advances in sensorimotor control will emerge. By definition, exciting advances are surprising and unexpected. My guess is that these advances will be seen in unexpected domains: for example, (i) cell cultures or organoids that are equipped with the ability to move manipulanda, (ii) autonomous vehicle and drone research in machine learning and artificial intelligence, (iii) developmental neurorobotics studying the emergence of intentional behavior and (iv) studies of how infants come to understand and use their bodies via intrinsic motivation.

Fan Wang: So far, most sensorimotor studies have focused on skeletal motor control and have largely overlooked the autonomic nervous system, even though many movements are accompanied by concerted autonomic responses such as changes in breathing rate, heart rate, blood pressure, regional blood flow, and salivary secretion (especially during feeding). Many movement disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease, involve pathophysiology and dysfunction of both skeletal motor and autonomic systems. Yet how the nervous system coordinates motor commands to skeletal muscles with autonomic outputs (e.g., to smooth muscle and glands) to support diverse sensory-guided behaviors remains an underexplored area of research.

Anne Churchland and Felicia Davatolhagh: First, I’d love to see more research connecting fundamental approaches in neuroscience to different brains: different moments in the lifespan, diseased brains, addicted brains, and so on. We have developed incredible tools for characterizing activity in young healthy animals, and these will be powerful when applied in a new context. Second, I’d like to see more research that considers the vast and numerous uninstructed movements that animals make. Not everyone needs to study these, of course, but they should at least be accounted for when trying to interpret neural activity.
