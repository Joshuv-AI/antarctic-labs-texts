Lipoprotein(a) and Peripheral Artery Disease as Independent Predictors of Arterial Stiffness in Patients With Hypertension: A Single‐Center Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Bibombe P. Mwipatayi, James Dodd, Revathy Carnagarin, Trevor A. Mori, Jonathan Golledge, Sally Burrows, Gerald F. Watts, Osamu Iida, Markus P. Schlaich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCarotid–femoral pulse‐wave velocity (cfPWV) is a marker of central arterial stiffness and an established predictor of cardiovascular risk in hypertension. Although elevated lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] concentrations and peripheral arterial disease (PAD) are associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, their independent associations with central arterial stiffness and any potential interaction remain incompletely defined. We conducted a cross‐sectional study of 366 adults with treated hypertension. Central arterial stiffness was assessed using cfPWV. Plasma Lp(a) concentrations were categorized into tertiles. PAD was identified using clinical history, physical examination, and available diagnostic investigations. Associations of Lp(a) tertiles and PAD with cfPWV were examined using robust linear regression. Multivariable models adjusted for covariates associated with cfPWV in univariable analyses, and interaction terms were used to assess effect modification by PAD. Mean cfPWV increased across ascending Lp(a) tertiles. After multivariable adjustment, cfPWV was higher by 0.82 m/s (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.31–1.32; p = 0.002) in the middle tertile and by 1.29 m/s (95% CI, 0.75–1.84; p < 0.001) in the highest tertile compared with the lowest tertile. PAD was independently associated with higher cfPWV (adjusted difference, 1.20 m/s; 95% CI, 0.78–1.62; p < 0.001). No interaction between Lp(a) tertiles and PAD was observed (p for interaction >0.10). In adults with treated hypertension, elevated Lp(a) concentrations and PAD were independently associated with greater central arterial stiffness, with no evidence of effect modification by PAD.

Introduction

Hypertension affects more than 30% of adults worldwide [1, 2], and among individuals with hypertension, increased arterial stiffness is strongly associated with excess cardiovascular mortality [3]. Arterial stiffness, which is most commonly quantified by carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV), is an important feature of vascular aging and independent predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [3, 4, 5]. Chronic hypertension accelerates arterial stiffening through sustained hemodynamic load and adverse structural remodeling of the arterial wall, thereby magnifying cardiovascular risk. Antihypertensive therapies have improved blood pressure control; however, as they do not fully mitigate vascular injury, a substantial residual cardiovascular risk remains.

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], a genetically determined low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) particle covalently linked to apolipoprotein(a), is an independent mediator of atherosclerosis and thrombosis [6, 7]. Lp(a) promotes vascular risk through pro‐inflammatory oxidized phospholipids, endothelial dysfunction, and antifibrinolytic/prothrombotic effects mediated by apolipoprotein(a) [8, 9]. Elevated levels of Lp(a), typically >50 mg/dL or approximately 125 nmol/L), are observed in nearly one‐third of individuals diagnosed with coronary artery disease. However, specific elevation thresholds vary across different studies [10, 11, 12]. Several novel therapies aimed at lowering Lp(a) levels are currently in advanced stages of clinical development [13, 14, 15]. These treatments are designed to address the clinical challenges posed by elevated Lp(a) levels and their contribution to cardiovascular disease.

The pathogenic contribution of Lp(a) to atherothrombotic disease has been suggested in recent epidemiological work, including studies by Aday et al. [16, 17], which demonstrated that elevated Lp(a) levels predict the incidence and progression of peripheral artery disease (PAD). PAD is a clinical manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis that is characterized by diffuse arterial remodeling, medial calcification, and progressive loss of arterial compliance [18]. Both Lp(a) and PAD are independently linked to arterial dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [19, 20, 21]. However, the degree to which Lp(a) and PAD exert additive or interactive influences on arterial stiffness is unclear. Therefore, we conducted this study to examine whether Lp(a) and PAD are independently associated with cfPWV and whether they have additive or multiplicative effects on arterial stiffness [22, 23].

Methods

Study Population

This single‐center, cross‐sectional study included consecutive adults with established hypertension who attended the Dobney Hypertension Centre, Royal Perth Hospital, Perth, Western Australia, between February 2016 and October 2019. All individuals who presented during this period were eligible for inclusion. No formal exclusion criteria were applied. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [24] and complied with International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The Royal Perth Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee provided ethical approval for the study (RGS0000006105) and waived the need for individual informed consent because of the observational, non‐interventional design. Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations [25] for cross‐sectional studies, with the completed STROBE checklist provided in Table S1.

Clinical Assessment

All participants underwent a standardized clinical evaluation that included a medical history, physical examination, and anthropometric assessment. Ambulatory blood pressure (BP) monitoring was performed using validated oscillometric devices (Spacelabs, Snoqualmie, WA, USA; Mobil‐O‐Graph, IEM GmbH, Stolberg, Germany; and OSCAR‐2, SunTech, Morrisville, NC, USA) in accordance with international recommendations [26, 27]. BP was recorded every 15 min during daytime (06:00–22:00) and every 30 min overnight (22:00–06:00). The measurement arm, cuff type, and cuff size of each participant were documented. Smoking status and body mass index (BMI) were also recorded.

Definitions

PAD was identified through systematic review of medical records and hospital coding data, supplemented by available clinical information, including documented symptoms of lower‐extremity arterial insufficiency, such as intermittent claudication, ischaemic rest pain, or tissue loss, and any previous lower‐extremity revascularization [28, 29]. Smoking status was defined according to lifetime tobacco exposure. Current smokers had used tobacco within the preceding 30 days, whereas former smokers had ceased tobacco use ≥30 days previously. Never smokers reported no previous tobacco use. Cumulative tobacco exposure was quantified in pack‐years [29].

Hypertension was defined as office systolic BP (SBP) ≥140 mmHg, diastolic BP (DBP) ≥90 mmHg, or current antihypertensive treatment [27, 30]. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5%, 2‐h plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during oral glucose tolerance testing, or use of glucose‐lowering therapy [31, 32]. Hyperlipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥5.2 mmol/L, LDL cholesterol (LDL‐C) ≥3.4 mmol/L, triglycerides ≥1.7 mmol/L, or current lipid‐lowering therapy [19, 33]. In individuals with very high cardiovascular risk, an LDL‐C target <1.8 mmol/L was considered optimal [19, 34]. A history of stroke was defined as an acute focal neurologic deficit attributable to cerebral infarction, intracerebral hemorrhage, or subarachnoid hemorrhage, confirmed by neuroimaging [35, 36]. Transient ischemic attack (TIA) was defined as a transient episode of neurologic dysfunction caused by focal cerebral ischemia without evidence of acute infarction [35, 36]. Chronic kidney disease was defined according to the 2022 KDIGO criteria as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for ≥3 months, albuminuria ≥30 mg/g, or structural renal abnormality [36].

For categorical arterial stiffness analyses, high arterial stiffness was defined as cfPWV ≥10 m/s in accordance with contemporary expert consensus recommendations for increased aortic stiffness after standardization of carotid–femoral distance measurements [37].

Arterial Stiffness Assessment

Arterial stiffness was assessed using cfPWV, which is the reference standard measure of central arterial stiffness [4, 38]. cfPWV was measured by applanation tonometry using the SphygmoCor XCEL system (AtCor Medical Pty. Ltd. Naperville, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Measurements were obtained with participants in the supine position after ≥10 min of rest. Pulse waveforms were recorded sequentially from the right common carotid and femoral arteries. Transit time was determined by electrocardiographic R‐wave gating and path length defined as the surface distance between recording sites. Two measurements obtained at trough were averaged for analyses [39, 40, 41].

Biochemical Analyses

Fasting venous blood samples were analyzed at PathWest Laboratory Medicine Western Australia, Royal Perth Hospital, using standardized accredited clinical chemistry methods. Complete blood count, lipid profile, fasting plasma glucose, glycated hemoglobin, renal and hepatic function test results, aldosterone, high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (CRP), and homocysteine concentrations were assessed. LDL‐C concentration was calculated using the Friedewald formula for triglyceride concentrations <4.5 mmol/L [42]. The 24‐h urine collections were used to quantify sodium, potassium, and protein excretion in accordance with institutional laboratory standards.

Lp(a) mass concentrations (g/L) were measured using an automated immunoturbidimetric assay (Abbott Diagnostics), which uses a monospecific polyclonal antibody against apolipoprotein(a) and is commonly used in clinical and epidemiological studies. Analytical validation showed strong agreement with molar‐based assays. An internal comparison involving the Roche Tina‐Quant Lp(a) assay (nmol/L) and World Health Organization/International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine reference material (SRM 2B) indicated that Lp(a) values were highly correlated (n = 110; r = 0.986; p < 0.001) [17, 43], thus supporting the validity of the immunoturbidimetric method despite differences in reporting units [6, 19, 44, 45].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or the median and interquartile range (IQR; first and third quartiles) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Between‐group comparisons were performed using Student's t test, Mann–Whitney U test, or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test. All tests were two‐sided. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the independent associations of lipoprotein(a) and PAD with cfPWV. Accordingly, the primary analyses modelled cfPWV as a continuous dependent variable to preserve information and avoid the loss of statistical power associated with dichotomization. Associations between cfPWV and clinical, biochemical, and hemodynamic variables were initially examined using univariable robust linear regression, which uses iteratively reweighted procedures to mitigate the influence of heteroscedasticity and outlying observations. Because the lower limit of detection of Lp(a) assays varied over the study period, Lp(a) could not be reliably analyzed as a continuous exposure in the primary regression models. Therefore, it was categorized into empirical tertiles to enable graded comparisons and accommodate censored values. Variables associated with cfPWV at p < 0.10 in univariable analyses were considered for inclusion in an initial multivariable robust regression model.

To improve clinical interpretability, we also performed supplementary analyses in which participants were categorized according to lower and higher arterial stiffness using prespecified cfPWV thresholds <10 m/s and ≥10 m/s, consistent with contemporary expert consensus recommendations [46]. Baseline demographic, clinical, biochemical, and hemodynamic characteristics were compared between these groups using Student's t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate, and the χ 2 test for categorical variables. To further examine the relationship between Lp(a) and arterial stiffness, continuous Lp(a) concentration was graphically assessed against cfPWV using a scatter plot with a fitted regression line. Because Lp(a) demonstrated a non‐normal distribution, the correlation between Lp(a) and cfPWV was quantified using Spearman's rank correlation. Additionally, an exploratory receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the discriminatory performance of Lp(a) for identifying high arterial stiffness (defined as cfPWV ≥10 m/s). The area under the ROC curve was calculated with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and the optimal cutoff was identified using the Youden index.

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the association between Lp(a) and cfPWV across clinically relevant strata. Subgroup analyses were prespecified according to age, sex, and 24‐h blood pressure control. Age was categorized as younger than 60 years or 60 years or older. Blood pressure control was defined using 24‐h ambulatory BP. Controlled BP was defined as mean 24‐h SBP <130 mmHg and mean 24‐h DBP <80 mmHg. Within each subgroup, robust regression models were fitted to estimate the association of Lp(a) tertiles with cfPWV after adjustment for the same covariates used in the primary multivariable model excluding the stratification variable when appropriate. These subgroup estimates were displayed graphically in a forest plot.

To limit multicollinearity, covariates that represented overlapping biological domains, including lipid parameters, glycemic indices, renal function measures, and inflammatory markers, were examined to determine correlations and clinical redundancy. Only one representative variable from each correlated set was retained for multivariable modeling. Backward manual stepwise elimination was performed with the least statistically significant term removed sequentially until all retained predictors met the prespecified retention criterion of p < 0.05. Effect modification was assessed by introducing a cross‐product interaction term between PAD and Lp(a) tertiles in the final model. Significance was evaluated using robust Wald tests. Model performance was summarized using pseudo‐R 2 because robust regression is estimated by iteratively reweighted procedures and the classical R 2 is not directly interpretable under these conditions [47]. Missing data were minimal across variables included in the primary analyses; therefore, a complete‐case analysis was used for regression modeling and the number of observations was reported for each model. Because of the low proportion of incomplete observations and substantial completeness of the principal exposure and outcome variables, this was considered a parsimonious and statistically appropriate strategy, and imputation procedures were not performed. Nevertheless, as with any complete‐case analysis, the risk of bias related to data missing not at random could not be excluded [48, 49]. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software (version 17, MP64 edition; StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Participant Characteristics According to Lp(a) Tertiles

A total of 366 adults with hypertension were included in this study and categorized according to Lp(a) tertiles. The median Lp(a) concentrations were 0.05 g/L (IQR, 0.03–0.09), 0.36 g/L (IQR, 0.22–0.58), and 1.28 g/L (IQR, 0.95–2.01) in tertiles 1, 2, and 3, respectively (Table 1). Baseline characteristics across tertiles, including age, sex distribution, BMI, and 24‐h ambulatory SBP and DBP, were not significantly different. Significant differences were identified across Lp(a) tertiles for concentrations of apolipoprotein B, triglycerides, homocysteine, and high‐sensitivity CRP; however, significant differences in other lipid, renal, metabolic, hormonal, and inflammatory variables were not observed. Additionally, statistically significant differences in comorbidities, cardiovascular risk factors, and medication use, including statins and antihypertensive agents, across Lp(a) tertiles were not observed (Tables 1 and 2).

Clinical Characteristics According to PAD Status

PAD was present in 98 of 366 participants (26.8%) (Table 3). Participants with PAD were older (median, 63 years [IQR, 53–73] vs. 54 years [IQR, 44–65]; p < 0.001) and less frequently male (40.8% vs. 61.9%; p < 0.001) compared with those without PAD. BMI and 24‐h SBP were modestly higher in the PAD group (p = 0.027 and p = 0.011, respectively), whereas DBP did not differ significantly (p = 0.463). Participants with PAD exhibited greater central arterial stiffness, as indicated by higher cfPWV values (12.0 ± 2.3 m/s vs. 10.2 ± 2.2 m/s; p < 0.001). Renal impairment was more pronounced in the PAD group, as reflected by the lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (p < 0.001) and higher albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio (p = 0.002). Concentrations of glycemic indices and systemic inflammation markers, including fasting glucose (p = 0.014), glycated hemoglobin (p = 0.018), and high‐sensitivity CRP (p = 0.008), were also higher among participants with PAD. Lipid parameters and homocysteine concentrations did not differ significantly between groups. Diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease were more prevalent in participants with PAD (p = 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively), and beta‐blocker use was more common (p = 0.028); however, the use of other cardiovascular medications did not differ significantly.

Determinants of cfPWV

Robust regression analyses of cfPWV determinants are presented in Table 4. In univariable analyses, Lp(a) tertiles were modeled categorically, with cfPWV compared to the lowest tertile. Clinical Characteristics According to Arterial Stiffness

To enhance clinical interpretability, participants were additionally categorized according to lower (cfPWV <10 m/s) and higher (cfPWV ≥10 m/s) arterial stiffness. Overall, 99 of 366 participants (27.0%) had high arterial stiffness. Compared with those with low arterial stiffness, participants with high arterial stiffness were older (68.0 years [IQR, 59.5–74.5] vs. 54.0 years [IQR, 39.0–66.0]; p < 0.001) and had higher 24‐h SBP (142.0 mmHg [IQR, 131.0–154.5] vs. 131.0 mmHg [IQR, 121.0–141.0]; p < 0.001). They also had higher median Lp(a) concentrations (0.75 g/L [IQR, 0.10–1.03] vs. 0.24 g/L [IQR, 0.08–0.775]; p < 0.001), higher homocysteine concentrations (13.0 µmol/L [9.9–16.9] vs. 10.0 µmol/L [8.0–15.5]; p < 0.001), and higher glycated hemoglobin values (6.1% [IQR, 5.3–7.35] vs. 5.5% [IQR, 5.0–6.1]; p < 0.001). In participants with high arterial stiffness, the estimated glomerular filtration rate was lower (82.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 [64.5–90.0] vs. 89.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 [74.0–90.0]; p = 0.001) and the albumin‐to‐creatinine ratio was higher (5.9 mg/mmol [1.15–14.0] vs. 2.3 mg/mmol [0.9–9.0]; p = 0.006). PAD was substantially more common among participants with high arterial stiffness (55.6% vs. 16.1%; p < 0.001) (Table 5).

ROC Analysis of High Arterial Stiffness

In an exploratory receiver‐operating‐characteristic analysis for high arterial stiffness, defined as a carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) of 10 m per second or higher, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] had an area under the curve of 0.64 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.58 to 0.70; p < 0.001) (Figure 2). The optimal threshold identified by the Youden index was approximately 0.77 g per liter, corresponding to a sensitivity of 49.5% and a specificity of 74.2%. These findings indicate modest discriminatory performance of Lp(a) for identifying high arterial stiffness.

Interaction and Sensitivity Analyses

There was no significant interaction between peripheral artery disease (PAD) status and Lp(a) tertiles with respect to carotid–femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) (p > 0.10 for interaction) (Table S2). In adjusted interaction models, the coefficient for the interaction between PAD and tertile 2 was 0.35 m per second (95% CI, −0.82 to 1.51; p = 0.56), and the coefficient for the interaction between PAD and tertile 3 was 0.91 m per second (95% CI, −0.19 to 2.01; p = 0.11).

In prespecified subgroup analyses, the association between higher Lp(a) levels and higher cfPWV was directionally consistent across age, sex, and 24‐h blood‐pressure control strata (Figure 3). For the comparison of tertile 3 with tertile 1, the adjusted increase in cfPWV was 0.97 m per second (95% CI, 0.28 to 1.66) among participants younger than 60 years of age and 2.02 m per second (95% CI, 1.13 to 2.91) among those 60 years of age or older. The corresponding adjusted increases were 2.01 m per second (95% CI, 1.08 to 2.93) among women and 0.93 m per second (95% CI, 0.26 to 1.60) among men. According to 24‐h blood‐pressure control status, the adjusted increases were 1.32 m per second (95% CI, 0.43 to 2.21) among participants with controlled blood pressure and 1.25 m per second (95% CI, 0.55 to 1.94) among those with uncontrolled blood pressure. No significant heterogeneity was detected across these clinically relevant subgroups.

Discussion

In this cross‐sectional cohort of adults with treated hypertension, elevated Lp(a) concentrations were independently associated with greater cfPWV, indicating increased central arterial stiffness. PAD was also independently associated with higher cfPWV; however, our findings indicated that PAD did not modify the relationship between Lp(a) and arterial stiffness. These findings suggest that while Lp(a) and PAD are related to arterial stiffening, their effects appeared to be largely independent rather than synergistic in this cohort. cfPWV reflects reduced aortic compliance and distensibility arising from structural and functional alterations of the arterial wall. Lp(a) carries pro‐inflammatory oxidized phospholipids and exhibits antifibrinolytic properties via apolipoprotein(a); these mechanisms may promote arterial remodeling, medial calcification, and elastic property loss [13, 50].

Several features of our analysis strengthened the interpretation of this association. Although many baseline characteristics did not differ materially across Lp(a) tertiles in descriptive analyses, higher Lp(a) tertiles were associated with higher cfPWVs in both univariable and multivariable robust regression models. After adjusting for age, 24‐h SBP, glycated hemoglobin, and homocysteine, the association remained graded and statistically significant. The stronger association observed after adjustment is likely due to negative confounding, in which certain covariates related to both Lp(a) and cfPWV diminished the initial association. This pattern suggests that Lp(a) may contribute to increased stiffness in large arteries through mechanisms not fully captured by traditional cardiovascular risk factors, rather than simply serving as a marker of widespread atherosclerotic burden. However, as is typical in observational studies, residual confounding cannot be ruled out.

The results of supplementary analyses further support the robustness and clinical relevance of the primary findings. When participants were categorized according to lower and higher arterial stiffness, those with higher arterial stiffness (cfPWV ≥10 m/s) had substantially higher Lp(a) concentrations, greater prevalence of PAD, and more adverse hemodynamic, metabolic, and renal risk profiles than those with lower cfPWVs. Additionally, continuous Lp(a) concentrations showed significant positive correlations with cfPWV in the scatter plot analysis, thus reinforcing the consistency of the association across categorical and continuous analytical approaches. In contrast, the ROC analysis showed only modest discriminatory performance of Lp(a) alone for identifying high arterial stiffness. This finding was not unexpected because arterial stiffness is a multifactorial vascular phenotype influenced by age, BP, metabolic function, renal impairment, inflammation, and established vascular disease. Therefore, the ROC analysis results should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive findings rather than as evidence that Lp(a) is sufficient as a standalone discriminator of arterial stiffness.

The multivariable models also identified homocysteine as an independent determinant of cfPWV. Elevated homocysteine has been implicated in oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and collagen cross‐linking, thus providing a biologically plausible link to large artery stiffening [9, 39]. These findings combined with the observed clustering of higher Lp(a) with adverse biomarkers, including apolipoprotein B, triglycerides, high‐sensitivity CRP, and homocysteine, highlight the multifactorial regulation of arterial stiffness and support the concept of vascular aging as a systemic process shaped by interacting genetic, metabolic, inflammatory, and hemodynamic influences [19].

Recent Mendelian randomization data suggest that genetically determined Lp(a) may not directly influence arterial stiffness [51]. Our findings are compatible with those of a context‐dependent model of vascular aging in which Lp(a) may exert greater biological relevance in individuals with hypertension and established vascular risk rather than function as an isolated causal determinant of arterial stiffness in the general population. The vascular effects of Lp(a) may be driven less by the total circulating concentration alone and more by its oxidized phospholipid burden and related bioactive component [50]. Therefore, oxidized phospholipid‐rich Lp(a), rather than the total Lp(a) concentration, may be the more biologically active mediator that links Lp(a) to arterial dysfunction and vascular aging.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support Lp(a) as a marker of diffuse arterial dysfunction rather than only as a surrogate of focal atherosclerotic disease. Therefore, Lp(a) measurements may help identify individuals with hypertension who are at increased risk for adverse arterial remodeling, even in the absence of clinically manifest PAD or overt dyslipidemia. Central arterial stiffness, as quantified by cfPWV, may be a biologically relevant intermediate phenotype that links elevated Lp(a) to downstream cardiovascular risk and may provide complementary information beyond that provided by conventional lipid measures [52, 53]. The absence of a statistically significant interaction with PAD did not diminish the clinical relevance of PAD itself, which remained independently associated with higher cfPWV and identified a subgroup with more advanced vascular injury.

Emerging Lp(a)‐lowering therapies, including antisense oligonucleotides and small interfering RNA agents currently undergoing advanced clinical development, provide an opportunity to directly test whether lowering Lp(a) modifies large artery stiffness and improves cardiovascular outcomes [13, 14]. Currently, whether pharmacological reduction of Lp(a) favorably alters arterial stiffness is uncertain. The incorporation of cfPWV as a mechanistic end point in longitudinal and interventional studies may help determine whether targeting Lp(a) can meaningfully attenuate arterial dysfunction and reduce residual cardiovascular risk in populations with hypertension and others at high risk.

This study has some limitations that warrant consideration. First, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and longitudinal studies are required to determine whether elevated Lp(a) precedes and contributes to progressive arterial stiffening. Second, PAD was identified using clinical history, physical examination findings, and available diagnostic information rather than systematic ankle–brachial index screening results of all participants. Therefore, subclinical or asymptomatic PAD may have been under‐recognized, and this under‐recognition would be expected to bias PAD‐related associations and interaction effects toward the null. Third, cfPWV reflects global central, predominantly aortic, stiffness and does not permit precise localization of vascular pathology. Fourth, oxidized phospholipids and other bioactive components carried by Lp(a) were not measured, which limited mechanistic inference. Fifth, the study population predominantly comprised individuals of White European ancestry with treated hypertension, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ancestral groups or normotensive populations. Sixth, the study was not specifically powered to detect modest interaction effects; therefore, the subgroup and ROC analyses results should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, despite multivariable adjustment, some residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

Among adults with treated hypertension, elevated lipoprotein(a) concentrations were independently associated with greater central arterial stiffness, and PAD was independently associated with higher cfPWV. PAD status did not have a significant modification effect. Lp(a) may be considered a marker of adverse arterial remodeling in hypertension and cfPWV may be a relevant intermediate vascular phenotype through which elevated Lp(a) is related to cardiovascular risk. Prospective interventional studies are required to confirm whether pharmacological lowering of Lp(a) favorably modifies arterial stiffness or improves clinical outcomes.
