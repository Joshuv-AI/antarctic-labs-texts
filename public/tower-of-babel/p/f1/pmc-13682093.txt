Nutritional status of school-going adolescent girls is associated with their cognitive function but not academic performance: insights from Ten2Twenty-Ghana baseline study
Authors: Nawaf Saeed, Mavis A Ayimbire, Addae Yaw Hammond, Gabriel Ajediwe Alatiah, Joseph Collins Kodom, Inge D Brouwer, Fusta Azupogo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe link between nutritional status and cognitive performance is well-documented in early childhood, but limited data exist on adolescents, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. This study examines the associations between nutritional status, overall cognitive function and its subdomains, and academic performance among adolescent girls in Ghana.MethodsCross-sectional data from the Ten2Twenty-Ghana study were analysed, including 845 girls aged 10–17 from 19 primary schools in the Mion District. Fluid cognition, a composite measure of cognitive function, was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Toolbox Cognition Battery. The cognition battery comprised five subdomains: executive function, attention control, processing speed, working memory, and episodic memory. Academic performance was assessed using examination scores in Mathematics, English, and General Science. Anthropometry was used to calculate the body mass index-for-age z-score (BAZ) and the height-for-age z-score (HAZ) using WHO AnthroPlus. Generalised linear mixed-effects models with a random intercept for school, assessed associations while adjusting for potential confounding variables, including age, anaemia, dietary diversity, menarche status, health complaints, school attendance, household food security, wealth, dependency, and literacy ratios, school feeding status, and interviewer effect.ResultsThe mean HAZ was -0.9 ± 1.2, BAZ was -0.7 ± 0.9, fluid cognition was 26.7 ± 9.6, and academic performance was 53.8 ± 13.1%. BAZ was positively associated with the fluid cognition composite score (adjusted β = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.39, 1.83) and with processing speed subdomain (adjusted β = 1.31, 95% CI: 0.49, 2.13). HAZ was positively associated with processing speed after full adjustment (adjusted β = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.06, 1.29). No significant associations were found between anthropometric indices and academic performance.ConclusionNutritional status was positively associated with the fluid cognition composite score and processing speed subdomain among adolescent girls, while no association was observed with academic performance. These findings underscore the importance of improving adolescent nutrition to support cognitive development in rural settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12937-026-01370-2.Keywords: Adolescent girls, Nutritional status, Academic performance, Cognitive function, Ghana

Introduction

Globally, adolescents contribute to a greater proportion of the population, and more than three-quarters of this age group live in developing countries [1, 2]. According to the 2021 Population and Housing Census of Ghana, the proportion of adolescents (10–19 yrs) is about 22.0% [3]. Adolescence is a period of rapid growth and maturation from childhood to adulthood, second only to the first year of life [4]. This period marks the initiation of sexual relationships, psychological development, and the transition from full socioeconomic dependence to greater independence.

Adolescent girls gain tremendous physical, mental, and emotional maturity during this transition period while facing disadvantages from several welfare indicators, including nutrition, health, and educational attainment [5]. Common nutrition-related problems affecting adolescents include stunting, underweight, and micronutrient deficiencies, particularly iron, zinc, and vitamin A deficiencies [6, 7]. Globally, about 10–15% of adolescents are underweight, and 20–30% are stunted [2]. Furthermore, approximately 38% and 23% of adolescents suffer from underweight and stunting, respectively, in developing countries [2]. In Ghana, 28% of adolescents are underweight, and 14% are stunted [1, 8]. The consequences of malnutrition on cognitive ability, school attendance, attention, and academic performance are well-documented among school-aged children, with one recent systematic review concluding that childhood malnutrition is associated with impaired neurodevelopment, academic achievement, and increased behavioural problems [9]. Evidence from the Young Lives Cohort shows that linear growth retardation in adolescents is associated with poorer cognitive skills and educational performance [10–12]. These findings suggest that the adverse effects of malnutrition on cognition and educational performance are not only limited to childhood but also manifest during adolescence. However, aside from Ethiopia [10–14], evidence from other settings in the context of Sub-Saharan Africa is limited. This highlights the need for more targeted research to clarify these relationships and inform effective interventions. Moreover, three out of ten students who finish basic education in Ghana do not progress to secondary school, primarily due to poor academic performance, with girls being disproportionately affected [15, 16]. The role of malnutrition in this trend remains uncertain.

Adolescents’ cognitive function can also be influenced by several factors, including genetic variation; socio-cultural factors such as school attendance, parental schooling, and household food security; psychosocial factors such as parental care and cognitive stimulation; and biological factors such as age, infections, and subjective health complaints [17–20]. This study examines the associations between nutritional status, cognitive functioning, and academic performance among school-going adolescent girls, while accounting for socio-cultural, biological, and psychosocial influences. Understanding the nutrition–cognition relationship is critical for informing targeted interventions to improve adolescent nutritional health and developmental outcomes.

Methods

Study design and study area

The study design and aims have previously been described [21]. In brief, we used an analytical cross-sectional study design using baseline data from the Ten2Twenty-Ghana by Azupogo et al. [21], among adolescent girls aged 10–17 years in the Mion district in North-Eastern Ghana. The Mion district is located in the Northeastern corridor of Ghana's Northern Region. The Mion district reflects a rural area constrained by limited resources, typical of northern Ghana. Studies have shown that this population is relevant for investigating nutrition and cognition due to the numerous educational and nutritional challenges faced by children in the setting [3, 22]. The district has 70 primary schools, 11 junior high schools, and one senior high school. Based on the overall aim of Ten2Twenty-Ghana, we excluded the junior high schools because an ongoing iron and folic acid supplementation programme was already in place for girls in these schools. Participation in the study was voluntary, and participants' parents signed informed consent. The interviews were conducted by trained research assistants from the University for Development Studies who could speak the local dialect (Dagbani and/or Likpakpa) and understood the culture of the study area. Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Navrongo Health Research Centre (NHRCIRB323). We also obtained permission from the district's Ghana Education Service and Ghana Health Service Directorates. Community meetings were held with various stakeholders to inform them about the study's objectives and its relevance. Finally, signed/thumb-printed parental informed consent and the girl's assent were obtained before the data collection.

Study population and sampling procedures

Participants in the study were school-going adolescent girls aged 10–17, selected from various primary schools within Mion District in Northern Ghana. Eligibility criteria required participants to be healthy, not pregnant, not lactating, free from mental, visual, or hearing impairments, and willing to participate voluntarily by signing an assent form. Schools were purposefully selected from a list provided by the Ghana Education Service in the District, focusing on those with larger enrolments of girls. A total of 19 schools were selected for the study. Within each school, participants were recruited using a proportionate convenience sampling approach, in which the number of girls selected was proportional to the school’s female enrolment. Eligible participants were then recruited based on their availability and willingness to participate in the study. The target sample size for the survey (n = 1,040) was estimated using the Randomized Multi-Arm Sample Size Software (RMASS; http://www.rmass.org/) [23] to ensure adequate statistical power to detect a minimum mean difference of 0.30 SD in mathematics and verbal skills z-scores between stunted and non-stunted adolescent girls [12]. The final analytic sample included 845 girls with complete data on cognitive and academic performance outcomes and key independent variables, including nutritional status (Fig. 1).

Nutritional status assessment

Haemoglobin concentration was assessed using capillary blood obtained via finger prick, while anthropometric measurements were collected by trained field assistants. The anthropometric measurements included weight (kg) and height (cm) at a central location in the selected schools. The girls were weighed without shoes and socks using a SECA electronic weighing scale to the nearest 0.1 kg, and height was measured using a SECA stadiometer to the nearest 0.1 cm. Measurements were taken twice, and the average was used. Age was recorded in years based on information from the girls’ records (birth certificates, birth record booklet, and health insurance card) and the date of the anthropometry. Haemoglobin status was determined with HemoCue 301 by experienced phlebotomists. Anaemia was defined according to the age-specific World Health Organisation criteria as haemoglobin concentration < 120 g/L for girls aged ≥ 12 years and < 115 g/L for girls aged < 12 years [24]. Body-mass index for age Z-score (BAZ) and height for age Z-score (HAZ) were computed using reference data from the WHO AnthroPlus software. Girls whose HAZ was below −2 standard deviations (−2SD) were considered stunted [25]. We classified the girls as underweight (BAZ < −2SD), normal weight (−2SD ≤ BAZ ≤ +1SD), and overweight/obese (BAZ > + 1SD) based on de Onis et al. [25].

Cognitive function assessment

Overall cognitive function was assessed using the standardised National Institutes of Health Toolbox (NIH Toolbox) Cognition Battery administered on iPads by trained interviewers. The assessment included the fluid cognition composite score and five subdomains: executive function, attention, processing speed, working memory, and episodic memory [26–28].

The NIH-TB has been validated across diverse populations in the United States but has not been formally validated among adolescent girls in sub-Saharan Africa. This tool was selected based on its non-verbal components, which reduce language-related bias, its tablet-based administration that minimises literacy requirements, and its standardised protocols, all of which are appropriate for adolescents. The cognition battery includes five tests assessing fluid cognition: the Dimensional Change Card Sort, Flanker Inhibitory Control and Attention, Pattern Comparison Processing Speed, List Sorting Working Memory, and Picture Sequence Memory tests [27, 28]. Fluid cognition involves reasoning, abstract thinking, and problem-solving in novel situations. It comprises five interrelated subdomains: executive function, attention, processing speed, working memory, and episodic memory [26, 29]. The Episodic Memory test included Picture Sequence Memory Tests A, B, and C. In these tests, the stimuli are pictured objects and related activities that are not ordered. Pictures were displayed on the screen and arranged in a fixed order, with their contents described by an accompanying audio file. The participant moved the pictures in sequence as displayed earlier on the screen. The fully corrected T-score determines the number of paired pictures correctly remembered over the learning trial [30]. The working memory test included the List Sorting Working Memory Test, which uses both visual and oral object displays. The participants were asked to order the objects by size, from smallest to biggest. Two different categories of animals and foods were displayed, and participants responded to all. This test lasted approximately 7 min, and the score was calculated as the number of correct items across all categories, recorded at the end [31].

Furthermore, the Flanker Inhibitory Control Attention Test assessed the attention dimension of cognitive function. The test measures participants' ability to pay attention to the visual dimensions of objects. In each trial, a central directional object was flanked to the left or right, and the task was to identify the direction of the central object. The total composite score is generated after 40 trials and expressed in percentages [30].

We also assessed executive functioning with the Dimensional Change Card Sort Test. Participants were given a mixed block of trials with critical and relevant dimensions. Here, the appropriate criterion word was ‘colour’ or ‘shape’, and this was displayed on the iPad screen [31]. Participants chose pictures with the same shape or colour as the picture displayed on the central screen of the iPad [31]. The total composite score was generated after 40 trials and expressed as a percentage [30]. Additionally, the girls' processing speed was assessed using the Pattern Comparison Processing Speed test. The study participants were asked to identify different visual patterns and tell whether they were identical. This was assessed by clicking the ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ button. This test required 3 to 7 min to execute, and the number of correct items was recorded as the score, expressed as a composite score percentage [26, 31]. The fluid cognition was calculated using the fully corrected T-score average, automatically generated at the end of each test. The fully corrected T-score is a composite test score that adjusts for possible confounding factors, such as age, race, and ethnicity, during the standardised test [30]. Generally, scores are standardised T-scores with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. Since our subjects were generally from rural settings, we recognised that they might have limited access to computer games, but the trial test somewhat helped them become more familiar with them.

Academic performance assessment

The academic performance of the study participants was assessed using the examination score from the continuous assessment booklet provided by the school headteacher for the 2017/2018 academic year. The evaluation of academic performance was based on cumulative grades for three core study subjects: English Language, Mathematics, and General Science. These subjects form the basic components of academic training in primary schools in Ghana. The average score for each of the three core subjects for the academic year was computed and used to assess the girls' academic performance. The continuous assessment booklet also captured the data on class and school attendance. School attendance was expressed as a percentage of the total number of school days for the academic year.

Measurement of other covariates

Girl-level

The analysis included variables such as the girl's age, class, school attendance, menarche status (pre- or post-menarche), dietary diversity, and subjective health complaints. Dietary diversity was measured using the minimum dietary diversity for women (MDD-W) metric through a 24-h recall method [32]. Participants reported food consumption from the previous day, including snacks, drinks, and fruits. Foods were categorised into MDD-W groups, with a score of 1 assigned for any consumption within a group. The total dietary diversity score (DDS) ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater dietary diversity. The data included the girl's age in years, class (categorical), school attendance (continuous), menarche status (pre- or post-menarche) based on recall, dietary diversity, and subjective health complaints.

Subjective health complaints among the girls were evaluated using the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) symptoms checklist [33]. The checklist, which covers a one-month recall period, measures a range of symptoms, including headaches, stomach aches, backaches, feeling low, irritability, nervousness, sleeping difficulties, and dizziness. Scores on the HBSC range from 0 to 4, with 0 indicating rare complaints, 1 indicating monthly complaints, 2 indicating weekly complaints, 3 indicating more than once a week, and 4 indicating daily complaints during the past six months. A summated health complaints score was generated for each participant, with a possible range of 0 to 32 [33]. Higher cumulative scores indicated a greater frequency and severity of health complaints among the adolescent girls.

Household-level covariates

Household food insecurity was assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), consisting of 8 yes/no questions [34]. Each affirmative response was assigned 1 point, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 8. The score was used to classify households into four levels: food secure (0 points), mildly food insecure (1–3 points), moderately food insecure (4–6 points), and severely food insecure (7–8 points), with higher scores indicating greater severity of food insecurity. In the analysis, we re-grouped household food security into food-secure and food-insecure.

The International Wealth Index [35], ranging from a minimum score of 25 to a maximum of 100, assessed household socioeconomic status based on possession of durable goods, access to basic amenities, and housing characteristics. A household roster was used to compute dependency and literacy ratios per Ghana Statistical Service guidelines [3]. The dependency ratio measured the proportion of dependents (ages 0–14 and 65+) to the working-age population (15–64), indicating economic burden. The literacy ratio, representing literate versus non-literate household members, reflected educational attainment. Data on parental education, literacy, and occupation were collected but not analysed due to the minimal variation observed.

Other covariates

The availability of the Ghana School Feeding Programme was documented as either present or absent, and girls’ participation was assessed using a single-item questionnaire. A categorical variable was also included to account for potential interviewer effects based on the field research assistant conducting the assessment.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and analysed using SPSS version 23. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion, whereby only participants with complete data on all key variables, cognitive performance, academic performance, and nutritional status were retained in the analysis. Girls with missing data on any of these variables (n = 212) were excluded, resulting in a final analytical sample of 845 participants. This approach is appropriate under the assumption that data were missing at random and that the excluded cases did not differ systematically from the included cases. The normality of the data distribution was checked using histograms with normal distribution curves and Q-Q plots. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene's test, and residuals were inspected for independence and homoscedasticity. Where assumptions were violated, appropriate transformations or non-parametric alternatives were applied.

The continuous variables demonstrated approximately normal distribution on visual inspection, confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk test results (p > 0.05), supporting the use of parametric statistical methods. Descriptive statistics of the continuous data were presented as means and standard deviations, while categorical data were presented as percentages and frequencies. We used generalised linear mixed-effects analysis with a maximum likelihood ratio to examine the association between nutritional status and cognition/academic performance. In the analysis, we accounted for the study design effect by including the school as a random intercept. Nutritional status indices (BAZ and HAZ) were the primary exposures, while cognitive and academic performance, including sub-scales, were continuous dependent variables. We adjusted for possible confounding variables by developing four different hierarchical statistical models. The potential confounding variables were chosen a priori based on the literature [17–19]. The first model was crude and included only the independent variable and study design. Model 2 adjusted for girl-level factors (age, anaemia, menarche status, DDS, school attendance, class, and health complaints), accounting for psychological and educational differences that may confound cognition and academic performance outcomes. Model 3 additionally adjusted for household-level factors, including food security, wealth index, and household dependency and literacy ratios. Model 4 was further adjusted to account for the presence of the GSFP (Ghana School Feeding Programme) in the school (dichotomous variable) and the interviewer effect. The association measured was the regression coefficient. The association measured was considered statistically significant if the 95% confidence interval did not include zero or if the P-value was ≤ 0.05. Given that the analyses were based on prespecified hypotheses examining associations between nutritional status indicators and cognitive outcomes, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Therefore, findings with borderline statistical significance should be interpreted cautiously.

Results

Socio-demographic and household characteristics of the girls

The population analysed comprised a total of 845 girls aged 10–17 years, with a mean age of 12.3 ± 1.8 years (Table 1). The majority (80.5%) of the girls were pre-menarche, and the majority identified as Muslims (67.3%) and belonged to the Dagomba ethnic group (66.4%). The mean DDS was 6.0 ± 1.2 out of 10, while the mean health complaint score was 3.6 ± 3.6, out of a maximum possible score of 32.

Covariates associated with cognitive performance

Outside nutritional status indicators, several covariates had significant associations with cognitive outcome variables in the fully adjusted models. Age proved to be a significant predictor throughout, showing an inverse correlation with both fluid cognition (β = −1.34, 95% CI: −1.75, −0.94) and processing speed (β = −0.78, 95% CI: −1.24, −0.32) in the BAZ model (Supplementary Table 1). Anaemia was indeed found to significantly correlate with fluid cognition scores (β = −1.43 with 95% CI: −2.66, −0.21 for HAZ model; β = −1.33 with 95% CI: −2.52, −0.14 for BAZ model), although it did not achieve statistical significance for processing speed. The strong gradient in academic class level across both cognitive domains suggested a downward effect, whereby girls in the lower classes scored significantly lower than those in the reference group (Class 6), with the effect declining across higher levels. The effect of the interviewer was again prominent, particularly for Interviewer 1, who recorded substantially higher scores for fluid cognition compared with the reference interviewer (β = 6.01, 95% CI: 4.30, 7.72 in the BAZ model; β = 5.79, 95% CI: 4.03, 7.57 in the HAZ model). Household-level factors such as wealth index, dependency ratio, food security, and literacy ratio showed no significant associations with either cognitive outcome in the final models (Supplementary Table 1 & 2).

Covariates associated with academic performance

The academic performance revealed a distinct pattern of covariate associations compared with the measures of cognitive functioning (Supplementary Tables 3 & 4). No nutritional status indicators (BAZ and HAZ) were significantly associated with any of the models, including the final adjusted Model 4, for composite academic performance, general science, mathematics, and the English language. The same was true for the majority of covariates analysed, which generally showed no statistically meaningful relationships with academic performance. However, school attendance and the household literacy ratio were consistently associated with overall academic performance in both adjusted models for HAZ and BAZ.

Discussion

The present study examined the association between nutritional status and overall cognitive function, measured using the fluid cognition composite score and its subdomains—executive function, attention control, processing speed, working memory, and episodic memory—among adolescent girls in the Mion District of Ghana. In addition, the study assessed the relationship between nutritional status and academic performance among the adolescent girls. Our findings indicate a significant positive association between BAZ and fluid cognition, as well as processing speed, in both crude and adjusted models. While BAZ initially showed a positive association with working memory, this relationship weakened after adjusting for confounders. Similarly, HAZ was positively associated with multiple cognitive domains in crude analyses, but these associations diminished after adjustment, with only processing speed remaining significant. No significant associations were found between anthropometric indices and academic performance, even after adjusting for potential confounders. These results highlight that nutritional status is associated with selected cognitive domains, but suggest a weaker link with academic performance.

The prevalence of underweight aligns with findings from urban Tamale, Northern Ghana [36], while the overweight/obesity rate was lower, likely due to urbanisation and increased access to processed foods in Tamale. The results of this study provide a comprehensive overview of adolescent girls’ cognitive function using the NIH-TB Cognition Battery on iPads. This is a standardised and validated tool widely used to quantify function across a broad age range and diverse backgrounds, including adolescents in low- to middle-income countries. Although fluid cognition was found to be low (i.e., 26.7± 9.6) compared to other studies elsewhere [37], it falls within the range when juxtaposed with studies within the Ghanaian context [8]. The observed positive associations between BAZ and fluid cognition and processing speed, even after adjusting for potential confounders, suggest that nutritional status may influence cognitive performance among adolescents. Adequate energy availability and body reserves support neurodevelopment and cognitive efficiency, particularly in tasks that require rapid information processing [38]. These findings align with previous studies indicating that higher BAZ within a healthy range may be linked to improved cognitive outcomes, possibly due to better metabolic reserves supporting brain function [39]. However, the attenuation of associations in working memory after adjustment suggests that factors such as socioeconomic status, physical activity, or diet quality may also play a role, warranting further investigation. Participation in the Ghana School Feeding Programme was associated with positive but non-significant cognitive outcomes. This programme provides nutrient-rich meals that may support brain development by improving energy and micronutrient intake [40, 41]. Strengthening such programmes could help enhance adolescent cognition, but further longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these effects. Anaemia was significantly linked to lower fluid cognition and processing speed. Common causes include iron deficiency and infections, which impair oxygen delivery and brain function critical for cognition [42]. Addressing anaemia through supplementation and improved nutrition is crucial for supporting cognitive development.

The observed positive associations between HAZ and multiple cognitive domains in crude models align with existing evidence on the critical role of early-life growth in cognitive development [43]. Findings from the Young Lives Cohort further demonstrate that linear growth retardation in adolescence is associated with poorer cognitive skills and educational performance [10–12]. Stunting has been linked to long-term cognitive deficits, likely due to the adverse effects of early-life malnutrition on brain development. Stunting due to protein-energy malnutrition affects brain development, reduced IGF-1 triggers inflammatory processes that extend beyond the effects of individual micronutrients [44]. However, after adjusting for potential confounders, most associations were attenuated, with only processing speed remaining significant. This suggests that height may function as a proxy for broader socio-environmental factors, such as household wealth or dietary diversity. These findings underscore the importance of combining early childhood nutrition interventions with strategies that address social determinants to promote cognitive development and academic potential.

Several plausible reasons exist for why enhanced linear growth may be associated with improved cognitive function. The observed association between linear growth or BAZ and cognitive function may reflect the broader influence of overall nutritional adequacy on both physical and brain development, even though specific micronutrient status was not assessed in this study. Growth-related nutrients such as iron, zinc, iodine, folate, and vitamins A and D are known to play key roles in physiological processes that support both somatic and neural development [45]. Adequate nutrition during critical developmental periods promotes cell growth, energy metabolism, and neurodevelopmental processes such as myelination, synaptogenesis, and neurotransmitter synthesis, which collectively contribute to cognitive performance [46–48]. Therefore, the relationship observed in this study may reflect the cumulative effects of adequate nutrition and health status on both linear growth and cognitive outcomes, rather than the influence of specific micronutrients alone [47, 49].

In this study, the mean academic performance was 53.8 ± 13.1%, below the international proficiency standard of > 70% [50]. However, it is just on the borderline for the Ghana National Examination Assessment proficiency cut-off point of > 55% [51, 52], indicating that academic performance in this cohort is slightly below the expected national threshold. The academic performance is similar to that of a study in Coastal Ghana [53] and Nigeria [54]. The availability of teaching and learning materials may account for differences in academic performance across geographic locations, as observed in this study and elsewhere. In low- and middle-income countries, for instance, where teaching and learning materials may not be readily available, students' performance, measured as mean scores in English, Mathematics, and Science, is often low [55].

While previous studies have reported positive associations between anthropometric indices and academic performance [13, 56, 57], no such relationship was observed in this study. Some studies suggest that cognitive function and academic performance are distinct constructs influenced by different factors [58, 59]. Overall, this finding suggests that educational and environmental factors may be more associated with academic achievement than biological determinants. Broader educational and home environments, including teaching quality, pedagogy, classroom resources, social interactions, and school infrastructure, may play a more substantial role in shaping academic outcomes, however, these factors were not systematically assessed in the present study. Additionally, the skills assessed in academic settings may be more complex or distinct from those directly affected by nutritional status, warranting further investigation.

We considered several cognitive assessment tools, including the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI-II) [60, 61], the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB) [62, 63] and the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery (NIH-TCB) [26–28], in consultation with in-house psychologists. The NIH-TCB was selected due to its ease of administration and lower cultural sensitivity. Although cognitive measures require careful cultural adaptation for validity, certain domains, such as processing speed and attention, are less dependent on linguistic or contextual familiarity [64]. The automated nature of the NIH-TCB trials minimised biases related to task familiarity, allowing participants to practice before the assessment. Additionally, interviewer assistance reduced potential screen-interaction-related biases, especially for participants unfamiliar with the interface. We controlled for any remaining bias in the statistical analysis by considering the interviewer's role as a potential confounder.

Importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; longitudinal studies are needed to assess how changes in nutritional status influence cognitive and academic outcomes among rural adolescent girls. Although anthropometric measures are widely accepted as valid indicators of chronic nutritional status in population research, they cannot capture acute nutritional deficiencies or specific micronutrient inadequacies, which may affect cognitive function through distinct pathways. Future studies, including biomarker assessments, would provide further evidence on micronutrient status and cognitive mechanisms. Additionally, the lack of locally validated normative data for Ghanaian adolescents limits the interpretation of their cognitive scores. Caution is thus needed when classifying fluid cognition and scores from other cognitive domains, as these may not reflect the cognitive functioning domain within the local cultural and educational context. Future research endeavours must focus on the validation and adaptation of cognitive tools to improve their pertinence and accuracy in sub-Saharan African contexts. The present study may have been underpowered to detect differences in educational outcomes after excluding incomplete data. Our null findings regarding educational performance should also be considered within the broader educational context. Given that the study population comprised school-going adolescent girls in a rural setting, the findings are primarily generalisable to in-school adolescent girls in rural northern Ghana and comparable contexts.

Conclusion

Nutritional status was positively associated with the fluid cognition composite score and processing speed among adolescent girls, but not with academic performance. These findings underscore the importance of improving adolescent nutrition to support cognitive development in rural settings.
