Double volcanic tracks at Hawaii caused by bridgmanite-enriched primordial mantle blobs
Authors: Hao Liu, Xin Deng, Wei Leng, Zhongqing Wu, Risheng Chu, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mantle plumes, rising from Earth’s deep interior, are expected to produce a single age-progressive hotspot track. However, Hawaiian volcanoes younger than ~7 million years form two subparallel volcanic chains, the Loa and Kea trends. The mechanism behind this double-track volcanism and its connection to deep mantle reservoirs remain unresolved. Here we show that bridgmanite-enriched primordial mantle blobs (BPMBs) with intrinsic viscosities 10–30 times higher than the ambient mantle can generate bilaterally zoned plumes using three-dimensional thermochemical mantle plume models. Enhanced viscous heating within BPMBs raises plume temperatures asymmetrically, reproducing observed contrasts in isotopic composition, plume temperature, melt flux, and seismic velocity between the Loa and Kea trends. The global occurrence of similar double-track volcanism suggests that BPMBs are scattered within large low velocity provinces (LLVPs), likely remnants of an ancient basal magma ocean. Our results provide a mechanism for double-track volcanism and constraints on the structure and composition of LLVPs.

Introduction

Classical mantle plume model posits that plumes originate from the core–mantle boundary (CMB) or the margins of large low velocity provinces (LLVPs), and predicts a single age-progressive volcanic chain on the Earth’s surface as a tectonic plate moves over a relatively stationary plume tail1–3. However, over the past ~5–7 million years (Myr), volcanoes along the Hawaiian chain have manifested as two subparallel volcanic tracks on the basis of their locations (Fig. 1a), called Loa-Kea tracks4–6. Indeed, double hotspot tracks have been reported at multiple hotspots in both the Pacific and Atlantic, and their duration varies from several million years to tens of million years7,8. For example, the geochemical zonation of the Galapagos and Tristan-Gough hotspot tracks can be traced back to ~20 million years ago (Ma) and ~70–100 Ma, respectively8,9. The detailed geochemical differences between the Loa and Kea tracks are well documented. Basalts exposed on the Loa trend have higher values of 87Sr/86Sr, 187Os/188Os, 3He/4He and 208Pb*/206Pb*, where 208Pb*/206Pb* represents the time-integrated 232Th/238U ratio since the formation of the Earth and is defined as: 208Pb*/206Pb* = (208Pb/204Pb-29.475) /(206Pb/204Pb-9.306), and lower values of 143Nd/144Nd and La/Yb than those on the Kea trend4–6,10,11 (Fig. 1b, c). The gradual transition from Kea-dominated to Loa-dominated compositions along the Hawaiian–Emperor chain began to appear in Kauai lavas6 around 5–7 Ma (Fig. 1a, b). Besides, the La/Yb ratio is regarded as a tracer of melt fraction since La is incompatible and varies inversely with the melt fraction. Thus, the melt fractions are higher in the Loa trend relative to the Kea trend11 (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, the asymmetry in plume potential temperature on the Loa-Kea tracks has been observed. The estimated temperature from the Loa trend is ~100 K higher than that in the Kea trend12–17 (Fig. 1d). In addition to geochemical observations, the obvious differences in geophysics are also observed on both sides. Over the past ~5–7 Ma, the volume flux along the Hawaii island chain18–20 increases by a factor of ~2 (Fig. 1d). On the other hand, a high-resolution regional tomographic model reveals an asymmetric shear-wave velocity (Vs) structure beneath the Hawaii Islands, with lower-velocity anomalies preferentially occurring beneath the Loa track. Specifically, Vs anomalies are about –1 to –2% beneath the Kea track, whereas they reach –2 to –4% beneath the Loa track21–23 (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). This asymmetry extends into the mantle transition zone as well24, and is also reflected, to some extent, in the asymmetric surface topography25. The observed variations in melt fraction and Vs most likely reflect differences in temperature between the Loa and Kea tracks, which may in turn be influenced by chemical heterogeneities within the ascending mantle plume.

Generally, three leading hypotheses have been proposed to explain the isotopic anomalies (e.g., Pb and Nb) observed in double volcanic tracks. The first attributes their formation to shallow-mantle processes, such as variations in lithospheric thickness or plate motion26,27. However, these models fail to simultaneously reproduce the observed isotopic, thermal, and melting asymmetries between the Loa and Kea trends, as well as the limited duration of the double tracks5–7,11, indicating that lithospheric effects alone cannot account for the systematic bilateral zonation expressed at the surface. The second hypothesis invokes two distinct mantle plumes rising from the lower mantle beneath Hawaii28–30. This model, however, lacks support from seismic observations and cannot explain why volcanism between ~80 and 7 Ma is dominated by basalts with Kea-trend compositions, which point toward the ambient lower Pacific mantle source4–6,31,32. An alternative and conceptually appealing hypothesis proposes a single, bilaterally zoned mantle plume that transports chemical heterogeneities inherited from the LLVPs to the surface4–8. Although several geodynamic models support this idea33–35, they commonly neglect key physical parameters of deep-mantle heterogeneities, particularly intrinsic density and viscosity contrasts. When density effects are explicitly considered, numerical models tend to produce concentrically rather than bilaterally zoned plumes36. A subsequent modification invoking slab–LLVP–plume interactions has been proposed to recover plume asymmetry37, however, this mechanism does not reproduce key characteristics of the Hawaiian plume and predicts bilateral zonation for only a limited number of hotspots (e.g., Galápagos, Easter, and Azores).

Moreover, most existing models focus solely on isotopic asymmetry (i.e., variations in chemical composition) while neglecting other key geochemical and geophysical observations highlighted in this study (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1), including the asymmetries in melt fraction, plume temperature, seismic wave velocities from the asthenosphere to the mantle transition zone, and the dramatic increase in volcanic flux at Hawaii over the past ~5–7 Ma. Therefore, the central scientific question becomes whether a mantle plume can develop a bilaterally zoned structure while transporting high-density chemical heterogeneities upward, which specific components within the LLVPs, such as primordial mantle materials, subducted oceanic crust, or ultralow-velocity zones (ULVZs), are responsible for the associated geochemical and geophysical signals, and what physical mechanisms regulate the observed surface features. The widespread occurrence of double volcanic tracks around the world may thus offer crucial insights into the intrinsic nature and internal structure of the LLVPs, which remain among the most enigmatic features of Earth’s deep mantle.

In this work, in order to explain these geochemical and geophysical observations at Hawaii, we further explore the key factors in controlling the formation of a bilaterally zoned mantle plume in detail, and link the surface observations relevant to the double volcanic tracks to the deep-mantle chemical heterogeneities within the LLVPs. Finally, we propose that bridgmanite-enriched primordial mantle blobs (BPMBs) with high-viscosity property dominate the formation of the bilaterally zoned mantle plume and regulate the surface observations at Hawaii. The identified double-track volcanism across the globe likely reflects that the BPMBs with intrinsic viscosities 10–30 times higher than the ambient mantle, as a significant component, are dispersed in the LLVPs.

Results

Model setup

To investigate the formation mechanism of the double volcanic tracks and how the deep-mantle chemical heterogeneities regulate the surface observations, we perform a suite of high-resolution three-dimensional (3-D) thermochemical mantle plume models in a regional spherical geometry using CitcomCU, along with incompressible fluids and an extended Boussinesq approximation including adiabatic heating, viscous heating, and latent heating (see “Methods” section)38,39. Meanwhile, temperature-, depth- and composition-dependent viscosity and major phase changes in the mantle transition zone are considered (see “Methods” section, Supplementary Fig. 2a). Two compositional components are simulated by the tracer ratio method40, which helps us easily incorporate the chemical heterogeneities with intrinsic density and viscosity contrasts with respect to the ambient mantle into our model (see “Methods” section). Considering that the current Hawaiian mantle plume is a mature system, active for at least 80 Myr and extending nearly 6000 km, and the majority of volcanoes between ~80 and 7 Ma form a single volcanic track and display Kea-like isotopic signatures similar to ambient lower Pacific mantle component4–6,31,32, we first initialize a purely thermal mantle plume rising from the CMB. The moment it reaches the base of the lithosphere is defined as modeling time 0 Myr, from which all subsequent models are initiated (Supplementary Fig. 2b). We then impose a nominal horizontal velocity of 7.5 cm/yr across the entire model surface to simulate Pacific plate motion, and introduce a chemical blob near the plume root on the Loa side to represent LLVP-derived material (Supplementary Fig. 2b; see “Methods” section for details). This setup enables us to explore how a single hotspot track can evolve into a double volcanic chain. When the chemical anomalies reach the base of the lithosphere, we define this time as ~7 Ma in geological time, which provides a convenient benchmark for comparison with geological observations. The melt fraction is calculated using an anhydrous melting formulation41 (Supplementary Fig. 3), while the melt production rate for each element is obtained using the Eulerian method42 (see “Methods” section). The intrinsic viscosity and density excess of the chemical heterogeneities are poorly known; thus, we vary these physical properties to investigate their influences on the formation of a bilaterally zoned plume. Seismic studies have revealed asymmetric low-velocity anomalies beneath the Hawaiian Islands21–25. Thus, we also explore the effects of different chemical heterogeneities, specifically primordial mantle materials and oceanic crust, along with temperature, on the Vs anomalies within the mantle plume in the upper mantle based on mineral elasticity data (see “Methods” section). The plausible compositions of the primordial mantle materials and oceanic crust, as well as the elastic properties of mantle minerals, are derived from previous experimental and theoretical studies (see “Methods” section).

Origin of Hawaiian double volcanic tracks

We first show a reference model with a chemical blob having a density contrast of 2% and a viscosity increase of 10 times relative to the ambient mantle (Supplementary Fig. 2b, Case 1). When the chemical anomalies are about to reach the bottom of the lithosphere, we define this time as 7.13 Ma (modeling time is 60.38 Myr) (Fig. 2a). The temperature of the mantle plume has changed from uniform distribution to asymmetric distribution at 140 km depth as the chemical anomalies are bilaterally zoned within the mantle plume ~7 Myr later (Fig. 2a–f). Besides, this phenomenon recurs at varying depths as the chemical anomalies propagate upward on the Loa side (Supplementary Fig. 4). For comparison, the temporal evolution of a purely thermal mantle plume is shown in Supplementary Fig. 5, where the temperature at different depths does not vary with time (Case 1.1). The increase in the mantle plume temperature on the Loa side is attributed to the additional viscous heating generated by the high-viscosity chemical anomalies43, while the strain rate remains relatively unchanged (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). An increase in mantle viscosity by a factor of 10 or 30 leads to an elevation of its temperature by approximately 100 °C and 300 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8, Cases 1, 1.1, and 1.2). Meanwhile, the asymmetric distribution of the mantle plume temperature also leads to the asymmetry in melting degree beneath the lithosphere (Fig. 2g–i). We further calculate the variations of the averaged potential temperature on both sides of the mantle plume over time. The potential temperature on the Kea trend remains invariant while that on the Loa trend significantly increases from ~1550 °C to ~1650 °C (Fig. 2j). Thus, the high-viscosity chemical anomalies derived from the deep Earth not only result in the bilaterally zoned isotopic anomalies observed on the surface, but also control the asymmetric distributions of mantle plume temperature and melting degree beneath the lithosphere. Simultaneously, the increase of the mantle plume temperature through time also dominates the increase in volcanic flux along the Hawaiian hotspot track over the past ~5–7 Myr (Fig. 2k). Given the large uncertainty in the intrinsic viscosity of deep-mantle chemical heterogeneities, particularly during their ascent into the upper mantle, we introduce an end-member model, Case 1.3, in which the chemical anomalies are assigned a 30-fold viscosity increase in the lower mantle, which is removed upon entering the upper mantle, allowing us to examine the potential influence of strong lower-mantle heterogeneities on plume dynamics while minimizing their effect in the upper mantle. This configuration also reproduces the observed asymmetries in isotopic anomalies, plume temperature, and volcanic flux at Hawaii by generating a bilaterally zoned plume and enhancing the Loa-side temperature anomaly (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). This outcome arises because the high-viscosity chemical anomalies substantially increase the plume temperature on the Loa side in the uppermost lower mantle, after which the plume ascends along its adiabatic gradient. Furthermore, we also consider a large volume of chemical anomalies with depth-dependent density variations, represented as recycled ancient oceanic crust44, which either exhibit no intrinsic viscosity reduction (Case 1C) or a 10-fold reduction (Case 2C) relative to the surrounding mantle. Our results (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13) demonstrate that these dense chemical anomalies preferentially accumulate beneath the Kea side, as their high density and weak rheology enhance lateral migration within the mantle plume, and their entrainment significantly reduces plume temperature, particularly beneath the Loa side. This thermal effect reflects the fact that the plume follows a constant adiabatic gradient, whereas the presence of dense chemical anomalies depresses the temperature at the plume root, thereby lowering the overall thermal state of the plume (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).

Additionally, we examine the impact of the chemical anomalies on Vs heterogeneities within the mantle plume in the upper mantle based on mineral elasticity data (see “Methods” section). The mechanical mixture of primordial and pyrolitic mantle lowers Vs within the plume. For instance, an excess of 10% primordial mantle materials can reduce Vs by 1.5% at 1800 °C (Fig. 2l). The effect is more pronounced at higher proportions of primordial mantle materials (40%), leading to lower Vs (Fig. 2l). In contrast, excess oceanic crust has the opposite effect by increasing Vs at temperatures below 1800 °C (Fig. 2l). Consequently, the lower Vs structure beneath the Loa side from the asthenosphere to the mantle transition zone may result from the contributions of primordial mantle materials and residual melt.

Formation of bilaterally zoned plumes

Finally, we investigate the required conditions for shaping a concentrically or bilaterally zoned plume comprehensively. Our simulations, which include 36 cases, reveal three distinct model types. In type I (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 14, Case 2), the chemical anomalies with high density and low viscosity preferentially accumulate on the Kea side, resulting in a bilaterally zoned plume. In type II (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 15, Case 3), the chemical anomalies characterized solely by high density relative to the ambient mantle give rise to a concentrically zoned plume. In type III (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 16, Case 4), the chemical anomalies with both high-density and high-viscosity concentrate on the Loa side, generating asymmetric viscous heating, elevated temperatures, and a stable bilaterally zoned plume. This regime provides the missing dynamical mechanism required to explain the Hawaiian double volcanic tracks. The formation of a concentrically or bilaterally zoned plume strongly depends on the physical properties of the chemical anomalies and their distance from the plume root at the CMB (Fig. 3d–g). Specifically, the chemical anomalies with lower intrinsic density excess, higher viscosity, or located farther from the center of the plume at the base of the mantle play a crucial role in promoting the formation of the anticipated bilaterally zoned (Hawaii-type) plume (Fig. 3d, e, Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18, Cases 1–18). In contrast, the width (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 19, Cases 19–27) and height (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 20, Cases 28–36) of the chemical blob exert only a minor influence on plume zonation. Discussion

The source region of the Hawaiian mantle plume is widely considered to be structurally, physically, and compositionally complex45. Global mantle convection models spanning a wide range of plate motion histories (from ~1 Ga to ~120 Ma) consistently show that slab subduction drives large-scale mantle circulation, which redistributes dense thermochemical materials at the CMB and promotes their aggregation into two major thermochemical piles beneath Africa and the Pacific46–49. Within this framework, the Pacific LLVP is inferred to have developed a coherent large-scale structure by ~200–300 Ma in models incorporating long-term plate evolution, and has since been continuously modified by ongoing subduction46,47. As a result, it remains surrounded by ambient mantle and subducted slabs, consistent with seismic tomography48–50. These results demonstrate that dense thermochemical materials (e.g., primordial mantle materials) are not randomly distributed throughout the mantle, but are preferentially concentrated within LLVPs. Notably, the northern margin of the Pacific LLVP has been strongly modified by subduction-driven mantle flow over the past ~200 Ma46–49. Oceanward migration of subduction zones around the North Pacific generates a coherent large-scale mantle flow field, in which southeastward flow beneath the northwest Pacific converges with southwestward flow from the northeast Pacific, producing a net southward flow. This flow deforms the northern boundary of the LLVP and is likely linked to the initiation of the Hawaiian plume30,49. Recent global mantle convection models further suggest that the Hawaiian plume root entrains LLVP-derived materials from the south and ambient mantle from the north30. This asymmetric sourcing provides a geodynamically consistent basis for our model framework, in which the plume root is compositionally zoned: the southern or Loa side preferentially samples thermochemical materials from the Pacific LLVP, whereas the northern or Kea side samples the surrounding mantle. Importantly, this asymmetry is not imposed arbitrarily, but reflects first-order mantle flow patterns shaped by Pacific subduction history. Such a configuration naturally explains the formation of two geochemically distinct volcanic tracks at the surface.

However, which specific components within the LLVPs control this geological process is still a matter of debate. Hypotheses with regard to the constitutions of the LLVPs involve recycled ancient oceanic crust51,52, primordial mantle materials53,54, or a combination of the two55 (Fig. 4). Additionally, ULVZs are typically located within or in proximity to the LLVPs56,57 (Fig. 4). Thus, the oceanic crust and ULVZs have been considered as the likely candidates contributing to the isotopic anomalies on the Loa side5–7,11,37. Nevertheless, the density of subducted oceanic crust is estimated to be approximately 4% denser than the surrounding mantle44, while recent high-temperature and high-pressure mineralogical studies suggest that the viscosity of the oceanic crust may be viscously weak throughout the lower mantle due to the existence of cubic CaSiO3 perovskite and CaCl2-type silica58,59. Our results show that most models incorporating chemical anomalies with high density and low viscosity predict a decrease in mantle plume temperature (Supplementary Fig. 21). In particular, our models (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13, Cases 1C and 2C), considering the chemical anomalies as the recycled oceanic crust, show that: (1) the dense oceanic crust preferentially accumulates beneath the Kea side, contrary to the geochemical constraints (Fig. 1); (2) its entrainment significantly lowers the plume temperature, particularly beneath the Loa side, also inconsistent with the geochemical evidence (Fig. 1); and (3) the resulting low-temperature mantle plume containing entrained dense oceanic crust produces high-velocity anomalies within the plume, which conflict with the seismological observations (Figs. 1 and 2). Taken together, these results indicate that recycled oceanic crust is unlikely to be responsible for the formation of the Hawaiian double volcanic tracks. Furthermore, ULVZs with a significant reduction in seismic velocity (~5–30%) can be tens of kilometers thick and hundreds of kilometers wide (Fig. 4), with density increases of up to ~10–30% relative to the ambient mantle57. It has been interpreted as partial melting, subducted oceanic crust, or solid iron-enriched assemblages57,60,61. Specifically, magnesiowüstite (iron-rich (Mg,Fe)O) residuum from magma ocean crystallization in the early Earth has been regarded as the most plausible candidate for explaining ULVZs, as even a small fraction can greatly reduce the average sound velocity of an assemblage62,63. Modeling viscosity of (Mg,Fe)O indicates that magnesiowüstite is 10 orders of magnitude weaker than ferropericlase64. Besides, the topography of ULVZs also suggests that their average viscosity is about 2 orders of magnitude lower than that of the ambient mantle65. Based on our modeling results (Fig. 3), both higher density and lower viscosity are unfavorable for shaping a Hawaiian-type bilaterally zoned plume. Therefore, ULVZs appear to be insufficient in explaining the formation of double volcanic tracks.

Here, we propose that BPMBs, characterized by intrinsically high viscosity and dispersed within the LLVPs, play a fundamental role in generating bilaterally zoned mantle plumes and regulating the associated surface expressions (Fig. 4). This interpretation is independently supported by the systematically higher maximum 3He/4He ratios on the Loa side relative to the Kea side (Fig. 1b), as high 3He/4He reservoirs are predicted to be Fe-rich, dense, and potentially derived from differentiation of a deep magma ocean66,67. The markedly elevated viscosity of BPMBs, estimated to be ~10–1000 times higher than that of the surrounding mantle, is most plausibly attributed to their enhanced bridgmanite content68–73. Independent constraints on lower-mantle composition further support this interpretation, indicating that the lower mantle is enriched in silicon relative to the upper mantle and that LLVPs contain higher proportions of bridgmanite (by ~6–20%) and exhibit modest intrinsic density anomalies (~1%) compared to the ambient mantle74–77. Crucially, however, if the LLVPs were coherent, compositionally homogeneous, bridgmanite-enriched bodies with uniformly high viscosity, several clear and testable predictions would follow. Because most major mantle plumes originate from LLVP margins3, such a configuration would imply that long-lived plumes should continuously entrain viscous LLVP material and transport it to the surface. In turn, dual volcanic tracks or Loa-type-enriched geochemical signatures should be a common and persistent feature along hotspot chains. This prediction is inconsistent with geological and geochemical observations. Along the Hawaiian–Emperor chain, the majority of volcanoes between ~80 and 7 Ma form a single volcanic track and display Kea-like isotopic signatures4–7,31,32. Similarly, other long-lived hotspot tracks, such as Louisville78 (~78.8 Ma) and Réunion79 (~60 Ma), do not exhibit persistent double-track volcanism. In contrast, well-documented double-track systems, including Society, Marquesas, Foundation, and Samoa, are temporally restricted, typically lasting only ~2–4 Myr, and are largely confined to the recent geological past7,27. Moreover, if the LLVPs were uniformly high-viscosity bodies, the Hawaiian plume would have persistently entrained such material throughout its evolution, leading to sustained anomalously high plume temperatures. This expectation conflicts with the observed temporal asymmetry in inferred plume temperatures along the Hawaiian–Emperor chain (Fig. 1). Taken together, these constraints indicate that treating the LLVPs as uniformly high-viscosity bodies is insufficient to explain the episodic emergence of double volcanic tracks and the associated geochemical asymmetry. Instead, our results support a model in which the early Hawaiian plume primarily sampled material from margins or interior of the Pacific LLVP that was compositionally similar to the ambient lower Pacific mantle component, producing Kea-like volcanism over most of its history. A bilaterally zoned plume structure developed only after the plume entrained internally dispersed, intrinsically viscous BPMBs, leading to the formation of dual volcanic tracks and Loa-type-enriched surface signatures (Fig. 4). This model is consistent with the spatial and temporal evolution of observed isotopic signatures along the Hawaiian–Emperor system.

As the mantle plume ascends into the upper mantle, BPMBs undergo a phase transition from bridgmanite-enriched to garnet-bearing assemblages. Rheological experiments demonstrate that pure garnetite is considerably stronger than both eclogite and harzburgite under upper-mantle conditions80,81. This suggests that garnetite-enriched assemblages (i.e., primordial mantle materials) may retain high viscosity even in the uppermost mantle. Importantly, such garnetite-enriched assemblages can also contribute to the mafic component observed in Hawaiian magmas, consistent with geochemical evidence4–6,12. Therefore, intrinsically high-viscosity BPMBs dispersed within the LLVPs, when entrained by the mantle plume, fundamentally control the development of bilaterally zoned plume structure and modulate the surface manifestations of the Hawaiian double volcanic tracks through the generation of compositional and thermal asymmetries within the plume (Figs. 2 and 4). Notably, the validity of the model is maintained even if the primordial mantle materials lose their high-viscosity characteristics in the upper mantle (Supplementary Figs. 9–11, Case 1.3). Building on these results, we tentatively propose that the intrinsic viscosity of the primordial mantle blobs could be approximately 10–30 times greater than that of the surrounding mantle, which substantially narrows the plausible viscosity range compared with the much broader estimates of 10–1000 proposed in previous studies68,71,72. This offers an important constraint on deep-mantle rheology and long-term thermochemical convection.

Importantly, high-resolution seismic tomography has revealed that the LLVPs exhibit pronounced multi-scale heterogeneities, with localized minima in Vs distributed within them (Supplementary Figs. 22–24)82–89. These low-Vs domains may correspond to BPMBs (Fig. 4), which can reproduce key seismic characteristics of the LLVPs77. Together with the double volcanic tracks reported at multiple hotspots on the Earth’s surface, they also support that the LLVPs are thermochemical and compositionally heterogeneous, internally hosting BPMBs of variable size and spatial distribution (Fig. 4). Although previous 2-D mantle convection models suggest that primordial mantle materials may be preserved as large, high-viscosity piles (thousands of kilometers across) with viscosities 10–1000 times greater than the ambient mantle (bridgmanite-enriched ancient mantle structures, BEAMS)71, our results show that when a plume interacts with such laterally extensive, high-viscosity domains, its excess temperature persists for an unrealistically long duration, resulting in melt production and volcanism far exceeding surface observations. Therefore, the preservation of primordial mantle materials as discrete, internally dispersed BPMBs within the LLVPs, each with viscosities approximately 10–30 times higher than the surrounding mantle, is more consistent with current observational constraints (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 22–24). Such moderate viscosity contrasts, consistent with the enrichment of bridgmanite (~6–20%) within the LLVPs75,77, would limit mixing while retaining the geochemical identities of the primordial mantle domains over geological time68. Future seismic investigations, leveraging expanded observational datasets and advanced imaging techniques, will be essential for resolving the fine-scale internal structure of LLVPs. Such efforts will help constrain the physical state and spatial distribution of these blocks and, when integrated with 3-D geodynamic modeling, will provide critical insight into the processes responsible for generating and sustaining deep-mantle thermochemical heterogeneity.

Finally, large igneous provinces (LIPs) on the surface generated by mantle plume heads exhibit a strong geographic correlation with the LLVPs over the past 320 million years3. If a mantle plume intermittently captures the highly viscous primordial mantle blobs scattered within the LLVPs, periodic eruptions of the LIPs may occur on the surface. In fact, multiple volcanic pulses of the LIPs have been identified, e.g., the Ontong Java plateau with two or three pulses between ∼122 and 90 Ma, respectively90. The multiple volcanic episodes of the LIPs, therefore, likely provide an additional surface expression of scattered BPMBs preserved within the LLVPs (Fig. 4).

In summary, our thermochemical mantle plume model bridges the gap between the surface observations and deep-mantle reservoirs. BPMBs in the Pacific LLVP, characterized by high-viscosity property, regulate the surface observations, including the asymmetries in isotopic anomalies, plume temperature, volcanic flux, and seismic wave velocities at Hawaii by shaping the bilaterally zoned plume and enhancing the plume temperature on the Loa side. The globally observed double-track volcanism likely indicates that BPMBs with intrinsic viscosities 10–30 times higher than that of the ambient mantle, as a significant component, are dispersed within the LLVPs, supporting the idea that the LLVPs may have originated from an ancient basal magma ocean. Thus, our findings provide a framework for understanding how the deep-mantle chemical heterogeneities translate into surface geological features, offering a more comprehensive view of mantle-surface interactions.

Seismic wave velocities of pyrolite, primordial mantle materials, and oceanic crust

Based on direct geological observations of upper-mantle rocks and magmatic processes at high pressures, the pyrolite model was proposed to represent the bulk mineral assemblages of the upper mantle95. At a depth of 300 km, the pyrolite model consists of 60% olivine ((Mg0.92Fe0.08)2SiO4), 20% garnet (Mg1.85Ca0.6Fe0.75Al1.6Si3.2O12), and 20% clinopyroxene (Mg0.9Fe0.1CaSi2O6). Two chemical heterogeneities are explored in this study, including primordial mantle materials and oceanic crust. Previous studies, combining seismic tomography models and mineral elasticity data, have found that the LLVPs should be composed of distinct chemical materials, especially large amounts of iron54,75,77,96,97, suggesting that the LLVPs may originate from an ancient basal magma ocean53,77. The composition of the LLVPs has been constrained by inversion from mineral elasticity and seismic tomography models77. Based on their results, a plausible composition for the primordial mantle materials at a depth of 300 km is 60% olivine ((Mg0.87Fe0.13)2SiO4), 20% garnet (Mg1.85Ca0.6Fe0.75Al1.6Si3.2O12), and 20% clinopyroxene (Mg0.84Fe0.16CaSi2O6). The composition of oceanic crust has also been experimentally constrained98. At 300 km depth, the oceanic crust is composed of 6.1% stishovite (SiO2), 55.1% garnet (Mg1.485Ca0.924Fe0.777Al1.628Si3.186O12), and 38.8% clinopyroxene (Na0.348Mg0.495Fe0.157Ca0.652Al0.348Si2O6).

The elastic properties of stishovite99 (SiO2), pyrope100 (Mg3Al2Si3O12), grossular101 (Ca3Al2Si3O12), almandine102 (Fe3Al2Si3O12), majorite103 (Mg4Si4O12), jadeite104 (NaAlSi2O6), diopside105 ((Mg,Fe)CaSi2O6), and olivine106 ((Mg,Fe)2SiO4) are reported in previous experimental and theoretical studies.
