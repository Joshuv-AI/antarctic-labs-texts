The development and validation of the prediabetes-related stress scale (PRSS) for adults with prediabetes in the United States
Authors: Deshira D. Wallace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetes prevention efforts involve lifestyle changes and, increasingly, stress reduction efforts. However, we lack a measure to assess the stress associated with living with prediabetes, a high-risk state of type 2 diabetes. We developed a novel scale, the Prediabetes-Related Stress Scale (PRSS) and assessed the reliability and validity of the newly developed scale among Latino adults in the United States (US).MethodsConducted a cross-sectional study from December 2024 to March 2025 with English and Spanish speaking Latino adults (18 + years old) in the US (n = 442) at elevated risk of type 2 diabetes. We used exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to examine factor structure, reliability testing, convergent validity, and measurement invariance testing for English and Spanish versions of the PRSS.ResultsParticipants’ mean age was 46 years old, 54% responded in English, and 57% were women. EFA results supported scale reduction from 29 items to 21 items, and a three-factor model. Reliability was excellent (composite reliability = 0.95). Model fit was appropriate. Convergent validity was achieved between PRSS, acute stress (r = 0.50), and psychological distress (r = 0.65). Configural and metric invariance were achieved across language, however, scalar invariance evidence was mixed.ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary evidence of the validity and reliability of a newly developed scale focused on prediabetes-related stress. Further validation and use of the PRSS can support the person-centered care that considers patient needs and preferences to guide clinical decisions and reduce diabetes disparities.

Background

Prediabetes is defined as a state of hyperglycemia, in which individuals in this state are at-risk for developing type 2 diabetes, with an HbA1c range of 5.7–6.4% (39–47 mmol/mol) [1]. In the United States (US), the prevalence of prediabetes is estimated at 97.6 million adults (one in three adults), yet only 19% of adults are aware of their high-risk status [2]. Prediabetes is an important state and risk factor to consider when treating patients to reduce the risk of progression to type 2 diabetes.

In general, the prevalence of prediabetes is higher in men than women, but similar across all ethnoracial group and educational levels [2]. Although prediabetes prevalence is similar across ethnoracial groups in the US, studies indicate that the conversion of prediabetes to type 2 diabetes is inequitable as Black, Latino, and Indigenous communities experience higher diabetes disease burden [3, 4]. According to estimates from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), since 1999, the Latino population has experienced an increasing trend of total diagnosed and undiagnosed diabetes compared to other racial and ethnic groups [4]. Prediabetes is also associated with a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes and chronic kidney disease [5]. Thus, intervening during the prediabetes state is important given the increasing evidence of the role of prediabetes in adversely affecting overall cardiometabolic health [5]. One such intervention is the Diabetes Prevention Program focused on prediabetes self-management behaviors to delay or prevent the onset of type 2 diabetes or diabetes-related outcomes [6]. The Diabetes Prevention Program includes dietary counseling, adjustment in physical activity levels, medication management if needed, and stress management highlighting the importance of stress in diabetes prevention [6].

Stress can also be acute or chronic, and the type and length of stress can influence a range of health outcomes [7]. Chronic stress, specifically, has been tied to changes to glucocorticoids and catecholamines, a hormonal response to stress [8]. Changes in these stress hormones in turn can affect the adequate absorption of glucose, thus making elevated blood glucose (hyperglycemia) a likely side effect [9]. Stress also affects how individuals engage in behaviors that are often associated with glucose control, primarily by disrupting stress-management behaviors such as physical activity engagement and dietary adjustments. Additionally, stress affects other forms of distress such as exacerbating depression and anxiety symptoms over the life course, which can adversely impact individual wellbeing [7, 10].

Adults with prediabetes have reported that stress from various sources can interfere with their diabetes prevention efforts. These sources of stress include stress from family and peers, finances, workplace, and from discriminatory experiences [11]. In addition to these stressors, Latinos may experience unique stressors that limit engagement with health systems for preventive care such as structural inequities, documentation status, transportation, limited language services, and provider bias [12]. One additional source of stress is their prediabetes. First, being “diagnosed” or told by a health professional about being in a prediabetes range was a source of stress [13]. Second, managing prediabetes itself can be a source of stress, including maintaining a recommended dietary plan, consistently engaging in physical activity, and taking needed medication [11, 13]. Relatedly, Latinos may react similarly to being diagnosed with prediabetes as they are with type 2 diabetes, as they may consider prediabetes an inevitable step towards developing type 2 diabetes. This reaction may stem from forms of social and self-stigma attached to diabetes itself, such as feeling judged or ashamed with not doing the “right” thing to stay healthy [11, 14, 15].

As prediabetes-related stress can contribute to glucose dysregulation that can lead to type 2 diabetes, addressing stress during this high-risk period is important to prevent the development of diabetes and reduce existing disparities in type 2 diabetes disease burden. Multiple scales have been developed to measure stress for individuals with type 2 diabetes, such a as the Diabetes Distress Scale [16] and the Problems Areas in Diabetes Scale [17]. However, these measures may not capture the nuances associated with a condition that is not only classified as a “risk of” another diagnosable condition but also evokes heightened feelings of stress that can affect overall wellbeing. Therefore, the goal of this study is to assess the reliability and validity of a newly developed Prediabetes-Related Stress Scale among Latino adults in the US. We also examine the stability across scale language with English and Spanish speaking US Latino adults.

Methods

Participants

Participants were Latino adults (18 years and older) with prediabetes in the general population. Prediabetes status was ascertained from self-report as either having clinician-reported prediabetes or passing a screener on diabetes risk that was adapted for this study from the American Diabetes Association Diabetes Risk Test. The adapted diabetes risk screener included the majority of the original screening questions such as age and gender, including having a parent with diabetes, having a prediabetes diagnosis, medication use for high cholesterol, high blood pressure diagnosis and/or medication use, self-reported physical activity, and – for those who were pregnant – being diagnosed with gestational diabetes [18]. The adjustment to the screener was in relation to race and ethnicity. As all participants were of Hispanic/Latino origin, questions on ethnicity were excluded. Additionally, participants were asked categorically if self-reported under, normal, or overweight rather than specifically asking about height and weight. Participants who screened positively for 6 of 9 items were indicated as high risk. Inclusion criteria for participants were self-identified as Latino/Hispanic adults living in the US, indicated prediabetes diagnosis or elevated diabetes risk, and could participant in the English or Spanish version of an online survey. If participants did not screen positive for prediabetes or elevated diabetes risk, they were excluded from completing the survey. As the survey was online, participants needed to have access to the internet and complete the survey without support from the research team. Participants completed the survey on their own time.

Recruitment

The participants were screened for inclusion within an existing Qualtrics Research Panel [19]. The Principal Investigator (first author) worked iteratively with the Qualtrics research team to specify the inclusion criteria and determine the appropriate sample selection of Latinos across the US, specifically a focus on age, prediabetes status, and a balance on language use to build the research panel. Once the sampling frame was refined, a screening survey was sent within the Qualtrics Research Panel based on inclusion criteria to initiate recruitment of adult Latinos with prediabetes in the US who could participate in English or Spanish. After reaching 10% of the expected sample of 400, recruitment was paused, demographics were reviewed by the Principal Investigator, and adjustments were made to ensure that the sample reflected the inclusion criteria.

Study design

This cross-sectional pilot study is the quantitative continuation of the scale development study. Information on the mixed-methods used to develop the 29-item Prediabetes-Related Stress Scale (PRSS) in both English and Spanish is published elsewhere [20]. For this cross-sectional study, we developed an English and Spanish language survey and worked with the Qualtrics™ research team to distribute the survey comprised of the PRSS-29 and scales related to other facets of psychological distress (e.g., depression, anxiety, acute stress) to test the psychometric properties of the PRSS-29. Participants spent approximately 20 min on the survey and were compensated $25. Recruitment took place between December 2024 and March 2025. The study was approved by the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill Institutional Review Board (#22-1630). Informed consent was obtained for all participants. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Measures

All measures were self-reported. The measures used in the study are the following:

Demographic variables included language of the survey, age (in years), gender, sex, highest level of education completed, marital status, self-reported race, Latin American and Caribbean heritage group, and if they were born in the US or abroad.

Prediabetes Related Stress Scale (PRSS-29) was developed to assess the extent of distress related to prediabetes experienced by adults. The PRSS-29 was initially developed in English and Spanish with Latinos in the US.20 The response options were 1 (not a problem) to 5 (a very serious problem).

Cohen’s Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) was used to measure acute or short-term general stress. Questions included “in the last month, how often have you felt confident about your ability to handle your personal problems?” and “in the last month, how often have you felt nervous and stressed?”. The scores ranged from 0 to 40 with higher scores indicating higher perceived stress. The PSS-10 has been validated in Latino populations in the US with a reliability range of a = 0.70–0.87 [21–23]. Cronbach’s alpha value for this study was 0.853. The alpha value for the English-specific survey was 0.840 and the alpha value for the Spanish-specific survey was 0.868.

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K-10) is a measure intended to yield a global measure of depression and anxiety symptoms experienced in the recent 4-week period. Scores range from 10 to 50 with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of a severe mental disorder [24]. The K10 has been validated in Latino populations in the US with reliability ranges of a = 0.80–0.94 [25–27]. Cronbach’s alpha value for this study was 0.955. The alpha value for the English-specific survey was 0.950, and for the Spanish-specific survey was 0.962.

Analysis

Prior to conducting any analyses, we evaluated survey data for missingness. No item was found to have missingness more than 5%. To assess the dimensionality of the PRSS we ran an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with full information maximum likelihood estimation. The EFA results along with theory supported the measurement model (i.e., internal validity) of the PRSS-29. Model fit was examined using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardized Root Mean Residual (SRMR). Chi-squared (χ2) and degrees of freedom (df) is reported although it is highly sensitive to sample size. The standard criteria used were: CFI > 0.90, TLI > 0.90, RMSEA < 0.06, SRMR < 0.60 [28]. Items with standardized factor loadings < 0.40 were removed prior to final model estimation. All factor analyses were conducted in Mplus version 8.11 and used a structural equation modeling framework.

We conducted reliability testing in two ways. The first was estimating Cronbach’s coefficient alpha (a) values for each subdomain and the full PRSS. Alpha values over 0.70 are acceptable, and those over 0.90 are excellent [29]. Second, we estimated composite reliability, which has the benefit of using a structural equation modeling approach to account for robust estimation and not requiring equivalent factor loadings, which Cronbach’s alpha assumes. Composite reliability over 0.70 indicates adequate reliability [30].

Then we conducted measurement invariance testing (i.e., multiple group comparisons), which involves increasingly constrained factor analysis models to determine if the English and Spanish language scales have similar factor structure (configural), factor loadings (metric), and thresholds (scalar). If the English and Spanish language scales test as equivalent, then the results across scale language can be compared [31]. Criteria used to evaluate model fit include the difference in chi-square (Δχ2) test for the nested models, with the aim of finding a non-significant change to suggest invariance [32]. Of note, Δχ 2 is overly sensitive to small deviations in large samples. Therefore, other criteria are used to examine model fit. For sample sizes with adequate power, and equal group sizes, one additional criterion is the ΔCFI of less than − 0.01 for nested models. The ΔCFI can also be paired with the ΔRMSEA of less than 0.03 for metric invariance or less than 0.015 for scalar invariance [32].

Finally, we assessed construct validity in multiple ways. Evidence for convergent validity included examining the correlation between PRSS scores and the Perceived Stress Scale and the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale scores, as distress measures are theoretically likely to be correlated. We also assessed average variance extracted (AVE), which is the average amount of variance that a construct explains in its items relative to the overall variance of the items [33]. An AVE > 0.50 demonstrates an acceptable level of convergent validity [33]. Lastly, we evaluated if the AVE was greater than the shared variance between each of the subdomains and calculated the correlations between subdomains to determine if the correlations were not greater than 0·70 as an evaluation of convergent validity [33].

Results

Participant demographics

A total of 442 Latino adults participated in the study, of which 54% (n = 244) responded to the English survey and 46% (n = 208) responded to Spanish survey. Participants reported an average age of 46 years old, with a range of 18–82 years old. Approximately 57% (n = 253) of the participants self-reported being women, and approximately one third (35%) indicated being born in the US. About one third of participants reported the presence of another comorbidity, such high cholesterol (35%) or high blood pressure (30%). Approximately 75% self-reported clinician-indicated prediabetes and the remainder were eligible through the screening process. Scale reduction

The initial PRSS had 29 items comprised of subdomains such as structural stressors, stress of the future, interpersonal stressors, and emotional-health related stress [20]. After screening for multicollinearity through inter-item correlation, we deleted eight items (three for low correlations [≤ 0.40], and five for high correlations [≥ 0.80]) for a total of 21 items. Reviewing eigenvalues and factor loadings resulted in a three-factor model. There was a fourth factor, however it only contained two items, and both items had lower standardized factor loadings (< 0.50) compared to the remaining factors, leading to an under-identified factor, and thus were removed from the model.

Results of the factor structure of the 21-item PRSS are presented in Fig. 1. Correlations between items were added due to modification indices results, theoretical understanding of the constructs, and parity. Items 2 and 29 were correlated because of the similarity of future orientation and items 13 and 27 were correlated for due to their similarity regarding food or diet. Further, Table 2 presents standardized factor loadings were highest within each subdomain: overwhelm, behaviors, and interpersonal information. For example, the overwhelm subdomain had item factor loadings ranging from 0.73 to 0.90. The behaviors subdomain had a factor loadings range of 0.83–0.92 and the interpersonal information subdomain had a tighter factor loadings range from 0.82 to 0.90.

Scale reliability

We assessed the reliability of the PRSS-21 in multiple ways. First, we evaluated Cronbach’s α for the three subdomains and for the PRSS-21 entire scale. The α for the overwhelm subdomain was 0.936, for the behaviors subdomain was 0.949, and the interpersonal information subdomain was 0.932. The α value for the overall scale was 0.972. Given the structural equation modeling framework used for these analyses, we also assessed composite reliability. Composite reliability values for each subscale ranged from 0.950 to 0.965. Both Cronbach’s α and composite reliability results indicate high reliability. Additionally, AVE was greater than 0·50, indicating further evidence of scale reliability. Factor structure and invariance testing

Fit statistics indicated appropriate fit [χ2(184) = 673.487 (p < 0.001), RMSEA = 0.079, SRMR = 0.027, TLI = 0.982, CFI = 0.984]. The model fit statistics for the English-language subsample were appropriate, although like for the full sample, the χ2 statistic is sensitive to sample size and was significant [χ2(184) = 478.082 (p < 0.001)]. RMSEA was higher than the expected 0.08 threshold, however, the 90% CI was under 1.0 (0.076, 0.094). The SRMR (0.038), TLI (0.971), and CFI (0.975) statistics were all within the established thresholds. A similar pattern of fit statistics was calculated for Spanish-language subsample [χ2(184) = 467.551 (p<·001), RMSEA = 0.086, SRMR = 0.028, TLI = 0.986, and CFI = 0.988]. To formally examine scale structure for the English and Spanish language versions of the PRSS-21, we tested measurement invariance. Configural invariance fit statistics were appropriate, as were those for metric and scalar invariance. Results show that when assessing the differences between each more restrictive model, configural invariance was achieved for both the English and Spanish versions of the PRSS, meaning that the latent factors have similar patterns of free and fixed loading across language groups. With configural invariance supported, we tested metric invariance by constraining item loadings on the factors. The model fit was not significantly worse between the metric and configural models with ΔCFI being 0.000 and ΔRMSEA being − 0.001; therefore, evidence of metric invariance was supported. Lastly, scalar invariance was tested and compared to the metric model. The overall model fit measured by Δχ2 was statistically significantly worse in the scalar model than the metric model, suggesting that there was at least one intercept difference across the English and Spanish language groups. However, when pairing these results with the ΔCFI (-0.003) and ΔRMSEA (0.000) these results provide evidence that scalar invariance may be supported and the difference in χ2 may be a result of sensitivity to sample size. Convergent validity

With the factor structure determined, we assessed convergent validity by comparing the correlations between the PRSS-21 and theoretically similar constructs. The Perceived Stress Scale was correlated as expected (r = 0.501) as was the PRSS-21 with the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (r = 0.658). Additionally, when evaluating the reliability measures (see Table 3), the AVEs were greater than 0.50 supporting the case for convergent validity. There was some mixed evidence for convergent validity such that AVE was greater than some shared variances. For example, the AVE for overwhelm (0.844) was greater than the shared variance between overwhelm and behaviors (0.861), but not for overwhelm and interpersonal information (0.642).

Discussion

The goal of this study was to build evidence for the reliability and validity of the novel three-factor Prediabetes-Related Stress Scale. The formative research underlying the development of the PRSS focused on adult Latinos in the US living with prediabetes, a group who experiences a disproportionate burden of diabetes risk. Our research allowed for the concurrent development and psychometric assessment of multilingual scales that capture the experience of living with prediabetes [11, 13, 20]. Assessing factor structure, items for the PRSS were reduced from 29 to 21. Using multiple reliability measures, such as Cronbach’s α and composite reliability, the PRSS-21 has high reliability within this sample. We examined measurement invariance to assess stability of functionality across English and Spanish versions of the PRSS-21. The study evidence supports that the PRSS-21 achieved configural and metric invariance, indicating that the overall factor structure and the factor loadings are similar for the English and Spanish language versions of the PRSS-21. When testing for scalar invariance (i.e., assessing equivalence of thresholds), mixed evidence was found based on the criteria used. Finally, construct validity was assessed by examining convergent validity using AVE and correlations with theoretically related constructs such as acute stress, measured by the Perceived Stress Scale, and anxiety and depressive symptoms, measured by the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale. Overall, these results demonstrate that the PRSS-21 is a viable multi-dimensional scale that can capture aspects of the stress associated with living with prediabetes.

Although there are scales that assess the role of the emotional and mental burden of managing diabetes and related conditions, these scales have not accounted for the needs of adults who may experience similar feelings of distress and complications with engaging in disease prevention behaviors. Other scales at the intersection of mental and physical health include the Diabetes Stress Scale (17 item), which has found strengths as a clinical tool to open conversations between clinicians and patients to address relevant mediators affecting diabetes-related outcomes [16, 34]. The Diabetes Distress Scale has multiple variations across languages, including in Spanish, a scale for adults with type 1 diabetes [35]. and for partners or parents of individuals with type 1 diabetes [36, 37]. Similarly, the Problem Areas in Diabetes Scale (20 items) is similarly focused as a checklist to facilitate conversations between a healthcare professional and a patient coping with diabetes [17]. However, these scales focus on the emotional and mental burden of living with a condition that has a perceived clearer diagnosis and sense of clinical urgency. Based on previous studies, we found that adults with prediabetes have a unique fear when they are told about their prediabetes, which was further exacerbated by the fear of developing type 2 diabetes and diabetes-related complications, such as amputation, in the future. As current scales do not have language regarding the future orientation of developing a diagnosable condition, in this case diabetes, having a scale that accounts for the stress experiences of adults with prediabetes can serve to support their diabetes prevention efforts in clinic settings. This PRSS-21 can also be used in community settings and complement public health interventions such as the National Diabetes Prevention Program’s stress modules for participants to better inventory the sources of stress affecting their prediabetes management [6].

The scale was concurrently developed in English and Spanish, which has validity as a methodology compared to focusing on one language at a time due to a focus on linguistic, parallel scale construction [20, 38]. For this study, evidence is provided for measurement invariance across language, a form of cross-cultural research. These results suggest that the items effectively reflect the construct, prediabetes-related stress, for both English and Spanish speakers (i.e., configural invariance), and that the factor loading patterns are identical across groups (i.e., metric invariance). Scalar invariance was mixed; however, there is some evidence that the items are equally easy or difficult across groups. These results provide initial support for cross-cultural comparison. Nevertheless, there is debate on the utility of achieving measurement invariance as a criterion for cross-cultural research, often because full measurement invariance is challenging to achieve [39]. Another critique focuses on how invariance testing can increase cultural bias in instruments, especially if validated first in one context or one language and then translated. To address this potential for cultural bias during scale development, we concurrently developed the PRSS in both English and Spanish [20]. Relatedly, measurement invariance testing implicitly assumes that achieving invariance statistically suggests that the construct is semantically similar the same across groups. However, this is not a sound assumption to make as semantic, or construct, “sameness” is not explicitly tested by statistical tests [39]. To address this critique, we assessed content validity at the initial development of the PRSS through a combination of group concept mapping and cognitive interviewing as a means of supporting semantic equivalence [20].

Overall, the strengths of this study are the robust testing of factor structure, reliability and convergent validity of the newly developed PRSS-21. Additionally, to account for the English and Spanish language version for the scale, our results support measurement invariance. Another strength is the focus on a prediabetes among a high-risk population first, particularly with an interest in Spanish-speaking Latino populations who may often be excluded from research. Limitations of this study include recruitment. The use of self-reported prediabetes diagnosis as well as the ADA screening questions adapted for the study may introduce bias in who participated in the study, compared to using HbA1c which would have confirmed prediabetes status. However, the survey was pilot tested, and responses were systematically reviewed by the study team to ensure the intended participants started the screener and for those who passed the screener, completed the survey. Relatedly, the use of the Qualtrics Research Panel, while broad in its reach, systematically excludes individuals who may not have access to technology or digital literacy to be recruited into a research panel. Time with prediabetes was not collected for the pilot, which is a limitation as time with prediabetes is an important factor for perceptions of prediabetes-related stress. For example, recently diagnosed individuals may not fully be able to respond to management questions as they in the process coming to terms with their management plan. The study focused on Latino populations first in the development of scale therefore limiting generalizability to other ethnoracial groups. Additional research is needed to identify scores and clinically-relevant cut points for the PRSS and continue to build the case for validity. Future studies are needed with a broader adult population to continue to build the case to validity across ethnoracial groups and other key demographic characteristics such as gender.

Conclusions

This study provides preliminary evidence of the validity and reliability of a newly developed scale meant to further probe into the stress related to living with prediabetes. With further validation, the use of Prediabetes-Related Stress Scale can support the person-centered care that (a) considers patient needs and (b) is responsive to patient preferences, needs, and values to guide clinical decisions. The scale can support collaborative, evidence-based decision-making among patients and providers to inform tailored treatment plans.
