Brain development mediates the relationship between self-reported poor parental monitoring and adolescent anxiety
Authors: Yiman Li, Zheyi Zhou, Yuqi Zhang, Hui Ai, Mingfang Liu, Jing Liu, Li Wang, Jiang Qiu, Zhuo Rachel Han, Zhanjun Zhang, Yue-Jia Luo, Pengfei Xu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The default mode network plays a key role in predicting adolescent brain age.•The development speed of adolescent brain networks is closely linked to parenting.•Inadequate parental monitoring plays a crucial role in the development of specific brain networks associated with GAD in adolescents.Keywords: Generalized anxiety disorder, Functional brain network, Brain age, Predicted age difference, Parental monitoring

Introduction

Anxiety is primarily triggered by uncertain anticipation of a threat and is characterized by a broader state of distress accompanied by symptoms of hyperexcitement and worry (Bishop, 2007, Lang et al., 2000). Although anxiety is an adaptive emotion, individuals prone to anxiety may be at risk of developing anxiety disorders (Cremers, 2010, Wang, 2020, Sylvester, 2012). Anxiety disorders tend to be characterized by early onset and chronic course and are one of the most common mental disorders in children and adolescents (Lazarus, 2016). Previous studies have shown that about 2.5 to 5 percent of children and adolescents meet the criteria for anxiety at a given time (Rapee et al., 2009, Costello, 2003). Therefore, exploring the possible risk factors of anxiety disorder in children and adolescents can help us better prevent and treat anxiety disorder.

Parenting is one of the important factors in childhood and adolescent anxiety, and it is also the main way to prevent and treat adolescent anxiety (Lazarus, 2016, Wang, 2021, Day et al., 2020). Previous studies have found that positive parenting behaviors such as moderate care and encouragement are beneficial to the prevention of anxiety disorders (Lazarus, 2016, La Buissonniere-Ariza, 2019). On the contrary, inappropriate parenting behaviors such as excessive involvement and control or total absence are early risk factors for anxiety disorders (Day et al., 2020, Ang and Sin, 2021, Chong et al., 2020, Guyer, 2015). Indeed, parenting can influence individual attachment styles (Ang and Sin, 2021, Karavasilis et al., 2016), emotion regulation (Morris, 2017), social behavior learning (Guyer, 2015, Bogels et al., 2011), and self-esteem (Ang and Sin, 2021) revealing the important role of parenting in various aspects of child and adolescent development. Although parental companionship and reasonable supervision are of great significance in the prevention of adolescent anxiety, the mechanism of parental rearing on individual anxiety has not been identified.

During childhood and adolescence, the brain is constantly developing, and the environment has a wide range of influences on individual brain development (Tooley et al., 2021). Structurally, after 2 years of age, the cerebral cortex becomes thinner with age due to continuous myelination and synaptic pruning (Stiles and Jernigan, 2010). A longitudinal study showed that positive maternal parenting was associated not only with reduced volume growth in the amygdala but also with accelerated thinning of the anterior cingulate cortex (Whittle, 2014). It has also been shown that adolescents who experienced consistent reactive mothering in childhood have a thinner cerebral cortex than those who experienced inconsistent reactive mothering (Frye, 2010). Functionally, previous network studies have found that with age increases, segregation and integration of brain functions continue, forming efficient and specialized functional networks (Dosenbach, 2010). Moreover, our recent meta-analysis has shown that anxiety disorders are associated with alterations in functional connectivity within and between different brain networks, most notably the default mode network (DMN), salience network (SN), affective network (AN), and executive control network (Xu, 2019). Therefore, in this study, we chose the Power (Power, 2011) template, which contains the functional networks of all the brain regions we focus on. In this study, we examined the relationships among parenting and brain development and anxiety based on these functional brain networks.

While it has been shown that functional brain connectivity is predictive of individualized anxiety (Wang, 2020, Duan, 2020), it remains unclear how anxiety influences the development of brain connectivity. “Brain age” quantifies neurobiological indicators of brain maturity using machine learning (Brouwer, 2021, Dosenbach, 2010, Mishra et al., 2023). The difference between actual age and predicted age (predicted age difference) is a measure of the rate of brain development (Mishra et al., 2023). Studies have examined the relationship between brain age and genes (Brouwer, 2021), mortality (Cole, 2018), and psychiatric symptoms (Lund et al., 2022, Sone, 2021), showing that brain age is an effective indicator of brain development speed and brain health (Mishra et al., 2023). In this study, we calculated brain age based on the functional connectivity of brain networks in three independent samples and examined the relationship among parenting, brain development and adolescent anxiety.

Methods

Participants

Data were obtained from the Healthy Brain Network (HBN), a biological database of 10,000 participants (ages 5–21) from the New York Area (Alexander, 2017). To control for the confounding effects caused by different collection sites and scanning machines, we only used the data acquired on a Siemens Prisma Fit with a 32-channel head coil located at the Citigroup Biomedical Imaging Center (CBIC) at Weill Cornell Medicine. Because the scan sequence of this site was changed during data collection, only 341 participants (129 females; age = 10.92 ± 3.15, M ± SD) from release7-9 were included in this study to ensure consistency of the scan sequence. Seventy-eight participants were excluded due to excessive head motion (FD > 0.5). Of the remaining 263 participants, 192 participants (HBN-1; 67 females, age = 11.97 ± 3.04, M ± SD) were used as the primary sample to predict brain age and for mediated analyses, and the other 71 participants (HBN-2; 31 females, age = 9.87 ± 3.53, M ± SD) were used as validation analyses for brain age prediction due to lack of data from behavioral questionnaires.

Two additional data sets were used for validation analysis. A data set containing 105 participants (26 females, age = 15.81 ± 6.25, M ± SD) from a healthy group of the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) project (Di Martino, 2014) was used for validation analysis of brain age prediction. Another dataset of 195 participants from the China color nest project (CCNP), was used for the validation of brain age prediction and correlation analyses (Yang, 2017, Liu, 2021, Zuo, 2017). Thirty-seven participants were excluded due to the absence of the anxiety questionnaire, and 157 participants (71 females, age = 11.68 ± 2.88, M ± SD) were eventually included in the analysis.

Behavioral assessment

The Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED) (Zibetti et al., 1997) was used to measure self-rated anxiety disorder in 9–18 years old. We obtained the SCARED scale of HBN-1. The scale consists of 41 items, which are divided into five dimensions: panic disorder, separation anxiety, generalized anxiety disorder, social anxiety disorder, and school avoidance. The scale was rated on a scale of 0–2 (0: not true; 1: sometimes true; 2: often true). Since this study only focused on generalized anxiety disorder, only the dimensional scores of generalized anxiety disorder were included in the analysis.

In a self-report format, the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire for Children (APQ) was used to measure parental practices. We obtained the APQ scale of HBN-1. This questionnaire contains 42 items and measures five dimensions: parental involvement, positive parenting, poor monitoring, inconsistent discipline, and corporal punishment. The items were reported on a Likert scale. There were five response categories ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always).

To assess family economic status, the Financial Support Questionnaire (FSQ), a self-developed instrument within the HBN dataset, was used on HBN-1. Within this questionnaire, we exclusively utilized a single query pertaining to family annual income. The scale for family annual income ranged from 1 to 11, corresponding to income levels spanning from below $10,000 to over $150,000.

Barratt Simplified Measure of Social Status questionnaire (BSMSS) was used on HBN-1 to gauge family socioeconomic status (Barratt, 2006). This assessment is predicated on marital status, retirement/employment status (retired individuals use their preceding occupation), educational attainment, and occupational prestige. Total scores on this questionnaire span from 8 to 66, with higher values indicative of elevated societal standing.

The Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC) (March 1997) is a 39-item self-report questionnaire. It can be used to measure anxiety in people aged 8–19 years. We obtained the MASC scale of CCNP. The items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1(never true) to 4(often true). The scale contains four dimensions: Physical Symptoms, Separation Anxiety, Harm Avoidance, and Social Anxiety. Previous studies have demonstrated that the total score of this questionnaire is a valid assessment of generalized anxiety disorder (van Gastel and Ferdinand, 2008). Therefore, we used the total score of this questionnaire as a measure of generalized anxiety in CCNP.

Specifically, within the HBN-1 sample, we utilized scales including the SCARED, the APQ, the FSQ, and the BSMSS. These scales enabled us to obtain participants' scores on generalized anxiety disorder, poor monitoring, family income, and family social status, respectively. In the CCNP sample, we employed The MASC to acquire participants' scores on general anxiety.

Image acquisition

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data of HBN were collected at CBIC on a Siemens 3 T Prisma scanner. With a T2*-weighted BOLD echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence, a total of 375 volumes was obtained: repetition time (TR) = 800 ms; slices = 60; echo time (TE) = 30 ms; flip angle = 31°; field of view = 204 × 204 mm2; multiband acceleration factor = 6; voxel size = 2.4 × 2.4 × 2.4 mm3.

Data from the CCNP were collected on a 3 T Trio scanner. Participants were asked to close their eyes but remain awake during the scan. A total of 184 volumes were obtained based on gradient-echo planar imaging sequences: TR = 2500 ms; slices = 32; echo time = 30 ms; flip angle = 80°; field of view = 220 × 220 mm2; and voxel size = 3.4 × 3.4 × 4 mm3.

Data from the ABIDE Ⅰ were collected at NYU Langone Medical Center on a Siemens 3 T Allegra scanner (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/abide/abide_I.html). Participants were asked to close their eyes but remain awake during the scan. A total of 180 volumes were obtained based on gradient-echo planar imaging sequences: TR = 2000 ms; slices = 33; echo time = 15 ms; flip angle = 90°; field of view = 240 × 192 mm2; and voxel size = 3 × 3 × 4 mm3.

Preprocessing

All functional MRI data were preprocessed using the DPABI (Yan and Zang, 2010), based on SPM12 (version no.7219; https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The process is as follows: 1) removing the first 10 images to suppress the equilibration effects; 2) slice timing correction; 3) realignment; 4) regression of nuisance signals, including white matter, cerebrospinal fluid signals, six rigid body motion correction parameters; 5) normalizing (MNI template); 6) detrending; 7) filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz); 8) smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (4 mm FWHM).

Functional network construction

Focusing on the cingulo-opercular task control network (CON), DMN, SN, fronto-parietal task control network (FPN), and subcortical network (SCN), we selected 128 nodes from the template defined by Power, Cohen (Power, 2011) to construct the functional networks. First, the average time series of 128 regions was extracted for each participant. Then the Pearson correlations were calculated between pairs of bold signals for all regions of each participant, resulting in a 128 × 128 correlation matrix for each participant. Fisher’s z-transform was then conducted to the correlation values.

Prediction of brain age

We performed leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to predict brain age (Fig. 1). For each fold of LOOCV, the prediction model is built based on the connectivity matrix of n − 1 participants. Then the brain ages of the rest of the participants are predicted. In each loop, the age vector is correlated with each functional connectivity of the training set. To avoid potential confounding effects, the gender was regressed out from FCs. The edge positively correlated with age (p < 0.05) was retained for the subsequent predictive model. The predictive model was built using a support vector regression (SVR) model which was conducted in the LIBSVM (https://www.csie.ntu.edu.tw/∼cjlin/libsvm/) toolbox and then used to predict the brain age of the test set. To test the predictive power of this model, the Pearson correlation coefficient between chronological and predicted age as well as mean absolute errors (MAE) were calculated. The nonparametric permutation analysis was conducted 1000 times to ensure that the predictions were above the random level. Specifically, the vectors of ages were first shuffled randomly, and the analysis process was repeated for each permutation. After the resulting null distribution was formed, the percentile of the predicted outcome of the true age was found. To investigate the significance of the five networks in prediction, we conducted virtual lesioning analyses in brain age prediction.

Flow charts of analysis. After the functional connectivity matrix of each participant was extracted, the brain age was predicted under the frame of LOOIC. The predictive power was then tested. Finally, the mediation model among predicted age difference, GAD, and poor monitoring was built.

Model of mediating effect

We calculated the brain predicted age difference (predicted age - chronological age) based on the brain age predicted by the SVR model. To examine the role of predicted age difference derived from five networks in the association between parental monitoring and anxiety, mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro designed for SPSS (Zhao et al., 2010). Poor monitoring score was taken as the independent variable, the predicted age difference was taken as a mediator variable, and the GAD score was taken as a dependent variable. In addition, gender was regressed as a control variable in the predicted age difference to control for the effect of gender on the predicted age difference. The indirect effect was considered significant if the value of 0 was not included in the confidence interval, and the standard error of the indirect effect was estimated by bootstrapping, accelerating the 95% confidence interval (with 1000 samples) (Preacher and Hayes, 2008).

Validation analysis

It has been shown that LOOIC can lead to a positive bias in prediction analysis (Varoquaux, 2017). Therefore, we repeated the prediction analysis of brain age using a 5-fold cross-validation in the HBN-1 sample. The sample was randomly divided into 5 sets, with 4 sets used as the training set to build the model, and 1 set used as the test set. We repeated this process 1000 times and calculated the mean predicted brain age and the predicted age difference.

Furthermore, in addition to the SVR algorithm, ridge regression and connectome-based predictive modelling (CPM) are often used in brain age prediction (Jirsaraie, 2023). To test the robustness of our results, we replaced the SVR model with these two algorithms and repeated the brain age prediction, as well as the mediation analysis using predicted age difference as the mediator.

To mitigate the influence of confounding variables, we implemented a series of analyses wherein we independently accounted for potential confounders, including head motion, family annual income, and family social status. Within the context of both brain age prediction and mediation analyses, we controlled the mean framewise displacement (FD) values for each participant. Furthermore, in the mediation analysis, we controlled variables of family annual income and family social status. It should be noted that due to the absence of family annual income data for certain participants, this subset of the validation analysis included only 166 participants.

Three more samples were used to test the robustness of the predicted brain age in HBN-1. Because the remaining features of each loop in the prediction analysis were not the same, we took the intersection of features of all loops as the final feature set. In the three validation samples, the functional connectivity matrix and 8128 FCs were obtained for each participant. Then, the final feature set was applied to the three samples and the SVR models were performed to predict the brain ages of each sample. The gender effect was also controlled in the three samples. To examine the validity of the prediction, the correlation coefficient between the actual and predicted age and MAE of each sample was also calculated. In addition, to examine the relationship between predicted age difference and GAD, we calculated the predicted age difference and its correlation with the total score of the MASC scale in CCNP.

Results

Brain-age prediction model

LOOCV revealed the functional networks of CON, DMN, FPN, SN, and SCN significantly predicted age (r = 0.406, MAE = 2.28, p < 0.001; Fig. 2a). Permutation tests showed that the prediction result was above random (p < 0.001; Fig. 2b and c).

The predictive power of brain age. (a) The predictive power of brain age: correlation between chronological age and predicted age. The permutation of (b) MAE and (c) correlation coefficient.

To investigate the significance of these five networks in prediction, we conducted virtual lesioning analyses in brain age prediction. By individually excluding each of the five networks, we obtained the following correlations between chronological and predicted age: r (without CON) = 0.458, p < 0.001; r (without DMN) = 0.235, p < 0.001; r (without FPN) = 0.438, p < 0.001; r (without SN) = 0.386, p < 0.001; r (without SCN) = 0.435, p < 0.001.

The contribution of five networks to age prediction was decreased by DMN, FPN, SN, CON, and SCN (Fig. 3d). In addition, nodes with degree greater than 30 mainly came from DMN, and the others belonged to FPN and SN (Table 1; Fig. 3a, b, and c). These nodes with degree greater than 30 mainly included the bilateral precuneus, bilateral middle temporal cortex, bilateral middle frontal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and medial superior frontal gyrus. Because the original numbers of FCs among the five networks were different, we controlled the total number of FCs of the five networks to calculate the relative contribution of each network. The results showed that FCs within DMN, FPN, and SCN and FCs between FPN and DMN contributed significantly to age prediction (Fig. 3e, f).

Predicted age difference mediated the association between poor monitoring and GAD

There was a significant negative correlation between the predicted age difference and GAD (r = -0.289, p < 0.001; Fig. 4a). The poor monitoring and predicted age difference were negatively correlated (r = -0.481, p < 0.001; Fig. 4b). The poor monitoring and predicted age difference were positively correlated (r = 0.158, p = 0.028; Fig. 4c). The mediation analysis showed significant direct effects of the poor monitoring on the GAD (p < 0.001), of the poor monitoring on the PAD (p < 0.001), and of the predicted age difference on the GAD (p < 0.001). The results of the mediated test at 95% confidence intervals did not include 0 (LLCI = 0.041, ULCI = 0.191), indicating predicted age difference played a mediating role between poor monitoring and GAD (p < 0.001; Fig. 4d).

Validation results

The result of 5-fold cross-validation showed a significant correlation between the predictive age and chronological age (r = 0.466, p < 0.001). Moreover, the correlation coefficient between the predicted age difference obtained through 5-fold cross-validation and those obtained through the LOOIC method reached a substantial magnitude of 0.976 (p < 0.001).

After predicting brain age using CPM, the correlation between chronological age and predicted age remained significant (r = 0.244, p < 0.001), and the predicted age difference obtained from CPM showed a strong correlation with those from SVR (r = 0.940, p < 0.001). After predicting brain age using ridge regression, the correlation between actual age and predicted age remained significant (r = 0.578, p < 0.001), and the predicted age difference obtained from ridge regression also exhibited a strong correlation with those from SVR (r = 0.738, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the mediating effect of predicted age difference on the relationship between poor monitoring and anxiety persisted, regardless of whether CPM (Indirect estimate = 0.095, 95 %CI = [0.035, 0.167]) or ridge regression (Indirect estimate = 0.059, 95 %CI = [0.202, 0.115]) algorithms were employed.

The results of controlling confounding variables show that the predicted brain age maintained a significant correlation with the chronological age (r = 0.414, p < 0.001) after controlling the head motion. The correlation in the predicted age difference before and after controlling head motion was strong (r = 0.844, p < 0.001). After controlling head motion, the mediation effect also persisted (Indirect estimate = 0.095, 95 %CI = [0.044, 0.158]). In addition, after controlling for family economic and social status factors, the predicted age difference still showed a significant mediating role between poor monitoring and anxiety (Indirect estimate = 0.088, 95 %CI = [0.034, 0.160]).

The validity analysis on the other three samples showed that the age prediction network generated by five networks in HBN-1 could significantly predict individuals’ brain age in the other three samples (HBN-2: r = 0.627, p < 0.001, Fig. 5a; ABIDE: r = 0.268, p = 0.006, Fig. 5b; CCNP: r = 0.282, p < 0.001, Fig. 5c). All these results of three samples HBN-2 (p = 0.001), ABIDE (p = 0.004), and CCNP (p = 0.001) passed the permutation tests over 1000 iterations. In CCNP, predicted age difference and anxiety score consistently showed a significant negative correlation (r = -0.416, p < 0.001, Fig. 5d).

Discussion

In the present study, we built a brain connectivity model that can effectively predict brain age in multiple samples. Specifically, we found that the five brain networks related to anxiety successfully predicted brain age in all samples, especially the DMN plays an important role in the predictive model. Importantly, the predicted age difference calculated from predicted brain age was significantly associated with anxious levels of GAD. Interestingly, the predicted age difference mediated the relationship between poor monitoring and GAD. These results imply that inadequate parental monitoring might play a role in the development of specific brain networks associated with GAD in adolescents.

To explore the relationship between brain development speed and anxiety, we selected the anxiety-related brain network to predict brain age (Xu, 2019). Previous studies have found that these brain networks change significantly during childhood and adolescence (Dosenbach, 2010), which may be one reason for the high incidence of anxiety disorders (Rapee et al., 2009). Many studies have found that the functional integration of large-scale brain networks is an important marker of brain development during childhood and adolescence (Stiles and Jernigan, 2010, Dosenbach, 2010, Chai, 2014, Xia, 2019). In the present study, functional connectivity of DMN, FPN, SN, SCN, and CON also successfully predicted brain age in the independent samples. Functional connectivity within the DMN and the SCN largely contributed to the prediction of brain age in this study. Previous studies have shown that reorganization of functional connectivity within the DMN occurs during adolescence (Fan, 2021), and abnormalities in this process may affect the individual's ability to recognize and regulate emotions by affecting cognitive control and the ability to learn cues, leading to depression (Ho, 2015), anxiety (Zidda, 2018), and other emotional disorders (Bathelt and Geurts, 2021). In addition, brain regions such as the amygdala and striatum in the SCN are also developing rapidly during adolescence (Casey, 2019). It has been shown that the amygdala is extensively connected to cortical and subcortical areas, contains sex hormone receptors, and is central to driving the development of adolescent brain networks (Scherf et al., 2013). The development of the amygdala-prefrontal circuit and the ventral striatum during adolescence is critical for stress coping (Tottenham and Galvan, 2016). Consistent with these studies, our results show that the development of DMN and SCN plays an important role in adolescent brain maturation, suggesting that this process may be associated with the development of emotion-related abilities.

More importantly, we found that the predicted age difference calculated from brain age predicted by these five brain networks was negatively correlated with anxiety scores, indicating the relationship between the delayed development of the emotion-related brain and the anxiety of adolescence. In recent years, neuroimaging-driven estimation of brain age has been progressively shown as a reliable biomarker for detecting different diseases and health conditions (Mishra et al., 2023). Depression (Han, 2021), schizophrenia (Shahab, 2019), and symptoms of emotional disorders (Drobinin, 2022) are widely associated with predicted age difference calculated through whole-brain functional connectivity, revealing that emotional disorders may be related to premature brain development. However, adolescent anxiety was negatively associated with the predicted age difference derived from five brain networks in the present study, implying that lagging rather than overt development of specific brain areas that associated with anxiety. Adolescence is the period with the highest incidence of many psychiatric disorders (Casey, 2019). The emotions of adolescents are highly susceptible to large fluctuations due to environmental influences (Stephanou, 2016). The development of brain areas related to emotion regulation is particularly important during this period (Casey, 2019, Ahmed et al., 2015). Recent studies have shown that the ability to regulate emotions develops rapidly during adolescence and is accompanied by the development of the limbic and cognitive control circuitry in the emotional brain (Casey, 2019, Ahmed et al., 2015). The negative association between emotion regulation ability and anxiety disorders has been widely observed (Norby, 2018, Bomyea, 2020). Therefore, in the present study, we suggest that the lag in the development of emotion-related brain networks may related to the level of anxiety.

The mediating model involving poor parental monitoring, predicted age difference, and anxiety suggested that poor monitoring may be related to GAD in adolescents through its connection with the development of brain networks. Adverse family environmental factors have been widely shown to be associated with abnormalities in functional and structural brain development (Guyer, 2015, Whittle, 2014, Palumbo et al., 2020). Several task-state neuroimaging studies have found that the more warmth an individual feels from his or her mother in early adolescence, the greater the activation of the striatum is observed in response to rewards (Morris, 2017, Morgan et al., 2014). Conversely, the more negative feedback from the mother, the weaker the activation of emotion-related brain regions (including the amygdala and anterior insula) (Tan, 2014). A longitudinal structural neuroimaging study of adolescents found that socioeconomic disadvantage had a significant effect on amygdala and prefrontal cortex development and that positive parenting moderated and mitigated this effect (Whittle, 2017). Together with previous findings, our results suggest a strong relationship between poor monitoring and delayed development of functional brain networks. On this basis, our results suggest that parental monitoring plays a role in the development of specific brain networks associated with anxiety in adolescents.

In conclusion, our study suggests that functional brain networks develop rapidly during childhood and adolescence, especially along with the development of higher cognitive functions during adolescence, in which the default network plays an important role. Importantly, adolescents with GAD show delayed development of specific brain networks, where inadequate parental monitoring plays an important role. These results remind us of the important role of parenting in child and adolescent development.
