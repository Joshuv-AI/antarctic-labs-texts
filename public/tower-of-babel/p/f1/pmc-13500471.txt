Influence of different bulb size and planting time on growth, morphology, and flower quality of Narcissus tazetta under greenhouse conditions
Authors: Zehra Çolak, Fulya Uzunoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Narcissus tazetta L. is an important ornamental geophyte widely cultivated for cut flower and bulb production; however, optimizing bulb size and planting time is essential to improve flower quality and production efficiency under greenhouse conditions. This study investigated the effects of bulb size and planting date on vegetative growth, flowering, and bulb characteristics during the 2022–2023 growing season. Bulbs collected from the natural flora of Hatay, Türkiye, were classified into four circumference groups (≤ 10 cm, 10–12 cm, 12–14 cm, and ≥ 14 cm) and planted on 15 September, 30 September, and 15 October at 15-day intervals in a greenhouse. Emergence time, flowering time, vegetative growth traits, flower quality parameters, bulb characteristics, and bulblet formation were evaluated. Bulb size significantly affected most measured variables. Although emergence occurred in all treatments, bulbs ≤ 10 cm did not flower at any planting date. Bulbs ≥ 10–12 cm were suitable for bulb production, while 12–14 cm and ≥ 14 cm bulbs were more appropriate for commercial flower production. The second and third planting dates improved flower quality and extended the harvest period. Optimizing bulb size and planting time can therefore enhance flower quality and prolong flowering duration in greenhouse-grown N. tazetta.

Introduction

The genus Narcissus L. (Amaryllidaceae) comprises approximately 80–100 taxa distributed predominantly across the Mediterranean Basin, with extensions into parts of Asia. Türkiye represents an important center of diversity for the genus, where nine taxa have been recorded, including both naturally occurring and naturalized populations1,2. Among these, Narcissus tazetta L. is one of the most widespread and economically significant species, particularly due to its early flowering habit, high environmental adaptability, and strong aromatic profile3,4. In Türkiye, N. tazetta subsp. tazetta occurs naturally in several regions, including Hatay, which constitutes a key biogeographical area supporting genetically diverse populations, especially double-flowered genotypes with high ornamental value5.

The increasing commercial demand for Narcissus as a cut flower and ornamental plant, together with its pharmaceutical importance due to bioactive alkaloids such as galantamine, has intensified research on production-oriented cultivation practices6–8. In this context, optimizing agronomic factors that directly influence plant growth, flowering performance, and bulb productivity is critical for sustainable and high-quality production systems.

Among these factors, bulb size and planting time are recognized as primary determinants of plant performance in geophytes. Bulb size reflects stored assimilates and developmental potential, thereby directly influencing vegetative vigor, scape elongation, and floral characteristics9,10. Previous studies have demonstrated that larger bulbs significantly enhance flowering quality and yield parameters in Narcissus species. For instance, Salem and Selem11 reported that bulb weight and cold storage duration jointly regulate vegetative growth and flowering responses in N. tazetta, while Slezák et al.12 identified bulb size as the most critical factor affecting plant performance in Narcissus poeticus.

Planting time, on the other hand, governs physiological processes such as chilling requirement fulfillment, root establishment, and floral initiation13. Early planting generally ensures better synchronization of vegetative and reproductive development, whereas delayed planting may negatively affect flower quality and stem elongation14. Under Mediterranean and greenhouse conditions, optimized planting schedules have been shown to significantly improve cut-flower performance. For example, Sarıçam et al.15 reported improved flower quality with early planting under protected cultivation, while Chaudhary et al.16 identified mid-September as the optimal planting period for N. tazetta ‘Paper White’.

Despite these findings, most studies have evaluated bulb size and planting time independently or under non-local conditions. Moreover, genotype-dependent variability has been shown to significantly influence growth and physiological responses N. tazetta populations17. However, studies integrating local genetic resources with key agronomic factors such as bulb size and planting date under greenhouse conditions remain limited, particularly for populations originating from Hatay, where ecological heterogeneity may lead to distinct genotype × environment interactions.

Therefore, this study specifically aimed to evaluate the combined effects of three bulb size classes and three planting dates on vegetative growth, flowering characteristics, and bulb productivity of locally collected Narcissus tazetta genotypes from Hatay under unheated greenhouse conditions. By focusing on region-specific genetic material and production variables, the study seeks to provide a practical and scientific framework for optimizing bulb grading and planting schedules in Mediterranean-type environments.

Materials and methods

The plant material used in this study was identified as Narcissus tazetta by Dr. Samim Kayıkçı. A voucher specimen (Fulya_05) has been deposited in the Herbarium of Hatay Mustafa Kemal University (HMKU). The bulbs were obtained in August from a local producer (Necati Sefa) located in Derince district, Antakya/Hatay Province, Türkiye (80 m a.s.l.; 36.3100840 N, 36.2154850 E). The plant material was supplied by the local producer and was not collected from natural populations. All research procedures complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. After lifting, bulbs were classified into four size classes based on circumference: 8.1–10 cm, 10.1–12 cm, 12.1–14 cm, and ≥ 14.1 cm. Planting was carried out in the research greenhouse of the Department of Horticulture, Hatay Mustafa Kemal University. The experiment was conducted under unheated greenhouse conditions. The experiment was established as a factorial arrangement consisting of four bulb size classes and three planting dates: 15 September, 30 September, and 15 October.

Bulbs were planted in 15 cm diameter plastic pots, with two bulbs per pot. The growing medium consisted of a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture of soil, peat, and perlite (Fig. 1). The experimental design included three replicates for each treatment combination. Each replicate consisted of 10 bulbs, resulting in 30 bulbs per bulb size × planting date combination. Prior to planting, bulbs were treated against fungal diseases by immersion in a 4% Benomyl fungicide solution for 10 min, following the protocol of Çığ and Alp18. Throughout the growing period, standard cultural practices were applied. Weeds emerging in the pots were removed manually, and the growing medium was loosened during weeding to improve root-zone aeration.

Fertilization was performed three times at 15-day intervals, beginning on 24 November. A compound NPK fertilizer (15 g per pot) was applied to the canopy projection area of each pot. Plants were grown under unheated greenhouse conditions and irrigated with tap water as needed, depending on substrate moisture status. Temperature data were recorded throughout the vegetative period from planting to bulb lifting using a data logger system installed in the greenhouse. The periodic variations in average temperatures recorded in the greenhouse during these months are presented in Fig. 2. Average temperatures during the 15-day intervals ranged between 29.6 and 30.5°C.

Bulbs ≤ 10 cm did not produce any flowers under greenhouse conditions and were therefore excluded from detailed statistical evaluation in later tables.

Floral traits were assessed through quantitative measurements, including flower diameter (cm), flower stalk length (cm), flower length (cm), pedicel length (cm), perianth tube length (cm), ovary length (cm), and the number of flowers per flower stalk. All linear measurements were recorded in centimeters using standard measuring procedures.Flowering time was determined as the number of days from planting to the opening of the first fully developed flower. Floral measurements were performed at the onset of flowering (anthesis), corresponding to January and February under the experimental conditions. All observations and measurement protocols were conducted in accordance with the methodology described by Zeybekoğlu18.

Leaf traits were evaluated after the completion of the flowering period. The assessed parameters included leaf number per plant, leaf length (cm), and leaf width (cm). Leaf length and width measurements were obtained from three randomly selected leaves per plant, and mean values were calculated for statistical analysis. Leaf measurements were conducted during March and April under the experimental conditions. All procedures were performed following Sarı16.

Bulb-related characteristics were determined at harvest and included bulb emergence rate (%), daughter bulb formation rate (%), bulb circumference (cm), and bulb width (cm). Bulb circumference was measured at the widest equatorial point using a measuring tape. Bulb measurements were conducted in June, at the end of the vegetation period, under the experimental conditions. All bulb measurements and rate calculations were carried out according to the methodology described by Sarı16.

The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized factorial design with three replications. The treatments consisted of two factors: planting time (three levels: 15 September, 30 September, and 15 October) and bulb size (four levels based on bulb circumference: ≥14 cm, 12.1–14 cm, 10.1–12 cm, and 8.1–10 cm).Data were subjected to two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SPSS statistical software. The main effects of planting time and bulb size, as well as their interaction effect (planting time × bulb size), were evaluated. When the F-test indicated significant differences, treatment means were separated using Duncan’s multiple range test at the 5% significance level (p ≤ 0.05).

Results

Effects of planting time and bulb size on floral traits

The effects of planting time and bulb size on flower diameter, flower stalk length, and flower length of Narcissus tazetta are presented in Table 1.

Flower diameter was significantly affected by both planting time and bulb size (P ≤ 0.05), whereas the interaction between the two factors was not significant (P > 0.05). Among planting dates, the first planting (15 September) produced the largest mean flower diameter (3.70 cm), while the third planting (15 October) resulted in the smallest (3.28 cm). Regarding bulb size, the largest flowers (3.60 cm) were obtained from bulbs ≥ 14 cm, and the smallest (3.30 cm) from 10 to 12 cm bulbs. The highest individual value (3.81 cm) was observed in ≥ 14 cm bulbs planted on 30 September, and the lowest (3.15 cm) in 10–12 cm bulbs planted on 15 October. These results indicate that earlier planting and larger bulbs independently enhanced flower diameter, likely due to longer vegetative growth and greater carbohydrate reserves (Table 1).

Flower stalk length was also significantly influenced by planting time and bulb size (P ≤ 0.05), but their interaction was not significant. The longest stalks were observed at the third planting (32.16 cm), while the shortest were at the second planting (23.83 cm). Considering bulb size, 12–14 cm bulbs produced the longest stalks (30.21 cm), and 10–12 cm bulbs the shortest (23.33 cm). The maximum individual stalk length (36.82 cm) occurred in ≥ 14 cm bulbs planted on 15 October (Table 1). These results suggest that later planting favored elongation of the flower stalk, possibly due to environmental conditions such as higher temperatures or light intensity during stem elongation, while larger bulbs provided more resources to support stem growth.

Similarly, flower length was significantly affected by both planting time and bulb size (P ≤ 0.05), with no significant interaction. The longest flowers were recorded at the third planting (40.85 cm), whereas the shortest were observed at the second planting (31.91 cm). Bulbs with a circumference of 12–14 cm produced the longest flowers on average (39.04 cm), while 10–12 cm bulbs produced the shortest (30.42 cm). The maximum flower length (46.29 cm) occurred in ≥ 14 cm bulbs planted on 15 October (Table 1). These findings indicate that both later planting and larger bulb size independently contributed to increased flower length, reflecting enhanced stem and floral organ development supported by carbohydrate reserves.

Pedicel length was significantly influenced by bulb size (P ≤ 0.05), but planting time and the interaction were not significant. Bulbs ≥ 14 cm produced the longest pedicels (5.20 cm), while the shortest pedicels (3.78 cm) were observed in 10–12 cm bulbs. These results suggest that pedicel length is more dependent on bulb reserves than on environmental conditions at planting (Table 2).

Perianth tube length was not significantly affected by planting time, bulb size, or their interaction (P > 0.05). The highest mean value (1.39 cm) was observed in the first planting, and the lowest (1.27 cm) in the third planting. Maximum individual measurements reached 1.54 cm, while the minimum was 1.07 cm (Table 2). This indicates that perianth tube length is relatively stable and may be largely genetically determined.

Ovary length was significantly influenced by planting time (P < 0.05) and by the interaction between planting time and bulb size (P < 0.05), while bulb size alone was not significant. The longest ovary (1.23 cm) occurred in the second planting with 12–14 cm and ≥ 14 cm bulbs, whereas the shortest (0.76 cm) was also observed in the second planting in ≥ 14 cm bulbs (Table 2). These results suggest that ovary length is affected by both environmental conditions associated with planting time and the availability of resources in bulbs, reflecting a combined effect on reproductive organ development.

The number of flowers per stem was significantly affected by planting time, bulb size, and their interaction (P < 0.05). The highest number of flowers (7.67) was observed in the second planting with 12–14 cm bulbs, while the lowest (3.33) occurred in the third planting with 12–14 cm bulbs. These results indicate that both factors and their interaction strongly influence floral productivity per stem (Table 2).

Effects of different planting dates and bulb sizes on leaf characteristics

The number of leaves per plant was significantly influenced by bulb size (P ≤ 0.05), while planting time and the interaction between planting time and bulb size were not significant (P > 0.05) (Table 4). The lowest mean leaf number (4.33 leaves plant⁻¹) was observed in 10–12 cm bulbs, whereas the highest (5.33 leaves plant⁻¹) was obtained from bulbs ≥ 14 cm. Across planting dates, the first planting produced the highest mean leaf number (5.11 leaves plant⁻¹), and the second planting the lowest (4.67 leaves plant⁻¹), though these differences were not statistically significant. Considering combined effects, the highest leaf number (5.67 leaves plant⁻¹) occurred in ≥ 14 cm bulbs at the second planting, while the lowest (3.00 leaves plant⁻¹) was recorded in 10–12 cm bulbs at the same planting date. These results indicate that larger bulbs promote leaf production, likely due to greater stored carbohydrate reserves supporting vegetative growth.

Leaf width was not significantly affected by either planting time, bulb size, or their interaction (P > 0.05). Mean leaf width ranged from 1.73 to 1.95 cm, with slightly wider leaves observed in bulbs ≥ 14 cm. The maximum individual leaf width (2.12 cm) occurred in ≥ 14 cm bulbs at the third planting, while the minimum (1.40 cm) was measured in 12–14 cm bulbs at the same planting (Table 4). These results suggest that leaf width is relatively stable and less responsive to environmental or bulb size variations under the tested conditions.

Leaf length was significantly influenced by planting time (P ≤ 0.05), while bulb size and the interaction were not significant. The longest leaves were recorded in the third planting (49.39 cm), and the shortest in the first planting (34.78 cm). Although leaf length tended to increase with bulb size, differences among bulb size means were not statistically significant. The maximum individual leaf length (54.10 cm) was measured in ≥ 14 cm bulbs at the third planting (Table 4). These findings indicate that later planting promotes longer leaves, likely due to favorable growth conditions such as temperature and light, while larger bulbs may provide additional resources supporting leaf elongation.

Effects of planting time and bulb size on bulb traits

Bulb emergence was generally high across all treatments, with 100% emergence recorded in most planting times and bulb sizes. However, emergence decreased slightly to 90% in 12–14 cm and ≥ 14 cm bulbs planted at the third date (Table 5). Both planting time and bulb size influenced bulblet formation, with larger bulbs producing more bulblets. The highest bulblet formation rate (11%) was observed in ≥ 14 cm bulbs at the first planting, whereas the lowest rate (1%) occurred in ≤ 10 cm bulbs at the second planting. These results suggest that larger bulbs have higher vegetative propagation potential through bulblet formation, while very small bulbs produce fewer offsets.

Bulb circumference was significantly affected by planting time, bulb size, and their interaction (P ≤ 0.05). The largest mean bulb circumference (13.11 cm) was observed in bulbs ≥ 14 cm, while the smallest (10.33 cm) occurred in ≤ 10 cm bulbs. The maximum individual circumference (14.64 cm) was recorded in ≥ 14 cm bulbs at the third planting, whereas the minimum (9.88 cm) occurred in ≤ 10 cm bulbs at the same date (Table 6). These results indicate that delayed planting combined with larger bulb size enhances bulb growth.

Bulb height was also significantly influenced by planting time and bulb size (P ≤ 0.05). The tallest bulbs were obtained at the third planting (6.11 cm), and the shortest at the first planting (5.54 cm). Bulb height increased with bulb size, reaching a maximum mean of 6.43 cm in ≥ 14 cm bulbs, and a minimum of 5.00 cm in ≤ 10 cm bulbs. The tallest individual bulbs (7.04 cm) were observed in ≥ 14 cm bulbs at the third planting, and the shortest (4.90 cm) in ≤ 10 cm bulbs at the second planting (Table 6). These findings confirm that larger bulbs and later planting promote overall bulb development.

Discussion

Flower diameter is a major determinant of ornamental quality in Narcissus tazetta and varies depending on genotype and cultivation conditions. Previous studies have reported values ranging from 67.13 to 88.41 mm under greenhouse and open-field conditions and 2.85 to 4.43 cm among different ecotypes19,20 with natural populations averaging approximately 4.0 cm21 In the present study, bulbs with a circumference ≥ 14 cm planted in mid-September produced flower diameters within or above these ranges. This confirms that larger bulbs and early planting enhance floral development, likely due to increased carbohydrate availability supporting cell expansion and organ differentiation3,8.

Flower stalk length was also significantly influenced by bulb size and planting time. Reported values in Narcissus spp. range from 11.3 to 35.84 cm depending on cultivation conditions22,23. In this study, early planting promoted stem elongation, suggesting that favorable temperature and light conditions during early growth stages enhance vegetative development. These findings are consistent with previous reports highlighting the sensitivity of stem elongation to temperature variation24,25.

Flower length and pedicel length exhibited similar responses to experimental factors. While flower length in N. tazetta has been reported between 29.2 and 61.7 cm18, pedicel length is known to decrease with delayed planting26. In the present study, pedicel length increased with bulb size but decreased under late planting conditions, indicating that both internal reserves and environmental cues regulate floral organ elongation. Differences among studies may reflect genotypic variation among ecotypes21. Similarly, perianth tube length was consistent with previous reports, whereas ovary length exceeded earlier values20,27, which may be attributed to controlled greenhouse conditions promoting stable growth and enhanced organ development.

The number of flowers per stem, a key yield parameter, ranged from 1 to 7 and was positively associated with bulb size. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that bulb size determines floral primordia formation and reproductive capacity8,28,29. Comparable relationships have also been reported in Narcissus poeticus, where bulb size strongly influences final plant performance11. In addition, flowering time varied considerably depending on bulb size and planting time. Previous studies have reported a wide range from 41 to 128 days18 and 49–79 days30. In the present study, early planting accelerated flowering, likely by promoting root establishment and chilling accumulation15.

Recent research in Turkey supports these observations. Balekoğlu et al.31 evaluated alternative fragrant Narcissus cultivars under Bayındır ecological conditions and reported significant variation in floral size, number of flowers per stem, and bulb yield among cultivars. Certain genotypes exhibited superior flower diameter and prolonged flowering periods, highlighting the importance of genetic background in shaping floral and agronomic traits. Similarly, Kırcı et al.32 reported that environmental conditions, including temperature and regional variation, significantly affect the volatile and biochemical profiles of N. tazetta, emphasizing that floral quality traits are sensitive to both genetic and environmental factors. These studies align with our results, indicating that bulb size and planting time, together with local environmental conditions, can modulate both morphological and reproductive traits in Narcissus tazetta.

From a practical horticultural perspective, our findings suggest that planting larger bulbs (≥ 14 cm) at earlier dates optimizes flower diameter, number of leaves, and flowering success, while slightly delayed planting may favor longer stalks and flower length, traits important for ornamental quality. Integrating these agronomic strategies with cultivar selection, as suggested by Balekoğlu et al.31 can enhance commercial flower performance and bulb yield in Mediterranean greenhouse conditions. Furthermore, the biochemical considerations highlighted by Kırcı et al.32 indicate that floral aroma and quality traits could also benefit from strategic planting schedules and bulb selection.

Vegetative growth parameters were also affected by bulb size and planting time. Leaf number increased with bulb size, consistent with earlier findings18,33. Leaf length exceeded previously reported values22,34–36 which may reflect favorable greenhouse conditions ande fertilizer.

Environmental factors, particularly light conditions, play a critical role in regulating growth and flowering. Studies on Narcissus tazetta subsp. chinensis have shown that adequate light enhances leaf development and flower initiation, whereas low-light conditions suppress photosynthetic efficiency. Recent studies further indicate that light spectra influence carbohydrate partitioning and flowering dynamics, with combined red, blue, and far-red light promoting earlier flowering and improved physiological performance. These findings provide a physiological explanation for the present results, suggesting that early planting allowed plants to benefit from more favorable light condition37. In addition to genetic and planting time effects, recent research has shown that environmental cultivation conditions such as light intensity and management practices significantly influence the vegetative and reproductive performance of Narcissus tazetta subsp. chinensis. For example Yang et al.38 reported that variations in light and cultivation conditions altered leaf and flower bud development, highlighting the importance of optimizing environmental parameters alongside planting time and bulb size to maximize ornamental quality.

These findings complement our results by suggesting that not only planting date and bulb size but also external cultivation variables (e.g., light and cultural practices) play crucial roles in shaping growth performance. Such multi-factor interactions deserve further attention in future optimization studies for greenhouse-grown N. tazetta.

Crop management practices also contribute significantly to plant performance. Previous studies have shown that early planting improves vegetative growth and flowering2,39,40. Similarly, Chaudhary et al.15 reported that mid-September planting resulted in superior growth and flower quality, while Acarsoy41 emphasized the importance of cultivation practices such as planting depth and production system. Larger bulbs enhanced vegetative growth, flowering capacity, and propagation potential due to higher carbohydrate reserves, whereas planting time and environmental conditions (temperature and light) modulated these effects.

The importance of bulb size is further supported by studies demonstrating that smaller bulbs often fail to flower due to insufficient reserve accumulation. Zeybekoğlu18 reported that bulbs smaller than 9–10 cm did not produce flowers, which is consistent with the present findings and confirms the existence of a threshold bulb size required for successful flowering.

Overall, larger bulbs provided greater carbohydrate reserves, enhancing vegetative growth and bulblet initiation. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the positive relationship between bulb size and propagation efficiency in N. tazetta19,42. Collectively, these results confirm that bulb size is a key determinant of growth and flowering traits, consistent with earlier reports on Narcissus species22.

Conclusions

The present study clearly demonstrated that bulb size is the primary factor influencing flowering performance and yield in Narcissus tazetta. Bulbs with a circumference of 10 cm or less failed to produce flowers at all planting dates, whereas bulbs measuring 14 cm and above exhibited the highest flowering rates and superior performance. These results indicate that bulbs with a minimum circumference of 12 cm should be used for commercial flower production, while smaller bulbs should be grown separately for bulb enlargement purposes.

Planting time also played a significant role in flower quality and production continuity. Under greenhouse conditions, the second and third planting dates (15 October and 30 September) provided more favorable results in terms of flowering quality and duration. Staggered planting dates successfully extended the flowering period from February to April, enabling a continuous flower supply, which is particularly important for commercial cut flower production. Overall, the combined use of appropriately sized bulbs and optimized planting schedules is recommended to improve yield, flower quality, and market sustainability in Narcissus tazetta cultivation.
