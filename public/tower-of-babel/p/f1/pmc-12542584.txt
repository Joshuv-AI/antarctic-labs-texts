Quality improvement project to reduce intraventricular haemorrhage in very preterm infants failed due to increased life-sustaining intensive care at low gestational age
Authors: Isabelle Erni, Dirk Bassler, David Glauser, Michael Wolff, Beate Grass, Mark Adams
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIn October 2016, a single centre quality improvement programme ‘IBIS’ (from the German “Intraventrikuläre Blutungs-Inzidenz Senken”), an intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH) prevention bundle had the Specific Measurable Achievable Reasonable Time-bound (SMART) Aim to reduce IVH incidence from 29.6% to 18.5% within 2 years for very preterm (gestational age (GA) between 23 0/7 and 29 6/7).DesignMonocentric retrospective cohort study of a failed quality improvement project using prospectively collected data including 1276 live-born very preterm infants between 2010 and 2023. Primary outcome measures were IVH, severe IVH and death or severe IVH. Statistical analysis included propensity score matching.InterventionsIBIS, an ongoing IVH prevention bundle based on a systematic literature search starting in October 2016.ResultsComparing pre-IBIS patients (2010–September 2016) to IBIS patients (October 2016–2023) revealed no reduction in IVH (25.1% vs 25.1%) or severe IVH (9.6% vs 9.1%). Instead, mortality (24.8% vs 13%) and delivery room mortality (9% vs 5%) were almost halved and fewer infants died after primary palliative care (8.5% vs 4.7%) or after redirection from intensive to palliative care (15.1% vs 7.9%). Longitudinal analysis revealed no trends for IVH or severe IVH over the entire period. Limiting the analysis to the IBIS period reveals a significant trend for fewer IVH (p=0.001). Propensity score matching revealed significant reduction for severe IVH (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.99) and death or severe IVH (OR 0.42, 95% CI: 0.3 to 0.59) but not for IVH.ConclusionsThe IBIS SMART Aim to reduce IVH failed. Simultaneously, survival was significantly increased by the increased provision of life-sustaining intensive care at lower GAs, which possibly led to an increased risk for acquiring IVH. Our results highlight the need for a better understanding of the effects of extending perinatal interventional activity to lower GAs on adverse outcome monitoring.Keywords: Quality improvement, Patient Care Bundles, Infant Mortality, Paediatrics, Child

Introduction

The incidence of intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH) at the Department of Neonatology at the University Hospital of Zurich in very preterm infants below 30 weeks gestation increased from 14.8% in 2010 to 29.6% in 2014 and was higher than international benchmarks such as the one from Vermont Oxford Network (18.5%).1

The development of IVHs is based on multifactorial genesis, which can be divided into vascular, intravascular and extravascular factors.2 The vascular cause is mainly due to the fragility of the immature germinal matrix in premature infants and therefore dependent on the gestational age (GA) at birth.3 The germinal matrix recedes around the 32nd week of GA.4 Intravascular factors include all factors that lead to a change in cerebral blood flow and platelet and blood clotting disorders. Extravascular factors include fibrinolytic activity and deficient vascular support.2

Many of the risk factors mentioned in the literature can be influenced by clinical management before, during and after birth. Risk factors and measures thereof mentioned in the literature can be divided into prenatal (male sex, multiples, low birth weight, low GA, pre-eclampsia, chorioamnionitis, no antenatal steroids), perinatal (caesarean section, umbilical cord pH<7.2, cord milking) and postnatal (5’ Apgar score<3, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), surfactant administration, hypocapnia and hypercapnia, arterial hypotension, hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, hypothermia, stress, neonatal transport (outborn), early-onset sepsis, patent ductus arteriosus).5,16 Most IVHs occur during the first 3 days of life in a period where preterm infants are limited in their ability to auto-regulate and control fluctuations in their cerebral perfusion.17,19

Preterm infants with IVH show an increased risk of mortality, permanent brain damage and impaired neuronal development such as cerebral palsy.20 21 This is why the prevention of IVH is important. Evidence from multiple studies suggests that prevention through so-called IVH bundles has significantly reduced IVH incidence. These bundles involve standardised care protocols managed by a multidisciplinary team. Regular monitoring of protocol adherence has proven effective in improving outcomes for at-risk preterm infants.22,25

Our quality improvement project IBIS (from the German ‘Intraventrikuläre Blutungs-Inzidenz Senken’) aimed to reduce IVH incidence from 29.6% to 18.5% within 2 years starting from October 2016. This study analysed the failure and subsequent continuation of the adapted measures. It outlines how the decision towards more life-sustaining intensive care at birth and reduced redirection of care, made 1 year earlier,26 may have resulted in an increased risk for IVH and thus masked the actual potential benefit of the evidence-based measures taken. Our public, tertiary level neonatal intensive care unit is the largest in Switzerland and has circa 100 newborns below 29 weeks gestation per year. It is also the largest referral centre in the Canton of Zurich but, on an international level, small to medium sized with 14 intensive and 16 intermediate care beds.27 The multifactorial basis of IVH with its many risk factors as well as the small sample size of very preterm infants in a single tertiary neonatal unit, particularly of those with IVH, has made monitoring IBIS outcome and determining possible reasons for its failure exceedingly difficult. Therefore, this study could not focus on the original period of 2 years allocated to IBIS but instead analysed the extended period of 2010–2023 to improve statistical power. This, however, limited us to analysing data available already prior to IBIS to be able to compare values before and after implementation of the quality improvement measures. We believe this to be important as the survival bias and subsequent rise in IVH risk, revealed in this study, may well be the reason for a limited effect or failure of other quality improvement projects in other neonatal units that struggle with the same issues of sample size and available manpower for conducting complex programmes.

Methods

Context

We conducted a retrospective monocentric cohort study using prospectively collected data of live-born infants with a GA between 23 0/7 weeks and 29 6/7 weeks in 2010–2023. We excluded 66 infants with a major congenital malformation, defined as being the primary cause of death or requiring surgery or a chromosomal anomaly and four outborn infants admitted after day 3 of life.

IVH prevention bundle (IBIS)

We defined IBIS based on concepts from the Institute of Healthcare Improvement using a key driver diagram and a Specific Measurable Achievable Reasonable Time-bound (SMART) aim. After an extensive literature review during 2015, we formed a team consisting of physicians and nurses from the department of neonatology and consulted members of the obstetrics team as well as midwives in the first half year of 2016. Together, we defined prenatal, perinatal and postnatal key drivers and interventions in an extensive key driver diagram (online supplemental file 1). The following measures were already standard practice but required strict monitoring for adherence: delivery at tertiary centre; avoidance of premature birth; avoidance of transmission of amniotic infection; (full course of) antenatal steroids; atraumatic birth; avoidance of endotracheal intubation; targeting normal platelet count and coagulation parameters; maintenance of vital parameters within normal limits (not further specified). The following measures were newly implemented: avoidance of relocation (same bed in delivery room and on the ward); avoidance of delayed surfactant administration (if surfactant indicated); strict maintenance of normothermia, normocapnia, normal pH, normotension, normoglycaemia; avoidance of procedural stress; provision of stress-reduced care, that is, nursing measures such as two-person handling. We did not expect the concepts, hypotheses and corresponding interventions taken from literature to impact only one driver on a 1:1 basis. Given the high number and the complexity of factors associated with IVH, we instead expected the interventions to interact. For this reason, we launched all measures at the same kick-off event in October 2016 in the attempt to achieve a maximum benefit for our patients. Checklists recorded the adherence to the perinatal and postnatal key drivers prospectively for each patient using a green symbol for good and a red one for missing adherence. Weekly progress meetings with representatives from all groups mentioned above were set up with a predefined structure discussing a sample of infants with a GA up to 29 6/7 weeks, focusing on checklist violations (red) and prioritising infants with IVH. After 2018, we stretched the meetings to quarterly to reduce time effort. We organised regular refresher training to raise awareness among all team members. As of 2020, we discussed IBIS measures on every morning handover. The SMART aim was: to reduce the incidence of IVH for infants born below 30 weeks gestation from 29.6% in 2014 to 18.5% (1st quartile of Vermont Oxford Network) within 2 years after implementation of IBIS. We maintained the measures when failure to reach the SMART aim became apparent after 2 years at the end of 2018. Delayed cord clamping replaced umbilical cord milking in 2021 because of the latter’s potentially harmful effect on IVH. Antenatal steroids were switched from betamethasone to dexamethasone when there was a supply bottleneck.28

Measures

Out of the researched risk factors, the following were collected over the entire study period and were used in this study: GA as the best estimate available based on prenatal ultrasound examination during the first trimester of pregnancy, birth weight z-score,29 male sex, multiple births, histological chorioamnionitis, antenatal steroids, caesarean section, 5 min Apgar score, pneumothorax (extra pleural air diagnosed by chest radiograph or needle aspiration), endotracheal intubation, surfactant treatment. We diagnosed IVH according to the Papile classification and defined severe IVH as IVH grade 3 or above.20

Statistical analysis

Analysed data items were ≥99% complete. For this analysis, we used the χ2 test with Yates correction for categorical and the Mann-Whitney U test for metric variables to compare two cohorts from 2010 to September 2016 (pre-IBIS) and October 2016 to 2023 (since IBIS). The Cochran-Armitage test was performed to retrospectively test trends of categorical variables over time and display whether there was an association between a categorical variable and year of birth over the entire time period from 2010 to 2023 or exploratively for a shorter time period to test for current trends. We selected the period 2010–2023 prior to analysis to ensure a sufficient sample size to uncover potential differences in IVH incidence proportion and mortality. The time periods for the current trend analyses were selected retrospectively and are of an explorative, hypothesis-generating nature only. We compared outcomes between infants born pre-IBIS with infants born since IBIS using propensity score matched cases and controls based only on factors not directly influenced by the IVH prevention bundle, that is, on GA, birth weight z-score, male sex and multiple births using the optimal full matching method. P values are used as quantitative measures of the strength of statistical evidence rather than rejecting null hypotheses. We performed all statistical analyses using R, V.4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The R package MatchIt was used for propensity score matching.30

Results

From the 1346 very preterm infants during the study period, we excluded 66 infants with a major congenital malformation and four outborn infants admitted after day of life 3 (figure 1). A total of 1276 newborns was included in the study. Overall, we compared 576 infants from 2010 to Sept 2016 (pre-IBIS) with 700 infants from Oct 2016 to 2023 (since IBIS). Pre-IBIS, 91% infants were admitted for treatment and 75% survived to discharge home. Since IBIS, 95% were admitted and 87% survived. According to table 1, mortality (24.8% vs 13%), and delivery room mortality (9% vs 5%) were almost halved and fewer infants died after primary palliative care (8.5% vs 4.7%) or after redirection from intensive to palliative care (15.1% vs 7.9%). Median GA and birth weight z-score were lower since IBIS, whereas antenatal steroids, caesarean section, 5 min Apgar score, surfactant treatment and continuous pulmonary airway pressure provision increased during the second period. Chorioamnionitis, endotracheal intubation and pneumothorax remained stable. Regarding the outcome measures, IVH and severe IVH remained stable, whereas death or severe IVH (29.7% vs 18.7%) was clearly reduced. However, the original comparison set by the SMART aim revealed a non-significant increase from 29.6% in 2014 to 32.4% (p=0.81) during the first 2 years since IBIS from 2017 to 2018.

Discussion

This study aimed to analyse why our IVH prevention bundle IBIS failed to achieve the SMART aim of reducing the incidence of IVH within 2 years from 29.6% in 2014 to 18.5% (1st quartile of Vermont Oxford Network). Instead, IVH rose non-significantly to 32.4% from 2017 to 2018. Whereas the targeted difference of the SMART aim would have been of sufficient statistical power to reject a null hypothesis with a 5% type 1 and a 10% type 2 error, any difference below the targeted one remained inconclusive in our small to medium sized neonatal unit. The lack of statistical power also made an indepth analysis of the failure at the end of the original IBIS period impossible. The need to implement all measures at once, due to their complex and multifactorial effect on IVH, ruled out looking at individual key drivers. Nevertheless, we chose to continue IBIS, as all measures implemented in IBIS were chosen after extensive literature research, had been used successfully elsewhere or had ample evidence base confirmed by additional research of new literature.1825 31,34 We focused on the risk profile difference of both cohorts pre-IBIS and since IBIS and postponed further analysis to when more data would be available. In 2023, we evaluated the sustainability of IVH outcomes, by which time IBIS had become standard of care at our unit. Whereas IVH and severe IVH remained stable between the two compared time periods 2010–September 2016 versus October 2016–2023, delivery room and overall mortality almost halved. We believe this to be the effect of a conscious decision to implement higher perinatal interventional activity providing life-sustaining intensive care in the delivery room 1 year before IBIS was launched, based on revised ethical considerations.35 Preterm infants with a lower GA were admitted and there was a reduction in redirection of care after hospital admission. Before, more newborns would have succumbed to their condition based on an adverse outcome prognosis that we understood to be accurate for the time. This led to lower delivery room death and lower mortality on the ward but also meant a higher risk for IVH and other complications. Sicker infants requiring more extensive interventions, which are known to be a risk factor for the formation of an IVH, contributed to these outcomes.18 36

Accordingly, the proportion of infants that were admitted to the ward and received cerebral ultrasound at 23 weeks’ and 24 weeks’ gestation rose from 9.6% to 15.5% between the two time periods. As displayed in figure 2 and known from literature,8 10 37 the risk of IVH is inversely associated to GA which may explain why the overall IVH incidence first rose on our ward from 2016 to 2018 (figure 4A). An overall trend analysis, however, reveals a stable IVH incidence over time, which was confirmed by a propensity score matched comparison for the odds of IVH between the two periods. A trend towards fewer IVH is observable as of shortly after the initiation of IBIS in 2017. Due to the retrospectively chosen time period, this analysis is therefore of a hypothesis-generating nature and the assumed trend will need to be further observed. However, a study on a sudden increase in retinopathy of prematurity incidence at our institution also concluded that an increase in adverse outcome was associated with an increased survival below 25 weeks.26 A recent large-scale study in the USA also observed an increase in active treatment and survival at the limit of viability.38

Alternatively, low adherence to the key drivers may have also thwarted the SMART AIM. Unfortunately, the documentation of adherence was done handwritten on paper forms and subsequent manual summaries presented at weekly meetings. When project failure was determined, no publication of the report was planned and no working capacity was available for digitalising as it was essentially needed to continue the IBIS measures and meetings. However, since weekly meetings were held during the entire period, adherence to meetings was generally good, and all key drivers were gradually implemented into routine care, we believe that failure to reach the SMART AIM was not due to low adherence.

Further research is needed to explore why the IBIS programme did not lead to a reduction in severe IVH. In the study by Persad et al, no significant improvement through an IVH prevention bundle was observed either. This study’s patient population also consisted of younger (preimplementation: 27.3±1.7 and post implementation: 26.8±2.1 weeks GA) individuals.18 In contrast, Kolnik et al reported successful outcomes with the implementation of a brain protection bundle.25 But, according to their own statement, these results might be attributable to an older patient population (preimplementation: 28 0/7, 25 0/7–29 0/7 and post implementation: 27 6/7, 26 1/7–29 3/7 weeks GA). At our ward, there may be a decline in severe IVH as of 2019, as revealed in figure 4B and observable by propensity score matched comparison for the odds of severe IVH between the two periods. This will need to be explored further in the coming years. Switching from umbilical cord milking to delayed cord clamping in 2021 may have supported this development.28 39 Figure 3 reveals that since IBIS, the proportion of survivors is higher in each GA, whereas the IVH incidence is lower. More survivors with lower GA demonstrate the intentional shift towards more intensive care in the second period. However, the shift towards lower GA brings a higher risk for an adverse outcome regarding IVH.8 10 According to a recent comparison between infants below 30 weeks gestation of 10 national neonatal networks including the Swiss network, the incidence proportion of severe IVH in our study (9.3%) was close to the overall rate reported for all networks combined (9.6%).34

The incidence of ‘death or severe IVH’ significantly decreased over time. This variable encompasses not only those very preterm infants who were diagnosed with severe IVH but also those who potentially had severe IVH but died before a diagnosis was made. This reduction suggests that the IBIS interventions have been effective at least in maintaining a stable proportion of severe IVH, as else we would expect a weaker trend for severe IVH than for mortality, which is not the case (figure 4C,D).

With the shift to providing neonatal care at lower GAs and the implementation of IBIS, several parameters associated with IVH have changed (table 1). The role of the delivery mode as a possible influence has been debated in different studies, whereas our study shows no significant evidence that caesarean section would be more protective than vaginal birth.912 40,42

After the implementation of IBIS, the 5 min Apgar score improved, with more infants achieving a score above 7. A 5 min Apgar score below seven is a significant risk factor for IVH, as it is often associated with hypoxia in newborns.43 44 Preterm infants are more vulnerable to such complications due to their underdeveloped germinal matrix.3 This improvement may be attributed to factors such as enhanced prenatal care and the increased use of antenatal steroids (both targeted by IBIS), all of which promote fetal lung maturation and reduce the incidence of RDS.845,47 The reduction in RDS contributed to a decreased risk of IVH, as respiratory complications and the resulting interventions are known risk factors for IVH.48

The rate of mechanical ventilation was somewhat reduced, whereas the rate of non-invasive ventilation increased. Typically, younger preterm infants require intubation more frequently. In our study, we would have thus expected a higher intubation rate since IBIS. However, this was not the case, indicating that the infants might have been born healthier, potentially reflecting an improvement in clinical outcomes due to the quality improvement measures.49

Out of the changes listed in table 1, the rise in antenatal steroids, 5 min Apgar score (through its association with enhanced prenatal care) and non-invasive respiratory support were intended as part of the IBIS programme. We cannot exclude that the changes were also associated with the increased perinatal interventional activity. As this latter change only concerned the smaller proportion of infants at the lower GAs, we, however, assume that they were more likely an effect of IBIS. The increase in caesarean section was not part of IBIS and may reflect the increase in perinatal interventions.

Strong teamwork between nurses and doctors cemented implementation and execution of the IVH prevention bundle IBIS. This multidisciplinary approach was well received as a quality improvement project and was, over time, integrated into standard care. Continuous effort, refresher training and regular evaluation, using the Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle, were essential at reducing the incidence of IVH. Many of the measures, such as reducing stress by minimal handling, were seen as innovative at the beginning of IBIS but have become established practices in neonatal care since. This evolution demonstrates the continuous effort to improve outcomes for preterm infants and highlights the importance of adapting to new evidence-based practices as they become available.

In the era of raising awareness for the need for quality improvement programmes because of large unwarranted variations in practice and outcomes between healthcare providers that serve the same clientele,50 we believe it is important to report failures and null results, particularly if they may have been beneficial for the patients although not measurable. Chen et al, for instance, report on various examples that may have been unsuccessful because of a ‘rising tide’ phenomenon in which both an intervention and a control group improve over time and therefore no difference between before and after is measurable.51 In our study, we did not assume a rising tide effect but instead that the effect of IBIS was not measurable because of a raise in risk that came simultaneously with the raise in prevention, that is, IBIS.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the study included the big sample size and the prospectively collected data for the longitudinal study. The prospective monitoring of adherence to the key drivers for each patient via Checklists and weekly case discussions was another major strength. The monocentric setup, small annual sample size and low incidence of IVH limit the representativity of our study. We cannot rule out that one or several of the key drivers implemented in IBIS may have contributed to the failure of IBIS, but we believe this chance to be small due to the high evidence base of most of the measures described. The aforementioned pragmatic resource limitations that impeded collection of durable adherence data made subsequent retrospective analysis of protocol adherence impossible. We learnt that a systematic, ongoing digital collection of the failed key driver interventions discussed at the weekly meetings would have sufficed. As already stated, there is also a survival bias, having more high-risk infants in the second period, and a severity of illness bias present in this study as sicker infants need more intensive interventions and are more prone to complications regardless of the interventions received. By examining mortality trends and GA stratification, we believe we have successfully eliminated this bias.

Another limitation of the study’s generalisability to other countries is the exclusion of outborn infants after day 3 of life. This exclusion represents a significant risk factor as referenced in the literature.10 However, given the low number of outborn cases in Switzerland, this limitation has otherwise limited relevance.

Also, there is a limitation in the validity of the propensity score because GA was included in the propensity score but also serves as one of the most relevant risk factors for IVH.8 10 This dual role might have confounded the results, as the adjustment may not fully account for the impact of GA on IVH incidence.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while the IBIS programme showed trends of improvement in IVH reduction and a significant reduction of mortality and morbidity, especially in higher GAs, the simultaneous increase in perinatal activity for younger preterm infants masked these effects. This could have led to the termination of IBIS to save resources, had other evidence on how to reduce IVH been available at the time. We believe this would have led to increased IVH and severe IVH. The effects of changes to the approach of perinatal interventional activity should thus be better researched and made aware of. Also, the positive trends highlight the need for further research under more controlled conditions to identify the relevant factors and measures for preterm infants below 25 weeks GA, as there is no significant improvement shown for these newborns. Another lesson learnt is that although the SMART aim was designed to deliver a measurable result in the case of a successful intervention, it was not designed to measure failure in a small to medium sized neonatal unit. Longer time periods or collaboration with other units in a stewardship programme may have prevented this.
