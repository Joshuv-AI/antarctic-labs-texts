Dual-chemokine-armed oncolytic Senecavirus A co-recruits cDC1 and CXCR3+ T cells to convert ‘cold’ melanoma and drive durable regression
Authors: Wenjie Li, Jingshuai Sun, Hanchen Tian, Xiaoyu Tang, Long Gao, Shuangshuang Liang, Zhuofan Zhang, Yuan Sun, Jingyun Ma, Xiaoya Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOncolytic virotherapy represents a promising anticancer strategy by combining direct tumor lysis with in situ immune activation. However, its efficacy remains limited in immune-cold tumors, which are characterized by poor T-cell infiltration and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Although engineering oncolytic viruses to deliver chemokines has been explored to modulate immune recruitment, most existing approaches activate only a single immune axis. Senecavirus A (SVA), a tumor-selective RNA virus with high genetic stability and flexible transgene capacity, offers an attractive platform for coordinated intratumoral delivery of immune payloads.MethodsWe engineered recombinant SVA vectors to achieve co-expression of CXCL11 and vXCL1 within tumor, aiming to synergistically recruit both T cells and cross-presenting dendritic cells and remodel the tumor immune landscape. In vitro and in vivo assays were conducted to assess viral properties (stability, replication kinetics, gene expression) and cytotoxicity against B16-F10 melanoma cells in different stages, with tumor burden, immune cell infiltration, and mouse survival in C57BL/6 mice analyzed to evaluate the overall therapeutic efficacy.ResultsSignal-peptide deletion significantly improved the genetic stability of transgenes and enhanced intratumoral payload retention, without compromising viral fitness. In murine models, CXCL11-expressing virus monotherapy achieved enhanced tumor control, prolonged survival, and provided preliminary evidence suggestive of protection on tumor rechallenge. Furthermore, the combination of CXCL11 and vXCL1 further augmented dendritic cell activation, promoted CD8+ T cell infiltration, and shifted macrophages toward an M1-polarized phenotype, while sustaining higher intratumoral viral loads. These coordinated immune changes led to deeper and more durable tumor regression.ConclusionsOur findings validate the combination of CXCL11- and vXCL1-armed SVA as a potent immunovirotherapy strategy and propose a design principle for multistage, multigene intervention: concurrently targeting leukocyte recruitment, antigen presentation, and effector activation provides complementary mechanisms that synergistically amplify therapeutic efficacy.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study provides direct evidence that coordinated intratumoral delivery of CXCL11 and vXCL1 via an oncolytic Senecavirus A (SVA) virus induces synergistic antitumor immunity, an effect unattainable by single-chemokine therapies. We further demonstrate that signal peptide deletion did not compromise the therapeutic outcome, as evidenced by well-preserved antitumor efficacy and a comparable overall survival benefit. Crucially, our results reveal that this combination therapy drives comprehensive remodeling of the immune-cold tumor microenvironment by enhancing dendritic cell activation, CD8+ T cell infiltration, and M1 macrophage polarization, which collectively leads to deep and durable tumor regression.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Our work defines a new principle for cancer immunotherapy: concurrently targeting cDC1 recruitment and T-cell effector functions achieves synergistic efficacy via multistage immune modulation. This dual-gene approach provides a framework for developing multimechanism therapies. The engineered SVA platform and this combinatory strategy offer a tangible pathway for further translational development in treating immune-cold tumors. Additionally, the signal-peptide deletion method may serve as a useful reference for improving genetic stability in other virotherapy research.

Introduction

Oncolytic virotherapy has emerged as a promising strategy within cancer immunotherapy, coupling direct lysis of tumor cells with in situ stimulation of systemic antitumor immunity. This dual functionality makes oncolytic viruses (OVs) attractive for highly heterogeneous and aggressive malignancies such as melanoma, with potential applications in both human and veterinary oncology.1 2 Nevertheless, clinical benefit remains limited in so-called ‘immune-cold’ tumors, which are characterized by scarce effector T-cell infiltration, inefficient antigen presentation by dendritic cells (DCs), and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that blunts adaptive priming.3 4

To overcome these barriers, recent work has incorporated immunomodulatory chemokines into viral payloads to reprogram the intratumoral (i.t.) landscape. CXCL11 recruits activated CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells via CXCR3, enhancing T-cell trafficking and responsiveness in preclinical melanoma and glioblastoma models.5 6 By contrast, XCL1, the sole ligand for XCR1, selectively attracts cross-presenting conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1), which are essential for cytotoxic T-lymphocyte priming and tumor-specific immunity.7 8 Although adenovirus or vaccinia platforms have delivered such chemokines, most strategies engage a single immune axis and do not coordinate cDC1 recruitment with T-cell effector activation.913 These limitations highlight the need for an OV platform capable of multi-signal to orchestrate complementary innate and adaptive responses.

Senecavirus A (SVA) is a non-enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus with natural tumor selectivity and a favorable biosafety profile.14 Our group previously isolated and characterized the first SVA strain in China (CH-01-2015), which exhibited potent oncolytic activity across multiple tumor cell lines—including PC-3, H1299, and Ishikawa—demonstrating efficient replication without significant cytotoxicity.15 Importantly, the SVA genome tolerates stable insertion of exogenous genes between the 2A and 2B coding regions, and ribosomal-skipping T2A sequences enable co-expression of multiple proteins without compromising replication efficiency.16 17

In this study, we engineer recombinant SVA vectors co-express CXCL11 and vXCL1, thereby conferring combined oncolytic and immunotherapeutic effects. Leveraging a signal-peptide deletion (Δss) strategy, we enhance transgene genetic stability and the precision of i.t. payload retention. We further evaluate CXCL11 and vXCL1 as single agents and as cocktails to rationally engage complementary immune circuits, with the goal of recruiting effector T cells and cDC1 in concert, remodeling the TME, and converting immune-cold tumors into immunologically responsive lesions.

Material and methods

Cell lines

The swine testicular (ST) cell line was obtained from ATCC (CRL-1746, Manassas, Virginia, USA). The IFN-α/β receptor knockout ST cell line, ST-R, was provided by Prof. Chen (Yangzhou University, China). ST and ST-R were cultured in Dulbecco‘s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Grand Island, New York, #C11995500BT), while B16-F10 was maintained in RPMI-1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #C11875500BT). All media contained 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cell-Box Biotechnology, Changsha, China), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China, #C100C5). Cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO₂.

Construction of recombinant SVA strains

Coding sequences for murine CXCL11 (NCBI Gene ID: 56066) and XCL1 variant (vXCL1) were synthesized (GENEWIZ, Suzhou, China) and cloned into pUC-GW-Kan.18 Signal peptides (residues 2–21, predicted by SignalP 6.0) were removed to generate Δss variants. Each CDS (CXCL11, ΔssCXCL11, XCL1, ΔssXCL1) was fused in-frame to T2A and flanked by homologous arms (primer sequences and PCR procedures in online supplemental tables S1 and S2). Inserts were recombined with the SVA-ccdB intermediate in GB08-Red E. coli by homologous recombination (online supplemental figure S1). Positive clones were screened by colony PCR (online supplemental table S3), validated by BbsI digestion and Sanger sequencing, and purified with a BAC/PAC large-plasmid kit (Aidlab, Beijing, China, #PL20).

Virus rescue and passage stability

Purified plasmids were transfected into BHK-21 to rescue recombinant viruses (rSVA-CXCL11, rSVA-vXCL1). Viruses were serially passaged in ST-R up to P15. Supernatants (P5, P10, P15) were collected, filtered by 0.22 µm PES membrane filter (NEST, Beijing, China, #331001), and stored at −80°C. Viral RNA was extracted (Magen, Guangzhou, China, #R441003), reverse-transcribed (ABclonal, Wuhan, China, #RK20433), amplified with transgene-flanking primers (online supplemental table S3), and assessed by gel electrophoresis and Sanger sequencing to evaluate genetic stability.

In vitro replication kinetics and cytotoxicity

For replication kinetics, B16-F10 cells were infected (multiplicity of infection [MOI] = 10) with parental or recombinant SVA. RNA was collected at 12, 24, 36, 48 hpi, reverse-transcribed, and qPCR targeting SVA VP1 was performed using SYBR Green (Xinkailai, Guangzhou, China, #XKL0412). Relative VP1 expression was calculated by 2−ΔΔCt, normalized to the parental SVA at each time point.

For cytotoxicity, B16-F10 cells (6 × 104/well, 96-well) were infected at MOI = 0.01–10. At 24 and 48 hpi, medium was replaced with 10% CCK-8 (NCM Biotech, #C6005) for 45 min and absorbance was read at 450 nm (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #1410101). Viability was calculated as [(As−Ab)/(Ac−Ab)]×100%.

Western blot

BHK-21 cells were infected at MOI = 1 for 48 hours. Lysates were prepared, and 25 µg total protein quantified using BCA kit (Aibimenzyme, Shanghai, China, #ZJ102) was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF (Merck, Germany, #IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked (Epizyme, China, #PS108P) and incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-CXCL11, anti-XCL1, and anti-GAPDH (online supplemental table S4), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescent detection (Azure Biosystems, USA, #A-6000).

Mouse tumor model and treatments

Female C57BL/6 mice (4 weeks, 16–18 g; Zhuhai Bestgene Biotechnology, Guangdong, China; production license SCKY (Yue) 2020-0051) were housed under SPF conditions (12-hour light/dark; 18°C–23°C; 40%–60% humidity). After shaving and disinfection, 0.5 × 106 B16-F10 cells were implanted subcutaneously (left axilla). When tumors reached ~50 mm³, 104 animals were randomized (n = 13/group) into eight treatment arms. i.t. dosing began on Day 0 and followed a q.d.×2 days then 1-day break schedule for six total doses (100 µL of 108.5 TCID50 per dose). The treatment groups consisted of a vehicle control (DMEM), parental SVA, monotherapy with recombinant SVA constructs (rSVA-CXCL11, rSVA-ΔssCXCL11, rSVA-vXCL1, and rSVA-ΔssvXCL1), and combination therapies (rSVA-CXCL11+rSVA-vXCL1, termed Combo; rSVA-ΔssCXCL11+rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, termed ΔssCombo). Tumor volumes and body weight were measured every 3 days (volume = length × width²/2). Tissue samples were collected at pre-specified interim time points: day 3 (prior to the third dose) and day 13 (6 days after the sixth dose). For each group at each time point, data from three animals were included in the analysis.

Bioluminescent imaging

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, injected intraperitoneally with D-luciferin, and imaged on IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer, USA). Images were acquired at defined exposure settings; regions of interest were analyzed in Living Image software and expressed as photons/s/cm²/sr.

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry

Tumors were fixed in 4% neutral formalin for 48 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 µm. Sections underwent H&E staining or immunohistochemistry (IHC) with anti-CXCL11 (1:50, Abmart, Shanghai, #PK79585) or anti-XCL1 (1:50, Abmart, Shanghai, #PK79585) following antigen retrieval and DAB detection; nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Stained sections were imaged on a Zeiss Axio Scope.A1 (Carl Zeiss Ltd., Oberkochen, Germany); DAB intensity was quantified using ImageJ across five random fields per section.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

Additional day 13 tumor sections (4 µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against CD8, granzyme B, or PD-1 (online supplemental table S4), followed by species-appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:300). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Representative tumor regions were imaged using a Zeiss LSM900 confocal microscope at 10× magnification under identical acquisition settings across groups. Quantitative analysis was performed on tumor fields using DAPI-based nuclear segmentation with uniform marker-positivity thresholds applied across all groups. Cell densities (cells/mm²) were calculated using calibrated pixel dimensions (1.0 µm/pixel), and CD8+GZMB+ and CD8+PD-1+ cell densities as well as corresponding percentages within CD8+ cells were determined per tumor.

Flow cytometry

Tumor and tumor-draining lymph node (tdLN) tissues were dissociated, filtered (70 µm), and mononuclear cells enriched by density gradient centrifugation (2000 rpm, 20 min, RT). Cells were stained with a viability dye and fluorescent antibodies (online supplemental table S5). Data were acquired on a NovoCyte 2060R (Agilent, USA, #2060R) and analyzed with FlowJo.

Tissue viral load quantification

Tissues were homogenized; RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and absolute qPCR targeting SVA VP1 was performed using standard curves from plasmid standards. Viral copy numbers were reported as copies/mg tissue. Each sample was assayed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism V.10.1.2. Data are presented as mean±SEM, with individual data points shown where applicable. For datasets with sufficient sample size, distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances using the Brown-Forsythe test. When assumptions were met, two-group comparisons were performed using two-tailed unpaired t tests and multigroup comparisons using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For datasets showing clear deviations from normality or heteroscedasticity, non-parametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis with Dunn’s correction) were applied. Given the small sample size in selected flow cytometry analyses (n=3), normality tests have limited power; therefore, parametric tests were used when no gross departures were observed, and results are interpreted with caution and supported by effect sizes and concordant biological replicates. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests. Tumor growth curves were analyzed by two-way ANOVA (or mixed-effects model if missing values) with multiple comparisons correction as specified in the figure legends. A p<0.05 was considered significant (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant).

Results

Construction and characterization of recombinant SVA

To enable transgene expression within the SVA genome, murine CXCL11 or vXCL1 coding sequences with or without signal sequences were fused to a T2A element and inserted between the viral 2A and 2B coding regions (figure 1A). The T2A peptide supports ribosomal skipping, permitting co-translational cleavage and independent transgene expression while preserving the native C-terminal sequence of the viral polyprotein. For plasmid construction, transgenes with flanking homologous arms were PCR-amplified and integrated into the SVA-ccdB intermediate by homologous recombination in GB08-Red E. coli (online supplemental figure S1). The resulting vectors, termed p15A-SVA-CXCL11, p15A-SVA-vXCL1, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, and rSVA-ΔssCXCL11, were sequence-verified and used to rescue recombinant viruses.

Following transfection of purified plasmids into BHK-21 cells, recombinant SVA was successfully rescued. Pronounced cytopathic effects—cell rounding, shrinkage, and grape-like clustering—were evident at 72 hours postinfection (hpi) in infected ST-R cells, but absent in mock controls (figure 1B).

Genetic stability was assessed by serial passaging to P15. Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) of viral RNA from P5 and P10 revealed lower-molecular-weight bands for rSVA-vXCL1 at P5, consistent with partial transgene deletion; aberrant bands persisted at P10 (figure 1C). In contrast, rSVA-CXCL11, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, and rSVA-ΔssCXCL11 yielded uniform amplicon, matching the expected size. Sanger sequencing of RT-PCR products of viral RNA from different passages confirmed that Δss variants (rSVA-ΔssvXCL1 and rSVA-ΔssCXCL11) exhibited enhanced genetic stability with no detectable mutations or deletions, whereas sporadic alterations occurred in signal-peptide–retaining constructs (online supplemental figure S2).

Balancing stability and potential adaptive changes, P5 stocks—rSVA-CXCL11 (W15R), rSVA-vXCL1 (G9D), rSVA-ΔssCXCL11, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, and parental SVA—were selected for amplification and downstream studies.

Replication dynamics were quantified by VP1 mRNA levels at 12, 24, 36, and 48 hpi in B16-F10 cells infected at MOI = 10. All recombinant SVAs showed reduced VP1 expression relative to parental SVA across time points, indicating a replication cost associated with transgene insertion (figure 1D–G). rSVA-vXCL1 consistently displayed the lowest VP1 (0.54–0.64 fold of the parental). While most constructs peaked at 24 hpi, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1 exhibited a modestly delayed peak at 48 hpi. Notably, Δss constructs produced higher VP1 transcripts than their signal-peptide–retaining counterparts, suggesting that removing the secretion signal lessens transcriptional burden and improves fitness.

At the protein level, Western blotting confirmed transgene expression in infected BHK-21 cells (figure 1H,I). Notably, Δss viruses yielded lower chemokine protein signals than signal-peptide–retaining constructs, implying that signal removal may reduce translational efficiency or protein stability.

Cytotoxic evaluation of recombinant SVA variants reveals dose-dependent and time-dependent oncolytic potency

We profiled cytolytic activity across MOIs 0.01–10 at 24 and 48 hpi by CCK-8 assay (figure 2). Compared with the control group, cells in the infected group exhibited extensive detachment from the culture surface, loose arrangement, irregular morphology, and a suspended or semi-suspended state, with some cells showing blurred cell membrane edges (online supplemental figure S3). At 24 hpi, all strains caused minimal cytotoxicity at MOI ≤ 0.1. At MOI = 1, parental SVA, rSVA-CXCL11, and rSVA-ΔssCXCL11 reduced viability to ~75%, whereas rSVA-vXCL1 and rSVA-ΔssvXCL1 remained near baseline. At MOI = 10, rSVA-CXCL11 and rSVA-ΔssCXCL11 produced robust cytolysis (~50% viability), comparable to parental SVA, while vXCL1-based constructs were modest.

At 48 hpi, cytotoxicity increased for all groups. rSVA-CXCL11 showed the strongest killing (~42.3% viability), followed by rSVA-ΔssCXCL11 and parental SVA (~50.3%). rSVA-vXCL1 and rSVA-ΔssvXCL1 improved over 24 hours but remained less potent than CXCL11-based viruses. Collectively, rSVA-CXCL11 displayed the most favorable dose-dependent and time-dependent oncolysis; lack of signal sequence did not diminish CXCL11-mediated activity, whereas vXCL1 constructs were consistently weaker.

In vivo antitumor efficacy and immune memory induced by recombinant SVA therapy

Therapeutic activity was tested in immunocompetent B16-F10/C57BL/6 mice. A schematic of the experimental design is shown in figure 3A. Bioluminescence imaging on day 11 showed high tumor signals in DMEM and parental SVA groups, whereas mice treated with rSVA-CXCL11, rSVA-ΔssCXCL11, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, Combo, or ΔssCombo exhibited markedly reduced signals (figure 3B). Tumor volumes corroborated these trends with significantly delayed growth in the same groups (figure 3C–F). rSVA-vXCL1 produced intermediate benefit.

Body weight monitoring revealed minimal or no weight gain during the early post-treatment period (0–3 days) in mice treated with rSVA-CXCL11, rSVA-ΔssCXCL11, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, Combo, or ΔssCombo (figure 3G), which paralleled the groups exhibiting robust antitumor responses. This trend suggests early immune activation may transiently suppress appetite or elevate metabolic demand.

Survival analysis revealed rapid mortality in DMEM and parental SVA cohorts within 20 days. rSVA-vXCL1, rSVA-ΔssvXCL1, and rSVA-ΔssCXCL11 conferred modest benefit. The greatest advantage occurred with rSVA-CXCL11, Combo, and ΔssCombo, yielding survivors beyond 60 days (figure 3H). Complete response (CR) was achieved in one mouse each from the rSVA-CXCL11 and Combo groups, and two mice in the ΔssCombo group.

To assess long-term immune protection, CR mice were rechallenged with B16-F10 cells. No tumors developed in rSVA-CXCL11 (1/1) or Combo (1/1) groups, and 1/2 ΔssCombo mice remained tumor-free, resulting in an overall tumor protection rate of 75% on rechallenge (figure 3I). In the relapsing mouse in the ΔssCombo group, tumor growth was markedly restrained, reaching only 863.3 mm³ by Day 20 compared with over 4000 mm³ in controls. These results suggest that i.t. delivery of rSVA-CXCL11 and the combination cocktails can induce potent tumor control, prolonged survival, and evidence of long-term protective immunity in this model.

Recombinant SVA therapy enhances cDC1 trafficking and activation, promotes proinflammatory macrophage polarization

We next evaluated the early immune events in the tdLNs. Combination therapy significantly increased migratory CD103+ cDC1 frequencies (figure 4A), indicating enhanced trafficking from tumor to tdLN. Major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression on cDC1 (figure 4B) and CD69 on T cells (figure 4C) rose following combination therapy or rSVA-vXCL1 monotherapy, consistent with improved cDC1 migration and subsequent T-cell priming. Notably, these effects were more pronounced in the combination group, implying a synergistic enhancement of antigen presentation and downstream priming of adaptive immunity.

To evaluate the remodeling of innate immune cells within the TME following treatment, we analyzed the infiltration and composition of cDC and macrophage subsets. Within tumors, combination therapy increased CD11c+MHC-II+ cDCs among CD45+ cells (figure 5A), driven by a selective expansion of XCR1+ cDC1 (figure 5B), a subset critically important for cross-presentation and CD8+ T cell activation, with concomitant reduction of CD11b+ cDC2 (figure 5C), shifting the cDC1/cDC2 ratio toward a cross-presenting phenotype. Macrophage profiling showed higher TAM infiltration (figure 5D), elevated CD86+ M1-like cells (figure 5E), reduced CD206+ M2-like cells (figure 5F), and an increased M1/M2 ratio (online supplemental figure S4). These results demonstrate that combination therapy not only promotes immunogenic DC responses but also effectively expands and reprograms TAMs toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype.

CXCR3+, CD4+ and CD8+ T cell frequencies versus control (figures6 7). rSVA-CXCL11 and Combo significantly enriched CXCR3+ T cells (figure 6A) in both CD4+ and CD8+ compartments (figure 6B,C), consistent with CXCL11–CXCR3-driven recruitment. Functionally, therapies enhanced activation (CD69+) and effector (IFN-γ+) responses across CXCR3+, CD4+, and CD8+ subsets (figure 7A–F), with Combo and rSVA-CXCL11 showing the strongest effects.

Notably, although rSVA-CXCL11 achieved comparable or higher CXCR3+ T cell infiltration than Combo, activation intensity (CD69 and IFN-γ) was greater with Combo. We interpret this ‘quantity versus quality’ gap as a consequence of antigen presentation capacity: CXCL11 drives T cell influx, but without vXCL1-mediated cDC1 recruitment, priming is limited; the combo therapy coordinates both axes, yielding superior effector function.

To further functionally validate the i.t. CD8+ T-cell response and to address the possibility that an increased T-cell frequency could reflect a terminally dysfunctional phenotype, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining on day 13 tumor sections for CD8 together with the cytotoxic effector molecule granzyme B (GZMB) or the activation/exhaustion-associated receptor PD-1 (online supplemental figure S5). Quantification showed that treatment groups with improved tumor control were associated with higher densities of cytotoxic CD8+GZMB+ cells, with the strongest enrichment observed in the combination (Combo) group (online supplemental figure S5A–C). In contrast, PD-1+ CD8+ cells did not accumulate to the same extent, supporting the interpretation that the antitumor response involves functional effector CD8+ T cells rather than a predominantly terminally exhausted population (online supplemental figure S5D–F).

As shown in figure 8A, tumors exhibited loosely arranged melanoma cells, extensive inflammatory infiltration, and extracellular melanin deposition colocalized with lymphocyte-rich areas—indicating therapy-induced melanolysis, with the most prominent effects observed in the combination group. To confirm i.t. expression level of the engineered transgenes, IHC was conducted. Marked positive staining for vXCL1 and CXCL11 was observed in tumors treated with respective rSVA constructs (figure 8B–E), while only faint background staining was noted in control and parental SVA groups.

We further evaluated the biodistribution of viral particles by quantifying viral RNA in tumor and other tissues (figure 8F–L). All treatment groups displayed a consistent pattern: viral loads were significantly higher in tumors than in non-tumor tissues, indicating preferential replication at the tumor site. The highest tumor titers occurred with rSVA-CXCL11, rSVA-ΔssCXCL11, Combo, and ΔssCombo, paralleling efficacy (figure 8M). Low-level signals in lung, liver, and spleen—organs permissive for SVA in swine—were not associated with overt toxicity, as supported by stable body weights. These data collectively suggest that rSVA maintains tumor-selective tropism, while enabling robust transgene expression and enhanced therapeutic efficacy, and a favorable biosafety profile.

Discussion

This study establishes chemokine-armed rSVA as a self-amplifying i.t. gene-delivery platform capable of converting immunologically ‘cold’ melanoma into a T-cell–inflamed state. Encoding CXCL11 and vXCL1 coordinated CXCR3-directed effector T-cell recruitment with XCR1+ cDC1 licensing, yielding deeper tumor control, prolonged survival, and providing preliminary evidence suggestive of protection on tumor rechallenge.19 These observations support a design principle for multi-gene OV strategies: concurrently targeting leukocyte recruitment, antigen presentation, and effector activation provides complementary mechanisms and amplifies therapeutic benefit.

Clinically, armed OVs have already shown meaningful activity; Talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC) improved durable response in phase III OPTiM, validating local vector-based delivery as a route to systemic benefit.20 At the same time, randomized data with T-VEC plus anti-PD-1 indicate that OV–PD-1 combinations are not uniformly superior to PD-1 alone, underscoring the need for rational payload selection, dosing sequence, and biomarker-guided use.21 22 Within this translational context, our data offer a mechanistic rationale for pairing chemokine-armed rSVA with checkpoint blockade: vXCL1-mediated cDC1 recruitment and activation lowers the priming threshold for CXCL11-recruited CXCR3+ T cells, a configuration expected to enhance the depth and durability of PD-1 responses.

The centrality of cDC1 to cross-priming and even to responses under PD-1 blockade is well established.23 24 25 XCR1 marks cross-presenting DCs across species, and XCL1 selectively attracts this subset.26 27 28 Consistent with these principles, vXCL1 increased migratory CD103+/XCR1+ cDC1 and MHC-I in tdLNs, while the CXCL11+vXCL1 combination maximized downstream T-cell activation (CD69) and effector function (IFN-γ).29 These data align with a cooperative model in which cDC1 licensing and T-cell trafficking act in concert to raise both the quantity and the quality of i.t. immunity.

CXCR3 ligands govern T-cell ingress into tumors and correlate with CD8+ infiltration and clinical responsiveness in melanoma.5 30 31 Among them, CXCL11 exhibits high-affinity/biased signaling via CXCR3 and can tune cytotoxic lymphocyte trafficking and function.32 33 Here, rSVA-CXCL11 robustly expanded CXCR3+ T cells (‘quantity’), whereas the combination with vXCL1 further enhanced activation and effector readouts (‘quality’), consistent with division of labor: CXCL11 drives ingress; XCL1 reinforces cDC1-dependent priming and costimulation.

Signal sequence deletion marked improved transgene cassette stability during serial passaging and modestly increased VP1 transcript levels, consistent with reduced fitness cost associated with secreted payload expression. Although Δss reduced the detectable chemokine protein signal by Western blot in vitro, Δss and signal-sequence–retaining viruses produced comparable tumor growth inhibition in vivo (figure 3F). We interpret this as a net balance between secretion efficiency and vector performance/payload retention under a repeated i.t. dosing context, in which sufficient local chemokine exposure can still be achieved. Collectively, these data support Δss primarily as an engineering strategy that improves manufacturability and long-term payload fidelity without compromising therapeutic potency in this model.34 35 36 In parallel, 2A context effects (peptide identity, gene order, linkers) influence ribosomal skipping and polyprotein processing37 38; mutations near the TNPG↓P site likely impaired co-expression in some vXCL1 constructs. Iterative optimization of 2A elements and exploration of membrane-anchoring/cleavable designs or microenvironment-triggered release may further balance secretion, stability, and replication fitness.

As a platform, SVA combines tumor selectivity, favorable biosafety, and genetic tractability.14 15 39 Our biodistribution data showed preferential tumor replication without overt histopathology in non-tumor tissues and with stable body weight, consistent with reported preclinical safety.39 Given the conservation of cDC1 and CXCR3 axes across mammals, this approach may extend to veterinary oncology (eg, canine melanoma). Combination regimens with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors or low-dose radiotherapy/chemotherapy, guided by biomarkers such as baseline cDC1 abundance, CXCR3-ligand signatures, and IFN-γ-related programs, warrant investigation.

Limitations include reliance on the B16-F10/C57BL/6 model and the need to validate across additional tumor types and spontaneous/engineered models. In addition, the number of complete responders available for rechallenge was limited, and Δss constructs were not included in the i.t. myeloid profiling; future studies will directly compare Δss and secretion-competent constructs to determine whether myeloid remodeling is quantitatively altered. In future studies, spatial multi-omics should be integrated to chart ‘cold-to-hot’ transitions, refine dose and sequence to exploit cDC1-T-cell cooperativity, and develop manufacturability-aware cassette designs. Overall, chemokine-armed rSVA provides a mechanistically coherent and translationally promising route to orchestrate DC–T-cell cooperation and overcome barriers in immune-resistant tumors.
