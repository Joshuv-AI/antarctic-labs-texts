Resource Use Following Atrial Fibrillation Ablation: Spending Resources to Save Resources
Authors: Emily P Zeitler, Michael H Kim
Published: 2023

There is no question about the tremendous burden that atrial fibrillation (AF) places on patients with regard to quality of life, and this is undoubtedly impacted by frequent interaction with physicians and health care facilities. In turn, this cumulative effect generates a substantial burden on the health care system at large. 1 For some time, a prevailing hypothesis has been that deployment of the most effective treatment of AF, ablation, despite its resource intensiveness, may ultimately benefit patients and the system alike. In an attempt to explore this hypothesis in a real‐world setting, Dhande et al evaluated health care resource use in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA). 2

In this analysis, Dhande et al pooled the experience at the multihospital University of Pittsburgh Medical Center system that included nearly 3500 patients who underwent AF ablation procedures over the past decade and compared resource use before and after AF ablation. Resources examined included hospitalizations, visits to emergency departments, cardioversions, stress tests, and echocardiograms. AF‐related anticoagulant and antiarrhythmic prescriptions were also reported. These end points have the benefit of being relevant at the patient and system levels. The authors found that in the 2 years after AF ablation, on average, health care resource use decreased for every measure compared with the year before ablation. Unsurprisingly, this reduction in health care resource use, specifically reductions in AF hospitalizations and emergency department visits, translated to estimated cost savings of ≈$8000 per patient per year compared with an estimated cost of (each) ablation of $21 500. These savings would be muted by repeated ablation, which would be expected in a significant minority of patients after AF ablation; in this series, that amounted to 10% of the cohort in the first 2 years following ablation.

This observed reduction in health care resource use across several end points could conceivably translate to reduced morbidity among patients with AF. These findings build on similar findings from other cohorts, including Catheter ABlation vs ANtiarrhythmic Drug Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation 3 and patients with both AF and heart failure (HF) in particular. 4 Consider the case of antiarrhythmic drugs and their well‐appreciated adverse effect profile. In the 20% of the cohort prescribed amiodarone before ablation, only a quarter were still prescribed the drug 2 years after ablation. The impact of averted adverse effects from amiodarone exposure are not included in the authors' estimated cost‐effectiveness analyses but surely amount to more than a trivial impact on the value of an AF ablation. Indeed, the average time between diagnosis and AF ablation in this cohort was 486 days, during which time more than half of patients were prescribed an antiarrhythmic drug. On the other hand, in the absence of an antiarrhythmic drug or ablation, many patients were not receiving rhythm control care despite clear benefits from early intervention. 5 , 6 So, in addition to health care resource use related to AF, losses in quality of life were likely accumulated for many patients during this time. This highlights the need for streamlined and multidisciplinary systems that bring optimal care to patients with AF in a timely manner, an aspirational goal for all.

Compared with prior studies, the study by Dhande et al is strengthened by its all‐comer population with regard to patients and operators. The patient population studied appears to generally reflect the contemporary group of patients with AF treated with ablation. This includes a high burden of comorbidities, including obesity, with nearly two‐thirds of patients having body mass index ≥30 kg/m2 (and 16% having body mass index ≥40 kg/m2) as well as a quarter having HF, although it is unknown what proportion had normal versus reduced left ventricular function. The multihospital system presumably includes a variety of operators using a variety of approaches, techniques, and technology over the study period. These factors improve the generalizability of the findings. The study is further strengthened by medium‐term follow‐up (2 years for each patient).

Like most real‐world cohorts of patients with AF receiving ablation, women were underrepresented, with just 34% of the cohort, despite making up ≈50% of patients with AF. This is notable because there appears to be differences in clinical variables, quality of life, and health care resource use between men and women with AF with and without ablation treatment. 7 , 8 Furthermore, because other demographic data were not reported, the applicability of these findings to other groups cannot be assessed. For example, the average age in this AF ablation cohort was 63 years, nearly 9 years younger than the average Medicare beneficiary undergoing AF ablation. 9 This is important as the population of patients undergoing AF ages because the complications from AF ablation are generally appreciated to increase with age, which leads to more resource use and reduced cost‐effectiveness. 10 , 11 Indeed, Dhande et al found just such a signal in their cohort, with age >65 years predicting increased health care resource use.

The period of study, 2013 to 2020, was a time of rapid evolution in the tools available for AF ablation that improve procedural speed, safety, and effectiveness. This includes things like dramatic improvements in electroanatomic mapping and intracardiac echocardiography, just to name 2 of the more expensive developments. On the other hand, as safety and efficiency of AF ablation have improved, eligibility for the procedure has expanded to less selected populations who may be more prone to emergency department visits and hospitalization, like patients with HF. Thus, it is possible that patients in the early versus later parts of the cohort had differential postablation resource use and cost‐effectiveness, but no associations between procedural date and resource use or patient demographics were reported.

Prior work has highlighted the variable nature in the cost of AF ablation driven by operator choices in equipment. 12 With an increase in options for AF ablation protocols ranging from energy sources to vascular closure devices, 13 this variability is likely to increase. No doubt this variability extends beyond operator choices to other aspects of the procedure, like facility protocols, 14 including the prevalence of same‐day discharge, 15 an increasingly popular strategy in the wake of COVID‐19. Furthermore, how this will change with ongoing evolution in AF ablation technology, like pulsed‐field ablation, or systems of care, like outpatient surgery centers, remains to be seen. Thus, although reassuring, these findings of cost‐effectiveness will not be the last word on the topic. Moreover, dramatic global variability in practice patterns and reimbursement for AF care means that the cost savings reported in this analysis should not be applied to other health care systems in which the cost‐effectiveness of AF ablation has been variable. 16 , 17 , 18

Dhande et al have given us a window into the day‐to‐day realities of arrhythmia care in clinical practice. For example, despite nearly a quarter of the cohort having HF, less than 2% were prescribed the newest additions to the contemporary 4‐drug approach to guideline‐directed medical therapy (angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitors and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors). Although data are conflicting about whether this contemporary medical therapy will have a major independent impact on AF burden among patients with HF, proper application could significantly impact health care resource use because these therapies have been demonstrated to be cost‐effective in their own right. 19 Given the substantial overlap between patients with HF and AF, there is clearly room to optimize the parallel administration of care for both conditions.

Visits to the emergency department and hospitalizations are critical health care resource use end points for patients with AF. However, given the highly complex and multidisciplinary nature of AF, not all measures that impact cost and resource use could realistically be captured in a single cohort study. Some important end points not captured here include things like dementia, death, stroke, and pacemaker implantation, not to mention patient‐reported outcomes and quality of life. Measuring the impact of contemporary AF ablation on end points like this will require ongoing investigation.
