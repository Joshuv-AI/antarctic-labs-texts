The Varying Faces of Vulvar Neoplasia
Authors: Kavitha Athota, Smitha S. Prabhu, Niranjana Gopinathan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vulvar neoplasia is on the rise and patients often present to dermatologists with lesions of varying morphology associated with pruritus, burning sensation, or pain. Early diagnosis results in an early treatment and improved quality of life. Differentiating vulvar neoplasia and intraepithelial malignancies from benign and inflammatory conditions is imperative. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC) are helpful. Herein, we report eight women who presented with varying features. Five of them had predisposing factors- four had lichen sclerosus, and one was human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) positive. All women were diagnosed and treated promptly. Vulvar malignancies encompass both invasive vulvar squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and its precursors, as well as other less common forms like melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and extramammary Paget’s disease. Vulvar SCC contributes to 80%–90% of vulvar malignancies. It’s precursor lesions, vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN), are now classified as differentiated VIN (dVIN), low-grade squamous intraepithelial neoplasias (LSIL), and high grade squamous intraepithelial neoplasias (HSIL). VIN are of varying morphology and are diagnosed by histopathology and IHC. Dermoscopy of VIN is still evolving. Herein, we also describe the dermoscopic features of lesions encountered. Treatment is resection, wherever possible.

Introduction

Vulvar neoplasia often presents to dermatologists due to associated symptoms of pruritus and burning sensation. Early diagnosis is desirable for early treatment to preserve the structural integrity. Non-specific pruritus, the dominant symptom, is often unhelpful in diagnosis as it occurs in other vulvar conditions. The lesions are generally mistaken for acute vulvitis, psoriasis, lichen simplex chronicus, lichen sclerosus (LS), or Paget’s disease. Only histopathology may help in diagnosis. In cases of multicentric lesions, multiple biopsies are advisable as prompt management goes a long way in preserving the quality of life of the patient.

We present a case series of eight patients with vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) or frank carcinoma, of whom some had benign vulvar precursor lesions.

Report of Cases

Case 1

A 42-year-old woman, a previously diagnosed case of vulvar LS, lost to follow-up during the COVID period. She presented 2 years later with pruritic painful whitish warty eroded growth on the left labia [Figure 1a]. Dermoscopy with a Dermlite DL4 dermoscope showed milky-white structureless areas, polymorphous vessels, and red globules [Figure 1b]. Histopathology showed a well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (SCC).

(a) Whitish warty eroded growth on left labia. (b) Milky white structureless areas, polymorphous vessels, red globules, and fingerlike pattern on dermoscopy with Dermlite D4, 10 X magnification, polarised mode

Case 2

A 60-year-old woman, who was undergoing treatment for vulvar LS, presented with dyspareunia and vulvar pain of 2 years duration. The vulvar architecture was completely distorted, and erosions were seen on the labia. The entire periurethral area was filled with pus. Multiple biopsies taken from different sites revealed features of LS in four and SCC in one.

Case 3

A 34-year-old woman presented with vulvar pain and dyspareunia and was prescribed oral antifungals from elsewhere. Vulvar examination revealed eroded reddish plaque [Figure 2a]. Human papillomavirus (HPV) genotyping was not done. Histopathology showed VIN, and hemi- vulvectomy was done. After 1 year, the patient presented with an asymptomatic large erythematous nodular mass arising from the left labia [Figure 2b], the histopathology of which revealed SCC.

(a) Eroded, reddish plaque on labia. (b) Erythematous nodular mass arising from left labia

Case 4

A 60-year-old woman presented with vulvar pruritus of 2 months. She was diagnosed as a case of vulvar LS but was not prescribed any treatment. On examination, a warty indurated growth with multiple erosions was seen on the left labia [Figure 3a]. Dermoscopy showed white to grey white structureless areas, polymorphous vessels, few red globules and dots, X-shaped ulcer, and peripheral pigmentation. Histopathology revealed VIN [Figure 3b].

(a) Warty indurated growth with central erosions on the left labia on a background of lichen sclerosus. (b) Histopathological examination showing hyperkeratosis and acanthosis, reported as VIN (H&E 20x)

Case 5

A 40-year-old woman on anti-retroviral therapy presented with pruritus vulvae of 3 months. Clinical examination revealed a warty growth from the clitoris and ulceration of the left labia with no inguinal lymphadenopathy. Histopathology revealed features of VIN.

Case 6

A 50-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with a painful, pruritic lesion of 6 months duration on the vulva. On examination, a whitish, hyperkeratotic warty growth with a well-defined margin was seen near the vulval fourchette. Dermoscopy showed whitish verrucous excrescences on an amorphous, pink background. Histopathology showed no signs of HPV infection. Nuclear atypia was seen throughout the thickness of the epidermis, with features suggestive of VIN.

Case 7

A 59-year-old woman with an 8-year history of vulvar LS presented with pruritic, indurated hyperkeratotic vulvar growth. Dermoscopy showed milky white verrucous areas with polymorphous vessels. Histopathology showed features of VIN.

Case 8

A 50-year-old post-menopausal woman presented with vulvar pain of 4- year duration. She had no relief with prior treatment with oral antifungals and topical steroid and antifungal combinations. On examination, reddish lesions on the periurethral part, vestibule, and clitoris were seen [Figure 4a]. Histopathology showed epithelium with nuclear pleomorphism and increased mitotic figures, which was suggestive of VIN [Figure 4b].

(a) Reddish erosion in the periurethral part, vestibule, and clitoris. (b) Histopathological examination showing epithelium with hyperkeratosis and acanthosis (H&E 20x)

Treatment administered

All patients, except the case 5, were referred to the department of surgical oncology for further management. The case 5 refused any sort of surgery, and she was started on imiquimod 5% cream application, 5 days a week for 4 months, with a good response.

Discussion

Vulvar carcinoma encompasses both invasive SCC and its precursor lesions. In the older nomenclature, precursor lesions (including high-grade premalignant disease and SCC in situ) were referred to as VIN and were subdivided into two categories. Usual-type VIN (uVIN) was associated with HPV and was mainly observed in younger women, leading to warty/basaloid SCC. Differentiated VIN (dVIN) was HPV-independent and predominantly seen after menopause in vulvar LS and lichen planus (LP), leading to keratinizing SCC.[1] The pathogenic mechanisms also corresponded - HPV-associated VIN in younger women lacked TP53 mutation, instead showed PIK3CA mutation and was vaginal. Older women with chronic vulvar inflammatory precursor lesions had associated TP53 mutation.[2]

Current classification groups all lesions from the vulva and anus into “lower anogenital squamous intraepithelial lesions,” with three subtypes occurring in the vulva: low grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL), high grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) and dVIN. HPV infection related changes are usually LSIL, whereas lesions previously designated as uVIN are HSIL.[3]

Unlike cervical cancer rates, vulvar cancer rates continue to increase exponentially with age, this being the most common anogenital cancer in women aged 70 and above. The total incidence has increased by 4.6% on an average every 5 years, and this increase is particularly notable in women younger than 60.[4]

Vulvar SCC is the most common vulvar malignancy (80%–90%), more common in the older age group at around 65–74 years of age, and is usually associated with a pre-existing VIN, in particular differentiated VIN (dVIN), which is often associated with vulvar LS and vulvar LP. Other vulvar malignancies include malignant melanomas, basal cell carcinoma, and verrucous carcinoma.[5]

In our case series, most women were peri- and post- menopausal and their age ranged from 42 to 60 years. In a hospital-based retrospective series of 77 patients, the most common presenting symptoms of vulvar cancers included growth on the genital area (88.3%), pruritus (71.4%), and bleeding (35%).[6] Three of our patients had only pruritus, whereas two had pruritus and pain, two had pain and dyspareunia, and one had pain alone. Warty indurated growth predominated, followed by erythematous plaques and erosions. Four of our patients had LS as a precursor lesion.

The diagnosis is usually made based on clinical suspicion, which is confirmed histopathologically. Dermoscopy of VIN is still an evolving science, and lesions in dVIN have been varyingly reported as having a cerebriform pattern, papillomatous lesions with hyperkeratotic scales, dotted and glomerular vessels, a pinkish-white background with curved and serpentine vessels. Pigmented dots in parallel fashion and well-defined borders are seen in uVIN.[78]

Prevention has been stressed upon. Primary prevention in the form of HPV vaccination, particularly for the HPV 16 subtype, which causes HSIL and SCC of the vulva, has been available to most communities for more than a decade now.[1] Though there is no specific screening for vulvar cancers, as a modality for secondary prevention, women should be encouraged to seek early treatment for any vulvar symptomatology, and all women diagnosed with vulvar LS and LP should be counseled regarding the possibility of malignant transformation and the need for regular follow-up. Any suspicious growth on the vulvar area should be promptly biopsied. Women with SIL of the perineal region should be thoroughly evaluated to rule out VIN. Tertiary prevention involves prompt diagnosis and treatment of all premalignant lesions, including VIN.

For dVIN, an excisional biopsy with a margin of 5 to 10 mm is sufficient, as the surrounding tissue is usually disease-free. For uVIN, excision with a margin of 5 to 10 mm and depth of 4 mm is commonly followed but can cause psychosocial morbidity if the lesion is huge. Less scarring alternatives include carbon dioxide laser ablation, 5% imiquimod cream, and topical cidofovir; however, these do not ensure adequate management of occult lesions, if any. The recurrence rate is more than 30% irrespective of the modality used; hence, regular follow-up is mandatory for at least 3 to 5 years.

In advanced cancer, which is the scenario in more than 50% of cases, chemo-radiotherapy remains the feasible option. Targeted therapy with epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitor erlotinib, vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor bevacizumab, and programmed cell death protein –1 monoclonal antibody pembrolizumab are being tried for metastatic vulvar cancer.[9]

Management outcomes depend on tumor size, depth, margins, lymph node invasion, and various other factors.[10] Hence, the stress is upon early diagnosis, which is often the forte of dermatologists. Treatment of involved lymph nodes, sentinel lymph nodes, and management of advanced vulvar cancer is out of scope of this article.

In conclusion, VIN and vulvar malignancies are on the rise, and a discerning eye of a dermatologist goes a long way in preserving the anatomical structure of the area as well as improving the quality of life of the patient.
