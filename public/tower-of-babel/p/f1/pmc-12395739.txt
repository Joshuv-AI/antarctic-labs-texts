Opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use among youth: psychometric properties of novel measures
Authors: Mallie J. Paschall, Joel W. Grube, Kirsten Helgeson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the psychometric properties of novel multi-item measures of exposure to opportunities for alcohol use, cannabis use, and simultaneous use (SAM) among youth.MethodsSurveys were administered to 1,082 15- to 20-year-olds in 40 California cities in 2023-24. The surveys included questions about how often respondents had an opportunity to engage in alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM in different contexts/situations in the past year (e.g., at home), beliefs related to these substance use behaviors, and demographic characteristics. A second-order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and correlation and regression analyses were conducted to assess the psychometric properties of the exposure to substance use opportunities measures.ResultsThe CFA indicated that survey items loaded on three primary factors (five items per factor) corresponding to opportunities for alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM. Factor loadings ranged from 0.61 to 0.90, and the model fit was acceptable [NFI = 0.91; CFI = 0.94; RMSEA = 0.043 (95% CI = 0.036, 0.049), and SRMRu = 0.085]. The internal reliabilities (ω) of the substance-specific latent variables ranged from 0.82 to 0.89. The three substance specific factors, in turn, loaded significantly on an overall opportunities factor (λs = 0.78 − 0.89). The internal reliability (ω) for the overall opportunities latent variable was 0.85. Correlation analyses indicated moderate to strong associations in the expected directions between the exposure to opportunities measures and normative beliefs, perceived availability and perceived harm of alcohol, cannabis, and simultaneous use and corresponding substance use behaviors. Regression analyses indicated significant unique associations between exposure to opportunities measures and substance use behaviors when controlling related beliefs and demographic characteristics.ConclusionsThis study provides support for the validity and internal reliability of novel exposure to substance use opportunities measures. Measures for exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM will be useful for research on the etiology and prevention of the substance use behaviors and related problems among youth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03384-7.

Introduction

Alcohol and cannabis remain the most commonly used psychoactive substances among youth [1, 2], and simultaneous use of these substances is of growing concern as more jurisdictions legalize adult recreational use of cannabis [3–5]. Studies conducted during the past decade with adolescents and young adults indicate that use and simultaneous use of alcohol and cannabis (SAM) is associated with a range of negative consequences, including cognitive impairment, driving while intoxicated, unwanted sex, hangovers, injury, and increased risk for alcohol, cannabis, and mental health disorders [3, 6–12]. Understanding and preventing these risky behaviors remains a public health priority [13, 14].

Social cognitive theories such as the Integrated Model of Behavior Change [15] and Reasoned Action Approach [16] posit that behavior is influenced by environmental constraints in addition to beliefs and other personal predispositions. Environmental constraints include formal and informal strategies to prevent alcohol and cannabis use, simultaneous use and related harms among youth (e.g., minimum legal age of 21 for alcohol and cannabis sales, service, possession and use; zero-tolerance drink-driving laws; social host liability laws; parental monitoring). These strategies are aimed at limiting opportunities for obtaining and using these substances in different contexts and situations [14]. Thus, exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM may mediate effects of environmental constraints such as legal age restrictions on these substance use behaviors. Exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM may also moderate effects of beliefs related to these behaviors such as normative beliefs or perceived harm.

Understanding whether and how exposure to opportunities affects alcohol and cannabis use and SAM among young people requires a direct assessment of this construct. However, no direct measures of exposure to substance use opportunities have been developed, and research to date has been based on indirect or proxy measures of opportunities, such as retail availability, use of commercial and social sources, and perceived availability of alcohol and cannabis [17–20]. Although these studies provide a basis for inferring that greater exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM is associated with a greater likelihood of these behaviors, they do not provide a clear understanding of the unique role that exposure to opportunities may play in the etiology and prevention of alcohol and cannabis use, SAM, and related harms among youth.

Opportunities for using alcohol and cannabis may occur in a variety of contexts and situations and may be planned or unplanned. For example, a young person may have the opportunity to drink an alcoholic beverage or use some form of cannabis when spending time with a friend or acquaintance; while at home, with or without their parents’ knowledge and permission; while at a restaurant, bar, or nightclub; while at a concert or sporting event; or while at an outdoor setting such as a park or beach [21–24]. In some contexts or situations (e.g., at home or an outdoor setting), a young person may be exposed to these substance use opportunities while alone. Youth who have previously engaged in alcohol or cannabis use may seek out opportunities to continue these behaviors (e.g., with substance using peers, obtaining these substances from trusted sources). Greater exposure to such opportunities directly increases social and physical availability of alcohol and cannabis. More frequent exposure may also contribute to beliefs supportive of alcohol and cannabis use and SAM, including greater perceived approval/disapproval and perceived use by friends or parents (i.e., prescriptive and descriptive norms); greater perceived availability of alcohol and cannabis; and lower perceived harm of using alcohol and cannabis [17– [19, 23]. Consequently, greater exposure to these opportunities is likely to be associated with higher levels of alcohol and cannabis use and SAM among young people.

This study addresses this research gap by evaluating the psychometric properties of novel multi-item measures of exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM. The measures were first developed through a pilot study with a convenience sample of 92 college students in the San Francisco Bay area. The pilot study included a survey with questions about various contexts/situations that could provide an opportunity for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM among adolescents and young adults. These questions were adapted from questions used in prior studies that investigated contexts/situations where alcohol and cannabis use and SAM often occur among adolescents and young adults (e.g., at home with or without parents’ permission; with friends who are under or over 21 years old; while at a restaurant, bar, or nightclub) [8, 21–26]. Specifically, the wording for the opportunity questions was: “During the past 12 months, how often did you have the opportunity to use [alcohol, cannabis, SAM] in the following situations even if you did not do so?” Preliminary analyses with data from this pilot study suggested that the multi-item measures have good psychometric properties with respect to internal reliability and construct validity, providing a basis for a more systematic investigation of these measures with a larger sample of youth.

We first assess a hypothesized three-factor structure corresponding to alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM with respect to convergent and discriminant validity and internal reliability of the multi-item measures. We then test the following hypotheses to assess their construct validity: greater exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM will be associated with (a) more favorable normative beliefs about these behaviors, (b) greater perceived availability of alcohol and cannabis, (c) lower perceived harm associated with these behaviors, and (d) higher levels of alcohol and cannabis use and SAM among youth. We also hypothesize that the multi-item opportunities exposure measures will be uniquely related to these behaviors when controlling for other important beliefs and youth demographic characteristics.

Methods

Study sample and survey methods

A sample of 1,082 15- to 20-year-olds was recruited for an online survey conducted in 40 California cities from December 2023 to September 2024. The study focuses on underage youth in order to improve understanding of the role of exposure to alcohol and cannabis use and SAM opportunities in the etiology and prevention of these behaviors. The cities are located in 20 counties in the northern, central and southern regions of California. Population sizes ranged from 83,676 to 1,021,795. Survey recruitment methods included sending postcards to randomly selected samples of households in each city and advertising on social media platforms (e.g., Meta, Instagram). Because it was not possible to determine the number of households that received a postcard and had an eligible participant or the number of eligible individuals who viewed the social media ads, we were not able to determine the survey response rate. Respondents provided informed consent/assent to participate in the study prior to beginning the survey. Parental consent was obtained for those under 18 years of age. All study procedures were approved by the Pacific Institute for Research and Evaluation IRB (Federalwide Assurance # FWA00003078).

Survey questions

Novel survey measures for this study include opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM, prescriptive and descriptive norms related to simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use, and perceived harm of simultaneous alcohol and cannabis use. The items for these measures are included as supplemental material. Other survey measures have been used in previous studies [1, 2, 4]17– [19].

Opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM

Respondents were asked, “During the past 12 months, how often did you have the opportunity to (a) drink alcohol?; (b) smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles?; (c) drink alcohol and smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles so the effects overlapped in each of the following situations, even if you did not do so?”. Based on the pilot study with college students in the San Francisco Bay Area, the following eight contexts/situations were included for each question: “With someone you know who is 21 or older,” “With someone you know who is under 21,” “At a restaurant,” “At a bar, pub, or nightclub,” “At home with your parents’ permission,” “At home without your parents’ permission,” “At an outdoor setting like a park or beach,” and “At a concert or sporting event.” We note that exposure to opportunities in some of these contexts/situations (e.g., at home or an outdoor setting) may occur when a young person is alone. Possible responses for each question were “Never,” “Once or twice,” “3 or 4 times,” “5 or 6 times,” “More than 6 times,” and “Prefer not to say.” The first five response options were coded 1 to 5 and the final response option was coded to missing.

Because they were highly correlated, items representing opportunities to use with friends ≥ 21 and < 21; at restaurants and at bars, pubs and night clubs; and at home with and without parent’s permission were combined into three measures corresponding to friends; restaurants, bars, pubs, and night clubs; and home, respectively, by taking the mean of the relevant items.

Alcohol use and heavy drinking

Respondents were first provided with a definition and examples of a whole alcoholic drink (e.g., 12 oz. can of beer, 5 oz. glass of wine, 1.5 oz. shot of liquor). Respondents were then asked, “Have you ever, even once, had a whole drink of an alcoholic beverage?” Respondents who answered “yes” to this question were then asked, “In the past 12 months, on about how many days did you have a whole drink of an alcoholic beverage?” and “In the past 12 months, on the days when you drank alcohol, about how many whole drinks did you usually have?” using an open-ended response format. Responses to these questions were multiplied to create a past-year frequency × quantity measure of alcohol use. Respondents were also asked, “In the past 12 months, on the days when you drank alcohol, on about how many days did you have (4 if female/5 if male) or more whole drinks in a row - that is within 2 hours?” Due to skewed distributions, these variables were log-transformed.

Cannabis use

Respondents were provided with a description and examples of different forms of cannabis, including marijuana that is smoked (leaves or buds), concentrates (e.g., oil, wax, shatter, dabs), and edibles (e.g., food, drinks, candies). Respondents were then asked, “Have you ever, even once, smoked marijuana, used cannabis concentrates, or consumed edibles?” Respondents who answered “yes” to this question were then asked, “In the past 12 months, on about how many days, if any, did you (a) smoke marijuana?; (b) use cannabis concentrates?; (c) consume edibles?” using an open-ended response format. Respondents were also asked, “On those days when you smoked marijuana in the past 12 months, about how many hits or puffs did you usually take?”; “On those days when you used cannabis concentrates in the past 12 months, about how many hits or puffs did you usually take?”; “On those days when you ate or drank edibles in the past 12 months, about how many edibles did you usually eat or drink (about how many gummies, beverages, etc.)?” Responses to each pair of questions were multiplied to create a frequency × quantity measure for each form of cannabis use. These three measures were summed to create an overall measure of cannabis use. Due to the skewed distribution, this variable was log-transformed.

Simultaneous use

Respondents who reported alcohol and cannabis use in the past 12 months were asked, “On the days when you drank alcohol in the past 12 months, on about how many of those days did you smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles at the same time or within 2 hours of drinking alcohol so the effects overlapped?” using an open-ended response format. A question about typical quantities of alcohol and different forms of cannabis when used together was not asked because of the difficulty of recalling and reporting such details. Due to the skewed distribution, this variable was log-transformed.

Prescriptive norms

Respondents were asked, “How much do you think your parents or guardians would disapprove or approve if (a) you were to regularly drink alcohol?; (b) you were to regularly smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles?; (c) once or twice you were to try smoking marijuana, using cannabis, or consuming edibles at the same time or within 2 hours of drinking alcohol so the effects overlapped?” Parallel questions were asked for “your closest friends” and “other good friends.” Four possible responses for these questions were “Strongly disapprove,” “Disapprove,” “Approve,” and Strongly approve.” Items for parents’ disapproval/approval of alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM were examined separately. The mean was computed for each set of items for friends’ disapproval/approval of alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM. The Sperman’s ρ reliability coefficients for the three friends’ disapproval/approval item sets were 0.82, 0.89, and 0.91, respectively.

Descriptive norms

Respondents were asked, “How often do you think your parents (a) drink alcohol?; (b) smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles?; (c) smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles at the same time or within 2 hours of drinking alcohol so the effects overlap?” Parallel questions were asked for “your closest friends” and “other good friends.” Seven possible responses to these questions ranged from “Never” to “Every day.” Items for parents’ alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM were examined separately. The mean was computed for each set of two items corresponding to friends’ alcohol use, cannabis use, and SAM. Spearman’s coefficients for these measures were 0.79, 0.80, and 0.80, respectively.

Perceived availability

Respondents were asked, “In general, how easy or difficult do you think it would be for you to get alcohol if you wanted to?”; and “In general, how easy or difficult do you think it would be for you get marijuana, cannabis concentrates, or edibles if you wanted to?” Four possible responses were “Very easy,” “Easy,” “Difficult,” and “Very difficult.” Response values were reverse coded so that higher values represented greater perceived availability of alcohol and cannabis.

Perceived harm

Respondents were asked, “How much do you think people risk harming themselves (physically or in other ways), if they (a) try alcohol once or twice?; (b) drink alcohol occasionally?; (c) drink alcohol regularly?” They were also asked, “How much do you think people risk harming themselves (physically or in other ways), if they try (a) smoking marijuana, using cannabis concentrates, or consuming edibles once or twice?; (b) smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles occasionally?; (c) smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles regularly?” Additionally, they were asked, “How much do you think people risk harming themselves, (a) if once or twice they try smoking marijuana, using cannabis concentrates, or consuming edibles at the same time or within 2 hours of drinking alcohol so the effects overlap?; (b) if they occasionally smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles at the same time or within 2 hours of drinking alcohol so the effects overlap?; (c) if they regularly smoke marijuana, use cannabis concentrates, or consume edibles at the same time or within 2 hours of drinking alcohol so the effects overlap?” Four possible responses were “No risk,” “Slight risk,” “Moderate risk,” and “Great risk.” The mean was computed for each set of three items corresponding to alcohol and cannabis use and SAM. The internal reliability (Cronbach’s α) for these multi-item scales was 0.78, 0.88, and 0.89, respectively.

Demographic characteristics

Respondents were asked to report their age in years, sex assigned at birth, whether their ethnicity is Hispanic or Latino/a, and their racial group(s) (American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, African American, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, White, Other). Respondents who selected more than one racial group were classified as “Multi-racial,” and those who didn’t select any racial group(s) were classified as “Unknown race.”

Data analysis

Of 1,082 youth recruited for the study, 1,051 (97%) had complete data for the opportunities measures that were included in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). For correlation and regression analyses, 962 (89%) had complete data for all study variables, and 74 (6.8%) had only one missing value. Little’s test indicated that the data were missing completely at random (χ2 [2,939] = 3057.5, p = .06). We used the expectation maximization (EM) procedure to generate imputed values for missing data using all study variables. Comparisons of EM and FIML show that they produce very similar estimates of model parameters and standard errors [27]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted without imputed values. Sample weights were developed using 2023 census population estimates to reflect age, gender, and ethnic/race group characteristics of the 15- to 20-year-old population in the 40 cities. Descriptive statistics were first examined for all study variables.

To assess convergent and discriminant validity of exposure to opportunities items, the hypothesized structure of the scale items was tested using maximum likelihood CFA using EQS version 6.3 [28]. Based on conceptual considerations, the model specified three first-order factors corresponding to exposures to substance-specific opportunities for use of alcohol, cannabis, and SAM. Items were only allowed to load on their substance use-specific factor. The underlying latent variables were expected to be correlated, but also distinct given differences in the social and legal contexts relating to each type of substance use. In addition, treating the substance use domains as separate factors in the analyses will assist researchers in making informed decisions when using these measures to study a single area of substance use. A second-order factor represented overall exposures to substance use opportunities across the substance use domains (i.e., a latent variable representing overall exposure to substance use opportunities). To account for shared measurement variances of opportunity contexts (e.g., home) across substances, the item uniquenesses (error terms) for a given context were allowed to correlate across alcohol, cannabis, and SAM. We assessed fit using the Normed Fit Index (NFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Root Mean-Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and the unbiased Standardized Root Mean-Square Residual (SRMRu) [29, 30]. We also report the χ2 statistic. Fit indices ≥ 0.90, a RMSEA ≤ 0.05, and an SRMRu ≤ 0.10 were considered indicative of adequate fit. Because the data were non-normally distributed, Satorra-Bentler robust estimates were obtained for the standard errors, the χ2, NFI, CFI, and RMSEA [31]. Sample weights were applied to approximate population values for the model parameters. Sensitivity analyses using the four combinations of weighted/unweighted data and imputed/non-imputed data were conducted.

To test study hypotheses regarding construct validity, correlations between the exposure to opportunities for alcohol, cannabis, and SAM measures and the corresponding beliefs and behaviors were first examined. Multi-level, mixed effects linear regression analyses were then conducted to examine associations between the opportunities exposure measures and corresponding substance use behaviors, controlling for beliefs and youth demographic characteristics. Analyses were conducted with Stata version 18 with sample weights and Huber-White robust standard errors adjusted for clustering of observations within cities [32].

The 0.05 threshold for statistical significant was used for all analyses. The “collin” procedure in Stata was used to assess the potential for multicollinearity among predictor variables in regression analyses. Variance inflation factors for all predictor variables were less than 5.0 (mean = 1.81, range: 1.02 to 3.40), indicating no significant multicollinearity. The relative strength of association between exposure to substance use opportunities and other study variables was based on standardized correlation coefficients in bivariate analyses and standardized beta coefficients for non-categorical variables in regression analyses. Based on Cohen [33], we interpreted coefficients < 0.3 as weak associations, 0.3–0.49 as moderate associations, and ≥ 0.5 as strong associations.

To examine whether or not exposure to these substance use opportunities is simply an indicator of anticipated or planned behavior, we also examined the percentages of youth who did not report any alcohol, cannabis, or simultaneous alcohol-cannabis use in the past year, but did report having at least one opportunity to engage in each of these substance use behaviors.

Results

Sample characteristics

Descriptive statistics for sample characteristics are provided in Table 1, indicating that the sample was demographically diverse (mean age = 18.8, 48.5% female, 55% Hispanic, 10.4% non-Hispanic [NH] Asian, 5.8% NH Black, 17% NH White, and 10.3% NH Multi-racial). About half of the sample (48.9%) reported any alcohol use in the past year, while 29.9% reported any heavy drinking, 37.5% reported any cannabis use, and 20.1% reported any SAM. Descriptive statistics also indicate that there was adequate variation in other study variables for analyses to assess the psychometric properties of exposure to substance use opportunities measures. We note that to reduce skewness, we winsorized and log transformed the alcohol use, heavy drinking, cannabis use, and SAM variables for subsequent analyses.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Detailed results of the CFA are presented in Table 2 and the standardized model is summarized in Fig. 1. Consistent with the hypothesized structure of the subscales, each of the opportunity items loaded significantly on its expected first-order factor relating to exposures within a substance use domain. All standardized factor loadings exceeded 0.60. The model fit was adequate: normed fit index (NFI) = 0.91; comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.94; root mean squared error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.043 [95% confidence interval (CI) = 0.036, 0.049]; and SRMRu = 0.085. The internal reliabilities (ω) for the three substance-specific latent variables were 0.82, 88, and 0.86. for alcohol, cannabis, and SAM opportunities, respectively. The three latent variables were significantly (ps < 0.01) correlated with one another: r = .61 for exposure to alcohol and cannabis opportunities; r = .63 for alcohol and simultaneous use opportunities; and r = .73 for cannabis and simultaneous use opportunities. All three loaded significantly on the second-order overall exposure factor with standardized factor loadings of 0.78 or higher in each case. The internal reliability for the overall substance use opportunities latent variable was good (ω = 0.85). Sensitivity analyses showed that the fit indices and model parameters (e.g., factor loadings, standard errors) were very similar across all four combinations of weighted/unweighted and imputed/non imputed data.

Correlations

Correlations between exposure to substance use opportunities measures, beliefs, and alcohol and cannabis use and SAM are provided in Table 3. The results indicate significant positive associations between exposure to substance use opportunities measures and normative beliefs, perceived availability of alcohol and cannabis, and past-year alcohol use, heavy drinking, cannabis use, and SAM. Exposure to opportunities for cannabis use and SAM were inversely associated with corresponding measures of perceived harm of cannabis use and SAM, while there was no association between exposure to opportunities for alcohol use and perceived harm of this behavior. Most of the associations between exposure to substance use opportunities and beliefs were moderate in size, while almost all associations with substance use behaviors were strong.

Sensitivity analyses

The correlation and regression analyses were also conducted without imputed values. Results of these analyses were very similar to results obtained with imputed data and are available upon request from the authors.

Exposure to opportunities among non-users

Among youth who did not report any past-year alcohol use, 63.4% reported exposure to at least one opportunity for alcohol use. Among youth who did not report any past-year cannabis use, 35.2% reported exposure to at least one opportunity to use some form of cannabis. Among youth who did not report any simultaneous alcohol-cannabis use in the past year, 41.8% reported exposure to at least one opportunity for this behavior.

Discussion

This study investigated the psychometric properties of novel multi-item measures of exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and simultaneous use of these substances. Although previous studies have included indirect or proxy measures for substance use opportunities [17–20], this is the first study to evaluate direct measures of exposure to alcohol and cannabis use and SAM opportunities in different contexts/situations. Results of CFA provide support for the discriminant and convergent validity and internal reliability of the three measures, while results of correlation analyses provide support for their construct validity. Results of regression analyses further indicate that greater exposure to opportunities to engage in alcohol and cannabis use and SAM are uniquely related to corresponding the substance use behaviors when controlling for important beliefs and demographic characteristics (e.g., age, normative beliefs, perceived harm). Additionally, substantial percentages of youth who reported never engaging in alcohol, cannabis or simultaneous use of these substances in the past year were exposed to at least one opportunity to engage in these behaviors, suggesting that exposure to opportunities for these forms of substance use are not just an indicator of anticipated or planned behavior.

The strength of the observed associations between measures for exposure to opportunities to engage in alcohol and cannabis use and SAM and other study variables supports the notion that exposure to substance use opportunities is not redundant with constructs such as perceived availability of alcohol and cannabis or normative beliefs, but may play a role in influencing or moderating effects of these beliefs as posited by social cognitive theories [15, 16]. Moreover, the exposure to substance use opportunities measures were more strongly associated with corresponding substance use behaviors than other covariates in regression analyses, indicating the importance of exposure to substance use opportunities in the etiology and prevention of substance use behaviors among youth.

Limitations

Findings of this study should be considered in light of several limitations. Although the sample is very diverse, it may not be representative of all 15- to 20-year-olds in California. Self-reports of exposure to opportunities for alcohol and cannabis use and SAM and corresponding substance use behaviors may be limited by social desirability and recall bias. The concern about social desirability (e.g., under-reporting of illegal substance use behaviors) is mitigated by survey self-administration and confidentiality. Use of frequency × quantity measures of alcohol and cannabis use and SAM based on open-ended questions may have introduced error variance due to highly skewed distributions. Questions regarding quantity of different types of cannabis are prone to variability due to differing potencies and dosing units. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the data limit our ability to make causal inferences regarding relationships between the exposure to opportunities measures and substance use behaviors.

Conclusions and implications

Our findings provide a basis for using these novel measures to further investigate the role that exposure to alcohol and cannabis use opportunities plays in the etiology and prevention of alcohol and cannabis use and SAM among adolescents and young adults. For example, exposure to such opportunities may affect substance use behaviors indirectly through their effects on normative beliefs, perceived availability and perceived harm. Exposure to such opportunities may also moderate effects of these beliefs on alcohol and cannabis use and SAM behaviors. These measures can be used to investigate whether formal and informal prevention strategies aimed at limiting opportunities for substance use among youth (e.g., minimum legal age of 21 for alcohol and cannabis sales, service, possession and use; zero-tolerance drink-driving laws; social host liability laws; parental monitoring) are effective.

Additionally, the exposure to substance use opportunities measures could be incorporated into interventions for prevention or treatment of alcohol and cannabis use and SAM as they help to identify contexts and situations that young people should avoid to reduce their risk for these substance use behaviors and related harms. They could also be included in assessments for substance use among youth and young adults to identify individuals at elevated risk for these behaviors and strategies to reduce their risk.
