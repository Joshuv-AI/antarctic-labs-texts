Identifying the Aggression Impulsive/Reactive (AIR) Profile in Youth With Behavioral Challenges
Authors: Ekaterina Stepanova, Joshua A. Langfus, Eric A. Youngstrom, Evangelia Fatourou, Kelsey M. Delph, Sebastian Nair, Robert L. Findling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlthough aggression in children and adolescents remains a leading cause of seeking mental health services, the nosology of aggression remains underdeveloped. Prior work characterized youth with aggression by using secondary analyses of large research protocol–based data sets, which revealed a profile characterized by high levels of aggression impulsive/reactive (AIR) and symptoms of hyperactivity/impulsivity. The goal of this study was to evaluate whether the AIR profile was present in a clinical sample using similar methodology with novel dimensional measures of AIR.MethodMedical records of patients 4 to 17 years of age who presented with behavior concerns to outpatient, inpatient, and emergency department settings were reviewed. Caregivers completed dimensional measures evaluating symptoms of aggression, mania, depression, and hyperactivity/impulsivity. Latent profile analysis was performed with indicators representing the same 6 content domains as in prior work: AIR, depression, mania, rule-breaking, self-harm, and hyperactivity-impulsivity.ResultsA total of 430 patients completed the questionnaires and were included in the analyses. Patients’ mean age was 12.52 years, and 53% were female. Four profiles were identified: predominantly AIR with hyperactivity-impulsivity (n = 83) and low mood symptoms; high AIR and high mood symptoms (n = 54); high AIR, mood, and self-harm (n = 56); and moderate overall symptom profile (n = 237). Children with high AIR and hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms were more likely to be younger and male (mean age, 9.8 years), to have younger ages of onset of aggressive behaviors (mean age, 5.1 years), and to be less likely to have an abuse history.ConclusionThis work further validated the previously described AIR profile in a new cohort of youth presenting for clinical care. Future studies should focus on developing diagnostic criteria for children with AIR.

Aggressive behaviors in children and adolescents can often have a significant impact on both patients and their families. These behaviors may impair the social interactions and academic performance of youth, who often require mental health services to evaluate and effectively manage these concerns.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 In fact, many children and adolescents present for help for aggressive behaviors through the emergency department (ED), which can contribute to patient and family stress and economic burdens, as well as using substantial resources.2,5,7, 8, 9, 10

Assessment and treatment of youth with aggressive behaviors can be particularly challenging because of their complex presentation and frequent comorbidity with other mental health problems.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 One of the challenges is that aggression does not present uniformly; it encompasses various types, with reactive (or impulsive) and proactive (instrumental) aggression being commonly described.15, 16, 17 Moreover, aggressive behaviors are not currently recognized as a distinct entity in the DSM-5-TR18 or the International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10).19 They can present in the context of multiple psychiatric conditions, such as attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), disruptive behavior disorders, intermittent explosive disorder (IED), mood disorders, psychotic disorders, and developmental disabilities, as well as separately from any mental health diagnoses.14 Only IED and disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) list aggression as part of their diagnostic criteria, whereas disruptive behavior disorders (DBDs) and depression may include aggressive symptoms but do not require them for making the diagnosis. IED likely reflects a phenotype different from the impulsive aggression often seen in childhood, given differences in age of onset, family history, and comorbidity.14 Similarly, DMDD is less applicable in many cases, as it requires chronic irritability, which many children with aggression do not display. One notable attempt to address the lack of nosology for severe emotional outbursts in children was the introduction of the “impairing emotional outbursts” R45.89 code in the DSM-5-TR.20, 21, 22 This code provides a broad description of significant verbal and/or physical aggressive symptoms.18 Intentionally designed as an umbrella term, it aims to assist clinicians in documenting various types of aggression without attributing them to a specific disorder, and to fill the gap in our current coding system.

Prior research by our group aimed to take a step further and help clarify the diagnostic boundaries of specifically impulsive aggression in children and adolescents using data-driven approaches. Building on work by Jensen and colleagues,23 we performed secondary analyses of the 3 large independent outpatient datasets that included patients with mood disorder symptoms, as well as other conditions.24,25 The samples represented significantly different demographics, referral patterns, and treatment settings. We started by analyzing item-level data from a range of parent-report measures to identify items that loaded strongly on common factors representing empirically supported syndromes. These analyses found a principal component suggestive of impulsive aggression alongside items of depression, mania, hyperactivity, and other mental health problems.24 Results of this work identified a subset of items that constitute an aggression-impulsive/reactive (AIR) dimension that appears to be distinct from others (depression, mania, rule-breaking, self-harm, hyperactivity). The AIR dimension was not captured by a single existing rating scale but rather by select items on several different rating scales (such as measures of oppositional behavior, manic symptoms, and others). An important finding in that study was correlation between higher AIR scores and increased impairment and comorbidity.

To take that work further, we explored whether a group of children with high AIR symptoms could be distinguished from children with other mental health problems. To answer this question, Youngstrom and colleagues conducted latent profile analyses of symptom indicators measuring AIR, depression, mania, rule-breaking, self-harm, and impulsivity.25 Grouping cases into profiles based on the symptom elevations revealed several profiles that were robustly identifiable across 3 independent data sets. One of those profiles, named “predominantly AIR,” had elevated symptoms of AIR, hyperactivity, and self-harm and much lower symptoms of depression and mania. This profile was notable for clinical diagnoses of ADHD and oppositional defiant disorder, but not mood disorders. Other profiles had variations of AIR severity and depression/mania symptoms. This work suggested that elevated AIR symptoms identify a distinct group of children and increase impairment when co-occurring with other disorders.

The current study extends our prior work by aiming to replicate previously identified AIR profiles in an independent clinical sample that includes outpatient, inpatient, and emergency room settings. This approach will further demonstrate generalizability and robustness of the AIR profiles across clinical settings with different demographics and referral patterns. Furthermore, our prior work focused on secondary analyses of previously collected datasets with higher rates of mood disorder symptoms. The current study does not select for any particular diagnosis and examines all medical records of children entering mental health services in 3 separate settings over a course of 6 months. In addition, the current study does not include longitudinal assessment across multiple time points. Although the present design does not allow for evaluation of profile stability over time, it provides valuable cross-sectional information, comparable to what a clinician might observe during an initial patient encounter.

One of the objectives of this project was to examine profiles within the current dataset and compare them to the profiles described by Youngstrom et al.25 We hypothesized that several profiles would emerge, including one characterized predominantly by elevated AIR symptoms, as well as other profiles featuring both AIR and mood symptoms. We also aimed to explore the differences between the profiles in terms of age of onset, chart diagnoses, and sex differences. Another objective of this study was to assess the incidents of specific clinical adverse events within various profiles, such as receiving more PRN medications, and seclusions or restraints while at the hospital, as well as 72-hour and 30-day readmissions upon discharge.

Method

Study Design

Data for the current study were originally gathered to assess, in part, the effectiveness of a brief behavioral intervention targeting aggressive behaviors in children seeking services at Virginia Commonwealth University Health System (VCUHS) in Richmond, VA. Of note, in the current sample, only 8 children (<1%) of the total sample received the behavioral intervention, and preliminary analyses did not reveal significant differences in outcomes for these children compared to those who did not receive the intervention. Study procedures were determined by the VCU Institutional Review Board (IRB) to be exempt from IRB review.

Medical records of patients 3 to 17 years of age who presented with mental health concerns to an academic medical center between June 2023 and December 2023 were reviewed in the following 3 settings: emergency department, pediatric inpatient psychiatry unit, and pediatric outpatient psychiatric clinic. As part of standard clinical care, parents or guardians of new and existing patients filled out paper-and-pencil measures of aggression, depression, mania, and ADHD symptoms during their first visit to the emergency room or outpatient clinic, or when they were admitted to an inpatient unit. Guardians of existing patients completed these assessments only once during the study period. These forms were scanned into the electronic medical record and used by clinicians as part of their initial assessment. For the purpose of this study, medical record data were collected only for patients with at least partially completed rating scales, and included child’s age, sex, race, ethnicity, item-level data on completed rating scales, history of abuse or neglect, chart psychiatric diagnoses, and specific negative clinical outcomes (seclusions, restraints, readmissions, use of PRN medications). Information from the medical records was entered into REDCap using a double entry system by 2 independent clinicians. Any discrepancies were reviewed and reconciled after the discussion with the principal investigator (ES).

All children were evaluated and diagnosed by mental health providers (social workers, psychiatrists, advanced practice providers) as part of standard clinical care. The chart diagnoses included those both currently active, as well as historical or in remission at the time of assessment. For ease of interpretation, chart diagnoses were recorded and grouped into broader diagnostic categories. For example, the category of “any depressive disorder” included major depressive disorder and all of its subtypes, persistent depressive (or dysthymic) disorder, and unspecified depression. Although disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) is classified as a mood disorder, we maintained it as a separate category because of its diagnostic criteria explicitly incorporating temper outbursts. Bipolar disorders 1, 2, unspecified, and cyclothymia were categorized as “any bipolar disorder.” Anxiety disorders were similarly grouped together and included generalized anxiety, panic disorder, separation anxiety, obsessive compulsive disorder, and phobias, but did not include adjustment disorders with anxious mood. A history of abuse or neglect was determined by a thorough review of initial assessments, provider, and social work notes. Different types of abuse were also combined and reported as “any abuse.”

Measures

General behavior inventory mania (10M) and depression (10Da) scales, parent version,26 evaluated symptoms of mania and depression. Both are brief 10-item versions of the larger general behavior inventory27 that have been validated in youth aged 5 to 18 years for assessment of depressive and manic symptoms with high internal consistency and high similarity to other measures of the same constructs. Caregivers rate the frequency of behaviors from “never” to “sometimes” to “often” to “very often.”

The Swanson, Nolan and Pelham rating scale (SNAP-IV) is a highly sensitive 26-item measure that evaluates symptoms of inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, and oppositional behaviors.28,29 Caregivers ranked the frequency of the child’s behaviors (eg, “never” to “just a little” to “quite a bit” to “very much”). SNAP-IV is commonly used in clinical practice and clinical trials.30,31

The Retrospective Modified Overt Aggression Scale (R-MOAS) and the Reactive Aggression Assessment (RAGA-16) measured aggressive behaviors. R-MOAS evaluates verbal aggression, physical aggression toward others, and aggression toward property and toward self.32 Caregivers rate the frequency of the child’s behavior, ranging from 0 to 5 or more times a week. R-MOAS has been used in clinical trials as a measure of aggressive behaviors.33, 34, 35

The second measure of aggressive behaviors used in this cohort is RAGA-16, which is a 16-item parent report measure of reactive aggression.36,37 The measure consists of 2 domains: temper outbursts and verbal or physical aggression. Caregivers rate the frequency of the child’s behaviors as “never,” “weekly,” “monthly,” or “yearly,” and rank their perception of severity of each symptom over the past 2 weeks on a 5-point scale, from “not a problem” to “mild,” “moderate,” or “severe.” In addition, information about the settings in which those behaviors occur is captured: “home,” “school,” or “other.” Parents were also asked to write in the age of onset of aggressive behaviors. The reason for including the second measure of aggressive behaviors was to gather detailed item-level data on the severity and frequency of aggressive behaviors.

Statistical Analyses

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to examine continuous indicators comprising items from previously validated rating scales (General Behavior Inventory 10M and 10Da, SNAP-IV, R-MOAS, RAGA-16) representing 6 symptom domains: AIR, depression, mania, rule-breaking, self-harm, and hyper-impulsivity. This approach mirrors our previous work,25 which used LPA with indicators aligned with the same symptom domains. Models with 1 to 10 clusters were fit using different assumptions for item variances and covariances (equal, fixed to 0, varying). The best model was chosen based on model fit indices.38 We used one-way analysis of variance with post hoc tests to assess differences across profiles in terms of the patient’s age, age of onset for outbursts, and number of adverse outcomes. We used the Tukey honest significant difference (HSD) test to control for multiple comparisons.39

The indicators for depression and mania came from the same parent scale as in Youngstrom et al.,25 namely, the Parent General Behavior Inventory (PGBI).40,41 However, for the current study, total scores from validated subscales measuring depression and mania were used, whereas in the original study items measuring these symptoms were selected using principal component analysis (PCA). Indicators for hyperactivity/impulsivity, rule-breaking, self-harm, and aggression were total or subscale scores from other published instruments: respectively, the hyperactive/impulsive and opposition/defiance scales from the SNAP-IV; the scale to harm to self from the R-MOAS; and the RAGA-16 total score. The content of these scales aligns with the PCA-derived composites used by Youngstrom et al.,25 which were based on the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL),42 Child and Adolescent Symptom Inventory (CASI),43 and PGBI.40,41 Median value imputation was used to handle missingness at the item level. A sensitivity analysis was conducted using multiple imputation of missing indicator values, and the results of the LPA did not substantively change.

Profile 2 (n = 83) showed above-average scores on AIR, rule-breaking, and hyperactive/impulsive symptoms, with below-average scores on all other indicators (Figure 1). Compared to other profiles, these youth were significantly younger (mean age = 9.8 years; mean difference vs profile 1: –2.56, 95% CI = –3.96, –1.17; vs profile 3: –3.00, 95% CI = –4.41, –1.59; vs profile 4: –3.72, 95% CI = –4.75, –2.70), and the majority (69%) were male (Table 2). Relatively few patients in this profile were seen in the ED and inpatient unit (20%). Most carried a chart diagnosis of ADHD (78%), with anxiety as the second most common diagnosis in this group (42%) (Figure 2). In addition, these children were less likely to have experienced abuse or to have a stress disorder diagnosis, when compared to individuals in profiles 1 and 3. Furthermore, 23% carried an ASD diagnosis, which was the highest rate of ASD among all profiles. This group was tied with profile 3 in terms of the proportion of assigned DMDD diagnoses (15%). Children in this profile were least likely to experience adverse clinical outcomes, compared to other profiles.

Profile 3 (n = 54) showed the highest overall symptoms of mania of any profile, with additionally elevated scores on AIR, depression, and rule-breaking indicators (Figure 1). Youth in this profile had a mean age of 12.8 years; more than half were female (57%); and about half (52%) were seen in the ED/inpatient setting (Table 2). The most common chart diagnosis was ADHD (61%), followed by depression (50%), anxiety (41%), and any stress disorder (39%) (Figure 2). Four children in this cluster (7%) had bipolar spectrum disorder diagnoses (3 with bipolar 2 disorder, one unspecified). In addition, 54% of these youth had a history of abuse. Along with profile 1, this cluster of patients had a high proportion of DMDD diagnoses (15%).

Profile 4 had the largest number of children (n = 237) and had moderate to low overall symptoms (Figure 1). Youth in this profile were older compared to those in other profiles, with a mean age of 13.5 years (Table 2). Approximately 69% of the profile were female. Close to half of the children in this profile (47%) were seen in the ED/inpatient setting. They had slightly higher levels of anxiety (58%) and depression (59%) diagnoses compared to youth in other profiles (Figure 2). There was also substantial representation of ADHD (44%) and any stress disorder diagnoses (34%), along with history of abuse (43%).

Age of Onset and Frequency of Aggressive Behaviors

When examining the overall sample, the distribution of parent-reported age of onset of aggression was bimodal, with 2 groups separating around age 6 years, with a means in each group around ages 3 and 11 years, respectively (Figure 3, Table 2). Evaluation of groups by sex across the whole sample showed an earlier separation for boys (5 years and under) compared to girls (7 years and under). When examining frequency of aggressive behaviors between different profiles, children with overall low to moderate symptoms (profile 4) had the lowest number of aggressive behaviors per week (1.9). Profiles 1 through 3 had more outbursts per week, ranging from 3.41 per week in profile 1 to 4.3 per week in profile 3. Profile 2 had slightly lower average number of weekly outbursts (3.6 per week), compared to profile 3. Only profile 4 differed significantly from the other profiles after correction (4 vs 1: mean difference = –1.5, 95% CI = –2.4, –0.61; 4 vs 2: –1.7, 95% CI = –2.5, –0.85; 4 vs 3: –2.4, 95% CI = –3.4, –1.5 [95% CIs include HSD correction for familywise error, p < .001]).

It should be noted that there was a high rate of missing data for the age of onset and frequency of aggression variables (Supplement 1, Missing Data, Tables S1 and S2, Figures S1 and S2, available online). Of 430 patients, 290 (67%) reported age of onset and 382 (89%) reported weekly frequency. Having missing data on outburst onset was significantly negatively associated with higher symptoms of AIR (r = –0.38, p < .001).

Discussion

The current work represents an important step in further validating the construct of AIR. As in our previous work, AIR appears to be a transdiagnostic phenomenon25 and often occurs in context of other mental health symptoms, such as ADHD, depressive symptoms, and manic symptoms, among others. Here we replicated prior results by identifying a profile that is characterized by children with high symptoms of AIR and hyperactivity/impulsivity but with low rates of mood disorder symptoms. This profile is distinct from others not only by different symptomatology but also in terms of demographic parameters, such as a higher prevalence of younger male participants and an earlier onset of aggressive behaviors. Notably, youth in the AIR profile were less likely to have a history of abuse compared to those in profiles associated with mood symptoms. In addition, unlike children and adolescents with mood disorders, those in the AIR profile were less likely to require higher levels of care, such as inpatient hospitalization or ED visits, and were more commonly treated in outpatient settings. This finding warrants further investigation, as it may have important implications for predicting clinical outcomes in aggressive children. Consistent with previous findings, AIR symptoms were also observed alongside manic and self-harm symptoms (profiles 1 and 3).

This work aimed to replicate and extend our prior research on latent profiles of AIR in clinic-referred youth.25 The results reported here go beyond the prior findings in several key ways. First, the current dataset comes strictly from clinical population of diverse levels of care, including psychiatric outpatient, inpatient, and ED settings. These settings differ from our prior study, which included outpatient youth recruited for research studies at academic medical centers. Second, conducting a chart review allowed us to bypass the potential selection bias by including data from all patients, both new and existing, who received services during the predefined study period. Therefore, this sample represents a good snapshot of families seeking services in this community. In contrast, samples used in previous work to identify an AIR profile used data from families who consented to be part of clinical research studies and met inclusion criteria. Third, this replication of profiles is especially encouraging, given that many of the symptom indicators were measured by scales different from those used by Youngstrom et al.,25 suggesting that the results are not instrument specific.

In this work we replicated 4 of the original 8 profiles. We believe that there are several reasons for this discrepancy. First, the sample size of the current study was significantly lower compared to that of Youngstrom et al.25 It is possible that, with increased power, more profiles could be identified. Second, the sample in Youngstrom et al.25 was intentionally enriched for mood disorders, resulting in a much higher proportion of mood symptoms in the sample than in the present study. Third, fewer measures were collected in the current study to decrease the burden on families that often presented in crisis.

An unexpected finding was the onset of aggressive behaviors in a subset of adolescent girls. This finding needs to be replicated and explored further, as it could suggest a different phenotype of aggressive adolescent youth.

Our current work aligns with the concept of “impairing emotional outbursts” and advances it by focusing specifically on impulsive aggression. It further clarifies diagnostic boundaries of aggression in relation to other mental health disorders and provides insights into clinical settings, comorbidities, and childhood adversity.

This study needs to be interpreted in light of several limitations. The retrospective and cross-sectional nature of this study precludes investigating the causal influences of other external factors. Furthermore, we are unable to ascertain whether the distinct profiles cross-sectionally may be stages of the same phenotype captured at different points across development. For example, younger children with predominantly externalizing symptoms may develop internalizing symptoms as they reach adolescence because of factors such as peer rejection and internalized negative beliefs about self. An additional limitation of this study is the absence of standardized anxiety symptom assessments in the dataset, which precluded the inclusion of anxiety-related constructs in our profile analysis. Another important limitation of this study is that it was conducted at a single center, which may affect the applicability of our findings to broader populations with varying backgrounds and characteristics. The sample may not fully represent the diversity found in different clinical settings, potentially limiting the extent to which our conclusions can be generalized to other contexts. For this reason, we also chose not to compare profiles in terms of participant race/ethnicity or other demographic characteristics. Not only did we lack the power to make meaningful comparisons, but also such differences may be confounded by, for example, demographic differences in accessing health care.

There are several limitations of a chart review design. The information derived from the medical record depends on documentation quality. It is not possible to verify the accuracy of retrospective reports, or to detect potential errors in recording. In addition, diagnoses were given by clinicians in a clinical setting. As reported previously, clinical diagnoses are often less precise when compared to research diagnoses.44 Furthermore, youth included in chart reviews from multiple clinical settings may not be representative of the broader population and may be subject to selection biases influencing profile extraction. For example, the apparent overrepresentation of adolescent girls with aggression could reflect characteristics of the inpatient population, and the finding of an earlier age of onset in youth with AIR should be interpreted with caution, given the potential influence of parental recall bias. Nevertheless, the findings reported here reflect real clinical work and therefore achieve a level of ecological validity.

Current work provided further validation of an AIR phenotype. Future directions should focus on establishing evidence-based criteria for the AIR phenotype and more comprehensively characterizing affected youth, including relevant demographic factors. In addition, future studies should focus on improving the identification of impulsively aggressive youth for clinical trials, leading to improved design and potentially identifying more effective treatments. Although treatment considerations were beyond the scope of the current study, this approach to profiles may ultimately inform more tailored intervention strategies, both pharmacologic and psychosocial, by supporting more refined inclusion criteria and enabling the study of more homogeneous clinical populations.
