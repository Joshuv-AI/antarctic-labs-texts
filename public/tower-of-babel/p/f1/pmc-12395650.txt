Improving classification on imbalanced genomic data via KDE–based synthetic sampling
Authors: Edoardo Taccaliti, Jesus S Aguilar–Ruiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractClass imbalance poses a serious challenge in biomedical machine learning, particularly in genomics, where datasets are characterized by extremely high dimensionality and very limited sample sizes. In such settings, standard classifiers tend to favor the majority class, leading to biased predictions — an especially problematic issue in clinical diagnostics where rare conditions must not be overlooked. In this study, we introduce a Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)–based oversampling approach to rebalance imbalanced genomic datasets by generating synthetic minority class samples. Unlike conventional methods such as SMOTE, KDE estimates the global probability distribution of the minority class and resamples accordingly, avoiding local interpolation pitfalls. We evaluate our method on 15 real–world genomic datasets using three classifiers –Naïve Bayes, Decision Trees, and Random Forests– and compare it to SMOTE and baseline training. Experimental results demonstrate that KDE oversampling consistently improves classification performance, especially in metrics robust to imbalance, such as AUC of the IMCP curve. Notably, KDE achieves superior results in tree-based models while dramatically simplifying the sampling process. This approach offers a statistically grounded and effective solution for balancing genomic datasets, with strong potential for improving fairness and accuracy in high–stakes medical decision–making.Keywords: Imbalance, Oversampling, Kernel density estimation, Classification

Introduction

Over the past few decades, the advent of new technologies, the proliferation of big data, and the increasing automation of decision–making systems have led to the rise of data imbalance across many application domains.

Class imbalance is a common characteristic in those domains where data is intrinsically imbalanced due to the nature of the problem domain, such as fraud detection [1], medical diagnostics [2], and anomaly detection [3]. Imbalance can also originate in an extrinsic manner through time and space from a source that was not originally imbalanced. Machine learning algorithms for classification are typically designed under the assumption of evenly distributed classes and equal misclassification costs. When these conditions are not met, the models tend to favor the majority class, resulting in biased predictions and poor generalization [4, 5].

Generally speaking, imbalance is defined as a skewed distribution in the number of instances belonging to two separate classes. More simply, imbalance in a dataset refers to a majority class out–numbering a minority class. Not uncommon are between–class imbalances on the order of 100:1, 1,000:1, and 10,000:1, where in each case, one class severely outrepresents another. As in the case of tumoral and non–tumoral patients, where healthy subjects outnumber non–healthy patients and their degree of imbalance is quantified by the Imbalance Ratio (IR), as the ratio of the number of examples in the majority class Nmaj over the number of examples in the minority class Nmin. For multiclass datasets (more than two classes), the IR is extended to each class in relation to the minority class.

In addition, the effects of varying imbalance class ratios on the prediction performance of a classifier have been explored [5, 6], prompting more research in this direction. However, more work must be done to investigate the internal characteristics of imbalanced datasets. The presence of rare instances and the lack of representative examples hinders the ability of the classifier to learn these minorities [4, 7]. Morevoer, the inability to define clear decision boundaries due to class overlapping and small disjunct are all contributing factors behind performance deterioration. The problem of high dimensionality and small sample size, common in areas such as gene expression data analysis, challenges classifiers to derive meaningful rules in such a sparse and complex feature space [8, 9].

The processing of imbalanced data has gained a lot of attention from the research and industrial community due to the increasing volume of data from large–scale and complex system domains. Various strategies have been proposed to mitigate the effects caused by the imbalance class distribution, which can be subdivided into two distinct and equally valuable approaches. The algorithmic–driven acts by improving the algorithm itself to be more responsive to these underrepresented groups. These techniques contribute to improving the overall performance, fairness, and generalizability of machine learning models when faced with imbalanced data [10] without modifying the data space. Another broad category includes data–driven approaches, which act directly on the data independently of the algorithm used [11]. These preprocessing techniques target the challenge of class imbalance by rebalancing the distribution of classes within the training dataset by either under–sampling majority class instances or adding new instances related to the minority class. In addition, the combination of data–driven and algorithmic–level approaches has resulted in hybrid techniques trying to combine the advantages of both strategies [12].

In this work, we focus on genomic datasets, characterized by small sample sizes and very high dimensionality, and on reliable classification performance metrics, aligned with the sensitivity to imbalance. The paper is organized as follows. “Related work” section provides a review of related work, with particular emphasis on oversampling strategies such as SMOTE and the application of kernel density estimation (KDE) as an alternative approach to address class imbalance. “Method” section presents KDE as a non–parametric statistical technique for generating synthetic samples by resampling from an estimated multivariate probability density function. “Experiments” section outlines the experimental design, including a dhescription of the datasets, the validation and evaluation procedures used for classification under unbalanced conditions, and the results obtained. “Conclusions and future work” section offers a critical discussion of the findings, examining the strengths and limitations of the KDE-based method, especially in the context of small sample sizes and high-dimensional data, and identifies avenues for future research.

Related work

Unlike undersampling, oversampling offers several advantages in addressing imbalanced data, including greater representation of the minority class, retention of original information, improved classifier performance, and reduced bias towards the majority class [12]. The most naive way would be to replicate existing instances of any minority class to balance those of the majority class. This is what underlies the idea of ROS (random oversampling), which suffers from overfitting and poor generalization [13], leading to worse performances in unseen testing data [4]. A more sophisticated approach to handling class imbalance is SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over–sampling Technique), which generates synthetic minority class examples through interpolation rather than simple duplication. The method oversamples the minority class by selecting a sample and synthesizing new instances along the line segments that connect it to its K nearest minority class neighbors. SMOTE has been widely applied and has proven effective in mitigating class imbalance [13]. However, it does not differentiate between noise and genuine minority instances near class boundaries, potentially increasing class overlap.

To address this limitation, adaptive oversampling techniques selectively generate synthetic samples from more informative minority instances. Notable examples include Borderline–SMOTE [14] and ADASYN [15]. Borderline–SMOTE first identifies “danger” samples —minority class instances at risk of misclassification— before applying SMOTE, ensuring that new samples are generated closer to the decision boundary, thereby improving class distinction. ADASYN, on the other hand, dynamically determines the number of synthetic samples needed for different regions of the dataset, prioritizing more complex areas. This not only balances the data distribution, but also encourages the learning algorithm to focus on harder–to–classify instances. Several studies have compared SMOTE and ADASYN in various data sets with different levels of imbalance. The results generally show no consistent superiority of one method over the other [16]. In some cases, baseline models perform similarly or even better than those using resampling. However, both generally improve the overall performance of the model [17]. Such methods rely exclusively on local information from anchor instances for interpolation and ignore the impact of the global neighborhood relationship on the minority instances. Additionally, in the presence of significant class overlap or noise, SMOTE can introduce unnecessary noise or further amplify class overlap issues [18].

Oversampling is still a preferable strategy in addressing data imbalance in a variety of applications, ranging from binary and multiclass settings [19–22], especially in the case of small datasets.

Recently, Kernel Density Estimation (KDE), a well–established statistical method to estimate the probability density distribution, was introduced as an alternative sampling technique to augment numerical data [23]. As a non–parametric method, it relies on available data and bandwidth for estimating the unknown probability density function. New synthetic data closely resembling the original data are augmented to the dataset prior to the training procedure. KDE–based resampling has been successfully used before training a Radial Basis Function on binary imbalanced datasets, providing better results than comparable techniques [24]. Through cross–validated fine–tuning, KDE generates good–quality synthetic samples relative to similar techniques and is robust to overfitting when applied to small datasets [25]. When tested against comparable oversampling techniques, KDE–based oversampling provided overall good results on both simulated and real–world datasets regardless of the classifier used.

In [23], 12 binary datasets were chosen from the UCI repository [26], with number of features ranging from 6 to 93. Also, k–NN (k–nearest neighbors), SVM (support vector machines), and MLP (multilayer perceptron with one hidden layer with 32 nodes) were selected to test the oversampling technique, and two scalar performance metrics were provided: G–mean and F1–score. Herein, we carry out a systematic study on the use of KDE–based sampling to augment imbalanced genomic datasets characterized by small sample sizes and very high dimensionality, providing more reliable classification performance metrics, aligned with the sensitivity to imbalance.

Consider a given training data set S={(xi,yi)}i=1,⋯,n, where xi∈X is an instance in the d–dimensional feature space, and yi∈Y={1,...,C} is a class identity label associated with instance xi.

In particular, C=2 represents the two-class (binary) classification problem. Let the subsets Smin∈S and Smaj∈S be the set of minority class examples in S, and the set of majority class examples in S, respectively, so that Smin∩Smaj=∅ and Smin∪Smaj=S. Any sets generated from sampling procedures on Smin are labeled as E representing the newly generated minority examples.

Given a KDE model with a Gaussian kernel function K and an optimal bandwidth parameter h selected by the Silverman rule of thumb [28], the object KDE builds an estimated probability density function f^ over Smin. Moreover, KDE generates a new set of examples E, in quantity specified by |E|=|Smaj|-|Smin| by randomly sampling from Smin by using f^. In this way, the number of total examples in Smin increases by E, and the class distribution of S is adjusted accordingly.

The same approach is applied to multi–class data in the same manner by oversampling each not–majority class by a quantity |Ec|, to adjust all classes c in S-Smaj to |Smaj|.

Validation for KDE

Validation and evaluation

To evaluate the overall performance on the classification task, KDE–based oversampling was compared to the most popular sampling technique, SMOTE [13], and tested using three different base classifiers: Naive–Bayes (NB) [32], Decision Trees (DT) [33], and Random Forests (RF) [34].

More sophisticated oversampling techniques such as Borderline–SMOTE [14] or ADASYN [15] have been proposed to improve upon the original SMOTE algorithm. However, these variants remain fundamentally dependent on local neighborhood interpolation and typically require additional hyperparameter tuning or class–boundary heuristics. In contrast, our KDE–based method follows a conceptually distinct strategy by estimating the full class–conditional distribution and sampling from it directly. For this reason, we chose to compare against SMOTE as the canonical reference, in order to isolate the effect of the underlying generative mechanism.

We selected three well–established classifiers commonly used in biomedical data analysis:

Naïve Bayes (NB): a simple probabilistic model that assumes conditional independence among features. It is computationally efficient and interpretable, making it a standard baseline in many studies.

These classifiers cover a range of learning paradigms—generative, discriminative, and ensemble–based—allowing us to evaluate how different oversampling strategies interact with diverse model assumptions.

As a validation procedure, a stratified 10–fold cross–validation was chosen to preserve the imbalance ratios between classes, as in the original dataset. At this stage, three different and distinct training subsets are created: a baseline, a SMOTE–oversampled, and KDE–oversampled. Each classifier trained with these three distinct training sets is then tested on the remaining partition, which maintains the original imbalance ratio. The whole procedure is repeated until each partition is served as a testing set. A detailed explanation of the validation framework used is reported in Algorithm 1.

A major issue in the classification of class imbalanced datasets involves the determination of the most suitable performance metrics to be used. Accuracy, which measures the proportion of correctly classified instances, provides a broad overview of the model overall performance, but cannot be used in classification of imbalanced data [35], since high accuracy might mask poor minority class performance. The Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) is a robust metric, less susceptible to misinterpretations [36], but still sensitive to imbalance. The area under the ROC curve, AUC(ROC), representing the relationship between the sensitivity and specificity of a classifier at each classification threshold, is a robust diagnostic measure insensitive to class imbalance [37]. In addition, it is able to graphically compare different classifiers in a single shot, providing a global understanding of classification behaviour. The Multiclass Performance Curve (MCP) is a metric specifically designed for multiclass scenarios, robust to class imbalance, and also provides an area under the MCP curve, the AUC(MCP) [31].

The package KernelDensity from sklearn.neighbors [38] was used as the preferred implementation for KDE, leaving the default parameters. The bandwidth was selected using Silverman’s rule of thumb (see Eqs. 5 and 6). Alternative strategies for bandwidth selection, such as Scott’s rule, plug–in methods, or cross–validation–based optimization, are also available and may influence the resulting density estimation and sampling behavior. In this work, we chose Silverman’s rule for its simplicity and robust general–purpose behavior across the 15 datasets, ensuring a consistent configuration without introducing additional tuning complexity.

SMOTE is implemented in the Imblearn Python library [39] with k–neighbors left as default to 5. Synthetic new minority class examples are generated by selecting as sampling strategy ‘not majority’. Although KDE-based sampling is more computationally demanding than SMOTE, particularly due to its O(nd) time complexity per sample versus O(kd) for SMOTE, our implementation using vectorized operations in scikit–learn proved feasible across all genomic datasets tested. In practice, the dominant factor is the dimensionality d, which appears in both methods, and the runtime remained acceptable even with over 54,000 features.

The implementations for the three machine learning algorithms were taken from scikit-learn [38] with default parameters. Validation procedure was developed based on the Python (3.9.3) programming language using StratifiedKFold implemented in sklearn.model_selection.

Results

The performance is calculated using three measures: accuracy (popular metric, which is well–known to be sensitive to imbalance), the area under the ROC curve (AUC(ROC)), and the area under the IMCP curve. For multi–class datasets, the AUC(ROC) score is provided as micro–averaged values. The IMCP (Imbalance Multi–class Classification Performance) curve [31, 40] is a measure based on prediction probabilities (instead of values from the confusion matrix), and it is insensitive to imbalance. For this reason, the IMCP curve is an ideal candidate to compare classification results when imbalance is present [41]. To generate ROC and IMCP curves, standard Python implementations have been used [38, 42].

Unlike DT and RF, NB uses the full feature space in the classification model, which in case of genomic datasets is composed by thousands of features. NB finds difficulty in improving the performance when oversampling techniques are employed. Table 2 (Mean: bottom row) reveals that the curse of dimensionality affects the quality of oversampled instances, as the complexity of providing good new instances increases with the number of dimensions. In general, oversampling techniques do not contribute to improving the performance of NB.

As DT uses a subset of attributes to build the classification model, it selects those that better contribute to overall performance. It is remarkable the improvement of KDE for the area of the IMCP curve, which is based on probabilistic measures.

Similarly, Random Forest (RF) tends to outperform Naive Bayes (NB). However, its performance relative to Decision Trees (DT) is not always superior, as it depends on whether the evaluation metric is sensitive to class imbalance. In this respect, while the mean area under the ROC curve for KDE (SMOTE) is 0.95 (0.943), the area under the IMCP curve is only 0.633 (0.62). However, when using DT, the area under the ROC curve for KDE (SMOTE) is 0.865 (0.842), and the area under the IMCP curve is 0.825 (0.784). Therefore, using probabilistic measures, DT outperforms RF, and both oversampling techniques provide better results (0.62 and 0.6.33) than without oversampling (0.589).

Interestingly, while tree–based classifiers (DT and RF) consistently benefited from KDE–based oversampling, Naïve Bayes showed mixed results, with occasional performance degradation compared to SMOTE and even the original baseline. This behavior can be attributed to the assumptions underlying the Naïve Bayes model, which relies on the independence of features within each class. Since KDE generates synthetic samples based on the full multivariate density estimate, it naturally introduces correlations between features—particularly in high–dimensional data—potentially violating the independence assumption. As a result, the Naïve Bayes model may misinterpret these dependencies as spurious patterns, negatively impacting its predictions. In contrast, decision trees and ensembles are more robust to such inter–feature interactions and are therefore better positioned to exploit the richer structure of KDE–generated samples.

To further investigate the generative behavior of KDE and SMOTE, we provide additional visualizations of the synthetic samples projected onto a reduced feature space. Specifically, we selected the Ovary dataset (GSE12470) and applied principal component analysis (PCA) to project the high–dimensional data onto its first two components.

Figure 3 shows the distribution of original and synthetic class samples generated by KDE (left) and SMOTE (right). The original data is represented with solid dots, while the synthetic samples are marked with crosses. As observed, SMOTE generates synthetic samples that are tightly aligned along linear interpolations between class neighbors, particularly within compact clusters. This behavior is evident in several localized formations (circled in red), where synthetic points cluster in–between or directly adjacent to the existing minority samples. While this approach preserves local structure, it may reinforce already–dense regions without significantly broadening the representation of minority classes distribution.

In contrast, KDE–based generation produces a more globally diffuse pattern, distributing synthetic samples across broader regions of the minority class manifold. This dispersion reflects the probabilistic nature of KDE, which estimates a continuous density function from which new samples are drawn. Consequently, KDE generates points that cover underrepresented areas of the feature space, potentially improving class boundary delineation in downstream classification.

It is important to note that these visualizations are based on a two–dimensional projection of very high–dimensional genomic data and should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, they provide qualitative support for the observed differences in classifier performance: the broader coverage achieved by KDE may contribute to better generalization and robustness in highly imbalanced settings.

To assess whether the observed improvements with KDE–based sampling over SMOTE were statistically significant, we applied the Wilcoxon signed–rank test across the 15 genomic datasets for each of the three evaluation metrics (Accuracy, ROC AUC, and IMCP AUC), with significance level α=0.05.

For Naïve–Bayes, the results indicate that none of the observed differences in Accuracy (p-value=0.998), ROC AUC (p-value=0.999), or IMCP AUC (p-value=0.997) were statistically significant. These findings suggest that KDE–based oversampling does not lead to consistent improvements in Naive Bayes performance, which may be attributed to the classifier’s strong assumptions and its limited capacity to exploit the more flexible sample distributions generated by KDE.

For Decision Tree, the results showed that KDE significantly outperformed SMOTE in terms of ROC AUC (p-value=0.023) and IMCP AUC (p-value=0.0036), while the improvement in Accuracy was not statistically significant (p-value=0.059). These findings confirm that KDE-based sampling yields superior class separation and performance under imbalance–aware metrics, even when used with interpretable classifiers such as decision trees.

For Random Forest, the test results revealed no statistically significant difference between KDE and SMOTE in terms of Accuracy (p-value=0.249) or ROC AUC (p-value=0.270). However, KDE significantly outperformed SMOTE in terms of IMCP AUC (p-value=0.002), suggesting that the proposed method provides better performance for imbalanced multiclass classification, particularly in capturing minority–class structure.

Conclusions and future work

This study presents a systematic evaluation of Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)–based oversampling as a method for addressing class imbalance in genomic datasets —a prevalent challenge in biomedical machine learning. Unlike traditional approaches such as SMOTE, which rely on local interpolation in the feature space, KDE generates synthetic minority class examples by sampling from a globally estimated probability density function. This enables a more principled and distribution–aware augmentation of underrepresented classes.

The evaluation, conducted across 15 real–world genomic microarray datasets characterized by extreme dimensionality and varying degrees of imbalance, demonstrates that KDE–based oversampling consistently improves classification performance when paired with tree–based classifiers such as Decision Trees (DT) and Random Forests (RF). These classifiers inherently perform feature selection, making them particularly well–suited to benefit from the KDE–generated synthetic samples. Moreover, performance gains were evident across metrics that are robust to imbalance, such as the area under the IMCP curve, highlighting KDE’s robustness in skewed–class scenarios.

Notably, while Naïve Bayes (NB) showed limited benefit from oversampling —likely due to its full–feature usage— KDE still maintained competitive performance, reinforcing its general applicability. Furthermore, KDE achieves these improvements without the need for complex neighborhood heuristics, offering a simpler yet statistically grounded alternative to popular oversampling methods. These findings are especially relevant in biomedical applications where rare disease subtypes must be detected accurately, and interpretability of feature–driven decisions is essential.

Future research directions include: a) Adaptive KDE, incorporating variable bandwidths or data–driven smoothing parameters to better handle class density heterogeneity; b) Feature–aware KDE, leveraging feature importance to guide the density estimation in ultra–high–dimensional settings.

Overall, this work establishes KDE–based oversampling as a viable and effective technique for improving classification performance on imbalanced, high–dimensional biomedical datasets, with direct implications for genomic diagnostics and precision medicine.
