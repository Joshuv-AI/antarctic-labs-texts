Effectiveness of lignans against severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus infection
Authors: Jun Huang, Jinpeng Lv, Xiaojie Zheng, Junjun Jiang, Wei Liu, Shuchen Liu, Hao Liang, Hao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV) is an emerging tick-borne bunyavirus that causes a life-threatening infectious disease with high mortality. Currently, there are no approved vaccines or specific therapeutic drugs for clinical use against SFTSV infection. Here, we screened a lignan compound library to identify candidates with anti-SFTSV activity. Two type I arylnaphthalide lignan lactones, Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I, were identified as potent inhibitors of SFTSV infection. Mechanistic investigations indicated that these lignans exert their inhibitory effects at the viral binding and internalization stages. Molecular docking analysis revealed that these lignans can bind to the conserved B domain of the SFTSV glycoprotein Gn, an interaction validated by subsequent experiments as the core mechanism underlying their antiviral activity. In a mouse model of lethal SFTSV infection, treatment with these compounds, particularly Procumbenoside I, significantly inhibited viral infection, attenuated histopathological abnormalities, and improved survival rates. The antiviral activity of these lignans was further expanded to other bunyaviruses, including Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) and Wetland virus (WELV). These findings uncover the broad-spectrum antiviral activity of type I arylnaphthalide lignans and support their potential as candidate therapeutic agents for the clinical intervention of bunyavirus infections.

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging acute infectious disease caused by Dabie bandavirus [formerly SFTS virus (SFTSV)], a novel tick-borne virus belonging to the genus Bandavirus, family Phenuiviridae, order Hareavirales, class Bunyaviricetes (ICTV, 2024). Since it was discovered in 2009, SFTS has been reported in multiple countries including China, South Korea, Japan, Vietnam, Myanmar, and Thailand, exhibiting a wide epidemic distribution (Yu et al., 2011; Li et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2025). The primary transmission of SFTSV is through the bites of Haemaphysalis longicornis ticks, while the virus can also spread via direct contact with infected blood or other bodily fluids (Chen et al., 2022; N Zhang et al., 2024). Recently, an increasing number of human-to-human transmission cases have been reported, further raising the risk of clustered infections (Fang et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022). Infection with SFTSV often leads to severe or critical consequences, including hemorrhage, encephalitis, and multiple organ failure, with mortality rates ranging from 12% to 50% among hospitalized patients (Takahashi et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2016; Kobayashi et al., 2020). Although the World Health Organization (WHO) had classified SFTSV as a priority pathogen on its global watch list (Mendoza et al., 2019), there are still no clinically approved specific antiviral drugs or effective preventive vaccines targeting SFTSV infection, highlighting the need for the development and evaluation of effective anti-SFTSV therapeutics.

Natural products, particularly plant-derived bioactive constituents, have emerged as a pivotal resource for the discovery and development of novel antiviral agents (Coronado et al., 2023; Putri et al., 2023; Obłoza et al., 2024). One important group of plant secondary metabolites commonly exploited for drug discovery and development is the lignans. Lignans are a group of natural phytochemicals polymerized from two C6-C3 phenylpropanoid monomers, widely distributed across diverse plant species, such as edible plants including sesame and flaxseed (Ushimaru, 2024). Natural arylnaphthalene lactones represent a major subclass of lignans (Zhao et al., 2018). These lignans are derived from two arylpropanoid units and structurally classified into two distinct subtypes, designated type I and type II. They have been extensively reported to possess diverse pharmacological activies, including anti-proliferative, antiplatelet aggregation, antifungal, neuroprotective, and anti-inflammatory effects (Park et al., 2021). Notably, podophyllotoxin (PTOX), a naturally lignan compound derived from the podophyllum plant, and its derivatives etoposide (VP-16) and teniposide (VM-26) have been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for cancer treatment (Motyka et al., 2023). This precedent greatly increases the possibility that lignans with antiviral activity may be developed into clinical therapeutic drugs. Accumulating evidence have confirmed that lignans exert potent inhibitory effects against multiple arthropod-borne viruses (arboviruses) such as Zika virus (ZIKV) (Chen et al., 2021), dengue virus (DENV) (Martinez et al., 2022), and chikungunya virus (CHIKV) (Shukla et al., 2024). Notably, lignans can employ distinct antiviral mechanisms and display variable efficacy across different viral infections (Li et al., 2021; Shukla et al., 2024).

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether lignans influence SFTSV infection and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Firstly, we screened a lignan compound library and identified twelve lignan compounds that inhibit SFTSV replication in vitro. Accordingly, we further confirmed the antiviral efficacy of two non-cytotoxic compounds, Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I, against SFTSV infection in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistic investigation revealed that these two lignans may directly bind to SFTSV glycoprotein Gn, thereby blocking viral binding and internalization. The antiviral efficacy of these two lignans was also evaluated in a lethal mouse mode of SFTSV infection. Addtionally, Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I were shown to exhibit significant inhibitory effects against other bunyaviruses, including Lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) and Wetland virus (WELV). This study revealed the broad anti-bunyaviruses effect of lignans and suggested their potential to be developed as an anti-viral drug for clinical use.

Results

Screening of lignan compounds with anti-SFTSV activity

A lignan compound library was screened in vitro to identify potential inhibitors against SFTSV infection (Fig. 1A). Briefly, Huh7 cells, known to be susceptible to SFTSV (Tani et al., 2016), were treated with the test compounds at concentrations of 2.5 μM and 10 μM, with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as the negative control. Following compound treatment, cells were infected with SFTSV, and viral loads in cell culture supernatants were quantified by qRT-PCR at 48 h post-infection (hpi). Among the 29 lignans tested, 12 showed significant suppression on SFTSV infection, with inhibition rates exceeding 80% and cell viability maintaining above 50% (Supplementary Fig. S1 and Supplementary Fig. S2A). In subsequent dose-response assessment, two type I lignans, Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I, exhibited dose-dependent anti-SFTSV activity without apparent cytotoxicity (Fig. 1B and C, Supplementary Fig. S2A). The half cytotoxic concentration (CC50) and half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of Pronaphthalide A were determined to be > 20 μM and 0.531 μM, respectively, resulting in a selection index (SI) of >37 in Huh7 cells (Fig. 1D). Similarly, the CC50 and IC50 of Procumbenoside I were >20 μM and 0.583 μM, respectively, yielding an SI of >34 (Fig. 1E). To identify the stage of the viral life cycle targeted by the two lignans, a time-of-addition assay was performed. Each compound was added either prior to or post SFTSV infection. Intracellular vRNA levels and viral protein expression levels were measured by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis at 24 hpi, respectively (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D–F, both vRNA and viral protein levels were significantly reduced when lignans were present prior to and during infection, in contrast to the more limited inhibitory effect observed upon post-infection addition. These results indicate that Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I inhibit SFTSV infection at the viral entry stage.

Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I impede SFTSV binding and internalization during viral entry

Viral entry encompasses three sequential steps: binding, internalization, and fusion. We further sought to determine which specific entry events targeted by Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I. For the viral binding assay, Huh7 cells pretreated with lignans or DMSO were incubated with SFTSV at 4 °C for 1 h to allow viral attachment while synchronizing the binding process. Unbound viral particles were removed by three washes, and the relative levels of viral particles bound to the cell surface were analyzed (Fig. 2G). As shown in Fig. 2H, the vRNA levels in the lignan-treated groups were significantly lower than those in the control group, indicating that the lignans inhibit SFTSV binding to host cells.

To evaluate the effect on viral internalization, Huh7 cells were infected with SFTSV in the presence of lignans or DMSO, and ammonium chloride was added to block low-pH-dependent membrane fusion, thereby blocking viral entry at the fusion stage. Intracellular vRNA levels were significantly reduced in the lignan-treated groups compared with the control group (Fig. 2I), indicating that Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I also inhibit SFTSV internalization.

Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I directly target SFTSV glycoprotein Gn to inhibit viral binding

Given that Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I block SFTSV binding and internalization, we investigated the underlying mechanisms. Since SFTSV entry into host cells is primarily mediated by the viral envelope glycoproteins Gn and Gc (Tani et al., 2016), we hypothesized that the lignans may directly interact with Gn or Gc to interfere with virion attachment and subsequent entry. To test this hypothesis, we initially performed molecular docking to predict potential interactions between the lignans and viral glycoproteins. Both Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I were predicted to bind to SFTSV Gn and Gc, with docking scores below −5 (Fig. 3A–D). Subsequent functional assays validated these in silico predictions. Cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA) across a range of temperatures revealed that Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I significantly enhanced the thermal stability of SFTSV Gn, suggesting a direct interaction between the lignans and Gn. In contrast, no obvious stabilization was observed for SFTSV Gc under the same conditions (Fig. 3E–J). To further characterize this interaction, we assessed Gn stability at a fixed temperature (70 °C) in the presence of increasing lignan concentrations. Both compounds stabilized Gn in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 3K and L). Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assays confirmed these findings, showing that Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I reduced pronase-mediated degradation of Gn (Fig. 3M and N). Finally, we performed a biolayer interferometry (BLI) assay, determining the binding affinities of Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I to SFTSV-Gn, with respective KD values of 7.40 × 10−4 M and 5.73 × 10−5 M (Fig. 3O and P). Collectively, these results suggest that the two lignans may directly bind to SFTSV Gn, which likely contributes to their inhibitory effect on viral entry.

Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I treatment protects mice from lethal SFTSV infection

To evaluate whether the lignans exhibit anti-SFTSV activity in vivo, we assess their therapeutic efficacy in C57BL/6J mice, a verified animal model for SFTSV infection. Following pretreatment with an IFNAR1-blocking antibody, mice were challenged intraperitoneally with SFTSV, and intraperitoneally treated with lignans or vehicle 2 hpi. Both compounds were administered daily at a low dose (1 mg/kg/day) or a high dose (5 mg/kg/day) for 5 consecutive days (Fig. 4A). Compared to vehicle treatment, both Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I treatment significantly reduced fatality rates (Fig. 4B and C). Procumbenoside I exhibited superior therapeutic efficacy, with survival rates of 66.7% and 83.3% at 1 mg/kg/day and 5 mg/kg/day, respectively. In contrast, Pronaphthalide A showed relatively weaker activity, with survival rates of 16.7% and 33.3% at the corresponding doses, respectively. Consistently, Procumbenoside I significantly reduced serum viral loads and splenic viral titers at both doses, whereas Pronaphthalide A only moderately suppressed viral infection at the high dose (Fig. 4D–G). Immunohistochemical analysis of spleen sections using an anti-SFTSV NP antibody further confirmed the superior inhibitory effect of Procumbenoside I on viral infection (Fig. 4H). In addition, H&E staining revealed that Procumbenoside I provided more significant alleviation of splenic pathological damage, such as attenuating the loss of white pulp (Fig. 4H). Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I exhibit antiviral activity against other bunyaviruses

We further evaluated the antiviral activity of the lignans against other bunyaviruses, including LCMV belonging to the family Arenaviridae and WELV belonging to the family Nairoviridae. Both Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I exhibited significant, dose-dependent inhibitory effects against LCMV and WELV in Huh7 cells (Fig. 5A–H), suggesting that the lignans may possess broad-spectrum antiviral activity against bunyaviruses. Discussion

SFTSV is an emerging tick-borne virus that poses a substantial public health threat in Asia. Its high fatality rate, coupled with the absence of approved vaccines or specific antiviral therapies, underscores an urgent unmet medical need (Casel et al., 2021; Rattanakomol et al., 2022; Kim and Park, 2023). In this study, we systematically screened a lignan compound library and identified two arylnaphthalene lactone lignans, Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I, which exerted potent anti-SFTSV activity in vitro without appreciable cytotoxicity. Mechanistic investigations revealed that both lignans block viral binding and internalization by directly targeting the viral glycoprotein Gn. Furthermore, Procumbenoside I conferred robust therapeutic efficacy in a lethal SFTSV infection mouse model, as evidenced by reducing viral loads and alleviating pathological damage.

As a major subclass of lignans, several arylnaphthalene lactone lignans have been reported to possess broad-spectrum antiviral effects against multiple human pathogenic viruses, including Hepatitis C virus (HCV) (Qian et al., 2016), Human rhinovirus (HRV) (Park et al., 2018), Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (Guan et al., 2016), and Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-CoV) (Xu et al., 2022). Notably, substituent modifications and chemical structural diversities lead to divergent antiviral modes of action for this type of lignans, which involve multiple pathways, including blocking viral glycoproteins-mediated attachment and host recognition (Qian et al., 2016), inhibiting RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to impair viral genome transcription and replication (Wang et al., 2026), disrupting lipid metabolism (Merino-Ramos et al., 2017), and maintaining mitochondrial integrity (Wang et al., 2025). Interestingly, such structural differences not only endow these compounds with distinct antiviral potency but also result in variable cytotoxicity. Type I arylnaphthalene lactone lignans showed potent anti-VSV activity with low cytotoxicity, whereas type II analogues displayed weaker anti-VSV effects but higher cytotoxicity (Asano et al., 1996; Park et al., 2021). Consistent with these previous observations, our screening results revealed that type I arylnaphthalene lactone lignans, including Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I, exhibited potent anti-SFTSV activity with low cytotoxicity. In contrast, among type II arylnaphthalene lactone lignans, only Justicidin D displayed measurable antiviral effect, albeit accompanied by significant cytotoxicity.

Lignans exert antiviral activity at various stages of the viral life cycle. Li et al. reported that lignans from Phellinus pini inhibit the interaction between SARS-CoV-2 Spike and ACE2, thereby suppressing viral infection at an early stage (Li et al., 2021b). Nordihydroguaiaretic Acid (NDGA), a lignan-based tumor agent, has been shown to suppress West Nile virus (WNV) replication at post-infection stages (Merino-Ramos et al., 2017). Moreover, arctigenin, a typical lignan isolated from Arctium lappa L., inhibits Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) infection throughout the entire viral life cycle (Shukla et al., 2024). Our mechanistic studies revealed that neither Pronaphthalide A nor Procumbenoside I exerted direct virucidal effect on SFTSV particles. Instead, both lignans specifically inhibit viral entry, a critical early stage in the infection cycle and an established target for antiviral drug development (Arya et al., 2023). Further dissection of this process revealed that the two lignans effectively block viral binding and internalization, two essential sub-steps of SFTSV entry.

SFTSV glycoproteins Gn and Gc play essential roles in mediating viral entry into host cells (Tani et al., 2016). SFTSV Gn serves as the primary determinant for host receptor recognition. Previous studies have demonstrated that Gn interacts with the C-type lectin receptor DC-SIGN and nonmuscle myosin heavy chain IIA (NMMHC-IIA) during viral attachment, facilitating viral binding and subsequent endocytosis (Lozach et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2014). More recently, C-C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2) and lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) have also been identified as host factors that mediate SFTSV infection via direct interaction with the Gn (Zhang et al., 2023; Xing et al., 2025). Consequently, SFTSV Gn has become a primary target for neutralizing antibodies that block viral infection (Wu et al., 2020; Ren et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024). In this study, we demonstrated that Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I inhibit SFTSV binding and internalization through direct targeting SFTSV Gn. Molecular docking analysis predicted that both compounds bind to the region between domain I and domain III of Gc, which undergoes substantial conformational rearrangements during membrane fusion (Halldorsson et al., 2016). This biochemical process may disrupt the interaction of lignans and Gc, potentially explaining the relatively weak binding observed in the CETSA assays.

In vivo investigation demonstrated that the therapeutic administration of both lignans protected mice against lethal SFTSV infection. Notably, Procumbenoside I exhibited more potent therapeutic efficacy, significantly reducing viral loads, alleviating pathological damage, and increasing survival rates at both low and high doses. In comparison, Pronaphthalide A only achieved significant antiviral inhibition at a high dose, with no comparable protective effects at lower dosages. This discrepancy is likely attributed to poor solubility and limited in vivo bioavailability, a common challenge in the translational development of natural product-derived therapeutics (Deleanu et al., 2023; He et al., 2023; Selc et al., 2024), which warrant further investigation. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing drug delivery strategies, such as nanoparticle-based formulations, to enhance the in vivo efficacy of lignan-derived antiviral agents (Huang et al., 2023, 2024; Yao et al., 2025).

Despite the promising antiviral activity of Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I against various bunyaviruses including SFTSV, this study still has several limitations. The molecular basis underlying their broad-spectrum antiviral actions have not been thoroughly explored, which is essential to advance the translational development of pan-bunyavirus antivirals. Since SFTSV infection often triggers excessive inflammatory responses and even cytokine storms in vivo, and lignans inherently possess anti-inflammatory properties, further studies are warranted to investigate the immunomodulatory and metabolic regulatory potentials of both compounds. Furthermore, to promote lignans clinical application, systematic assessments on their toxicity and pharmacokinetic profiles in vivo are urgently required.

Conclusions

The present study identifies Pronaphthalide A and Procumbenoside I as effective anti-SFTSV candidates that inhibit viral entry by specifically binding to the viral glycoprotein Gn. Procumbenoside I exhibits potent therapeutic efficacy in vivo, highlighting its potential as a lead compound for antiviral drug development. These findings provide new mechanistic insights into lignan-mediated antiviral activity against bunyaviruses. Future studies should focus on elucidating the precise molecular basis of lignan-viral-host interactions, evaluating their broad-spectrum antiviral potential, and developing advanced drug delivery systems to enhance the solubility and bioavailability of these promising natural products.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and compounds

Huh-7 cells obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC) and Vero cells obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) were maintained in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco Invitrogen, New York, America) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco Invitrogen) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco Invitrogen). THP-1 cells were obtained from ATCC and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco Invitrogen) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. Lignan compounds used in the experiment were provided by the collaborating unit. Drugs information is listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Viruses

The SFTSV strain HBMC16_human_2015 and the LCMV strain Armstrong were obtained from the National Virus Resource Centre of China (Han et al., 2024). The Wetland virus (WELV) strain PS1 was isolated from a febrile patient (XA Zhang et al., 2024). SFTSV, WELV, and LCMV were propagated in Vero cells and titrated on the cells using an immunological focus assay. Viruses were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until use to avoid any unnecessary freeze-thaw cycles. All infection experiments were performed in biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) or BSL-3 facilities, respectively, in accordance with the institutional biosafety operating procedures.

In vitro antiviral activity assay

Cells were seeded in 48-well plates and infected with SFTSV, WELV or LCMV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.2 for 2 h at 37 °C. After removing the supernatant, cells were cultured with vehicle (DMSO, drug dissolvent) or serially diluted lignan compounds. Intracellular viral RNA levels and supernatant viral loads were measured at 48 hpi using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) assay. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated through nonlinear regression analysis with the use of GraphPad Prism software.

Determination of cytotoxicity

Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells in 96-well plates and treated with different concentrations of lignan compounds for 48 h. The median cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of the compounds was detected using a CCK-8 Kit (APPLYGEN, Beijing, China). The absorbance at 450 nm was measured by using a microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany).

RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells, cell culture supernatants, and tissues using the RNAprep Pure Cell/Bacteria Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China), TIANamp Virus RNA Kit (TIANGEN, China), and RNA Easy Fast Tissue/Cell Kit (TIANGEN, China), respectively. Intracellular viral RNA levels were examined using the HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR SYBR Green Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Viral loads in the supernatant were examined using the HiScript III U + One Step qRT-PCR Probe Kit (Vazyme, China) on a LightCycler 480. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Briefly, plasmids containing known copy numbers of amplification targets were included to generate a standard curve for quantification. Viral copy number (SFTSV/WELV/LCMV) were determined by comparing samples with a serially diluted standard plasmids of known concentration. All samples were quantified in at least duplicate wells. To minimize risk for contamination, template isolation and PCR setup were performed with specified pipettor sets in separate rooms, and certified DNA/RNase-free filter barrier tips were used to prevent aerosol contamination. All PCR assays were performed with appropriate controls.

Immunological focus assay

Cell culture supernatants or homogenized mouse tissue suspensions were serially diluted 10-fold to incubated on confluent Vero cell monolayers for 2 h. The culture medium was then replaced with DMEM containing 2% FBS and 1.25% carboxymethylcellulose (Millipore, Billerica, America). At 96 hpi, Vero cell monolayers were fixed with 4% formaldehyde in PBS and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China) in balanced salt solution. Cells were stained with a mouse monoclonal antibody against SFTSV NP (0.75 μg/mL) (Zhang et al., 2023) followed by a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:1000 dilution).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing 1% PMSF (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, America) and a phosphatase inhibitor mixture (APPLYGEN) on ice. Protein concentration of cell lysates was determined using a BCA Protein Quantification Kit (Vazyme, China). Protein samples were boiled with 5 × SDS sample buffer at 100 °C for 5 min prior to immunoblot analysis. Protein lysates were separated by 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked using 5% skim milk (BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, America) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using the Chemi Dog 5200T Automatic Luminescence Imaging System (Tanon, Shanghai, China) after being treated with an enhance chemiluminescence kit (Vazyme, China). Sources and dilutions of the above antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Virus inactivation assay and time-of-addition assay

For the virus inactivation assay, SFTSV was incubated with lignans at 37 °C for 1 h, and then diluted 1000-fold for cell infection. Infected cells were collected at 24 hpi and subjected to qRT-PCR. The time-of-addition assay was performed as described previously with modifications (Li et al., 2021a). Huh7 cells were infected with SFTSV at an MOI of 1 (0 h), and the initial inoculum was removed at 2 hpi. For the pre-treatment group, cells were pre-treated with lignans for 1 h, followed by 2 h of virus incubation, and then removal of both drugs and virus. For the post-treatment group, cells were infected with SFTSV for 2 h, and lignans were added after virus removal. For the whole-process group, cells were treated with lignans from 1 h pre-infection to 24 hpi. Infected cells were collected at 24 hpi for qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis.

Binding assay

Huh7 cells were pre-treated with different concentrations of lignans or vehicle (DMSO) at 37 °C for 1 h, and then chilled at 4 °C for 15 min. Cells were incubated with SFTSV (MOI = 5) at 4 °C for 2 h to allow virus binding without internalization. Unbound virions were removed by washing with pre-cooled PBS. Cells were harvested, lysed, and the relative level of bound virions was quantified by qRT-PCR.

Internalization assay

Huh7 cells pre-treated with lignans or DMSO for 1 h were chilled at 4 °C for 15 min and incubated with SFTSV (MOI = 5) for 1 h. After washing with pre-cooled PBS to remove unbound virions, cells were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h in the presence of ammonium chloride (20 mM). Cells were then washed with PBS and treated with trypsin to remove the non-internalized virions. The relative level of internalized virions was determined by qRT-PCR.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

CETSA was performed as described previously (Ye et al., 2025). Huh7 cells infected with SFTSV (MOI = 5) were cultured in 6-well plates for 48 h, and cellular proteins were extracted. For the temperature-dependent CETSA, protein extracts were divided into aliquots, incubated with 100 μM of lignans or DMSO for 1 h at room temperature, then heated at gradient temperatures (37−82 °C) for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to separate supernatants (soluble proteins) and precipitates. Target protein stability was analyzed by Western blot. For concentration-dependent CETSA, protein extracts were incubated with serial concentrations of lignans (25, 50, 100, 200, 400 μM) for 1 h at room temperature, then heated at 70 °C for 5 min. Target protein analysis was performed as above. Protein band intensities were quantified via ImageJ software.

Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS)

DARTS was performed as described previously (Ye et al., 2025). Huh7 cells infected with SFTSV (MOI = 5) were cultured in 6-well plates for 48 h, and cellular proteins were extracted. Proteins extracts were divided into aliquots, incubated with 100 μM of lignans or DMSO for 1 h at room temperature, then further split and treated with PBS or pronase E (1.6 and 0.8 mg/mL, 1:12.5 and 1:25 dilution) (MCE; Monmouth Junction, America) for 20 min at room temperature. Reactions were terminated by adding loading buffer and boiling. Target protein expression was detected by Western blot, and band intensities were quantified via ImageJ software.

BLI experiment

The interaction between lignans and SFTSV-Gn protein was scrutinized in a real-time setting at 25 °C, utilizing the Sartorius Octet-R96 system (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) according to manufacturer's instructions. SFTSV-Gn protein with His-tag was diluted to a concentration of 10 μg/mL in kinetics buffer [1 × PBS (pH 7.4), 0.01% BSA, 0.0005% Tween-20]. Each well contained a total volume of 200 μL. The experiment consisted of the following steps: (1) Baseline: HIS1K biosensors (Sartorius) were hydrated in kinetics buffer for 10 min, followed by a 60 s baseline acquisition in kinetics buffer. (2) Loading: Biosensors were immersed in SFTSV-Gn protein solution for 300 s. (3) Baseline 2: A second 180-s baseline was established in kinetics buffer to stabilize the signal. (4) Association: Biosensors were transferred to wells containing a serial dilution of lignans (concentration gradient 1000−125 μM) in kinetics buffer for 300 s to monitor binding. (5) Dissociation: Finally, biosensors were moved back to kinetics buffer for 300 s to monitor complex dissociation. A reference sensor, subjected to the same steps but without lignans association, was used for background subtraction. Data was aligned using ForteBio data analysis software.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was conducted to investigate interactions between lignans and core target proteins. Lignan component files in SDF format were retrieved from the collaborating units, and three-dimensional structures of target proteins were sourced from Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). Docking simulations were performed using the CB-DOCK platform (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/index.php) (Liu et al., 2022).

Animal study

Six-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were kept in an environmentally controlled (22 ± 2 °C, 50% ± 10%) specific pathogen-free animal facility in the State Key Laboratory of Pathogens and Biosecurity (Beijing, China). A lethal SFTSV infection mouse model was established by pretreating mice with an anti-interferon α/β receptor subunit 1 (IFNAR1) blocking antibody (Bio X Cell; West Lebanon, America). Briefly, mice were treated with anti-IFNAR1 IgG (300 μg per mouse) by intraperitoneal injection 1 day prior to the infection. Mice were intraperitoneally infected with 100 μL of SFTSV solution (1.5 × 105 PFU/mL). Lignan compounds were administered via intraperitoneal injection at indicated doses, and control mice received an equal volume of the vehicle (5% DMSO, 50% PEG300, 5% Tween 80 and 40% PBS). The treatment regimen lasted for 5 days. All mice were monitored daily for clinical signs (hunched posture, ruffled fur, decreased activity and response to stimuli), body weight, and survival for 12 days. At 5 day post-infection (dpi), serum and spleen samples were collected for quantification of viral loads, and spleen tissues were subjected to histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry analysis

Spleen tissues from the mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) or processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC). A rabbit polyclonal antibody against SFTSV NP was used as the primary antibody for IHC staining.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized as the means and SDs. Student's t-test was used for comparisons of continuous variables between two groups, and One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was used for comparisons of continuous variables among multiple groups. The Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test were used to analyze time-to-event data for treatment effect analysis. A two-sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.
