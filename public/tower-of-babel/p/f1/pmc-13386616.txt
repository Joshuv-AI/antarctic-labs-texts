PAX1 methylation for triage of HR-HPV-positive women: a comparative analysis with cytology and HPV16/18 genotyping
Authors: Zhengjiao Tong, Saiping Mao, Hong Tao, Peng Gan, Hongtao Li, Xiaozhu Pei, Xing Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate PAX1 hypermethylation (PAX1m) as a triage strategy test for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 or worse (CIN3+) in high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV)-positive women versus liquid-based cytology (LBC) and HPV16/18 genotyping.MethodsThis prospective, diagnostic accuracy study enrolled 1,461 h-HPV-positive women. All underwent colposcopy and biopsy. Triage strategies (PAX1m, LBC [ASC-US+], HPV16/18, combinations) were assessed against histology.ResultsPAX1m test exhibited comparable sensitivity (90.1% vs. 94.4%, P = 0.366) but significantly higher specificity (77.2% vs. 18.6%, P < 0.001) than LBC for detecting CIN3+. Combined strategies showed performance: the HPV16/18 + PAX1m test achieved the highest specificity at 64.1% and 2.16 times higher positive predictive value (PPV) than HPV16/18 or LBC. PAX1m and HPV16/18 + PAX1m test achieved the area under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curves (AUC) for CIN3 + lesions with 0.837 and 0.792, respectively. The PAX1m test alone had the highest positive likelihood ratio (LR + = 3.95) and PPV (16.8%). Colposcopy referral rates were reduced to 26.1% (95% CI: 23.9%–28.4%) with PAX1m test and 38.7% (95%CI: 36.3%–41.3%) with HPV16/18 + PAX1m test compared to LBC at 82.1% (95%CI: 80.0%–83.9%). No cervical cancer cases were missed by PAX1m test.ConclusionThe PAX1m test demonstrated favorable specificity compared to cytology in this cohort, though its CIN3 + sensitivity was slightly lower. This approach may offer an adjunctive strategy for resource-limited settings, though prospective validation in screening populations is needed.Trial registrationNot available.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16235-4.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Colposcopy, Triage strategy, High-risk human papillomavirus, PAX1 methylation

Introduction

Major guidelines, including the ASCCP 2019 risk-based management consensus guideline, the 2021 WHO cervical cancer (CC) screening guideline, and the CC screening guidelines in China as well as other countries’ recommendations, advocate for high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) testing as the primary method for CC screening. This recommendation is based on its sensitivity (90.1% for CIN3+) and objectivity when compared to cytology, effectively addressing the limitations of low specificity that are inherent in traditional screening approaches [1–5]. Compared with cytology, objective HPV testing detects more high-grade precancerous lesions and offers a higher negative predictive value; however, its specificity remains suboptimal [6]. Approximately 90% of HPV infections are transient and spontaneously clear within 6–18 months of infection [7]. While CIN1 lesions rarely progress directly to CIN3+ (median progression rate ~ 2% over 2–3 years of follow-up in HPV-persistent cases ), this risk increases significantly in the presence of HR-HPV subtypes (e.g., HPV16/18) and host factors such as immunosuppression or persistent infection, where progression rates may rise to 10–15% over 5–10 years [8]. Without effective triage strategies, directly referring all HR-HPV-positive women for colposcopy can lead to over-referral, increased healthcare burden, patient anxiety, and unnecessary treatments.

Triage strategies for HR-HPV-positive women include cytology, p16/Ki67 dual immunostaining, and HPV16/18 genotyping [9]. Cytology and cellular p16/Ki67 immunostaining faced challenges in real-world applications, particularly in resource-limited regions where there is a scarcity of skilled cytotechnologists or automated instruments. For instance, while high-resource countries may prioritize p16/Ki67 dual-staining due to their advanced immunohistochemistry infrastructure and lower per-test costs, its clinical utility remains limited by subjective interpretation thresholds and variability in staining quality. In contrast, the implementation of p16/Ki67 dual-staining often faces challenges due to limited personnel and resources [10]. Furthermore, HPV16/18 genotyping in HR-HPV-positive women has shown only moderate sensitivity and specificity (74.8% and 75.6%, respectively) [11]. Thus, improving the accuracy, reproducibility, and scalability of triage methods for HR-HPV-positive women remains a clinical challenge.

Recently, DNA methylation has emerged as a promising biomarker for the detection of CC in HPV-induced cervical lesions [12]. The evidence of DNA hypermethylation in key genes such as PAX1, JAM3, and ZNF671 serves as a clinically relevant biomarker for CC detection, providing valuable insights into early diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and therapeutic monitoring [13–19]. Notably, the PAX1 methylation (PAX1m) test kit has been approved by the China National Medical Products Administration for use in women aged > 30 years with non-16/18 HR-HPV positivity, aiding in clinical decision-making regarding colposcopy referral. The number of publications indicating PAX1m test has clinically validated utility in detecting CIN2 + and serves as a primary triage method for non-16/18 hrHPV-positive women. Nevertheless, while it demonstrates greater specificity than cytology in identifying HR-HPV positivity, its clinical application and potential have yet to be thoroughly investigated.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the performance of the PAX1m test in a hospital outpatient setting as a triage strategy for women with HR-HPV-positive results and to compare it with traditional cytology, HPV16/18 genotyping, and their combinations.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This prospective study recruited consecutive patients undergoing CC screening based on HR-HPV testing at the Cervical Lesions Clinic of Changsha Hospital for Maternal & Child Health Care between July 2023 and July 2024. A total of 1,545 patients with HR-HPV-positive test results were initially enrolled. Inclusion criteria: aged 16 years or older and sexually active; intact cervix with no history of cervical conization; no serious immunodeficiency conditions; no history of organ transplantation; and full consent. Exclusion criteria: current treatment for malignant tumors of the female genital tract or other sites; pregnant or breastfeeding. All HR-HPV-positive participants were recommended to undergo cytology testing and colposcopy-directed biopsy. Of these, 19 patients did not undergo cytology, and 1,526 residual cytology specimens were tested for PAX1m. Twenty-one (1.4%) of these tests were invalid and excluded. Additionally, 13 patients did not undergo colposcopy, and 31 did not complete colposcopy-directed biopsy. Ultimately, 1,461 patients were included in the final statistical analysis. However, these exclusions are detailed in the Fig. 1, and analyzed for potential bias in Supplementary Table 1.

The study was approved by the Research and Clinical Trial Ethics Committee of Changsha Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. EC-20230726-02), and all participants provided written informed consent.

The Cervical Lesions Clinic at Changsha Hospital for Maternal & Child Health Care serves as a tertiary referral center for cervical pathology. During the study period, approximately 60% of HR-HPV-positive women were referred from community-based screening programs, while 40% presented directly to our clinic for symptoms, abnormal screening results from other facilities, or follow-up of previously abnormal findings. Referral patterns remained stable throughout the study period as no regional screening policies changed. Importantly, consistent with clinical guidelines, all HR-HPV-positive women were recommended for colposcopy and biopsy regardless of cytology or HPV16/18 results, minimizing the influence of these test results on verification.

HR-HPV DNA genotyping test

Genotyping for 21 HPV types was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (HybriBio Ltd., Guangzhou, China). Patients were classified as HR-HPV-positive if they tested positive for any of the following types: 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66, or 68.

Liquid-based cytology

For HR-HPV-positive women, liquid-based cytology (LBC) is used for triage. LBC was conducted in three steps: (1) Cervical epithelial cells were collected using a cervical brush, which was then rinsed three times in a ThinPrep cell collection vial containing cytoprotective solution; (2) an automated system was used to prepare thin-layer slides and perform detection; (3) cytologic diagnosis was performed according to Bethesda categories. For those with inconsistent cytological results, a third senior cytologiest made the judgment.

PAX1m assay

After cytological examination, the residual exfoliated cervical cells were used for DNA methylation testing. DNA extraction was performed within 5 working days; the DNA could be stored at -20 °C, and the PAX1m test was completed within three months. The PAX1m test (Hoomya, Changsha, China) was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the technicians were unaware of the cytological and histopathological results. DNA from each sample was first treated with a bisulfite conversion kit (Hoomya), converting unmethylated cytosines to uracils, while leaving methylated cytosines unchanged. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using a Roche thermal cycler (Roche Applied Science, Penzberg, Germany), with 50 amplification cycles to determine the cycle threshold (Cp). The ΔCp value was calculated as the difference between the Cp of the internal reference gene Col2A1 and the Cp of PAX1m. If no Cp was detected for PAX1m, a value of 50 was imputed. A ΔCp ≤ 11 indicated a positive methylation result; ΔCp > 11 was considered negative, refering to the instruction manual.

Diagnosis of cervical lesions

Colposcopy was performed by a specialist in a gynecological colposcopy unit. Multipoint biopsies of any suspected lesions were collected; in cases where no lesion was visible, random biopsies were not routinely performed. All biopsied tissues were independently assessed by two pathologists. The most severe lesion identified was considered the final diagnosis. Lesions were classified as cervicitis, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 1 (CIN1), grade 2 (CIN2), grade 3 (CIN3), or CC. In cases of disagreement, a third pathologist reviewed the histology for final adjudication.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1 (Vienna, Austria). Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Triage strategies for HR-HPV-positive women included individual markers such as atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance or worse (ASC-US+), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion or worse (LSIL+), HPV16/18 genotyping, and PAX1m. Combination strategies were denoted by slashes (e.g., HPV16/18/ASC-US+), with a positive result defined as positivity in any component, and a negative result defined only when all components were negative.

Diagnostic performance metrics—including sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios (LRs), positive predictive value, relative positive predictive value, relative negative predictive value, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs)—were calculated using the R packages “DescTools” and “DTComPair.” The comparisons of sensitivity and specificity between the two methods were conducted using the McNemar test. Relative risk (RR) values for positive vs. negative results in each strategy were computed using “epiDisplay,” and visualized with forest plots. The area under the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for each triage method (binary classifier) was determined using the “pROC” and “ggplot2” packages. All P-values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive baseline characteristics

The study included 1,461 h-HPV-positive women, comprising 868 with cervicitis, 431 with CIN1, 91 with CIN2, 60 with CIN3, and 11 with CC. Regarding age distribution, 186 women (12.7%) were aged < 30 years, 1,250 (85.6%) were aged 30–65 years, and 25 (1.7%) were aged > 65 years. The majority of participants had a single HR-HPV infection (74.5%, n = 1,089), followed by double infections (19.8%, n = 290); the maximum number of subtypes detected in a single individual was six. RR of various triage markers and combinations

Among HR-HPV-positive women, the RR for detecting CIN3 + was highest for PAX1m (RR: 25.92; 95% CI: 15.54–43.24). Other significant RRs included cytology (ASC-US+, RR: 3.66; 95% CI: 1.38–9.69), HPV16/18 (RR: 3.28; 95% CI: 2.09–5.14), HPV16/18/ASC-US+ (RR: 9.26; 95% CI: 1.70–50.32), HPV16/18/LSIL+ (RR: 10.2; 95% CI: 5.84–20.05), HPV16/18/ PAX1m (RR: 26.49; 95% CI: 13.70–51.22), and PAX1m /LSIL+ (RR: 26.03; 95% CI: 12.17–55.66; Supplementary Fig. 1). For CIN2+, the highest RRs were observed for PAX1m (RR: 11.98; 95% CI: 8.96–16.01) and HPV16/18/ PAX1m (RR: 12.65; 95% CI: 8.82–18.13).

Performance of triage markers and combinations

The sensitivities for detecting CIN3 + were as follows: ASC-US+ cytology (94.4%), LSIL+ cytology (73.2%), HPV16/18 (40.8%), PAX1m (90.1%), HPV16/18/ASC-US+ (97.5%), HPV16/18/LSIL+ (90.1%), and HPV16/18/ PAX1m (94.4%) (Table 2). HPV16/18 alone had the highest specificity (83.8%), followed by PAX1m (77.2%). Among combination strategies, HPV16/18/PAX1m demonstrated the highest specificity (64.1%).

In terms of positive predictive value, PAX1m showed the highest value at 16.8%, followed by HPV16/18/ PAX1m (11.8%). The relative positive predictive value of PAX1m was significantly higher than that of ASC-US+ (RR: 3.01; 95% CI: 2.66–3.40; P < 0.001). The positive predictive value of HPV16/18/PAX1m was significantly higher than that of HPV16/18/ASC-US+ (RR: 2.16; 95% CI: 1.99–2.34; P < 0.001). However, there was no statistically significant difference between the negative predictive values of PAX1m and ASC-US+ (98.5% vs. 99.4%; P = 0.271).

The AUCs for PAX1m for detecting CIN3 + and CIN2 + were 0.837 (95% CI: 0.800–0.873) and 0.808 (95% CI: 0.776–0.840), respectively. The AUCs were observed for HPV16/18/PAX1m: 0.792 (95% CI: 0.762–0.822) for CIN3 + and 0.782 (95% CI: 0.755–0.809) for CIN2+ (Fig. 2).

Colposcopy referral and diagnostic efficiency for CIN3+

Among the 1,461 women included in the final analysis, 82.1% would have been referred for colposcopy using the ASC-US+ criterion. By contrast, only 26.1% would have been referred to using PAX1m as the triage strategy. Referral rates for HPV16/18/ASC-US + and HPV16/18/PAX1m strategies were 88.7% and 38.7%, respectively. The number of CIN3 + cases missed by ASC-US+, PAX1m, HPV16/18/ASC-US+, and HPV16/18/PAX1m were 4, 7, 0, and 4, respectively.

The number of colposcopies required to detect one case of CIN3 + was lowest for PAX1m (6.0), followed by HPV16/18/ PAX1m (8.4), and higher for ASC-US+ (17.9) and HPV16/18/ASC-US+ (18.3) (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In our cohort (CIN3 + prevalence 4.9%), PAX1m demonstrated comparable sensitivity (90.1% vs. 94.4%; P = 0.366) and significantly higher specificity (77.2% vs. 18.6%; P < 0.001) compared to cytology (ASC-US+), with a 100% positivity rate in CC cases. In contrast, HPV16/18 genotyping alone had the lowest sensitivity at 40.8% (Table 2). Among combined triage strategies, HPV16/18 plus PAX1m achieved the favourable AUCs for detecting CIN2 + and CIN3+ (0.782 and 0.792, respectively; Fig. 2). This combination required fewer colposcopies per CIN3 + case detected compared to HPV16/18/ASC-US+ (8.4 vs. 18.3; Supplementary Table 2). These findings suggest that PAX1m alone or combined with HPV16/18 offers a favorable balance of diagnostic performance and resource efficiency, characterized by lower colposcopy referral rates, higher detection rates of CIN2+ / CIN3+, and higher positive predictive value (Fig. 3).

Currently, cervical cytology and HPV16/18 genotyping are the most commonly used triage approaches [20]. In this study, ASC-US+ cytology had very high sensitivity for detecting CIN3+ (94.4%), consistent with previous findings [21]. However, this high sensitivity may be influenced by the subjective nature of cytologic interpretation, which varies across countries and clinical settings [22–25]. Of the cytologic evaluations, 82.1% were classified as abnormal, and ASC-US accounted for 60.9% (730/1,199; Table 1), contributing to the high sensitivity but low positive predictive value (5.6%) of this method. Compared with cytology, more CIN2 or CIN3 cases were missed by PAX1m (Supplementary Table 2). Analysis of the seven CIN3 + cases missed by PAX1m (Supplementary Table 3) reveals important patterns: three were HPV16/18-positive, 4 had high-grade cytology (AGC, HSIL or ASC-H), and one case had a borderline negative ΔCp value (11.07) just above the threshold, another case was older (55 years old).These findings suggest that PAX1m negativity in the presence of HPV16/18 positivity or high-grade cytology should not preclude colposcopy referral. Notably, all missed cases were CIN3 rather than cancer, and no cancers were missed. A possible reason is that the DNA methylation testing was performed on residual specimens from cytological examination. HPV genotype distribution is provided in Supplementary Table 4. The LSIL+ triage strategy had moderate sensitivity (73.2%) and specificity (70.0%), though it was significantly less sensitive than PAX1m (P = 0.007). We note that the triage methods are binary classifiers (positive/negative). The reported AUC values, while useful for comparative purposes across strategies, should be interpreted cautiously.

In this study, PAX1m and ASC-US + had similar negative predictive values for detecting CIN3+ (99.4% vs. 98.5%; P = 0.271), and a positive predictive value 3.01 times greater (95% CI: 2.66–3.40; Table 2). To contextualize PPV for screening populations, we modeled expected PPV at lower CIN3 + prevalence levels using the sensitivity and specificity estimates from our cohort according Bayesian method. At a prevalence of 0.5% (typical of screened populations), the projected PPV for PAX1m would be 1.9% (vs. 0.6% for ASC-US+). While PAX1m maintains relatively higher PPV across prevalence levels, these projections underscore that absolute PPV values from our enriched cohort should not be directly generalized to screening settings.

Additionally, among women with non-HPV16/18 HR-HPV infections, PAX1m yielded a higher AUC, specificity, and positive predictive value for CIN2+ / CIN3 + detection than ASC-US+ (Supplementary Table 5), consistent with findings from a metropolitan hospital study [26]. among HPV16/18-positive women, the sensitivities of PAX1m and ASC-US+ were 89.7% and 86.2%, respectively (Supplementary Table 6). Triage strategies involving ASC-US+ alone or in combination had colposcopy referral rates exceeding 80%, whereas PAX1m-based strategies—except when combined with ASC-US+—had referral rates below 50% (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 3A and C). The LR + and positive predictive values of PAX1m and HPV16/18/PAX1m were superior to other strategies (Fig. 3B and D), particularly in high-prevalence settings, such as colposcopy clinics. Based on our findings and current clinical practices, we present a hypothetical framework, a triage protocol using PAX1m for HR-HPV-positive women (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similar protocols have recently been proposed by institutions, such as Peking Union Medical College Hospital, Xiangya Hospital, and the International Peace Maternity and Child Health Hospital [23, 27, 28].

PAX1, as a tumor suppressor gene, sssuppresses the CC cells proliferation [29], and its methylation level correlates with p16/Ki67 expression, an important tumor suppressor marker [30]. In Asian populations, PAX1m shown promising results in CC screening [31], with a meta-analysis reporting pooled sensitivity and specificity of 80% and 89% [32]. Performance across studies may be attributable to differences in thresholds and population characteristics [33]. For instance, the threshold for predicting CC progression in patients with CIN3 has been defined as ΔCp ≤ 8.55 [34], and for evaluating endocervical curettage utility, as ΔCp < 6 [35]. Unfortunately, other currently investigated molecular triage approaches, such as dual-stain p16/Ki67 [36] or other methylation markers (e.g., FAM19A4/miR124-2 [16], CADM1/MAL [37]), were not compared in the study.

This study had several limitations. First, not all HR-HPV-positive participants underwent histopathological confirmation, potentially introducing verification bias, and potentially overestimate the PPV. Second, the study was conducted at Cervical Lesions Clinic of a single center, limiting the generalizability of results across regions or populations. Third, data comparing PAX1m with other triage strategies in primary screening settings are limited. We used ASC-US as the cytologic cutoff due to its routine use in real-world practice. Notably, PAX1m does not rely on subjective cytological analysis and can be detected using only DNA samples, which makes an automated molecular screening strategy based on HPV primary screening with PAX1m triage possible. Future, prospective and multicenter randomized controlled trials with cost-effectiveness analyses are needed to confirm its clinical utility and inform large-scale implementation.

Conclusion

In conclusions, PAX1m, either alone or combined with HPV16/18 genotyping, offers a triage approach with high diagnostic accuracy, lower colposcopy referral rates, and high positive predictive value for detecting CIN3+ / CIN2+.
