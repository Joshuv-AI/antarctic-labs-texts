Comprehensive characterization of volatile compounds in Iranian black teas using chemometric analysis of GC-MS fingerprints
Authors: Adineh Aminianfar, Mohammad Hossein Fatemi, Fatemeh Azimi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBlack tea, a widely popular non-alcoholic beverage, is renowned for its unique aroma and has attracted significant attention due to its complex composition. However, the chemical profile of Iranian tea remains largely unexplored. In this research, black tea samples from key tea cultivation regions in four geographical areas in northern Iran were firstly analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction followed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS) to separate, identify, and quantify their volatile organic compounds. Subsequently, employing a robust investigative strategy, we utilized for the first time the well-known multivariate curve resolution-alternating least square (MCR-ALS) method as a deconvolution technique to analyze the complex GC–MS peak clusters of tea samples. This approach effectively addressed challenges such as severe baseline drifts, overlapping peaks, and background noise, enabling the identification of minor components responsible for the distinct flavors and tastes across various samples. The MCR-ALS technique significantly improved the resolution of spectral and elution profiles, enabling both qualitative and semi-quantitative analysis of tea constituents. Qualitative analysis involved comparing resolved peak profiles to theoretical spectra, along with retention indices, while semi-quantification was conducted using the overall volume integration (OVI) approach for volatile compounds, providing a more accurate correlation between peak areas and concentrations. The application of chemometric tools in GC–MS analysis increased the number of recognized components in four tea samples, expanding from 54 to 256 components, all with concentrations exceeding 0.1 %. Among them, 32 volatile compounds were present in every tea sample. Hydrocarbons (including alkenes, alkanes, cycloalkanes, monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes), esters and alcohols were the three major chemical classes, comprising 78 % of the total relative content of volatile compounds. Analyzing black teas from four distinct regions revealed variations not only in their volatile components but also in their relative proportions. This integrated approach provides a comprehensive understanding of the volatile chemical profiles in Iranian black teas, enhances knowledge about their unique characteristics across diverse geographical origin, and lays the groundwork for quality improvement.Keywords: Iranian black tea, Volatile organic compounds, HS-SPME-GC–MS, Multivariate curve resolution-alternating least square, Identification

Introduction

Tea is a globally consumed beverage, second only to water in terms of consumption. The acceptance and preference of tea across various countries and cultures are closely linked to its unique sensory characteristics such as aroma, taste, and color, as well as its health-promoting effects and socio-cultural factors. Both tea consumption and production have increased continuously in recent years, with black tea accounting for approximately 80 % of the international tea market (Qin et al., 2023; Qu et al., 2020). Given the growing demand for tea, the quality evaluation of tea has garnered significant attention from researchers. Consequently, developing accurate methods for characterizing and identifying constituent profiles is crucial in the rapidly expanding global tea market (Wang et al., 2021).

According to the FAO, nearly 30 million tons of tea leaves were produced in humid tropical and subtropical regions worldwide in 2021, with Iran contributing 113,899 tons to that total (FAOSTAT, 2021). Iranians consume roughly 1.5 kg of dry black tea per person year, indicating that tea drinking is deeply embedded in Iranian daily life (Miri et al., 2018). Gilan, located in northern Iran, is one of the country's leading tea-producing regions, with tea being the main crop in the eastern parts of the province (Rahimi-Ajdadi & Khani, 2021). Despite this, few studies have investigated the chemical structure of Iranian tea, and the composition of teas from different geographical origins remains poorly understood.

The various volatile compounds in tea contribute to its unique and characteristic aroma. Aroma, defined by a blend of intricate volatile compounds, is a critical sensory attribute that signifies the tea quality, strongly influences drinkers' perceptions, and significantly impacts the economic value of tea (Qin et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023). Although volatile compounds make up only about 0.01 % of the dry weight of tea, they have a profound effect on its quality due to their low detection thresholds and high odor sensibility (Hao et al., 2023). To date, nearly 700 volatile constituents have been identified in previous studies. The complexity of tea volatiles, their trace levels, and the variation in aroma even within the same tea category make detecting, identifying, and characterizing these compounds challenging due to their wide variability (Xu et al., 2022). Today, the chemical composition of aroma compounds in black tea has been extensively studied. Among the many reported analytical procedures, gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) combined with solid-phase microextraction (SPME) is a widely used and effective technique for both the qualitative and quantitative analysis of food-borne VOCs, including those found in tea (Wang et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023).

Despite the high separation and identification capabilities of hyphenated methods, these instruments face persistent issues such as baseline drift, elution time shifts, and low signal-to-noise ratio (S/N), which can compromise the accuracy of qualitative and quantitative results (Anzardi, Arancibia, & Olivieri, 2021). Additionally, identifying components within complex matrices presents significant challenges, particularly when using similarity indices (SIs) from straightforward MS database searches. This challenge is especially pronounced for constituents present at low concentrations, where residual gas and column background peaks can obscure results. Moreover, library searches may identify different constituents at various scan points within a single chromatographic peak (Azimi & Fatemi, 2016). It is increasingly recognized that, due to both physical and computational limitations, achieving sufficient resolution to efficiently separate all compounds in highly complex mixtures through sample preparation and different hyphenated chromatographic systems is practically impossible (Anzardi et al., 2021).

In recent years, chemometric techniques based on multivariate data analysis, have been proposed to address the challenges mentioned above. These techniques extract information by deconvolution and resolving spectral and chromatographic data, enabling more accurate quantification (Parastar & Tauler, 2014). Multivariate data analysis methods leverage second-order data integrity to resolve complex chemical datasets into pure component profiles using mathematical decomposition tools. Currently, chemometric “mathematical separation” is increasingly applied in a wide range of fields to overcome chromatographic separation difficulties, eliminating the need for laborious pretreatment steps. This approach reduces the use of hazardous solvents, saves time and money, and effectively addresses retention time (RT) shifts and baseline drifts in chromatographic analysis, leading to more precise quantitative data (Feizi, Hashemi-Nasab, Golpelichi, Saburouh, & Parastar, 2021). Among different multivariate methods, multivariate curve resolution-alternating least square (MCR-ALS) is a widely used approach. It employs a bilinear soft modeling algorithm to resolve a mixture of signals, iteratively providing pure concentration profiles and spectra (Parastar & Tauler, 2014). MCR-ALS is applied across various data sets, ranging from first-order data to multiway analysis, though it is primary used for chromatographic data (Feizi et al., 2021). This method can recover pure spectra and corresponding elution profiles of components while resolving co-eluted peaks, despite retention time shifts, using only the data from raw chromatographic runs (Peng et al., 2021).

Numerous reports highlight the applications of MCR methodology to multivariate data analysis. For instance, Azimi et al. (Azimi & Fatemi, 2016) employed the MCR-ALS approach to perform a comprehensive GC–MS analysis of the volatile chemical components in Iranian Citrus aurantium L. peel. Furthermore, Ten-Doménech et al. (Ten-Doménech et al., 2023) applied the MCR-ALS method to identify metabolic pathways associated with each dynamic component in longitudinal metabolomic profiles. Given the limited research on volatile compounds in Iranian black teas and the lack of comprehensive reports on their chemical composition, this study employs MCR-ALS to unravel the complex GC–MS aroma peak clusters of black teas from various key geographical regions. By leveraging advanced curve resolution techniques, our work has the potential to uncover previously undetected compounds, representing a significant advancement in this field.

In this study, the recovered mass spectral profiles and their associated retention indices were used to identify the resolved peaks. Moreover, the relative abundance of each compound was calculated using the peak areas of the elution profiles resolved by MCR-ALS. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive report has been published on the chromatographic fingerprinting of regional Iranian teas using any MCR approach, highlighting the pioneering nature of this research. The findings provide a detailed fingerprint of the volatile constituents based on the resolved pure contribution profiles, identifying characteristic volatile compounds in black tea samples from various Iranian regions. These results may also prove valuable in identifying the key volatile organic compounds that are crucial for quality assurance and enhancing the flavor of black teas.

Materials and methods

Tea samples and chemicals

Black tea samples were collected from four different geographical regions in Gilan province, a major tea-producing area in northern Iran. The locations of the samples, including Shaft, Lahijan, Amlash, and Rudsar, along with their geographical coordinates and IDs, ST, LT, AT, and RT, respectively, are shown in Fig. 1. These samples, which belong to the Bazri cultivar (Rahimi, Kordrostami, Mohamadhasani, & Chaeikar, 2021), were harvested and produced using the orthodox method, which includes withering, rolling, fermentation, and drying (Pou, Paul, & Malakar, 2019), during the spring harvest in May 2021. Their authenticity was verified by the Iranian National Standardization Organization (INSO). The black tea samples were sealed in aluminum foil bags and stored in a cool, dry place to ensure the stability of their VOCs. Helium of 99.999 % purity was sourced from Sabalan Co. in Iran and the n-alkane mixed standards (C7-C40) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich.

Details of the tea samples examined. (A) Geographical origin of the tea samples. (B) IDs and production areas of the various teas.

Extraction procedure

Volatile compounds were extracted from black tea using the headspace solid phase microextraction (HS-SPME) method with a manual SPME holder and a fiber coated with 100 μm polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (Bellefonte, PA, USA). Prior to use, the SPME fiber was conditioned for 30 min at 270 °C in the GC–MS injection port to prevent contamination. A total of 6.0 g of homogenized ground tea sample was transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial, which was then tightly sealed using a PTFA septum. The vial was equilibrated in a 70 °C water bath for 10 min, after which the SPME fiber was exposed to the sample headspace for 40 min under the same conditions to absorb the volatile compounds. The absorbed volatiles were then desorbed from the SPME fiber in the GC–MS injector at 250 °C for 3 min and immediately analyzed by GC–MS.

Chromatographic conditions and instrumentation

Following HS-SPME extraction, the volatile compounds in the tea samples were separated and identified using an Agilent Technologies 7890 A gas chromatograph (Santa Clara, CA, USA), equipped with an Agilent 5975C mass spectrometry detector (MSD). Thermal desorption of the SPME fiber was performed at an injector temperature of 250 °C in spitless mode for 3 min. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a DB-1 MS capillary column (60 m length, 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 μm film thickness). Helium (GC-grade) was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The temperature gradient program began at 50 °C, held for 2 min, followed by an increase at 2 °C/min to 120 °C, where it was maintained for 3 min. The temperature was then ramped to 150 °C at 3 °C/min and held for 10 min. Finally, the temperature was increased to 200 °C at 6 °C/min, with a final hold time of 5 min. The total GC runtime was 73.33 min. Mass spectrometry was performed using electron-impact (EI) positive ionization mode with a 70 eV electron energy. The transfer line, ion source, and quadrupole analyzer temperatures were set to 280 °C, 230 °C, and 150 °C, respectively. Full-scan mode was used, ranging from 30 to 500 aum, with a solvent delay time of 2.50 min.

Constituent identification and quantification

Tentative identification of volatile compounds was performed using retention indices and mass spectral matching from the NIST mass spectral library v2.0. The reverse match factor (RMF) from the NIST software was applied to select the best match for MCR-ALS resolved spectra, with scores ranging from 0 (no match) to 1000 (perfect match). A score of 900 or above indicates a very good match, 800–900 is considered a good match, 700–800 is fair, and below 600 is deemed a poor match (Tang et al., 2022). Data analysis

Data pretreatment

In the first stage of our study, preprocessing was conducted using MCRC software, a chemometric tool for analyzing two-way chromatographic data. Congruence analysis and least-square fitting, as proposed by Liang and Kvalheim, were applied to correct baseline and background variations, effectively eliminating baseline drift from the chromatographic data (Kvalheim & Liang, 1992). The second preprocessing stage involved denoising using the morphological score approach, which reduces homoscedastic noise by distinguishing signal from noise and removing noisy spectral channels (Jalali-Heravi, Parastar, Kamalzadeh, Tauler, & Jaumot, 2010). Subsequently, the Savitzky-Golay filter was applied for smoothing, further reducing heteroscedastic noise (Brereton, 2003). This approach facilitates the effective analysis of fingerprint data using the MCR-ALS method, even in the presence of overlapping peaks, unknown interferences, baseline drift, and other challenges (Parastar & Tauler, 2014).

Multivariate curve resolution-alternating least squares (MCR-ALS) analysis

Multivariate data analysis plays a critical role in exploring chemical data, especially spectroscopic and chromatographic data. Among these techniques, multivariate curve resolution (MCR) is a widely used and highly effective tool for modeling and analyzing multi-way data across various application domains. MCR techniques sequentially identify components in unknown mixtures and have proven successful in accurately separating highly overlapping compounds (Rutan, de Juan, & Tauler, 2020). Currently the most widely used and flexible MCR algorithm is multivariate curve resolution-alternating least squares (MCR-ALS), first proposed by Tauler in 1995 (Tauler, 1995).

The development of the MCR-ALS methodology has evolved alongside its expanding applications in the analytical field. Its widespread use is attributed to its versatile design, which allows for the implementation of new application-specific constraints and addresses a wide range of problems and data scenarios (Ruckebusch & Blanchet, 2013). The MCR-ALS algorithm has been extensively explored in the original literature (Jalali-Heravi et al., 2010; Jaumot, de Juan, & Tauler, 2015), so only a brief description is provided here. In this model, the GC–MS dataset is organized into an experimental data matrix, representing a specific chromatographic region, with elution times as rows and m/z values as columns. This matrix is then analyzed using MCR-ALS. The residuals, or the portion of the measured signal not explained by the MCR bilinear model of the k components present in the sample, are contained in matrix E. The superscript T denotes the matrix transpose operation (Tauler, 1995).

Determining the chemical ranks within the system is a crucial first step in MCR-ALS-based research, as it significantly influences the subsequent qualitative and quantitative outcomes. Ideally, the number of chemical constituents in the sample should correspond to the appropriate number of components. Various methods can be employed to establish the correct chemical rank of a dataset, with principal component analysis (PCA) and singular value decomposition (SVD) being commonly used (Jaumot et al., 2015). In high-noise environments, such as GC–MS analysis, other approaches like morphological score and subspace comparison may be preferable. These methods focus on analyzing key factors rather than full rank matrices, as in PCA, to mitigate the impact of noise (Azimi & Fatemi, 2016).

Evolving factor analysis (EFA) and fixed-size moving window-evolving factor analysis (FSMW-EFA) are methods designed to improve the accuracy of estimating chemical constituents and assessing peak purity in bilinear data. Unlike chemical rank determination methods, which provide only an estimate of the actual number of components, EFA and FSMW-EFA offer local rank information for more precise estimations. FSMW-EFA further enables the determination of peak purity by identifying selective regions, zero-component regions (ZCR), and overlapped regions within each peak cluster. It provides more reliable results than the EFA method, especially in detecting impurities or minor compounds beneath a primary peak.

An initial estimate for the spectral or concentration profiles, which can be derived from the original data, is required to begin the ALS iterations after determining the number of components in the desired data matrix. Several approaches, including evolving factor analysis, simple-to-use interactive self-modeling mixture analysis (SIMPLISMA), and the orthogonal projection approach (OPA), have been documented in the literature for obtaining these initial estimates (Jalali-Heravi et al., 2010). One notable limitation of the MCR technique is the issue of ambiguity, which represents a significant challenge. This means that various solutions (and their linear combinations) can equally fit the experimental data. To minimize the effects of ambiguities and produce chemically interpretable profiles, various constraints, imposing basic chemical or physical assumptions on the data, such as non-negativity, closure, unimodality, spectral normalization, and selectivity can be applied during each iteration of the resolution process. In fact, the core strength of MCR methods lie in the proper formulation and implementation of these constraints (Ruckebusch & Blanchet, 2013).

Appropriate statistical parameters, such as lack of fit (LOF) and explained variance (R2) are calculated to assess the reliability of the resolution outcomes. Optimal model fitting is achieved when LOF approaches zero and R2 approaches one, indicating a strong alignment between the experimental and resolved matrices. Once the pure spectra and resolved chromatographic profiles for each component have been extracted, identification can be performed by conducting similarity searches in databases using the match factor or reverse match factor, along with comparing GC retention indices. After decomposing the data matrix D using MCR-ALS, the concentration data obtained from the elution profiles (C matrix) can be used for quantitative predictions. This can be accomplished using the overall volume integration (OVI) method, which involves summing the peak area integration at each m/z point for each component to determine the relative amounts of the constituents within the total ion chromatogram (TIC). Fig. S1 in the supplementary data illustrates the steps involved in the data analysis (Jalali-Heravi & Parastar, 2011; Malekzadeh & Fatemi, 2015).

Software

The GC–MS data were collected and converted to American Standard Code for Information Interchange (ASCII) format using the “Tools/Export 3-D Data” tab in the enhanced MSD ChemStation software package (G1701 DA, version D.00.01.27). All chemometric resolution methods were performed using the MCR-ALS toolbox available from the MCR homepage in the MATLAB R2016a environment. The MCRC software v1.0 was used for data preprocessing, determination of chemical rank, local rank analysis, and MCR-ALS analysis (Jaumot et al., 2015). All calculations were carried out on an ASUS personal computer equipped with an Intel Core™ i5 processor. Graphing was conducted using RStudio and ArcMap (Version 10.6.1).

Results and discussion

Volatile compounds profiling by GC–MS

In this study, black tea samples cultivated in northern Iran were first analyzed using headspace SPME coupled with GC–MS. This approach allowed for the identification, determination and comparison of their volatile chemical constituents across four different geographical origins, including ST, AT, LT and RT. All samples were processed using orthodox methods, which convert fresh tea leaves into finished products. It is important to note that not all VOCs contribute equally to the aroma of tea. The sensory impact of VOCs depends not only on their concentration and odor characteristics but also significantly on their odor thresholds for perception (Hao et al., 2023). Methods for extracting VOCs from tea leaves and infusions include solvent extraction (SE), distillation, headspace extraction, and liquid absorption. However, each method has its limitations, often leading to incomplete recovery and discrimination of flavor compounds present in tea. Among the reported studies, HS-SPME has proven to be an effective extraction technique for analyzing complex matrices. It is highly regarded for its exceptional linearity, reproducibility, and low detection limits, and it integrates sampling, pre-concentration, and sample introduction into a single, continuous step (Hao et al., 2023; D. Wang et al., 2023; Y. Xu et al., 2023). In HS-SPME, the choice of sorbent material used for the fiber coating is crucial for determining selectivity and recovery. PDMS, an absorbent-type fiber, is well-suited for extracting non-polar compounds, as demonstrated in this study. In contrast, fibers, such as polyacrylate (PA) are more effective for polar compounds (Yang et al., 2018). Fig. 2 illustrates the total ion chromatograms of volatile compounds from teas sourced from four distinct regional locations.

Chromatograms of the volatile profiles obtained from black tea samples from four different geographical regions using HS-SPME-GC–MS.

A total of fifty-four volatile compounds were detected and identified by comparing their mass spectra and retention indices with those in the NIST 2017 database, providing tentative characterizations. Of these, 36 volatiles were present in the ST sample, 32 in the AT sample, 20 in the LT sample and 17 in the RT sample. Although the VOCs of the four black tea samples were comparable, several distinct differences were observed, indicating significant variation in VOCs among teas from different producing areas. It is important to note that the aroma profile is influenced by many factors, including the geographical position of the tea plantation, cultivation methods, weather, soil conditions, and other elements that affect the formation and release of flavor (Wang et al., 2021). To accurately assess each factor's impact on tea aroma, all other influencing variables must be controlled. As illustrated in Fig. S2 in the supplementary data, the Venn plot demonstrates the numerical intersection and differentiation of volatile compounds found in the four tea samples. Compounds common to all four samples were identified, comprising only about 12.96 % of the total chemical compounds detected. Additionally, 8, 7, 1, and 8 unique volatile organic compounds were identified exclusively in ST, AT, LT and RT samples, respectively. Among these, cis-3-hexenyl-α-methylbutyrate, cis-geraniol, pristane, cyclotetradecane, heptadecane, 2-ethylhexyl salicylate, octadecane and nonadecane were found only in the ST sample. The VOCs unique to AT included undecane, (R,S)-5-ethyl-6-methyl-3E-hepten-2-one, trans-2-hexenyl butyrate, 2,6-dimethyl-undecane, trans-geraniol, trans-geranylacetone, and phytone. Hexanedioic acid, bis(2-ethylhexyl) ester was the sole volatile organic compound specific to LT. Finally, the VOCs exclusive to RT were 3-methyl butanal, o-cymene, limonene, β-phellandrene, cis-linalool oxide, α-terpineol acetate, and 2,6-octadien-1-ol, 3,7-dimethyl-, acetate. The presence of these unique VOCs makes it easier to differentiate black tea samples from various regions. The black tea samples also shared 7 VOCs, including linalool, dodecane, tridecane, trans-β-ionone, butylated hydroxytoluene, tetradecane, and hexadecane. In previous studies, these compounds were recognized as the primary flavor constituents in various types of tea, including black, green, and dark tea (Teng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2022).

To provide clarity, the identified compounds were classified into nine chemical subclasses consisting of 7 ketones, 19 hydrocarbons, 15 esters, 6 alcohols, 3 aldehydes, and 4 other compounds (Fig. S3 in the supplementary data). Hydrocarbons were the most abundant, representing 34–44 % of the total, followed by esters (12–35 %) and alcohols (10–22 %). Ketones accounted for 5–13 %, aldehydes for 2–6 % and phenols for 2–8 %. Ethers, nitrogenous compounds, and furans, were present in smaller amounts, each ranging from 1 to 3 % (Fig. S4 in the supplementary data).

Resolution by MCR-ALS and qualitative analysis

HS-SPME-GC–MS was employed to analyze the volatile compounds in tea samples from different regions of Iran. The similarity search was then performed in the NIST mass spectral library to tentatively identify compounds of interest. As shown in Fig. 2, the GC–MS total ion chromatograms of the tea samples are highly complex, reflecting the large number of components, significant baseline drifts, and high background noise in the data. Additionally, certain peaks overlap due to variations in their mass spectra at different scan points, despite appearing as single peaks. Furthermore, there is a high probability of detecting embedded peaks. In such cases, performing a direct library search using the NIST MS database without additional data processing may lead to inaccurate identifications. Under these conditions, match indices for some chromatographic peaks tend to be low when using direct MS database searches. A direct mass spectrometry analysis of the four tea samples identified only 54 components through a straightforward search in the MS database. This approach makes it difficult to obtain accurate chemical information regarding the composition of the components and their relative abundance. To address these challenges and enable precise qualitative and quantitative analyses, the chemometric multivariate resolution method was employed to explore the pure chromatograms and mass spectra of two-way bilinear multi-component data. First, the total ion chromatogram was segmented into the required sub-matrices, with retention time (min) as rows and m/z values as columns, based on visual observation of the zero component chromatographic areas in the extraction elution sequence.

As outlined in section 2.5.1, before MCR-ALS fitting, the GC–MS fingerprints of all samples underwent baseline/background correction and Savitzky-Golay smoothing (using a window of 5 or 7 points with second-order polynomial fitting) in MCRC software. These preliminary steps are crucial for obtaining reliable resolution outcomes and can directly impact subsequent data processing procedures. The pre-processed data sets were then analyzed using multivariate curve resolution-alternating least squares techniques via the MCR-ALS toolbox, a user-friendly graphical interface that operates within the MATLAB environment (Jaumot et al., 2015). Next, the number of components in each data matrix was estimated based on morphological score. Due to noise accumulation in GC–MS data, relying on a single method often yields unreliable results. Therefore, subspace comparison techniques, such as OPA, SIMPLISMA, and PCA were also employed to determine the chemical rank. These estimates were further validated using the local rank map generated from EFA and FSMW–EFA, which provides local information on the elution patterns of constituents within each region. The initial estimations of the pure spectra (S initial) obtained from SIMPLISMA and OPA, along with the concentration profiles extracted via the EFA method, were used to initiate the ALS algorithm and iteratively solve Eq. (2). MCR-ALS was performed with a maximum of 500 iterations, applying various constraints such as non-negativity (in both C and ST) using the fnnls algorithm, unimodality (in C), and normalization of spectra (to unit height) for all peak clusters during ALS optimization to improve the retrieval of meaningful solutions. After the ALS algorithm converged, the quality and reliability of the MCR-ALS results were assessed using metrics such as LOF and R2. Each constituent was subsequently identified by matching the resolved spectral profiles with stored mass spectra in the NIST mass database using MF or RMF. Further confirmation was achieved by comparing their indices.

To demonstrate the effectiveness of incorporating MCR-ALS in enhancing compound identification accuracy during data analysis, two clusters, A from the LT chromatogram and B from the RT chromatogram, were selected as case studies. The local TICs of peak clusters A (an embedded peak cluster) and B (a highly overlapped peak cluster) are shown in panels a and d of Fig. 3. Panels c and f of Fig. 3 illustrate the MCR-ALS resolved elution profiles for chromatographic segments A and B, respectively, with only a single compound identified through direct matching. As shown in Fig. 3c, the green chromatographic profile entirely masks the blue one, resulting in significant spectral overlap. The optimal two-component MCR-ALS model achieved an R2 of 99.40 %, with LOF values (for PCA and exp) of 5.27 % and 7.68 %, respectively. These results were considered satisfactory given the noise level in the region. The peak cluster A contains components of 3-methyltetradecane and trans-β-ionone, though the first constituent did not yield a direct match in the NIST MS library. It is worth noting that the peak areas of the original constituents decreased after preprocessing and resolution with MCR-ALS, as baseline and noise were removed, resulting in purer chromatographic profiles. Similarly, Fig. 3e shows a TIC curve for peak cluster B, where only one constituent, β-Phellandrene, could be directly matched using the NIST MS library. However, the mass spectra of different segments within peak cluster B suggest the presence of additional constituents or significant noise interference. After MCR-ALS analysis, the results for this peak cluster provided more thorough insights than previous findings. The optimized MCR-ALS model yielded LOF percentages of 0.73 (for PCA) and 3.17 (for exp), with an R2 value of 99.90 % for peak cluster B. A comparison of the resolved and standard mass spectra, along with retention index analysis, confirmed the method's reliability and revealed two new constituents in region B: β-terpinyl acetate and 1,8-cineole, in addition to the previously identified compound. It is important to note that the identified components exhibited appropriate RMF values (see Table 1).

A similar procedure using MCR-ALS was applied to obtain pure chromatographic and spectral profiles of each chemical component in other chromatographic segments, which are subsets of the total ion chromatogram of tea samples. The MCR-ALS models exhibit R2 values exceeding 96 % and LOF (exp) values below 12.0 %, indicating acceptable results considering the noise level and complexity of the fingerprint data. Additionally, RMF values range from 711 to 973 across all peak clusters. In cases of very low noise, LOF provides greater discrimination between comparable models when the fit is strong. On the other hand, R2 is favored in more complex data fields where data fitting may be less effective due to higher unexplained variances. It is essential to evaluate both LOF and R2 in the context of expected experimental noise. Notably, RMF is preferred for complex systems with significant background noise and non-selective separation, such as co-elution issues in GC columns. Unlike MF's peak-by-peak matching approach, RMF focuses solely on matching the abundance values of the resolved mass spectra with each reference spectrum (Azimi & Fatemi, 2016).

Semi-quantitative and comparative analysis by MCR-ALS

The overall volume integration (OVI) technique is employed to calculate the quantity of each component after resolving the two-dimensional GC–MS data into pure chromatograms and mass spectra for each component. The total two-way response of each component is determined by calculating the outer product of its resolved elution and spectrum vectors. Therefore, the total relative content of each component is directly proportional to the overall volume of its two-way response. This technique offers an advantage over general peak-area integration by considering all mass spectral intensities, leading to more accurate results and avoiding the peak splitting issue associated with traditional peak-area integration (Azimi & Fatemi, 2016; Jalali-Heravi & Parastar, 2011) It is important to note that the results reported in this study represent the relative concentrations of each constituent within the overall TIC, rather than absolute values. Accurate quantitative measurements require actual standards for all constituents. In this context, percentages are determined after the internal normalization of all the resolved peak areas. Detailed information on the resolved peaks with concentrations exceeding 0.1 % is presented in Table 1. Chemometric methods were used to determine the relative concentrations of the constituents, which accounted for 99.22 %, 97.93 %, 99.12 % and 99.14 % of the total relative content in ST, AT, LT and RT tea samples, respectively.

There were significant differences in the relative amounts of volatile compounds in black teas from different geographical areas. Notably, the most abundant volatile components varied across these samples. For example, ST tea showed high concentrations of diisobutyl phthalate (8.185 %), trans-β-ionone (7.693 %), cis-geraniol (4.738 %), linalool (4.500 %), trans-linalool oxide (furanoid) (4.313 %) and tridecane (2.876 %). In AT tea, the highest concentrations were of linalool (5.514 %), followed by dodecane (5.452 %), trans-linalool oxide (furanoid) (4.453 %), tridecane (4.480 %), trans-β-ionone (3.950 %), and phytol (2.513 %). LT tea was dominated by hexanedioic acid, bis(2-ethylhexyl) ester (12.350 %), tridecane (7.943 %), butylated hydroxytoluene (5.570 %), 1,2-epoxylinalool (4.681 %), α-farnesene (4.164 %) and linalool (4.130 %). In RT tea, linalyl anthanilate (10.352 %) had the highest intensity, followed by α-terpineol acetate (7.258 %), β-phellandrene (5.455 %), geranyl vinyl ether (5.169 %), linalool (4.754 %) and diethyl phthalate (4.346 %). Notably, linalool was a common component with high relative abundance across all tea samples. The peak intensities of these key compounds varied across the four black tea samples, contributing to the unique flavors of each tea. Tea aroma results from the combined composition and concentration of volatile organic compounds. The variation in flavor characteristics, including both aroma and taste, arises from several factors, such as cultivar, growing conditions, geographical origins and manufacturing process (Qin et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022). Direct comparisons of teas with the same genotype and processing methods, but from different ecological environments and geographical origins, provide valuable insights into their similarities and differences. It can be concluded that the geographical location of cultivation significantly affects the composition of volatiles in different teas, influencing the variations in quality among the tea samples.

The volatile profile of tea is composed of a complex mixture of numerous chemical compounds in trace amounts (Xu et al., 2023). The VOCs identified through GC–MS-MCR-ALS can be classified into distinct chemical groups based on their functional groups, with hydrocarbons being the most predominant, accounting for 35–45 %, followed by esters 13–30 % and alcohols 12–22 %. Other chemical categories, including ketones, aldehydes, ethers, phenols, furans, nitrogen compounds, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, acids, pyrazoles, and pyrazines, were also present, ranging between 0.10 % and 12 % (Fig. S5 in the supplementary data).

In this study, various hydrocarbons (alkenes, alkanes, cycloalkanes, monoterpenes, and sesquiterpenes) were identified, making up a significant proportion of the total volatile compounds across all four tea samples. Specifically, 57, 71, 54, and 44 different hydrocarbons were found in the ST, AT, LT, and RT tea samples, respectively. These compounds accounted for 35.04 %, 45.29 %, 38.29 %, and 39.77 % of the total volatile content in each tea sample, based on the overall TIC. While saturated hydrocarbons typically contribute minimally to tea flavor, unsaturated hydrocarbons are crucial for imparting aroma. The hydrocarbons present in relatively high concentrations were tridecane, β-phellandrene, dodecane, and α-farnesene, with reported aroma thresholds of 42 μg/L, 36 μg/L, 10,000 μg/L, and 87 μg/L, respectively. These compounds have also been previously reported in oolong tea (Guo et al., 2021). Tridecane, dodecane, and α-farnesene were detected in all samples, while β-phellandrene was found in LT and RT. Notably, α-farnesene is a common volatile released by tea plants under attack (Guo et al., 2021).

Regarding ester compounds, a particularly high relative concentration of hexanedioic acid, bis(2-ethylhexyl) ester was detected exclusively in LT at 12.350 %. This compound was previously reported in dark tea (Deng et al., 2024). Meanwhile, linalyl anthranilate, diisobutyl phthalate and α-terpineol acetate were found in relatively lower concentrations in RT, ST and RT, respectively. Studies have shown that linalyl anthranilate and α-terpineol acetate are also present in sweet tea, a multifunctional plant used for medicinal purposes, tea, and as a sugar substitute (Huang et al., 2022). Over the past 20 years, the detection of phthalate esters (PAEs) in tea has become increasingly common, with a 100 % detection rate at all stages of tea production. The most frequently detected PAEs in tea include diisobutyl phthalate (DiBP), di-n-butyl phthalate (DBP), diethyl phthalate (DEP), di-(2- ethyl) hexyl phthalate (DEHP), and dimethyl phthalate (DMP) (Tang, Wang, Pan, Mi, & Han, 2022).

Alcohols found in relatively high concentrations include linalool, trans-linalool oxide (furanoid), cis-geraniol, phytol, and trans-nerolidol. Linalool was the predominant alcohol in all black tea samples, accounting 4.130 % of VOCs in LT and occurring in even higher concentrations in ST (4.500 %), RT (4.754 %) and AT (5.514 %). Globally renowned black teas have documented that 19 specific compounds, including linalool and trans-linalool oxide (furanoid), play significant roles as key volatile components (Wang et al., 2021). Linalool and geraniol were identified as the key flavor constituents in Keemun black tea, while phytol and trans-nerolidol were present as minor components (Xu et al., 2023).

All tea samples contained trans-β-ionone, α-ionone, and β-ionon-5,6-epoxide in relatively high concentrations. Specifically, trans-β-ionone was most abundant in ST (7.693 %) and least in LT (2.841 %). β-Ionone, a key aromatic compound found in various teas, has been identified as a promising candidate for chemopreventive and chemotherapeutic applications against cancer, based on in vitro and in vivo studies (Ansari & Emami, 2016). α-Ionone and β-ionon-5,6-epoxide are important volatile compounds in black and white tea, respectively (Hao et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2023).

Aldehydes, produced through the thermal Strecker oxidative degradation of amino acids and fatty acids, significantly contribute to tea aroma due to their relatively low odor thresholds (Yang et al., 2018). Among the aldehydes, nonanal, hexanal, and β-cyclocitral were found in relatively high concentrations. Nonanal and β-cyclocitral were detected in all tea samples, while hexanal was absent in AT. These compounds were previously identified as key aroma contributors in Echa 10 Lichuan black tea (Qin et al., 2023). Regarding ether compounds, geranyl vinyl ether was the most prevalent, accounting for 5.169 % of the total volatile constituents found exclusively in RT. Studies have shown that increasing the fermentation temperature in black tea can significantly elevate the levels of geranyl vinyl ether (Qu et al., 2020).

In terms of phenol compounds, butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) was present in all samples at relatively high concentrations, with a maximum of 5.570 % in LT and a minimum of 1.321 % in RT. BHT is a non-staining hindered phenol antioxidant that can be either synthetic or naturally occurring, such as in sun-dried black tea. Hindered phenols are widely used, particularly as inhibitors of free radical chain reactions (Yehye et al., 2015). Furan and nitrogen compounds were detected at minimal levels, constituting only 1.55 % and 0.70 % of all volatile components, respectively. The predominant furans across all tea samples were 2-pentyl furan and 5,6,7,7a-tetrahydro-4,4,7a-trimethyl-2(4H)-benzofuranone. The former has been detected in fresh tea leaves, black tea, and green tea, while the latter was previously reported in Phoenix Dancong tea (Xu et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024). Caffeine was the main nitrogen-containing compound, which, while influencing the taste of tea, contributes minimally to its aroma (Yang et al., 2018).

Acid, pyrazole, pyrazine and PAH compounds were exclusively identified through the resolution method. Trans-13-Octadecenoic acid was the only acid detected, previously reported in Camellia sinensis (Gupta & Kumar, 2017). However, the absence of acids in the samples may be due to most acids being present in ester form or having been transformed into aldehydes, alcohols, hydrocarbons, or other secondary metabolites. It is well-established that branched-chain acids detected in tea samples typically originate from the breakdown of branched-chain amino acids, while straight-chain acids arise from the degradation of fatty acids (Kraujalyt\.e, Pelvan and Alasalvar, 2016). Pyrazines, such as 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethylpyrazine, are known to form through the Maillard reaction via Strecker degradation, as documented in green tea (Yang et al., 2018). A pyrazole-type compound, 4,5-dihydro-5,5-dimethyl-4-isopropylidene-1H-pyrazole, was identified in AT and LT samples and has been previously reported in oolong tea (Wang et al., 2023). The main polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon identified was 1,1,6-trimethyl-1,2-dihydronaphthalene, with a water threshold of 2.5 μg/L, and it has also been reported in Wuyi rock oolong tea. PAHs are present in the fresh leaves and roots of tea plants due to environmental absorption and accumulation, and they are also formed during the tea production process (Liu et al., 2021).

Furthermore, Table 1 reveals that the components with the highest relative concentrations were hexanedioic acid, bis(2-ethylhexyl) ester, linalyl anthanilate, diisobutyl phthalate, tridecane, trans-β-ionone, α-terpineol acetate, butylated hydroxytoluene, linalool, β-phellandrene and dodecane. Consequently, these compounds could significantly influence the flavor and aroma profiles of Iranian black teas. The distribution of these compounds in each tea sample is depicted in Fig. 7. After employing the MCR-ALS method to address chromatographic artifacts and separate the TIC curve into pure chromatographic and mass spectral profiles, and using overall volume integration, more precise and reliable results were achieved. A comparison of the relative concentrations of major constituents before and after applying chemometric techniques (see Table S1 in the supplementary data) revealed that MCR-ALS reduced the original peak areas of certain constituents. This reduction is attributed to the removal of baseline noise and the elimination of overlapping areas, resulting in pure chromatographic profiles. Of the 256 components identified with concentrations above 0.1 % as volatile chemical constituents in the analyzed black tea samples, 202 were identified using MCR-ALS. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the MCR-ALS technique in analyzing complex samples, including the accurate identification of minor constituents and overlapped peaks, which are difficult to identify and quantify using GC–MS alone.

Conclusions

In this study, a combination of the HS-SPME-GC–MS method and chemometric resolution techniques was employed to comprehensively identify and characterize the volatile components of black teas produced in different regions of northern Iran. First, HS-SPME extraction followed by GC–MS analysis was used to separate, qualitatively, and quantitatively determine the volatile constituents of four black tea samples. Based on our findings, 54 volatile compounds were tentatively identified using fingerprint analysis technology. Following preprocessing, complex GC–MS data were analyzed using the MCR-ALS technique, generating pure chromatographic, spectral, and concentration profiles for each chemical. Despite challenges such as baseline drifts and noisy data, this combined strategy proved efficient and robust. Ultimately, 256 components were identified, with 202 resolved using the MCR-ALS method.

The results revealed significant variation in the levels of volatile compounds among tea samples from different regions. Hydrocarbons represented the most substantial proportion among the various chemical groups in different black teas. The major compounds included hexanedioic acid, bis(2-ethylhexyl) ester, linalyl anthranilate, diisobutyl phthalate, tridecane, β-ionone, and α-Terpineol acetate. This study demonstrates the effectiveness of chemometrics as a crucial and precise approach for chemical characterization and comprehensive analysis of complex samples, providing more reliable results. To our knowledge, this study is pioneering in employing a chemometric resolution techniques to unveil the chemical composition of volatile components in Iranian black teas. These findings could serve as a theoretical guide for future research endeavors focusing on chemical screening and quality control in black tea production.
