Prevalence and risk factors for high-risk human papillomavirus infection in patients with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases: a prospective study
Authors: Tiphaine Goulenok, Charlotte Sierra, Aline Marie Florence, Clementine Gonthier, Margot Bucau, Thomas Papo, Arthur Mageau, Valentine Marie Ferré, Karim Sacre, for the APOSY study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective Women with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMID) are at increased risk of cervical cancer. We aimed to determine the prevalence of carcinogenic high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection and identify factors associated with infection in women with IMID.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, single-centre study at a French university hospital. We assessed compliance with cervical cancer screening (CCS) recommendations among all patients aged 30–65 years who attended follow-up visits between March 2024 and May 2025. HPV screening with vaginal self-sampling (VSS) was offered to all women not up to date with CCS. The primary outcome was HR-HPV infection prevalence.ResultsAmong the 485 patients eligible for CCS, 296 were not up to date with CCS and were therefore eligible for VSS. Of these, 276 (mean age: 48±10 years) consented to participate and were included in the study. HR-HPV was detected in 55 patients (20%, n=55/276), among whom 35% (n=19/55) harboured multiple HR-HPV infections (≥2 types). In multivariable analysis, age at first sexual intercourse (OR (95% CI) 0.85 (0.73 to 0.96)) and number of lifetime sexual partners (OR 1.19 (1.05 to 1.38)) were associated with HR-HPV infection. In exploratory multivariable analyses, longer duration of biological therapy was associated with multiple HR-HPV infections (OR 1.57 (0.99 to 2.85)).ConclusionsThe prevalence of HR-HPV infection in patients with IMID who are not up to date with CCS is 20%. Behavioural factors were associated with HR-HPV infection, while treatment-related findings regarding multiple HR-HPV infections were exploratory and warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies.Trial registration numberNCT06302725.Keywords: Autoimmune Diseases; Communicable Diseases, Imported; Connective Tissue Diseases; Epidemiology; Devices

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted infection worldwide.1 Up to 90% of individuals are exposed to HPV during their lifetime.2 HPV is responsible for almost all cervical cancers, accounting for nearly 630 000 new cases each year.3 Cervical cancer develops through a multistep process beginning with HPV infection, followed by viral persistence, precancerous lesions and invasive disease.4

Women with immune-mediated inflammatory diseases (IMID) are at increased risk of cervical neoplasia.5 The mechanisms underlying the increased susceptibility of patients with IMID to high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection are unclear but appear to include immune dysregulation, impaired viral clearance and exposure to immunosuppressive (IS) therapies.5 In systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), HPV prevalence is higher than in the general population, around 25%,5,9 with frequent multiple-type infections.10,12 A retrospective cohort study conducted in Australia compared cervical abnormalities in patients with various IMID with those in a control group who were free of IMID. The results showed that patients with SLE or mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) were more likely to have high-grade cervical abnormalities than the control group.13 A Chinese cohort study evaluating the risk of cancer in patients with five IMID (including SLE, rheumatoid arthritis (RA), systemic sclerosis (SSc), Sjögren’s syndrome (SS) and idiopathic inflammatory myositis) found an increased risk of cervical and vulvar cancer.14 A study of nearly 500 000 patients in the UK revealed that those with various IMID (80 types) were almost twice as likely to develop HPV-associated diseases as the control group.15 A cohort study investigating the risk of HPV-induced cancer and precancerous conditions at multiple sites in patients with SLE revealed a higher risk of cervical, vulvar, vaginal and anal precancerous conditions, as well as oropharyngeal cancer, compared with the general population.16 In a recent nationwide matched cohort study involving over 100 000 patients with IMID with SLE, RA, SSc, SS and MCTD, the risk of HPV-induced cancer or severe dysplasia of the cervix, vagina, vulva, anus or penis was almost twice as high in the IMID population as in the control group. This excess risk was found to be more than three times higher in patients with SLE.17

Despite national and international recommendations, current cervical cancer screening (CCS) rates among patients with IMID are low.518,20 Our group reported that vaginal self-sampling (VSS) could improve the screening of HPV-HR infection in patients with IMID.21 Our aim was to assess the prevalence of HR-HPV infection and its associated risk factors in women with IMID who were not up to date with their CCS and for whom VSS was proposed.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

We conducted a single-centre, prospective observational study (APOSY study, ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06302725) at the Department of Internal Medicine, Bichat-Claude Bernard Hospital, AP-HP, Paris, France. This hospital belongs to a reference centre network for rare autoimmune diseases in France. As previously described,21 from March 2024 to May 2025, all consecutive patients with IMID between 30 and 65 years of age who attended a scheduled visit in our department were asked whether CCS was up to date according to the French national guidelines.22 Patients for whom CCS was not indicated (due to factors such as a hysterectomy or a current pregnancy) and those who were unable or unwilling to provide consent were excluded. Otherwise, all women who were not up to date with CCS were offered HPV screening with VSS. To minimise participation bias, all eligible patients were systematically approached by trained investigators and received standardised verbal and written information regarding the study and VSS procedure. According to French23 24 and international25 recommendations, a second VSS was proposed at an interval of 1 year in patients with positive HR-HPV testing at baseline. The APOSY study primarily aimed to evaluate VSS as a strategy to improve screening uptake in a population at increased risk of HPV-related disease. Women already compliant with screening recommendations were therefore not offered additional HPV testing within the study protocol.

Data collected

Demographics, HPV vaccination status, IMID features, treatment exposure, behaviours and comorbidities were systematically collected. Behavioural variables included age at first sexual intercourse and number of lifetime sexual partners, both of which were self-reported during the standardised data collection process at the time of enrolment. Data on the frequency of abnormal cervical cytology (ie, cervical smears showing atypical squamous cells (ASC), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL) or high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions) and histology findings were collected from HR-HPV-positive patients who underwent cervical smear testing and colposcopy after VSS. The trial investigators used a secure Web-based electronic data-capture tool (Research Electronic Data Capture) to record trial data.26

Sample processing and virological analysis

VSSs were collected using a FloqSwab and discharged into 5 mL of MSwab medium (Copan, Brescia, Italy). Nucleic acids were extracted using the STARMag96×4 Universal Cartridge Kit (Seegene, Seoul, Korea) on the Microlab NIMBUS (Seegene). HPV typing was performed using the Anyplex II HPV HR Detection kit (Seegene), which detects 14 HR-HPV types: 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the prevalence of HR-HPV infection. The secondary outcomes were (i) the frequency of multiple HR-HPV infection (>2 HR-HPV in the same sample) and (ii) HR-HPV infection persistence in two consecutive positive VSS 12 months apart.

Statistical analysis

As this was an exploratory prospective observational study primarily designed to estimate HR-HPV prevalence in a real-life hospital-based cohort, no formal sample size calculation was performed a priori. Descriptive statistics for the overall population and according to HPV status were presented as follows: mean and SD for quantitative variables; number and percentage for qualitative variables, adjusted for missing values where appropriate. For the primary analysis, factors suspected of being associated with positive HR-HPV status were studied with univariable logistic regression, including clinically relevant variables and known risk factors. For a given covariate, if the p value associated with the OR and its 95% CI was <0.20, the covariate was selected for inclusion in the multivariable analysis. The results of the final multivariable logistic regression were reported with adjusted OR (aOR), 95% CI and p value. The same approach was used for the secondary analysis to study factors suspected of being associated with multiple HR-HPV infection status. For a given covariate, if the p value associated with the OR and its 95% CI was <0.1, the covariate was considered for multivariable analysis. Different thresholds were used for the primary outcome (p<0.20) and the secondary outcome (p<0.10). The secondary analysis of multiple HR-HPV infections involved a substantially smaller number of events so the multivariable model was intentionally restricted according to the principle of parsimony. Variables with >30% missing data were excluded from multivariable analyses to limit instability and potential bias. Remaining analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. Statistical analysis was performed using R software (V.4.2.1).

Patient and public involvement statement

Patient or the public were not involved in the design or the conduction of the study.

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 514 women with IMID were seen during the study period. Of these, 485 were eligible for CCS. According to the current guidelines, however, 296 (61%) were not up to date with their screening. Of these, 276 (93%) gave consent for VSS and were included in the study (figure 1). The mean age was 48 (±10) years. Most patients (74%, n=204/276) had autoimmune connective tissue disorders (table 1) including SLE (30%, n=83/276), SS (22%, n=60/276), antiphospholipid syndrome (6.5%, n=18/276), MCTD (5.4%, n=15/276), immune-mediated myositis (4.7%, n=13/276), autoimmune cytopenia (3.3%, n=9/276) and SSc (2.2%, n=6/276) (online supplemental figure S1). The mean disease duration was 9 (±8) years. Steroids, IS drugs and biologics had been used by 58% (n=159/276), 36% (n=99/276) and 19% (n=53/276) of patients, respectively, with mean durations of use of 6.0 (±7.0), 5.3 (±4.4) and 3.0 (±3.4) years (online supplemental figure S2). Thirty-two patients (20%; n=32/159) had received steroids and IS drugs and biologics. At the time of VSS, 32% (n=87/276) of patients were still taking corticosteroids, 18% (n=49/276) were taking IS drugs and 10% (n=28/276) were taking biologics.

HR-HPV prevalence

Of the 276 patients who performed VSS, 20% (n=55/276 patients) tested positive for at least one HR-HPV type. Prevalence of HR-HPV decreased with age: from 29% (n=19/66) in women aged 30–40 years to 12% (n=10/83) in women aged 51–60 years. The highest prevalence of HR-HPV was found among patients with SSc (50%, n=3/6), immune-mediated myositis (31%, n=4/13), SLE (25%, n=21/83), sarcoidosis (25%, n=1/4) and MCTD (20%, n=3/15) (online supplemental figure S3).

Almost 90% (n=49/55) of the infections were caused by five HR-HPV types: 68, 51, 52, 58, 33 (figure 2). Of note, three types (HR-HPV 58, 52 and 33), which accounted for 45% (n=25/55) of cases, are covered by the 9-valent HPV vaccine (figure 2). Overall, 40% (n=22/55) of the HR-HPV-positive women harboured at least one HR-HPV included in the 9-valent HPV vaccine.

Of the patients who tested positive for HR-HPV (n=55), 65% (n=36/55) underwent cervical smear testing after VSS. Abnormal cervical cytology was observed in 31% (n=11/36) of HR-HPV-positive patients. This included ASCUS (n=6/11), LSIL (n=3/11), ASC-H (n=1/11) and AGC (n=1/11). Colposcopy and biopsy showed CIN1 (n=5/11), CIN2 (n=1/11) and a glandular precancerous lesion (n=1/11). One patient had HPV-associated vulvar cancer.

Univariable analyses (table 2) showed that age (OR (95% CI) 1.0 (0.99 to 1.0)), high-level education (OR 1.12 (1.02 to 1.24)), autoimmune connective tissue disorders (OR 1.18 (1.01 to 1.38)), sarcoidosis (OR 1.20 (0.96 to 1.50)) (with vasculitis as reference); combination of steroids, IS drugs and biologics (OR 1.22 (1.05 to 1.41)), number of lifetime sexual partners (OR 1.04 (1.02 to 1.05)) and age at first sexual activity (OR 0.95 (0.97 to 0.99)) were associated with HR-HPV infection. Multivariable logistic regression analysis involving those covariates showed that only the number of lifetime sexual partners (OR 1.19 (1.05 to 1.38)) and the age at first sexual intercourse (OR 0.85 (0.73 to 0.96)) were associated with HR-HPV infection (table 2).

Multiple HR-HPV infection

Of the 55 patients who tested positive for at least one HR-HPV, 19 (35%; n=19/55) had multiple HR-HPV infections. Overall, 12 patients (63%, n=12/19) had two different HR-HPV types, five patients (26%, n=5/19) had three HR-HPV types and two patients (11%, n=2/19) had more than three HR-HPV types. The most prevalent types of HR-HPV involved in multiple infections were 68, 52, 58 and 33 (figure 2). Multivariable logistic regression analysis, adjusted for two covariates, showed that duration of biologics (OR 1.57 (95% CI 0.99 to 2.85), p=0.08) was independently associated with multiple HR-HPV infections (table 3). An exploratory analysis suggested an association between HR-HPV infection and belimumab exposure (OR 1.45 (95% CI 1.11 to 1.88); online supplemental table S1).

Persistent HR-HPV infection

Of the HR-HPV-positive patients at the initial VSS, 30 (55%; n=30/55) underwent a second VSS, on average 12.2 (0.98) months later. Of these patients, 23 (77%, n=23/30) had persistent HR-HPV infection. Twelve HPV genotypes were involved in the persistent infections, including five covered by the 9-valent HPV vaccine (figure 2). Univariable analysis revealed that both the duration of biologics and the presence of multiple HR-HPV infections at the initial VSS were associated with persistent infection (table 4).

Discussion

Our prospective study aimed to determine the prevalence of HR-HPV infection in women treated for IMID who were not up to date with CCS. VSS was used as a screening tool in the hospital setting to improve screening for high-risk patients (figure 3).21 HR-HPV prevalence was 20%, which is higher than the estimated 10%–14% reported in unselected adult women in France28 and comparable to that reported in other immunocompromised populations, such as solid organ transplant recipients and people living with HIV.10 29 30 These results are consistent with prior studies in IMID populations,5 although prevalence estimates in the literature remain highly variable (3%–80%), depending on virological assays, geographic settings and IMID subtypes. Differences in HPV detection methods may partly explain discrepancies in prevalence estimates and genotype distribution, as studies variably relied on cytology-based screening, HPV DNA testing or high-risk HPV genotyping panels with different sensitivities. In addition, geographic variability likely contributed to the observed differences, since the prevalence and distribution of HPV genotypes are known to vary along a north-south gradient.3

Among the IMID subgroups, HPV prevalence was highest in patients with SLE and MCTD, consistent with our previous work21 and other published data.11 31 32 The results for sarcoidosis and SSc should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample sizes. The distribution of HR-HPV genotypes (primarily HPV 33, 51, 52, 58 and 68) in patients with IMID differed from the general French screening population,33 with a notable excess of HPV 51 and HPV 68. Similar shifts have been documented in kidney transplant recipients,34 suggesting that immunosuppression may promote the persistence of HR-HPV genotypes that are generally eradicated in immunocompetent individuals. This could have potential implications for future vaccination strategies, as both types are not covered by current nonavalent vaccines.

HPV vaccination rates were strikingly low in our cohort (4%), despite the nonavalent Gardasil vaccine covering approximately 45% of the HR-HPV types detected. This finding is consistent with previous reports identifying low perceived risk and insufficient physician recommendation as the main barriers to vaccination in patients with IMID.35 Several health authorities, including the French HAS36 and the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR),27 now support catch-up vaccination up to the age of 26 years, and potentially beyond, in immunocompromised populations. These strategies could become a public health cornerstone.

In our cohort, independent predictors of HR-HPV infection were exclusively behavioural (younger age at first sexual intercourse and higher number of lifetime sexual partners) rather than disease-specific or treatment-related variables. These findings are consistent with the established biology of HR-HPV acquisition, which is primarily driven by sexual exposure.1 2 HPV acquisition appears to be governed by the same behavioural determinants as in the general population, whereas immune dysregulation appears to affect subsequent events such as viral persistence and multiple infection, rather than initial acquisition itself.

As previously reported in SLE9,11 and SSc,37 the proportion of multiple HR-HPV infections was high (35%) in IMID. In immunocompetent individuals, immune response typically eradicates HR-HPV infection within 12 months, diminishing consequently the risk for multiple infections.38 Thus, multiple infection likely reflects inadequate viral clearance,39 knowing that viral persistence is the main risk factor for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia or cancer recurrence.40 In a similar way, the persistence of HR-HPV infection at 1 year (75%) was higher than reported in previous IMID cohorts.10 12 An exploratory association was observed between cumulative exposure to IS therapies, particularly biologics, and multiple HR-HPV infections; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of events and requires confirmation in larger studies. Biologics have been particularly implicated, especially belimumab, suggesting that targeting B-cell may impair viral clearance, as reported for other viral infections.41 Several longitudinal studies have associated persistent HPV infection with prolonged corticosteroid exposure42 and the use of IS agents such as azathioprine and cyclophosphamide.11 43 In our study, the findings relating to treatment were exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and should not be used to inform decisions about modifying IS regimens.

Cervical cytological abnormalities were common in our cohort, consistent with previous studies.21 44 45 A recent large-scale French study17 shows that IMID (SLE, Sjögren’s disease, SSc, MCTD and RA) are associated with an increased risk of HPV-induced cancers or high-grade dysplasia at cervical, vaginal, vulvar and anal sites. These findings underscore the need for intensified surveillance and annual screening in HPV-positive patients with IMID.18 VSS with its high level of acceptability and tolerance compared with cervical smears performed by clinicians35 could help achieve this objective, particularly in hard-to-reach or immunocompromised populations.

This study has some limitations. First, the number of patients included is small, and recruitment was monocentric. Second, missing data for some behavioural variables reduced the effective sample size of the multivariable analysis, potentially limiting statistical power and introducing residual selection bias. In addition, other important confounding factors, such as those relating to socio-economic status beyond education and HDI, were not addressed. Furthermore, as the behavioural variables were self-reported, they were potentially subject to recall and reporting bias. Although data collection was standardised using structured questionnaires administered during inclusion visits, no validated behavioural instruments were used. Third, given the relatively small number of events, particularly for multiple HR-HPV infections, the regression analyses should be interpreted cautiously and may be underpowered to detect modest associations. Due to the borderline statistical significance, limited number of multiple HR-HPV infection events, and restricted multivariable model, the association between biological therapy and multiple HR-HPV infection should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, patients receiving biologics may experience more severe symptoms, have greater cumulative exposure to IS agents and exhibit altered immune function. These factors may independently contribute to impaired viral clearance, regardless of the effects of biologic therapy itself. Moreover, the analysis of persistent HR-HPV infection was exploratory and based on a small, highly selected subgroup of patients who underwent repeat testing after 1 year. Fourth, according to the study design, no comparative virological data were available for women who were already adhering to screening recommendations. Therefore, the reported prevalence may not accurately reflect the true prevalence of HR-HPV infection in the general IMID population, as the study specifically included women who were not up to date with CCS. These women may represent a higher-risk subgroup. Furthermore, women who do not adhere to screening recommendations may exhibit different behavioural and socio-economic characteristics that influence HPV exposure. This potential selection bias limits the generalisability of the results. Finally, this study was conducted at a tertiary referral centre for rare autoimmune diseases. The patient population—which was largely composed of patients with SLE and SS—may not be representative of the wider IMID population. To our knowledge, our study is however the first to assess prospectively HPV prevalence across several IMID and to link behavioural and treatment factors to HPV outcomes.

In conclusion, VSS stands as a safe and reliable tool with a high degree of acceptability and represents a promising strategy to improve screening accessibility and adherence. Given the need for repeated long-term surveillance in patients with IMID receiving IS therapies, as recommended in EULAR guidelines,46 47 VSS may constitute an effective and pragmatic complementary screening tool in routine care. The high-risk HPV infection rate observed in patients with IMID reflects a cumulative estimate of sexual exposure, whereas multiple HR-HPV infections, which confer a higher risk of cervical cancer, were related to IS therapy. The 2019 EULAR recommendations for vaccination in adult patients with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases48 strongly support HPV vaccination in patients with IMIDs, particularly in SLE populations. Our findings further support the importance of implementing these recommendations earlier and more systematically in clinical practice, especially in patients exposed to prolonged corticosteroid or IS treatment. Collectively, these data underline the need for multidisciplinary preventive strategies integrating rheumatologists, gynaecologists, primary care physicians and public health programmes to optimise HPV vaccination coverage and cervical cancer prevention in high-risk IMID populations.
