Multi-bubble dynamics considering the direction of bubble–bubble interactions under acoustic field excitation
Authors: Yu Zhang, Yu Wu, Jin Zhang, Xiaobo Rui, Yong Chen, Xin Peng, Lei Qi, Hexin Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the influence of bubble–bubble interactions on the vibrational dynamics of multi-bubble systems in cavitation fields, a multi-bubble dynamics model was established based on the Keller-Miksis equation. The interaction term was modified to better reflect the spatial inhomogeneity of the interactions. Numerical simulations of cavitation bubbles were conducted using this theoretical model. The study analyzed the effects of initial bubble radius, external acoustic excitation, inter-bubble distance, relative position, and bubble number on bubble–bubble interactions, radial pulsations, and frequency variations. A 2D Fluent simulation model was developed to observe the cavitation process under acoustic excitation in a three-bubble system. The reliability of the theoretical model was validated by comparing changes in bubble radius and frequency. Results indicated that when the acoustic excitation amplitude significantly exceeded the interaction pressure amplitude, bubble–bubble interactions positively correlated with the amplitude variations of the high-frequency vibration component. When the acoustic excitation amplitude was comparable to the interaction pressure, the strength of bubble–bubble interactions influenced the relative radial vibration amplitude and resonant frequency. Factors like initial bubble radius, acoustic excitation, bubble distance, relative position, and bubble number influence radial vibrations. Due to coupling interactions, the resonant frequency of bubbles in a cluster shifts to lower values. The Fluent simulations confirmed that the theoretical model accurately captured the bubble vibration processes and characteristics.Keywords: Multi-bubble system, Bubble–bubble interactions, Ultrasonic cavitation, Finite volume method simulation

Introduction

With the advancement of aerospace technology, bubble detection is becoming a key indicator for in-orbit inspection of spacecraft propulsion pipelines. In-orbit refueling, essential for extending satellite lifespans, requires avoiding bubble transmission, as gas–liquid mixing in fuel tanks can lead to mission failure. Gas-liquid two-phase flow caused by bubbles is common in propulsion systems, making accurate in-pipeline bubble detection crucial. Research on bubble vibrations under acoustic waves and improvements to fundamental theory can greatly enhance measurement performance, offering significant theoretical and engineering values for improving in-orbit inspection and extending spacecraft service life [1]. Researchers have extended foundational models—from Rayleigh's bubble dynamics theory to the Rayleigh-Plesset (R-P) equation—to advance the study of bubble physics. Subsequent theoretical work on single bubble has progressively refined the R-P equation by incorporating complexities such as acoustic radiation, liquid properties, and gas compressibility [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Herring [11] and Trilling [12] introduced liquid compressibility effects, while Kolodner [13] analyzed bubble-wall vibrations in two-phase flow under the acoustic fields. Keller and Miksis [14] synthesized these advances into the compressible Keller-Miksis (K-M) equation, applicable even to large-amplitude oscillations. Collectively, these developments established robust theoretical models for simulating single-bubble dynamics.

Acoustic field excitation is a primary method employed for studying bubble vibration dynamics and acoustic cavitation phenomena. Under the action of the acoustic field, the bubbles show regular or irregular oscillations [15], which have been studied by many scholars. Key advances are as follows. Bai et al. [16] observed cavitation structures (20–50 kHz) evolution; Kyuichi [17] linked ultrasonic frequency to multi-bubble luminescence; Tronson et al. [18,19] established frequency-dependent cavitation bubble lifetimes. Yun Zhao et al. [20] developed a surface-active bubble model with nonlinear tension to examine stability and rupture under low-frequency waves. Bouchet et al. [21] pioneered bubble-environment interaction studies using 3D-printed confined bubbles as acoustic probes for near-field microscopy. Environmental effects are also critical, as seen in cryogenic ultrasonic cavitation studies [22].

The aforementioned theoretical studies on single bubble vibration models and acoustic cavitation phenomena have laid a crucial foundation for subsequent bubble research. However, in practical liquid systems, bubbles rarely exist in isolation; they predominantly form bubble clusters. Within these clusters, the presence of bubble–bubble interactions introduces significant complexities to the system dynamics [[23], [24], [25]]. Consequently, the vibration characteristics of bubbles within a cluster exhibit marked distinctions from those of individual bubbles.

To better explain the complex dynamics within the multi-bubble system, researchers have initiated a more in-depth study of the bubble–bubble interactions, the vibrational characteristics of the double bubbles, and the dynamics of the multi-bubbles. The secondary Bjerknes force [26] has been studied in depth by many researchers. Li et al. [27] correlated sub-Bjerknes forces with bubble structure stability; Zabolotskaya and Ida [28,29] modeled linear two-bubble coupling to analyze phase-dependent interaction directionality; Vikash Pandey [30] derived higher-order nonlinear coupled K-M equations; Haiyan et al. [31] developed machine-learning models for precise Bjerknes force magnitude/sign prediction.

The two-bubble model is the simplest case to analyze the bubble–bubble interactions, which is important for predicting the interaction between bubbles and the trend of motion evolution. Zhang et al. established a coupling model of two bubbles and obtained the equations for the resonant frequency and resonant amplitude of the two bubbles to study the influence of the coupling effect between bubbles [32]. Qin et al. numerically analyzed the radial oscillation and translational motion of two bubbles of different sizes in a viscoelastic medium [33]. Shen [34] and other scholars investigated the effect of bubble–bubble interactions on the radial pulsation of bubbles in double bubbles by numerical simulation. Yusefi et al. [35] developed a finite element model to simulate the coupled vibrations of two individually phospholipid-encapsulated microbubbles in close proximity. Xiang et al. from our team [36] developed a compressible dual-bubble dynamics model based on the R-P equation, incorporating the secondary Bjerknes force. The model captures axial bubble interactions under acoustic excitation in pipelines and compares the dynamics of free versus dual bubbles.

In multi-bubble systems, acoustic excitation causes bubbles to oscillate, leading to periodic volume changes that compress the surrounding liquid and generate outward-propagating pressure disturbances. During expansion, bubbles push fluid outward, creating low-pressure regions; during contraction, fluid is drawn inward, forming high-pressure regions [3]. These alternating disturbances propagate as longitudinal waves, influencing neighboring bubbles. Traditionally, fluid dynamics treats pressure as a scalar, lacking directional information. As a result, the interaction term in the K-M equation is commonly interpreted as the spatial superposition of pressure disturbances from each oscillating bubble, reflecting pressure field coupling based on superposition principles [[37], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. Building on this framework, studies on multi-bubble systems have often started with simplified models of three or four bubbles to investigate coupling effects. Chen et al. [43] conducted theoretical and numerical studies on three-bubble systems, revealing that the modulation of the secondary Bjerknes force by nearby bubbles depends strongly on their relative sizes. Haghi et al. [44] analyzed bubble interactions in three- and four-bubble clusters, finding that the largest bubble dominates clustering dynamics and is minimally influenced by others. Jinfu Liang and Juanxiu Liu [45] derived new equations for pulsation and symmetric deformation of three aligned cavitation bubbles, offering insights into bubble–bubble interactions. Qin Yupeng [[46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]] systematically studied multi-bubble systems with regular geometric arrangements (e.g., spherical, circular, polygonal, or cubic), developed improved coupled R-P equations, and derived elliptical function solutions for synchronized dynamics, advancing the understanding of geometric dependence and predictability in multi-body interactions. Zhang et al. [53] proposed a unified bubble theory incorporating boundary effects, bubble–bubble interactions, ambient flow, gravity, compressibility, and other key factors, enabling more accurate predictions of bubble dynamics.

Since the last century, significant progress has been made in the study of large-scale bubble clusters. Takahira et al. [54] derived governing equations for interacting spherical bubbles considering liquid compressibility, exploring their collective nonlinear oscillations in acoustic fields. In the last decades, modeling and application of bubble clusters advanced further: Bremond et al. [55] observed non-spherical collapse patterns in 2D clusters using surface cavitation control. Ilinskii et al. [56] proposed models accounting for pulsation and translation of arbitrary numbers of bubbles, enabling stable simulations of large systems. Zeravcic et al. [57] introduced a modal response theory to analyze acoustic energy localization in polydisperse clouds. Fuster & Colonius [58] developed a multi-scale compressible model linking single-bubble dynamics to macroscopic cavitation, aiding deep-sea propeller protection. Brennen’s team [59] refined the theoretical framework of cavitation dynamics. Matthieu [60] analytically predicted subharmonic threshold shifts in monodisperse clouds due to bubble interactions. Sojahrood et al. [61] studied resonant frequency variations in acoustically driven, randomly distributed clusters. Maeda’s group [62,63] established data-driven models linking cavitation intensity to bubble cluster anisotropy, enabling precise spatial control in ultrasound ablation. The aforementioned studies on bubble clusters highlight the engineering value and practical significance of large-scale bubble systems.

Furthermore, some scholars have analyzed and studied the finite propagation speed of acoustic information and the associated time-delayed interactions in multi-bubble systems. Fan et al. [64] studied acoustically driven bubble screens using coupled R-P equations, and proposed a model with compressibility corrections and time delays. Coulombel et al. [65] developed a similar interaction model and validated it experimentally. These works underscore the limitations of traditional instantaneous coupling and the need for more refined models under high-frequency, dense, nonlinear, or non-uniform conditions. To focus on the research objectives and simplify calculations, this study concentrates on low-frequency (<1 MHz), short-range (bubble spacing D ≤ 5R0) microbubble systems, emphasizing the modulation mechanisms of bubble–bubble interactions on bubble vibration patterns. Under these conditions, the acoustic wavelength significantly exceeds the bubble spacing, and phase differences in acoustic pressure are negligible, satisfying the quasi-static approximation. Since our research primarily focuses on the measurement and early warning of low-concentration bubbles in pipelines within the aerospace application context, the early warning threshold for bubble concentration set in this background is relatively low. Referring to the three papers by Bouchet et al. [21], Yusefi & Helfield [35], and Coulombel [65], it has been theoretically and experimentally demonstrated that the time delay effect is negligible at lower concentrations but becomes significant at higher bubble concentrations. Additionally, in biomedical applications, the bubble concentration is far below that of “bubble screens,” making this effect unnecessary to consider. We believe that under our experimental and modeling conditions, the time delay effect can similarly be neglected. Therefore, the infinite sound speed assumption is adopted in constructing the bubble–bubble interaction model, neglecting time delays to better capture the core physical mechanisms of bubble interactions.

Current studies on multi-bubble dynamics commonly introduce a linearly superimposed pressure term into the K-M equation to describe bubble interactions. Traditional models are based on the assumption of isotropic interactions, considering only inter-bubble distance while ignoring directional effects in space. This makes it difficult to accurately describe complex near-field interaction mechanisms driven by pressure gradients. It also fails to fully represent the dynamic behavior of bubble clusters in spatially non-uniform acoustic fields. To address this key issue, this study aims to establish a theoretical model for multi-bubble interactions that incorporates directional corrections. While maintaining the spherical symmetry vibration assumption, the model proposes a theoretical method to convert vector mechanical effects into equivalent scalar numerical corrections. A cross-validation framework integrating theoretical modeling and Fluent finite volume method simulations has been developed to systematically evaluate the reliability of the modified model. Based on this theoretical model, the influence of key parameters such as acoustic field characteristics and bubble spacing on bubble radial oscillations has been thoroughly analyzed.

By accounting for the vector nature of mechanical interactions, a directional correction framework has been established. This significantly enhances the physical authenticity in characterizing multi-bubble system dynamics. Methodologically, a reliable cross-validation system has been formed through systematic comparison between the modified theoretical model and rigorously validated finite volume method simulation results. These innovations make the directionally corrected model an effective tool for analyzing near-field spatially non-uniform multi-bubble systems. The model provides a more physically accurate explanation of collective bubble dynamics while improving the numerical computation framework for small-scale clusters. Ultimately, it builds a theoretical bridge for large-scale bubble system analysis. This work not only deepens the understanding of acoustic cavitation cluster dynamics but also improves existing models and enhances traditional methods' descriptive capabilities. Furthermore, it provides new theoretical foundations for applications involving dense bubble clusters, such as ultrasonic bubble detection in spacecraft pipelines for aerospace engineering. This research holds significant value for advancing development in related engineering application fields.

Numerical analysis of multi-bubble vibration characteristics

This study is grounded in the application requirements of ultrasonic bubble detection technology within spacecraft pipelines in aerospace engineering. Addressing this project context, the research focuses on modeling and analyzing microbubble distribution characteristics in pipelines, aiming to provide theoretical underpinnings for research parameter selection and experimental condition settings in practical applications. When air bubbles are introduced into liquids, the resulting bubble size distribution typically approximates a normal distribution, with the central radius concentrated in the 10–100 μm range. Differences in bubble generation methods lead to variations in distribution characteristics. Based on literature data and experimental observations, even low concentrations of microbubbles during spacecraft operation may pose potential threats to system safety. Consequently, to achieve early warning and prevent bubble coalescence from evolving into high-concentration bubble clusters, this study concentrates on analyzing the dynamic behavior of bubble clouds under low void fraction (low bubble concentration) conditions.

This study begins with a three-bubble system, using the three-bubble model as the foundation for investigating multi-bubble dynamics. The primary focus is on the dynamic behaviors of three arbitrarily distributed bubbles in a liquid under external acoustic excitation. The effects of initial bubble radius, inter-bubble distance, and spatial distribution on bubble dynamics were analyzed through numerical simulations. Additionally, the influences of different excitation frequencies and amplitudes on bubble dynamics are compared, enabling the analysis of both linear and nonlinear vibration characteristics of bubbles. Subsequently, the study was extended to a four-bubble system, where small coupled units were used to reveal the fundamental principles of interactions within multi-bubble systems. This approach facilitates a gradual transition to the study of multi-bubble dynamics, ultimately establishing a more universal theoretical model for multi-bubble systems.

In the process of numerical calculations, the environmental constants commonly used under standard atmospheric pressure conditions are set, including the static pressure of the liquid at infinity p0 = 101325 Pa, the density of the liquid ρL = 998 kg/m3, the coefficient of surface tension of the liquid σ = 0.0725 N/m, the coefficient of viscosity of the liquid μ = 1*10−3 kg/(m*s), the speed of propagation of the acoustic wave in the stationary liquid cL = 1480 m/s, and the specific heat ratio of the gas in the bubble γ = 1.4. The excitation signal of the external acoustic field in the calculation process is in the form of P=PAsin(2πf), where PA is the excitation acoustic wave amplitude and f is the excitation acoustic wave frequency, and the loss of the excitation acoustic wave in the propagation process is not considered. The theoretically obtained multi-bubble vibration system equation model is computationally and analytically simulated using the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method to find the relative radius of the bubble vibration transient that varies with time and to analyze the bubble vibration process.

Building upon prior research [66], [67], [68], [69], the typical parameters adopted in our simulations are as follows: the primary initial bubble radius is set to 10 μm, the inter-bubble spacing ranges from 3 to 10 times the bubble radius, and the number of bubbles considered is 3 or 4, constituting a low-concentration, multi-bubble system. For external excitation, the acoustic field is configured as a near-field excitation with a frequency below 1 MHz and pressure amplitudes ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 atm. Boundary reflections and bubble migration effects are neglected to simplify the system modeling. Regarding the initial conditions, it is assumed that the bubble wall is stationary and the initial internal pressure of the bubble follows the thermodynamic equilibrium principle, which is included in Equation (2). The initial static pressure of the liquid at infinity is set to standard atmospheric pressure, and the temperature is constant at room temperature. For the boundary conditions, the numerical computation domain is considered as an infinite fluid region. At the bubble surface, the pressure continuity and interfacial velocity continuity conditions are satisfied. In terms of thermodynamic assumptions, the bubble vibration process follows an isentropic process without phase change. The above research conditions and parameter settings provide the physical foundation and numerical guarantee for establishing the multi-bubble dynamic model and analyzing the interaction mechanisms between bubbles in this study.

Under the theoretical framework of the modified model, this study validates the frequency dependence and amplitude sensitivity of bubble dynamic responses, with results consistent with classical resonant theory. When the frequency of the external driving force matches the natural frequency of the system, energy is efficiently coupled, leading to a significant increase in the system's amplitude. Bubble vibration exhibits frequency selectivity and amplitude sensitivity, demonstrating a linear amplification in response to excitation intensity. Moreover, due to its filtering properties as a resonant cavity, excitation near its natural frequency generates a stronger response.

Since the distribution of Bubble 2 and Bubble 3 is symmetrical, the conditions are the same, and the vibration situation is also the same, the vibration situation of Bubble 2 is selected among the two bubbles to be analyzed. The excitation amplitude of the external acoustic wave is set 0.7 atm, and the fixed excitation frequency is 25 kHz, which is much smaller than the corresponding bubble resonant frequency of the bubble scale. The calculation results are shown in Fig. 3.

The vibration processes of three bubbles when changing the initial radius R10 of Bubble 1 and changes in the total interaction on the bubbles.

As illustrated in Fig. 3(a), the vibration amplitude of Bubble 1 is smaller at an initial radius (R10) of 5 μm compared to that at R10 = 20 μm. Fig. 3(b) demonstrates that the relatively small radius change of Bubble 1 under these conditions, at a fixed bubble spacing, results in correspondingly more negligible radius changes in Bubbles 2 and 3. Conversely, when Bubble 1 undergoes larger radius variations, the radius changes in Bubbles 2 and 3 become more significant. Analysis of the interaction radiation sound pressure acting on the bubbles, presented in Fig. 3(c), reveals that the magnitude of the total force variation experienced by Bubbles 2 and 3 increases significantly with the initial radius of Bubble 1. However, the absolute value of this bubble–bubble interaction force remains small relative to the external acoustic field excitation. Consequently, the overall influence of bubble–bubble interactions on the bubble dynamics is secondary; the primary driver of bubble vibration remains the external acoustic field. Examination of the instantaneous vibration radius curve for Bubble 2 in Fig. 3(b) indicates that bubble–bubble interactions primarily affect the higher-frequency components of its vibration. Furthermore, as the initial radius of Bubble 1 increases, the amplitude of its instantaneous radius variation becomes more pronounced. This leads to an enhancement in the amplitude of the interaction forces exerted on Bubbles 2 and 3, thereby increasing both the amplitude and prominence of their high-frequency vibrations. Thus, the bubble–bubble interaction exerts an enhancing effect on the high-frequency vibrational component within the bubble oscillation cycle.

Based on the physical mechanisms analyzed, the significant variation in the vibration amplitude of Bubble 1 with its initial radius corresponds to the variation pattern between its natural frequency and the external acoustic field frequency. The vibration of Bubble 1 generates a secondary sound field, which exerts radiation force on neighboring bubbles. When its amplitude increases, the surface acceleration intensifies, leading to enhanced radiated sound pressure. Consequently, the force acting on Bubbles 2 and 3 strengthens. The bubble–bubble interaction primarily affects the high-frequency components of Bubble 2′s vibration, and this effect becomes more pronounced as the initial radius of Bubble 1 increases. This phenomenon likely arises because the external acoustic field primarily governs the fundamental-frequency vibration, while bubble coupling amplifies the high-frequency components.

Effect of bubble spacing on bubble vibration characteristics of the three-bubble system

The change of bubble vibration is observed in the four cases. The excitation amplitude of the external sound wave is set to 0.9 atm, and the fixed excitation frequency is 25 kHz. The calculation results are shown in Fig. 4.

The vibrational processes of the three bubbles and the change of the total interaction on the three bubbles when changing the bubbles’ distances.

Analysis of bubble vibration across the four cases, as depicted by the instantaneous radius changes of the three bubbles in Fig. 4(a), reveals that bubble–bubble interactions under this excitation primarily modulate the high-frequency vibrations occurring during the latter half of each oscillation cycle. Due to the symmetric bubble distribution with Bubble 1 at its center, the net interaction radiation sound pressure on Bubble 1 is zero under equidistant spacing conditions. However, when the spacing is unequal (e.g., D12 = 30 μm, D13 = 300 μm or D12 = 300 μm, D13 = 30 μm, as shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 4), the interaction force variation acting on Bubble 1 is equivalent in both configurations. Consequently, the resulting vibrational changes in the bubbles are consistent across these unequal spacing scenarios. Under equidistant spacing, Bubble 1 can be considered effectively free from bubble-bubble interaction effects. When Bubble 1 is subjected to interactions from adjacent bubbles, the magnitude of the change in its high-frequency vibrational component is reduced compared to scenarios where interaction forces are absent. Overall, the presence of bubble–bubble interactions in this context suppresses the relative radial amplitude variation of the bubble's vibration. Such suppression of the expansion ratio aligns with the findings presented in reference [34].

Equation (3) theoretically confirms that both the amplitude of variation and the maximum value of the interaction force decrease as the inter-bubble spacing increases. This is corroborated by the vibration radius characteristics of Bubbles 2 and 3 shown in Fig. 4, Fig. 4: smaller bubble spacings correspond to larger interaction amplitudes and greater maximum force values. This enhanced interaction force amplifies the suppression of bubble vibration, leading to a reduction in the amplitude variation of the instantaneous relative radius specifically during the high-frequency phase of the bubble's oscillation cycle. These computational results reveal the intrinsic asymmetry arising from differential bubble positioning. The observed suppression effect at high-frequency amplitudes during the latter half of the oscillation cycle likely stems from phase differences in acoustic field propagation to the bubbles, resulting in anti-phase superposition during the late oscillation phase.

Since the magnitude of the interaction effect on the bubble under these conditions is comparable to the excitation amplitude, the instantaneous radius change during vibration is governed by the coupling of both the external excitation and bubble–bubble interactions. Fig. 6(a) shows that for Bubble 1, varying θ1 (i.e., altering the angular bubble position distribution) leads to changes in its vibration amplitude. As θ1 increases, the interaction pressure acting on Bubble 1 decreases, as shown in Fig. 6(c). This indicates that the strength of the bubble–bubble interaction has a certain influence on the overall bubble vibration amplitude, particularly playing a dominant role during the initial stage of bubble vibration. At the beginning of the vibration, the amplitude of Bubble 1 is largest at θ1 = 45°, gradually decreases at θ1 = 90° and θ1 = 135°, and becomes smallest at θ1 = 180°. For symmetrically distributed Bubbles 2 and 3, the variation in the amplitude of the interaction force is influenced by the coupled effects of changes in angle θ1 and inter-bubble distance, making the situation relatively complex. As shown in Fig. 6(c), the interaction reaches its maximum at θ1 = 45°. In contrast, at θ1 = 90°, 135°, and 180°, the interaction remains largely similar, resulting in comparable amplitudes of bubble vibration.

Analysis of the bubble vibration spectrum, as shown in the spectrograms of Fig. 6, Fig. 6, reveals distinct peaks near the excitation frequency corresponding to the resonant frequencies of the bubbles. Since both the bubble–bubble interaction strength and the radial vibration amplitude vary with θ1, the corresponding resonant frequencies also shift with angular position. Overall, an increase in the bubble–bubble interaction leads to a leftward shift in the resonant frequency. This frequency reduction becomes more pronounced as the coupling strength between Bubble 1 and Bubble 2 increases.

We maintain that the core mechanism of θ1 modulation lies in angle-dependent energy transfer efficiency, primarily governed by the geometric symmetry of bubble distribution and attenuation in radiative energy transfer due to path differences. Physically, the resonant frequency migration observed in computational results demonstrates the transition of bubbles from independent vibration under weak coupling to collective vibration behavior under strong coupling.

Multi-bubble dynamics characterization based on the four-bubble model

The four-bubble model is a further study based on the three-bubble model, which is used to analyze the more complex multi-bubble vibration model characterization. In this section, the four-bubble structure on the two-dimensional plane is studied, so that the changes in the vibration characteristics of the original three bubbles in the plane and the vibration characteristics of the fourth bubble can be analyzed after the number of bubbles is increased. The effect of the number of bubbles on the bubble dynamics characteristics within the multi-bubble system can be investigated. Based on the three bubbles previously distributed in a straight line, a fourth bubble is added, so that Bubble 4 is located directly above Bubble 1. The changes in the vibration characteristics of the three bubbles as well as the vibration characteristics of the fourth bubble are analyzed. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the introduction of a fourth bubble yields a relatively smaller increase in the radial vibration amplitude of the three originally collinear bubbles as the bubble count rises. A lateral comparison of vibrational responses among all four bubbles (Fig. 9(a)-(b)) reveals the following amplitude relationships under identical excitation: Bubble 2 and Bubble 3 exhibit identical and overlapping amplitudes—the largest observed—marginally exceeding Bubble 4, while Bubble 4′s amplitude exceeds that of Bubble 1.

Analysis of the bubble distribution reveals that when radial vibration amplitudes exhibit comparable magnitudes, the angular configuration governing bubble–bubble interactions becomes the primary factor influencing vibrational behavior. Under these conditions, interaction strength exhibits a positive correlation with vibration amplitude. Within this symmetric arrangement, Bubbles 2 and 3 experience the largest interaction force due to their symmetric positioning. Consequently, they display the greatest radial displacement amplitude and most significant relative radius change, followed by Bubble 4. Conversely, Bubble 1 experiences the smallest interaction force—substantially weaker than those acting on Bubbles 2, 3, and 4—resulting in its minimal vibration amplitude. The physical origin of this disparity primarily lies in differences in the spatial symmetry-dominated distribution of radiation forces. Bubbles at distinct positions within the cluster are subjected to spatially heterogeneous acoustic fields, resulting in significantly differentiated vibrational responses. These findings are consistent with experimental results in reference [55], further validating the reliability of this study.

Spectral analysis of radial vibration amplitude variation further demonstrates that increasing bubble count enhances coupling effects, progressively reducing the peak frequency near resonance. In the four-bubble system (Fig. 9(a)-(b)), interaction strengths vary according to spatial distribution and relative angles. Despite minimal differences in individual bubble vibration amplitudes under these excitation conditions, bubble–bubble interactions emerge as the dominant factor governing system dynamics. Correspondingly, resonant frequency peaks exhibit a leftward shift with intensifying interactions. This phenomenon aligns with established research: Studies by Dorian [21], Hossein [35], Matthieu [60], and Sojahroo [61] consistently report an inverse relationship between resonant frequency and inter-bubble distance in multi-bubble systems. Reduced spacing significantly enhances bubble–bubble coupling interactions, which not only modifies system dynamics but also reduces resonant frequency. Our numerical simulations strongly corroborate this behavior, validating the rationality and accuracy of the proposed multi-bubble coupling model. This agreement provides critical support for understanding collective phenomena in multi-bubble systems.

Simulation modeling and validation

Finite volume method model

In order to simulate the influence of the bubble–bubble interactions on the vibration characteristics of multi-bubbles, a three-bubble finite element simulation model is established applying Fluent based on the fluid dynamics governing equation and the fluid volume fraction model. The simulation analysis and theoretical model verification are carried out to prove the reliability of the theoretical model. To simplify the model, facilitate computation, and eliminate complications arising from compressibility effects during acoustic propagation, thereby focusing on the influence of bubble–bubble interactions on vibrational behavior within the multi-bubble system, the liquid state in Fluent was modeled as incompressible. Furthermore, to maintain alignment between theoretical and numerical assumptions, the theoretical model based on the R-P equation incorporating the directionality of bubble–bubble interactions under incompressible conditions was comparatively analyzed. The numerical model was constructed using the well-validated Volume of Fluid (VOF) model within ANSYS Fluent.

Since our multi-bubble theoretical model treats bubbles within a one-dimensional modeling framework—assuming they remain spherical throughout their motion—bubble dynamics in the theoretical model are primarily characterized by variations in radius. This rationale underpins our use of a two-dimensional simulation approach. Although real-world scenarios may involve spatial pressure inhomogeneity that could lead to localized asymmetric forces on bubbles in a three-dimensional context, our current theoretical method incorporates these effects through equivalent global spherical variations. Under the assumption of sustained spherical oscillation, the model focuses exclusively on radial dynamics. Therefore, a two-dimensional axisymmetric simulation is considered sufficient to capture essential physical effects.

This 2D numerical simulation approach has been previously applied and validated in earlier work. For instance, Yun Zhao et al. [20] employed an identical Fluent 2D VOF (Volume-of-Fluid) setup to investigate single-bubble dynamics under low-frequency, high-intensity acoustic excitation. Their comparison between numerical results and theoretical models demonstrated the effectiveness and feasibility of this simulation strategy for modeling bubble dynamics.

This study specifically focuses on multi-bubble systems confined to a two-dimensional plane, emphasizing the vibrational and frequency characteristics of the bubbles. The primary dynamic processes—including the externally applied standing wave field and the Bjerknes forces between bubbles—are strictly axisymmetric with respect to the central axis of the bubble cluster. Thus, key phenomena such as radial oscillations can be accurately represented in two dimensions. Building on the aforementioned research, this study establishes a two-dimensional VOF numerical model suitable for multi-bubble systems, which is used for comparative analysis with multi-bubble theoretical models.

In order to simulate the evolution of the radial pulsation radius of three bubbles in water under ultrasonic driving, a two-dimensional axisymmetric model is constructed as shown in Fig. 10. This modeling strategy is based on the following considerations: First, the studied bubble arrangement possesses axisymmetric characteristics. Under this geometric configuration, the acoustic radiation coupling between bubbles and their vibrational behavior can be effectively captured by a two-dimensional axisymmetric model, preserving the key physical mechanisms. Second, compared to full three-dimensional simulations, the two-dimensional model significantly reduces computational resource requirements and time costs. This is particularly efficient in scenarios demanding high interfacial resolution at gas–liquid boundaries. The fluid environment region in the figure is a semicircular region with a radius of 900 μm, which is much larger than 20 times of the radius of the bubbles, simulating the excitation at infinity and ignoring the influence of the boundary reflux on the interior of the flow field. Conclusions

This study investigates the dynamics of multi-bubble systems under acoustic fields by considering the directional nature of bubble–bubble interactions. A three- to four-bubble system is constructed to investigate the multi-bubble model and introduced directional corrections to the bubble–bubble interaction terms in the K-M equation to improve the dynamic characterization of multi-bubble oscillation systems. The vibrational changes of bubbles influenced by other bubbles in the acoustic field are numerically analyzed in combination with interaction variations, and the vibrational behaviors of different bubbles are compared. The effects of driving frequency, amplitude, bubble initial radius, inter-bubble distance, angular bubble distribution and number of bubbles on bubble interactions, radial pulsations, and frequency characteristics are investigated. The theoretical model is validated using the Fluent finite volume simulation method. The results demonstrate that the modified theoretical model, which accounts for the directionality of bubble interactions, is feasible and more accurately reflects bubble vibration processes and frequency characteristics. Specifically, when the acoustic excitation amplitude significantly exceeds the interaction pressure wave amplitude, bubble interactions primarily affect the high-frequency vibration components. When the acoustic excitation amplitude is comparable to the interaction pressure wave amplitude, the magnitude of bubble interactions influences the amplitude of bubble radius oscillations. Variations in driving amplitude, frequency, inter-bubble distance, angular bubble distribution and number of bubbles differentially impact the relative radius changes of bubbles and alter their resonant frequencies. The directionally corrected theoretical model proposed in this study demonstrates good agreement with simulation results obtained using the Fluent finite volume method. However, its inherent assumptions and applicable scope require clarification: the theoretical model is entirely based on the fundamental assumption of spherical bubble oscillations. All directional effects are ultimately incorporated into the K-M equation through scalar numerical corrections to the interaction terms. The calculated results still represent isotropic average pressures, without directly resolving asymmetric pressure fields or bubble shape variations. This theoretical foundation determines that the model is primarily suitable for conditions where non-spherical deformation is insignificant, including moderate acoustic pressure intensities, appropriate bubble spacing (particularly in the near-field region of D < 5R0 emphasized in this study), and spatial configurations that do not induce strong instabilities.

To maintain focus and simplify computations, this study concentrates on low-frequency (<1 MHz), short-range (bubble spacing D ≤ 5R0) microbubble systems, specifically examining how bubble–bubble interactions modulate bubble vibration patterns. Under these conditions, the acoustic wavelength significantly exceeds bubble spacing, making pressure phase differences negligible and satisfying the quasi-static approximation. Consequently, an infinite sound speed assumption is adopted in modeling bubble interactions, neglecting time delay effects to better capture the core physical mechanisms of interactions. Based on practical applications such as aerospace pipeline ultrasonic inspection and biomedical contexts where bubble concentrations remain below the “bubble cloud” threshold, the study also employs the assumption of a spatially uniform external acoustic excitation field, meaning all bubbles in the theoretical model are driven by a uniform acoustic field.

The theoretical model for multi-bubble dynamics developed in this study is specifically designed for analyzing vibrations and interactions in low-concentration bubble environments. It primarily addresses systems containing three to four bubbles, with a typical configuration featuring bubble equilibrium radii of approximately 10 μm and inter-bubble spacing ranging from 3 to 10 times the radius. The model is established under standard atmospheric pressure and room temperature conditions, effectively representing common operational scenarios in practical engineering applications. In terms of acoustic excitation, the model focuses on near-field acoustic excitation with frequencies below 1 MHz, covering excitation pressure amplitudes within the typical range of 0.1 to 0.9 atm. Based on application characteristics such as aerospace pipeline inspection, the model incorporates specific boundary conditions including the neglect of boundary reflection effects under the assumption of acoustic wave propagation in an infinite fluid domain, and the exclusion of bubble translational motion to concentrate on vibration characteristics and interaction mechanisms. The initial conditions configured for the model comprise bubble walls initially at rest and internal gas pressure satisfying initial thermodynamic equilibrium. The model describes the dynamic process under an isentropic assumption, with the understanding that the internal gas maintains thermodynamic equilibrium and undergoes no phase change. This comprehensively defined set of initial and boundary conditions provides a reliable theoretical basis and numerical assurance for investigating both linear and nonlinear dynamic behaviors of low-concentration bubble clouds. It is particularly suited for simulating dynamics and analyzing interaction mechanisms in multi-bubble systems under near-field conditions. The framework not only ensures model applicability in specific application scenarios but also establishes a consistent benchmark for comparative analysis with simulation results.

In summary, this model should be regarded as an effective approximation that provides a refined description of multi-bubble interactions within the spherical oscillation framework. Building on the spherical vibration assumption, the research primarily investigates two-dimensional multi-bubble models, focusing on vibration and frequency characteristics of multiple bubbles within the same plane. It bridges classical models and full three-dimensional fluid models, offering a more accurate tool for analyzing near-field, spatially non-uniform multi-bubble systems—particularly suitable for early-warning scenarios in aerospace pipelines with low cavitation rates, where bubble sizes typically range from 10–100 μm. The model's establishment provides theoretical support for parameter selection and experimental setup in practical engineering applications, enhancing predictive capability for collective bubble dynamics under specific conditions.

It should be noticed that the non-uniform radius change due to pressure nonuniformity around the bubble wall is not strictly modelled. Instead, we introduce a scalar correction to describe the pressure non-uniformity. Through this simplification, the resonant frequency distributions as well as averaged bubble radius can be quickly predicted without using time-consuming 2D/3D simulations. It should be noticed that this proposed model cannot describe the dynamics of non-spherical bubbles. The proposed model may be valuable for applications where bubbles’ resonant frequency distributions is more important than the bubbles’ exact shapes. However, the model shows clear limitations under extreme conditions. When acoustic field intensity is extremely high, bubble spacing becomes excessively small, or strong shear flows exist, bubbles will undergo significant non-spherical deformation, fragmentation, or coalescence. In such cases, the model's fundamental assumptions no longer hold, and more advanced methods capable of directly resolving interface deformations are required to accurately capture the actual physical processes.

In future work, we aim to extend this research by constructing bubble distribution models in three-dimensional space, including systems with four or more bubbles. To account for the influence of three-dimensional spatial arrangements on bubble dynamics, we will employ more realistic three-dimensional simulation models in subsequent studies. This approach will enable a more comprehensive exploration of complex vibrational behaviors under varied bubble distributions and larger populations. Furthermore, we plan to develop a three-dimensional four-bubble distribution model in Fluent for simulation analysis and model comparison, thereby enhancing the understanding of the interaction mechanisms among bubbles. In the current study, we adopted a combined approach of finite volume simulation using Fluent and theoretical modeling, and validated the modified model accordingly. Conducting experimental validation of the theoretical model under our laboratory conditions presents significant technical challenges at this stage. We acknowledge that the lack of direct experimental validation is one of the limitations of this study. In future work, we will try to address this by performing experimental validation through precise control of the initial positions, sizes, and synchronization of multiple bubbles, as well as by capturing transient flow field details using high-speed imaging.
