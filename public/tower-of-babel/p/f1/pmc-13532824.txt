The TRPM2–PARP‐1 Axis Involvement in Bisphenol A and Nonylphenol‐Induced Ferroptosis in Trigeminal Ganglion Cells
Authors: Betül Yazğan, Musa Tatar, Yener Yazğan, Kıymet Kübra Tüfekci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnvironmental contaminants like bisphenol A (BPA) and nonylphenol (NP) are recognized neurotoxicants; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying their impact on sensory ganglia, specifically the trigeminal ganglion (TG), remain critically underexplored. This study explored the potential of BPA and NP to drive neuronal injury and ferroptosis linked to oxidative stress, acting through the transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2)–poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase‐1 (PARP1) signaling axis. Forty‐two adult male Wistar rats were allocated into seven groups (control, low‐dose [25 mg/kg]/high‐dose [100 mg/kg] BPA, low‐dose [25 mg/kg]/high‐dose [100 mg/kg] NP, and low‐dose [25 mg/kg]/high‐dose [100 mg/kg] BPA + NP) and treated orally for 21 days. We employed a combinatorial approach of biochemical assays and immunohistochemistry to evaluate oxidative stress markers, ferroptosis hallmarks (glutathione peroxidase 4 [GPX4], solute carrier family 7 member 11 [SLC7A11], and transferrin receptor [TfRC]), apoptotic mediators (Caspase‐3 and Caspase‐9), and inflammatory cytokines, as well as the expression of TRPM2 and PARP‐1. Results demonstrated that BPA and NP exposure triggered a robust, dose‐dependent accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation, concomitant with downregulation of anti‐ferroptotic proteins (GPX4 and SLC7A11) and upregulation of TfRC. This toxic insult simultaneously activated apoptotic and inflammatory cascades. Crucially, TRPM2 and PARP‐1 were significantly upregulated, implying a potential role for the TRPM2–PARP‐1 axis as an upstream modulator of oxidative stress‐induced ferroptosis and neuroinflammation. Collectively, these findings provide novel mechanistic insights into phenol‐induced neurotoxicity, highlighting the inhibition of the TRPM2–PARP‐1 axis as a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate environmental neurodegeneration in sensory neurons.

Introduction

Endocrine‐disrupting chemicals such as bisphenol A (BPA) and nonylphenol (NP) have become significant subjects of research in recent years due to their harmful effects on the environment and human health. These substances are surfactants commonly found in various products, including food packaging, plastic manufacturing, detergents, soaps, paints, herbicides, resins, cosmetics, emulsifiers, and solvents (Amaro et al. 2014; Çiğ and Yildizhan 2020). These chemicals are persistent in living organisms and the environment, exhibiting mutagenic, estrogenic, toxic, and carcinogenic properties (Amaro et al. 2014). They can lead to significant pathophysiological changes and diseases by affecting estrogen, progesterone, and androgen receptors (X. Li et al. 2010). Reports have been presented on the prooxidant behavior of BPA, antioxidant depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, alteration in cell signaling pathways, and induction of cell death (Gassman 2017). NP spreads to the central nervous system via the bloodstream and eventually crosses the blood–brain barrier. Because of its lipophilic properties, it can accumulate in various fatty tissues such as the brain (Lotfi et al. 2021). NP is a resistant toxic substance that can affect brain functions in various ways (Xia et al. 2008). Exposure to NP has been found to disrupt dendrites and synaptic growth in the hypothalamus of mouse fetuses, increase caspase expression in hippocampal and cortical cells, and induce apoptosis in these cells (Yokosuka et al. 2008; Mao et al. 2008). Despite numerous studies on the neurotoxic effects of BPA and NP, the precise pathophysiological effects of these substances remain unclear. Research has shown that exposure to these chemicals leads to various structural and molecular changes in the neurological system. As a result, alterations in gene and protein expression levels in the brain, decreases in axon length and myelination in neurons, and disruptions in neurotransmitter synthesis and release, as well as neuroinflammation, have been observed (Jie et al. 2017; Lotfi et al. 2021; Costa and Cairrao 2024).

A central mechanism proposed in these pathologies is the disruption of intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis, leading to the induction of oxidative stress (OS). This imbalance triggers the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to lipid peroxidation (LPx) and the depletion of antioxidant defences, such as glutathione (GSH) (Ijaz et al. 2021; Olukole et al. 2019). However, the specific signaling pathways that translate this OS into regulated cell death in sensory neurons have not been fully elucidated.

The trigeminal ganglion (TG) is a critical structure that governs craniofacial sensation and is intimately involved in pain pathologies, such as migraine and trigeminal neuralgia (Yazğan and Nazıroğlu 2021; Kc et al. 2022). Although BPA has been linked to migraine‐like symptoms and allodynia via estrogen receptors (Vermeer et al. 2014; Soriano et al. 2019), a notable gap exists in the literature regarding the direct cytotoxic mechanisms of BPA and NP on TG neurons. We hypothesize that these toxins may trigger a specific, dual‐death mode involving both apoptosis and ferroptosis. Apoptosis is typically a caspase‐dependent form of cell death, while ferroptosis is characterized as a caspase‐independent mechanism due to LPx and reduced GSH depletion (Kuang et al. 2020; Hirata et al. 2023; Çiğ and Yildizhan 2020). Ferroptosis has been associated with the pathogenesis of various neurodegenerative diseases, and ferroptosis inhibitors have been proposed as potential new therapeutic targets for neurological disorders (Y. H. Zhang et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2024). Recently, the transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2) channel has emerged as a potential regulator of this process. TRPM2 is a nonselective cation channel highly sensitive to OS and ADP‐ribose (ADPR). Its activation leads to massive Ca2+ influx, which can exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS production (Yazğan and Nazıroğlu 2021; Çiğ and Yildizhan 2020).

These channels play a crucial role in regulating various cell death pathways (Q. Wang et al. 2016; H. Wang et al. 2017). Because ferroptosis is a recently defined form of cell death, only a few studies have been conducted regarding the effects of TRPM2 channels on this pathway (Shi et al. 2021; Li, Wang, et al. 2022; Zhong et al. 2023; Yazğan et al. 2025). Crucially, TRPM2 activation may act as a bridge between OS and ferroptosis by disrupting intracellular ion homeostasis and promoting iron accumulation (Shi et al. 2021).

In this study, we aim to unravel the molecular crosstalk between TRPM2 channel activation and ferroptosis in BPA‐ and NP‐induced neurotoxicity. Unlike previous studies that have focused solely on hormonal effects, we investigate whether high‐dose exposure to these agents triggers a “dual mode of cell death” of apoptosis and ferroptosis in rat TG neurons. Specifically, we examine the roles of TRPM2‐mediated Ca2+ overload, transferrin receptor (TfRC) regulation, and antioxidant depletion (solute carrier family 7 member 11 [SLC7A11], reduced GSH, GSH peroxidase 4 [GPX4], GSH peroxidase [G‐Px]) using detailed immunohistochemical (IHC) and biochemical analyses.

Experimental Design

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments). The experimental protocol was approved by the Kastamonu University Local Ethics Committee for Animal Experiments under approval number 01.08.2023/28. In this study, 42 male Wistar albino rats (average 200–250 g, 10–12 weeks old) were used. Animals were randomly allocated to experimental and control groups. Each group consisted of six animals (n = 6). The care, feeding, experimental procedures, and sacrifice of the rats were carried out at the Kastamonu University Experimental Animals Research and Application Center. The animals were housed in standard polypropylene cages under carefully controlled environmental conditions, including a temperature of 24 ± 2°C, relative humidity ranging between 50% and 60%, and a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle (lights on from 08:00 to 20:00). Throughout the experimental period, rats were provided with standard chow and drinking water ad libitum. Rats were anesthetized with ketamine (50 mg/kg)/xylazine (10 mg/kg) and euthanized by cervical dislocation technique. The experiment was terminated after 21 days to examine the TG tissues.

Preparation of TG

Following euthanasia, the heads of the rats were removed, and the cranial cavity was carefully opened to extract the brain without causing structural damage. The left and right TG tissues were then dissected from within the skull (Figure 6A). The collected TG samples were subsequently processed for biochemical, IHC, and antioxidant analyses via spectrophotometry. Procedures for the biochemical and antioxidant assessments were performed in line with methodologies described in a previous report (Yazğan and Nazıroğlu 2021). Briefly, isolated TG tissues were incubated in 5 mL of DMEM containing type XI collagenase (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich) and type III trypsin at 37°C in a shaking water bath for 45 min. Enzymatic digestion was terminated using a soybean trypsin inhibitor (1.25 mg/mL, type II‐S1, Sigma‐Aldrich). Afterward, the DMEM was removed via sterile syringe aspiration and centrifugation at 1500 g. Neuronal cells intended for biochemical and antioxidant measurements were transferred into sterile Falcon tubes containing fresh DMEM.

Cell Homogenate Preparation Steps

All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions provided with the commercial kits obtained from BT LAB and SunRed Biotech. Briefly, the cells were placed into Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. After carefully removing the supernatant, the resulting cell pellets were resuspended in PBS (pH 7.4) to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 104 cells/mL. Cell lysis was performed through repeated freeze–thaw cycles, followed by the removal of cytoplasmic contents. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatants were collected with a pipette and used for further analyses (Yazğan and Nazıroğlu 2021).

The supernatants were collected with a pipette and used for the quantitative determination of intracellular biochemical parameters, including OS markers (ROS, GSH, and G‐Px), LPx, caspases (Casp‐3 and Casp‐9), TRPM2/PARP‐1, and pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6) using ELISA and spectrophotometric methods.

Tissue Preparation

TG tissues obtained from all groups were placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for fixation. After routine tissue processing procedures, 5‐μm sections were cut from tissue blocks and placed onto adhesive slides after embedding in paraffin. Sections were stained immunohistochemically with anti‐GPX4, anti‐SLC7A11, and anti‐TfRC. All tissue sections were processed and analyzed under strict quality assurance procedures using identical staining, imaging, and analysis parameters, obtained from the same anatomical plane and predefined regions of interest, and evaluated by an investigator blinded to group allocation. All histological examinations were conducted in the Department of Histology and Embryology research laboratory at Kastamonu University Faculty of Medicine.

IHC Method

The streptavidin–biotin complex (sABC) staining method was applied to demonstrate the immunoreactivity of ferroptosis markers in 5‐μm serial sections taken at 100‐μm intervals on adhesive slides. Anti‐GPX4 (1:1000), Anti‐SLC7A11 (1:250), and anti‐TfRC (1:200) antibodies were applied to the section. According to this method, paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval by incubation in citrate buffer solution (pH 6.0) heated to 96°C for 30 min to unmask antigenic sites. The sections were then washed three times for 5 min in Tris‐buffered saline containing Tween 20 (TBS‐T; pH 7.6) and incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After washing three times for 5 min in TBS‐T (pH 7.6), the sections were incubated in protein blocking solution at room temperature for 10 min.

Primary antibodies, diluted to appropriate concentrations, were applied, and the sections were incubated overnight at +4°C in a humidified chamber. Following incubation, the sections were washed twice for 5 min in TBS‐T (pH 7.6) and then incubated with a biotin‐labeled secondary antibody for 30 min. After washing, the sections were incubated with HRP solution for 30 min, followed by washing twice for 5 min in TBS‐T (pH 7.6).

To visualize immunopositive cells, the sections were incubated with 3‐amino‐9‐ethylcarbazole (AEC Chromogen & Substrate System; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 3–5 min. Counterstaining was performed with Gill's hematoxylin to visualize nuclei. For negative controls, sections were incubated with TBS‐T instead of the primary antibody (Tüfekci and Tatar 2023; Tüfekci 2023).

H‐Score

The levels of staining intensity in each section were expressed as 0 (−): no staining, 1 (+): weak staining, 2 (++): moderate staining, and 3 (+++): strong staining, and the percentage (%) of cells at each staining intensity level was calculated according to the formula below (Tüfekci and Tatar 2023; Tüfekci 2023).

H‐score formula = (0 × 0%) + (1 × 1%) + (2 × 2%) + (3 × 3%).

For each animal, at least three tissue sections obtained from the same anatomical region were analyzed. From each section, five regions of interest (ROIs) were selected at ×400 magnification, resulting in a minimum of 15 ROIs per animal. ROIs were defined as standardized areas of approximately 10,000 μm2, selected from regions exhibiting preserved tissue architecture, sufficient cell density, and no processing or staining artifacts. All analyses were conducted using identical imaging and analysis parameters. Evaluations were performed by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups to ensure objectivity and reproducibility.

Measurement of Biochemical Parameters in TGs by ELISA Kits

The concentrations of caspases, PARP‐1, ROS, TRPM2, and inflammatory markers in the cell supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits. Following the manufacturers' instructions, the assay procedures were carried out sequentially as follows: supernatants were first incubated at 37°C, after which both samples and standards were dispensed into 96‐well plates. The plates were incubated for 60 min, subjected to the recommended washing cycles, and then treated with the chromogenic reagents, followed by a 15‐min incubation period. Finally, a stop solution was applied, and absorbance measurements were recorded using an ELISA microplate reader (BioTek EL808) (Yazğan and Nazıroğlu 2021; Yazğan 2024).

Oxidant and Antioxidant Levels

GSH, G‐Px, and LPx levels in TG cells were quantified using a V‐730 UV spectrophotometer (Japan). For LPx analysis, cell samples were diluted at a ratio of 1:9 (2.25 mL) with thiobarbituric acid (TBARS) reagent. A mixture containing 1:9 TBARS and 0.25‐mL phosphate buffer served as the blank. Both samples and blanks were heated in boiling water, allowed to cool, and subsequently centrifuged at 3500 rpm. The upper pink phase was collected and measured at 532 nm in a 1‐cm light path cuvette against the blank.

For GSH quantification, 10% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and Tris‐II buffer were used. A total of 0.1 mL of cell homogenate and 0.4 mL of TCA were combined in an Eppendorf tube, mixed thoroughly, and then centrifuged. Then, 0.4 mL of the resulting supernatant was transferred to a glass tube, followed by the addition of 2.0‐mL Tris‐II buffer and 0.1‐mL DTNB. Absorbance was recorded at 412 nm.

For G‐Px assessment, the required reagents included Tris‐I buffer, GSH solution, CHPO (cumene hydroperoxide), 10% TCA, Tris‐II buffer, and DTNB, 5,5′‐dithiobis(2‐nitrobenzoic acid). A mixture of 0.5‐mL cell homogenate, 0.3‐mL Tris‐I HCl, and 0.1‐mL CHPO was prepared, after which 0.1‐mL GSH was added. Following a 10‐min incubation at room temperature, 1.0‐mL TCA was introduced, and the sample was centrifuged. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a glass tube, mixed with 2 mL of Tris‐II buffer and 0.1 mL of DTNB, and the absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a spectrophotometer (Yazğan and Nazıroğlu 2021; Yazğan 2024).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS version 17.0. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene's test. For variables exhibiting a normal distribution and homogeneous variances, one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was applied. Nonnormally distributed data were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Tamhane post hoc comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and a p value of ≤ 0.05 was accepted as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

IHC Evaluation of GPX4

When GPX4 immunostaining in TG tissue was assessed using H‐score analysis, higher levels of positive staining were detected in the control group compared to all other groups (p ≤ 0.001). In contrast, GPX4 expression was significantly decreased in the BPA‐low, NP‐low, and mix‐low groups, with a statistically significant decrease compared to the control group (p ≤ 0.001). Comparing these three low‐dose groups with each other, the H‐score values were found to be statistically no different (p > 0.05).

In all high‐dose groups, GPX4 immunostaining was significantly decreased compared to the control group (p ≤ 0.001). Furthermore, the high‐dose groups exhibited significantly lower scores compared to the low‐dose groups (p ≤ 0.001).

These results show that BPA and NP exposure, especially at high doses, significantly suppresses GPX4 expression and impairs the oxidative balance (Figure 1).

IHC Evaluation of SLC7A11

When SLC7A11 immunoexpression was evaluated in the TG tissue, intense and pronounced cytoplasmic positivity was observed in the control group, and the highest H‐score values were recorded in this group (p ≤ 0.001). A significant decrease in SLC7A11 expression was detected in the BPA‐low group, resulting in lower H‐score values compared to the control group (p ≤ 0.001).

Expression levels were further reduced in the NP‐low and mix‐low groups compared with the BPA‐low group, and these two groups had similar but lower H‐score values. Both groups differed significantly from the control and BPA‐low groups (p ≤ 0.001).

SLC7A11 staining was weaker in the BPA‐high group compared to the low‐dose groups, and H‐score levels were statistically lower. The NP‐high and mix‐high groups had the lowest H‐scores among all groups (p ≤ 0.001) and showed similar results (p > 0.05) (Figure 2).

TfRC staining intensity gradually increased in the NP‐low and mix‐low groups, and the H‐scores of these groups were higher than those of the BPA‐low group (p ≤ 0.001).

Antioxidant Balance (GSH and G‐Px)

GSH and G‐Px levels were significantly lower in all low‐dose exposure groups (BPA, NP, and Mix) compared to the control group (p ≤ 0.001; Figure 4A,B). High‐dose exposure to BPA, NP, and their combination led to a further significant depletion of these antioxidants compared to both the control and their respective low‐dose counterparts (p ≤ 0.001). Notably, the reduction in antioxidant capacity was more pronounced in the NP‐exposed groups than in the BPA‐treated groups at both dose levels.

These findings suggest that both BPA and NP induce a dose‐dependent impairment of the enzymatic antioxidant system, potentially predisposing TG neurons to oxidative damage.

LPx

Conversely, LPx levels in TG cells were significantly elevated following low‐dose BPA, NP, and mix exposures compared to the control group (p ≤ 0.001; Figure 4C). A dose‐dependent increase was observed, with high‐dose exposure causing a further significant surge in LPx levels relative to the control and low‐dose groups (p ≤ 0.001). Similar to the antioxidant markers, NP exposure induced significantly higher levels of LPx compared to BPA exposure.

These results indicate that the depletion of antioxidant defenses is accompanied by substantial lipid membrane damage, a hallmark feature that aligns with the induction of ferroptosis in TG neurons.

Inflammation Levels

The levels of inflammation in rat TG cells exposed to low and high doses of BPA and NP are shown in Figure 4. IL‐1β (Figure 4D), IL‐6 (Figure 4E), and TNF‐α (Figure 4F) levels in TG cells were significantly increased with the following low‐dose BPA, NP, and mix exposures (when we compare all low‐dose groups with the control group, p ≤ 0.001). High‐dose exposure to BPA, NP, and mix further increased IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels (when we compare all high‐dose groups with the control group and all low‐dose groups, p ≤ 0.001). The observed increases were more pronounced in the NP groups than in the BPA groups at both dose levels.

These results indicate that BPA and NP exposure triggers a robust neuroinflammatory response in TG neurons, which may further exacerbate OS‐induced cellular damage.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates for the first time that BPA and NP exposure induce a coordinated pattern of OS and ferroptosis in TG neurons, associated with the TRPM2/PARP‐1 axis. Notably, the results further implicate TRPM2 as a potential molecular link between toxin‐induced OS and TG injury, thereby providing new insight into the neurotoxic actions of BPA and NP (Figure 6).

(B) Molecular pathway linking ferroptosis and TRPM2 activation: Schematic representation of the signaling mechanisms triggered by BPA and NP toxicity in TG neurons. (1) Oxidative trigger and TRPM2 activation: BPA and NP exposure induce severe oxidative stress and ROS‐associated DNA damage. This activates PARP‐1, leading to the production of ADP‐ribose (ADPR). ADPR binds to the Nudix box of TRPM2 channels, triggering channel opening and massive Ca2+ influx. (2) Mitochondrial dysfunction: Intracellular Ca2+ overload causes mitochondrial membrane depolarization (ΔΨm), which exacerbates cytosolic ROS production and dysregulation of cytokine levels and disrupts the oxidant/antioxidant balance. (3) Dual cell death pathways: (a) Ferroptosis: The toxicity severely disrupts iron homeostasis through the upregulation of the transferrin receptor (TfRC). This increased expression facilitates excessive iron uptake, expanding the intracellular labile iron pool (Fe2+). Concurrently, the suppression of the SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 axis reduces antioxidant defense. The combination of iron overload and diminished GPX4 activity drives toxic lipid peroxidation (LPx), ultimately executing ferroptosis. (b) Apoptosis: Simultaneously, the ROS/Ca2+ axis activates the caspase‐9 and caspase‐3 pathways. Conclusion: BPA and NP toxicity promote TG cell death by synergistically triggering ferroptosis (via TfRC upregulation and GPX4 failure) and apoptosis through TRPM2‐mediated signaling. Our findings regarding ROS elevation and iron accumulation align with the hallmark biochemical events of ferroptosis (Hirata et al. 2023). Recent studies have demonstrated that exposure to BPA and related compounds in PC12 cells and primary rat hippocampal neurons leads to elevated intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, increased ROS production, and higher malondialdehyde (MDA) levels (Meng et al. 2021; Y. Zhang et al. 2021). Within this framework, we examined the IHC expression of GPX4, SLC7A11, and TfRC—key markers of ferroptosis—to determine whether the increases in ROS and LPx levels were associated with the induction of ferroptotic cell death. The dose‐dependent suppression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 observed in our study suggests a critical breakdown in the antioxidant defense system of TG cells, making them highly vulnerable to LPx. The strong positivity observed in the control group is consistent with the essential roles of these molecules in maintaining redox homeostasis by limiting LPx. In contrast, the marked dose‐dependent reduction in GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression observed in the exposure groups indicates a weakened antioxidant defence system. These results suggest that BPA and NP enhance ferroptosis‐associated vulnerability in TG cells by disrupting key pathways that regulate antioxidants and LPx. Deficiency or dysfunction of GPX4 and SLC7A11 is known to disrupt cellular redox balance, leading to the pathological accumulation of toxic LPx products and subsequent membrane instability (Chen et al. 2021). Accordingly, the downregulation of these proteins is widely recognized as a key molecular trigger of ferroptotic cell death (Hirata et al. 2023). Previous studies support our findings, showing that BPA reduces GPX4 expression in gametes (Nguyen et al. 2022) and induces ferroptosis in various tissues through redox imbalance linked to the suppression of GPX4 (K. Wang et al. 2025).

The marked increase in TfRC expression following BPA and NP exposure indicates an accelerated iron uptake, which likely fuels the Fenton reaction and exacerbates ferroptotic stress in TG tissue (Yi et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2024). This pattern indicates a substantial rise in iron‐mediated LPx susceptibility, consistent with previous reports showing that BPA elevates TfRC while suppressing GPX4 (Fauzi et al. 2025). Furthermore, the concurrent reductions in GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression demonstrate a weakened antioxidant defense, disruption of the glutamate–cystine cycle, and heightened cellular vulnerability to LPx. Elevated LPx levels corroborate these findings, confirming the biochemical progression toward ferroptotic stress. Notably, this study reveals that BPA and NP exposure increased TRPM2 and PARP‐1 expression in TG cells in a dose‐dependent manner, aligning with fundamental changes associated with ferroptosis, as detected by IHC analyses. As these results provide evidence that the TRPM2/PARP‐1 signaling axis is closely involved in these toxic effects, they significantly enhance the originality of our study by identifying a potential pathway for phenol‐induced neurotoxicity.

One of the principal toxic mechanisms underlying BPA and NP exposure is well established to be OS. Recent studies have demonstrated that these compounds elevate ROS and LPx levels while weakening cellular antioxidant defence systems, thereby promoting cellular injury (Lotfi et al. 2021; Meng et al. 2021). The ferroptosis‐related literature is consistent with this observation, reporting that increased ROS and LPx production, together with reductions in GSH and G‐Px levels, trigger ferroptotic cell death (Kuang et al. 2020; Costa and Cairrao 2024). These results appear to be closely associated with TRPM2/PARP‐1 overexpression. The resulting Ca2+ overload likely disrupts mitochondrial electron transport, creating a feed‐forward loop of oxidative damage and ferroptotic signaling. In our study, biochemical analyses revealed that BPA and NP markedly disrupted redox homeostasis, with NP emerging as a more potent inducer of OS than BPA. The IHC findings for GPX4 and SLC7A11, which mirrored these biochemical outcomes, further indicate that ferroptotic susceptibility is enhanced at the molecular level. Our findings also demonstrated a dose‐dependent increase in TRPM2 expression following exposure to BPA and NP. Consistent with our results, Çiğ and Yildizhan (2020) reported that TRPM2 is involved in BPA‐induced OS. This increase in TRPM2 expression suggests that TRPM2 plays a significant regulatory role in OS‐driven cellular processes and is central to ferroptosis‐related mechanisms. Supporting this interpretation, Güzel et al. (2025) showed that TRPM2 inhibition elevates GSH levels while reducing Fe2+, ROS, and LPx—key indicators of ferroptosis. Overall, this study demonstrates that BPA and NP suppress antioxidant defenses in TG cells and activate TRPM2‐related signaling pathways that contribute to ferroptotic processes. The more pronounced depletion of GSH, GPX4, and SLC7A11 in NP groups compared to BPA suggests that NP might have a higher affinity for triggering the TRPM2‐mediated calcium influx, which subsequently exhausts the cellular antioxidant pool more rapidly.

The dose‐dependent increases in Casp‐3 and Casp‐9 observed here suggest that the disruption of antioxidant defenses leads to mitochondrial apoptosis even at early stages of oxidative challenge. Notably, the consistently greater pro‐apoptotic activity observed in NP‐ exposed groups compared with BPA‐ exposed groups suggests that NP exerts a comparatively more substantial neurotoxic effect. The TRPM2/PARP‐1 signaling axis is well recognized as a key pathway linking ROS elevation to caspase activation and subsequent apoptotic cell death (Çiğ and Yildizhan 2020; Yazğan et al. 2025). In line with this, our findings demonstrate that BPA and NP exposure induce dose‐dependent increases in both TRPM2 and PARP‐1 expression in TG cells, concomitant with heightened ROS production. When considered together with the study's OS (ROS, LPx, GSH, and G‐Px), ferroptosis (GPX4, SLC7A11, and TfRC), and apoptosis (Casp‐3 and Casp‐9) profiles, these results strongly suggest that TRPM2 constitutes a major regulatory component in BPA‐ and NP‐induced neurotoxicity.

One of the most novel aspects of this study is the demonstration that BPA and NP robustly activate the TRPM2/PARP‐1 signaling axis in TG neurons. PARP‐1, a key DNA damage sensor, undergoes overactivation in response to persistent genomic instability and strand breaks, generating ADPR that subsequently triggers TRPM2‐ linked cation influx (Yazğan et al. 2025). Our findings of dose‐dependent increases in both proteins suggest that BPA‐ and NP‐induced oxidative DNA damage may trigger PARP‐1 activity, contributing to TRPM2‐related Ca2+ influx. This mechanism is particularly notable because TRPM2 activation has been shown to amplify prooxidant generation, disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, and facilitate both ferroptotic and apoptotic signaling. Integrating our results with existing literature supports the interpretation that the TRPM2/PARP‐1 axis acts as a major molecular signaling pathway linking DNA damage‐induced signaling to regulated cell death and ferroptosis in TG neurons exposed to environmental phenols.

Consistent with neuroinflammatory responses observed in other brain regions (Gu et al. 2018), our results indicate that the TG is also highly susceptible to BPA‐ and NP‐induced inflammatory responses. Low‐dose BPA, NP, and mixture exposures significantly elevated IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α levels, whereas high‐dose exposures further amplified these increases, indicating a more substantial inflammatory stimulus at higher concentrations. Notably, cytokine elevations in NP‐exposed groups exceeded those observed in BPA‐exposed groups at both dose levels, suggesting that NP may exert a more potent proinflammatory effect on TG tissue.

Our findings delineate a multifaceted and interactive mechanism of neurotoxicity in which the TRPM2/PARP‐1 signaling axis functions as a major molecular signaling pathway. The dose‐dependent upregulation of TRPM2 and PARP‐1 provides a direct mechanistic explanation for the observed biochemical shifts; we propose that BPA‐ and NP‐induced oxidative DNA damage is associated with the activation of PARP‐1, which subsequently triggers TRPM2‐mediated Ca2+ influx. This ionic imbalance not only exacerbates mitochondrial ROS production and antioxidant depletion (GSH/GPX4) but also aligns with pro‐inflammatory signaling (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) to amplify cellular injury. The synergy between these pathways facilitates a transition from initial oxidative challenge to regulated cell death, effectively enhancing both ferroptotic and apoptotic pathways within the TG. Collectively, these results demonstrate that environmental phenols do not merely induce isolated damage but rather orchestrate a complex molecular cascade where redox imbalance, pro‐inflammatory signaling, and TRPM2‐mediated cation influx progressively drive sensory neurodegeneration through a self‐amplifying toxic loop.

Although the present study primarily focuses on characterizing the neurotoxic profiles of BPA and NP, our findings regarding the TRPM2/PARP‐1 signaling axis provide a plausible mechanistic rationale for potential therapeutic interventions. The dose‐dependent activation of this pathway suggests that TRPM2 antagonists (e.g., N‐acetylcysteine or specific channel blockers) or PARP‐1 inhibitors could serve as promising candidates to mitigate phenol‐induced sensory neurotoxicity. By identifying TRPM2‐mediated signaling as a major regulatory component of prooxidant accumulation to ferroptosis and apoptosis in TG neurons, this study establishes a necessary foundation for future pharmacological studies aimed at attenuating the ion‐dependent neurotoxic cascades associated with environmental contaminants.

Study Limitations

Despite the significant insights provided by this study, certain limitations warrant consideration. First, protein expression was primarily assessed through semi‐quantitative IHC scoring and ELISA assays.

Although Western blot (WB) analysis is often used to confirm specific protein cleavage (e.g., PARP‐1 or caspases), the high spatial resolution offered by IHC allowed us to specifically localize these changes within the TG architecture—a nuance that total protein homogenates in WB might mask.

However, we ensured the robustness of our IHC data through blinded evaluations by an independent histologist and utilized high‐sensitivity ELISA kits specifically designed for activated markers. Consequently, the high degree of correlation between our IHC and ELISA datasets provides a robust internal validation of the observed molecular shifts.

Future studies with larger tissue volumes may further validate these findings using complementary proteomic techniques.

Although interspecies differences in metabolism and immunity exist, the Wistar rat remains a robust model for sensory neurotoxicity due to the high conservation of the TRPM2/PARP‐1 and ferroptotic pathways. The outcomes observed here serve as a relevant proxy for assessing the potential neurotoxic risks posed to human health by environmental phenol exposure.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that simultaneous exposure to BPA and NP activates OS, ferroptosis, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses in TG neurons in a dose‐dependent manner, providing a comprehensive overview of the neurotoxic potential of these environmental phenolic pollutants.

The long‐term consequences of these molecular disturbances for pain transmission, somatosensory function, and neurodegenerative processes warrant further cellular and behavioural investigation.

A key finding of this work is the involvement of the TRPM2/PARP‐1 signaling axis as a major pathway that converts OS into both ferroptotic and apoptotic cell death pathways. This highlights the pathway as a promising therapeutic target for phenol‐induced neurotoxicity. Additionally, NP exerted more substantial neurotoxic effects than BPA across multiple parameters, underscoring the need to re‐evaluate environmental exposure risks.

Although human exposure levels often fall below the tolerable daily intake set by international authorities, the potential for nonlinear low‐dose biological effects, along with the emerging evidence that BPA/NP replacement analogues may share similar toxicity profiles, emphasizes significant limitations in current risk assessment frameworks.
