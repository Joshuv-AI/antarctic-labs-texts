Molecular diversity and population structure of Mediterranean fig (Ficus carica L.) germplasm: implications for conservation and climate-resilient breeding
Authors: Emilia Moisescu, Floriana Bonanno, Sevin Teoman Duran, Tiziano Caruso, Francesco Paolo Marra, Claudio Di Vaio, Rocco Mafrica, Rubén Jiménez-Canino, Silvia Fretto, Antonio Giovino, Marco Tolone, Gaetano Alessandro Vivaldi, Florin Stănică, Annalisa Marchese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe common fig offers significant potential for addressing agricultural challenges, particularly in climate-vulnerable regions of Mediterranean countries and beyond. Its ease of propagation makes it a low-input solution for smallholder farmers. Here, we present a genetic analysis of 351 fig accessions from Mediterranean and underrepresented regions, using 11 polymorphic Simple Sequence Repeats loci to assess genetic diversity and population structure.ResultsA total of 281 unique genotypes was identified, revealing substantial synonymy within the germplasm. Population STRUCTURE analysis detected three main genetic groups; however, overall structure was weak, with extensive admixture and limited geographic clustering, reflecting long-term human-mediated exchange and clonal propagation. These patterns were consistently supported by Principal Component Analysis and Analysis of Molecular Variance, confirming low genetic partitioning and the predominance of variation within genotypes. Importantly, the inclusion of Italian and, for the first time, Romanian germplasm expands current knowledge beyond existing genomic datasets, providing new insights into previously underrepresented genetic resources. The identification of rare and private alleles further highlights the presence of unique and potentially adaptive variation.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate that fig germplasm harbors high and largely unstructured genetic diversity shaped by extensive gene flow and vegetative propagation. This study provides a complementary perspective to recent SNP-based analyses, highlighting the practical advantage of SSR markers for detecting clonal variation and for the cost-effective, scalable, and easily comparable management of germplasm collections. The identified diversity supports targeted conservation strategies (on-farm, in situ, and ex-situ) and represents a valuable resource for breeding climate-resilient cultivars in marginal environments. These findings contribute to global efforts aligned with Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 13 (Climate Action).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09492-9.Keywords: Climate change, Common fig, Conservation strategies, Fingerprints, SSR markers

Introduction

The common fig (Ficus carica L., Moraceae; 2n = 2x = 26), domesticated earlier than millet and wheat [1, 2] and easily vegetatively propagated [3], represents a valuable crop for sustainable agriculture. Native to the Mediterranean Basin and Middle Asia [4, 5], fig is well adapted to arid and semi-arid environments due to its xerophytic nature [6], drought tolerance, and ability to produce under low-input conditions, making it particularly relevant for climate resilience and food security [7, 8]. Since ancient times, fig fruits have been consumed fresh or dried, enabling year-round availability and promoting the spread of the species through trade and cultural exchanges [9]. The domestication and spread of figs are closely linked to human migration and trade across the Mediterranean region. Today, global production remains concentrated in traditional areas such as Türkiye [10]. However, underutilized genetic diversity has hindered broader cultivation [11]. While North Africa, particularly Egypt, Morocco, and Algeria, plays a major role in international markets [9], other countries, including Iran, Spain, and southern Italy, also contribute significantly to production [12]. Despite its adaptability and historical importance, the global cultivation area has decreased from approximately 622,000 hectares in 1960 to 300,000 hectares in 2022 [9, 10], highlighting the underutilization of this species.

The species exhibits remarkable genetic diversity, estimated at 600–800 cultivars [4, 13], which represents an important resource for breeding programs. However, this diversity remains partially unexplored and difficult to manage due to issues such as synonymy and homonymy among cultivars [14], as well as environmentally driven phenotypic variability [15–17]. These factors complicate germplasm identification, conservation, and efficient utilization.

Molecular characterization has become essential to overcome these limitations, with Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR) markers representing the most widely used tool. Due to their high polymorphism, codominant inheritance, and reproducibility, SSRs enable reliable cultivar identification and genetic diversity analysis. Since the pioneering work of Khadari et al. [18], SSR markers have been extensively applied and further developed by Giraldo et al. [19, 20], Bandelj et al. [21], and subsequent studies [22–26], contributing to the establishment of standardized protocols and the valorisation of ex-situ germplasm collections.

Population genetic studies have revealed complex patterns of genetic differentiation shaped by domestication history, geographic origin, and human-mediated dispersal. Fig cultivation is also expanding into new regions, including Eastern Europe, such as Romania and Croatia. In Romania, fig cultivation reflects historical introductions through Greek, Roman, and Ottoman influences [27], with recent expansion driven by climate change [28]. However, the absence of registered cultivars and reliance on Adriatic-type parthenocarpy limit commercial development [29], emphasizing the need for genetically informed breeding strategies.

The fig’s dual role as a nutritional source and ecological keystone amplifies its societal value. Its syconium fruits—rich in antioxidants [30], polyphenols [5], and micronutrients [31]—address nutritional needs while sustaining over 1,274 wildlife species [32]. Nutrient-dense crop use on marginal lands and low-input fig cultivation in marginal agroecosystems also support Sustainable Development Goals, established by the United Nations in 2015, such as, respectively, SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 13 (Climate Action).

Despite its agronomic and ecological importance, several challenges still limit the effective management of fig germplasm. These include inconsistent nomenclature systems [14], limited availability of certified plant material, and phytosanitary constraints such as Fig Mosaic Disease (FMD), which restrict the safe exchange of genetic resources. Furthermore, the obligate mutualism with Blastophaga psenes wasps and the complexity of different cultivation systems (Smyrna, Adriatic, and San Pedro types) impose additional constraints on breeding and conservation efforts [33].

Although the increasing availability of molecular and genomic tools has greatly advanced fig research, significant gaps remain in the representation of global genetic diversity. Recent genomic studies have provided high-resolution insights into fig diversity across several Mediterranean collections – including Spain, Türkiye, and Tunisia [34]; however, germplasm resources from historically important Italian regions and emerging areas in Eastern Europe remain lacking. To address this gap, we analysed a broad collection of 351 fig accessions using a standardized SSR approach, including Italian germplasm and, for the first time, Romanian accessions. This strategy enables direct comparison with existing datasets and offers a cost-effective tool for large-scale germplasm characterization, facilitating the identification of locally adapted genetic resources.

Therefore, our study aims to characterize the genetic diversity and population structure of fig accessions from the Mediterranean areas and beyond, with particular emphasis on underrepresented Mediterranean and Eastern European germplasm. By improving the representation of these genetic resources, this work supports their conservation and utilization in breeding programs and climate-resilient cultivation systems.

Methods

Plant material

This study was conducted on 351 fig genotypes originating from various countries: 256 from Italy, 47 from Romania, 18 from the Canary Islands, 16 from Türkiye, 4 from Croatia, and 2 from Iraq. Furthermore, 4 control genotypes from California (Mission, Calimyrna, Kadota, Brown Türkiye) and 4 non-edible caprifigs were included (Additional file 1). Plant materials derived from various field collections: Fig Collection of the Faculty of Horticulture in Bucharest; Carboj Field Experimental Farm of the Sicilian Agricultural Development Agency in Castelvetrano (TP) and Germplasm Collection Field located at University of Palermo, Italy; Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry Fig Research Institute, Aydın, Türkiye; Collection of Native Campania Fruit Germplasm located at the Improsta Experimental Farm in the Campania Region of Eboli (SA), Italy; Pomona Gardens Botanical Conservatory of the Itria Valley (BR), Italy; Casello Demonstration Experimental Center of the Regional Agency for the Development of Calabrian Agriculture in San Marco Argentano (CS), Italy; Agrodiversity Collection in La Palma, Spain; CREA ex-situ Germplasm Collection Fields in Bagheria (PA), Italy; US Department of Agriculture located in Davis, California, USA indicated by letters a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, j in Additional file 1.

All plant material was obtained in accordance with institutional agreements and national regulations. Plant material consisted of accessions from different germplasm collections, without a formal experimental design. Given the geographical dispersion and heterogeneity of the collections, the study focused exclusively on molecular characterization, and no direct phenotypic comparison was performed.

Genomic DNA extraction

DNA was extracted from young leaves stored at -80 °C using a modified CTAB method [35]. Leaf tissue (20 mg) was frozen in liquid nitrogen and pulverized using a microdismembrator. After adding the preheated extraction buffer (2% CTAB, 1.4 M NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 100 mM Tris pH 8, 2% PVP 40), the lysate was incubated at 65 °C for 3 min. Chloroform alcohol was added, mixed, and centrifuged; the aqueous phase was transferred, and DNA was precipitated with cold isopropanol. After washing with ethanol, the pellet was dried and resuspended in TE buffer, treated with RNase A, and stored at -20 °C. DNA concentration and quality were assessed using a Nanodrop2000 spectrophotometer.

SSR characterisation

Molecular characterisation was conducted using a set of 11 microsatellite markers (SSR) to differentiate the studied fig genotypes, as detailed in Table 1. Four different MIX combinations were used in multiplex PCR (Table 1). PCR amplifications were performed in a thermal cycler using a final reaction volume of 8.5 µL containing 1.5 µL of template DNA (100 ng/µL), 4 µL of 2× Qiagen Master Mix, 0.8 µL of primers (2 µM), and 2.2 µL of sterile nuclease-free distilled water. The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 15 min, followed by 10 touchdown cycles including denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing starting at 60 °C for 1.5 min with a decrease of 1 °C per cycle, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. Subsequently, 25 amplification cycles were performed at 94 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 1.5 min, and 72 °C for 1 min. A final extension step was carried out at 60 °C for 30 min. PCR amplicons were analysed using capillary electrophoresis on a Genetic Analyzer.

Analysis of amplified SSR profiles

Amplified fragments were analyzed using the GeneScan™ 500 LIZ™ Size Standard (ThermoFisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with allele calling performed in GeneMapper® Software v4.0 (Applied Biosystems).

SSR allele data were recorded by fragment size and analysed as codominant markers. Allelic profiles were formatted according to the requirements of downstream software (e.g., CERVUS), without conversion into a binary matrix, ensuring full preservation of codominant information.

Diversity indices were computed: Number of alleles per locus (Na), Observed heterozygosity (HObs), Mean expected heterozygosity (HExp), Mean polymorphic information content (PIC), Combined non-exclusion probability (identity) (NE-I), Combined non-exclusion probability (sib identity) (NE-SI), Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HW) by using the statistical software CERVUS v3.0.7 [37, 38].

Structure analysis

Genotypic data were formatted according to the standard input requirements of STRUCTURE for codominant SSR markers. Genetic clustering was performed using STRUCTURE v2.3.1 [39] under the admixture model with correlated allele frequencies. After excluding 100 genetic duplicates (identical SSR profiles) and retaining 281 unique genotypes for downstream analyses, we tested hypothetical population numbers (K) from 1 to 10, performing 20 independent runs per K with a 30,000-step burn-in followed by 100,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations.

No prior population information was applied to constrain clustering. This approach was chosen to avoid introducing bias and to allow the identification of natural genetic structure within the dataset. The selected parameters (range of K values, burn-in length, and number of iterations) follow commonly adopted practices in SSR-based population genetic studies of perennial species.

The optimal K was determined using the ΔK method [40] implemented in pophelper R package [41], which evaluates the rate of change in log-likelihood [lnP(K)] between successive K values. The same R package, pophelper, was used to create the barplot for the STRUCTURE analysis at the best K value.

Multivariate and molecular variance analyses (PCA and AMOVA)

Genetic structure was further investigated using multivariate and variance-based approaches implemented in the R packages adegenet [42] and poppr [43]. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was executed on the allele dataset without scaling, as appropriate for codominant SSR markers. The principal components were retained based on their associated eigenvalues, and the relationships among individuals were visualized using scatterplots of the first two and first and third principal components. The proportion of variance explained by each component was calculated and expressed as a percentage of the total variance.

Genetic differentiation among populations was also investigated by computing the Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA), implemented in the poppr package [43]. The total genetic variance was partitioned into components attributable to differences among and within populations. The statistical significance of variance components was assessed using permutation testing with 999 permutations.

Results

Molecular characterization and genetic identification of cultivars

The 351 fig accessions were genotyped using 11 polymorphic SSR loci, revealing 96 alleles across all loci. After identifying 100 identical genetic profiles, 281 unique genotypes were retained for downstream analyses. Allelic richness per locus ranged from 6 alleles (LMFC26 and LMFC13) to 15 alleles (FCUP038_6), with a mean of 8.73 alleles/locus. Four loci (MFC3, FCUP027_4, LMFC19, FCUP038_6) demonstrated exceptional allelic polymorphism, while all markers exhibited near-complete genotyping success (99.97% loci typed). The codominant nature and high allelic diversity of these SSRs enabled precise discrimination of genetic relationships (Table 2).

Eight loci exhibited heterozygosity excess (HO > HE), while three (LMFC19, LMFC13, MFC9) showed heterozygote deficiency (HO < HE), suggesting potential null alleles. After Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05), 7 loci deviated significantly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (Table 2).

Polymorphic Information Content (PIC) values ranged from 0.304 (LMFC26) to 0.738 (FCUP038_6), with a mean of 0.549. Eight markers were highly informative (PIC > 0.50), and three were moderately informative (0.25 < PIC < 0.50). The combined probability of identity was 2 × 10− 8, confirming extreme discriminatory power, effectively eliminating genotype duplication risks.

Analysing allelic frequencies revealed rare and private alleles (Additional file 2). Among the studied fig genotypes, 29 rare alleles were identified, constituting 30.21% of the total, with 14 alleles being private, found in only one accession. The FCUP038_6 marker had the highest number of rare alleles (5) and the highest number of alleles per locus (15). In contrast, the LMFC26 marker had only two rare alleles, while LMFC13 showed no private or rare alleles. All primers except LMFC13 and MFC11 exhibited private alleles, with MFC2 and FCUP027_4 having three alleles each, and others having 2 or 1. A total of 13 fig genotypes contained private alleles, indicating unique genotypes that may be valuable for crop breeding and improvement (Additional file 2).

Principal component analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed extensive overlap among fig accessions, particularly in the central region of the ordination space, indicating a high level of admixture and weak genetic structuring (Fig. 3).

PCA (Principal Component Analysis) plot of fig accessions based on SSR markers. Points represent individual genotypes, colored according to geographic origin. The extensive overlap among accessions indicates high admixture and weak population structure

A large proportion of accessions, including many Italian genotypes, clustered in this central region together with several other individuals (Fig. 3). Notably, accessions related to the ‘Dottato’ group were also included in this cluster, confirming the polyclonal nature and genetic similarity reported for this cultivar complex.

On the negative side of PC1, Romanian accessions such as ‘Oradea 1’ (ORA1), ‘Sahateni 1’ (SAH1), ‘Nectar’ (NEC), and ‘Galben Mare’ (GALM), together with ‘Higuera Blanca’ (NBLA, Spain), ‘Schiava’ (SCH, Italy), and ‘Zimica’ (ZIM, Croatia), showed a more dispersed distribution, indicating higher genetic variability and suggesting relatively more differentiated genetic backgrounds.

Similarly, genotypes located at extreme values of PC2, including ‘Sarizeybek’ (SARZ), ‘Devetabani’ (DEV), ‘Halebi’ (HAL), and ‘Troiano’ (TROIT), appeared more isolated from the main cluster, indicating a relatively higher degree of genetic differentiation (Fig. 3).

A comparable pattern was also observed along the third principal component (PC3), accounting for about 7% of the total genetic variance, further supporting the presence of a limited number of more differentiated accessions within an otherwise highly admixed genetic background (Additional file 3).

Overall, PCA results were consistent with STRUCTURE analysis, supporting the presence of weak population structure and extensive genetic admixture across the studied germplasm.

Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA)

The AMOVA enabled to deeply explored the fig genetic variation, showing that it was partitioned both among and within populations (Table 4). The variance component attributed to differences among populations was statistically significant (Obs = 0.797, p = 0.001), indicating detectable genetic differentiation among groups.

Discussion

The integration of SSR-based genetic data with known historical and geographical information provides valuable insights into the conservation of fig genetic resources and useful knowledge to address two critical global challenges: (1) safeguarding agrobiodiversity in the face of climate change and (2) utilizing neglected crops for sustainable nutrition.

The 96 alleles identified across 11 SSR loci, including 30.21% rare alleles, of which 14 private alleles (Additional file 2), represent a vital reservoir of genetic diversity. The 2 × 10− 8 probability of identity underscores the high discriminatory power of the marker set and the uniqueness of the identified genotypes, which are at risk from urbanization and monoculture expansion. Overall, this value was higher than that of similar work exploring genetic diversity in fig germplasm collection [44].

Overall, genotypes carrying private alleles deserve particular attention in conservation programmes because they contribute disproportionately to the overall genetic diversity detected. Examples include ‘Storck’, ‘Irak 2’, ‘Higuera Boba or salvaje’, ‘Siyah incir’, ‘Devetabani’, and ‘Bursa Siyah’, all of which represent unique genetic resources within the analysed collection.

Private alleles represent useful tools for genotype identification, gene mapping, and the development of cultivar-specific molecular markers in Ficus carica.

The 100 identical genotypes revealed extensive synonymy and redundancy within the germplasm. Among Italian accessions, a large group of ‘Dottato’ genotypes showed high genetic similarity, supporting its polyclonal nature, likely shaped by somatic mutations, as previously reported by Mafrica et al. [45]. The ‘Dottato’ cultivar has ancient origins, dating back to Greek times, and was introduced into Southern Italy, where centuries of cultivation led to the emergence of ecotypes and synonymous varieties (e.g., ‘Kadota’, ‘Avvitato’, ‘Rigato’). Accordingly, several accessions identified in this study appear to represent synonyms or closely related variants within the ‘Dottato’ complex, while others likely correspond to somatic mutants, including dark-skinned forms (‘Dottato nero’) [45].

The remaining accessions—namely ‘Dottato Luzzi’, ‘Dottato Rocca Imperiale 1’, ‘Fico bianco cl4’, ‘Dottato Montalto Uffugo 1’, ‘Ottato bianco Pedivignano’, and ‘Dottato Francavilla Marittima’—display only minor phenotypic differences, likely resulting from local adaptation or environmental influences rather than reflecting genuine genetic divergence.

The wide diffusion of ‘Dottato’ in Italy led to the development of locally adapted ecotypes, some of which are recognized under PDO labels, such as ‘Fichi di Cosenza’ and ‘Fico Bianco del Cilento’ [46]. In particular, ‘Fico Bianco del Cilento’, a PDO product since 2006, represents a well-characterized biotype of ‘Dottato’, valued for its light skin, stable color during ripening, and sweet amber pulp [46].

Molecular studies further support the complexity of the ‘Dottato’ group, showing genetic relationships with other cultivars. For instance, Ciarmiello et al. [47] found a good correlation between the cv. ‘Fico Bianco del Cilento’/ ‘Dottato’ and the cvs. ‘Columbro bianco’ and ‘Ziuliedda’, while possible synonyms with ‘Avvitato’ [48] and identity with ‘Rigato’ [49] have also been described.

In other cases, genetic and phenotypic patterns were only partially concordant. For example, ‘Dottato nero Calabria’ and ‘Zingarello nero’ show strong genetic affinity despite slight morphological variation, possibly due to environmental effects or recent divergence.

Similarly, although synonymy can be excluded for ‘Fico Bianco CS139’ and ‘Fico Bianco CS157’, as well as for ‘FCS166’ and ‘FRC6’, their shared geographic origin and molecular similarity suggest a close genetic relationship or common ancestry [50, 51].

Additional cases of synonymy were observed (e.g., ‘Regina Nera’, ‘Rosso Triggiano’, ‘Zingarello Nero’), as well as groups of closely related genotypes sharing geographic origin and phenotypic similarity, suggesting common ancestry or local selection rather than true genetic distinctiveness.

Among Romanian fig accessions, several groups of identical genotypes were identified. A first group includes ‘Galben Svinița’ (syn. ‘Tri Kule’), ‘Fântânele’, ‘1 Mai’, ‘Ploiești’, ‘Oli Timiș’, and ‘Piața Obor’, showing > 90% similarity with ‘Otiman galben’ and ‘Galben Săvârșin’, characterized by medium-sized fruits with brownish-green skin and amber pulp [52].

Similarly, ‘Viișoara’ and ‘Otiman negru’ cluster with ‘Negru Svinița’ (syn. ‘Ada Kaleh’), ‘Viscool’, and ‘Smochin negru’, forming a group of highly similar genotypes (> 90%) producing small fruits with brown skin and red pulp [52].

Three additional clusters of highly similar genotypes (> 90%) were also identified, including (‘Mangalia 5’, ‘Mangalia 7’, ‘Oradea 1’), (‘Oradea 3’, ‘Tuzla’, ‘Tulcea 2’, ‘Săhăteni 1’, ‘Secuilor 1’), and (‘Brazi’, ‘MMM’, ‘Vera’ [syn. ‘Nectar’]), all characterized by medium-sized fruits with light green skin and amber pulp [52]. The occurrence of identical genotypes across different regions suggests that vegetative propagation by cuttings is the main driver of diffusion, likely due to climatic conditions limiting the activity of Blastophaga psenes.

Among fig accessions from the Canary Islands, two groups of identical genotypes were identified. The first includes ‘Higuera Cotia Negra’ and ‘Higuera Negra’, both originating from La Palma Island and characterized by medium-sized fruits with black skin and red pulp [53, 54].

The second group comprises ‘Higuera Gomera 1’, ‘Higuera Gomera 2’, ‘El Golfo’, ‘Yaiza’, and ‘Tinajo’, with the first two from La Palma and the latter from Lanzarote, all producing medium-sized fruits with purple skin and red pulp, derivatives of clonal propagation and local selection [53, 54].

The STRUCTURE analysis identified three genetic clusters (K = 3). While this subdivision highlights the presence of underlying genetic patterns, the overall structure remains weak, reflecting extensive admixture within the dataset.

Previous SSR-based studies have consistently reported high genetic diversity and limited population structuring in fig germplasm, largely driven by long-term human-mediated movement and clonal propagation [18, 36, 55], a pattern fully supported by our results. Notably, a similar configuration of three major groups with widespread admixture has also been observed in recent SNP-based analyses [34], confirming the robustness of these patterns across marker systems and highlighting the complementary nature of SSR and SNP approaches.

Information on fruiting habit (Unifera, Bifera, and San Pedro types) is available for a proportion of the accessions and is reported in Additional file 1. STRUCTURE clustering did not reveal any clear correspondence with fruiting habit, as Unifera, Bifera or San Pedro type accessions are distributed across the three inferred genetic groups.

Within this general framework, the three inferred clusters (K = 3) are better interpreted as broad genetic affinities rather than discrete populations.

Group 1 (K = 1), the most populous cluster, is predominantly composed of Italian genotypes, with the large group of well-known ‘Dottato’ genotypes, which can be considered a polyclonal cultivar, including ‘Kadota’, and accessions like ‘Calabrese’, ‘Gialloro’, and ‘Natalese’ alongside the Romanian ‘Negru Savarsin’. This group encompasses both green and purple figs, suggesting a shared lineage that may trace back to an ancient Mediterranean origin.

The high membership coefficients observed within this group (ranging from 0.794 to 0.982) reflect strong genetic similarity, consistent with extensive clonal propagation and widespread cultivation. The presence of both green and purple figs (e.g., ‘Fiorone Bianco Oria’ vs. ‘Fiorone Nero Oria’) further supports a shared genetic background, with phenotypic variability likely influenced by environmental conditions and local selection practices [56]. Only one non-edible caprifig ‘Caprifico Nero’ was found in this group.

Group 2 (K = 2) presents a diverse composition featuring internationally significant cultivars and regional accessions. This group prominently includes the iconic ‘Brown Türkiye’ (membership 0.960) and ‘Mission’ (0.938), alongside Romanian genotypes like ‘Mangalia’ and ‘Lunca’, and traditional Italian varieties such as ‘Verdone’. The historical complexity of these cultivars is particularly noteworthy.

The ‘Black Mission’ genotype, which has a pan-Mediterranean distribution and beyond, is believed to have originated from the Balearic Islands in Spain. Its widespread cultivation under various synonyms - ‘De la Reina’ (Spain), ‘Abeberiera’ (Portugal), ‘Noire de Caromb’ (France), and ‘Corvo’ or ‘Melanzana’ (Italy) - exemplifies centuries of agricultural exchange. The ‘Brown Türkiye’ cultivar shows disputed origins, with competing claims tracing it to Provence (France), Türkiye, or even Italy, though no definitive genetic link to specific Italian varieties has been established. This group’s genetic architecture, combining internationally distributed cultivars with regional varieties, is consistent with panmictic population patterns shaped by historical trade and modern cultivation practices also reported in similar molecular studies by Aradhya et al. [55], Ait Haddou et al. [36] and Ergul et al. [57].

Group 3 (K = 3), though smaller in size, exhibits pronounced genetic diversity and is primarily composed of genotypes from Türkiye and the Middle East (‘Yesitguz’, ‘Horasan’, ‘Sultan Selim’, ‘Irak’), together with Spanish and Italian accessions such as ‘Higuera Blanca’ and ‘Bianco Sicilia’. The predominance of Anatolian genotypes aligns with the domestication origins [1], while Italian representatives (51%) may reflect the influence of Bronze Age trade routes [58].

The clustering of Turkish and Iraqi accessions — regions considered primary centers of fig domestication — reflects deep genetic lineages shaped by millennia of localized selection. This group includes key cultivars such as ‘Sarilop’ (0.903) and ‘Bursa Siyah’ (0.955), both central to modern Turkish fig breeding programs. The presence of accessions from different geographic regions within this group further supports the role of long-term dissemination and local selection in shaping fig genetic diversity.

These patterns are consistent with Caliskan et al. [59], who identified multiple genetically distinct Turkish fig groups, while also highlighting subsequent redistribution of fig germplasm across the Mediterranean.

In a broader analysis of fig germplasm, Aradhya et al. [55] reported weak genetic structuring across Mediterranean, Caucasian, and Central Asian accessions, reflecting admixed ancestry, extensive gene flow, clonal propagation, and human-mediated dispersal. Similarly, Ait Haddou et al. [36] observed limited differentiation among Moroccan fig populations, with most variation occurring among individuals and high levels of heterozygosity, as well as in Eastern Adriatic Coast cultivated figs by Radunić et al. [24], where synonymy and local naming practices further contribute to the apparent complexity of population structure.

The PCA also revealed extensive overlap among accessions and the absence of clear geographic separation, supporting the interpretation that genetic variation is distributed along a continuum rather than partitioned into discrete groups. This pattern was further confirmed by AMOVA, which indicated that, although genetic differentiation among populations is statistically significant, its magnitude remains limited, with most variation occurring among individuals. Similar patterns have been reported in fig germplasm collections from the Mediterranean Basin, where extensive exchange of plant material and long-term vegetative propagation resulted in weak geographical structuring and high admixture levels [56, 60, 61].

The presence of genetically differentiated accessions, particularly those located at the extremes of the multivariate space, highlights the existence of valuable genetic resources that may be exploited for breeding and conservation purposes. Notably, accessions such as ‘Oradea 1’ (ORA1), ‘Sahateni 1’ (SAH1), ‘Nectar’ (NEC), ‘Galben Mare’ (GALM), ‘Higuera Blanca’ (NBLA), ‘Schiava’ (SCH), and ‘Zimica’ (ZIM), together with ‘Sarizeybek’ (SARZ), ‘Devetabani’ (DEV), ‘Halebi’ (HAL), and ‘Troiano’ (TROIT), showed consistent separation from the central cluster, suggesting more distinct genetic backgrounds. Similar observations have been reported in Tunisian, Turkish and Croatian germplasm collections were a limited number of highly differentiated genotypes disproportionately contributed to total genetic diversity [56, 62].

From a breeding standpoint, the weak population structure and extensive admixture indicate that gene flow among genotypes has been substantial, facilitating the combination of desirable traits such as fruit quality, adaptability, and stress tolerance, a finding consistent with previous studies highlighting the extensive plant material exchanges, frequent synonymy and centuries of clonal propagation of figs across the Mediterranean region [9, 23, 56].

In this context, molecular tools are essential for accurate cultivar identification and for distinguishing true genetic variation from synonymy or closely related genotypes. From an applied perspective, the diversity detected in this study may provide useful genetic resources for future breeding programmes aimed at improving adaptation to changing environmental conditions. However, the present work is based on neutral SSR markers and did not directly assess adaptative traits or stress tolerance.

Moreover, the presence of genetically diverse accessions in regions beyond the traditional core of fig cultivation, such as Romania, highlights the remarkable ecological plasticity of Ficus carica and adaptive potential, suggesting new opportunities for the expansion of fig cultivation into emerging production areas. This aspect is particularly relevant for the development of new markets under changing climatic conditions. The long history of adaptation to contrasting environments has previously been documented across the Mediterranean Basin and adjacent regions [9].

From a conservation perspective, these findings emphasize the need for efficient germplasm management strategies aimed at reducing redundancy while preserving unique genetic resources. The identification of distinct genotypes and private alleles is particularly relevant for the development of core collections and for the long-term conservation of fig biodiversity.

Conclusions

The identification and characterization of fig accessions in our study reveal high genetic diversity coupled with weak population structure, largely shaped by clonal propagation and long-term human-mediated dispersal. The detection of rare and private alleles, together with genetically distinct accessions, highlights a valuable reservoir of diversity that warrants priority conservation through integrated on-farm, in-situ and ex-situ strategies.

At the same time, the high level of synonymy and redundancy underscores the need for reliable molecular tools, such as cost-effective and transferable SSR markers, to support germplasm management, development of core collections possible certified at the phytosanitary level, and gene banks.

The substantial genetic diversity detected in the analysed germplasm provides an important resource for future breeding and selection programmes aimed at broadening the genetic base of cultivated fig and supporting adaptation to changing environmental conditions. Moreover, the presence of locally adapted genotypes offers opportunities for the valorization of traditional varieties and the development of niche markets, supporting both conservation and rural economies under changing climatic scenario.

Taken together, these findings highlight how the integration of genetic, historical, and geographical information can support both agrobiodiversity conservation and the sustainable valorization of underutilized crops such as fig.
