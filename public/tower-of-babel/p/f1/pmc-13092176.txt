A Prospective, Multicenter, Open-Label Assessment of Efficacy and Safety of the Quanta Dialysis System for Home Hemodialysis
Authors: Wael Hussein, Prashanth Kumar, Naveen Atray, Doreen Bett, Christopher Chan, Matthew Diamond, Gopa Green, Nupur Gupta, Bethany Karl, Andrea Kattah, Justin Merszei, Melvin Seek, Suzanne Watnick, Eric Weinhandl, Reid Whitlock, Lisa Bismarck, Kelley Gorbe, Paul V.J. Komenda
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Most patients with end-stage kidney failure in the United States receive facility-based hemodialysis (HD).1 Although home hemodialysis (HHD) provides more freedom, flexibility, and the opportunity for more frequent and longer dialysis sessions, HHD uptake in the United States is lower than peer countries.1

The Quanta Dialysis System (Quanta Dialysis System [formerly SC+]) is a modern, portable HD device with a touchscreen that displays instructions and guidance on resolving alarms2 (Item S1; Figs S1-S5).

We sought to determine noninferiority of efficacy and safety when the Quanta Dialysis System was used for delivery of self-care HHD versus delivery of HD in a facility.

Methods

This was a single-arm prospective interventional study set in 13 US sites from November 2021 to October 2023 (Table S1). Adults with end-stage kidney failure currently undergoing dialysis for ≥90 days were eligible if they were cleared by their physician, had a care partner, and suitable internet, electricity, and water supply at home. Internet connectivity was required as the system is connected to a cloud-based digital health platform (Item S2).

First, participants were administered facility-based HD by clinical staff 3 × /week, 4 hours each session, for a minimum of 4 weeks and a maximum of 8 weeks (in-clinic phase). While dialyzing, participants and caregivers were trained to use the Quanta Dialysis System at home. Starting at week 4, participants attempted self-care until their nephrology team considered them competent. If competency was not achieved after 8 weeks, they were withdrawn.

Participants then performed HHD 4 × /week, 3.5 hours each session, supervised by a professional for one transition week before performing HHD with their caregiver for 8 weeks without direct supervision (in-home phase). The difference of frequency and length reflects the typical standard of care in each setting. A blood flow rate of 250-400 mL/min and a dialysate flow rate of 500 mL/min were prescribed in both phases.

The primary efficacy outcome was a mean standardized weekly Kt/V ≥2.1 calculated using the Leypoldt chain3,4 (Item S3). The primary safety outcome was a composite outcome of device-related or procedure-related adverse events.5 Adverse events were assessed weekly and patients continually recorded adverse events between study visits (Item S4). Secondary outcomes included changes in biochemistry and hematology values, patient-reported outcomes (time to recovery after dialysis, EuroQol quality of life scores, Medical Outcomes Study Sleep Index Scale Scores, and Edmonton Symptom Assessment System Revised Renal Scores), and individual components of the primary safety endpoint (Item S5).

Statistical analyses were conducted on the evaluable population of participants who completed ≥75% of treatments. We evaluated Kt/V using the least squares mean estimate from a repeated-measures analysis of variance and assessed differences in counts of adverse events generating a least squares mean estimate from a repeated-measures Generalized Estimating Equations model with a Poisson distribution. A 95% confidence interval (CI) upper bound <10% was considered an acceptable adverse event rate. Detailed methods are presented in the Supplementary Material (Item S6).

Results

Thirty-four individuals were included in the evaluable population (Fig S6). One participant withdrew because of a possible device-related adverse event (thrombosis) related to a vascular access complication. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. Mean weekly standardized Kt/V exceeded 2.1 during the in-clinic phase (Kt/V = 2.4; 95% CI, 2.31-2.48) and the in-home phase (Kt/V = 3.0; 95% CI, 2.89-3.04) (Table S2). The composite adverse event rate was 6.3 events per 100 treatments during the in-clinic phase (6.3%; 95% CI, 3.02-9.60) and 5.2 events per 100 treatments during the in-home phase (5.2%; 95% CI, 3.29-7.11). The mean difference was 1.1 events per 100 treatments (1.1%; 95% CI, −4.65 to 2.43) (Table 2). Discussion

The results suggest patients experience adequate dialysis clearance and a low rate of adverse events with the Quanta Dialysis System and that self-care HHD is noninferior to facility-based HD for these outcomes. Compared with a previous study,5 our participants achieved a higher level of dialysis adequacy during the in-home phase but had a higher adverse event rate. Our cohort on average was older, more overweight, and newer to HHD.

In the United States, younger patients, males, and non-Hispanic Whites are overrepresented in HHD versus facility-based HD because of patient preferences, social differences, and health disparities.6,7 Because more intensive HHD prescriptions are associated with improved morbidity, mortality, and quality of life,8,9 equitable access to HHD should be a policy imperative.

The Quanta Dialysis System has several features that may increase HHD uptake but economic incentives discouraging HHD must change. Funding for dialysis providers is low for HHD training sessions and HHD can be financially burdensome for patients with potential renovations, installation costs, and increased electric and water bills.10

The main strength of the study was the cohort was representative of a US facility-based HD population. The main limitation of the study was the nature of the intervention precluded a randomized crossover design.

In conclusion, performing self-care HHD with a care partner with the Quanta Dialysis System is safe and efficacious. Future directions include assessing technique survival of the Quanta Dialysis System in the home setting over a longer period.
