Urinary Carbohydrate Antigen 19-9 in Children with Unilateral Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction: Does Bladder Urine Reflect Changes in Renal Pelvis?
Authors: Ashwin Rajkumar, Sangeetha Geminiganesan, Arunaa Sathyamurthy, Heera Tharanendran, Babu Ramesh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Aims:Urinary carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) has emerged as a promising noninvasive biomarker in the diagnosis of ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO) in children. However, whether bladder urine reliably reflects biomarker concentrations in the renal pelvis remains uncertain. The aims are to evaluate the diagnostic utility of urinary CA 19-9 levels in children with UPJO and determine if bladder urine levels reflect those from the renal pelvis.Methodology:This prospective case–control study included 72 children from July 2023 to June 2024. The control group (n = 38) comprised asymptomatic children with normal ultrasound, while the UPJO group (n = 34) included children with unilateral UPJO requiring pyeloplasty. Urine samples were collected from the bladder in both the groups and from the renal pelvis during surgery in the UPJO group and analyzed for CA 19-9 concentration using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Median values were compared using nonparametric tests.Results:There was no significant difference in age/sex distribution between the groups. Median CA 19-9 levels (U/mL) were significantly higher in the UPJO group (pelvic: 274.14; bladder: 281.22) compared to controls (3.78, P = 0.001). There was no significant difference between bladder and pelvis CA 19-9 levels in the UPJO group (P = 0.134). A moderate positive correlation was observed between pelvic and bladder CA 19-9 levels (r = 0.6055).Conclusions:CA 19-9 levels in bladder urine are significantly elevated in children with UPJO and closely correlate with renal pelvic levels. These findings support the use of bladder sampling as a noninvasive test in diagnosing and monitoring UPJO, potentially simplifying clinical evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Children with hydronephrosis undergo repeated imaging in the form of ultrasound and nuclear renogram to evaluate renal function, particularly if the drainage curve is equivocal. Multiple urinary biomarkers have been reported to be useful in the diagnosis of ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO) in children. Carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) is a complex glycoprotein marker (sialylated form of Lewis blood group antigen). Ten percent of the population lacks this antigen and is unable to produce this marker. Of the many urinary biomarkers studied in UPJO, CA 19-9 has been found to be particularly superior to other biomarkers in identifying children with UPJO.[123] Expression of CA 19-9 in healthy renal tubular cells is very rare. However, the level increases in hydronephrosis and decreases after the release of UPJO.[456]

In hydronephrosis, CA 19-9 is secreted by the urothelium of the renal pelvis, ureter, and bladder due to mechanical stretching, inflammation, or obstruction.[789] The pelvic urine, which is collected directly from the hydronephrotic kidney in one study, has shown higher CA 19-9 levels compared to bladder urine.[10] This might be due to the close proximity to the site of obstruction and stasis. However, sampling of the urine from the obstructed pelvis makes it an invasive procedure. The biggest question often asked by researchers is whether the bladder sample, which stores urine from both kidneys, can be representative of unilateral UPJO.

The first objective of the study was to compare the levels of bladder urine CA 19-9 in children with UPJO with the control group without any obstruction. The second objective was to determine whether the bladder CA 19-9 level correlates with pelvis CA 19-9 levels in those with unilateral UPJO.

METHODOLOGY

It is a prospective case–control study, conducted between July 2023 and June 2024 after getting the clearance from institutional ethics clearance (IEC-NI/22/JUL/83/63) at a tertiary care hospital. Children with normal antenatal ultrasound (Society of Fetal Urology (SFU) grade 0) and no urinary symptoms were included to study the baseline urinary CA 19-9 biomarker levels. These asymptomatic children were recruited from the general pediatric clinic as outpatients and grouped as controls (Group 1, n = 38). Children with unilateral hydronephrosis were included as study population (Group 2, n = 34). The sample size was estimated based on the studies reported by Atar et al. With 15% relative precision and 95% confidence interval (CI), the minimal sample size required was calculated to be 32 for the cases and controls.[11]

Children with severe hydronephrosis who worsened to SFU grade 4 on ultrasound, or with worsening of differential renal function (10% drop) and/or obstructive curve on renogram during follow-up, or those who became symptomatic (abdominal pain, infection, and hematuria) were considered to have UPJO, and these children underwent pyeloplasty. Children with bilateral hydronephrosis and other gastrointestinal or renal anomalies, such as horseshoe kidney, pelvic kidney, posterior urethral valve, vesicoureteric reflux, urinary tract infection, renal stone disease, neurogenic bladder, and previous surgery on the urinary tract, were excluded from the study.

A midstream clean-catch urine sample was collected from the control (Group 1, n = 38); wherever clean catch failed, a catheter sample was obtained. In the study group with UPJO (Group 2, n = 34), a sample from the renal pelvis (2A) was collected during surgery; a bladder sample (2B) was collected in all these patients by either clean catch/catheterization as in controls. The collected samples were transferred to a 50-mL conical centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 3000 RPM for 10 min. As biomarkers are low-molecular-weight proteins, they get concentrated in the supernatant, which was then aliquoted separately. All processed samples were transferred to cryotubes for storage at −80°C within 2 h of collection. Urinary biomarker CA 19-9 quantification was done using the solid phase sequential sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay technique (RayBiotech Inc., USA).

All the collected data, including biomarker concentration, were entered using Microsoft Excel (version 15.24) and analyzed using SPSS, version 26, IBM, USA. The data were not normally distributed when we tested it with the Shapiro–Wilk test, which yielded P = 0.02. Hence, nonparametric tests were used. The biomarker concentrations were continuous nonparametric data; therefore, they were expressed as the median and interquartile range. These results were represented in the form of Box and whisker plots. Differences in median values of urinary biomarkers between the groups were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and P < 0.05 was considered significant. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the medians of more than two groups.

DISCUSSION

Of late, there is growing interest in the utilization of novel urinary biomarkers in the noninvasive evaluation of children with suspected UPJO.[2121314] CA 19-9 is a sialylated Lewis A antigen produced in normal epithelial cells of the pancreas, bile ducts, stomach, colon, and kidney (particularly renal tubular epithelium and urothelium). CA 19-9 elevation in UPJO reflects epithelial stress due to obstruction and is modulated by coexistent infection or inflammation. It usually declines postrelief of obstruction, making it a potential adjunct biomarker for diagnosis and postoperative monitoring. CA 19-9 has been shown as the most superior marker in differentiation of UPJO from Non obstructive dilatation (NOD) in recent studies.[123] While urinary levels of biomarkers are often tested on a bladder sample, there is always a concern that whether they are representative of the actual biomarker elevation in the renal pelvis of affected UPJO. Dilution of marker levels from the opposite normal kidney has been a concern, and previous studies have shown that renal pelvis marker levels are significantly higher than bladder levels. Pelvic urine may reflect the localized production from the obstructed renal pelvis or ureter. Hence, the levels are often significantly elevated due to direct epithelial irritation. The bladder urine represents a diluted mixture of urine from the UPJO kidney and the normal kidney, hence the biomarker may be lower due to dilution and degradation over time. The current study demonstrated a significantly elevated level of CA 19-9 in both renal pelvic and bladder urine of children with UPJO, as compared to age- and gender-matched controls. Interestingly, while the difference between pelvic and bladder CA 19-9 levels was not statistically significant, both were markedly higher than in the control group, suggesting that bladder samples may effectively represent pelvic urine levels. This finding is of particular clinical relevance as it supports the noninvasive collection of voided urine for diagnostic and follow-up purposes.

Nabavizadeh et al.[5] reported that urinary CA 19-9 was an independent factor to predict the need for pyeloplasty with a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 70.9%. They proposed a CA 19-9 level of 52.6 U/ml in predicting failure of conservative management. Atar et al.[11] reported that urinary CA 19-9 level was higher in the UPJO patients compared to NOD. Kajbafzadeh et al.[10] reported a urine CA 19-9 cutoff of 30.6 U/mL with a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 82.6%. Geminiganesan et al.[2] revealed a significantly higher CA 19-9 level in UPJO compared to NOD and control group (P = 0.001) and proposed a cutoff of 95 U/ml for CA 19-9 in diagnosing UPJO with a sensitivity of 95% and specificity of 96% (area under the curve: 0.99).

Only one study[10] compared CA 19-9 levels between intrapelvic urine and voided samples. They noted a strong correlation between the two, advocating for the practical utility of voided urine CA 19-9 in both diagnosis and monitoring postpyeloplasty. The present study also showed a moderate positive correlation between bladder and renal pelvis levels of CA 19-9. Our study, by not finding a statistically significant difference between pelvic and bladder levels, supports the use of bladder samples, making biomarker assay a useful noninvasive test.

Kajbafzadeh et al.[10] also analyzed serum CA 19-9 levels and observed that relief of obstruction was followed by improved renal function, accompanied by a significant decrease in both urinary and serum CA 19-9 levels. There are two possible explanations for the correlation between serum and bladder urinary CA 19-9 levels and the presence of UPJO: CA 19-9 elevation could be part of a systemic inflammatory response that mimics UPJO-related changes, or CA 19-9 produced in the renal pelvis due to UPJO may be absorbed into the bloodstream and also excreted into the bladder, leading to elevated levels in both serum and bladder urine. Further in-depth research into the pathophysiology of CA 19-9 elevation in UPJO is needed to clarify this mechanism. However, both the present study and the findings of Kajbafzadeh et al.[10] suggest that CA 19-9 levels in bladder urine samples may serve as a useful marker for unilateral UPJO.

Despite promising findings, our study is not without limitations. The sample size was relatively modest, and while efforts were made to exclude confounding conditions, subclinical infections or variations in renal function might have influenced the biomarker levels. Moreover, longitudinal follow-up was not part of the current study, limiting our ability to assess changes in CA 19-9 levels postsurgery or correlate them with long-term renal outcomes. We did not include serum CA 19-9, which may have provided additional insights into the systemic versus local origin of the biomarker. Lack of normalization of biomarkers to urine creatinine as a ratio is another limitation; we could not do this due to erratic urine creatinine kits. Several studies, however, have reported that absolute values of biomarkers are as good as creatinine ratios.

Future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger cohorts and include longitudinal sampling to assess the prognostic value of CA 19-9 levels postpyeloplasty. Incorporating serum levels and correlating urinary CA 19-9 with imaging findings and functional parameters like differential renal function[15] could strengthen the biomarker’s clinical utility. The cutoff values for CA 19-9 in the diagnosis of UPJO are quite variable between studies, and standardization of this is a must to make the biomarker assay a universal diagnostic and prognostic tool.

Further studies should explore the potential clinical utility of bladder or urinary CA 19-9 as a surrogate marker for obstruction severity in UPJO. Specifically, a more detailed discussion is warranted on when and how CA 19-9 could replace or complement traditional modalities such as diuretic renography or serial ultrasound. If urinary CA 19-9 correlates reliably with functional obstruction in larger studies, it may help reduce reliance on radionuclide scans, thereby minimizing radiation exposure, especially in infants and young children. Additionally, if threshold values can be defined, CA 19-9 might offer a noninvasive, repeatable tool to guide conservative versus surgical decision-making in borderline or equivocal cases. Future studies should also evaluate cost-effectiveness, diagnostic thresholds, and integration into existing clinical decision pathways.

CONCLUSIONS

Our findings support the diagnostic potential of urinary CA 19-9 in pediatric UPJO, particularly emphasizing the feasibility of using bladder samples. This noninvasive approach could greatly simplify diagnosis and monitoring, potentially reducing the need for invasive sampling and imaging in certain clinical scenarios.
