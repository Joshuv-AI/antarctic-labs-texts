Effects of different dietary algal species on growth performance, meat quality, and intestinal morphology in broilers
Authors: Amr Abd El-Wahab, Dana-Carina Schubert, Svenja Starke, Marwa FE Ahmed, Carsten Krischek, Christian Visscher, Jan Berend Lingens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effects of incorporating different microalgal species into broiler diets on growth performance, meat characteristics, serum fatty acids profile, and intestinal morphology. Day-old chickens (240 male Ross 308 broilers) were randomly divided into six groups with five replicates each and eight animals per replicate up to day 35 of life. Five different experimental treatments were designed for the trial along with a control group receiving a basic diet (no additional algae). The five different algae diets (A1-A5) each incorporated 1% of the specific algae added on top of the basic diet. The algae included were Arthrospira platensis (A1), three different Chlorella products (different cultivation methods and processing stages; A2-A4), and Schizochytrium limacinum (A5).The final body weight did not differ between the groups (p = 0.467) and neither did the feed conversion ratio (range: 1.34-1.39). No significant changes among groups were observed in the histopathological parameters of the jejunum and ileum. When it came to serum fatty acids, most of them did not show significant changes among the experimental groups. However, γ-linolenic acid levels were significantly higher in groups A1-A4, which ranged from 2.81 to 3.56 µg/mL, compared to group A5 (1.27 µg/mL). Arachidonic acid levels were also higher in A1-A4 groups (216-257 µg/mL) than in group A5 (124 µg/mL). On the other hand, group A5 had much higher levels of docosahexaenoic acid (117 µg/mL) compared to the control and A1-A4 groups (16.7-18.9 µg/mL). In addition, the quality of breast muscle in birds fed different algae types showed distinct variations. Birds in group A4 had the highest b*-value for their breast muscle (12.1), while groups A3 and A4 displayed the lowest a*-values (1.13 and 1.18, respectively) compared to the control, A1, and A5 groups (2.19, 2.23, and 2.25, respectively). The results indicate that algae supplementation can be a valuable functional feed ingredient enhancing meat quality parameters with no effect on growth performance parameters. Schizochytrium (A5) is particularly effective in enriching broiler with omega-3 fatty acids (docosahexaenoic acid). Future studies should focus on determining optimal inclusion levels and investigating the long-term effects to maximize benefits while controlling costs.Keywords: Algae, Broiler, Body weight, Meat quality, Fatty acids

Introduction

In response to rising consumer demand for natural, organic, and sustainable farming, the farming industry is investigating the use of naturally occurring vitamins and minerals, as present in some microalgae, in animal feed as a new source of nutrients for health benefits (Saadaoui et al., 2021). One sustainable and potential solution to the challenges livestock farming is facing, is the use of microalgae as animal feed, mostly for poultry (Martins et al., 2021). As the cultivation of microalgae does not require arable land or potable water, it does not compete with human food production (Winckelmann et al., 2015). Moreover, microalgae are gaining increasing interest as sustainable feedstuff due to their ability to bind carbon dioxide through the photosynthetic process (Kiseleva et al., 2024). Furthermore, researchers are focusing more on producing chicken meat that is enhanced with beneficial components due to the chicken broiler's quick growth, the availability of its high-quality meat at low retail prices, and its quick responsiveness to changes in ration composition (Stangierski and Lesnierowski, 2015).

Microalgae are microscopic, photosynthetic single-celled organisms that naturally occur in aquatic environments (Dolganyuk et al., 2020; Pestana et al., 2020). They contain a diverse range of nutrients and bioactive compounds, including proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, vitamins, minerals, carotenoids, and n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) (Martins et al., 2021; Valente et al., 2021)). The aim of the present study was to explore whether selected algal species when included at practical levels can positively influence health- and performance-related parameters in broilers. This initial screening serves as a basis for identifying promising algae candidates that could be tested in future studies under commercial or challenge conditions where health benefits might be more evident.

Spirulina (Arthrospira) spp. and Chlorella spp. are the most utilized microalgal species in various industries, followed by the genus Schizochytrium (Abreu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2018). Arthrospira platensis has blue-green, prokaryotic, multicellular, and filamentous microalgae belonging to the group of cyanobacteria (Araújo et al., 2021). Arthrospira platensis has emerged as a promising source of protein, amino acids as well as polysaccharides and antioxidants (Holman and Malau-Aduli, 2013; Long et al., 2018; Park et al., 2018). According to Kang et al. (2013), Chlorella is a unicellular green microalgae that inhabits freshwater and contains a unique and diverse composition of functional macro- and micro-nutrients including proteins, polysaccharides, vitamins, and minerals. Beta-1,3-glucans (β−1, 3-glucans) are essential polysaccharides with immunomodulatory properties that play a significant role in poultry immune function and inflammation (Kang et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2023). Although comparative studies have investigated the effects of various microalgal species on different animals, particularly poultry, with a focus on fatty acid profiles, immune responses, intestinal morphology, and meat quality, further research is still needed (Boskovic Cabrol et al., 2024; Fernandes et al., 2024; Long et al., 2018; Park et al., 2018; Varzaru et al., 2024).

Schizochytrium spp. is known to be rich in PUFAs, such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (Valente et al., 2021). Up to 49% of DHA makes up the majority of the PUFAs in the algal oil (Ren et al., 2010). Furthermore, Schizochytrium spp. has gained increasing attention as a potential poultry feed supplement due to the high content of DHA and EPA (Park et al., 2015).

Chlorella and Arthrospira have been shown to positively affect performance data (An et al., 2016; Park et al., 2018), intestinal morphology and intestinal microbiology (Kang et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2023) as well as systemic and local immunological parameters (An et al., 2016; Tavernari et al., 2018) in poultry. Also, meat and egg quality could be influenced by some microalgae by improving the fatty acid profile (Coelho et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025) or increasing natural colorants, such as lutein or beta-carotene (Ritu et al., 2023), in food products.

Several studies have demonstrated beneficial effects of individual microalgal species such as Spirulina platensis and Chlorella spp. on broiler performance, immune response, lipid metabolism, and meat quality (Pestana et al., 2020; Coelho et al., 2021; Zampiga et al., 2024; Mendes et al., 2025), but not for Schizochytrium limacinum for example. However, most of these reports examined a single algal species under differing dietary inclusion levels, rearing environments, or genetic strains of broilers, making direct comparisons among species difficult. However, the particular effects of different dietary supplements of microalgae (Spirulina, Chlorella, and Schizochytrium), specifically on growth performance, lipid profiles, intestinal morphology, and meat quality indices in chickens, have not been studied under comparative experimental conditions so far and thus, species-specific effects of different microalgae have not been determined yet. Therefore, the present study aimed to comparatively evaluate different dried microalgal species in broiler diets to identify promising candidates for further application in broiler feed. The focus was to assess whether these supplements affect key performance, health and quality parameters, without compromising feed acceptability or growth, as a first step toward achieving a more targeted use under practical conditions.

Materials and methods

The experiments were carried out in accordance with German regulations. The animal experiments required no approval in accordance with the Animal Protection Act (§ 7, paragraph 2, sentence 3), as no interventions were performed before dissection. At the end of the trial, animals were killed in accordance with § 4, paragraph 3 of the Animal Protection Act exclusively to use their organs or tissues for scientific purposes. This was approved by the Animal Welfare Officer of the University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover, Foundation, Hannover, Germany (reference: TiHo-T-2023-1).

Animals and housing

The trial was performed at the Institute for Animal Nutrition of the University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover, Foundation with 240 male fattening broilers (as hatched, ROSS 308, BWE-Brüterei Weser-Ems GmbH & Co. KG, Visbeck-Richterfeld, Germany). Day-old chickens were randomly divided into six groups with five replicates each and eight animals per replicate. The six feeding groups were homogenously distributed in the stable to minimize the effects of location in the stable on the experimental results.

Each replicate of eight broilers was housed in a box (AviMax – modified, Big Dutchman International GmbH, Vechta-Calveslage, Germany) littered with 1 kg of wood shavings (GOLDSPAN®, Goldspan GmbH & Co. KG, Goldenstedt, Germany) so that the floor of the boxes was covered with shavings at least 1 cm high. The boxes were equipped with a diagonally mounted drinking line with two drinking nipples (Big Dutchman International GmbH) and hanging-type feeders (Klaus Gritsteinwerk GmbH & Co. KG, Bünde, Germany) providing feed and water ad libitum. Additionally, an infrared heat lamp was installed in each box. The stable was ventilated by a vacuum ventilation system installed on opposite walls of the barn above the boxes. The ambient temperature was maintained within an adequate range using space heating, and the infrared heat lamps in each compartment. During the first three days of the trial, the lighting remained switched on permanently. Thereafter, the 16 h photoperiod was set from 06:00 to 22:00, while from 22:00 to 06:00 there was dimmed night lighting for 8 h.

Experimental design and diets

The trial was designed with five experimental treatments alongside a control group (in total six groups). The control group was fed a basic diet without any additional algae, while the other five experimental groups (A1-A5) received diets with different algal species, each containing 1% of the respective algae incorporated into the basic diet. The algae used in the trial were Arthrospira platensis (A1), three Chlorella products differing in cultivation and processing (A2–A4), and Schizochytrium limacinum (A5). Arthrospira platensis is a photosynthetic cyanobacterium with characteristics of both bacteria and plants, cultivated phototrophically and possessing a relatively digestible bacterial-type cell wall. Chlorella sorokiniana (A2) was grown heterotrophically in the dark with added carbohydrates, while Chlorella vulgaris was cultivated phototrophically in both group A3 and group A4. Group A3 retained an intact cell wall, whereas in group A4, the cell wall was mechanically disrupted to improve digestibility. Schizochytrium limacinum (A5) was cultivated heterotrophically, also lacking both chlorophyll and a plant-type cell wall. The algal species tested in this study (Arthrospira platensis, Chlorella sp., Schizochytrium limacinum) were selected based on (i) commercial availability with documented batch consistency, (ii) established nutritional and safety profiles suitable for animal feeding, and (iii) relevance to current and near-term poultry feed formulations. While this approach favors translational applicability over exploratory novelty, it establishes a comparative benchmark for evaluating emerging algal strains in future work.

The 35-day-long trial was divided into three distinct feeding stages: a starter diet provided from day 1 to day 11, a grower diet provided from day 12 to day 21, and a finisher diet provided from day 22 to day 35 for each of the respective groups. This resulted in a total of six starter diets, six grower diets, and six finisher diets. It should be noted that the dietary algae groups (except group A5) had a greenish hue. The feed was obtained in meal form from a commercial feed producer (BEST 3 Geflügelernährung, Twistringen, Germany) and pelleted at the Institute for Animal Nutrition, University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover, Foundation, Hannover, Germany. Prior to pelleting, the feed was supplemented with algae. The feed mixture was based on wheat, soybean extraction meal, and corn. The chemical analysis of the basic diets can be found in Table 1.

Measurements

During the trial, technical performance, dry matter (DM) content of excreta and litter as well as food pad health were assessed. At the end of the trial, at day 36, three birds from each replicate (n = 15 per group) were randomly selected for further evaluation. Assessments included analyses of blood parameters, internal organs, and meat quality by means of dissection.

Technical perfomance

Birds were weighed weekly (on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35) as well as additionally at the time of feed changes if not done already at the same time according to weekly procedure (day 11). The data from the body weight (BW) measurement was used to calculate average daily weight gain (ADWG) per bird and day. Parallel to weighing the animals, feed and water intake were recorded to calculate average feed and water intake per bird and day, water-to-feed-ratio, and feed conversion ratio (FCR).

Litter and excreta sampling

Samples were collected once weekly at day 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35. Litter was obtained from the same three sampling points in each box, which were located on a diagonal line through the box, with two points at the corners and one in the center of the box. Fresh excreta from the top of the litter without wood shavings were collected to estimate DM content.

Foot pad dermatitis scoring

Food pad health was evaluated according to the foot pad dermatitis score (FPD) developed by Mayne et al. (2007), which ranged from 0 to 7. A score of 0 indicates a healthy foot pad when the footpad skin shows no signs of swelling or redness, whereas a score of 7 represents severe dermatitis, characterized by necrosis affecting more than 50% of the foot pad.

Dissection

The dissection of the animals (N = 90, n = 15) was carried out at day 36. Blood was collected into serum tubes (10 mL, 95 ✗ 16.8 mm, Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Nümbrecht, Germany) and was allowed to clot at room temperature before serum was separated by means of centrifugation (3000 ✗ g, 10 min). Serum was stored at −20 °C until fatty acid analysis. The left breast muscle was sampled for evaluation of meat quality. After opening the abdominal cavity, samples from jejunum and ileum were obtained for histological investigations. The liver, spleen, and bursa cloacalis were removed and weighed.

Meat quality

Evaluation of meat quality was carried out at the Institute of Food Quality and Food Safety. In the Musculi pectorales superficiales (MPS) 24 h after slaughter of the animals, the pH value, electrical conductivity as well as color values (L* - lightness, a* – redness, b* – yellowness) were determined. For pH measurement, a pH meter (Portamess® Type 911 pH, Knick Elektronische Messgeräte GmbH & Co. KG, Berlin, Germany) was used. Electrical conductivity (EC) was determined by using a LF-Star® (Matthaeus GmbH, Pöttmes, Germany). On a fresh cut of the MPS, color values were measured using a colorimeter (Minolta CR 400®, Konica Minolta GmbH, Langenhagen, Germany).

Blood analysis

Serum samples were analyzed to determine the levels of short chain fatty acids (SCFAs). The SCFA concentration was measured in the samples using a gas chromatograph (GC-2014, Shimadzu Deutschland GmbH, Duisburg, Germany). Therefore, the samples were mixed with distilled water at a ratio of 1:4 and centrifuged. Afterwards, the samples were mixed with internal standard solution (10 mL of phosphoric acid (17%) and 0.025 mL of 4-methylvaleric acid), centrifuged, and subsequently subjected to gas chromatography with a column temperature of 225 °C (injector: 220 °C, detector: 240 °C). Finally, the fatty acids were separated within a running time of 12 min and then determined by means of a flame ionization detector (FID).

Histological investigations

During dissection, tissue samples approximately 1 cm² in size were collected from both the jejunum and ileum to assess villus height and crypt depth. Jejunal samples were taken from the middle portion of the small intestine, whereas ileal samples were obtained from the area midway between the Meckel's diverticulum and the proximal cecum. The collected tissues were rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 48 h (Abd El-Wahab et al., 2020). Following fixation, the samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 µm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, following the protocol described by Slaoui and Fiette (2011). Microscopical evaluation of the histological slides was performed using a Zeiss Axioscope (Carl Zeiss Jena GmbH, Jena, Germany). The length of the villi was measured from the villus crypt junction to the tip of the villus. The width of the villi was defined as the distance between the crypt-villus junction, and the depth of the Lieberkuhn crypts was measured from the villus crypt junction to the basal lamina of the crypts. Additionally, the ratio of villus height to crypt depth was calculated (Abd El-Wahab et al., 2020).

Chemical analysis

The DM content and nutrients were analyzed by standard procedures in accordance with official VDLUFA methods (Naumann and Bassler, 2012). Samples were dried at 103 °C until weight constancy to determine DM. Crude ash was analyzed by means of incineration in the muffle furnace for 6 h at 600 °C. Content of total nitrogen was determined using the DUMAS combustion method (Vario max®, Elementar, Analysen-Systeme GmbH, Hanau, Germany). To calculate the crude protein content, the total nitrogen was multiplied with a constant factor of 6.25. The crude fat content was quantified using automatic hydrolysis and extraction with Hydrotherm and SOXTHERM 416 (C. Gerhardt GmbH & Co. KG, Königswinter, Germany). The crude fiber content was analyzed after washing the samples in diluted acids and alkalis. The starch content was measured by means of polarimetry (Schmidt + Haensch GmbH & Co., Berlin, Germany), while the Luff-Schoorl method was used for analyzing sugar. An ion-exchange chromatography (Biochrom 30+, Biochrom Ltd., Harvard Bioscience, Inc., England) was applied for determining amino acid content.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Analysis System for Windows, SAS® Enterprise Guide® version 9.3 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). For all parameters, the mean and corresponding standard deviation were calculated. Measurements such as feed intake, water intake, and DM content were evaluated at pen level. In contrast, variables including BW, FPD scores, and histological assessments were analyzed on an individual bird basis. Normality of data distribution was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that met the assumption of normality, differences among groups were evaluated using the Ryan-Einot-Gabriel-Welsch test (simple ANOVA). Not normally distributed data were checked for significant differences with a Kruskal-Wallis test followed by a Wilcoxon two-sample test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Dry matter content of excreta/litter and foot pad health

Data that explore the effects of using different algae types in the chicken diet on litter DM content are shown in Table 7. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of incorporating different algal species into broiler diets on welfare, growth performance, serum fatty acid profiles, intestinal morphology, and meat characteristics. The use of algae in poultry diets is gaining attention due to the potential benefits of algae-derived bioactive compounds, such as omega-3 fatty acids, carotenoids, and non-starch polysaccharides such as beta-glucans (Swiatkiewicz et al., 2015), in addition to their high protein level (Calder, 2017; Tavernari et al., 2018). The results of this study provide first insights and suggest areas where the effects may be subtle and require further investigation.

Feed intake and growth performance

In the current study, a significant increase in total feed intake was observed during the first week for group A3 (Chlorella) compared to group A2 (Chlorella). The incorporation of microalgae into the feed, resulting in its green coloration, may positively influence the birds' appetite and lead to increased feed consumption, ultimately contributing to higher body weight, as observed by Farghly and Abdelfattah (2017). Similarly, improved feed intake has been reported in poultry reared under white lighting conditions when offered red or green-colored diets (Rierson, 2011), which aligns with the findings of this study. Nonetheless, since all dietary algae groups (except group A5) also received green-colored feed, the effect of feed color alone cannot be isolated or confirmed in our study. Moreover, it is important to note that after the first week, feed intake across the different groups did not show any significant differences. The absence of significant differences in feed intake during the grower and finisher stages suggests that although certain algae supplementation may influence early feeding patterns, it does not seem to substantially alter the long-term consumption of broilers.

In the current study, there were no significant differences in BW during the finisher stage or overall experimental period, suggesting that the effects of Schizochytrium (A5) on growth performance (d 15-21) may not be sustained across all dietary stages. Our study found that, similar to Schizochytrium (A5), the other microalgae types (A1–A4) also had no significant effect on final body weight. Thus, the evidence of high growth performance connected with algae-derived compounds may be inconsistent. In the same trend, the FCR did not differ significantly across the dietary groups, which indicates that the algae supplementation did not substantially improve feed efficiency.

The addition of algae did not significantly affect the FCR of broilers, but it did enhance ADWG, suggesting that the changes in underlying physiology did not negatively impact the birds' performance (Kalia and Lei, 2022). However, high inclusion levels of Arthrospira platensis (14–15%) in broiler diets have been associated with reduced ADWG (Pestana et al., 2020). This may be due to factors such as lower protein digestibility or a sub-optimal amino acid profile of the algal biomass compared to the control diet. Moreover, it has been suggested that at higher inclusion rates, around or above 10%, the strong immune-stimulating properties of microalgae may divert metabolic energy from growth processes to immune function (Spínola et al., 2024). Toyomizu et al. (2001) observed that supplementing the diet with Arthrospira platensis at a level of 4% or 8% for 16 days starting at 21 days of life, had no significant impact on growth performance. Similar results were observed in other studies, where varying levels of Arthrospira platensis inclusion (ranging from 0.5 to 1% for 42 days (Bonos et al., 2016), 1.5-2.5% for 4 weeks (Zahroojian et al., 2013), and 6-21% for 21 days (Evans et al., 2015) had no notable impact on ADWG or FCR. Also, Yan and Kim (2013) did not observe any effect of including Schizochytrium in the diet at 10 and 20 g/kg feed on growth performance of Ross 708 chickens. Given that the inclusion levels in our own trials were limited to 0.5%, it is reasonable to assume that any potential reductions in digestibility would have been too minor to significantly affect performance outcomes such as ADWG or FCR.

This outcome is somewhat surprising, as previous studies have shown that certain algal species can improve FCR by promoting more efficient nutrient utilization or by influencing gut microbiota composition (Evans et al., 2015; Kang et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2020; Park et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2012). Adding various doses of Arthrospira platensis (0.3% and 0.5%) in broiler diets achieved a significantly high final BW at d 35 (2254 and 2289 g, respectively, vs 2168 g for control group) and a favourable FCR (1.58 and 1.55, respectively, vs 1.64 for control group) (Abdelfatah et al., 2024). Including 0.14% Chlorella vulgaris in the diet fed to 1-42-day-old Ross 308 broilers led to an improvement in FCR from 1.60 to 1.54 (Rezvani et al., 2012). When supplemented at a rate of 1%, Chlorella induced an increase in ADWG (1603 vs 1549 g/bird) in 28-day-old Ross broilers (Kang et al., 2013). Baéza et al. (2015) reported that 2% dietary supplementation with Schizochytrium spp. in Ross 308 broiler chickens improved FCR (1.67 vs 1.73) over the total rearing period (d 0-42), with no effect on final BW. Also, Ribeiro et al. (2013) demonstrated that the dietary supplementation with Schizochytrium at 74 g/kg between 21 and 35 days of age had a positive effect on BW (2048 g vs 1844 g) and improved the FCR (0.54 vs 0.47) in comparison with control broilers. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with 1% DHA-rich microalgae (Schizochytrium limacinum) achieved a significantly high BW at d 42 (2632 g vs 2380 g) and improved FCR (1.52 vs 1.60) of broilers (Long et al., 2018). Overall, the effects of algal supplementation on broiler growth performance appear to be highly variable and dose-dependent. While some studies reported improvements in ADWG and FCR with low to moderate inclusion levels, others showed no significant effects or even negative outcomes at higher doses. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in algal species, biomass quality, dosage, and bird age at supplementation.

Excreta and litter quality/foot pad health

The absence of significant effects on excreta DM suggest that the algal species used in this study did not directly alter the water retention or moisture content of the feces. Algae-based diets, particularly those rich in fibers or polysaccharides, may have the potential to affect gut motility or water absorption. However, also in a previous study of Abd El-Wahab et al. (2018), no significant differences were noted in the DM content of excreta and litter when the level of soyabean meal in the control diet was partly replaced by 4.0% micro-algae (Chlorella).

Although group A3 (Chlorella) showed a statistically significant reduction in litter DM content on day 7 compared to the other groups, the absolute values remained within a range that is not considered critical or problematic for broiler management at this age. Therefore, this observation likely reflects a minor and temporary fluctuation rather than a biologically relevant effect. Nevertheless, maintaining appropriate litter moisture is important in poultry production, as excessive moisture can contribute to poor litter quality and negatively affect foot pad health (Abd El-Wahab et al., 2013). However, the fact that no further significant differences in litter DM were observed later in the experiment indicates that any effects of algae on litter moisture were likely transient or influenced by other factors as the trial progressed.

It is well known that high concentrations of non-starch polysaccharides (NSPs) can contribute to increased digesta viscosity, which in turn may affect nutrient absorption and litter moisture content (Pestana et al., 2020). Specifically, arabinoxylans are known to enhance digesta viscosity, potentially slowing passage rate and impairing nutrient uptake (Bedford and Classen, 1992; van Krimpen et al., 2017). Viscosity can also increase water retention in the digesta, leading to sticky droppings and wetter litter (Choct and Annison, 1990; Knudsen, 2014).

However, since NSP content and digesta viscosity were not measured in this study, we cannot confirm whether such mechanisms might play a role. Given the relatively low inclusion levels of microalgae used in this experiment, it is unlikely that NSP-like substances had a measurable effect on litter DM content. Such mechanisms may become relevant only at higher inclusion levels. It may be worthwhile in future studies to investigate whether specific components in the algal biomass, such as NSP-like substances, influence gut viscosity and, in turn, litter quality - particularly when used at elevated dosages. In the current experiment, no indications of such effects were observed. Foot pad dermatitis scores remained low (≤ 1.0) across all groups throughout the trial, indicating generally good litter quality and bird welfare. While slight improvements were observed in some algae-supplemented groups at early time points, these differences are not considered biologically relevant, as all scores reflected only minimal changes, such as slight redness or swelling without necrotic lesions (Mayne et al., 2007). Under the controlled experimental conditions of this study, foot pad health was consistently maintained. However, potential effects of algae supplementation on FPD may be more relevant under commercial or field conditions with higher environmental challenges.

Meat quality parameters

Meat color is among the most critical quality attributes that influence the consumer acceptance (Lee et al., 2022; Yue et al., 2024). Preferences for meat color vary widely, based on factors such as geographic location, dietary habits, and environmental conditions (Altmann et al., 2023). In this study, the inclusion of algae, with the exception of Schizochytrium limacinum, in the diet significantly influenced the color properties, especially the a* and b* values, of the breast meat after slaughter. The a* values are significantly influenced by the addition of Chlorella vulgaris (A3, A4) with lower a* values compared to the breast muscles of nearly all other groups (not A2). In addition, the higher yellowness results in the MPS of the Chlorella vulgaris fed birds, which are probably due to the deposition of algal pigments into the meat, support the clear impact of this algae type on the meat color results. The b* results principally agree with the studies of Alfaia et al. (2021) and Pestana et al. (2020) who demonstrated that the inclusion of 10% C. vulgaris and 15% Arthrospira in a broiler diet led to a significant increase of the chlorophyll and carotenoid contents of the breast muscles compared to controls. Since animals cannot synthesize chlorophylls or carotenoids de novo (Maoka, 2020), these pigments in the breast muscle are likely derived from the dietary source. While the carotenoids in the control group were primarily obtained from corn (rich in xanthophylls), the pigments in the muscles of the Chlorella fed birds, which resulted in higher b* values of the breast muscles, originated mainly from these microalgae.

Microalgae can provide around 200 different carotenoids (Banskota et al., 2019), with some being present in higher concentrations compared to soybean meal (Gohara-Beirigo et al., 2022). This supports the use of microalgae in poultry feed as a sustainable and natural alternative to synthetic pigments and vitamins, offering higher bioavailability (Martins et al., 2021; Saadaoui et al., 2021). Among carotenoids, the xanthophyll subgroup, characterized by hydroxyl groups, is particularly relevant to poultry nutrition. Important xanthophylls include astaxanthin, β-carotene, canthaxanthin, and lutein, which can impart a yellowish color to poultry meat. However, very low levels of microalgae inclusion (0.05% to 0.5%) have been found insufficient to produce noticeable changes in meat color (An et al., 2016). In line with carotenoid deposition, a more intense yellow coloration of beaks and shanks was also observed in algae-fed birds in the present study, (data not shown), further supporting the role of microalgae as a pigment source in poultry diets.

As chlorophylls and carotenoids play essential roles as vitamin precursors, antioxidants, immunity boosters, and anti-inflammatory agents, possible higher contents of these additional functional ingredients in breast muscles, like in the present study, might offer potential (health) benefits for consumers (Alfaia et al., 2021; Christaki et al., 2015). However, beside these possible health benefits the color differences, especially the yellow appearance, might influence the buying behaviors of the consumers. For example, Altmann et al. (2023) presented that the yellow color of corn fed chicken reduces the sensory overall liking results.

Unlike the b*-value, which increased with the addition of Chlorella, a* values in breast muscles of Chlorella vulgaris fed chicken were lower compared to the other groups. The a* values are influenced by the myoglobin content and the percentages of the myoglobin redox forms (Cabrol et al., 2022). It is unlikely that the myoglobin content was influenced by the diet. However, as the chlorophylls and carotenoids have an impact on the oxidative metabolism, an influence on the redox form percentages chould considered (Alfaia et al., 2021; Christaki et al., 2015). Another reason for the a* reductions could be the high chlorophyll content of Chlorella vulgaris (approximately 2000-2500 mg/100 g), which can impart the green hue and as red and green colors work in opposition in colorimetric measurements, increased chlorophyll deposition in muscle tissue may have reduced the redness values in these groups. The latter assumption is partly supported by the fact that algae like Arthrospira platensis and Chlorella sorokiniana contain significantly lower chlorophyll concentrations (around 1000 mg/100 g), which may explain the higher redness values compared to the muscles of Chlorella vulgaris fed birds. Further investigations are therefore useful. Furthermore, elevated redness in other treatments may be associated with higher iron and mineral content (Kalia and Lei, 2022; Toyomizu et al., 2001).

The post-mortem pH value is an important quality parameter of meat. Right after slaughter, meat typically has a pH of around 7, which decreases to approximately 5.7 to 6.0 in chicken breast muscles (Hertanto et al., 2018). Higher or lower pH values might be associated with quality differences considering parameters like the color, texture or water holding capacities. The comparable pH values in the present study indicate no quality differences. The effects of the diet on the electrical conductivity values of the breast muscles of Chlorella vulgaris fed animals compared to the other groups indicate that the Chlorella vulgaris supplementation affectedmeat quality , as higher muscle conductivity is often associated with changes in muscle fiber composition, which could have implications for meat tenderness, juiciness, or overall consumer acceptance (Joo et al., 2013). However, as higher EC values are often negatively correlated to lower pH values and vice versa (Joo et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2022), the missing pH effect is not understandable. As unpublished data from another study showed that adding Chlorella vulgaris to the chicken diet also resulted in higher EC values and comparable pH values of the breast muscles, further investigations are useful to clarify these questions.

Serum fatty acid profiles

Microalgal lipids are receiving growing interest from the food industry as an edible plant and fish oil substitute and as an alternative source of PUFAs such as omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, which provide health benefits when used as a dietary supplement (Ghiffary et al., 2019). Regarding polyunsaturated fatty acids in microalgae, DHA and EPA are important. These are essential for animal nutrition, as they have antioxidant and other beneficial properties (Al-Khalaifah and Al-Nasser, 2021; Gohara-Beirigo et al., 2022).

In the present study, the analysis of serum fatty acid levels revealed significant differences in certain fatty acids between the experimental groups. Notably, group A5 (Schizochytrium) had the lowest γ-linolenic acid levels, while groups A1-A4 exhibited higher concentrations of this fatty acid. This result highlights the specific effects of different algal species on fatty acid metabolism in broilers.

Interestingly, only the group supplemented with Schizochytrium (A5) showed significantly lower levels of arachidonic acid compared to the control group, while no significant differences were observed in the other algae-supplemented groups (A1-A4). Arachidonic acid is a precursor to pro-inflammatory eicosanoids (Pereira et al., 2024; Sala et al., 2018), and its reduction in the A5 group may suggest a potential anti-inflammatory effect of Schizochytrium-based supplementation. This aligns with previous studies highlighting the high DHA content of Schizochytrium and its ability to modulate inflammatory pathways. In contrast, Chlorella and Arthrospira platensis supplementation did not significantly alter Arachidonic acid levels in this study. The notable increase in DHA levels in group A5 (Schizochytrium) aligns not only with the known fatty acid profile of this microalga-rich in omega-3 fatty acids, but also confirms previous findings that dietary Schizochytrium supplementation increases DHA deposition in poultry tissues (Long et al., 2018; Tolba et al., 2019). Omega-3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, are associated with numerous health benefits such as improved cardiovascular function and anti-inflammatory effects in human (Banaszak et al., 2024; Calder, 2016; Sun et al., 2018). The elevated DHA levels observed in the birds' serum may therefore not only enhance the nutritional value of the meat for human consumption but could also positively influence the birds' own health status by modulating inflammatory responses. This is further supported by the concurrent reduction in arachidonic acid levels, a known precursor of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids. This increase in DHA could also have implications for the nutritional value of broiler meat, potentially enhancing its health-promoting qualities, particularly in terms of fat composition (Schreiner et al., 2005). Schizochytrium spp., the marine algae utilized in the present study, is a rich source not only of ω−3 FAs (especially DHA) but also of antioxidants. Schizochytrium spp. have been generally recognized as safe for use in chicken feed at levels up to 2.8% for broilers and 4.7% for layers (Baldwin, 1997).

Although the present dataset does not include molecular endpoints, several plausible mechanisms may explain the observed physiological changes. Algae-derived long-chain ω−3 and ω−6 PUFAs (EPA, DHA) can modulate hepatic and adipose lipid metabolism via peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor signaling, alter membrane phospholipid composition, and influence eicosanoid synthesis, thereby affecting inflammatory tone and metabolic efficiency (Da Cosat et al., 2016; Khan et al., 2018). Carotenoids (e.g., astaxanthin, lutein, β-carotene) and phenolic compounds present in algae may reduce oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species and upregulating endogenous antioxidant enzyme systems, potentially preserving intestinal barrier integrity and liver function (Magnuson et al., 2018). Algal polysaccharides (e.g., β-glucans, sulfated polysaccharides) may act as prebiotics, modulating cecal and ileal microbiota composition, increasing production of short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate), and enhancing gut-associated lymphoid tissue immune competence (Liu et al., 2021). Species-specific differences in cell wall structure and secondary metabolite profiles may further explain differential efficacy. Future mechanistic studies should integrate targeted lipidomics and fatty acid profiling, hepatic and intestinal transcriptomics (RNA-seq), serum cytokine and acute-phase protein quantification, antioxidant enzyme activity assays (plasma and tissue), 16S rRNA gene sequencing of cecal microbiota with predicted functional metagenomic analysis, intestinal morphometry and histopathology, and in vitro digestibility and fermentation assays to substantiate these hypothesized pathways and identify bioactive fractions responsible for observed effects.

Intestinal morphology

As the primary site of digestion and nutrient absorption in broilers, the small intestine plays a critical role in overall health and performance. Therefore, assessing its structural and functional integrity is essential. Longer villi along with a higher ratio of villus height to crypt depth are considered crucial indicators of gastrointestinal health, as they are linked to a greater capacity of nutrient absorption (Adil et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2020). A deeper crypt may indicate faster tissue turnover to permit renewal of the villus, which suggests that the host's intestinal response mechanism is trying to compensate for normal sloughing or atrophy of villus due to inflammation from pathogens and their toxins (Baurhoo et al., 2007; Gao et al., 2008). The ratio of villus height to crypt depth is a useful criterion for estimating the digestive capacity in the small intestine (Montagne et al., 2003). It may provide a protective function to mucosa by preventing pathogens from binding to the villus and allowing fewer antigens to be in contact with the villus.

The lack of significant differences in intestinal morphology across the experimental groups suggests that algae supplementation did not markedly alter the gut histopathological investigations of broilers in this study. This finding is somewhat surprising, as certain algal species are known to exert prebiotic effects on gut health by promoting beneficial microbial populations or by providing fiber that supports gut motility. It has been demonstrated that inclusion of Chlorella in diets (10 g/kg diet) increases villus height and crypt depth in jejunal and ileal sections (Mirzaie et al., 2020). Another study showed an improvement in these parameters after administering extracts enriched with Arthrospira platensis at a dose of 1 g/kg diet (Chang et al., 2021). Furthermore, villus height was higher (linear and quadratic, p < 0.05) and the villus height:crypt depth ratio lower (quadratic, p < 0.05) with an inclusion level of Chlorella in diets (prepared by supplementing 25, 50, or 75 g/kg of Chlorella by-product to the basal diet) compared to the non-supplemented group (Kang et al., 2017). However, it is possible that the level of algae added to the diet was not enough to observe significant changes in gut structure. Further research, perhaps with higher or longer supplementation periods or more targeted algal species, could provide more definitive insights into the potential effects of algae on gut morphology.

Environmental and economic considerations

Environmental and economic considerations are critical for translating these results to commercial practice. Our study focused on biological endpoints; therefore, we did not perform cradle-to-farm-gate life-cycle assessment (LCA) or techno-economic analysis (TEA). The environmental footprint and cost competitiveness of algal feed ingredients depend strongly on cultivation method (open ponds vs. photobioreactors; phototrophic vs. heterotrophic), energy source, downstream processing (drying, milling), transport logistics, and co-product valorization strategies. Key metrics that should be evaluated in future LCA/TEA work include: greenhouse gas emissions (CO₂e kg⁻¹ chicken produced), cumulative energy demand, freshwater consumption, land use, eutrophication potential, and production cost per kg of dried algal biomass. Additionally, displacement credits for replaced conventional ingredients (e.g., soybean meal, synthetic pigments) should be factored into both environmental and economic models. We recommend targeted LCA and TEA studies, conducted in parallel with scale-up feeding trials, to identify production scenarios and algal species for which inclusion in poultry diets is both environmentally sustainable and economically competitive. Such analyses will be essential for industry adoption and should account for regional variations in energy grids, water availability, and feedstock markets.

Conclusions

This study was conducted under controlled conditions with a rather typical baseline level of broiler performance, which may have limited the observable impact of algae supplementation on parameters such as growth performance and intestinal morphology. Nevertheless, the study successfully met its objective of comparatively assessing different microalgal species. Distinct biological effects were observed between algal species, particularly in terms of serum fatty acid profiles and meat coloration. For instance, Schizochytrium led to a marked increase in DHA and a decrease in arachidonic acid, suggesting potential anti-inflammatory effects, while Chlorella influenced yellowness and redness values in breast muscle. Although no clear performance improvements were observed under these optimal rearing conditions, algal supplementation may still hold potential in more challenging or commercial environments to improve animal health, performance, or product quality. To better assess performance-related effects, future studies should include more detailed timepoint-specific measurements, should test additional inclusion levels, and further explore the species-specific impacts of different microalgae.
