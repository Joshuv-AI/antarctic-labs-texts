BIBSNet: A deep learning baby image brain segmentation network for MRI scans
Authors: Timothy J Hendrickson, Paul Reiners, Lucille A Moore, Jacob T Lundquist, Begim Fayzullobekova, Anders J Perrone, Erik G Lee, Julia Moser, Trevor KM Day, Dimitrios Alexopoulos, Martin Styner, Omid Kardan, Taylor A Chamberlain, Anurima Mummaneni, Henrique A Caldas, Brad Bower, Sally Stoyell, Tabitha Martin, Sooyeon Sung, Ermias A Fair, Kenevan Carter, Jonathan Uriarte-Lopez, Amanda R Rueter, Essa Yacoub, Monica D Rosenberg, Christopher D Smyser, Jed T Elison, Alice Graham, Damien A Fair, Eric Feczko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesBrain tissue segmentation of infant magnetic resonance (MR) images is important for studying typical and atypical brain development. The infant brain undergoes rapid changes throughout the first years of postnatal life, making tissue segmentation difficult for most existing algorithms. We introduce a deep neural network BIBSNet (Baby and Infant Brain Segmentation Neural Network), an open-source model for robust and generalizable brain tissue segmentation leveraging data augmentation and a large sample size of manually annotated images.Experimental designModel training included MR brain images from 90 participants with an age range of 0–8 months (median age 4.6 months). Using manually annotated real images along with synthetic segmentation images produced using SynthSeg, the model was trained using a 10-fold procedure. Model performance was assessed by comparing BIBSNet, and joint label fusion (JLF) inferred segmentations to ground truth segmentations, and an ad-hoc analysis with iBeat inferred segmentation, using Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC). Additionally, MR data along with the FreeSurfer compatible segmentations were processed with the DCAN labs infant-ABCD-BIDS processing pipeline from ground truth, JLF, and BIBSNet to produce anatomical and resting state functional derivatives to further assess model performance on processed derivatives.Principal observationsBIBSNet outperforms JLF based on DSC comparisons especially with gray matter (BIBSNet = 0.849, JLF = 0.713) and white matter (BIBSNet = 0.862, JLF = 0.791). Additionally, with processed derived metrics, BIBSNet inferred segmentations outperforms JLF inferred segmentations across nearly all anatomical and functional metrics. Ad-hoc analyses of cortical segmentations – iBeat does not perform subcortical segmentations – showed that there is no significant difference between iBeat and BIBSNet segmentation for infants 0–5 months, but iBeat performed significantly better for infants 6–8 months.ConclusionsBIBSNet shows marked improvement over JLF across all age groups analyzed. The BIBSNet model is 600x faster compared to JLF at segmentation inference, produces FreeSurfer-compatible segmentation labels, and can be easily included in other processing pipelines. BIBSNet provides a viable alternative for segmenting the brain in the earliest stages of development.Keywords: Infancy, MRI, Deep Learning, Anatomical Brain Tissue Segmentation, Processing Methods

Introduction

The first years of postnatal life are marked by significant neuronal development through synapse growth, myelination of axons, and programmed cell death (Marsh et al., 2008, Bethlehem et al., 2022). Converging evidence suggests that neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders throughout the lifespan are influenced by atypical brain development during this highly dynamic period (Hazlett et al., 2005, Hazlett et al., 2017, Gilmore et al., 2010, Wolff et al., 2015, Shen et al., 2022). This has led to major investments to characterize brain trajectories during this sensitive period, namely the PING, dHCP, and YOUth cohort studies (Jernigan et al., 2016, Edwards et al., 2022, Hughes et al., 2017) and the recently launched Healthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study (Nelson et al., 2024) ™ - a large (N ∼ 7200) multi-site, longitudinal, effort to examine the developing brain from 0 to 10 y to study typical and atypical brain development.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a non-invasive tool that can be used to study developmental brain health and disease. It is capable of producing multiple types of MR data, including diffusion, spectroscopy, functional, and quantitative MRI data (Le Bihan et al., 1986, Ackerman et al., 1980, Ogawa et al., 1990, Tofts and du Boulay, 1990). Most analysis strategies across all of these modalities depend on well-annotated, or segmented, MRI data - that is, data that delineates various brain tissues. Traditionally, structural MR images (i.e., T1-weighted (T1w) or T2-weighted (T2w) anatomical images) are used to create annotations that segment tissue types, such as white matter, gray matter, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and subcortical structures (Li et al., 2019). Accurate segmentation of cortical gray matter is also necessary to produce more advanced morphological metrics, such as cortical thickness, surface area, and gyrification (Dale et al., 1999). Finally, other modalities such as functional MRI (fMRI) and diffusion MRI (dMRI) rely on accurate segmentations to produce more computationally sophisticated metrics like functional or structural connectivity (Glasser et al., 2013, Fair et al., 2020, Feczko et al., 2021, Kardan et al., 2022, Nielsen et al., 2023). Studies such as HBCD require efficient and reliable methods to conduct this critical step required for many derived measures of MRI.

Automated brain tissue segmentation algorithms often rely on high-resolution (e.g. ∼1mm3 or smaller) T1w and T2w anatomical images to annotate tissue types. These algorithms depend on voxel contrast and intensity differences across differing brain tissue and regions to delineate brain tissue and region boundaries. Brain tissue and region boundaries are, for the most part, easily delineated within the adult and child brain; however, they are often less accurate in infant data. This difficulty is due to the significant changes that the brain undergoes during the first years of postnatal life, such as myelination (Marsh et al., 2008, Huttenlocher and Dabholkar, 1997, Levitt, 2003). For example, T1w gray matter voxel contrast is greater than T1w white matter voxel contrast in infants 0–3-months (Fig. 4, Supplemental Figure 1), T1w gray matter and T1w white matter are approximately equal in contrast from about 3–6 months causing the tissues to look very similar (Fig. 4, Supplemental Figure 1), and at > 6 months and on the T1w gray matter is less than T1w white matter, emulating the tissue contrast of an adult brain (Li et al., 2019, Paus et al., 2001, Dolz et al., 2020). Common infant processing pipelines such as Infant Freesurfer and dHCP are restricted to only using a T1 or a T2, and therefore unsuitable for brain tissue segmentation across infant development from newborn to > 6 months.

Standard infant MRI processing pipelines across development, fMRIPrep Lifespan (Goncalves et al., 2025) and Developmental Cognition and Neuroimaging (DCAN) labs infant processing pipeline (Glasser et al., 2013, Fair et al., 2020, Autio et al., 2020, Donahue et al., 2016) use the popular joint label fusion (JLF) algorithm to produce accurate segmentations for infants from newborn to > 6 months (Wang and Yushkevich, 2013, Wang et al., 2013). JLF relies on manually annotated segmentations in multiple individual atlases. From there an individual's brain is non-linearly registered to each atlas. A ‘winner take all’ approach is then used to assign labels to each voxel based on local cross correlations between voxel intensities in the subject and atlas. While this strategy has shown to be successful compared to single atlas approaches (Collins and Pruessner, 2010, Wang et al., 2011), the approach is time-consuming (computation of 2–3 days) and still error-prone. Furthermore, results are quite variable at different ages, which by themselves may take months to optimize. Exploring alternative solutions is warranted.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) are an attractive alternative to JLF and have shown significant promise in adult samples (Henschel et al., 2020), CNNs are fast and take only minutes to segment an infant’s brain once properly trained. CNNs can be further trained with new data from other datasets to boost generalizability quickly. Prior work in infants has attempted to build CNNs for fast brain tissue segmentation (Dolz et al., 2020, Nie et al., 2016, Nie et al., 2019, Dolz et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2015, Mostapha and Styner, 2019). Supplemental Table 1 showcases a review of the deep learning infant brain tissue segmentation literature along with limitations of deep learning algorithms. Each study reviewed has limited sample sizes of high-quality ground truth segmentations, with no study exceeding more than 25 infants. Most studies focus on limited age ranges, specifically between 5 and 9 months, limiting the generalizability of these models to other age groups. Outside of Moeskops et al (Moeskops et al., 2016)., all studies within Supplemental Table 1 also removed the skull, cerebellum, and brain stem from the images for training and prior to segmentation generation. This pre-processing likely reduces the generalizability for images in which the skull remains and increases the burden for performing inference. Even in the case where sufficient data is used, training outputs often don’t follow common labels and standards, such as the frequently used Freesurfer software. Last, implementing a model that is open-source, community-centered and follows Findable Accessible Interoperable and Reusable (FAIR) principles (Poldrack et al., 2019) is vital for the development and progress in future model generation and general usage.

BIBSNet (Baby and Infant Brain Segmentation Neural Network) provides a new open-source software to advance brain tissue segmentations. BIBSNet was implemented to add to and improve upon previous deep learning infant and baby brain tissue segmentation research by creating a model that can handle variability of infant MR images based on age, neuronal developmental status, and data acquisition and quality. BIBSNet uses a larger sample size, n = 90, than has been previously published and employs a relatively wide age range — 0–8 months. This wide age range allows BIBSNet to capably segment neonate and infant brains across important brain developmental boundaries (i.e., 0–3 months, 3–6 months, and 6–8 months). BIBSNet was built upon the proven deep learning architecture, nnU-Net (Isensee et al., 2021), for brain tissue segmentations. To improve generalizability and robustness, SynthSeg was used to create augmented images (Billot et al., 2021). Unlike models described in the previous literature, BIBSNet does not require that the skull, brainstem, or cerebellum be removed before segmentation; hence, much less pre-processing before inference is needed. The segmentations produced from BIBSNet are BIDS-compatible and conducted using input data in the subject’s native space. This choice was intentionally done so that BIBSNet segmentations can easily be transformed to fit whatever space is required for processing and analysis (Dufford et al., 2022). BIBSnet is fully open-source, so other researchers can look at methodological detail and train their own models if needed. Finally, BIBSNet outputs can be directly input into a pre-processing pipeline, such as fMRIPrep Lifespan (Goncalves et al., 2025) and the DCAN labs infant processing pipeline (Glasser et al., 2013, Fair et al., 2020, Autio et al., 2020, Donahue et al., 2016), in particular because it follows the standard Freesurfer anatomical labels, making it a turn-key solution for subsequent analyses. Here, we test BIBSnet’s validity by comparing BIBSnet to manually annotated freesurfer segmentation labels, representing a gold standard for brain tissue segmentation. Apart from JLF, no such automated freesurfer segmentation tool exists for infants 0–8 months for T1 and T2, we also compared BIBSnet coarse brain tissue segmentations to iBEAT. iBEAT is a CNN algorithm that performs coarse brain tissue segmentation (i.e. grey/white/CSF) viable for infants 0–8 months of age (Wang et al., 2023, Dai et al., 2013).

Methods

We used MR images and manually annotated segmentations from infants aged 0–8 months from the BCP and ALBERTs datasets for the present study

MR image collection procedure

MR images were collected from 72 participants within the BCP study (Howell et al., 2019) — median age at scan, based on postmenstrual age = 444 days (standard deviation = 68 days), 43 female — with a 3 T Siemens Prisma at the University of Minnesota’s Center for Magnetic Resonance Research (CMRR). Images used for this study included T1w (echo time = 2.24 ms, repetition time = 2400 ms, inversion time = 1060 ms, sagittal slices = 208, flip angle = 8°, matrix = 320 ×320, voxel sizes = 0.8 ×0.8 ×0.8 mm3), T2w (echo time = 564 ms, repetition time = 3200 ms, sagittal slices = 208, flip angle = variable, matrix = 320 ×320, voxel sizes = 0.8 ×0.8 ×0.8 mm3), and approximately 12 min of resting state MR images (echo time = 37 ms, repetition time = 800 ms, axial slices = 72, flip angle = 52, matrix = 104 ×91, multi-band acceleration factor = 8, voxel sizes = 2.0 ×2.0 ×2.0 mm3) concatenated across two separate collections with reverse phase encoding (Anterior - Posterior and Posterior - Anterior).

To supplement the sample with additional neonates, 18 infants that were used to generate the ALBERTs neonatal atlas was added (Gousias et al., 2012). The MRI data for the ALBERTs infants — median age at scan, based on postmenstrual age = 285 days (standard deviation = 15 days) were acquired on a 3.0 T Philips Intera scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands). The relevant images utilized included T1w (echo time = 4.6 ms, repetition time = 17 ms, sagittal slices = 124–150, flip angle = 30°, matrix = 256 × 256, voxel sizes = 0.82 × 0.82 × 1.6 mm3) and T2w (echo time = 160 ms, repetition time = 8000 ms, sagittal slices = 88–100, flip angle = 90°, matrix = 224 × 224, voxel sizes = 0.86 × 0.86 × 2.0 mm3) data.

All 90 participants, 72 from the BCP study and 19 from the ALBERTs dataset, were included in subsequent steps. For the resting state fMRI analyses only 64 participants were included as 7 BCP study participants and all 19 ALBERT dataset participants did not have resting state fMRI data.

Manually Annotated Segmentation Procedure

Following data collection, the T1w and T2w MR images from the BCP study were used to produce manually annotated brain tissue segmentations based on FreeSurfer’s aseg atlas (Fischl et al., 2002), similar to the 29 anatomical labels that BIBSNet segments as shown in Supplemental Table 2. As a starting point for the annotations, model-assisted segmentations for each BCP participant were produced by either running JLF or by a prototyped version of the BIBSNet algorithm, a critical time saving step so annotators could focus on the areas there were mislabelled rather than starting from scratch. Led by an expert (E.F.), the cortical and subcortical structures from the BCP participants were then extensively manually annotated with highly trained staff to generate accurate segmentations. This work, known as the Baby Open Brains (BOBs) repository, is available to others as a resource (Feczko et al., 2025). The ALBERTs MR images were included in the training to have approximately equal sample sizes of infants from 0 to 8-months based on gestational age. The segmentations produced from the ALBERTs MR images had been previously manually annotated but did not have extensive quality control procedures and were not reviewed by an expert.

The BCP and ALBERTs MR images and manual annotated segmentations were used for BIBSNet model training and subsequent data processing and analyses.

BIBSNet combines the nnU-Net model and SynthSeg software, to produce generalizable segmentations

nnU-Net model design

As can be seen within Fig. 1, nnU-Net plays a central role in the BIBSNet model design. nnU-Net is a model that is based upon the CNN based network, U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015). In the last several years, U-Net has shown state-of-the-art performance in segmentation problems, specifically biomedical image segmentation. U-Net consists of two separate elements, the contracting path and the expanding path. The contracting path progressively down-samples the input image/s while gaining progressively more in-depth features, known as feature maps, to represent the image. Following the contracting path, the expansive path takes the feature maps and progressively up-samples them until they are the same size as the originally input image/s. The down-sampling and up-sampling is what gives this model its characteristic “U” shape, hence the “U” in U-Net. Despite the success of U-Net, a major pitfall was its difficulty with applying it to different image analysis problems. It required a multitude of expert decisions in the form of adaptation and modification of parameters to be made without clear guidance or obvious parameter defaults. nnU-Net sought to correct this by 1) fixing parameters that do not require adaptation; 2) tuning parameters that needed to be adapted based upon the inputted dataset; and 3) for any parameters that remain, making decisions empirically from the data (Isensee et al., 2021).

SynthSeg

While nnU-Net plays the integral role of segmenting the inputted images, SynthSeg is vital to improve the generalizability of the model. SynthSeg is software that can produce synthetic MR imaging data. Here, the manually annotated segmentations from the BCP and ALBERTs were inputted into SynthSeg to produce synthetic MR images. SynthSeg randomly modified the features of each image in four separate ways to emulate different acquisition parameters and data quality (Billot et al., 2021). First, an affine and non-linear transformation was applied to spatially deform the data, simulating a head tilt or changes in head size. Second and third, a randomized Gaussian mixed model and bias corruption were applied. These steps are meant to mimic the global and local pixel intensity differences that can occur across different MRI scanners, such as bias field artifacts. Fourth, to mirror acquisition differences resulting from slice thickness, collection orientation, or voxel size the image was randomly down-sampled and blurred. For the current study, 9000 T1w, T2w, and segmentation datasets were produced based on the 90 participant datasets, a 100x increase in training data. Henceforth, images from the BCP and ALBERTs will be referred to as “real images”, while the synthetic images produced by Synthseg will be referred to as “synthetic images”.

BIBSNet cross validation, training and implementation details

As shown in Fig. 1, the same BCP and ALBERT participants were used for training and analysis, thus, a k-fold training strategy was employed to avoid data leakage. The first step included splitting the real images into ten approximately equally sized pseudorandom testing datasets stratified by age. Each of the ten testing datasets was paired with a training dataset. Each training dataset fold was used to produce 900 synthetic outputs from SynthSeg and combined with the real images. This strategy ensured that when inference was performed the real images were previously unseen by at least one fold. Importantly, the imaging data were not skull-stripped, as a desired feature was for BIBSNet to perform inference on non-skull stripped images.

We implemented a 3D U-Net with 6 encoder and 5 decoder stages, each comprising two 3x3x3 convolutional blocks with Instance Normalization and Leaky ReLU activation function blocks per convolutional block. The encoder feature widths progressed from 32 to 320 channels, where the decoder channels decreased from 640 to 32 channels, with skip connections between matching resolutions. Additional model implementation details included a batch size of 2, 1000 maximum epochs, an initial learning rate of 0.01, Dice plus cross entropy for a loss function and stochastic gradient descent for the optimizer.

As one might expect, BIBSNet training necessitates considerable time and resources, requiring approximately 3.5 days of compute time with 1 NVIDIA V100 GPU, 6 CPUs, and 90 GB of RAM. This resulted in a total clock time of 5–7 days factoring in average delays on the Minnesota Supercomputing Institute (MSI) systems.

BIBSNet pre-processing and post-processing procedures

To prepare the anatomical images for inference several pre-processing steps occur within the BIBSNet application (Hendrickson et al., 2022). Prior to inference with the BIBSNet model the T1w and T2w images are co-registered and then resampled to match the MNI space matrix size (i.e. 182 ×218 x 182), the matrix size that the BIBSNet model expects. Additionally, a small crop along the z dimension is used to remove the possible existence of the body in the image.

Once the BIBSNet model produces the inferred segmentation, the segmentation is resampled back into the original matrix sizes that the T1w and T2w images were provided in.

The BIBSNet application, provided as a BIDS app, requires minimal time and computing power to perform inference on unseen T1w/T2w image pairs. Details for downloading and usage of the BIBSNet application can be found on Zenodo (Hendrickson et al., 2022), GitHub (https://github.com/DCAN-Labs/BIBSnet), and Read the Docs (https://bibsnet.readthedocs.io/en/stable/).

iBeat segmentations were generated for coarse tissue comparisons

Because we wanted to compare 0–8 month lifespan BIBSnet segmentations to a comparable CNN algorithm, we derived iBeat coarse brain tissue segmentations from T1w and T2w images. T1/T2 images were run through iBeat version 2.0 via the container available on GitHub (Wang et al., 2023), to produce segmentations for grey/white/CSF.

JLF segmentations were generated for all comparisons

JLF was performed using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) Joint Label Fusion (Wang and Yushkevich, 2013), implemented via the Nipype AntsJointFusion interface within the containerized DCAN-infant processing pipeline. The use of Nipype and containerization ensures consistent software environments to support reproducible, standardized execution (Gorgolewski et al., 2011). For each subject, the T1w is first nonlinearly registered to a set of 10 age-specific atlas templates using ANTs registration. These atlases, curated in-house, provide distinct templates for each month of age. The resulting nonlinear transformations are applied to corresponding atlas segmentations, yielding 10 overlapping candidate segmentation maps. Following the JLF framework, a weighted voting scheme is used to compute a consensus segmentation across atlases, with the label of highest consensus assigned to each voxel based upon voxel intensity. JLF’s dominant computational cost arises from the

nonlinear registration of each subject image to multiple atlas templates, which is an inherently resource-intensive step in ANTs.

MRI data were processed with the DCAN lab’s infant-ABCD-BIDS processing pipeline using segmentations produced from ground truth and the JLF and BIBSNet models

To test for the effect of segmentation type in subsequent analyses, each of the 90 participants were processed through the DCAN labs infant processing pipeline (Glasser et al., 2013, Autio et al., 2020, Donahue et al., 2016, Sturgeon et al., 2023) in three separate ways: 1) running JLF using the age-matched templates produced from the manually annotated segmentations (excluding the same-subject template) 2) using the externally generated segmentations based on the manual annotations; and 3) using the externally generated segmentation inferred by the trained BIBSNet model. From here on, the processing strategies will be referred to as “JLF”, “ground truth” and “BIBSNet”, respectively. Beyond the three separate processing strategies mentioned above, the data were processed using the exact same procedures. iBeat inferred segmentations were not included in this processing, in part, because iBeat does not segment subcortical structures so there was concern regarding the ability to process the data and the accuracy of the potential outputs. However, iBeat was included in Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) comparisons.

The DCAN lab’s infant processing pipeline is based on the Human Connectome Project minimal processing pipeline (Glasser et al., 2013) with key changes to accommodate for the differences in the developing brain of infants. Additionally, the design of DCAN lab’s infant processing pipeline draws heavily on ABCD-BIDS processing pipeline to accommodate and attenuate scanner effects found within major MRI vendors GE, Philips, and Siemens (Feczko et al., 2021). The pipeline is split into several stages: “PreFreeSurfer”, “FreeSurfer”, “PostFreeSurfer”, “FMRIVolume”, “FMRISurface”, “DCANBOLDProcessing”, and “ExecutiveSummary”.

Data processing failed for a total of three participants. Two participants were from ALBERTs and one was from the BCP study. All three failures used the JLF inferred segmentations. These failures were removed from subsequent analyses, including cortical thickness comparisons and, in the case of the BCP, participant functional connectome and DSC analyses. Further details on failures are highlighted in Supplemental Table 3.

Segmentation overlap and structural and functional MRI metrics were used to perform statistical analyses comparing JLF versus ground truth, iBeat versus ground truth and separately, BIBSNet versus ground truth

Segmentation similarity analysis

To evaluate how similar the segmentations produced by JLF and BIBSNet were to the ground truth segmentations, DSC and 95% Hausdorff Distance (HD95) was used (Dice, 1945, Sorensen, 1948, Hausdorff, 1978).

Equation 1| Dice Similarity Coefficient.

As shown in Equation 1, given two segmentations, “X” and “Y” where |X| and |Y| represent the number of elements in each segmentation, the Dice Similarity Coefficient equals twice the number of elements common to both sets divided by the sum of the number of elements in each set.

Equation 2 | Hausdorff Distance.

As shown in Equation 2, given two segmentations “X” and “Y” where d(x,Y) represents the distance of closest point “x” in segmentation “X” to any point in segmentation “Y” and symmetrically d(y,X) represents the distance of closest point “y” in segmentation “Y” to any point in segmentation “X”. P95 returns the value below which 95% of distances lie.

DSC and Hausdorff distance were calculated thrice, first to compare JLF and ground truth, second comparing BIBSNet and ground truth, and third, as a post-hoc analysis, to compare iBeat and ground truth. As iBeat does not segment subcortical structures, manually annotated images were used to mask out the would-be subcortical structures.

To assess DSC for each segmentation type by brain region, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) to the ground truth was used.

Equation 3 | Root Mean Squared Error.

As shown in Equation 3, root mean squared error is a statistical measure that indicates the average magnitude between predicted and observed values, where: yi: observed value for the ith observation, yˆi: predicted value for the ith observation, N: number of observations, and P: number of parameter estimates, including the constant, RMSE compared to other metrics like mean absolute error, punishes larger errors. We find that this feature is particularly useful for our comparisons as even subtle differences in derived metrics could result in different conclusions when studied or analyzed, let alone patently large errors.

As an added assessment of method effectiveness paired T-tests are also used.

Structural MRI analyses

In addition to evaluating the similarity of resulting segmentations, morphological metrics were analyzed. Using outputs from the FreeSurfer processing stage, four different morphological metrics – gray matter, white matter, bilateral amygdala, and bilateral hippocampal volume – and one cortical sheet metric – cortical thickness – were analyzed. To measure similarity of cortical thickness, a Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated per participant between the ground truth and BIBSNet and separately JLF and ground truth. The Spearman’s rank correlation ranges from 0.0, meaning absolutely no similarity, and 1.0 meaning perfect rank similarity. To assess method effectiveness, the outcome metrics produced from BIBSNet and JLF segmentations (volume, cortical thickness) were directly compared to the ground truth segmentation calculating the Intra Class Coefficients for the volumetric comparisons, conducting the Brunner Munzel test for the cortical thickness, and calculating the RMSE for all metrics.

Functional MRI analysis

In addition to structural or structurally derived metrics, functional MRI metrics were analyzed. The functional MRI metrics were derived from a cortical surface which is reconstructed using the volumetric cortical ribbon that the segmentation provides, highlighting the importance of an accurate segmentation for functional analyses. The Gordon (Gordon et al., 2016) cortical parcellated resting state time series generated by the DCANBOLDProcessing processing stage were used to produce pair-pair correlation matrices with the tool “cifti-connectivity” (https://github.com/DCAN-Labs/cifti-connectivity). Second, timepoints were censored out that exceeded a framewise displacement of 0.3 mm (Power et al., 2012). Censoring out time points in this fashion had the effect of removing sufficiently large motion events that could cause spurious correlation. With these motion events removed, each so-called “filtered” Gordon parcellated resting state time series was temporally correlated with itself to produce a pair-pair correlation matrix, also known as a functional connectome matrix.

Like all other metrics, the functional connectome matrices were produced based on each of the three segmentation strategies. Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated per participant between the ground truth and BIBSNet and, separately, ground truth and JLF. To evaluate the comparisons RMSE was calculated, and the Brunner Munzel test was used.

Results

BIBSNet application is efficient

While model training is resource-intensive, using the trained model for inference is straightforward. The inferences for the present study only required 4 min per subject using 2 CPUs and 20 GB of RAM on the MSI systems. BIBSNet far out-performs the resources and time required by JLF, which, including the pre-processing steps required (i.e. nonlinear registration of a set of atlas images to the subject brain) can take up to 2–3 days.

Dice similarity coefficient comparisons

DSCs were used to assess overlap in label assignments for BIBSNet- and JLF-derived segmentations compared to ground truth segmentations for cortical and subcortical structures in the left and right hemispheres (Fig. 2). For cortical structures, there was more similarity between BIBSNet and ground truth structures (gray matter: DSC = 0.84, HD95 = 1.38, DSC RMSE = 0.157; white matter: DSC = 0.86, HD95 = 1.45, DSC RMSE = 0.144 r) than between JLF and ground truth (gray matter: DSC = 0.71, HD95 = 3.18, DSC RMSE = 0.30, white matter: DSC = 0.79, HD95 = 2.79 DSC RMSE = 0.24),. The DSCs were further compared with a repeated sample T-test, revealing that both the gray matter and white matter DSCs produced from BIBSNet were significantly larger than those from JLF (gray matter: T = 14.08, p < 1 ×10−23; white matter: T = 6.21, p < 1 ×10−7). For subcortical structures, amygdala and hippocampus DSCs were compared because they are typically more difficult to accurately segment due to their small size. The bilateral amygdala and hippocampus comparisons revealed no significant statistical differences between JLF and BIBSNet (Amygdala: T = 1.74, p = 0.08; Hippocampus: T = 0.41, p = 0.67), in any of the evaluated metrics (Amygdala JLF: DSC = 0.80, HD95 = 1.69, DSC RMSE = 0.24; Amygdala BIBSNet: DSC = 0.83, HD95 = 1.49, DSC RMSE = 0.17; Hippocampus JLF: DSC = 0.84, HD95 = 1.5, DSC RMSE = 0.18; Hippocampus BIBSNet: DSC = 0.83, HD95 = 1.6, DSC RMSE = 0.16). While no subcortical comparisons were statistically significant, there was much less variability in DSC values from BIBSNet vs ground truth (average standard deviation across regions = 0.04) compared to JLF vs ground truth (average standard deviation across regions = 0.12) (Fig. 2a).

An ad hoc comparison of BIBSNet to iBeat, an existing deep learning model for infant brain tissue segmentation (Wang et al., 2023, Dai et al., 2013), was performed. BIBSNet and iBeat serve different purposes, BIBSNet produces FreeSurfer-compatible segmentations as well as segmenting cortical and subcortical structures, whereas iBeat segments white and grey matter only. Therefore, the analyses were limited to comparisons of white matter and gray matter. BIBSNet and iBeat performed similarly with no significant differences across ages within gray matter (T = 1.30, p = 0.19) (Supplemental Figure 2a). However, in white matter, while both performed similarly from 0 to 5 months (T = 0.69, p = 0.49), iBeat outperformed BIBSNet at months 6–8 significantly (T = 6.9, p < 1 ×10−6) (Supplemental Figure 2b).

Volumetric, cortical surface, and functional connectivity comparisons

We assessed the similarity of cortical and subcortical volumes in BIBSNet- and JLF-derived segmentations compared to ground truth segmentations. For both gray matter (BIBSNet: RMSE = 38945.72, ICC = 0.96, F = 58.93, p < 1 ×10−53; JLF: RMSE = 42515.47, ICC: 0.78, F = 8.20, p < 1 ×10−19) and white matter (BIBSNet: RMSE = 38414.41, ICC = 0.89, F = 17.97, p < 1 ×10−32; JLF: RMSE = 47704.83, ICC = 0.68, F = 5.35, p < 1 ×10−13), BIBSNet volumes were more similar to ground truth than JLF (Fig. 3a,c). For subcortical volumes, we compared hippocampus and amygdala volumes to match the DSC comparisons above (Fig. 3b, d). Bilateral hippocampus volumes produced from BIBSNet (BIBSNet: RMSE = 397.03, ICC = 0.90, F = 20.43, p < 1 ×10−34) segmentations were more similar to ground truth than JLF (JLF: RMSE = 465.72, ICC = 0.83, F = 11.05, p < 1 ×10−24). Similarly, ground truth and BIBSNet bilateral amygdala volume were more similar (BIBSNet: RMSE = 231.96, ICC = 0.91, F = 22.76, p < 1 ×10−36) compared to JLF and ground truth (JLF: RMSE = 237.51, ICC = 0.76, F = 7.59, p < 1 ×10−18).

Additionally, cortical thickness derived from JLF and BIBSNet segmentation were compared to ground truth derived cortical thickness. After collecting the pair-pair Spearman’s rank correlations of the cortical thickness maps a Brunner-Wunzel non-parametric test revealed that BIBSNet cortical thickness maps were statistically more similar to ground truth than JLF (W = 9.95, p < 1 ×10−17, Spearman’s rank: BIBSNet = 0.42, JLF = 0.23) (Fig. 3e,f)).

Finally, we also analyzed how functional connectivity compares between BIBSNet- and JLF-derived segmentations and ground truth. The similarity between BIBSNet and ground truth functional connectome matrices based on Spearman’s rank was greater than the similarity between the JLF and ground truth based on a Brunner-Wunzel test (W = 10.34, p < 1 ×10−17; BIBSNet: Spearman’s rank = 0.91, RMSE = 0.09; JLF: Spearman’s rank = 0.82, RMSE = 0.19) (Fig. 3g,h,i).

Discussion

BIBSNet is the only open-source pre-trained deep neural network model that we are aware of to construct high-quality cortical and subcortical segmentations in infant brains with 29 FreeSurfer compatible labels (Supplemental Table 2). This highlights its utility for use in infant processing methods and pipelines such as fMRIPrep Lifespan (Goncalves et al., 2025) and DCAN infant pipeline (Glasser et al., 2013, Fair et al., 2020, Autio et al., 2020, Donahue et al., 2016). BIBSNet has four important distinguishing features that stand out compared to the extant literature. 1) Nearly all models require pre-processing to remove part of the anatomy, particularly the skull. 2) The training samples from models not used here, but present in the literature involve infants between 5 and 9 months of age, based on gestational age (see: Supplemental Table 1). BIBSNet, on the other hand, is trained on a much larger age range, between 0- and 8-months gestational age. 3) Unlike DrawEM in MCRIBS (Adamson et al., 2020), or Infant Freesurfer’s (Zöllei et al., 2020) segmentation algorithm, BIBSNet incorporates a T1 and T2, enabling automated segmentation accuracy across the 0–8 month infant timespan tested. 4) BIBSNet is nearly 600x faster than JLF and requires minimal computational resources for model inference. Our extensive examination of BIBSNet compares it to JLF, the method used in fMRIPrep Lifespan across DSC, morphometric, and functional metrics, as well as iBeat, a deep-learning cortical tissue segmentation algorithm used across the infant development timespan

BIBSnet segments cortical and subcortical ROIs with state-of-the-art accuracy

The analyses presented here further confirm that BIBSNet segments cortical and subcortical structures accurately. Prior literature DSC findings (Supplemental Table 1) show that gray matter ranged from 0.84 to 0.92 with a mean of 0.879, while white matter ranged from 0.85 to 0.93 with a mean of 0.885 (Dolz et al., 2020, Nie et al., 2016, Nie et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2015, Moeskops et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2023, Moeskops and Pluim, 2017, Dolz et al., 2018, Zeng and Zheng, 2018, Wang et al., 2018), with BIBSNet firmly fitting into this. The resulting tight standard deviation distributions further highlight BIBSNet’s high reliability. Performance is also evident within the single subject comparisons as shown with Fig. 4. Notice that there is high correspondence between the BIBSNet and ground truth segmentations more generally, whereas the red arrows highlight the errors from the JLF segmentations.

In addition to DSC comparisons with JLF, a comparison with iBeat was performed. iBeat and BIBSNet DSC comparisons revealed largely comparable performance. iBeat slightly outperformed BIBSNet when segmenting white matter for 6–8 month infants. It is worth highlighting that the differences between the white matter means and standard deviations are quite small (BIBSNet: mean = 0.862, standard deviation = 0.04; iBeat: mean = 0.876, standard deviation = 0.05), indicating that the differences between BIBSNet and iBeat DSCs are subtle. The similarity between BIBSNet and iBeat can also be seen within the single subject comparisons in Supplemental Figure 1.

BIBSnet improves accuracy of brain morphometric and functional measures over JLF

When the segmentations from ground truth, JLF, and BIBSNet were used to derive volumetric and cortical surface metrics, BIBSNet outperformed JLF across nearly all volumetric and cortical surface derived metrics in its similarity to the ground truth. The effects were particularly pronounced with gray and white matter volume, with JLF showing nearly double the RMSE compared to BIBSNet (Fig. 3a, c). Differences in RMSE highlights two details: 1) that JLF made more errors than BIBSNet; and 2) the errors that JLF made were markedly larger compared to BIBSNet. Both JLF and BIBSNet segment all subcortical structures, however, the amygdala and hippocampus were of particular focus as they are smaller structures and typically more difficult for automated methods to segment. Once again, BIBSNet outperformed JLF across both the hippocampus and amygdala. It is likely that there is considerable variability across JLF template participants for both structures causing it to make some mistakes as it attempts to segment unseen data. On the other hand, BIBSNet seemed to have learned the latent patterns of each region allowing it to more accurately segment structures despite potential variabilities.

In addition to morphometrics, fMRI data produced from the DCAN infant pipeline is being affected by the underlying brain tissue segmentation, since the BOLD data is projected onto the cortical surface, based on the segmented cortical ribbon (Glasser et al., 2013). Therefore, functional connectivity matrices were compared across segmentation types. Using Spearman’s rank correlation to generate a measure of similarity of paired functional connectivity matrices, RMSE revealed that functional connectome matrices derived from BIBSNet segmentations were much more similar to ground truth than JLF derived matrices (Fig. 3h, i). This effect is also apparent within the group averaged based difference matrices (Fig. 3g), notice that values from the BIBSNet and ground truth difference matrix hovering closer to zero, indicating no difference, compared to the JLF and ground truth difference matrix.

Impact and future directions

BIBSNet provides a critical need for infant neuroimaging studies. Many neuroimaging pipelines (Adamson et al., 2020, Zöllei et al., 2020, Goncalves et al., 2021, Cieslak et al., 2021) require FreeSurfer compatible labels from segmentations, a feature which is unavailable with existing deep learning based infant segmentation methods. With the use of FreeSurfer compatible labels in BIBSNet, it is an easy turnkey solution for incorporation into existing pipelines and can be relied upon for segmentation within yet to be designed pipelines. The addition of subcortical segmentations in BIBSNet is useful for handling multimodal infant studies, such as HBCD (Nelson et al., 2024). For example, a subcortical segmentation is needed for diffusion (Cieslak et al., 2021) and MRS (Oeltzschner et al., 2020) data processing. Moreover, it is needed to derive measures from fMRI for subcortical structures (Goncalves et al., 2021) as well. Additionally, our hope is that the morphological and resting state fMRI analysis performed, paired with the images showcased within Fig. 4 indicating the per subject improvement in accuracy of BIBSNet over JLF, will allow researchers to see the practical benefits of using BIBSNet. Finally, as a resource the BIBSNet lookup table is provided in Supplemental Table 2 so others can easily know the mapping between label numbers within the segmentations and their corresponding label names.

Given the success of BIBSNet, future directions are warranted. A primary feature of the BIBSNet model proposed in this work is that it currently requires a T1w and T2w image pair. In practice, infant MRI research studies typically only acquire one of the two anatomical images. Since infant MR scans are typically acquired while the infant is asleep, time is at a premium. Therefore, once a single anatomical image is collected, researchers often move on to collecting other images such as resting state or diffusion weighted imaging. As a result of this, we have trained additional BIBSNet models, one that requires just a T1w and the other just a T2w image. A future study will evaluate the performance of these additional BIBSNet models. Furthermore, BIBSnet uses the generation of synthetic images, yet there is no explicitly recommended real to synthetic image ratio within the medical imaging literature, with some prior works leveraging 100x ratios like was performed here (Shiode et al., 2021, Yamashita and Markov, 2020). Future work can investigate how adapting this ratio can potentially alter model performance. Ordinary least squares regression results revealed that there is a significant negative trend between white matter DSC and participant age, for the BIBSNet model, but not for gray matter DSC (Supplemental Figure 3, Supplemental Table 4–5), showcasing some room for improvement with BIBSNet. iBeat trains separate models for different ages (Fischl et al., 2008), which may explain why it showed no such effect. Therefore, allowing independent models to learn slightly more nuanced patterns than a model trained on sample across multiple developmental stages, like BIBSNet.Additional future work will involve the incorporation of large and diverse datasets, such as HBCD, to widen the age range and acquisition parameters of images in the training sample. Dice similarity coefficient results from a more recent BIBSNet model that incorporates HBCD participants shows promising performance across brain structures (Supplemental Figure 4).

Conclusion

In conclusion, BIBSnet shows state-of-the-art performance when compared to iBeat, JLF and models used in prior literature. BIBSNet performed well across multiple ages and was robust to the existence of the skull in images. Finally, the pre-trained model is fast (at least 600x speed up compared to JLF), requires minimal high performance computing resources, and can easily be included in other pipelines. Future studies will extend BIBSnet’s performance and flexibility across more datasets and longitudinal timepoints.
