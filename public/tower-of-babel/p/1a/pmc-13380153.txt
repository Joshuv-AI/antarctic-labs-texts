Inflammatory Bowel Disease and Periprosthetic Joint Infection Following Total Joint Arthroplasty: Crohn’s Disease and Ulcerative Colitis Associated With Varying Risk
Authors: McKenzie W Culler, Matthew A Lim, Sahil S Telang, Avinash Iyer, Kevin Sun, Jay R Lieberman, Nathanael D Heckmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe risk of periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) in patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) who undergo total joint arthroplasty (TJA) remains unclear as reports in the literature are inconclusive. This study sought to clarify the relationship between IBD and PJI risk after TJA.MethodsAn all-payer claims database was used to identify patients who underwent primary elective TJA between 2016 and 2023. Patients who had a diagnosis of Crohn’s disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC) were identified. Propensity score matching (1:7) was performed for the UC, CD, and aggregate IBD cohorts based on age, sex, and comorbidities. Ninety-day PJI risk was assessed using multivariable regression.ResultsIn total, 10,040 patients with IBD were identified and matched to 70,203 controls. Separately, 5474 CD patients were matched to 38,230 controls, and 4555 UC patients were matched to 31,865 controls. PJI rates were similar between the IBD and control cohorts (0.71% vs 0.61%; P = .245). However, patients with CD had a significantly higher PJI rate compared to controls (0.86% vs 0.62%; P = .043), while patients with UC had a similar rate compared to controls (0.53% vs 0.64%; P = .365). After adjusting for confounders, CD was associated with an increased risk of PJI (adjusted odds ratio: 1.38; 95% confidence interval: 1.01-1.89; P = .046). No increased risk was detected in the UC or combined IBD cohorts.DiscussionCD is independently associated with increased PJI risk following TJA, while UC is not. This distinction may account for inconsistencies in the literature evaluating IBD as a risk factor for PJI.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Total knee arthroplasty, Total hip arthroplasty, Total joint arthroplasty, Inflammatory bowel disease

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic inflammatory condition characterized by immune dysregulation within the gastrointestinal (GI) tract. While IBD currently affects approximately 3.1 million individuals in the United States, or 1.3% of the total population, the prevalence of this condition has grown substantially in recent decades [[1], [2], [3]]. Although both are classified as chronic autoinflammatory disorders of the GI tract, CD and UC are distinct conditions that differ substantially in their underlying pathology and clinical presentation. CD is characterized by transmural inflammation and may affect any portion of the GI tract, whereas UC is limited to the colon, with inflammation confined to the mucosal and submucosal layers. In addition to GI complications, both conditions are also frequently associated with extraintestinal manifestations, including musculoskeletal sequelae, as patients with IBD are known to exhibit decreased bone mineral density and an elevated risk of fractures [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Furthermore, these patients remain vulnerable to degenerative joint disease and may require total joint arthroplasty (TJA) for the treatment of osteoarthritis.

There is growing concern that patients with IBD may be at an increased risk of postoperative complications following TJA [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome, a common feature of IBD, has been associated with an elevated risk of postoperative periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) [11,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. This is particularly alarming given PJI is a highly morbid complication that is associated with increased healthcare resource utilization, the need for revision surgery, and other deleterious consequences [12,17]. Furthermore, the immunologic disturbances observed in CD and UC and the immunomodulatory drugs commonly prescribed in their management may also increase susceptibility to infectious complications following TJA [11,[18], [19], [20]].

Despite interest in elucidating a possible association between a diagnosis of IBD and PJI after TJA, the current body of literature remains inconclusive. While some studies have identified an increased risk in patients who have IBD relative to controls, others have failed to detect a difference [10,11,21]. Similarly, analyses considering CD and UC as distinct diagnoses have yielded inconsistent findings [8,9,21]. Clarifying this relationship is essential for risk stratification and perioperative management in this growing patient population. Using a large, nationally representative database, the present study examined the relationship between IBD and the 90-day risk of PJI, as well as other medical and surgical complications following elective total hip or total knee arthroplasty (TKA). We hypothesized that patients who had a diagnosis of either CD or UC would exhibit an increased risk of PJI.

Material and methods

The Premier Healthcare Database (PHD) (Premier, Inc., NC, USA) was queried to identify all patients who underwent primary elective TJA between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2023. The PHD is a national, all-payer repository containing claims from approximately 25% of inpatient admissions throughout the United States and over 1 billion outpatient visits across ambulatory surgery centers, emergency departments, and other sites of care [22]. The PHD contains information pertaining to patient demographics, Current Procedural Terminology codes, International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, medications, 90-day complications, hospital characteristics, and billing records. All patient information in the PHD is deidentified in compliance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. As such, the present study was exempt from institutional board review.

ICD-10 and Current Procedural Terminology codes were used to identify patients who underwent primary elective TKA and total hip arthroplasty (THA). All patients who had a diagnosis of CD or UC prior to TJA were identified using relevant diagnosis codes. Patients under the age of 18 years old and individuals who underwent TJA for nonelective indications were excluded from the study (Supplemental Table 1).

To minimize confounding due to selection bias and optimize the ability to make causal inferences in this context, a propensity score matching algorithm was implemented. An aggregate IBD cohort of patients with a diagnosis of either CD or UC was matched 1:7 based on age, sex, and comorbidities to those who did not have CD or UC. Nearest-neighbor matching without replacement using a standard caliper of 0.05 was employed, and postmatch balance was assessed by ensuring that all covariates had a standardized mean difference of <0.10. Separate 1:7 matches were performed for the isolated CD and UC cohorts using the same methodology.

The primary outcome of interest was the 90-day incidence of PJI following TJA. PJI was identified using the ICD-10 diagnosis code T84.5 (“infection and inflammatory reaction due to internal joint prosthesis”). Secondary outcomes included the 90-day incidence of medical complications (acute kidney injury, pneumonia, acute respiratory failure, urinary tract infection, sepsis, any postoperative infection, wound dehiscence, seroma), hospital readmission, and mortality.

Univariable regression was utilized to determine the risk for all primary and secondary outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to address potential confounders, incorporating all patient demographics, hospital characteristics, and comorbidities not included in the propensity matching that minimized Akaike Information Criteria and Bayesian Information Criteria [23]. Statistical significance was determined at P < .05. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA (version 18.0; StataCorp, College Station, Texas).

Results

A total of 10,040 elective hip and knee arthroplasty patients (37.09% THA; 62.91% TKA) with a history of IBD (54.64% CD; 45.36% UC) were identified and matched to 70,203 controls. (Table 1, Supplemental Table 2) Separately, 5474 CD patients (37.08% THA; 62.92% TKA) were matched to 38,230 controls, while 4555 UC patients (36.40% THA; 63.60% TKA) were matched to 31,865 controls (Supplemental Tables 3-6).

Primary outcome

Univariable regression analysis demonstrated that patients who had IBD developed PJI at a similar rate relative to matched controls (0.71% vs 0.61%; P = .245) within 90 days postoperatively (Table 2). Subanalysis revealed patients who had CD had higher rates of PJI compared to controls (0.86% vs 0.62%; P = .043) while patients who had UC developed PJI at a similar rate (0.53% vs 0.64%; P = .365) (Table 3, Table 4). After adjusting for confounding factors, multivariable models found that patients who had CD continued to exhibit a significantly increased risk of developing PJI relative to matched controls (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 1.38; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01-1.89; P = .046). No differences were observed for risk of PJI in the UC (aOR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.55-1.29; P = .436) or aggregate IBD (aOR: 1.20; 95% CI: 0.93-1.55; P = .158) cohorts.

Secondary outcomes

Patients who had IBD demonstrated increased rates of acute kidney injury (2.85% vs 2.23%; P < .001), acute respiratory failure (1.31% vs 1.09%; P = .042), sepsis (0.63% vs 0.48%; P = .043), and hospital readmission (3.84% vs 3.09%; P < .001) within 90 days postoperatively relative to matched controls. No significant differences were detected for rates of pneumonia, urinary tract infection, wound dehiscence, seroma, or 90-day mortality. Multivariable analysis found that patients who had IBD continued to have a significantly increased risk of acute kidney injury (aOR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.12-1.45; P < .001), acute respiratory failure (aOR: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.01-1.47; P = .036), sepsis (aOR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.01-1.73; P = .044), and hospital readmission (aOR: 1.26; 95%-CI: 1.13-1.41; P < .001) (Table 2).

On subgroup analysis, CD patients had increased rates of acute kidney injury (2.98% vs 2.25%; P = .001), pneumonia (0.80% vs 0.57%; P = .037), sepsis (0.68% vs 0.45%; P = .024), any postoperative infection (0.69% vs 0.47%; P = .025), and hospital readmission (3.82% vs 3.12%; P = .006) compared to controls. Multivariable regression found that patients who had CD continued to have a significantly increased risk of acute kidney injury (aOR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.11-1.57; P = .002), sepsis (aOR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.03-2.11; P = .033), any postoperative infection (aOR: 1.48; 95% CI: 1.04-2.10; P = .031), and hospital readmission (aOR: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.05-1.41; P = .011). No significant differences were detected for risk of pneumonia, acute respiratory failure, urinary tract infection, wound dehiscence, seroma, or mortality (Table 3).

In contrast, UC patients had an increased rate of hospital readmission relative to controls (3.84% vs 3.14%; P = .013) which was also observed on multivariable regression (aOR: 1.22; 95% CI: 1.04-1.44; P = .017). However, no significant differences were identified for risk of acute kidney injury, pneumonia, acute respiratory failure, urinary tract infection, sepsis, any postoperative infection, wound dehiscence, seroma, or mortality (Table 4).

Discussion

Utilizing a cohort of 10,040 patients with IBD who underwent primary elective TJA, the present study identified an increased risk of PJI among patients who had a pre-existing diagnosis of CD compared to matched controls. Conversely, our analysis found that patients who had a diagnosis of UC were not at an increased risk of PJI. Though IBD encompasses both CD and UC, these 2 conditions demonstrate considerable differences in pathology and clinical manifestation. As such, surgeons should carefully consider the increased risk of PJI in patients who have CD and ensure these patients are appropriately optimized prior to arthroplasty surgery in order to minimize infectious complications.

Though prior studies have associated a diagnosis of IBD with an increased risk of PJI after TJA, these results have not been consistent. Several retrospective studies have reported an increased risk of PJI, ranging from 1.6-fold to 5.4-fold, among patients who had IBD relative to controls [[9], [10], [11],24,25]. Interestingly, studies considering CD and UC as separate diagnoses have produced less consistent findings [9,21,26,27]. Schaffler et al. did not detect a significant difference in the risk of revision for PJI after THA among UC or CD cohorts relative to controls. In contrast, Remily et al. identified a 1.37-fold increase in risk of PJI following TKA among patients who had CD and a 1.31-fold increase among patients who had UC [9,21]. In a retrospective claims database study of 16,039 CD patients who underwent primary TKA matched to 80,190 non-CD controls, Pandya et al. found that CD patients had a 1.52-fold increase in risk of PJI. Similarly, Hinkle et al., in a retrospective analysis of 367,890 THA patients included in the National Inpatient Sample, determined that patients who had CD were at a 1.57-fold increased risk of PJI after primary THA [27]. The present study, through its use of a large, nationally representative matched cohort and ability to control for confounding variables, clarifies discrepancies in the existing body of literature and demonstrates that a diagnosis of CD is associated with a 1.38-fold increased risk of PJI. In contrast, patients with UC have a similar risk of PJI as matched controls.

IBD is generally understood to result from a dysregulated host immune response to certain organisms present in the gut microbiome, the community of commensal microbes that colonizes the GI tract [28]. The resulting pro-inflammatory cascade may disrupt the homeostatic balance of these resident organisms and cause dysbiosis, which is known to increase gut permeability to commensal microbes [29]. These organisms may then translocate from the gut into the systemic circulation and travel to the joints to seed infection [13,15,30]. Alternatively, translocated microbes may also be engulfed by circulating “Trojan Horse” monocytes and macrophages, which inadvertently function as mobile bacterial reservoirs as they transport these organisms to the joints to cause PJI [14,31,32]. Serum zonulin, a physiologic modulator of epithelial tight junctions in the GI tract, has emerged in recent decades as a biomarker of intestinal permeability [33,34]. Chisari et al. detected significantly increased levels of zonulin among patients with PJI relative to noninfected controls and, in a follow-up study, identified a direct correlation between increasing serum zonulin and the likelihood of PJI caused by a commensal GI organism [13,14]. Interestingly, evidence suggests serum zonulin may be higher among patients with IBD. In a series of 118 patients, Caviglia et al. determined that baseline serum zonulin levels were significantly higher among patients who had IBD relative to controls [35]. However, Málíčkova et al. found that, while both serum and fecal zonulin were significantly elevated among patients who had CD, patients with UC and healthy controls demonstrated comparable zonulin levels [36]. Though further research is needed to clarify this discrepancy, the existing body of evidence suggest patients who have CD likely demonstrate increased intestinal permeability relative to patients who have UC.

Differences in the pathophysiology of CD and UC may further contextualize the variability in the risk of PJI observed. CD can develop in any part of the GI tract from the mouth to the anus and presents with lesions that span the entire thickness of the bowel wall [[37], [38], [39]]. The depth of these lesions may exacerbate gut permeability and facilitate bacterial translocation, possibly increasing infection risk. Additionally, although rare, reports in the literature have implicated communicating fistulas between the bowel and the hip joint as a source of PJI caused by enteric bacteria among patients who have CD [40,41]. In contrast to CD, UC generally begins in the rectum and extends proximally and continuously through the colon with shallow erosions confined to the mucosal and submucosal layers [42]. Additional preclinical and clinical research is necessary to explore how variations in the anatomic region of involvement, the depth of inflammation, and the degree of associated dysbiosis may modulate the risk of PJI in CD and UC.

Our study has several potential limitations. The patients included in our study were identified using relevant diagnosis codes for CD and UC. As such, we were unable to account for the relative severity or status of their disease. Additionally, patients with IBD demonstrated a higher burden of systemic comorbidities, including known risk factors for PJI, which may independently contribute to infection risk and are difficult to isolate from the effects of IBD itself. It is also important to acknowledge that patients included in the present analysis likely represent the spectrum of disease severity, ranging from those with complicated fistulizing disease to those in clinical, endoscopic, or histologic remission. Similarly, we were unable to control for a history of prior corrective surgery (such as colectomy or bowel resection) or the use of anti-inflammatory medications, including corticosteroids, 5-aminosalicylates, immunomodulators, and biologics, that may critically influence susceptibility to infection, as described in previous studies [[43], [44], [45]]. Furthermore, the database does not provide reliable information regarding nutritional status or malabsorption, which may differ between CD and UC and influence susceptibility to infection. The impacts of disease status, comorbidities, medications, and the surgical management of CD and UC on the risk of PJI warrant thorough investigation in future studies. Our analysis also considered THA and TKA patients in aggregate and did not subanalyze to determine the risk of PJI by joint. Additionally, although the database used is among the largest healthcare databases available, it only captures approximately 25% of all inpatient admissions in the United States, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, as with all retrospective database studies, it is susceptible to coding errors that may be present in the source data. PJI was also identified using administrative coding, which may not fully distinguish between deep PJIs requiring surgical intervention and more superficial postoperative infections managed nonoperatively. As such, there is potential for misclassification, and the true incidence of clinically significant PJI may not be fully captured within this dataset. Despite these limitations, we do not believe that any coding errors, if present, would systematically skew our results as our findings are not suggestive of disproportionate bias in any subset of patients.

This study also has several strengths. The patients included in this study underwent TJA between 2016 and 2023, representing a contemporary cohort that is reflective of modern medical and surgical practices. The statistical power conferred by our large sample sizes allowed us to meaningfully detect the rates of rare complications, including PJI, that have proven difficult to achieve in single-institution and prospective studies. Additionally, the PHD is a national multicenter database that provides both a robust reflection of the arthroplasty patient population in the United States and a high degree of granularity for each individual patient, including information pertaining to demographic characteristics and comorbidities. This allowed us to appropriately match our experimental cohorts to control groups and create robust multivariable models with the ability to account for potential confounders. In doing so, we were able to detect the true risk of PJI among patients who had an existing diagnosis of either CD or UC.

Conclusions

CD is associated with an increased risk of PJI in the 90 days following elective TJA. However, UC does not confer an increase in the risk of PJI. These findings demonstrate the need for preoperative risk stratification and optimization for patients with CD prior to arthroplasty surgery. Future research should aim to clarify how pathological differences between these 2 conditions, particularly with regard to dysbiosis and the resulting increase in GI tract permeability, influence infectious risk.
