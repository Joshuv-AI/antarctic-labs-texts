Exploring the optimal follow-up time for resectable colorectal cancer patients: a multicenter, five-year longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Boyu Kang, Yihuan Qiao, Yiqian Wang, Yunlong Li, Baoliang Hu, Zecheng Zhang, Qi Wang, Jun Zhu, Jipeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollow-up after colorectal cancer surgery is crucial for the early detection of recurrence and metastasis. We aimed to develop a comprehensive mixed-effects model using multidimensional clinical data to explore the optimal postoperative follow-up time, thereby improving patient survival and economic benefits. This study was a cohort study that collected data from patients who underwent radical resection for rectal cancer at Xijing Hospital from 2011 to 2023, as well as data from PLA General Hospital and Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital. The development cohort and internal validation were from Xijing Hospital, with the remaining two centers serving as external validation sites. Based on both conventional and dynamic data, a multivariate mixed-effects model was constructed, and the model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, F1 score, absolute difference parameters, Brier score and calibration curves. This study included 5,848 patients with a total of 78,885 patient-years of data. Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that age, BMI, gender, operation, number of lymph nodes dissected and positive lymph nodes, S100, CD34, D2-40, MMR, TNM stage, chemotherapy, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, surgery time, obstruction, differentiation and tumor site were risk factors for colorectal cancer patients. The China Colorectal Cancer - Dynamic and Integrated System for Predicting Outcomes (CCC-DISPO) model demonstrated high performance, with the high values reached in the external validation at Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital and PLA General Hospital, with AUC values of 0.87 and 0.78. Subgroup analysis showed that in stage I patients, stable high values were maintained throughout the first 12 months, while for stage II, III and IV occurred after the third month. As a dynamic prediction model, CCC-DISPO continuously updates and achieves high precision and stability. Based on the model performance over time, we believe that follow-up at 6 months postoperatively for all patients, 3 months for stage II, III, IV and within 12 months for stage I are of significant importance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41611-2.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Dynamic prediction, Prognosis, Longitudinal study, SurgerySubject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, imposing a significant global health burden1. Although surgery is the primary treatment method, postoperative follow-up is also of paramount significance to patient survival2. Acknowledging the potential for relapse and metastasis, coupled with the financial implications, it is frequently a substantial challenge for patients to adhere to the scheduled follow-up visits at hospitals as stipulated by clinical guidelines. Furthermore, it poses a significant difficulty for physicians to distinguish genuinely pertinent information from the vast array of follow-up reports. Therefore, constructing a dynamic and high-performance prediction model and exploring the optimal follow-up time and the number of follow-ups based on it can enhance clinical application and patient management3.

Since 2015, numerous predictive models have been developed, yet their accuracy and stability still need to be improved4. Current research indicates that incorporating comprehensive indicators can enhance the performance of predictive models5. Therefore, fully capturing comprehensive patient information and incorporating temporal changes to construct dynamic predictive models based on longitudinal data holds significant importance for patient prognosis.

This study pioneered the development of a comprehensive dynamic prediction model for postoperative prognosis in colorectal cancer patients. The AUC values at distinct time points were posited to reflect the prognostic utility of follow-up assessments at those junctures for long-term survival outcomes. Consequently, we elected to scrutinize time points characterized by elevated AUC values in order to discern the most advantageous intervals for conducting follow-up evaluations. This work was carried out in a large retrospective patient cohort from Xijing Hospital, as well as Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital and the PLA General Hospital. We constructed the China Colorectal Cancer - Dynamic and Integrated System for Predicting Outcomes (CCC-DISPO) model using a multivariate mixed-effects model, which is effective for integrating longitudinal parameters recorded at any time interval and prognostic factors that do not change over time6,7. This approach has been found to be clinically relevant in multiple health domains, including oncology8, infectious diseases9, cardiovascular disease10. This work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria and no artificial intelligence tools were employed in the conduct of this research or in the preparation of the manuscript11.

Methods

Research design and participating centers

In this multicenter cohort study, we collected data from 5,848 patients across three centers from January 1, 2011 to December 1, 2024, with the longest follow-up period reaching 5,077 days. The follow-up schedule consisted of clinic visits every three months during the first three years and every six months thereafter. Follow-up was conducted through scheduled in-hospital evaluations supplemented by telephone interviews. To thoroughly validate the model’s effectiveness and generalizability, we conducted model testing through three phases: training, internal validation, and external validation. The patient cohort from Xijing Hospital was completely randomly divided into a training group and an internal validation group in a 7:3 ratio. The cohorts from Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital and the PLA General Hospital served as the external validation sets.

The data from the Xijing Hospital cohort were derived from a clinical research, which has obtained ethical approval and has been registered. The data from the other centers were collected from patients who underwent colorectal cancer surgery at Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital, and the PLA General Hospital in China between 2011 and 2023.

This study is a multicohort, observational study using de-identified data. All participants signed a paper-based informed consent form. Meanwhile, informed consent from patients had been obtained during the clinical research phase for the data used in this study. The study protocol follows the ethical guidelines of the 1995 Declaration of Helsinki, and prior approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Xijing Hospital.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age ≥ 18 years. (2) Primary colorectal carcinoma confirmed by preoperative pathology. (3) Pathologically diagnosed as adenocarcinoma. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Non-unifocal primary cancer lesions; (2) Incomplete clinical data. (3) Abnormal or unclear clinical data.(4) All cases with severe heart, lung, brain diseases or serious infections. (5) All cases with coagulation dysfunction or severe blood disorders. (6) Unresectable colorectal cancer. Unresectable colorectal cancer. The flowchart of patient selection and model construction was illustrated in Supplymentary Figure.

Data collection and preprocessing

The diagnosis and treatment of colorectal cancer are based on the National Cancer Comprehensive Network (NCCN) 2021 Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines. The data from Xijing Hospital was obtained from clinical research, so it did not include any missing values. The data from PLA General Hospital and Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital contained less than 20% missing values for CD34, D2-40, and CA125, which were imputed using the random forest algorithm.

The repeated data from the Xijing Hospital center were collected from previous clinical studies, while the external validation data were obtained from the online medical record systems of each center. The initial data collection corresponded to the hematological tests conducted on the day of surgery. According to the requirements of the NCCN guidelines, Stage I patients: Postoperative follow-up with CEA testing once a year. Stage II and III patients: Postoperative follow-up with CEA testing every 6 to 12 months for the first 5 years. Stage IV patients: Postoperative follow-up with CEA testing every 3 to 6 months for the first 2 years, and then every 6 to 12 months for a total of 5 years12. In this study, initial data were collected from examinations performed within 24 h post-operation. Each patient should undergo at least four repeated measurements. Any additional data measurements beyond these were determined based on clinical necessity and the patients’ own preferences.

Statistical analysis

This study aimed to predict the postoperative survival of colorectal cancer patients by establishing a dynamic multivariate mixed-effects model. The endpoint event was defined as death due to the tumor or tumor-related causes.

We described continuous variables using the median and categorical variables using frequencies. We compared medians between groups using analysis of variance (or the Mann-Whitney U test, if appropriate) and compared the frequency distributions of categorical variables using non-parametric tests (or the Pearson chi-square test, if appropriate).

To construct a comprehensive dynamic prediction model based on patients’ clinical, histological, and repeatedly measured hematological indicators, we reviewed previous studies and ultimately employed a joint model combining multivariate mixed-effects models and Cox proportional hazards regression. This is a dynamic prediction model for longitudinal data, which demonstrates strong performance in evaluating the impact of influencing factors on patients’ survival over time and is the most widely used dynamic prediction model by researchers13. To assess the impact of repeatedly measured data at a specific time point, we successively considered the updated measurements, the slope of all observed measurements, and the cumulative effect of the area under all observed measurements.

The accuracy performance of the dynamic prediction model was assessed through the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. In terms of model validation, we evaluated the model’s performance using absolute difference parameters, Brier score, and calibration curves. The absolute difference parameters demonstrated the similarity between predicted risks and actual outcomes14. The Brier score demonstrated a robust capacity to quantify predictive inaccuracies15.

We have verified the accuracy and robustness of the model under different circumstances, and the results are highly credible.

Data processing, statistical analysis, and visualization were conducted using R (version 4.4.1).The R packages involved in this study include: JMbayes2, survival, dlookr, visdat, gtsummary, DT, PerformanceAnalytics. Statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Develop and validate CCC-DISPO model

To explore the independent risk factors affecting the survival of patients with colorectal cancer after surgery, we conducted a Cox multivariate analysis on the non-dynamic factors in the internal validation cohort. The results showed that surgical method, gender, age, number of lymph nodes dissected, number of positive lymph nodes, CD34, D2-40, TNM stage, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, mismatch repair protein, surgical time, presence of obstruction, degree of differentiation, tumor location, ki67, and BMI were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Therefore, we included these 18 variables in the Cox survival analysis model. Additionally, through multicollinearity analysis, we excluded neutrophil percentage and hematocrit. The remaining 8 longitudinal variables were fitted into 8 mixed-effects models, which, together with the Cox model, constitute the CCC-DISPO joint model.

We developed the CCC-DISPO model in postoperative colorectal cancer patients. The model demonstrated its performance in predicting the prognosis of individual patients (Fig. 1). Through the function of cumulative survival of patients with three variables, as independent risk factors, albumin and white blood cells had a smaller slope of the fitted line, while the tumor marker alpha-fetoprotein negatively regulated patient survival with increasing time. The CCC-DISPO model demonstrated in the internal validation that, during the five-year follow-up, the dynamic AUC of the model in colorectal cancer patients ranged from 0.65 to 0.81, with a final stable AUC of 0.80. The model achieved the highest AUC of 0.81 at six months postoperatively. The calibration plot was used to display the model’s accuracy and stability16, indicating that the model has long-term robust predictive power (Fig. 2). Then, we tested the external applicability of the model. In the external validation cohort at PLXijing Hospital, the model achieved its peak performance at the patient’s 9-month follow-up, with an AUC of 0.87, and then slightly declined, stabilizing after two years (Fig. 3A). In the cohort at Shaanxi Provincial People’s Hospital, the model still reached a high value at 9 months, with an AUC of 0.78, and then stabilized (Fig. 3B).

Explore the optimal follow-up time and conduct subgroup analyses

Previous studies have already established postoperative follow-up strategies for colorectal cancer patients12. The outcomes were based on circulating tumor DNA and colonoscopy17,18. Therefore, this study investigates the optimal follow-up intervals for postoperative colorectal cancer patients by utilizing a novel dynamic prediction model based on follow-up examination items.

We combined patients from three centers and categorized them into Stage I to Stage IV based on the presence or absence of lymph node metastasis, which has different follow-up standards in the NCCN guidelines. We then used the CCC-DISPO model to make predictions for patients in stage I and those in stages II to IV separately (Fig. 4). The calibration plot indicated that the model performs stably in both groups. For stage I patients, the model achieved the highest prediction performance at 3 and 6 months, with an AUC of 0.97. In patients with stages II, III, the model reached its peak performance at 6 months, with an AUC of 0.85, after which the model’s performance stabilized. The model performance for stage I patients was relatively high within the first year, decreased during the period from 1 to 3 years, and then stabilized after 3 years. Moreover, the model performance for patients with stage II, III cancer remained consistently good over 5 years, demonstrating long-term stability. For stage IV patients, the model achieved the highest AUC in the first month of follow-up. However, the calibration curve performed poorly and failed to reflect its predictive performance. Subsequently, the model demonstrated sustained high performance. The detailed AUC change charts showed that in all groups of patients, the model performance reached a relatively high level only after 3 months.

Discussion

In this multicenter observational study, based on 5,848 patients from three national centers, we developed and validated the first China Colorectal Cancer - Dynamic and Integrated System for Predicting Outcomes (CCC-DISPO) model to predict the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients. Based on the CCC-DISPO model, we explored the optimal follow-up time for colorectal cancer patients, aiming to optimize the balance between patient prognosis and medical costs. This model is the first dynamic prediction model for the prognosis of colorectal cancer patients to date, and this study is the first to provide a theoretical evidence for determining the optimal follow-up time through longitudinal data.

Our dynamic prediction model showed improved discrimination and calibration compared with earlier static models19,20. In this study, we employed a joint modeling approach to establish a multivariate mixed-effects model. Considering individual patient differences and measurement errors, we treated patients as random effects. Fixed effects are generally stable factors associated with the outcome21. Since gender is a prognostic-related risk factor with a stable influence, we selected gender as a fixed effect. On the one hand, by continuously integrating all tumor markers and other parameters assessed during follow-up, the model guides clinicians in monitoring and managing postoperative colorectal cancer patients. On the other hand, based on the model’s high performance and generalizability, we explored the optimal follow-up intervals and frequency for postoperative colorectal cancer patients, offering our team’s insights for standardizing follow-up strategies.

The analysis of influencing factors and cumulative survival of patients demonstrated the model’s individual prediction performance. This is of great significance for guiding clinical practice. Previous predictive models were only limited to a single time point22. Whether data from a single time point can predict the long-term condition of patients, and whether patients who miss the time point can be predicted for survival, have all raised questions for previous predictive models. The CCC-DISPO model has provided the answer from our team. Through validation using data from several representative centers in China, we believe that integrating longitudinal data from multiple time points is more accurate and practical.

Based on the good performance of the CCC-DISPO model in longitudinal data, we explored the optimal follow-up time for colorectal cancer patients after surgery. The follow-up guidelines from NCCN and ESMO are primarily based on colonoscopy rather than on tumor markers, liver function, and nutritional indicators found in routine follow-up examinations. Additionally, there are differences in the follow-up strategies between the NCCN and ESMO guidelines23,24. This study directly utilized follow-up examination items to summarize the optimal follow-up times, and its conclusions are characterized by direct credibility25. In settings where colonoscopy is unavailable, the model’s 6-month optimal time point can guide targeted use of limited imaging resources (e.g., abdominal ultrasound) for high-risk patients identified by the model. However, this study is only a Phase I effort; the potential of interventions implemented within specific time windows to improve patient prognosis warrants further in-depth investigation. The model was constructed based on dynamic re-examination data, and we judged the optimal prediction time through changes in the model’s predictive performance. In the overall patient cohort, the model’s AUC reached the highest value at six months and continued to perform well over the first three years, with excellent calibration curves. Accordingly, the follow-up outcomes within the first six months were more effective in predicting and guiding long-term outcomes. After three years, the frequency of check-ups can be reasonably reduced to avoid over-treatment and mitigated the economic strain on the healthcare system. Our results also explained the follow-up strategy recommended by the NCCN guidelines. For patients with stage I colorectal cancer, the model performance was relatively high at 3 and 6 months. After one year, the performance decreased. We suggest that the frequency of follow-up examinations can be appropriately increased within the first year, and the first examination can be moved forward to 3 months. After one year, the number of follow-ups can be reduced. For patients with stage II, III colorectal cancer, the model performance was relatively high at 3 and 6 months and remains stable during the follow-up period. Therefore, we recommend starting follow-up examinations at 3 months and continuing regularly. For stage IV patients, we cannot ascertain the stability of the model’s prediction at the 1-month time point. Therefore, based on the performance at other time points, we suggest that follow-up should commence at 3 months and be maintained regularly for at least 3 years. For all groups of patients, we found no evidence that examinations performed earlier than three months provide significant prognostic value for long-term prognosis. This result further proves the rationality of the NCCN guidelines.

Our study has several limitations. First, the existing model only includes convenient data and does not incorporate imaging indicators such as colonoscopy. Although the model parameters can reflect the overall condition of patients, combining it with image prediction models based on artificial intelligence may further enhance its performance26–28. Second, although the included centers are all national centers in China, the number of centers from other countries still needs to be increased to derive universally applicable standards. Moreover, the insufficient sample size of patients in subgroup analyses may introduce additional bias. Third, although the model has demonstrated high predictive performance in multicenter data, it needs to be implemented in real-life settings. Due to the lack of multicenter clinical validation and long-term patient follow-up, the conclusions of this study should only serve as a reference for methodological innovation rather than a basis for inclusion in clinical guidelines. Furthermore, this study is still subject to selection bias and potential confounding factors, to accurately assess the actual application of the model and its impact on the medical and economic burden of patients, the research team plans to conduct a randomized controlled trial, while also verifying the benefits to patients brought by the number of follow-ups.

Conclusion

We constructed and validated a dynamic and comprehensive system for predicting postoperative survival rates in cancer patients and exploring the optimal follow-up time. For stage I patients, follow-up examination results within the first year after surgery are significantly instructive for survival rates, and postoperative follow-up examinations should be conducted every 3 to 6 months. For patients with stage II to IV disease, results after three months should be focused on.
