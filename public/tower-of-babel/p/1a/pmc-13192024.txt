Identification of ovarian cancer-associated antigens forming complexes with autoantibodies
Authors: Mimori Fujimori, Kai Uryu, Makoto Kobayashi, Kotaro Sugimoto, Yuta Endo, Shigenori Furukawa, Shu Soeda, Yoshio Kodera, Keiya Fujimori, Hideki Chiba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumor-associated antigen (Ag)-autoantibody (AAb) complexes are known to be present in the circulation of patients with multiple types of cancer; however, their significance remains poorly understood.MethodsWe performed immunoblotting of whole cell extracts of the human ovarian cancer cell lines AMOC2, OVCAR3 and SKOV3 with either free antibodies (Abs) or antigen-dissociated Abs obtained from ovarian cancer ascites and healthy sera. In addition, Ag-AAb complexes isolated from ovarian cancer ascites were analyzed by mass spectrometry to identify tumor-associated antigens. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was then used to evaluate the presence of one extracellular matrix (ECM) candidate antigen, SPON1, in its free versus complexed form in ascites and sera.ResultsImmunoblotting revealed that tumor-associated AAbs in ovarian cancer ascites predominantly exist in Ag-AAb complexes rather than as free AAbs. Proteomic profiling identified 72 tumor-associated antigens enriched in ovarian cancer ascites compared with healthy and disease control sera; more than half were ECM- or epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related proteins. ELISA demonstrated that SPON1 was preferentially detected as Ag-AAb complexes, but not in its free form, in both ascites and sera of ovarian cancer patients.ConclusionWe uncovered that AAbs predominantly form complexes with tumor-associated antigens in ovarian cancer patients. Since the presence of Ag-AAb complexes has been overlooked in many reports, the identification of tumor-associated antigens and AAbs from Ag-AAb complexes would provide novel insights into their relevance to a variety of cancers and other diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02089-8.Keywords: Autoantibody, Tumor-associated antigen, Antigen-autoantibody complex, Cancer, Epithelial ovarian cancer, SPON1

Introduction

It was estimated that in 2024, globally, over 320,000 people were diagnosed with ovarian cancer, and nearly 210,000 died of the disease [1]. High-grade serous carcinoma represents the most common histological subtype, and others include low-grade serous, endometrioid, clear cell and mucinous ovarian cancers, each of which exhibits distinct histological and genetic features [2]. Ovarian cancer has the worst outcome among gynecological malignancies because the diagnosis tends to be delayed due to the asymptomatic nature and the lack of effective screening tools [3]. Multiple studies have been directed towards the development of biomarkers that detect ovarian cancer at early and treatable stages. Cancer antigen-125 (CA-125) is currently the best-characterized biomarker for ovarian cancer [4]. However, its sensitivity and specificity are not enough to effectively screen ovarian cancer. The sensitivity of CA-125 is quite low not only at the early stages of ovarian cancer (about 25% of cases), but also in ovarian cancer types other than high-grade serous ovarian cancer. Additionally, CA-125 levels can be high in various benign and malignant diseases. Moreover, the UK Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening (UKCTOCS), in which more than 200,000 participants were included, reported no significant benefit in ovarian cancer survival with CA-125 testing together with transvaginal ultrasound screenings [5]. Human epididymis protein 4 (HE4) is another Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved biomarker for ovarian cancer, but its sensitivity and specificity are insufficient, like CA-125 [6]. Furthermore, many investigations have failed to identify biomarkers with higher sensitivity and specificity than CA-125 [7].

Tumor-associated autoantibodies (AAbs) are produced against overexpressed, mutated or post-translationally modified proteins in patients with a range of cancer types [8, 9]. They can be detected at an early stage of cancer in sera even before the onset of clinical symptoms, and possess high stability [10–12]. However, only a limited number of tumor-associated AAbs have been so far approved by the FDA as cancer biomarkers mainly due to the absence of an appropriate validation method. With regard to ovarian cancer, the anti-TP53 AAb is most extensively studied [13], and a combination of serum anti-TP53 AAb and CA-125 contributes to enhanced early detection of ovarian cancer [14]. Additionally, it has been attempted to isolate various AAbs for ovarian cancer diagnosis [15–18].

In addition to free AAbs as described above, the presence of antigen-AAb (Ag-AAb) complexes in the circulation of cancer patients has been reported. For example, Sato et al. demonstrated that the cofilin1-AAb complex is detected in the sera of patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and serves as a poor prognostic biomarker [19]. It has also been reported that the HE4-AAb complex occurs in the sera of ovarian cancer patients, and could complement CA-125 to diagnose the early stage of ovarian cancer [20]. Furthermore, a variety of the Ag-AAb complexes have been detected in the sera of patients with several types of cancer [21–25]. Nevertheless, compared to free AAbs, the importance of the Ag-AAb complexes in cancer remains unclear. Moreover, there are few reports on comprehensive analyses of tumor-associated antigens dissociated from Ag-AAb complexes.

Cancerous ascites is useful as a starting material to identify tumor-specific biomarkers, though it happens at a late stage of the disease. In fact, concerning ovarian cancer, cancerous ascites fluid is used as a source to find tumor-selective AAbs [16, 18, 26]. Of note, Mazor et al. revealed that the amount of tumor-associated AAbs in ovarian cancer ascites is significantly greater than that in ovarian cancer sera [27]. Therefore, we utilized in the present study ovarian cancer ascites at the first step to determine the relevance of the Ag-AAb complexes. We herein showed that tumor-associated AAbs are predominantly present as Ag-IgG AAb complexes, but not as free IgG AAbs, in ovarian cancer ascites. We also identified, via proteome analysis, 72 tumor-associated antigens from the Ag-AAb complexes, whose amounts in ovarian cancer ascites were much greater than those in healthy or disease control sera. Among them, via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, we demonstrated that the ECM protein spondin-1 (SPON1) predominantly appears as the Ag-AAb complex beyond free SPON1 in ovarian cancer ascites and sera.

Materials and methods

Key experimental procedures are described in the Materials and Methods, and detailed protocols are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Sample collection

Information on samples from ovarian cancer patients, disease controls (gynecological patients), and healthy controls is listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture

The human ovarian cancer cell lines OVCAR3 (RCB2135) and SKOV3 (HTB-77) were obtained from RIKEN bioresource center (Ibaraki, Japan) and American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), respectively. AMOC2 [28] was kindly provided by Dr. Saito (Sapporo Medical University). They were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Merck Millipore) and a 1% penicillin–streptomycin mixture (Merck Millipore).

All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C under normal oxygen conditions (5% CO2), used at low passages, and routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination by DAPI staining every month. Mycoplasma-positive cells were treated for one week using mycoplasma removal reagent (MC-210, Wakenbtech, Kyoto, Japan). In addition, we have confirmed that SKOV3 cells match those of the referenced SKOV3 cells in the CLASTR 1.4.4 database by short tandem repeat analysis (BEX Co., Ltd, Tokyo, Japan). Since OVCAR3 was recently obtained from RIKEN BioResource Center and AMOC2 was directly provided by a establisher of the cell line, STR analysis was not performed for these cell lines.

Purification of immunoglobulins from serum and ascites fluid

Five-hundred mL of ascites fluid was obtained from ovarian cancer patients and subjected to centrifugation at 2,000 rpm for 15 min to eliminate cellular debris. To precipitate immunoglobulins (Igs), 100 µL of either ascites fluid or healthy serum was diluted at a 1:4 ratio with PBS, followed by the addition of saturated ammonium sulfate to achieve a final concentration of 37.5%. For the isolation of free Abs, the resulting pellet was resuspended in PBS. To dissociate Ag-Ab complexes, the pellet was resuspended in 0.1 M glycine at pH 3.0.

Protein pretreatment and digestion for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis

Igs were purified from healthy sera, disease controls (gynecological patients) sera or ovarian cancer ascites as described above, and diluted to 1:10 with PBS, followed by addition of 400 µL pretreated PureProteome Protein G Magnet Beads (LSKMAGG, Merck Millipore). They were rotated gently for 1 h at RT, and the tubes were then placed onto the magnetic stand. After removing and discarding the supernatant, the beads were washed 10 times with PBS, and IgG-bound antigens were extracted as described previously [29, 30] with the following modifications. Eighty µL of a solution of 7 M urea, 2 M thiourea, and 20 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to each tube and vortexed for 1 h. The magnetically separated eluates were slowly dropped into 2 mL of ice-cold acetone, immediately stirred at 4 ℃ for a few seconds, and then centrifuged at 19,000 ×g for 15 min at 4℃. The precipitate was resuspended in 400 µL of 70% acetonitrile containing 12 mM HCl, mixed at 4℃ for 1 h, centrifuged again, and the supernatant was lyophilized and stored at -80 ℃. The immunoglobulin-bound proteins were dissolved in 40 µL of 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate/12 mM sodium deoxycholate/12 mM sodium lauryl sulfate, mixed for 10 min at RT and sonicated for 10 min at 4 ℃. These samples were centrifuged at 19,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 ℃, and 20 µL of the supernatant was digested essentially as described by Masuda et al. [31] with the following modifications. Two µL of solution of 200 mM tris (2-carboxylethyl) phosphine hydrochloride and 120 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate was added to samples, and incubated at 50℃ for 30 min. After adding 2 µL of 375 mM iodoacetamide, the samples were incubated in the dark for 30 min. Two µL of Lys-C (100 ng/µL, 125–05061, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemicals) and 2 µL of trypsin (100 ng/µL, 90057, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to each sample, which was then incubated at 37℃ for 18 h. Thirty µL of 10% acetonitrile and 15 µL of 5% trifluoroacetic acid were added to the digest of each sample, which was centrifuged at 19,000 ×g for 15 min and the supernatant was recovered. The samples were desalted using Stop and Go Extraction Tips (Stage Tips) filled with Empore C18 (Octadecyl) sealant (3 M Co., Saint Paul, MN, USA). Peptides were eluted with 80 µL of 70% acetonitrile containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The sample solution was then lyophilized and redissolved in 20 µL of 3% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis and protein identification

Samples were injected onto a C18 0.075 × 120 mm analytical column (Nano HPLC Capillary Column; Nikkyo Technos, Tokyo, Japan) attached to an EASY-nLC 1000 HPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid and 90% acetonitrile), and the mobile phase gradient was programmed as follows: (0–5 min, 0–5% B; 5–42 min, 5–32% B; 42–57 min, 32–55% B; 57–58 min 55–95% B). Separated peptides were subjected to Q Exactive for data-independent acquisition (DIA)-MS. MS1 spectra were obtained at 17,500 resolutions in the range of 350–770 m/z to set an automatic gain control target of 3 × 106 ions and a maximum injection time of 20 msec, and MS2 spectra were collected at > 200 m/z at 35,000 resolutions to set an automatic gain control target of 3 × 106 ions, the maximum injection time to “auto”, and collision energy of 22%. The isolation width for MS2 was set to 9 m/z, and window patterns of 500–770 m/z were used. DIA files were assessed using the DIA-NN software (v.1.6.0) with fixed modifications of carbamidomethyl and a variable modification of Met oxidation. Protein identification was filtered to a protein and precursor-level false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1% and required a minimum of one unique peptide.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Fifty-microliters of an anti-SPON1 polyclonal antibody (2 µg/mL) (AF3135, R&D systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was coated onto Nunc-Immuno MaxiSorp plates (439454, Thermo Fisher) overnight at 4℃. After washing with PBS, non-specific reactivity was blocked by 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA)/PBS for 1 h at 37℃. The wells were incubated with 100-fold diluted free Abs or antigen-dissociated Abs for 1 h at 37℃. After washing with tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T), the plates were incubated with an anti-SPON1 monoclonal antibody (2 µg/mL) (sc-390182, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) for 1 h at 37℃, followed by 1:2,000-times diluted biotinylated donkey anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (715-065-150, Jackson Immunoresearch) for 1 h at 37℃. Plates were washed 3-times with TBS-T, then incubated with 1:10,000-diluted HRP-conjugated streptavidin (P03970, Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for 1 h at 37℃. Signal development was performed using 3,3’,5,5’ tetramethyl benzidine substrate kit (421101, Biolegend, San Diego, CA). Absorbance at 490 nm was measured by Microplate Reader (AS ONE, Osaka, Japan) and signal was quantified using GraphPad Prism v10.5.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.5.0 software (GraphPad Software). Differences between free Abs and Ag-AAb, as well as between healthy controls and ovarian cancer samples, were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test (two-tailed). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA v4.3.2 software and hallmark gene sets which are publicly available from the Broad Institute. Statistical significance between free and dissociated SPON1 levels were evaluated using the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test (two-tailed). Correlation between two independent mass spectrometry analyses was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient.

Results

Ascites from ovarian cancer patients contains tumor-associated AAbs

We first determined, by immunofluorescence analysis using frozen sections, whether ovarian cancer tissues exhibit different reactivities against ascites from ovarian cancer patients and sera from healthy controls. As shown in Fig. 1A, strong nuclear and/or cytoplasmic signals were observed in ovarian cancer tissues treated with the ovarian cancer ascites (cases #1 and #2). These positive signals disappeared when the sections were exposed to the ascites, in which IgG had been absorbed by Protein G. However, upon addition of healthy sera (cases #1 and #2), no obvious reaction was detected in ovarian cancer tissues (Fig. 1B). These results reinforced that ascites from ovarian cancer patients includes tumor-associated AAbs in good agreement with previous reports [18, 27, 32, 33].

Frozen sections of ovarian cancer tissues were exposed to ascites from ovarian cancer patients (A) or healthy serum (B) with and without IgG absorption, followed by incubation with goat-anti human IgG secondary antibody. Scale bars, 100 μm

Tumor-associated AAbs are primarily present as the Ag-AAb complexes in ascites from ovarian cancer patients

Since ovarian cancer ascites contained tumor-associated AAbs as described above, we subsequently verified the relevance of Ag-AAb complexes using them. In brief, we performed immunoblotting of total proteins derived from AMOC2, OVCAR3, and SKOV3 cells with either free Abs or antigen-dissociated Abs, both of which were obtained from Ig proteins of ovarian cancer ascites and healthy sera (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Figure S1A and B), as primary Abs. The signal intensity of positive bands against antigen-dissociated IgG Abs from ovarian cancer ascites was stronger than against free IgG Abs from healthy sera (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Figure S2). On the other hand, there were no apparent differences in responses to either free and antigen-dissociated IgA Abs or free and antigen-dissociated IgM Abs between ovarian cancer ascites and healthy sera.

C, D The representative 2-DE immunoblot images and the quantitative signal intensity for free IgG Abs and antigen-dissociated IgG Abs from healthy serum or ovarian cancer ascites in OVCAR3 cells

Since AAbs in ovarian cancer ascites mainly existed as the IgG class, we then applied 2-DE using whole cell extracts of OVCAR3 cells prepared from an equal mixture of three cell lines (AMOC2, OVCAR3, and SKOC3), and immunoblotted them with either free IgG Abs or antigen-dissociated IgG Abs that were obtained from ovarian cancer ascites and healthy sera (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Figure S3). As shown in Fig. 2D, the signal intensity of positive spots against antigen-dissociated IgG Abs from ovarian cancer ascites was significantly stronger than that against free IgG Abs (P = 0.016). The positive signals against antigen-dissociated IgG Abs from ovarian cancer ascites were also significantly high compared with those from healthy sera (P = 0.042). Thus, these results indicated that AAbs predominantly form complexes with tumor-associated antigens in ovarian cancer ascites.

Identification of ovarian cancer-associated antigens from the Ag-AAb complexes

To identify ovarian cancer-associated antigens from the Ag-AAb complexes, we next conducted LC-MS/MS analysis (Fig. 3A). In brief, free IgG Abs, as well as dissociated antigens and IgG Abs, were purified from healthy sera, disease control (gynecological patients) sera, and ovarian cancer ascites, and subjected to tryptic digestion, followed by proteome analysis. LC-MS/MS analysis was performed twice for each sample, and the correlation coefficients in healthy control, disease control, and ovarian cancer groups were 0.999, 0.999, and 0.997, respectively (Supplementary Figure S4; P < 0.001). Among a total of 1,697 proteins, we selected 331 non-Ig proteins and focused on 72 proteins, which levels in ovarian cancer ascites were 3-fold higher level than those in healthy and disease control sera (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S2). The signature of the identified ovarian cancer-associated antigens, which formed complexes with AAb in ascites fluid, consisted of 24 ECM (extracellular matrix) proteins (33.3%), 18 EMT (epithelial-mesenchymal transition)-related proteins (25.0%), 12 cytoskeletal proteins (16.7%), nine immunity-related proteins (12.5%), and eight metabolism-related proteins (11.1%). Moreover, the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that EMT-related proteins, including MMP2, TIMP1, and VIM, were significantly enriched in the Ag-AAb complexes obtained from ovarian cancer ascites (Fig. 3C; P = 0.03).

SPON1 preferentially exists as the Ag-AAb complex in ascites and sera of ovarian cancer patients

To verify whether ovarian cancer-associated Ag in ascites and sera presents as a free form and/or the Ag-AAb complex, among the identified cancer-associated antigens, we focused on SPON1 because of the following reasons: (1) The SPON1 gene is highly expressed in ovarian cancer tissues but not in other cancer tissues [34, 35]; (2) Strong expression of SPON1 protein is observed not only in high-grade serous carcinoma cases but also in non-high-grade serous carcinoma subjects [35]; (3) High SPON1 expression represents an independent prognostic biomarker for ovarian cancer [35]. To this end, 293T cells were transiently transfected with the His-tagged hSPON1 expression vector, and SPON1 protein was purified and used as a standard control (Fig. 4A and B; Supplementary Figure S5). As shown in Fig. 4C, dissociated SPON1, but not free SPON1, was detected in ascites of 2 (each one case of high-grade serous carcinoma and clear cell carcinoma) out of 7 cases of ovarian cancer. Dissociated serum SPON1 was also identified in 9 (four cases of high-grade serous carcinoma, three cases of clear cell carcinoma, and two cases of endometrioid carcinoma) out of 18 cases of ovarian cancer (Fig. 4D), while only one case (clear cell carcinoma) out of these 9 cases showed the detectable level of free serum SPON1. Additionally, the serum levels of dissociated SPON1 were significantly higher than those of free SPON1 (P = 0.004). Thus, SPON1 principally appeared as the Ag-AAb complex in ascites and sera of patients with ovarian cancer.

Discussion

We found in the present study that tumor-associated AAbs predominantly exist as Ag-IgG AAb complexes, but not as free IgG AAbs, in ovarian cancer ascites (Fig. 5). This conclusion was evidenced by comparing the signal intensity of one-dimensional SDS-PAGE and 2-DE immunoblots of ovarian cancer cell extracts with either free IgG Abs or antigen-dissociated IgG Abs from ovarian cancer ascites and healthy sera. Since a series of studies regarding tumor-associated AAbs have focused only on free AAbs (For review, see references [15, 17]), the presence of Ag-AAb complexes has been overlooked in such reports. The research on dissociated Ag-AAb complexes would bring a new era of tumor-associated antigens and/or AAbs. Additionally, our results revealed that free and dissociated AAbs in ovarian cancer ascites mainly appeared as the IgG class, which is in good agreement with previous studies reporting that tumor-associated AAbs in sera are IgG class [36]. However, large-scale analyses would be required to obtain more solid conclusions on the significance of the Ag-AAb complexes in ovarian cancer ascites.

Schematic illustration showing a possible abundance of the Ag-AAb complexes in ovarian cancer serum and ascites. A variety of tumor-associated antigens (Ags) and autoantibodies (AAbs) are indicated as different colors

Our proteome analysis identified 72 tumor-associated antigens, whose levels in ovarian cancer ascites were much higher than those in healthy and disease control sera, from the Ag-AAb complexes. Among their signature, the most frequent ones represented ECM-associated proteins (33.3%), such as COL1A1, COL3A1, COL6A1, COL6A3, EFEMP1, EMILIN2, FBLN1, FBLN5, HSPG2, LAMA5, LAMB1, LAMC1, and SPON1. The second most common ovarian cancer-associated antigens were EMT-related proteins (25.0%), including DMKN, MMP2, TIMP1, TIMP2, and VIM. GSEA also revealed that EMT-related proteins were significantly augmented in the Ag-AAb complexes obtained from ovarian cancer ascites, further supporting the significance of EMT molecules in tumor-associated antigens for ovarian cancer. Thus, ECM and EMT proteins accounted for more than half of the ovarian cancer-associated antigens that formed complexes with AAbs. It is unknown why HE4 [37] and nuclear proteins [38, 39] were not isolated by our proteome analysis using ovarian cancer ascites, but it might be explained by the differences of the detection system between ours and previous reports. In fact, nuclear signals were detected in ovarian cancer tissues reacted to the ovarian cancer ascites (Fig. 1), suggesting a probability of this notion.

Concerning MMP2, it has been reported that serum levels of free anti-MMP2 AAbs are higher in bladder cancer patients than in normal control [40]. On the other hand, free anti-TIMP1 AAbs are reported to be detected in the sera of patients with esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cancer [41]. Additionally, free anti-VIM AAbs are known to be present in ovarian cancer sera [42]. Furthermore, the VIM-AAb complexes can be detected in sera of patients with breast cancer [43]. It should also be noted that MMP14 is known to be overexpressed by ovarian cancer, resulting in production of MMP14 AAbs [27]. Taken collectively, these results indicate that EMT-related proteins serve as a major source of tumor-associated antigens to produce AAbs.

It is also noteworthy to discuss the spondin-1 (SPON1), which was identified in the present study as one of the ovarian cancer-associated antigens and recognized by antigen-dissociated Abs in ovarian cancer ascites and sera. SPON1 is an ECM protein that belongs to the thrombospondin family [44]. We have recently developed a monoclonal antibody that selectively recognizes SPON1 and showed that SPON1 mRNA and protein were highly expressed in ovarian cancer and serous intraepithelial carcinoma tissues [35]. By contrast, it is not detected in a variety of normal tissues, such as the brain, heart, liver, kidney, lung, pancreas, testis, thyroid, and uterus tissues. We have also demonstrated that the 5-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) rate in the SPON1-high group was significantly lower than that in the SPON1-low group and that SPON1-high is an independent prognostic biomarker for RFS of ovarian cancer. In addition, it has been very recently shown that SPON1 could be potentially used as a serum biomarker for early-stage diagnosis of ovarian cancer [45]. In the present study, we found that SPON1 is predominantly present as the Ag-AAb complex in ovarian cancer ascites and sera (Fig. 5). Taken collectively with previous findings showing that tumor-associated AAbs can be detected in sera at an early stage of cancer even before the development of clinical symptoms [10–12, 46], it would be quite interesting to determine whether measurement of dissociated SPON1 and/or its AAbs from the SPON1-AAb complexes in sera contributes to early diagnosis for ovarian cancer using a large number of patients with this malignancy.

The present study demonstrated that AAbs primarily form complexes with tumor-associated antigens in ovarian cancer patients. The identification of tumor-associated antigens from Ag-AAb complexes would provide new insights into their relevance in a variety of cancers and other diseases. Since there are few appropriate validation systems to detect tumor-associated AAbs, tumor-associated antigens isolated from Ag-AAb complexes may be beneficial as biomarkers for cancers. Further studies are required to determine whether dissociated tumor-associated antigens and/or their AAbs from Ag-AAb complexes could be used to detect cancer at early and treatable stages, as well as diagnostic and the therapeutic targets.
