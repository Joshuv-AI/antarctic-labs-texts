Regular parental pre-mating exercise reduces offspring white adiposity and is associated with hypothalamic neurodevelopmental changes during lactation
Authors: Celia Muñoz, Carla Canal, Joel Vizueta, Paula Sanchis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Physical inactivity is highly prevalent worldwide and affects not only individual health but also the health of future generations. However, the impact of physical activity limited to the pre-mating period on offspring body weight and composition remains poorly understood. Using a voluntary wheel running approach in mice, we uncovered that post-weaning offspring body weight and composition are modulated by the combined effects of parental pre-mating exercise and parental age. During the mid-stage of lactation, pre-mating exercise in young parents reduced offspring visceral and subcutaneous adiposity, shortened tibial length in female offspring, and influenced offspring transcriptomic profiles of the hypothalamus, the central regulator of energy balance. Pre-mating exercise also led to modest long-lasting changes in the expression of lactation-related genes in maternal subcutaneous fat, as well as breastmilk nutritional composition and miRNA content. Nevertheless, these subtle milk-derived miRNA changes may influence offspring hypothalamic regulatory networks, providing initial evidence of how pre-mating maternal exercise may affect offspring hypothalamic development during the mid-stage of lactation. Together, these data provide a comprehensive understanding of how parental age and pre-mating exercise interact to shape post-weaning offspring body weight and composition, and offer deeper insights into how regular parental pre-mating exercise may influence offspring physiology and neurodevelopmental adaptations during lactation.

Introduction

Physical inactivity has a range of health implications not only for the individual [1], but also for their progeny [2]. In fact, children of active parents are less likely to develop overweight than those born to inactive parents [3]. Furthermore, the high prevalence worldwide of physical inactivity, together with the delay in the timing of conception of the first child [4], is resulting in an elevated risk of a child being born to inactive and older parents [[5], [6], [7]].

In rodents, maternal exercise before and during pregnancy improves offspring glucose metabolism and ameliorates the effects of maternal high-fat-diet on metabolic health in both female and male adult offspring [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Maternal exercise before and during pregnancy also promotes interscapular brown adipose tissue activity and browning of inguinal white adipose tissue in offspring [14], and increases exercise-induced placental superoxide dismutase 3, leading to hepatic epigenetic changes in the offspring [[9], [11]]. Moreover, maternal exercise before and during pregnancy protects adult mouse offspring from diet-induced obesity through circulating and peripheral changes in skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [15,16]. Similarly, paternal exercise improves offspring metabolic health and cognitive function, and mitigates the detrimental effects of a paternal high-fat diet on offspring health [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. Changes in miRNAs content and methylation status of paternal sperm have been proposed as possible mechanisms that program beneficial effects of paternal exercise [[17], [18], [19],21]. Interestingly, the combination of maternal and paternal (parental) exercise exerts additive effects on offspring glucose tolerance associated with endocrinal adaptations in the pancreas [22], and grandparental exercise also impacts metabolic health of second-generation offspring [23,24]. Maternal age at childbirth might represent another contributor to the rising prevalence of obesity, as advanced maternal age is associated with pregnancy complications, adverse perinatal and metabolic outcomes in offspring [25,26]. In the same sense, inherited alterations in sperm due to advanced paternal age have also been associated with increased chronic metabolic risk of their offspring [26,27]. However, it remains unclear whether regular pre-mating exercise in dams and sires is sufficient to modulate offspring body composition and energy balance both under basal and metabolically challenged conditions, and whether these effects are influenced by parental age.

Physical activity is known to regulate energy balance through a complex interaction between energy intake and energy expenditure [28]. The brain, and specifically the hypothalamus, plays a central role in the regulation of whole-body energy balance by controlling both food intake and energy expenditure [29,30]. Previous studies have shown that maternal obesity influences the postnatal hypothalamic transcriptome of offspring [31], underscoring the influence of parental lifestyle on the hypothalamic profile of the offspring. Currently, the direct effect of parental regular exercise on the transcriptomic signature in offspring hypothalami remains unknown. Therefore, we have conducted a comprehensive physiological study of body weight and composition in offspring, under both baseline and metabolic challenge conditions, to uncover the effects of regular pre-mating exercise and age in both parents on offspring phenotype. Furthermore, we have examined how parental pre-mating exercise affects the offspring phenotype during the mid-stage of lactation, providing new insights into hypothalamic adaptations mediated by parental exercise before mating.

Methods

Mouse experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the European directives for the care and use of experimental animals and were approved by the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate (2020-15-0201-00599). Experiments were conducted in adherence with ARRIVE guidelines. Two independent cohorts of mice were purchased from Janvier Labs and housed at the University of Copenhagen (Denmark). In both cohorts, 7-week-old C57BL/6NRj young parents (20 females and 10 males), and 26-week-old C57BL/6JRj old parents (20 females and 10 males) were included. Body composition was assessed using EchoMRI Body Composition Analyzer, and mice were equally assigned, according to body fat, into an active group (with a functional 4¾” x 4.5” running wheel; Starr, Life Sciences Corp.) or a sedentary group (with a blocked running wheel). All animals used in this study were single-housed at 22 ± 2 °C under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to water and chow diet (SAFE D30, Safe Diets, 3.389 kcal/g; 22% protein, 5.3% fat, and 50.8% carbohydrates; www.safe-lab.com). Environmental enrichment included nesting, bedding material, small plastic house and cardboard tube.

Cohort 1

Food intake, running distance and body weight were measured daily during running period and body composition was assessed weekly at ZT10-12 (Figure 1A). Energy expenditure and energy balance was estimated using Virtual Calorimeter v1.82 [32]. After 5 weeks of voluntary wheel running under single-housing conditions, mice from each group were mated separately by housing 2 females and 1 male per cage, matched for fat mass. Litter number and size were recorded during the breeding period. Males were removed from cages after 19 days of mating. In most cages, females delivered on different days, allowing visual differentiation of litters. Pups were tail-labelled, and ear-marked was performed from postnatal day 14 onward. If both females in the same cage delivered on the same day, the mean number of total and surviving pups per female was calculated and considered as 1 litter. Siblings from a given litter remained housed together and offspring body weight was monitored from 2 weeks of age, and offspring body composition was measured weekly after weaning. To minimize the litter effects on the results, one male and one female per litter with more than six pups were selected based on the proximity to mean body weight on day 14, separately by sex. In the two cages where both females delivered on the same day, only a female and a male per cage were included. After weaning, female offspring were fed ad libitum HFD (D12492, 5.24 kcal; 20% energy from protein, 60% energy from fat and 20% energy from carbohydrates, www.researchdiets.com) for 14 days, whereas male offspring were subjected to voluntary wheel running to evaluate the intergenerational effect of running on offspring performance and phenotype. Cohort 2

Running distance was monitored throughout the experiment. Body weight and body composition were assessed at baseline (one week after arrival) and after 6 weeks of running (day 47, Fig. S3A). Given that behavioral studies were conducted sequentially between days 15 and 26, the running period was extended to 6 weeks instead of 5 to minimize potential behavioral testing effects on mating. A polygamous trio breeding strategy, as mentioned above, was used for 5 days to reduce birth timing discrepancies. Each female was then single-housed in a separate cage to accurately record litter identification. During the subsequent 3-week gestation period, birth dates and litter size were recorded. On postnatal day P2, litters were recounted to assess pup loss (including potential maternal cannibalism) and litters were standardized to six pups via random culling [33]. On postnatal day P7, maternal behavior was analyzed by video recording cages for 1 h (3–5 PM). To acclimate animals, cages were removed from the racks and placed in the recording area 24 h prior to filming. On postnatal day P9, both offspring and dams were euthanized, and maternal breastmilk and maternal and offspring tissues were collected.

Specific datapoints from voluntary wheel running protocol were excluded due to measurement errors in both sedentary and running mice, and missing values were replaced by the mean of preceding and subsequent day. When daily measurements were missing entirely, the last and first available values were averaged. Several datapoints were also missed owing to technical problems with the counters. Investigators (C.M. and P.S.) were aware of group allocations in different experiments.

Behavioral tests

Parental behavioral testing was conducted at ZT9-12. Mice were habituated to the behavioral room one week prior to testing. The behavioral battery was performed in the following order: Y-maze, elevated plus maze (EPM) and open field (OF), with several days of resting between tests.

The Y-maze test was used to assess short-term spatial working memory [34]. Mice were placed at the end of one arm of a Y-shaped maze and allowed to explore freely for 8 min. The number of arm entries and spontaneous alternations were recorded. An alternation was defined as successive entries into all three arms without repetition. Arm entries were noted in situ during the test, and the number of correct alternations was calculated using a custom Python script.

The Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) evaluates anxiety-like behavior in a novel and unrestricted environment [35] by analyzing the number of closed- and open-arm entries and the time in each arm for 5 min in Ethovision XT version 11.5 (Noldus) software. Mice with recording issues were excluded from the analysis.

The Open Field (OF) test was used to assess general locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior. Mice were placed in a large square arena (50 × 50 cm) with clear walls and allowed to explore for 5 min. Total distance travelled, time spent in the center and time spent in the periphery were recorded and analysed using the Ethovision XT version 11.5 (Noldus) software. Mice with recording issues were excluded from the analysis.

Nesting behavior, an indicator of general health and parental care ability [36,37] was assessed overnight. A 3-gram cotton fiber material (Ancare Corporation NES3600) was placed in the home cages of female mice at ZT11-12. The following day at ZT4, nests were scored using a 5-point nest-rating scale [36].

Integrated anxiety z-scores were calculated by applying behavioral z-score normalization to open field and elevated plus maze data in males, and to nesting behavior in females, as previously [38].

Maternal presence in the nest was assessed on postnatal day 7 during a 1-hour observation period. Dams were recorded as being present in the nest at 3-minute intervals. To minimize disturbance, observations were conducted in the home cage, which were open-top to allow recording. If a dam was found outside the cage during the observation period, the experimenter briefly entered the room to gently return; these intervention periods were excluded from analysis. Dams that spent more than 50% of the observation period outside the cage were excluded from analysis.

Breast milk collection and nutritional content

Milk was collected from dams of the cohort 2 dams on postnatal day 9. Briefly, dams were separated from their pups for ∼3 h starting at ZT6. During this period, dams were housed in transparent cages placed close to pups’ cage to maintain the sensory stimulus while preventing sucking. At ZT9, dams were intraperitoneally injected with 2 IU/kg oxytocin (Orifarm 10 IE) and rapidly anesthetized by inhalation with 3.0–3.5% isoflurane in oxygen. Breast milk was carefully collected under sterile conditions using a vacuum-assisted syringe connected to a home-made device, as described by Klein and collaborators [39], and stored at −80 °C. One aliquot was used for miRNA content analysis and another for nutritional content analysis.

For nutritional content analyses, an aliquot of breastfeeding milk from dams with 6 pups per littermate was divided into three aliquots for the analysis of carbohydrates, free fatty acids (FFA), and proteins. Carbohydrate content was analyzed in duplicates using a 1:300 dilution prepared according to the Sigma–Aldrich Total Carbohydrate Assay kit protocol (Cat# MAK559). For FFA analysis, 1:50 dilutions were prepared in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 176 μL of kit's assay buffer, 10 μL of 5% Triton X-100, 10 μL of 5% isopropanol, and 4 μL of milk. Milk was homogenized using plastic pestles, and analyzed in duplicate with the Sigma–Aldrich Free Fatty Acid Assay Kit (Cat# MAK466). Protein concentration was determined using Thermo Scientific PierceTM BCA Protein Assay kit (Cat# 23225) relative to a Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) standard. Breastmilk samples were diluted 1:100 in distilled water and absorbance for both standards and samples were quantified. All absorbance measurements were performed using a TECAN Sunrise™ microplate reader, and results were processed and analysed in Excel.

Tissue harvesting

Pups from the cohort 1 were sacrificed by decapitation after the running period (males) or after HFD feeding (females). The right hind limbs were removed and stored in 5 mL tubes containing tap water for one week to facilitate skin removal. Tibial length was then measured using a manual vernier caliper. The hypothalamus, visceral fat and subcutaneous fat were dissected, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Dams from the cohort 2 were sacrificed by decapitation following breast milk collection, and subcutaneous fat samples were collected. Pups from cohort 2 were weighed and euthanized alternately across litters on postnatal day P9 to ensure consistent endpoint timing. Trunk blood was collected and peripheral tissues, including subcutaneous fat, visceral fat, and interscapular brown adipose were weighed, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. The hypothalamus was also snap-frozen immediately for later analysis. The right hind limbs were collected and processed as described above for tibial length measurements.

Offspring sex determination

Sex determination of cohort 2 was performed by genomic DNA extraction followed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the Sry gene, which is exclusive to the Y chromosome. Presence of Sry expression indicated male offspring, while its absence indicated female offspring. DNA extraction was performed following the manufacturer's instructions provided by the Omega Biotek E.Z.N.A.® Circulatory Tissue DNA Kit (Catalog# D3396-02), which was used for efficient DNA isolation from tissue samples. qPCR amplification of the Sry gene was subsequently performed to determine offspring sex.

Gene expression analysis

Subcutaneous fat, visceral fat and hypothalamus from offspring cohort 1, subcutaneous fat from dams of cohort 2 with litters standardized to 6 pups, and subcutaneous fat, brown fat and hypothalamus from male and female offspring of the cohort 2 were used for RNA extraction. RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596018). Tissue samples were homogenized in 1 ml TRIzol® using a TissueLyser (Qiagen) with a metal bead at 25 Hz for 2 cycles of 1,5 min. Homogenates were incubated for 5 min at room temperature (RT), then centrifuged at 12000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The TRIzol-containing supernatant was carefully transferred to a new Eppendorf tube, avoiding the pellet and the upper transparent fat layer. To this new tube, 200 μl of chloroform (Sigma Aldrich, Cat# 34854) was added, samples were shaken for 20 s, incubated for 5 min at RT and centrifuged at 12000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to allow phase separation. The upper aqueous phase, containing RNA, was carefully collected and mixed with an equal volume of ice-cold isopropanol (Sigma Aldrich, Cat# 34863). After vortexing and incubation at RT for 10 min, samples were centrifuged at 12000×g for 15 min at 4 °C to pellet RNA. The RNA pellet was washed twice with ice-cold 75% ethanol (Sigma Aldrich, Cat# 64-17-5), and centrifuged at 8000×g for 5 min at 4 °C. After air-drying, RNA was resuspended in nuclease-free water (Invitrogen, Cat# 10977-035) and stored at −80 °C. RNA concentration was measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and reversed transcribed using cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (1 μl Reverse Transcriptase (RT), 2 μl 10x RT buffer, 0,8 μl dNTP, 2 μl 10x RT random primers, 1 μl RNase inhibitor and 3,2 μl ultrapure nuclease free water, Cat# 4368813, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturers’ instructions. Reverse transcription was carried out under the following program: 25 °C for 10 min, 37 °C for 120 min, 85 °C for 5 min, 4 °C ∞.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using a PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Cat# A25780, ThermoFisher Scientific) with 300 nM forward and reverse primers on a ViiA 7 Real Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Amplification conditions consisted of an initial hold stage at 95 °C for 20 s, followed by 40 PCR cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C and annealing, extension and fluorescence reading at 60 °C. Relative gene quantification was calculated using ΔCt normalized to the sedentary group (fold change). Gadph was used as the housekeeping gene for all tissues. Most primer sequences were obtained from PrimerBank [40] and primers were synthetized by TAG Copenhagen (see below). Bulk transcriptomic profiling by RNA sequencing

Five litters with 6 pups from cohort 2, and within each litter, individuals whose weight at P9 was closed to the mean were selected for transcriptomic analysis. RNA from offspring hypothalamus was extracted using the TRIzol-phenol method as previously described and submitted to Genewiz from Azenta Life Sciences for sequencing. Stranded RNA-seq libraries were prepared for high-quality RNA samples using poly(A) mRNA enrichment. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform (paired-end PE 2 × 150bp) generating ∼50 million reads per sample. Quality control of the raw sequencing data was performed with SOAPnuke v2.1.5 with default options [41]. Reads from each of the samples were mapped to the Mus musculus genome assembly GRCm39 using STAR v2.7.11b with default parameters [42]. Gene counts were retrieved using featureCounts v2.0.6 [43], and then built into a matrix. Low-count genes were filtered based on counts-per-million and sample library size (genes expressed in at least one replicate). Expression abundance was quantified using cufflinks v2.2.1 [44], retrieving the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values. Functional protein–protein association networks were analysed using the STRING database [45].

Transcriptomic deconvolution

Bulk RNA-seq data from hypothalamic samples were variance-stabilized using DESeq2 v1.46.0 [46], and estimated cell-type deconvolution was performed with MuSiC algorithm [47]. HypoMap single-cell RNA-seq atlas [48] was used as a reference dataset for curated annotations of hypothalamic cell populations. MuSiC [47] was applied independently to female and male datasets to estimate cell-type proportions based on the single-cell reference. The resulting weighted proportion matrices were merged, normalized per sample, and averaged within each experimental group. Data processing and visualization were carried out in R (v4.3.2) using the tidyverse R library [49], and hypothalamic cell-type composition was summarized using a stacked bar plot.

Dam breast milk and hypothalamic miRNA content

Dam milk from the litters selected for bulk transcriptomic analyses and hypothalamic tissue from male and female from the same litters were used for obtaining copurification of miRNA content and total RNA using miRNeasy kit (Cat number #1071023). Samples were submitted to Genewiz from Azenta Life Sciences for sequencing. Small RNA-seq libraries were prepared for all high-quality copurification of miRNA content and total RNA samples. Libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina platform (PE 2 × 150bp). An estimated data output of ∼20 million paired-end reads per sample were generated.

Following the workflow described by Xu and colleagues [50], raw read quality was assessed with FastQC v0.11.9 [51], adapters were trimmed using Cutadapt v3.4 [52], and filtered reads were aligned to mature Mus musculus sequences from miRBase v22 [53] using Bowtie v1.3.1 [54]. Alignment files were processed with SAMtools v1.17 [55], and counts were aggregated into two matrices, one for pup hypothalamus and one for milk. For milk samples, miRNAs with fewer than 10 raw counts in at least 5 samples were excluded prior to differential expression analysis.

Transcriptomic and functional enrichment analyses

Differential expression analysis for both RNA-seq and miRNA-seq was performed in R v4.3.2 using DESeq2 v1.46.0 [46]. Variance-stabilizing transformation was applied for visualization and exploratory analysis, including principal component analysis (PCA), volcano plots, and heatmaps. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes and predicted targets of differentially expressed miRNAs retrieved with multiMiR [56] was conducted with clusterProfiler [57] using Gene Ontology Biological Processes and Mus musculus annotations from org.Mm.eg.db [58]. Statistical significance was evaluated using Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p ≤ 0.05, and fold enrichment was calculated as the ratio of observed to expected genes per term.

Hypothalamic regulatory networks

A custom circular network diagram was developed to visualize regulatory interactions between RNAs and miRNAs from dams and their offspring. Male and female offspring were analyzed separately. Raw count matrices for mRNA and miRNA (both from breastmilk and pup hypothalamus) datasets were imported and pre-processed separately for each experimental group. Data normalization and differential expression analysis were processed using DESeq2 v1.46.0 [46] with identical design formulas and statistical thresholds applied to all datasets. Differentially expressed transcripts were subsequently classified as upregulated or downregulated.

To explore post-transcriptional regulatory relationships, predicted and experimentally validated miRNA–mRNA interactions were retrieved using the multiMiR database (v2.1) [56] for Mus musculus. Interactions were retained only when both the miRNA and its target gene were significantly regulated within the respective datasets. Shared miRNAs between dams and pups were cross-compared to identify concordant or discordant regulatory trends. A global regulatory network, integrating maternal miRNAs, pup miRNAs, and mRNAs, was assembled and visualized as a chord diagram using the circlize package [59] with nodes color-coded by expression direction and chords representing distinct interaction types (co-expression, de-repression, or post-transcriptional repression).

Data presentation and statistical analysis

The exact sample size is indicated in the figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.1.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The specific statistical tests applied to each dataset are detailed in the corresponding figure legends. Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests. For experiments involving two or three independent factors, two-way or three-way ANOVA was used, respectively. When appropriate, post hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Bonferroni's correction. If assumptions of normality and/or homogeneity of variance were violated, a Generalized Linear Model (GzLM) was applied. For longitudinal experiments, one-way or two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to assess the effect of one or two factors across time, respectively. Assumptions of normality and sphericity were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Mauchly's test. When these assumptions were not met, repeated-measures Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were applied. Data are presented as mean ± SEM, otherwise it is indicated in the figure legend. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Significance levels are indicated as ∗P ≤ 0.05, ∗∗P ≤ 0.01, and ∗∗∗P ≤ 0.001.

Results

Running increased food intake in mice and yielded sex-specific age-dependent dimorphic changes in body composition

To examine the effect of parental regular pre-mating exercise and age in both parents on offspring phenotype, we first exposed single-housed ad libitum-chow-fed 7-week-old (young) males and females, and 26-week-old (old) males and females to sedentary (blocked wheel) or active (functional wheel) conditions for 5 weeks (Fig. 1A). In males, voluntary wheel running progressively increased in both young and old mice, triggering an increase in energy expenditure, food intake, and fat loss (Figure 1B–E and S1A-B) as previously reported [60]. Young groups gained lean mass attributable to ongoing somatic growth whereas old groups lost lean mass, resulting in accentuated body weight loss in old runners (Figure 1F–G). Despite these changes, no significant differences in estimated energy balance were observed between age-matched groups (Fig. S1C). In females, running distance also increased over the time and was accompanied by higher energy expenditure and food intake (Figure 1H–J and S1D-E). Old runners displayed reduced fat mass, whereas young runners were resistant to exercise-induced fat loss (Fig. 1K). Lean mass increased by running, but more markedly in the younger group, resulting in body weight gain comparable to sedentary mice (Fig. 1L-M). As observed in males, running resulted in no significant differences in energy balance between age-matched groups (Fig. S1F). Collectively, these findings reveal sex-specific age-dependent differences in the body composition in response to voluntary exercise.

We next evaluated whether regular running exclusively during the pre-mating period affects pregnancy outcomes, and whether these effects were influenced by parental age. Pregnancy was successful in 39 of 40 female mice (Fig. 1N), and all pregnant females gained similar body weight during gestation regardless of age or regular pre-mating exercise (Fig. S1G-H). However, after giving birth, older dams weighed more than younger dams, consistent with their pre-pregnancy phenotype, with pre-mating exercised dams weighing more than their sedentary counterparts (Fig. S1G-H).

At weaning, older dams exhibited higher body weight primarily due to greater fat mass, while lean mass did not differ between groups (Fig. S1I-K). Moreover, an attenuated gain in lean mass was observed in pre-mating exercised dams (Fig. S1L). Neither paternal age nor regular parental pre-mating exercise influenced litter size. However, pre-mating exercise increased pup survival rate and trended toward a higher proportion of female pups in littermates from younger groups (p = 0.052; Fig. 1O-Q), in line with the previously reported female-biased litters following parental exercise [61].

Post-weaning offspring body weight and composition changes are influenced by the interaction of regular pre-mating exercise, parental age, and offspring sex

To evaluate the effects of regular parental pre-mating exercise and parental age on offspring body weight and composition, the litter was defined as the experimental unit. To minimize littermate effects, only one male and one female were selected from those containing more than six pups [33]. Parental age increased offspring body weight at postnatal day 14 (Figure 2A–B) whereas parental pre-mating exercise enhanced body weight gain at postnatal day 21 (Figure 2C–D). At weaning, parental age was associated with increased fat mass in the offspring, whereas parental pre-mating exercise was associated with increased lean mass (Figure 2E–F). After weaning, offspring from older parents exhibited increased fat mass during youth compared to those from young parents (Fig. 2G). Lean mass, and consequently body weight, were higher in male offspring during youth. Furthermore, both parental age and pre-mating exercise modulated offspring lean mass, with significant interactions observed between these factors and offspring sex (Figure 2H–I). In male offspring, one male from each litter with more than six pups was given access to a running wheel while a littermate had wheel access blocked (Fig. 3G). Running distance was not affected by parental age or pre-mating exercise (Fig. 3H). As expected, voluntary wheel running increased energy expenditure and food intake while reducing fat mass (Figure 3I–K and Fig. S2E-2I), resulting in a reduced total energy balance in running mice (Fig. S2J). Parental age and regular pre-mating exercise interacted to modulate lean mass and body weight during running (Fig. S2F-G) and no changes were observed regarding tibial length (Fig. S2K). These findings suggest that the exercise-induced increases in food intake and energy expenditure, and the fat loss observed in offspring runners, are conserved mechanisms of running, independent of parental age or pre-mating exercise.

Given the limited effects observed during the running period, we next focused on elucidating the effects of parental age and pre-mating exercise on sedentary offspring. While regional adiposity and liver mass were unaffected by parental age and exercise (Fig. S2L), parental pre-mating exercise upregulated the expression of Adrb3, Lrrc8aR, and Lipe in subcutaneous fat (Fig. 3L). However, this effect was not observed in the visceral fat (Fig. S2M), suggesting a specific role of pre-mating exercise on offspring subcutaneous fat. In the offspring hypothalamus, parental exercise downregulated Pomc (Fig. 3M), which encodes an anorexigenic peptide precursor, but did not affect other genes related to feeding behavior (Fig. 3M and S2N). Together, these data reveal that even when restricted to the pre-mating period, parental exercise can induce tissue-specific changes in gene expression in offspring during youth.

As observed in the initial cohort, 7-week-old (young) and 26-week-old (old) males and females in the second cohort exhibited age- and sex-dependent differences in body composition in response to exercise (Fig. S3A-F). During the running period, parental behavior was analyzed in response to exercise and age (Fig. 4A). Aging increased total distance traveled, and decreased central rearings and fecal excrements in the open field (OF; Figure 4B–F), and increased both total distance traveled, and time spent in the open arms of the elevated plus maze (EPM; Figure 4G–I). Together these findings indicate increased locomotor activity and reduced anxiety-like behavior in old mice. Given the limited number of older mothers, subsequent analyses of maternal care which, in turn, influence early offspring development and survival [36,37], focused exclusively on young dams. Parental pre-mating exercise did not significantly affect nest-building behavior on day 17 post-breeding (Fig. 4L), nor did it enhance the time dams spent with their pups on day 7 postpartum (Fig. 4M), suggesting that pre-mating exercise does not interfere with nursing behavior in young dams.

Gene expression analyses in subcutaneous and brown adipose tissues of male and female offspring during lactation revealed no changes during lactation regarding parental pre-mating exercise in genes related to lipid metabolism, adipogenesis, thermogenesis, or cell proliferation (Fig. S4A-C). Given the central role of hypothalamus in regulating whole-body energy balance [29,30] and growth [68], the impact of maternal obesity on offspring hypothalamic transcriptomics during lactation [31], and our prior observation that parental exercise downregulated Pomc expression in the offspring hypothalamus during youth (Fig. 3M), we hypothesized that offspring hypothalamic development may be influenced by parental pre-mating exercise during lactation. Therefore, we evaluated the hypothalamic transcriptomic signature of male and female offspring in response to parental pre-mating exercise during lactation. A clear clustering by sex was observed in PCA, with minimal clustering according to parental pre-mating exercise (Fig. 5G). Similarly, the distribution of cell proportions estimated from transcriptomic deconvolution revealed sex-specific differences in dividing and immune cell proportions, while no effect of regular pre-mating exercise was detected (Fig. S4D). Applying a threshold of fold change >2 and p < 0.05, we identified 15 differentially expressed (DE) genes (1 upregulated and 14 downregulated; Fig. 5H) in females and 11 DE genes (3 upregulated and 8 downregulated; Fig. 5I) in males. Notably, 2 genes Samd3 & Gbx2 were downregulated in both comparisons. Surprisingly, most of the downregulated genes by parental pre-mating exercise were associated with neurodevelopmental processes rather than feeding behavior or energy balance as we expected (Figure 5J–K and S4E-F). Validation by qPCR confirmed the downregulation of Samd3, Gbx2, Gbx1, and Crh in response to parental pre-mating exercise (Fig. S4G).

MiRNAs are emerging as epigenetic regulators of gene expression both at translational level and through the regulation of DNA methylation [69]. Therefore, we next assessed the hypothalamic miRNAs content in response to parental pre-mating exercise. PCA again showed sex-dependent clustering due to sex with minimal clustering regarding parental pre-mating exercise (Fig. 5L). Using the same statistical threshold, we identified 15 DE (5 upregulated and 10 downregulated; Fig. 5M) in females and 6 DE (4 upregulated and 2 downregulated; Fig. 5N) in males. Although no miRNAs were commonly dysregulated in both comparisons, those dysregulated were predominantly related to neurodevelopmental processes (Fig. 5O-P). Together, these data suggest that parental pre-mating exercise influences offspring neurodevelopment during the mid-stage of lactation.

Pre-mating maternal exercise may influence offspring hypothalamic regulatory networks through milk-derived miRNAs

The specific developmental window during which parental exercise is performed appears to have beneficial effects on offspring metabolism through distinct mechanisms. Pre-conception maternal exercise primarily affects oocyte metabolic profiles, reducing lipid content and improving markers of oocyte quality in obese dams [70]. In parallel, paternal exercise alters sperm physiology, affecting motility, oocyte fusion capacity, and epigenetic features such as miRNA content and DNA methylation, thereby promoting multiple beneficial effects on offspring health [2]. Maternal exercise during gestation affects the placenta. Indeed, improved offspring glucose homeostasis has been attributed to epigenetic reprogramming of offspring liver mediated by increased placental expression and release of superoxide dismutase 3 [11]. Maternal exercise during lactation remodels mammary gland metabolism and milk fatty acid composition in obese dams, thereby enhancing offspring lipid oxidation and energy expenditure [71]. However, whether pre-mating exercise could be a critical factor for offspring development beyond gamete quality remains unclear. Considering that offspring from parental pre-mating exercise displayed reduced visceral and subcutaneous adiposity on postnatal day 9, we hypothesized that pre-mating maternal exercise may induce long-lasting changes in subcutaneous fat, potentially modifying milk composition and nutritional content. Analysis of maternal subcutaneous fat at postnatal day 9 revealed a significant downregulation of Leptin, along with a downregulation of Fasn and trends toward reduced expression of Lipe and Lrrc8aR (Fig. 6A and S5A). Exercise is known to modulate adipose tissue and decrease circulating leptin levels in parallel with reductions in total fat mass [72]. In this context, the downregulation of Leptin may reflect diminished maternal fat reserves during lactation in pre-mating runners. No differences were observed in the expression of genes related to adipogenesis such as Fatty acid desaturase 1 (Fads1); thermogenesis such as Adrb3; the cell proliferation marker Ki67; Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 5 (STAT5), a key regulator of mammary gland and milk production [73], or other milk-associated lactation genes such as Casein Beta (Csn2) and Milk Fat Globule-EGF Factor 8 (Mfge8) in the adipose tissue of pre-mating running dams (Fig. S5A). In contrast, a trend toward downregulation of Prolactin receptor and Lactalbumin Alpha (Lalba) expression was observed in pre-mating running dams (Fig. S5A). Next, we evaluated milk nutritional composition, which revealed no differences in free fatty acids or proteins (Figure 6B–D). However, there was a trend toward decreased milk carbohydrate concentration in response to pre-mating exercise (Fig. 6D). Beyond its nutritional value, breast milk has recently been identified as a rich source of miRNAs that may modulateoffspring metabolism [74]. We therefore analyzed whether maternal pre-mating exercise alters milk miRNA profiles and whether these changes may influence hypothalamic regulation in the offspring. Using a threshold of fold change >2 and p < 0.05, we identified 2 downregulated miRNAs (miR-150-5p and miR-122-5p) in breastfeeding milk from pre-mating exercised dams (Figure 6E–F). MiR-150-5p has previously been detected in the human milk content and associated with immune responses [75], as well as in milk of preterm mothers compared to that of term mothers [76]. MiR-122-5p has been reported in denatured milk from stomachs of wild-type offspring aged 2, 8 days and 14 days [77], and miR-122-3p, derived from the same hairpin precursor as miR-122-5p, has been identified in both fat and skim fractions of mouse milk [50]. To identify how these milk-derived miRNAs are potentially coupled to hypothalamic regulation in the offspring, we mapped pairwise comparisons between differentially expressed hypothalamic genes/miRNAs and differentially expressed milk miRNAs. The results show that the downregulated milk-derived miR-122-5p and miR-150-5p may modulate offspring hypothalamic networks in a sex-dependent manner (Figure 6G–H). Together, these data provide initial evidence that pre-mating maternal exercise may influence offspring hypothalamic development during lactation through subtle alterations in milk-derived miRNAs.

Discussion

In the present study, we report that regular pre-mating running affects offspring adiposity and, in females, growth during lactation. Moreover, we uncover that the effects of regular pre-mating running on offspring body weight and composition after weaning are modulated by parental age and offspring sex. Although numerous rodent studies have reported intergenerational effects of physical activity on offspring metabolism, often highlighting the protective effects of both maternal and paternal exercise against metabolic dysfunction in adulthood, some inconsistencies remain [2]. Our data suggest that parental age may represent an important variable contributing to the heterogeneity observed in body weight and composition in studies on the intergenerational effects of physical activity [2].

Energy homeostasis regulation is conserved in mice [78]. Given our previous observations that energy demand and body composition change throughout the lifespan of male mice [60], and considering that metabolic responses differ between sexes at baseline and in response to exercise [79,80], we first evaluated how energy balance and body composition from young and old dams and sires respond to voluntary exercise. While increased energy expenditure was matched by compensatory increases in food intake in both young and old males and females, only young females were resistant to exercise-induced fat loss. A comparable pattern of fat mass regulation has been reported in both female and male mice during the second week of running [81]. Interestingly, fat loss linked to caloric restriction was also observed in aged female mice and women, but not in young females [82]. This metabolic resistance of young females to calorie restriction has been attributed to the activation of relevant pathways by endogenous estrogen [82]. Although it remains unclear whether a similar mechanism underlies resistance to fat loss during voluntary wheel running, our findings highlight the importance of considering both sex and age-related adaptations to exercise.

From a translational perspective, many couples are now having children at ages when female fertility is already declining, with a significant decrease starting at age 25 and accelerating after age 35 [83]. Ageing not only contributes to a natural increase in fat accumulation in the individual but also in the offspring [25,27]. In line with this, we observed that offspring from older parents displayed greater body weight at 2 weeks old and increased fat mass during youth. These results highlight that parental age may contribute to the rising prevalence of obesity. In addition to parental age-related fat gain during youth, we observed that parental age interacts with regular parental pre-mating exercise to influence offspring body weight and lean mass after weaning. Moreover, female offspring from older parents exhibited a stronger acute susceptibility to the detrimental effects of HFD exposure. However, no effects on body weight changes or tisssue weight were observed at second week of HFD feeding in response to parental age and exercise. Similarly, no changes in food intake or body composition changes were observed in male offspring at the end of voluntary wheel protocol in response to parental age and regular pre-mating exercise. However, since we observed some gene expression changes in subcutaneous fat and hypothalamus of male sedentary offspring in response to regular parental pre-mating exercise, and the beneficial metabolic effects of maternal exercise on offspring frequently emerge during adulthood [74], further longitudinal studies are warranted to investigate the effects of parental age and pre-mating exercise on offspring adulthood and aging.

Offspring from pre-mating exercised parents displayed less subcutaneous and visceral fat than those from sedentary parents at postnatal day 9, as well as modest but consistent hypothalamic changes associated with neurodevelopmental alterations detected by both bulk RNA-seq and miRNA-seq. Although we did not detect changes in the expression of key hypothalamic neuronal markers, such as Pomc, Agrp and Npy, or other genes related to circuits directly controlling energy balance, we observed differential regulation of genes involved in hypothalamic neurodevelopmental pathways. In particular, Gbx2, Gbx1, and Crh are involved in neurodevelopmental processes, including nervous system development and neuronal differentiation, which may indirectly modulate the connectivity and functional responsiveness of circuits regulating energy homeostasis and contribute to changes in offspring adiposity and growth. Future studies exploring cell-type-specific expression changes will be necessary to clarify how neural populations involved in energy balance respond to parental exercise. Moreover, tibial length was reduced in female offspring from exercised parents, further supporting a role of parental pre-mating exercise in shaping offspring development. Previous work has speculated that parental exercise may induce developmental delays, based on delayed eye opening in offspring of dams exposed to exercise before, during and after mating, with sires also allowed to use running wheels for 18 mating days [61]. During the transitional feeding phase, we observed that parental pre-mating exercise accelerated body weight changes before weaning, increasing offspring birth weight, consistent with previous reports in offspring from exercised dams with access to a running wheel before and during pregnancy [84]. At weaning, the increased body weight from offspring of pre-mating exercised mice was associated with higher lean mass, as previously observed in offspring of dams that exercised until mating [85]. Taken together, these results indicate that regular parental pre-mating exercise may influence offspring growth trajectories and body composition in a lactation stage-specific manner. Importantly, the lactation-associated changes observed in our study appear to be independent of maternal care, as pre-mating exercise in young dams had no effect on nest-building behavior or time spent with pups. Whether parental age influences maternal care behaviors has yet to be determined.

Since maternal exercise during lactation may improve breast milk composition and metabolome potentially impacting offspring adiposity [74,86] and breastmilk miRNA content has been associated with both maternal and offspring body composition [87,88], we next evaluated whether pre-mating maternal exercise could induce long-lasting effects in subcutaneous fat that could, in turn, affect differentially in the breastfeeding milk content and offspring adiposity during the mid-stage of lactation. Pre-mating maternal exercise led to modest transcriptional changes in subcutaneous fat and produced minor changes in milk nutritional content and miRNA levels at 9 days postpartum. In line with this, maternal exercise during lactation has been reported to induce only minor alterations in mammary epithelial cell metabolism in lean mice while partially reversing obesity-induced changes in mammary gland metabolism and milk fatty acid composition [71]. Evidence suggests that small extracellular vesicles and their miRNA cargos in breast milk may resist offspring gastrointestinal passage and cross the offspring blood–brain barrier [[89], [90], [91], [92]]. Surprisingly, our integrative network analysis linked differentially expressed milk-derived miRNAs with offspring hypothalamic changes in a sex-dependent manner. Although initial, these findings may open new avenues for understanding how parental exercise prior to mating might influence offspring brain development. Whether parental age impacts breastfeeding composition, and its downstream effects remains to be investigated.

Several limitations of the current study should be considered. Although voluntary wheel running limits precise control over exercise volume, intensity and timing, it closely resembles interval training and active lifestyle in humans [93] and induces similar adaptations comparable to treadmill training regarding body composition changes without introducing a stress response [94,95]. In mice, however, energy balance is primarily regulated by total energy expenditure, particularly at standard housing temperatures (∼22 °C) [96,97], which differs from humans, who typically live within their thermoneutral comfort zone [96,98]. Since our experiments were not conducted at thermoneutrality, the translational relevance of these preclinical findings to humans is questionable. Moreover, substantial differences in the timing of neuroembryological events between mice and humans [99], further limit direct extrapolation. It is important to note the different substrains used between different age groups and that the present work was not statistically powered, all mice in the running group were included regardless of running distance, and the distance run by each parent was not accounted for in offspring analyses due to the limited number of littermates. Here, both parents were exposed to exercise or sedentary conditions, allowing assessment of combined maternal and paternal exercise/sedentary effects but preventing the disentangling of parent-specific contributions. Moreover, the litter was considered the statistical unit, thereby limiting the offspring sample size. The estrous cycle of dams was not monitored during testing, and this may have impacted the outcomes during running period and pregnancy [100,101]. Although our study explored the offspring phenotype resulting from maternal and paternal running from young and old parents before mating, we only focused on the underlying mechanisms of exercise from young parents due to limited litter sizes in old mothers in the second cohort. Moreover, we investigated milk-derived signaling during lactation as a potential mechanism underlying long-term changes induced by maternal exercise. Emerging evidence indicates that offspring of treadmill-exercised sires exhibit intrinsic exercise adaptations and improved metabolic parameters compared with those from sedentary sires, which may be mediated by the remodeling of sperm miRNAs [21]. In addition, other paternal epigenetic mechanisms such as alterations in sperm DNA methylation induced by exercise as well as persistent changes in oocyte epigenetic programming following maternal exercise [[2], [17]], may influence tissue-specific gene expression in the offspring and contribute to the observed reductions in adiposity and developmental changes. Given that a full spermatogenic cycle lasts approximately 35 days [102], the regular voluntary wheel running performed by sires in our study could plausibly induce germline epigenomic changes and impact the reduction of offspring adiposity. Nevertheless, as our research did not directly assess sperm or oocyte epigenetic modifications induced by parental exercise, we cannot establish a causal mechanistic link between exercise-induced epigenomic changes in the germline and the observed offspring phenotypes, representing a limitation of our study. Finally, although offspring hypothalamic transcriptomic and miRNA-seq analyses revealed modest changes in specific genes after correction for multiple comparisons, we explored the biological mechanisms in both datasets as exploratory analyses. Future studies targeting the validation of miRNA-seq are required to confirm these omics-based findings.

In conclusion, our work underscores the need to consider parental age when evaluating offspring phenotypes, and uncovers that regular parental exercise, even when limited to the pre-mating period, can influence offspring phenotype during the exclusively breastfeeding milk period. Moreover, the study highlights the importance of studying both female and male offspring to show sex-specific effects in offspring. Together, these results contribute to a growing body of evidence supporting the intergenerational impact of parental lifestyle factors on early-life development.
