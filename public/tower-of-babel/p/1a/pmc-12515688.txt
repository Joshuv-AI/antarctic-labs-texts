Primary Esophageal Small Cell Neuroendocrine Carcinoma: A Case Report With a Review of Presentation, Treatment, and Survival
Authors: Zi‐Han Zhou, Cheng‐Hao Zhu, Hua‐Zheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCharacterized by early lymphatic metastasis, aggressive growth, and a weak prognosis, small cell esophageal carcinoma (SCEC) lacks established standard treatment guidelines. This report discusses SCEC by case study and includes a relevant literature review. A 66‐year‐old male was diagnosed with SCEC through computed tomography (CT), esophagoscopy, and pathological examination, presenting with multiple mediastinal lymph node metastases at diagnosis. The patient underwent chemotherapy, receiving 120 mg/m2 etoposide (D 1–3) and 60 mg/m2 cisplatin (D 1) every 4 weeks. Dysphagia improved significantly and the mediastinal lymph node size was reduced after 4 cycles. However, lymph node enlargement recurred after 8 cycles, and the patient could no longer tolerate chemotherapy. Subsequently, the patient was arranged to be administered radiotherapy (54 Gy). The patient has completed 10 radiotherapy sessions and survived 8 months post‐diagnosis, continuing treatment. Early initiation of chemoradiotherapy is recommended for patients with locally advanced SCEC.Keywords: case report, literature review, prognosis, small cell esophageal carcinoma, treatment

Introduction

Small cell esophageal carcinoma (SCEC) is a rare and highly malignant esophageal tumor, comprising only 0.4%–2.8% of all esophageal malignancies [1]. SCEC was first reported by McKeown in 1952, and there are < 300 cases of SCEC recorded in the global medical literature. SCEC is pathologically distinguished by densely packed cells with scant cytoplasm, hyperchromatic nuclei, and positive immunohistochemical staining for neuroendocrine markers (CD56, chromogranin, synaptophysin, and neuron‐specific enolase) [2, 3]. Its aggressive nature, high metastatic potential, rapid progression, and frequent misdiagnosis contribute to its poor prognosis and complicate the conduction of large‐scale controlled clinical trials.

Over recent decades, comprehensive retrospective clinical studies have been performed, and genetic research has extensively explored the genomics and mutation types of SCEC. Nonetheless, deficient prospective studies together with the limited clinical application of findings have impeded significant advancements in its treatment. Here, we discuss the case of a 66‐year‐old male with SCEC treated using a platinum‐etoposide (PE) regimen, which is the conventional therapeutic regimen for small cell lung cancer (SCLC). Following 8 cycles of chemotherapy and subsequent radiotherapy, the patient experienced significant symptom relief and tumor stabilization. Furthermore, we review the recent literature from the past decade to explore potential developments in SCEC treatment and prognosis.

Case Presentation

History and Examination

A 66‐year‐old male was admitted due to progressive dysphagia. Approximately 20 days before admission, the patient developed difficulty swallowing without apparent triggers. Symptoms such as heartburn, regurgitation, cough, sputum production, nausea, vomiting, chest tightness, or chest pain were absent. No significant weight loss was reported. The patient had no known underlying medical conditions or history of infectious diseases. It is noteworthy that the patient reported a history of heavy smoking (≥ 30 cigarettes/day for over 30 years) and alcohol consumption, with no family history of malignancies.

The general physical examination was unremarkable, with no palpable lymphadenopathy in the supraclavicular, axillary, or cervical regions. Tumor markers were not assessed. Routine laboratory tests also revealed no significant abnormalities. An irregular rod‐shaped neoplasm protruding into the esophageal lumen was identified on the anterior wall at 30–38 cm from the incisors. The lesion demonstrated erythematous mucosal changes with erosions and was covered by fibrinous exudate. (Figure 1).

Endoscopic examination of the esophageal tumor endoscopy. (A) The initial diagnostic endoscopy revealed a mass obstructing approximately three‐quarters of the esophageal lumen, with ulceration and bleeding observed on the surface of the lesion. (B) After 10 sessions of radiotherapy, two‐thirds of the esophageal lumen was obstructed. The mucosal surface of the lesion appeared intact, with no evidence of bleeding or necrosis.

CT revealed a tumor in the lower esophagus with suspected metastasis to the mediastinal and retroperitoneal lymph nodes (Figure 2).

CT imaging showing changes in the primary lesion and mediastinal lymph nodes following treatment. (A) Initial CT scan at the time of SCEC diagnosis. (B) CT scan after 4 cycles of PE chemotherapy showed a reduced mediastinal lymph node size with no significant progression of the primary lesion. (C) CT scan after 8 cycles of PE chemotherapy indicated no significant changes in the primary lesion but an increase in mediastinal lymph node size.

Histopathological examination of the tumor biopsy specimens revealed small, oval‐shaped tumor cells with increased nuclear atypia and uneven chromatin distribution under hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) displayed a Ki‐67 index of approximately 70%, with positive staining for synaptophysin (Syn), CD56, and cytokeratin pan (CKpan) (Figure 3). The patient declined positron emission tomography‐CT (PET‐CT) for further staging.

Histopathological and IHC examination. (A) H&E staining showed a dense field of tumor cells (20× magnification). (B) At 40× magnification, small round tumor cells were noted to have an infiltrative growth pattern with marked nuclear atypia. (C) Ki‐67 index > 70%. Positive staining for (D) synaptophysin (Syn). (E) Cytokeratin pan (CKpan). (F) CD56.

Final Diagnosis

Primary SCEC, clinical stage cT4aN2M0, stage IIIC.

Treatment and Follow‐Up

The patient was administered 120 mg/m2 etoposide (D 1–3) and 60 mg/m2 cisplatin (D 1) every 4 weeks. After 8 cycles, the patient's dysphagia had markedly improved. However, following the 8 cycles, as revealed in CT imaging, the mediastinal lymph nodes underwent recurrent enlargement, and the patient developed severe chemotherapy‐induced vomiting, making further chemotherapy intolerable. Consequently, the treatment plan was adjusted to include radiotherapy (54 Gy in total, 1.8 Gy X 30 fractions, delivered twice daily). The patient has successfully completed 10 sessions of radiotherapy to date. The patient has been diagnosed with SCEC for over 8 months. His dysphagia has significantly improved, the primary lesion remains stable, and no further lymph node metastasis has been observed. He continues to receive follow‐up monitoring and treatment.

Discussion

SCEC is an extremely rare pathological subtype of esophageal cancer, comprising approximately 0.4%–2.8% of all esophageal cancer cases [4]. It is predominantly observed in regions such as East Asia and East Africa [3, 5, 6, 7]. Due to its exceptionally low incidence, no comprehensive global epidemiological data on the incidence and mortality of SCEC have been reported in the medical literature. Although large‐scale epidemiological studies are absent, SCEC has garnered attention due to its early lymphatic metastasis, high invasiveness, aggressive nature, extremely high mortality, and brief survival period. Studies suggest that the median survival time (MST) for SCEC ranges from 14 to 28 months, with a 5‐year overall survival (OS) rate ranging from 6.7% to 18% [7, 8, 9]. Patients with limited disease (LD) have MST of 20.5 and 17 months, while those with extensive disease (ED) have MST of 4.5 and 8.5 months [10]. (The Veterans Administration Lung Study Group classifies SCEC into LD and ED, with the former referring to tumors confined to a local anatomical region capable of being safely encompassed within a radiation field, and the latter referring to tumors that have spread beyond the local region.) According to Yang et al., patients with tumors in the lower part of the thoracic esophagus and at the EGJ exhibited considerably higher OS (27.0 vs. 13.2 months, log‐rank = 5.806, p = 0.016) and disease‐free survival (DFS, 27.0 vs. 11.3 months, log‐rank = 5.665, p = 0.017) versus those with tumors in the upper and middle parts. N0 status patients exhibited a remarkably better OS (21.4 vs. 11.6 months, log‐rank = 6.293, p = 0.012) and DFS (21.4 vs. 8.6 months, log‐rank = 6.305, p = 0.012). Additionally, tumor length influenced prognosis; patients with tumor length < 5 cm showed a better OS (17.4 vs. 5.7 months, log‐rank = 4.424, p = 0.035) versus those with tumor length > 5 cm [11]. Clinical data from 119 patients were subjected to a retrospective analysis in the study by Zhao et al.; according to univariate analysis results, patients with (66) and without lymph node metastasis (53) presented MST of 19 and 48 months, respectively. The log‐rank test ascertained considerably different MST across different pN stages (p < 0.05). Similarly, MST across different pT stages were 38, 36, 19, and 8 months, with the difference exhibiting statistical significance (p < 0.05). In pTNM stages, stage I‐IIb patients had MST of 38 months, while patients with stage ≥ IIIa had MST of 19 months, with the difference exhibiting statistical significance (p < 0.05) [4].

The clinical manifestations of SCEC include progressive dysphagia, a sensation of food sticking in the throat, acid reflux, heartburn, and retrosternal pain, although early symptoms lack distinguishing features. According to Wei et al., major risk factors for SCEC encompass smoking, alcohol consumption, low vegetable intake, and a high body mass index (BMI) [5]. SCEC is “small, round, oval or spindle‐shaped cells with scant cytoplasm, ill‐defined cell borders, finely granular nuclear chromatin, and absent or inconspicuous nucleoli (World Health Organization (WHO))”. IHC demonstrates positive expression of tumor markers such as CK8, AE1/AE3, EMA, Syn, NSE, CD56, Cg‐A, and TTF‐1 [12, 13, 14].

The histopathological origin of SCEC remains uncertain. Some researchers propose that SCEC originates from neuroendocrine cells (APUD cells) located at the basal layer of the esophageal mucosa or within submucosal glands, a hypothesis supported by the pronounced neuroendocrine features of SCEC pathology [3] and reports of SCEC's earliest detection in the submucosal layer [15]. Alternatively, another theory posits that SCEC arises from a pluripotent primitive cell, serving as a precursor shared by all esophagus epithelial neoplasms, capable of differentiating into carcinomas of squamous cells, small cells (neurosecretory granules), glandular elements, and reserve cells (without granules) [16]. This theory stems from pathological findings where other tumor cell types, such as squamous cell carcinoma, were also present; however, further research is necessary for confirmation. Pathological analysis in the reported case did not indicate the coexistence of other carcinoma subtypes.

SCEC exhibits strikingly similar pathological features to SCLC. A study by Fan et al. confirmed that SCEC and SCLC share over 800 pairs of up‐regulated DEGs [false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01; fold change ≥ 2] [17]. Functional annotation revealed numerous common biological processes between SCEC and SCLC, involving the cell cycle, telomere maintenance, DNA repair, DNA replication, and the p53 pathway (Benjamini p < 0.05), elucidating the similar biological characteristics between SCEC and SCLC and providing a theoretical basis for SCEC treatment.

No clear standards have been formulated in guiding the diagnosis and treatment specific to SCEC by now. Available diagnostic methods include CT, endoscopy, pathological examination, and PET, with pathological diagnosis remaining the gold standard for confirming SCEC. Treatment options include surgery (S), radiotherapy (RT), chemotherapy (CT), and immunotherapy. SCEC has been increasingly recognized as a systemic disease, with chemotherapy as the cornerstone of treatment [4, 10, 18, 19]. Current debates on treatment plans focus primarily on identifying which patients would benefit most from surgery, the efficacy of radiotherapy, and the most beneficial combination of concurrent or sequential therapy. In this context, retrospective studies published over the past decade have been compiled and analyzed to propose evidence‐based treatment strategies for the future management of SCEC.

For early‐stage SCEC patients, the study by Mohammad et al. revealed that those receiving palliative chemotherapy, concurrent chemoradiation, and sequential chemoradiation had MST of 7 (95% CI: 1.5–12), 45 (95% CI: 38–), and 20 months (95% CI: 17–25), respectively, with a significant prolongation observed in patients undergoing concurrent chemoradiation, strongly supporting concurrent chemoradiation for limited stage (LS)‐SCEC patients without significant comorbidities [18]. Meng et al. gathered data from 72 LS‐SCEC patients, showing that those receiving chemotherapy achieved an obviously longer median OS (22.8 months) versus those who did not (10.0 months) (p = 0.03). Patients undergoing multimodality therapy (RT+CT [18%], S+CT [40%], or S+RT+CT [17%]) vs. monotherapy (typically S [18%]) presented considerably better OS (15.5 vs. 9.3 months) and complete response rates (p = 0.02). Multivariate analysis identified tumor location (upper third of the esophagus) and treatment type (monotherapy) as predictive factors for OS. According to Meng et al., for patients in LS, S+CT+RT, S+CT, or CT+RT are more suitable instead of S [6]. Zhu et al.'s study also indicated the effectiveness of RT or RT+CT in locoregional disease control. In LS patients, RT+CT significantly prolonged OS compared to RT alone (HR = 0.204, 95% CI = 0.050–0.839, p = 0.046), and a radiation dose > 56 Gy also significantly extended OS (HR = 4.212, 95% CI = 1.024–17.335, p = 0.027) [20].

Regarding the benefits of postoperative adjuvant therapy, Lu et al. collected data from 150 surgically treated SCEC patients and found that LS I patients who received surgery alone (S), surgery‐chemotherapy (S‐CT), or surgery combined with chemoradiation (S‐CRT) did not differ a lot in terms of their median DFS/OS/DMFS/LRFS (DMFS = distant metastasis‐free survival, LRFS = local recurrence‐free survival). For patients in LS II, subgroup analysis revealed divergences in DFS (S vs. S‐CT: 9.0 vs. 11.3 months, p = 0.029), OS (S vs. S‐CT: 14.0 vs. 19.5 months, p = 0.021), and LRFS (S vs. S‐CT: 13.0 vs. 18.0 months, p = 0.050), with a tendency noted in DMFS (S vs. S‐CT: 11.0 vs. 18.0 months, p = 0.094). Comparatively, with radiotherapy being added to postoperative chemotherapy (S‐CRT) in LS II patients, the OS and DFS did not present an obvious difference from the case of postoperative chemotherapy (S‐CT). They concluded that adjuvant chemotherapy could contribute to a higher DFS rate in patients with completely resected LS II SCEC, while postoperative chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy could not elevate the DFS rate in those with completely resected LS I. Li et al.'s study reached similar conclusions [21, 22].

For patients with advanced SCEC, no large‐scale retrospective study currently guides clinical treatment. It is generally agreed that advanced‐stage SCEC patients should undergo chemotherapy [6, 11, 20, 22, 23]. Research by Li et al. has shown that stage III/IV SCEC patients who receive only radiotherapy experience poorer OS (HR = 2.95, 95% CI: 1.43–6.11, p = 0.003). Those who did not undergo chemotherapy exhibited a higher tumor‐related mortality risk versus those receiving chemotherapy (HR 2.36, 95% CI: 1.82–3.11; p = 2.013 for stage III/IV; HR 2.13, 95% CI: 1.02–4.45; p = 2.044 for stage III/IV) [23]. Li et al. also indicated that stage III PSCCE patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy (nCT) presented an obviously higher OS rate versus patients who received surgery alone or did not receive surgery (p = 0.021 and p = 0.026, respectively); nCT also increased DFS (p = 0.031) [22]. Although most advanced‐stage SCEC patients already present with distant metastasis, Meng et al.'s study suggested that stage IIB and III patients in the RT+CT group presented a considerably better OS versus the S+CT group (p < 0.05) [8], indicating that radiotherapy contributes to prolonging OS in advanced‐stage patients. However, Zhao et al., after analyzing clinical information from 119 SCEC patients, pointed out that for patients with stage III or higher, the treatment modality had no significant impact on their OS [4]. In conclusion, the treatment methods for advanced‐stage SCEC patients continue to be a topic for further discussion according to the currently published global medical literature.

Regarding the prognosis of SCEC, high Ki‐67 expression is an independent factor favoring the prognosis of PSCEC patients [18]. In the case we reported, the IHC showed a Ki‐67 index of approximately 70%. When the TNM stage was I–IIb, lymph node metastasis (p = 0.003) or adjuvant therapy (p = 0.004) could independently affect OS. For patients with stage IIIa or higher, the OS had no obvious influencing factors (p > 0.05) [4]. Zhang et al.'s study highlighted that negative PD‐L1 expression, high CD4 and FoxP3 expressions, a low CD8/CD4 ratio, and a high FoxP3/CD8 ratio could lead to weak prognosis [24]; Huang et al. employed multiplex fluorescent IHC to detect TCF1+CD8+T cells in tumor tissues of 79 PSCCE patients. Their findings revealed that high infiltration of TCF1+CD8+T cells was associated with longer OS compared to low infiltration (p = 0.009, HR = 0.506). Furthermore, higher TCF1+CD8/CD8 ratios (> 21%) correlated with better OS compared to lower ratios (≤ 21%) (p < 0.001, HR = 0.394) [25]. Some scholars have developed models to predict patients' prognosis by detecting inflammatory markers [13]. Although these models have demonstrated good accuracy in prognostic prediction [2, 26], they have yet to be validated in large‐scale clinical trials.

In the case we reported, several limitations were noted. Firstly, strict chemoradiotherapy was not initiated early, whether sequential or concurrent. Compared to chemotherapy alone, chemoradiotherapy might have prolonged the patient's survival and inhibited tumor lymph node metastasis. Secondly, the patient did not undergo PET‐CT to confirm the presence of lymph node metastasis. Thirdly, the relatively short follow‐up period made it difficult to assessthe long‐term impact of the treatment on prognosis.

In conclusion, treatment of SCEC should primarily depend on the patient's clinical TNM staging. For early and intermediate‐stage patients, particularly those with surgical opportunities, early surgery followed by active adjuvant chemotherapy is recommended. Based on the currently published retrospective studies, concurrent chemoradiation is recommended to extend patient survival. For advanced‐stage patients, chemotherapy is still the cornerstone of treatment, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy also serves as a proper option for patients with surgical opportunities post‐treatment. Overall, multiple treatment modalities, compared to single treatments, have significantly extended patient survival.

Conclusion

SCEC lacks standard treatment guidelines due to early metastasis, rapid progression, and poor prognosis. The OS rate of patients is influenced by tumor location, size, and pTNM staging. Prognosis is associated with treatment modality, lymph node involvement, and cTNM staging. Treatment approaches vary according to the patient's clinical stage. While definitive treatment guidelines are still lacking, employing multiple treatment strategies has been shown to contribute to prolonged patient survival.
