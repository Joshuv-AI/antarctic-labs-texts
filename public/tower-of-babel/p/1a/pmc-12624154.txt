Endothelial activation and stress index (EASIX) as a biomarker to predict ruxolitinib failure for steroid‐refractory acute graft‐versus‐host disease treatment
Authors: Sergio Rodriguez‐Rodriguez, Nihar Desai, Carol Chen, Kareem Jamani, Catherine Sohier‐Poirier, Christopher Lemieux, Jennifer White, Mohamed Elemary, Michael Kennah, Tommy Alfaro Moya, Eshrak Al‐Shaibani, Igor Novitzky‐Basso, Ivan Pasic, Arjun Datt Law, Fotios V. Michelis, Auro Viswabandya, Rajat Kumar, Jonas Mattsson, Dennis Dong Hwan Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummarySteroid‐refractory acute graft‐versus‐host disease (SR‐aGvHD) remains a significant challenge after haematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT). While ruxolitinib (RUX) has shown efficacy for SR‐aGvHD, failure predictors are poorly defined. We assessed 78 patients from six Canadian centres who received RUX for SR‐aGvHD. Failure‐free survival (FFS) was the primary end‐point. Endothelial activation and stress index (EASIX) scores were calculated at defined time points, dichotomized using recursive partitioning. A risk score for RUX failure was developed based on key variables. At RUX initiation, 76% of patients had grade 3–4 aGvHD. The best overall response rate was 75%; 58% experienced RUX failure, while the 6‐month FFS was 25.7%. EASIX gradually increased over time even after RUX (p < 0.001). Patients with high EASIX at RUX ≥1.11 had a lower FFS at 6 months (17%) than the patients with low EASIX (54%) (hazard ratio [HR] 2.75 [95%CI 1.23–6.15], p = 0.014). A three‐factor RUX failure risk score—gut aGvHD, grade 4 aGvHD and high EASIX at RUX start—stratified patients by 6‐month FFS rates: 85.7%, 28.0% and 11.0% for score 0 (n = 28), 1 (n = 25) and ≥2 (n = 34) (HR 2.25[1.42–3.55], p < 0.001). High EASIX can predict the risk of RUX failure in addition to gut aGvHD and grade 4 aGvHD. We propose to add other therapeutic interventions to RUX therapy pre‐emptively to high EASIX patients.

INTRODUCTION

Acute graft‐versus‐host disease (aGvHD) continues to be one of the challenging complications following allogeneic haematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT). Even with high‐dose corticosteroids (CS) therapy, which is the current treatment of choice as first‐line therapy for aGvHD, nearly half of the patients eventually fail CS therapy, resulting in steroid refractoriness with aGvHD progression or ending up with CS dependency 1 ; these conditions collectively define steroid‐refractory aGvHD (SR‐aGvHD).

Ruxolitinib (RUX), an oral selective Janus kinase (JAK) 1/2 inhibitor, first demonstrated its efficacy in the REACH1 trial, an open‐label phase 2 study that reported an overall response rate (ORR) of 55% by day 28 and an overall survival (OS) rate of 51% at 6 months. 2 The efficacy of RUX in treating SR‐aGvHD was established in the REACH2 trial, a multicentre, randomized controlled trial (RCT) that compared it with the best available therapy (BAT). The study demonstrated 62% and 40% ORR by days 28 and 56 in patients treated with RUX, 3 compared to 39% and 22% in the BAT group respectively; moreover, RUX achieved a lower cumulative incidence of failure at 6 and 12 months (53% and 59%) when compared to BAT (81% and 82%), confirming its superior clinical efficacy.

However, although RUX has significantly improved outcomes, it does not mean all the patients treated will respond and survive. Recently, a provisional definition of RUX‐refractory aGvHD (RuxR‐aGvHD) has been proposed, 4 consisting of the progression of aGvHD after 5–10 days of RUX treatment, lack of improvement after at least 14 days of RUX treatment or loss of response to RUX therapy at any time. Identification of high‐risk patients who failed RUX treatment is urgently required to improve clinical outcomes in this cohort of patients.

Predicting outcomes in patients with aGvHD is still the area of active research for biomarker development. An exploratory analysis of the REACH2 trial developed a pretreatment and day 14 model to identify biomarkers associated with the probability of response, suggesting a potential prognostic score using cytokines, chemokines, immune cell types and inflammatory protein values 5 ; however, limited availability of such immunoassays diminishes its applicability. The endothelial activation and stress index (EASIX) is a simple composite score calculated by a biomarker‐based formula using the patient's creatinine, lactate dehydrogenase and platelet count that has been shown to reflect endothelial dysfunction. 6 It was reported to predict mortality in patients after allogeneic HCT both pre‐ and post‐transplant (day 30 and 100), 7 specifically when measured at the time of aGvHD. 8 The present study aimed to demonstrate the prognostic relevance of the EASIX score as a predictor of outcomes in SR‐aGvHD treated with RUX.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients and treatment

A total of 78 patients were included and evaluated retrospectively as previously reported. 9 We evaluated the risk factors for failure in patients with SR‐aGvHD treated with RUX at six Canadian transplant centres: Calgary, Toronto, Vancouver, Quebec, Ottawa and Saskatoon. Patients were included if they had SR‐aGvHD and were started on RUX. The definition of SR‐aGvHD was as follows: disease progression after at least 3 days of high‐dose systemic CS therapy, no response within 7 days or failure to taper the CS dose to <0.5 mg/kg of methylprednisolone. 3 RuxR‐aGvHD was defined as the progression of aGvHD after 5–10 days of treatment, lack of improvement after at least 14 days or loss of response at any time. 4 All research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Research Ethics Board (REB) approved this retrospective study at the University Health Network, Toronto.

Clinical outcomes assessment

The primary end‐point was failure‐free survival (FFS) at 6 months following RUX therapy initiation. The FFS was calculated from the RUX therapy start until treatment failure or the last follow‐up. Treatment failure was defined as 1 a treatment switch due to lack of response or toxicity to RUX, 2 non‐relapse mortality and 3 primary relapse disease. Secondary outcomes were the overall response rate (ORR) at day 28 and day 56 after initiating RUX and OS after RUX therapy initiation for SR‐aGvHD. The ORRs were evaluated according to changes in baseline organ staging, with complete and partial responses combined as recommended by the National Institutes of Health criteria. 10 , 11 Overall survival was calculated from the start of RUX therapy until death or the last follow‐up.

EASIX calculation formula and the time points for calculation

The EASIX score was estimated in 68 (87%) patients with available laboratory information, calculated as lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) (IU/L) × creatinine (mg/dL)/platelet count (109/L), applying a base 2 log transformation (log2) to the calculated score to reduce skewness. 7 , 12 We employed a recursive partitioning method via the rpart R package to determine the optimal cut‐off for predicting a failure event (FFS) at different time points 13 ; the model was fit with a minimum number of observations in a node (minsplit) of 20; and a complexity parameter (cp) of 0.02, to favour interpretable splits and reduce overfitting. 14

Statistical analysis

Baseline clinical and demographical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, with laboratory values reported as mean with standard error or median with range. Fisher's exact and chi‐squared tests were used to compare categorical variables; Mann–Whitney U‐tests were used to compare numerical variables. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to evaluate FFS and OS. Cox proportional hazard models were used for survival analyses, including all variables.

A Cox proportional hazard model was adopted for the 6‐month FFS as the end‐point using hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals [95% CI]. The following variables were evaluated: aGvHD subtype at initial presentation (classical vs. overlap syndrome), resistant or refractory aGvHD (vs. steroid‐dependent aGvHD), three or more lines of treatment before RUX (vs. 1–2 previous lines), three organ aGvHD (skin, liver and gut vs. 1–2 organs), gut aGvHD at RUX start (vs. no gut aGvHD), grade 4 aGvHD at RUX start (vs. grade 1–3 aGvHD) and a high EASIXRUX (vs. low). After the univariate analysis, a multivariate analysis was performed using Cox's proportional hazard regression model, with a backward stepwise selection procedure applied using a p = 0.05 for variable entry and a p = 0.10 for variable removal. From those variables identified as significant in the multivariate analysis, a prognostic risk score system was generated by assigning one score to each risk factor, with four groups created: low‐risk group without any risk factors, intermediate‐risk group with one risk factor and high‐risk group with two or more risk factors. Finally, a log‐rank test and a Cox proportional hazard regression analysis were performed according to the prognostic risk score model for FFS.

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29.0.2.0, Armonk, NY, USA: IBM Corp) and EZR (version 1.68). 15

RESULTS

Basal characteristics and aGvHD profile

We included 78 patients treated with RUX for SR‐aGvHD, with a median age of 56, and 47% were female. Acute myeloid leukaemia and myelodysplastic neoplasm were the most common indications for allogeneic HCT (49%). Most patients received an allogeneic HCT from a matched unrelated donor (45%), with reduced intensity conditioning being the most common regimen (58%); the most common GvHD prophylaxis platforms were anti‐thymoglobulin (ATG) based (50%), and ATG with post‐transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) (32%). The median onset of aGvHD was 64 days (range 15–265) after allogeneic HCT; it affected skin (72%), gut (59%) and liver (28%). Most patients had classical aGvHD (78%), and 55% had grade 3–4 aGvHD at diagnosis. Besides corticosteroids (100%), calcineurin inhibitors (73%) were the second most frequently used systemic therapy before RUX treatment (Table 1).

Ruxolitinib therapy for SR‐aGvHD and overall response rate

RUX was administered as second‐line therapy in 16 (21%), third‐line therapy in 49 (63%), fourth‐line therapy in 9 (11%) and fifth‐line therapy in 4 (5%) patients. Fifty‐two (67%) patients started RUX at 5 mg twice daily, 22 (28%) at 10 mg twice daily and 4 (5%) at 5 mg daily. The median time from aGvHD diagnosis to RUX start was 21 days, with 76% being resistant or refractory to the previous line of treatment and 22% being responsive but steroid‐dependent; 76% of the patients had grade 3–4 aGvHD at the time of RUX start. The best ORR for RUX was 75% (n = 50/67); the ORRs on day 28 and day 56 to RUX were 72% (n = 48/67) and 78% (n = 43/55) (Figure 1A), respectively, and the CR on days 28 and 56 was 18% (n = 12/67) and 34% (n = 19/55) respectively (Table 1).

Ruxolitinib failure with ruxolitinib treatment for SR‐aGvHD

With a median follow‐up for survivors of 4.2 months (range 0.1–72) after RUX therapy started, patients who were RUX responsive had a median follow‐up of 8.7 months (range 0.6–72 months) and of 2.6 months (range 0.1–46.4 months) in patients with RUX failure; 33 patients (42%) achieved a durable response with RUX therapy. RUX failure was observed in the remaining 45 patients (58%) (Table 2). RUX failure was due to (1) progression of GvHD within 5–10 days after RUX (n = 4, 9%), (2) lack of GvHD improvement after 14 days of RUX (n = 9, 20%) or (3) loss of the durable response to RUX after the initial response (n = 32, 71%). When comparing the RUX failure group with those with a durable response, a statistically significant difference was found in the ORRs at day 28 (57% vs. 88%, p = 0.007) and day 56 (59% vs. 100%, p < 0.001), as well as in day 28 CR (6% vs. 31%, p = 0.010) (Figure 1B). There was no statistically significant difference in RUX failure between patients who had a RUX starting dose of 5 mg twice daily (60%) and 10 mg twice daily (54%) (p = 0.798).

FFS and OS with ruxolitinib for SR‐aGvHD treatment

The median FFS after RUX start was 2.3 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.6–3.0), with an FFS rate at 6 months of 25.7% [16.3–36.1%] (Figure 1C). Of 78 patients, 59 (76%) experienced a failure event during the RUX therapy. The details of failure events are as follows: non‐relapse mortality (n = 28, 47%), requirement for further treatment due to no response (n = 17, 29%), intolerance mainly due to thrombocytopenia (n = 9, 15%) or primary disease relapse (n = 5, 9%); at the last follow‐up, 18 of 78 (23%) patients remained on active RUX therapy. The median OS after RUX start was 5.4 months [3.1–42.4], with an OS rate at 6 months of 49.5% [37.7–60.3] (Figure 1D). Overall, 44 patients (56%) experienced mortality due to GvHD‐related causes (n = 22, 55%), infections (n = 16, 36%) or relapse (n = 4, 9%).

EASIX score changes before and after ruxolitinib therapy for SR‐aGvHD

Of 78 patients, 68 (87%) had available data for the log2‐EASIX scores calculation, presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). The log2‐EASIX values significantly increased over time after HCT: 1.39 ± 0.21 for EASIXHCT, 2.04 ± 0.19 for EASIXaGvHD, 2.06 ± 0.16 for EASIXRUX, 2.73 ± 0.20 for EASIXD14, 3.07 ± 0.22 for EASIXD28 and 3.61 ± 0.26 for EASIXD56 (Figure 2A; p < 0.001). When log2‐EASIX values were compared between patients who were RUX responsive and those with RUX failure (Figure 2B), no statistically significant difference was found for any time point (p = 0.433).

AGvHD, acute graft‐versus‐host disease; D, day; EASIX, endothelial activation and stress index; FFS, failure‐free survival; HCT, haematopoietic cell transplantation; RUX, ruxolitinib.

To properly discern the cause of the continuous log2‐EASIX increase, we analysed each of the variables individually. LDH (IU/L) continuously increased after RUX start: 309 ± 33 at EASIXHCT, 303 ± 16 for EASIXaGvHD, 300 ± 13 for EASIXRUX, 364 ± 18 for EASIXD14, 390 ± 24 for EASIXD28 and 421 ± 28 for EASIXD56 (p = 0.014). In contrast, creatinine (μmol/L) values increased after transplant and stabilized after RUX start: 67.5 ± 2.5 at EASIXHCT, 89.2 ± 4.3 for EASIXaGvHD, 67.5 ± 2.5 for EASIXRUX, 85.3 ± 6.8 for EASIXD14, 85.5 ± 6.5 for EASIXD28 and 82.3 ± 4.5 for EASIXD56 (p < 0.001). Similarly, the platelet count (109/L) decreased after transplant and stabilized after RUX start: 100 ± 10 at EASIXHCT, 94 ± 9 for EASIXaGvHD, 79 ± 7 for EASIXRUX, 63 ± 8 for EASIXD14, 57 ± 8 for EASIXD28 and 60 ± 10 for EASIXD56 (p = 0.003) (Figure 2C). Interestingly, the bilirubin (μmol/L) values did not vary with statistical significance: 30.8 ± 4.9 at EASIXRUX, 34.9 ± 7.2 for EASIXD28 and 40.5 ± 9.1 for EASIXD56 (p = 0.249).

We employed a recursive partitioning method at each time point to divide patients into a low and high EASIX category, based on their relationship to the optimal cut‐off. The optimal log2‐EASIX cut‐offs were −0.11 for HCT (EASIXHCT; n = 68), 0.51 at the onset of aGvHD (EASIXaGvHD; n = 68), 1.11 at the start of RUX therapy (EASIXRUX; n = 67), 3.59 at day 14 (EASIXD14; n = 64), 4.91 at day 28 (EASIXD28; n = 58) and 3.12 at day 56 (EASIXD56; n = 47) after RUX start. When comparing outcomes according to the EASIXRUX cut‐off, the patients with a high EASIXRUX had a higher chance of failure events after RUX (86%, n = 45/52), compared to those with low EASIXRUX (40%, n = 6/15) (p = 0.001). The EASIXRUX cut‐off showed a sensitivity of 86%, specificity of 60%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 88%, negative predictive value (NPV) of 56% and an accuracy of 81% to predict RUX failure (p < 0.001). When comparing the FFS rate at 6 months according to the EASIXRUX cut‐off, the patients with a high EASIXRUX had a lower FFS than those with a low EASIXRUX (17.3% [8.3–28.9] vs. 54.2% [27.1–75.0]) (HR 2.75 [1.23–6.15], p = 0.014) (Figure 2D).

Failure‐free survival analysis and risk score model generation

In both the univariate and multivariate analyses for FFS, gut involvement of aGvHD at RUX start (HR 1.885 [1.055–3.369], p = 0.032), grade 4 aGvHD before RUX start (HR 2.102 [1.053–4.196], p = 0.035) and a high EASIXRUX (HR 2.592 [1.154–6.150], p = 0.021) were independent risk factors for FFS following RUX therapy for SR‐aGVHD (Table 3).

A RUX failure risk score was generated based on the previously identified risk factors: gut involvement of aGvHD at RUX start, grade 4 aGvHD before RUX start and a high EASIXRUX. According to the risk score, we stratified patients into four groups: low‐risk group (n = 8) without any risk factors, intermediate‐risk group (n = 25) with one risk factor and high‐risk group (n = 34) with two or more risk factors.

The proposed RUX failure risk score stratified the patients according to the 6‐month FFS rate: 85.7% [33.4–97.9] for low‐risk patients, 28.0% [12.4–46.0] for intermediate‐risk patients and 11.0% [3.2–24.4] for high‐risk patients (HR 2.248 [1.422–3.555], for each additional risk factor, p < 0.001) (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

The present study is a multicentre, real‐world data study that assessed 78 patients treated with RUX for SR‐aGvHD. RUX failure was noted in 45 patients (58%) with 6‐month FFS and OS rates of 25.7% and 49.5% respectively. In the risk factor analysis for RUX failure with respect to FFS following RUX therapy, three risk factors were identified: gut involvement of aGvHD and grade 4 aGvHD before starting RUX, and elevated EASIXRUX scores ≥1.11, which were associated with lower FFS. These values were incorporated into the risk score system, enabling risk stratification into three groups for RUX failure.

Although RUX is being used for SR‐aGvHD, there is no biomarker established for steroid failure or RUX failure. An exploratory analysis of the REACH2 trial examined 21 blood biomarkers in SR‐aGvHD and suggested their potential use in predicting outcomes. 5 Other biomarkers like suppression of tumorogenicity 2 (ST2), 16 amphiregulin and regenerating family member 3 alpha (REG3α) 17 have been proposed, yet the training and validation cohort in one of the studies only included 18% and 19% of patients with grade III‐IV GvHD at diagnosis. 17 However, most recommended biomarkers require using different immunoassay platforms that are not easily accessible outside clinical trials.

GvHD is endothelialitis, an inflammatory condition targeting the endothelium in diverse organs including skin, liver and gut via donor T‐cells, resulting in damage, structural changes and dysfunction of the vasculature. 18 Recently, a report highlighted the correlation between EASIX and markers of endothelial dysfunction, 6 concluding that a high EASIX score may suggest an increased risk for aGvHD. We hypothesized that the EASIX score would reflect inflammation of endothelialitis mediated by donor T‐cell‐derived GvHD and could be used to predict RUX failure in SR‐aGvHD. While multiple previous studies have reported that calculating EASIX before conditioning or transplant correlates with decreased OS, 7 , 19 there were few studies investigating the dynamics of EASIX score over post‐transplant courses and EASIX at the onset of aGvHD. 7 , 8 , 20

In the present study, exclusively investigating patients with SR‐aGvHD, we have observed a rapid increase in EASIX from HCT to acute GvHD onset, remaining elevated even after RUX therapy started. When analysing the dynamics of each EASIX individual laboratory value, creatinine increased and platelets diminished, achieving a plateau between day 14 and day 56 of treatment; interestingly, LDH continued to increase up to day 56. This finding is supportive of our hypothesis that the EASIX score reflects the inflammatory activity of endothelialitis from donor T‐cell‐derived GvHD. Importantly, we showed that an EASIXRUX ≥1.11 had a sensitivity of 86%, specificity of 60%, a PPV of 88% and an accuracy of 81% to detect failure.

In addition, the present study supports that EASIX score at RUX initiation can be a promising biomarker predicting RUX failure with respect to FFS analysis. The high EASIX score group showed inferior FFS, which is also consistent with our hypothesis. Those with high EASIX score appear to have a greater endothelial dysfunction or endothelialitis activity, thus less likely to respond to RUX therapy. Based on the multivariate analysis of FFS, we then created a RUX failure risk score based on gut involvement of aGvHD and grade 4 aGvHD before starting RUX and elevated EASIXRUX. To our knowledge, only the previously reported and validated Minnesota aGvHD risk score identifies patients with high‐risk aGvHD based on the involved organs and the severity of GvHD 21 , 22 ; these patients were less likely to respond to steroid therapy and had a higher risk of mortality. In our cohort study, patients with two or more risk factors had dismal outcomes, with an FFS rate at 6 months of merely 11%.

Our study's limitations are mainly related to its retrospective nature and the lack of a direct comparison to assess the benefit of RUX therapy in SR‐aGvHD properly. Additionally, the proposed cut‐off for EASIX and the risk stratification score need to be validated in different and independent populations. A strength of our study is its multicentre nature, which limits bias related to single‐centre practice and may allow for generalizability. These data require external validation to determine the impact of EASIX and the generalization of such a risk score.

In the current study, EASIX was suggested to help determine high‐risk patients for RUX failure. It could be utilized in the clinic to identify high‐risk patients for RUX failure, thus moving forward to pre‐emptive therapeutic intervention. For example, those patients could be treated with combination therapy of RUX with additional treatment such as extracorporeal photopheresis, vedolizumab or natalizumab.
