Inadvertent hypothermia in patients undergoing brachytherapy under monitored anesthesia care: A prospective observational cohort study
Authors: Ismail Aytac, Betül Guven Aytac, Aysun Postaci
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Brachytherapy, which is often performed under anesthesia, is one of the main treatment options for cervical cancer. It is unclear whether hypothermia and its associated negative outcomes are encountered during this procedure. This prospective observational cohort study aimed to investigate the prevalence and adverse effects of hypothermia during serial brachytherapies under deep sedation for cervical cancer. Female patients over the aged > of 18 years who underwent were taken to serial brachytherapy sessions under deep sedation on alternate dates at most twice a week for the treatment of cervical cancer were included. A total of 23 female were screened for initial and post-procedural hypothermia using infrared thermometers without contact to the skin at forehead between July and October 2022 at tertiary education and research hospital. Hypothermia was detected in 2 2 (8.7%) of the 23 patients and 5 5 (5.4%) of the 92 sessions. A negative correlation was found between the anesthesia time and post-procedural body temperature values (r = −0.385, P < .001). It was observed that there was a decrease in body temperature of at most −1.3 degrees and at least −0.1 degrees during the sessions. A decrease of ≥ 0.4°C was detected in any session in 16 (69.9%) of the 23 patients. A decrease of ≥ 0.4°C was detected in 34 (37%) of the 92 sessions. Involuntary hypothermia may occur during brachytherapy sessions performed under sedation. Institutions should encourage routine temperature monitoring and active warming to prevent hypothermia and adverse outcomes.

1. Introduction

Owing to advances in medical technology, increased knowledge of disease pathogenesis, and some financial factors, a significant number of diagnostic and therapeutic procedures are performed strictly outside traditional operating rooms (ORs). Non-operating room anesthesia (NORA) is common in endoscopy rooms, radiotherapy units, cardiac catheterization laboratories, and invasive radiology units. In parallel to this global trend, NORA have become an important key factor in patient safety and quality care.[1]

Perioperative inadvertent hypothermia, defined generally as body temperature ≤36°C despite the definition variances the literature, is a frequent and well-known finding associated with numerous complications, from thermal discomfort of patients to more serious ones.[2] Hypothermia may increase the recovery time due to impaired drug metabolism and lead to adverse respiratory events. Perioperative cardiac events can also occur because of increased oxygen consumption and vasoconstriction during hypothermia. Increased hemorrhage and postoperative wound infections are related problems.[3]

Brachytherapy is a form of high-dose radiation therapy in which a sealed applicator radiation source is inserted intracavitarily near the tumor tissue. Brachytherapy plays a pivotal role in cervical cancer.[4] Painful brachytherapy is performed as an outpatient procedure for cervical cancer using either conscious sedation or spinal anesthesia in a few sessions on alternate days to allow sufficient normal tissue recovery.[5] Although cervical cancer is mostly diagnosed in young women, frail and elderly patients prone to hypothermia can also receive brachytherapy treatment.[6] Anesthetic drugs that are used during monitored anesthesia care with sedation, general anesthesia, or regional anesthesia can cause hypothermia by inducing vasodilation and redistribution of heat from the core to the periphery.[7]

Hypothermia studies have generally focused on major surgical procedures. Despite the routine monitoring recommendations of various medical associations, it can be overlooked in the management of monitored anesthesia care in minor surgeries and NORA, where the risk is thought to be less or the service is busy.[7–12]

This single-center, prospective, observational, cohort study was conducted to determine whether deep sedation causes inadvertent hypothermia and its prevalence during serial brachytherapy sessions in female patients under monitored anesthesia care. The secondary aim of our study is to assess the factors and adverse consequences of inadvertent hypothermia.

2. Material methods

This prospective observational cohort study was approved by the Institutional Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number: E1-22-2699; date:29/06/2022). This study was conducted between July and October 2022 at a tertiary education and research hospital.

2.1. Study population

Female patients over 18 years of age who received brachytherapy sessions under deep sedation with monitored anesthesia care on alternate days at most twice a week for non-metastatic, histologically confirmed cervical cancer were included in the study. Patients who refused to participate and those with chronic nausea and vomiting complaints; active infection; heart, kidney, liver, and respiratory failure; anticipated difficult airway; and neurological, psychiatric, or muscular diseases were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

2.2. Body temperature measurement

The body temperature was measured before and after brachytherapy. The temperature was measured at the forehead in celsius (°C) with an infrared thermometer without contact with the skin (Braun No touch + forehead thermometer, BTF300USV1, Germany). The initial body temperature was measured before transportation to the brachytherapy suite. Post-procedural body temperature was measured after the placement and fixation of the applicator with vaginal packing. The body temperature was recorded by the same anesthetist. The value of ΔT is calculated by subtracting the initial body temperature from the body temperature measured after the medical procedure. The staff was blinded to the temperature measurements to avoid changes in the clinical management of the patient. The Room temperature was maintained at 24°C.

2.3. Anesthesia management

All patients underwent pre-anesthetic assessment and were classified according to the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification a few days before at the police clinic. Patients fasted for a minimum of 6 hours. A peripheral intravenous cannula (20–22G) was placed in the upper limb, and 1 to 2 mg of midazolam was administered before lithotomy and Foley catheter insertion. Standard ASA monitoring was applied throughout the procedure, including noninvasive arterial blood pressure, heart rate, and pulse oximetry in every 5 minutes. Oxygen was administered using a facemask at a rate of 4 L/min. After initial induction dose of 50 to 100 mcgr fentanyl and 0.8 to 1 mg/kg propofol procedure began. The patients were given fractionated propofol throughout the medical procedure and monitored on a Ramsey sedation scale ranging from 3 to 5. Additional 0.2 mg/kg propofol doses was administered if needed during the procedure. In line with the decisions of the blinded staff anesthetist, this protocol was applied for anesthesia management of the patients. Anesthesia duration was defined as the time from midazolam administration to the completion of vaginal packing. Recovery time is defined as the time from completion of the procedure to patients with sufficient recovery levels that respond purposefully to verbal commands at a conversational level (Modified Ramsay Sedation Score of 2). After recovery, the patient was transported for computed tomography. In this period, 100 mg intravenous tramadol infusion was administered to the patients for analgesia.

2.4. Data recorded

Patient age, body weight, height, body mass index (BMI), ASA physical status, comorbidities, fasting time, anesthesia and recovery time, and initial and postprocedural body temperature were recorded. Hemodynamic data, including heart rate, systolic and diastolic arterial pressure, and SpO2 levels, were also recorded.

2.5. Statistical analysis

According to the studies of Kim and Yi hypothermia rates were reported as 42.3% and 17.1%, respectively.[8,9] We assumed that we would detect a lower prevalence of hypothermia due to the deep sedation. Assuming that the rate of hypothermia with deep sedation could be 12%, which is lower than general anesthesia in patients receiving brachytherapy treatment, the estimated sample size was determined to be at least 23 patients, with g = 0.30 effect size, 80% power, and α = 0.05. The sample size was calculated using the “GPower 3.1.9.2” package program.

The IBM SPSS version 20 (Chicago, IL, USA) statistical package program was used for the data analysis, and the significance limit was set at P < .05. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviation when normally distributed, and as medians, minimum, and maximum values for those without a normal distribution. Discrete data are expressed as numbers and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to examine the compatibility of the data for a normal distribution. The Wilcoxon test was used to compare the body temperature, pulse rate, systolic/diastolic arterial pressure, and saturation values before and after the procedure. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the fasting time, anesthesia time, and recovery time between sessions with and without hypothermia. Correlations between continuous data were analyzed using Spearman correlation coefficient.

No correlation was found between post-procedure body temperature and fasting time (Spearman Correlation Coefficient (r) = −0.092; P = .385). A negative correlation was found between anesthesia time and post-procedural body temperature values (r = −0.385, P < .001).

The mean ± SD body temperature change values (∆T = Postprocedural BT- Initial BT) in the sessions were found to be −0.32 ± 0.17. It was observed that there was a decrease in body temperature of at most −1.3 degrees and at least −0.1 degrees during the sessions. A decrease of ≥ 0.4°C was detected in any session in 16 (69.9%) of the 23patients. A decrease of ≥ 0.4°C was detected in 34 (37%) of the 92 sessions.

In our study, no complications related to hypothermia were identified in the patients. There were no complaints or dissatisfaction due to chills among the patients.

4. Discussion

This study demonstrated hypothermia in 2 (8.7%) of the 23 patients and 5 (5.4%) of the 92 outpatient gynecological brachytherapy procedure sessions, which may be attributed to the disruptive effect of anesthesia on thermoregulation.

Perioperative hypothermia is a preventable anesthetic complication associated with poor outcomes, although its prevalence has decreased over the years with increasing awareness, routine monitoring, and the use of various warming methods.[8,13–15] Although there are many studies related to inadvertent hypothermia in major surgical procedures in the literature, there are few studies on non-operating room anesthesia applications.[16,17] In daily outpatient anesthesia practice, hypothermia may be overlooked due to various factors, such as the common sense that the risk and prevalence are low, and lack of monitoring opportunity due to rapid and intense service.[7–12]

Brachytherapy is usually performed as an outpatient for gynecologic cancer treatment. The procedure consisted of painful and uncomfortable urinary catheter insertion, cervical dilatation, radiation applicator placement, and vaginal gauze packing. It is usually performed under different anesthesia techniques, which may lead to hypothermia, such as spinal/epidural, general anesthesia, or deep sedation, depending on the center.[5] Both spinal and intravenous anesthetics impair thermoregulation and may cause hypothermia.[7] At our center, we provide deep sedation and analgesia using midazolam, propofol, fentanyl, and (or) ketamine. Although there is an undeniably young portion of the gynecologic cancer population, another significant proportion consists of frail and elderly patients who are more prone to hypothermia.[6]

Many studies have shown that the duration of anesthesia and advanced age are risk factors for hypothermia.[8,16] Since the number of patients was limited, our study showed no significant difference in terms of anesthesia and recovery times between the 5 hypothermic sessions and the 82 normothermic sessions. However, our study showed a negative correlation between anesthesia time and post-procedural body temperature values. As the duration of anesthesia increases, lower end-of-procedure body temperature values are achieved. Although statistics cannot be obtained in terms of demographic data due to the limited number of patients, the contribution of patient characteristics to hypothermia may be highlighted. One of these 2 patients with postprocedural hypothermia was older in age 79 years, but the other was 26 years old. The elderly patients had a BMI of 42.2 kg/m2. The other patient was anemic, with a hemoglobin level of 9.5 mg/dL.

Therefore, it can be assumed that hypothermia may cause adverse clinical outcomes by prolonging recovery time in at-risk patients. Although the present study did not show negative clinical results, it showed that hypothermia occurs in radiotherapy procedures performed in a remote location outside the operating room, for which recovery time is therefore more important.

Our study may contribute to patient safety by encouraging the anesthesia community to perform temperature monitoring, which is often overlooked in areas where day-to-day procedures are performed outside the operating room.

The limitation of our study is that although it shows body temperature decrease and hypothermia in NORA procedures, it does not indicate negative effects and clinical results. Further studies are needed to reveal the prevalence of hypothermia in different anesthesia methods, such as spinal/general anesthesia in brachytherapy, and to investigate preventive measures in outpatient areas.

In conclusion, inadvertent hypothermia can be observed during brachytherapy for cervical cancer under sedation. This study shows that inadvertent hypothermia, a preventable complication associated with adverse clinical outcomes after general anesthesia, can also occur during NORA procedures. Further research is needed to investigate adverse clinical outcomes of NORA procedures. Routine temperature monitoring should be encouraged by NORA institutions.
