Characterization of bacterial microbiome and molecular detection of rickettsiosis in free-living ticks (Amblyomma sculptum Berlese, 1888)
Authors: André Pinheiro Almeida, Cristina Tschorny Moncau-Gadbem, Carolina Purcell Goes, Ingrid Soares Garcia, Thais Ribeiro da Silva, Luan Gaspar Clemente, Luiz Lehmann Coutinho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmblyomma sculptum is a major tick species in southeastern Brazil and an important vector in the epidemiology of Brazilian Spotted Fever (BSF). This study characterized the bacterial microbiome of free-living A. sculptum ticks in a BSF-endemic area, focusing on differences among developmental stages and sexes, and investigated rickettsial agents using 16S rRNA gene (V3–V4) sequencing. A total of 154 ticks were collected and analysed as 13 pooled samples grouped by stage and sex. Sequencing identified a diverse bacterial community of 180 genera, dominated by Sphingomonas, Nocardioides, Actinomycetospora and Methylobacterium, alongside genera of potential zoonotic relevance such as Rickettsia, Anaplasma, Ehrlichia and Coxiella, mainly in nymph pools. Alpha and beta diversity analysis showed that microbial community composition differed among stages and sexes, with adult males exhibiting higher richness and compositional dispersion, whereas nymphs showed reduced diversity and tighter clustering. Because 16S sequencing does not allow species-level identification of rickettsiae, positive samples were further analysed by PCR amplification and sequencing of the gltA gene, which identified Rickettsia bellii. Together, these results highlight stage- and sex-associated patterns in the microbiome of free-living A. sculptum and support the use of integrated microbiome profiling and targeted rickettsial detection in ecological and epidemiological studies of tick-borne diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38069-7.Keywords: Brazilian spotted fever, Next generation sequencing, Rickettsiosis, MetabarcodingSubject terms: Diseases, Microbiology

Introduction

Ticks are hematophagous arachnids of the order Ixodida and represent the most efficient vectors of animal diseases among invertebrates1,2, while also hosting complex microbial communities composed of symbionts and potential pathogens that may be associated with pathogen persistence and transmission dynamics. In São Paulo State, southeastern Brazil, the tick Amblyomma sculptum Berlese, 1888, is the main vector of rickettsioses3 and can also transmit other pathogens, such as Theileria equi in horses4.

The species of Rickettsia genus belonging to the Spotted Fever Group (SFG) such as Rickettsia conorii, Rickettsia parkeri, Rickettsia australis, and Rickettsia rickettsii can cause pathologies in humans, with the latter being the most lethal5. Rickettsia rickettsii occurs in less than 1% of free ticks in the environment6 and cannot maintain high levels of bacteremia through stage change or transovarial transmission7,8. This bacteria is also responsible for the zoonotic disease known as Brazilian Spotted Fever (BSF) and, in recent years, it has been associated with hundreds of cases, a majority of which have resulted in fatalities. This scenario is accentuated in the southeastern region, which concentrates most of the Brazilian cases, with the state of São Paulo (the most populous state in Brazil) being even more worrying, as it presents 90% of the cases and high lethality9.

Given the low prevalence of rickettsial infection in free-living ticks and the complex ecological dynamics underlying pathogen maintenance, approaches that consider the broader microbial context of tick vectors may provide complementary insights into rickettsiosis ecology. Extensive research has been conducted to investigate the presence of endosymbiotic bacteria or disease-causing parasites within the tick microbiota, primarily focusing on the use of specific genetic markers targeting particular pathogen groups10–13. These studies have revealed variations linked to the geography, species, and sex of these arthropod vectors14. By examining the composition of different prokaryotic groups in the samples, researchers can identify the co-occurrence of zoonotic species and generate hypotheses about how these associations may affect pathogen transmission risk15.

Ticks are increasingly recognized as complex microecosystems in which diverse bacterial communities coexist with symbionts and potential pathogens. The composition and structure of these microbial communities may vary according to biological factors such as developmental stage and sex, as well as environmental exposure, particularly in free-living ticks16. Although such associations do not imply direct causal effects on pathogen transmission or infection risk, they provide an important ecological framework for interpreting patterns of microbial coexistence and detection within tick vectors.

Genetic markers such as the highly conserved 16S ribosomal gene have been progressively used to study the tick microbiome17, along with molecular technologies such as next-generation sequencing (NGS)18, mainly in Europe, North America, and Asia. However, the 16S ribosomal gene sequence does not allow for the discrimination of rickettsiae at the species level. The sequencing of the gltA gene, which encodes the citrate synthase enzyme, which is common to all rickettsiae, has been applied for species discrimination19,20.

In field studies with free-living ticks, sample pooling is frequently used due to low infection rates and the need to optimize sample processing. Although pooling limits the estimation of individual-level prevalence and reduces statistical power for some comparisons, it allows more efficient and comprehensive screening of microbial communities and pathogen occurrence across developmental stages and sexes. In contrast to several other tick vectors of zoonoses, the microbiome of A. sculptum has not yet been described in endemic BSF areas. Regarding the constraints of pooled sampling, the objectives of this study were to characterize the bacterial microbiome associated with free-living A. sculptum ticks across developmental stages and sexes, and to investigate the occurrence of rickettsial agents in a BSF endemic area using complementary molecular approaches.

Results

Tick collection and extraction of genomic DNA

Samples were collected at six sites in the municipality of Piracicaba, São Paulo, with CO₂ traps. A total of 154 free-living Amblyomma sculptum ticks were collected, comprising 135 nymphs and 19 adults (8 males and 11 females). Ticks collected at each site were grouped into pools according to developmental stage and sex, resulting in 13 pooled samples (nymphs, n = 4; females, n = 4; males, n = 5), which constituted the analytical units in this study. Pool size varied according to local infestation intensity and developmental stage, ranging from 10 to 65 individuals for nymph pools, 2 to 3 individuals for female pools, and 1 to 4 individuals for male pools (Table 1). At all sampling sites, nymphs were more abundant than adults, consistent with the seasonal peak of this stage and the period of highest incidence of Brazilian Spotted Fever in the region during winter21.

Microbiome with 16S gene

All 13 pooled samples were successfully amplified for the V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene. Sequencing generated 966,996 raw reads, which were inspected for quality (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). After quality filtering, denoising, read merging, and chimera removal, 105,843 high-quality sequences were retained for taxonomic assignment. Taxonomic classification using the SILVA v138 database initially yielded 2,752 ASVs. Subsequent filtration removed seven non-bacterial ASVs, as well as two ASVs identified as potential contaminants using the frequency-based method implemented in the decontam package (Supplementary File 1). The final dataset comprised 2,743 bacterial ASVs (Supplementary Files 2, 3 and 4) and was used for all downstream analyses, including diversity metrics, rarefaction curves (Fig. 1), and statistical testing. The bacterial community encompassed 15 phyla, 27 classes, 63 orders, 91 families, and 180 genera, with Proteobacteria predominating overall, particularly in nymph pools. In contrast, Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes were more abundant in adult male pools (Fig. 2). The most abundant genera included species of Sphingomonas, Nocardioides, Actinomycetospora, Methylobacterium, Pseudonocardia, Allorhizobium–Neorhizobium–Pararhizobium–Rhizobium, Chryseobacterium, Hymenobacter, Brevundimonas, and Spirosoma (Fig. 3). Among genera containing species of potential zoonotic relevance, Rickettsia (19 ASVs) and Anaplasma (1 ASV) genera co-occurred in a single nymph pool (sample 4-N). In addition, the Ehrlichia genus (5 ASVs) was detected in two female pools (samples 2-F and 6-F), and the Coxiella genus (4 ASVs) was detected in three pools from two locations (samples 6-N, 6-F, and 5-N) (Supplementary Fig. 3). Detection at the genus level does not allow for the inference of species-level or pathogenic status in this dataset. All ASVs assigned to the order Rickettsiales were further evaluated by manual BLASTn searches against the NCBI nucleotide database, and the corresponding best hits are summarized in a supplementary table (Supplementary File 5).

Alpha diversity

Alpha diversity was assessed to describe within-sample microbial diversity (richness and evenness) and to compare groups based on tick developmental stage and sex (nymphs, adult males, and adult females). Richness estimators (Observed ASVs, Chao1, ACE, and Fisher’s alpha) and diversity indices that incorporate evenness (Shannon and inverse Simpson) were calculated for the 13 samples (Fig. 4). Richness estimators showed lower values in nymphs compared to adult ticks, with pairwise comparisons indicating differences primarily between nymphs and males (Observed ASVs, Chao1, ACE, and Fisher’s alpha; adjusted p < 0.05 in all nymph-male comparisons) (Supplementary File 6 and 7). In contrast, diversity indices that incorporate evenness showed less pronounced differences among groups. The Shannon index, which integrates both richness and evenness, did not differ significantly between developmental stages, whereas the inverse Simpson index, which is more strongly influenced by the dominance structure of abundant taxa, indicated only a tendency toward lower diversity in nymphs compared to males.

Beta diversity

Beta diversity was evaluated using Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac distance matrices to assess differences in microbial community structure among tick developmental stages, sex, sampling locations, and pool size. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) showed clearer separation among groups using Bray–Curtis distances, whereas patterns based on weighted UniFrac were less distinct (Figs. 5 and 6). PERMANOVA based on Bray–Curtis distances indicated a significant effect of developmental stage and sex on microbial community composition (F = 10.75, R² = 0.177, p = 0.047). The number of ticks per pool (Collected_Ticks) was also significant when tested individually (F = 11.39, R² = 0.094, p = 0.012). However, models including both variables did not show a significant interaction, indicating that pool size did not modify the differences observed among stage/sex groups. In weighted UniFrac analyses, developmental stage and sex did not reach statistical significance (F = 17.33, R² = 0.257, p = 0.073), but showed a trend toward differentiation. Pool size was significant when tested individually (F = 30.88, R² = 0.219, p = 0.009), whereas combined models did not improve explanatory power. Sampling location had a strong effect on weighted UniFrac distances (F = 18.50, R² = 0.569, p = 0.020). However, beta dispersion analyses showed significance among sampling locations was significant for Bray–Curtis distances (PERMDISP p < 0.001) and showed a near-significant trend for weighted UniFrac (PERMDISP p = 0.056). Due to this heterogeneity of dispersion, PERMANOVA results involving location were interpreted with caution. In contrast, beta dispersion showed no significant differences among stage/sex groups for either distance metric (PERMDISP p > 0.30) (Supplementary File 8).

Detection of rickettsiae species by PCR and sequencing for gltA gene

The gltA gene was screened by PCR in all 13 samples, of which only one tested positive and was selected for amplicon sequencing. The positive sample corresponded to pool 4-N, originally composed of 10 nymphs, and was the same sample previously identified as Rickettsia-positive based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Because ticks were analyzed in pools, the individual-level prevalence cannot be directly estimated. Assuming a single infected tick in the positive pool, the minimum infection rate (MIR) was 0.65% (1/154 ticks). The amplicon from the positive sample was sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq platform, generating 131,784 raw reads, and assembled de novo in Geneious, yielding a single dominant gltA contig with high read support. The contig measured 889 bp before primer trimming and 731 bp after removal of the RpCS.409d/RpCS.1258n primer regions, matching the expected internal fragment of the gltA amplicon (Supplementary File 9). A total of 42,538 reads were assembled into this contig. Manual BLASTn analysis of the trimmed contig showed 100% query coverage, 100% nucleotide identity, and an E-value of 0.0 with Rickettsia bellii strain Peruíbe gltA (GenBank accession JQ906786.1). Phylogenetic analysis based on a maximum likelihood tree of partial gltA sequences (1,237 aligned positions) placed the contig obtained in this study within a well-supported clade of R. bellii, clearly separated from R. parkeri and R. rickettsii (Fig. 7).

Discussion

All samples in our study were obtained from a BSF endemic region. The metataxonomic approach applied to A. sculptum ticks represents an innovation that could enhance future research. The choice of the V3–V4 regions of the 16S gene was based on their broad taxonomic resolution and previous successful applications in tick microbiome studies22. Our results differ from two recent studies that reported no amplification and sequencing failures for the V3-V4 regions of the 16S gene, likely due to limited amounts of extracted DNA from samples ranging from one to nine adult A. sculptum ticks23 (some experimentally fed). These failures contrast with successful amplification in other Amblyomma species24, and with our study, that achieved successful amplification in all 13 samples, suggesting that the applied extraction technique appears to be effective for studying A. sculptum nymphs and adults. This aligns with recent findings using a modified protocol with proteinase K25.

16S gene sequencing revealed Proteobacteria as the predominant phylum in A. sculptum, a pattern consistent with the bacteriomes reported in other hard and soft tick species, including Ornithodoros brasiliensis, Amblyomma aureolatum, Rhipicephalus sanguineus, Haemaphysalis punctata, Dermacentor marginatus, and Ixodes ricinus26,27. Actinobacteria and Bacteroidetes were also predominant, but in lower abundance, resembling patterns found in Ixodes ricinus26.

At the genus level, species of Sphingomonas, Nocardioides, Actinomycetospora, Methylobacterium, and Pseudonocardia were among the most abundant taxa in our samples, together with less abundant genera such as Pseudomonas and Rhodococcus. Similar environmentally associated genera have been frequently reported in field-collected ticks, even after surface sterilization, and according to Portillo et al.26, some of these genera may be acquired from the environment and soil and are thought to reflect a combination of endogenous, host-associated and soil-derived bacteria. In Dermacentor marginatus, Dermacentor reticulatus and Amblyomma americanum (also washed before DNA extraction), species of Pseudomonas, Sphingomonas, Methylobacterium, Rhodococcus and Williamsia have likewise been reported at high relative abundances28,29, supporting the view that these taxa are recurrent components of the bacteriome of questing ticks across different habitats. In the present study, ticks were not surface-sterilized before DNA extraction, and these genera should therefore be interpreted cautiously, as they may represent both cutaneous/environmental bacteria and internal microbiota rather than strictly endogenous symbionts. To mitigate this limitation, we adopted a conservative bioinformatic approach and applied the decontam frequency-based method, which identified and removed two ASVs assigned to the Sphingomonas and Actinomycetospora genera before downstream analyses.

Sequencing also identified genera that include pathogenic species. ASVs assigned to Rickettsia were detected in one nymph pool (4-N) that was co-infected with Anaplasma, similar to previous reports of Rickettsia–Anaplasma co-occurrence in ticks14. Additionally, ASVs assigned to Coxiella (6-N, 6-F and 5-N) and Ehrlichia (2-F and 6-F) were detected in other pools. Ehrlichia and Anaplasma include species that are major tick-borne pathogens of cattle and can negatively affect livestock production, and coinfections involving these genera have been documented in several tick species, both in questing stages and on domestic animals30. Coinfections between pathogens of these genera have been previously reported in other tick species, both in the free-living phase and parasitizing domestic animals as well31,32. Although the genus Coxiella can cause Q fever in various hosts worldwide and disease in birds33–36, in ticks, its presence is usually attributed to endosymbionts rather than zoonotic species, consistent with previous 16S-based studies37. The co-occurrence of these genera in our pools may reflect neutral coexistence within the tick microbiome, but the available 16S data do not allow inference of synergistic or antagonistic ecological interactions among these taxa. Future studies combining experimental infections, longitudinal sampling and higher-resolution genomic markers will be required to clarify whether such coinfections represent neutral associations or ecologically structured interactions.

The taxonomy assigned in this study partially agrees with the hypothesis of shared bacterial families among blood-feeding arthropod vectors. Recent analyses question whether blood-feeding insects and mites share microbial symbionts despite evolutionary differences, highlighting in their analyses families such as Rickettsiaceae, Anaplasmataceae, Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonadaceae, Moraxellaceae, Bacillaceae, Sphingomonadaceae and Staphylococcaceae38. Interestingly, our survey lacked representatives of Moraxellaceae, Bacillaceae, and Staphylococcaceae, suggesting that microbial compositions may be associated with tick species, life stage, and feeding status. Only a subset of bacteria persists throughout the tick’s life cycle, as well as endosymbionts that can adapt to different internal organs such as midgut, ovary, Malpigui tubules, and salivary glands39,40. Nevertheless, the absence of some bacterial families in our study, reported in other vectors, suggests that microbiome composition remains strongly context-dependent, varying with species, life stage, and ecological conditions.

Among the 180 genera identified, Arthrobacter, commonly associated with soil, was detected in adult samples (6-M and 2-F). One of the attributed origins is exogenous and suggested surface contamination during sample preparation26, while another study classified this genus as the fourth most abundant genus in Amblyomma ticks41. Furthermore, a microbiome analysis of unfed ticks that applied 16S gene sequencing and culture media identified this genus only through isolated culture42. A previous report identified species of this genus as the cause of severe allergic reactions and erythema after skin infection in humans in a forested region endemic for Lyme disease43.

The Enterobacillus genus, found in adult samples (2-F, 5-F, 5-M and 6-F), was initially observed in the intestinal tract of beetles44. This genus is from the Enterobacteriaceae family, known as primary and opportunistic pathogens, and has been reported as the most abundant and to infect ticks at all stages of development in some areas of a large study14. This study suggests a potential competitive interaction with rickettsiae, indicating the likely importance of Enterobacillus as a commensal. Interestingly, in our study, rickettsiae were likewise absent in the samples where Enterobacillus was detected, similarly to the suggested interaction from that study.

Microbial diversity varied significantly across developmental stages and sexes. According to the seven alpha diversity indices and richness estimators calculated, the highest richness, equitability, and abundance were observed in adult males, followed by adult females, with nymphs showing the lowest values. These differences between males and females are consistent with previous studies14,26,29,45, which observed distinctions in the microbial community of tick vectors of borreliosis and rickettsiosis by sex, regardless of the different sampling locations. The studies cited above observed that this difference was linked to reduced richness, attributed to the prevalence of Rickettsia in females, or distinct host preferences and relationships among tick microorganisms, resulting in a higher incidence of suppressive endosymbionts and pathogens of other taxa in females. However, in our study, lower diversity was observed in nymphs (where Rickettsia and other genera were also present), indicating that the reduced diversity in nymphs is also associated with the dominance of specific bacterial taxa at this life stage, as previously noted in females.

Environmental factors, such as temperature and seasonality, may influence the microbial composition of free-living ticks45. In our study, samples were obtained during the dry winter typical of southeastern Brazil, a period that can impose stress on ticks; future collections across different seasons may help clarify how climatic variation shapes microbial communities.

Developmental stage and feeding history are also important. A previous study46 reported higher microbial diversity in unfed larvae than in nymphs that had fed once, indicating that blood meals can reduce richness. Supporting this, Amblyomma aureolatum collected from crab-eating foxes showed extreme dominance of Francisella genus (> 97%)27, whereas no single genus prevailed in our samples. Based on this, in free-living ticks, the absence of recent blood meals appears to preserve higher microbial diversity and equitability. However, because blood meals are essential throughout the tick life cycle, they not only reshape microbial communities during feeding but also represent the primary route of acquisition and transmission of pathogen47,48. Future sampling of engorged A. sculptum may help clarify how blood meals reshape microbial diversity across the tick life cycle. Variations related to tick sex have also been documented. A recent microbiome study identified differences in microbial composition between adult males and females49, as well as significant contrasts in Observed ASV and Shannon alpha diversity between nymphs and adults. Differential abundance analyses further indicated a higher representation of the order Rickettsiales in nymphs than in adult males. In partial agreement with these findings, our data showed that Rickettsia and Anaplasma were exclusively detected in nymphs, whereas Ehrlichia was only identified in adult females. Notably, no genus from this order was detected in adult male samples, reinforcing sex- and stage-related patterns in microbial community structure.

In agreement with the alpha diversity patterns, Bray–Curtis-based analyses indicated significant differences in bacterial community composition among developmental stages and sexes, with nymphs showing tighter clustering and adult males exhibiting greater compositional dispersion. These patterns suggest that differences among groups are driven mainly by shifts in relative abundances of shared taxa rather than by increased within-group heterogeneity. Similar patterns have been reported under both experimental and observational contexts. Maitre et al.50 demonstrated significant Bray–Curtis dissimilarities between Rickettsia helvetica-infected and uninfected ticks, with pathogen-free groups showing higher compositional dispersion. Likewise, a study on free-ranging Amblyomma americanum reported that samples with lower bacterial diversity were characterized by a higher relative abundance of Rickettsiales lineages12. Although our data are observational, we also observed reduced diversity in nymphs concomitant with the presence of Rickettsiales, whereas adult males displayed broader compositional variation. In contrast, developmental stage and sex were not significant when phylogenetic distances were considered using weighted UniFrac, suggesting that these groups differ primarily in the dominance structure of closely related bacterial taxa rather than in deep phylogenetic turnover. Effects of sampling location and pool size were detected but should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited and unbalanced sampling design.

In the present study, we confirmed the presence of Rickettsia by amplifying and sequencing the gltA gene, which also allowed species-level identification in the same sample previously identified as positive through 16S sequencing. This pathogenic genus was identified in samples taken from A. sculptum ticks found in areas near a stream with preserved riparian forest in Piracicaba, São Paulo. However, sequencing revealed, through both BLASTn and phylogenetic analysis, that it was Rickettsia bellii. This species is non-lethal to animals and humans and does not belong to the Spotted Fever Group (SFG)51. R. bellii has been detected in more than 25 species of ticks and can negatively affect the transovarial transmission of SFG species between tick generations52. Interestingly, a study conducted in Uberlândia, state of Minas Gerais, showed that A. sculptum may be refractory to R. bellii infection; a negative correlation was observed between the presence of A. sculptum and R. bellii infection in A. dubitatum populations53. This suggests that the dynamics of local tick species may influence infection patterns.

Estimating the prevalence of Rickettsia in free-living ticks from endemic areas requires careful consideration of sample size, particularly when pooled samples are used. A previous study using the conventional PCR with free-living adult A. sculptum ticks in a BSF transmission area in the state of São Paulo showed the presence of the Rickettsia genus in 0.8% of ticks, with a sampling of 353 specimens7. Other investigations have reported rickettsial occurrence rates ranging between 0 and 1.28%, including endemic areas6,54. In the present study, a total of 154 ticks were analyzed in pools, and a single pool composed of 10 nymphs tested positive for Rickettsia. Because individual infection status could not be determined within this pool, the minimum infection rate (MIR), assuming one infected tick per positive pool, was estimated at 0.65%. The maximum theoretical infection rate would be 6.5% if all ticks in the pool were infected. The true prevalence is therefore expected to lie between these bounds. Comparable infection rates have been reported in different regions of Brazil. For instance, the infection rates in Amblyomma ticks range from 0.58% in free-living ticks from Southeast Brazil (with a sample size of more than 1,800 ticks) to as high as 7% in A. cajennense ticks from Maranhão, which were infected with R. amblyommatis (with a sample size of 99 adult ticks)55. Other recent studies found 7.7% of Amblyomma dubitatum ticks positive for R. bellii56, and 6.6% in 137 ticks of the same species in the Brazilian cerrado57, and 6,6% in 137 Amblyomma ticks for two Rickettsia species in the state of Espírito Santo58. Taken together, although our MIR estimate is lower than several reports based on individual testing, it falls within the range of values described for free-living Amblyomma ticks in Brazil. Increasing sample size and adopting smaller pool sizes or individual-level testing in future studies would improve the precision and resolution of infection rate estimates.

Environmental factors such as climate have also been associated tick abundance and rickettsial infection rates. A large-scale study in central Brazil (municipality of Brasília) suggested that climatic factors, rather than capybara presence and the type of vegetation, played a larger role in shaping tick populations (more than 1700), with 13.3% of A. sculptum ticks testing positive for R. bellii59. Seasonal patterns were also noted in Amblyomma tick populations (more than 2000 ticks) from Iguaçu National Park, with a 9.3% infection rate for R. bellii60. In this same study, a negative correlation was observed between the presence of R. bellii (non-pathogenic) and R. parkeri (pathogenic) DNA in Amblyomma ovale tick populations. The absence of Rickettsia rickettsii in our study samples may reflect a combination of factors, including low prevalence, sampling limitations, pooling strategy, local ecological conditions, or may also reflect control measures adopted in the area in recent years, such as monitoring the number of free-living ticks, chemical control of infested areas, physical barriers and reproductive control of capybaras (primary hosts), and serological monitoring of other hosts carried out in recent years61,62.

This study provides the first characterization of the microbiome in free-living nymphs and adults of A. sculptum from a Brazilian spotted fever (BSF) endemic area. Our analysis revealed differences in microbial composition across life stages and sexes, as demonstrated by multiple diversity metrics. The detection of R. bellii through gltA metabarcoding supports the utility of combining 16S metabarcoding with targeted gltA sequencing for rickettsial species identification and confirms its presence in this tick population. While no pathogenic R. rickettsii was detected, this absence aligns with previous reports63,64 that observed A. sculptum exhibiting greater resistance to R. rickettsii infection compared to other Amblyomma species, such as A. aureolatum. Furthermore, the ongoing occurrence of BSF cases and fatalities in recent years still highlights the continuous public health concern. Our study additionally revealed the presence of other potential pathogen genera beyond Rickettsia species, enhancing the complexity of tick-borne disease dynamics.

Overall, our findings contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of A. sculptum microbial ecology and support the implementation of integrated surveillance strategies. Future studies are necessary to expand high-throughput metabarcoding sequencing approaches to different seasons and areas with a high risk of human exposure to ticks. Moreover, this study opens perspectives for experimental research exploring how the tick microbiota interacts with R. rickettsii infection and potential control targets. These efforts will provide critical data to assist vector control measures and disease prevention strategies in BSF risk and endemic areas.

Methods

Sample collection and tick identification

Free-living ticks were collected using CO₂ traps65,66 containing approximately 250 grams of dry ice cubes on voile fabric cloths (400 cm²), at six points frequented by host animals in the municipality of Piracicaba, São Paulo (22º42’30” S, 47º37’43.0”, Fig. 8) for one hour during August 2022 (field surveys were conducted using permethrin-treated protective clothing). All collected ticks were analyzed under a stereomicroscope and with taxonomic keys to confirm species67,68, then kept at -20 °C until the subsequent analysis. Each sampling point was treated as an independent collection unit, and ticks collected at the same point were grouped into pools before DNA extraction. Pooling was adopted to represent local infestation units and to optimize subsequent molecular analyses, with the primary objective of detecting circulating Rickettsia taxa at each location. Each pool consisted exclusively of ticks from a single sampling point that shared the same developmental stage and/or sex. Pool size varied according to natural differences in local infestation intensity. For statistical analyses, samples were grouped according to developmental stage and sex (females, males, and nymphs), which represented the primary biological factor tested in this study (Supplementary Fig. 4). Tick collection did not require collection permits or approval from institutional ethics committees, as no vertebrate animals were captured, handled, or experimentally manipulated, and only free-living ticks were collected from vegetation.

DNA extraction

For extraction of microbiome DNA, each tick pool sample was macerated in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle. The homogenate was then incubated in lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 100 mM EDTA, 2% SDS) supplemented with proteinase K (20 mg/mL), followed by incubation at 55 °C and 1500 rpm for 30 min to promote protein digestion. Protein precipitation was performed by adding ammonium acetate (7.5 M), followed by incubation on ice for 20 min and centrifugation at 14,000 rpm at room temperature (RT). The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube, and DNA was precipitated with cold isopropanol by gentle inversion and incubation at − 20 °C for 1 h. After centrifugation, the DNA pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol, air-dried (RT), and resuspended in ultrapure water. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop® ND-2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). DNA integrity was evaluated by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and final quantification was carried out using the Invitrogen Qubit™ fluorometer. During field collection and laboratory handling, ticks were manipulated using disposable gloves, and instruments and working surfaces were routinely decontaminated with ethanol, sodium hypochlorite (2%), and UV exposure before processing (for 15 min).

16S conventional PCR

All samples were amplified for the 16S gene in the V3 and V4 regions, in a first reaction containing 0.5 µL of the primers 341 F (5’-TCG TCG GCA GCG TCA GAT GTG TAT AAG AGA CAG CCT ACG GGN GGC WGC AG) and 785R (5’-GTC TCG TGG GCT CGG AGA TGT GTA TAA GAG ACA GGA CTA CHV GGG TAT CTA ATC C) at 10 µM. The reaction occurred under conditions of 95 °C for three minutes and 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, in addition to a final extension of five minutes at 72 °C. The reaction was performed with PCRBio Ultra Mix (PCR Biosystems, cat. #PB10.32-01). Positive samples were verified by electrophoresis in a 1.5% agarose gel, based on the expected fragment size. PCR reactions and library purification steps included a laboratory-standard positive control for 16S amplification and a negative control consisting of ultrapure water used in the DNA extraction process.

16S sequencing and taxonomy identification

Positive samples amplified for the 16S gene were prepared for sequencing using Illumina 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation protocol (Illumina, cat. #15044223). The amplicons underwent purification with AMPure XP beads, followed by second amplification for ligation of the adapters, purification of the second amplification, normalization, and pooling of the libraries, followed the manufacturer’s protocol69. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina MiSeq® (2 × 250 bp paired-end) and the FASTQ files processed using R software (version 4.2.2) within the RStudio environment. Sequence processing was conducted using DADA2 v1.34.0 (Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm)70. Paired-end reads were filtered and trimmed using the filterAndTrim function with truncation lengths of 245 bp (forward) and 225 bp (reverse), maximum expected errors of 2 for both reads, no ambiguous bases allowed (maxN = 0), and quality truncation at Q2. PhiX reads were removed. Truncation lengths were selected based on visual inspection of per-base quality score profiles for forward and reverse reads generated within DADA2. Error rates were learned separately for forward and reverse reads using the default DADA2 error model. Sequence variants were inferred with the dada algorithm, and paired reads were merged (with a minimum overlap of 10 base pairs). Chimeric sequences were removed using the consensus method. Taxonomic assignment was performed using the SILVA v138 database71. ASVs assigned to the order Rickettsiales were further evaluated by manual BLASTn72 searches against the NCBI nucleotide database to refine taxonomic identification. For each ASV, the best hit was recorded based on percent identity, query coverage, and E-value. Potential contaminant ASVs were further evaluated using the R package decontam v1.26.0, applying the frequency-based method with DNA concentration of each sample as input. ASVs identified as contaminants were removed prior to downstream analyses. In addition, only ASVs taxonomically assigned to the kingdom Bacteria were retained, and all non-bacterial taxa were excluded. The resulting filtered dataset was used for all subsequent analyses, which were conducted in R using the PhyloSeq package v1.50.0.

16S alpha diversity

Alpha diversity analyses were conducted exclusively to compare developmental stage and sex groups (females, males, and nymphs). Richness was estimated using Observed ASVs, Chao1, ACE, and Fisher indices, whereas diversity and evenness were assessed using Shannon and Inverse Simpson indices. Data were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test), both using native R functions. Depending on the distributional assumptions, comparisons among groups (females, males, and nymphs) were performed using either Student’s t-test (parametric) or the Mann–Whitney U test (non-parametric). Pairwise comparisons were conducted between all groups, and p-values were adjusted (only for multiple testing) using the false discovery rate (FDR) method (Benjamini–Hochberg). Statistical analyses and plots were performed using the packages ggplot2 v4.0.0, ggpubr v0.6.1, dplyr v1.1.4, tidyr v1.3.1, and purrr v1.0.4.

16S beta diversity

Beta diversity analyses were performed to evaluate differences in microbial community composition primarily according to developmental stage and sex. Sampling location and pool size (number of ticks per pool) were included as additional variables to assess their potential influence on community structure. The number of ticks in each pool was included as a variable to evaluate its impact on microbiome analysis. Bray-Curtis and weighted UniFrac distance matrices were calculated to capture differences in microbial composition based on taxonomy and phylogenetic relationships. Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) plots were generated to visualize clustering or separation patterns between samples. Statistical significance was tested using PERMANOVA (vegan v2.7-1) with 999 permutations to assess group differences (stage/sex, sampling location, and pool size). PERMANOVA models were fitted separately for each variable and for additive and interaction models involving developmental stage/sex and pool size. Interactions between variables and homogeneity of dispersion were also evaluated. To calculate the weighted UniFrac matrix, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using ASV sequences (phangorn v2.12.1 and phytools v2.4-4). Sequences were aligned and converted into distance matrices using a maximum likelihood approach. A Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree was optimized under the GTR (General Time Reversible) model. The tree was midpoint-rooted and incorporated into the phyloseq object for graphical visualization. The phylogenetic tree was used exclusively to calculate weighted UniFrac distances and was not intended for taxonomic inference.

gltA conventional PCR

The samples were also amplified with PCRBio Ultra Mix for the gltA gene using the primers RpCS.409d (5’-CCT ATG GCT ATT ATG CTT GC) and RpCS.1258n (5’-ATT GCA AAA AGT ACA GTG AAC A) in order to amplify an 850 bp fragment common to the Rickettsia genus. The reaction occurred under conditions of 95 °C for 3 min and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension of 5 min at 72 °C. Positive samples were also verified by agarose gel electrophoresis.

gltA sequencing, taxonomy, and phylogenetics analysis

Positive samples amplified for the gltA gene were gel-purified with the PureLink™ Quick Gel Extraction Kit (Invitrogen, cat. #K2100-12 and #K2100-25) to ensure recovery of amplicons with the expected size and to minimize non-specific products. Purified amplicons, derived from respective individual samples, were used to prepare sequencing libraries with the Illumina DNA Prep Kit, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq® platform using 2 × 250 bp paired-end. Sequences reads were assembled and analyzed using the Geneious software (Biomatters Ltd.)73, to generate a de novo contig, followed by BLASTn analysis for species-level taxonomic assignment based on the highest percentage of identity in GenBank (database of reference). The contig assigned to a determined species (with the de novo method) was used to construct the phylogenetic tree. Reference gltA gene sequences from Rickettsia parkeri (MH160728.2), Rickettsia rickettsii (MT958042.1) (from Spotted Fever Group – SFG), and Rickettsia australis (U59718.1) (serving as a SFG outgroup)74 were retrieved from GenBank to contextualize phylogenetic relationships with representative species. In addition, a sequence from Rickettsia bellii (KU586331.1), an ancestral group species known to occur in the study region75, was included in the alignment. All sequences (sample-derived and references) were aligned using MUSCLE v5.1 under default parameters76. The resulting alignment was used for phylogenetic inference. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE v2.4.0, with the best-fitting nucleotide substitution model automatically selected by ModelFinder. Branch support was assessed using 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and 1,000 SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test (aLRT) replicates. The tree was rooted using Rickettsia australis as outgroup. The resulting tree was exported in Newick format and visualized and annotated using the Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL v7.2.2). For descriptive purposes, the minimum infection rate (MIR) for gltA-positive samples was calculated assuming one infected tick per positive pool and expressed as the number of infected ticks divided by the total number of ticks tested77.
