Multi-view deep learning framework for the detection of chest X-rays compatible with pediatric pulmonary tuberculosis

ABSTRACT
Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health burden, particularly in low-resource, high-prevalence regions. Pediatric TB diagnosis poses challenges with non-specific symptoms and less distinct radiological manifestations than adult TB. Many affected children remain undiagnosed or untreated. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends chest X-ray (CXR) for TB screening and triage, given its accessibility and rapid assessment of pulmonary TB-related abnormalities. We present pTBLightNet, a multi-view deep learning framework to detect pediatric pulmonary TB by identifying TB-compatible CXRs with consistent radiological findings. Leveraging both frontal and lateral CXR views, our framework is pre-trained on adult CXR datasets (N = 114,173), then fine-tuned or trained from scratch, and subsequently evaluated on CXR datasets (N = 918) from three pediatric TB cohorts. It achieves an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.903 and 0.682 on internal and external testing, respectively. External evaluation supports its effectiveness and generalizability using CXR TB compatibility, expert reading, microbiological confirmation and case definition as reference standards. Age-specific models (<5 and 5–18 years) perform competitively with those trained on larger undifferentiated populations, and adding lateral CXRs improves diagnosis in younger children. These results highlight the robustness of our approach across age groups and its potential to improve TB diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a long-standing infectious disease still considered one of the leading causes of death worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries1. Children face particularly high risks due to challenges in timely and accurate diagnosis2. TB is caused by a small aerobic bacterium called Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which usually affects the lungs, although other parts of the body can also be impacted3. After the coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic, TB is again the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent, surpassing HIV/AIDS1. However, during the pandemic, the number of people newly diagnosed and reported with TB dropped significantly—from 7.1 million in 2019 to 5.8 million in 2020—before rebounding to 8.2 million in 2023. This decline was attributed to reduced access to TB diagnosis and treatment rather than an actual reduction in cases1. Reduced access to TB diagnosis and treatment has led to an increase in TB deaths, which are expected to have worsened in subsequent years1. Early detection and treatment are crucial for reducing mortality, prevention of spread of TB in communities and for minimizing the risk of severe, long-term health issues.

Significant challenges remain in the diagnosis and treatment of pediatric TB infection and disease. Indeed, most children who die from TB are never diagnosed or treated4. Each year, 7.5 million children are infected with M. tuberculosis and 1.3 million children develop TB disease globally, accounting for 12% of the global TB burden1,5. Young children, defined as those under five years of age, have a lower estimated pediatric case detection ratio compared to children aged 5–14 years. This is due to difficulties in diagnosis, such as non-specific symptoms, challenges in obtaining respiratory samples and the lower bacterial loads in younger children6. Additionally, young children are of special concern since they have the highest progression risk for disseminated TB and related mortality7. Screening may be useful to identify children with potential TB and refer them to further testing, otherwise they should be considered for preventive treatment8.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends chest X-rays (CXRs) as a screening and triage tool for TB because of their wide availability, lower cost compared to other imaging techniques, and rapid acquisition with portable equipment to reduce the risk of cross-infection9,10. Besides, CXRs, along with symptom inquiry, are considered the best TB screening method and the primary imaging modality for assessing children with presumed intrathoracic TB11,12. The latest WHO guidelines further emphasize the role of CXRs in TB management by advocating for the inclusion of TB-compatible CXRs in existing clinical protocols13,14, which offer structured approaches for screening TB in children and young adolescents. In clinical practice, trained radiologists or TB experts detect TB by visually inspecting CXRs for characteristic signs of the TB disease. However, this subjective and time-consuming process can be prone to error or confusion with other diseases of similar radiological patterns, particularly in the case of pediatric TB due to their non-specific symptoms and less defined radiological features compared to adult TB15–17. The most frequent CXR findings in pediatric TB are lymphadenopathy, airway compression, air space consolidation, pleural effusion, cavities, miliary patterns, and Ghon focus18–20. The CXR lateral view (LAT) is considered a valuable complement to the frontal view, either antero-posterior (AP) or postero-anterior (PA), as it facilitates the assessment of mediastinal lymphadenopathy, one of the most relevant signs to confidently diagnose TB on CXRs21,22.

In this context, computer-aided diagnosis (CAD) systems supported by artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms can play a paramount role in both mass screening of TB and in assisting radiologists with the analysis of CXR images. CAD tools, particularly those using deep learning, have shown potential to improve the ability of radiologists to interpret images accurately and with fewer errors23, particularly in low-income countries with high TB burden24, thus addressing a gap in adult TB diagnostic expertise. In recent years, several AI-driven solutions, some CE-certified, have been validated as accurate and effective triage tools for adult pulmonary TB detection in high-burden regions, with performances comparable to or surpassing that of experienced readers25–27. However, existing methods designed for adults often fail to perform well in pediatric participants. Only a few certified systems currently address pediatric TB detection, focusing primarily on children older than five years, as monitored by the Stop TB Partnership and the FIND resource center (AI Products for Tuberculosis Healthcare, AI4HLTH website: https://www.ai4hlth.org). However, the efficacy of these products is not yet well established.

Despite numerous studies reporting good performance of AI systems in detecting adult TB from CXRs28–34, research on pediatric TB remains limited35,36. This highlights a pressing need to develop AI solutions tailored to infants and children37, particularly those under five years of age. Only a few studies have explored deep learning-based approaches for pediatric TB38–41, some employing transfer learning from adult CXR datasets to pediatric contexts39,40. For instance, Mouton et al.38 reported an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) of 0.676, while Palmer et al.39 employed a fine-tuned version of CAD4TB, a prominent CAD tool for TB detection42, achieving an AUC of 0.72 when fine-tuned on pediatric patients. In contrast, zero-shot classification tasks, i.e., training on adult data and testing on pediatric data (distribution-shifted data), yielded AUCs of 0.58, 0.697, and 0.70 in different studies39–41, indicating the data mismatch between adult and pediatric data, as well as the potential for further improvement in this domain. The main limitations of these studies include the small sample sizes, data from specific geographic regions, potential class imbalances between TB and non-TB patients, limited evaluation across diverse pediatric age groups and cohorts, and the absence of lateral CXRs, which are particularly relevant in clinical practice for TB diagnosis in younger children.

To address these challenges, we introduce pTBLightNet, a multi-view deep learning framework built on an efficient ResNet-like architecture, designed to assist in the detection of pediatric pulmonary TB from CXRs by identifying TB-compatible CXRs with consistent radiologic findings. To this end, we train pTBLightNet on a curated, multi-site pediatric TB dataset comprising data from both low- and high-TB-burden settings. In this study, we also examine the benefits of pre-training on adult CXR datasets, evaluate the impact of incorporating lateral CXRs in young children to improve the algorithm’s performance, explore the performance of age-specific models compared to all-age models, and apply explainable AI (XAI) techniques to interpret its outputs. Finally, we conduct both internal and external validations to assess the method’s effectiveness, with a particular focus on performance across different pediatric age groups.

We hypothesize that: i) pre-training on adult CXR data improves pediatric TB detection in AI models; ii) age-specific AI models are preferred over all-age models due to significant differences in TB disease development between younger and older children, leading to data mismatch; and iii) the LAT CXRs are particularly useful for diagnosing pediatric TB in younger children.

Results

Multi-view deep learning framework for pediatric TB-compatible CXR detection

The framework proposed (see Fig. 1-II) is a multi-view pipeline which consists of two main branches: one for AP/PA CXRs and another for LAT CXRs, which are later combined to deliver a classification result to score if the case is compatible with pediatric TB. Each branch is responsible for the automatic feature extraction of information compatible with pediatric TB findings in the lung and mediastinal regions within its corresponding CXR view. Both CXR images are preprocessed and fed into an efficient ResNet-like architecture (same architecture, different weights for each branch), which will output two different feature vectors, one for each view, and which will be later concatenated and linearly combined in a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), delivering a TB classification prediction for that specific case. The framework allows a flexible configuration of either AP/PA CXRs as the sole input or multi-view AP/PA and LAT input. To ensure our model learns features that are not overly specific to a single healthcare setting or population, we integrated datasets from both high- and low-resource environments. This approach aims to enhance model robustness and reduce context-specific biases that may arise when training solely on data from a single region. Pediatric TB diagnosis is highly variable across settings, influenced by differences in radiographic quality, disease burden, and clinical presentation. Including data from a high-resource setting helps expose the model to higher-quality imaging and broader diagnostic standards, while data from a low-resource, high-burden setting ensures relevance in areas where TB remains a major public health challenge. Importantly, we included an external validation cohort from a low-burden region to evaluate the model’s generalizability in contexts where TB is less prevalent but still clinically relevant. Across all cohorts, sites adhered to international pediatric TB diagnostic guidelines and included both microbiologically confirmed and clinically diagnosed cases, ensuring consistency in case definitions while capturing the clinical variability seen in practice.

We used four CXR datasets from three pediatric TB cohorts (N = 918): three datasets for training and internal evaluation, and one for external evaluation. The first two datasets (A and B) used for training and internal evaluation come from: dataset A) Manhiça Health Research Centre (CISM, Mozambique), referred to as cohort 17,18, with 218 participants; dataset B) the Spanish Pediatric TB Research Network (pTBred, Spain), referred to as cohort 244,45, with 565 participants. These two datasets comprised 783 participants with frontal (AP/PA) and lateral (LAT) CXRs. However, LAT images were not available for all patients. These data were used to train the models from scratch or to fine-tune the pre-trained models, and to perform internal evaluation on independent test subsets unseen by the model. For further evaluation, we used two additional datasets: dataset D, coming from cohort 1, with 265 unlikely TB patients to assess the model’s false positive rate (FPR); and dataset C, coming from Children’s National Hospital (CNH-TB-PEDs, DC, USA), referred to as cohort 3, with 71 participants, which served as an external evaluation dataset, with children ≤18 years diagnosed with TB (microbiological confirmation) and non-TB patients referred to the emergency department for respiratory diseases. Across these cohorts, there were confirmed TB patients without CXR TB compatibility (N = 5, 34, and 9 for cohorts 1, 2, and 3, respectively). To comprehensively assess pTBLightNet, we conducted evaluations across several key aspects: model training, age-specific performance, the role of the lateral views in enhancing diagnostic accuracy, and model performance on external datasets.

We first compared two model training strategies: one pre-trained on large-scale, publicly available adult CXR datasets covering a broad spectrum of thoracic diseases (N = 114,173), and another trained from scratch. The pre-training dataset was not TB-specific but included diverse control and diseased patients, enabling the model to learn general radiologic patterns. The pre-trained model was then fine-tuned on pediatric TB data. Both versions were evaluated on internal test data from datasets A and B to assess their effectiveness in identifying TB-compatible CXRs in children. This comparison aimed to determine whether pre-training on adult CXRs could enhance performance in the pediatric TB detection task.

To further assess the impact of patient age distribution on the pediatric TB CXR compatibility detection performance of our framework, we trained several models (see Fig. 1-C) on different age groups: an all-age model (ALL), trained on all the available training data (N = 432), i.e., all age groups included; an age-specific model with all the training data from younger children (YC, < 5 years, N = 261), and an age-specific model with all the training data from older children (OC, 5-18 years, N = 171). All these models were evaluated on the independent internal test sets from cohorts A and B, for all age groups (N = 160), YC (N = 80) and OC (N = 80).

Additionally, we investigated the impact of incorporating LAT CXRs alongside frontal views to improve CXR based diagnostic accuracy, particularly for young children. This multi-view approach was tested to determine whether the additional anatomical information from LAT views could enhance pediatric TB classification.

Finally, we evaluated the performance of the proposed framework on two additional datasets, C and D, aiming to assess the model’s false positive rate and its effectiveness on a completely independent dataset, respectively.

Model training approaches: from scratch vs. fine-tuning

We assessed two model training approaches — training from scratch and fine-tuning a pre-trained model — using an all-age model (also referred to as ALL) trained on all available data across age groups. Here, a total of 432 patients from datasets A and B were used to train an AP/PA model (see Fig. 2 for further information on the training data). The results when validating on the independent internal test set are included in Table 2. The mean improvements in AUC when pre-training the models, referred to as (P) in Table 2, were 7.56%, 7.37% and 7.75%, respectively, when compared to trained from scratch approaches (S). Age-specific models: YC and OC

We studied whether age-specific models would positively impact TB-compatible-CXR classification performance. To this end, we trained models with data distributed in different age groups. Training data were clearly reduced (N = 261 for YC, N = 171 for OC), but more homogeneous in terms of age distribution. Results from validating both the model trained with YC and OC’s data on the corresponding testing subsets from datasets A and B, as well as out-of-distribution (OOD) cross-testing on opposite age groups, are displayed in Table 2.

The model trained on YC data achieved lower yet comparable performance to the all-age model when tested on YC, with 39.58% less training data (N = 261). On the other hand, the model trained on OC data achieved greater performance than the all-age model when testing on the OC group, using 60.42% less training data (N = 171). When performing OOD cross-testing, i.e., testing on other age groups in which the models were not trained on, we notice a decrease in performance. Finally, generalized mean improvements were observed when pre-training the models, with increments of 4.7% and 7.6% in AUC when testing the YC and OC models, respectively, on their corresponding age groups. These results suggest that age-specific models may be more convenient for age-specific cohorts. Further details on the performance and scores of these age-specific models are included in the Supplementary Note 2 (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3).

Lateral view: impact on pediatric TB-compatible CXR detection

In clinical practice, lateral CXRs are used to deliver a more accurate diagnosis when screening for pediatric TB, particularly in young children under 5 years of age. Consequently, data were split in a way that younger children aged < 5 years from the independent test set (datasets A and B, from cohorts 1 and 2, respectively) had always both frontal and lateral CXRs.

To evaluate the contribution of lateral CXRs to pediatric TB classification according to the proposed framework (see Fig. 1), we conducted experiments comparing the performance on the independent test set using AP/PA images alone versus using both AP/PA and LAT images as input data to the pipeline.

The results for the all-age and YC models are shown in Table 3. The OC model was not trained on LAT images, as the clinical relevance of lateral CXRs primarily pertains to younger children, making training data for this subgroup and CXR view scarcely available. The results obtained show a slight improvement in AUC when both views are used, with increases of 0.6% and 2% in the all-age and age-specific YC models, respectively. To further validate our models, we conducted experiments on two additional independent datasets: an external dataset from a low TB endemic area (dataset C, cohort 3) and an internal dataset from a high TB endemic area (dataset D, cohort 1).

Validation on an independent internal dataset from a high TB endemic area

Finally, we conducted an experiment to evaluate our models’ performance on dataset D, i.e., extra unlikely TB patients from cohort 1 (CISM). This cohort consisted of presumed TB patients finally ruled out as unlikely TB patients, i.e., patients in which TB diagnosis was discarded but they could potentially present other pathologies and some signs in their CXRs. The aim of this experiment was to assess the FPR of our solution when predicting on non-TB-compatible children CXRs. The data used in this experiment comprised 265 non-TB-compatible CXRS of participants aged between 0 and 3 years, and both the all-age and YC models were evaluated.

Both models performed fairly well on this independent set, with the all-age model achieving an accuracy of 0.864 and the YC model achieving an accuracy of 0.951, outperforming the all-age model. This difference in performance may be influenced by the age distributions of the training data. Metrics such as F1 or AUC were not computed in this experiment as the independent cohort included only one class (non-TB-compatible CXRs).

Discussion

In this study, we have proposed pTBLightNet, a deep-learning framework aimed at pediatric TB screening. The models in this framework were trained using a CXR TB compatibility reference standard, incorporating both TB class definitions and expert readers’ assessments, to develop a system capable of identifying TB-compatible findings in CXRs while remaining consistent with established standardized clinical procedures14. The use of this reference standard avoids training the models with TB-positive patients without radiological evidence (e.g., confirmed TB patients without a TB-compatible CXR), as it could lead to potential biases.

We conducted multiple experiments using both all-age models, trained on all age groups, and age-specific models. Age-specific models appear to be more suitable for testing within their respective population groups; for instance, cases involving children aged < 5 years should be tested using the YC model, making these models unsuitable for cross-testing across different age groups. However, a limitation is the need for more data to develop robust age-specific models, as the training data for the age-specific population were significantly reduced when this was considered. Nonetheless, the performance of these models remained competitive compared to those trained with larger, but more age-heterogeneous, datasets. Notably, the OC model outperformed the all-age model trained on all ages despite using 39.6% less training data, whilst the model trained on YC data demonstrated better generalizability when cross-tested on other age groups compared to the OC model.

We also compared different training strategies, both pre-training on adult CXR data and training from scratch. Pre-training on adult data enabled for better performance, addressing data scarcity issues and providing models with greater robustness.

Additionally, our study uniquely incorporates both frontal and lateral chest X-rays. Lateral views are rarely used in AI development pipelines, despite evidence suggesting their added diagnostic value48. For instance, our experiments show that including lateral CXRs improved diagnostic performance, particularly in children under 5 years of age. This likely reflects the additional anatomical information provided by the lateral view, which can help compensate for the nonspecific radiological signs often seen in younger children. Recent reviews also highlight the scarce inclusion of lateral projections in AI research21,49, further emphasizing the novelty of our approach.

We also examined CXR image quality across cohorts, as this may impact model performance. While suboptimal image quality is an inherent limitation of pediatric radiology in general50,51, previous studies have indicated that only 60% of pediatric CXRs were classified as moderate to good quality52. In our case, among the internal data (training and testing), 10.3% (N = 47) of AP/PA and 16.4% (N = 61) of LAT CXRs were reported as poor quality, while in the external low-endemic cohort, poor quality was noted in 4.7% of AP/PA and 12.1% of LAT CXRs. Further details are included in the Supplementary Note 5. Additionally, we explored HIV infection status and malnutrition variables among the cohorts used in this study. Specifically, 3.5% of the training cases had HIV co-infection, compared to 4.4% in the internal testing set. Similarly, 20.4% of the training cases and 29.4% of the internal testing cases exhibited malnutrition. Further details are included in the Supplementary Note 6. These findings underscore the variability in clinical and image characteristics across settings and support the importance of considering heterogeneous data (i.e., from different sources and geographic locations) during model development and evaluation. Further analysis of these characteristics across diverse clinical settings is needed to better understand their impact on TB detection performance.

According to related work in the field, Palmer et al.39 reported a pediatric TB detection AUC of 0.58 when performing zero-shot pediatric TB classification using CAD4TB, using a consensus reading as TB reference standard. They also reported higher AUC scores of 0.72 with the same software and reference standard when fine-tuning their models on pediatric data from the same distribution, i.e., internal data, as the test set on which those metrics were reported. When considering case definitions as the reference standard, they reported AUC scores of 0.67 and 0.59, with and without fine-tuning, respectively. On the other hand, when considering microbiological confirmation as the reference standard, they reported AUC scores of 0.78 and 0.68, with and without fine-tuning, respectively. Similarly, Edem et al.41 evaluated CAD4TBv7 for pediatric TB detection, reporting an AUC of 0.70 against a microbiological reference standard, with an overall sensitivity (SN) of 0.19 and specificity (SP) of 0.99. Notably, sensitivity dropped to just 5% in children under 5 years of age, underscoring the system’s limited performance in younger children despite its high specificity. Together, these studies underscore the promise of deep learning and CAD systems in supporting pediatric TB detection, while also highlighting the increased task complexity of pediatric TB detection and, therefore, the pressing need for further research to enhance sensitivity, particularly in young children, ensure consistent specificity, and improve generalizability across age groups, imaging conditions, and diverse manifestations of the disease.

In our solution, we report AUC scores of around 0.90 when fine-tuning and testing on datasets of the same distribution, and AUC scores of 0.68 when testing our proposal on external datasets, also exploring the inclusion of LAT CXR views. These numbers were obtained using CXR TB compatibility as a reference standard. When using microbiological confirmation, we obtain an AUC score of around 0.80, reaching a sensitivity of 0.77 and a specificity of 0.63, when testing on an independent internal test set, and an AUC of 0.68 when testing on external cohorts. We also used expert readers’ criteria as a sole reference standard, getting AUC scores of 0.90 on the internal test cases. When using the case definition as a reference standard, our model is able to achieve an AUC score of 0.80 on the internal test cases and 0.62 when testing on the external set. In our case, we use data from diverse sources, combining data from both high TB endemic and low TB endemic area cohorts, and results against a microbiological reference standard tend to align to the WHO’s target product profile (TPP) on adult TB screening tests (0.90 SN, 0.70 SP).

One of the strengths of our study is the use of a flexible pipeline that could be configured to fuse the information from both LAT and AP/PA views, which enhances the overall diagnostic accuracy. We also analyzed explainability maps from both views to assess whether the model’s predictions were grounded in clinically meaningful radiographic features and showed that our approach consistently focused on regions relevant to TB-related findings, supporting the plausibility of its decision-making process. Although the benefit for clinicians was not directly assessed in our study, previous studies32 have shown that AI outputs can improve clinician diagnostic accuracy. Further research to assess the clinical impact is guaranteed as part of our future work.

However, there are also limitations that should be acknowledged. Although efforts have been put into validating our proposal on external data, the retrospective nature of the study and the reliance on data-limited cohorts (namely, cohorts 1, 2, and 3) may limit the generalizability of our findings. Therefore, future studies considering larger cohorts and data should aim to validate our results in larger, more diverse populations. In particular, further prospective validation in primary care settings with a broad disease spectrum is needed to better assess real-world performance. We plan to address this in future work by including more representative and prospectively collected presumed, positive and negative patients. Further research is needed to understand potential effects on performance due to data variability concerning acquisition parameters, equipment, and recruitment criteria. Performance differences observed between high- and low-endemic settings may be partly explained by differences in population characteristics, cohort recruitment and imaging protocols. More specifically, the external cohort included non-TB patients that were symptomatic participants (e.g., prolonged cough and fever) from the emergency room, and therefore, lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) may be present in some of these patients, which may have introduced greater diagnostic complexity. The additional AUC gain from lateral views in this cohort may reflect their utility in capturing TB-compatible findings potentially present throughout the cohort, including among non-TB patients with overlapping presentations such as LRTIs. Also, although this tool has been developed and validated specifically for pediatric pulmonary TB, and its performance in extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) remains unknown, it is important to consider that a large proportion of children with EPTB also present concomitant pulmonary involvement. In a previous study from the pTBred cohort53, 72.1% of children diagnosed with EPTB had associated pulmonary TB. Therefore, despite EPTB falling outside the direct scope of this study, it may still be useful in supporting diagnosis in many EPTB cases with pulmonary manifestations. Nonetheless, dedicated tools will be needed to assess isolated forms of EPTB, which remain a diagnostic challenge in pediatric TB.

Finally, to our knowledge, we are the first to describe a method for CXR-based pediatric TB detection that employs and assesses LAT CXRs and their impact on CXR TB compatibility detection performance, and we are also the first to explore how age-specific models performed when comparing to all-age models trained on all ages, in a pediatric TB context.

Future directions include identifying specific TB-compatible radiological findings in CXRs using multi-class classification approaches, which may enhance screening in pediatric patients where clinical and radiological features are often nonspecific. Differentiating TB from non-TB pneumonia or other LRTIs also represents a valuable area for further research. In addition, future work will focus on developing foundation models for diverse tasks, leveraging rich learning representations from large CXR datasets via self-supervised learning, which is especially beneficial in data-scarce fields like pediatrics54–56. These models have the potential of being incorporated in clinical practice through the already available standardized clinical algorithms14. Additionally, integrating AI-based models into telemedicine platforms could enable remote screening and diagnosis, improving TB care in resource-limited settings57.

In conclusion, our study underscores the use of deep learning approaches leveraging multiple CXR views in pediatric TB detection. This approach aims at bridging the gap in healthcare accessibility, ensuring that children in remote or resource-limited settings receive timely and accurate TB screenings and interventions.

Methods

Datasets

In this work, we employed multiple CXR datasets, both pediatric and non-pediatric (i.e., predominantly non-pediatric). For pre-training, we used a non-pediatric large-scale database of 367,924 CXR images, 335,352 frontal (AP/PA) and 32,572 lateral (LAT), comprising multiple public CXR datasets (N = 114,173). CheXpert58 and ChestXray-1459 are large public datasets labeled for the presence of 14 common thoracic pathologies, such as pneumonia, atelectasis, consolidation, pneumothorax, edema, pleural effusion or cardiomegaly, among others. COVIDx CXR-360 is another dataset of CXR images for supporting COVID-19 computer vision research, entailing the most extensive and diverse COVID-19 CXR dataset in open access form. Additionally, the Shenzhen Hospital X-ray Set61, Belarus62, and Montgomery County X-ray Set61 datasets are intended for TB research. Only a sample of images from Belarus was obtained from a Github repository (https://github.com/frapa/tbcnn).

For fine-tuning and training from scratch, we collected data from two pediatric TB cohorts, which were retrospectively categorized using updated international standards43 as confirmed TB, unconfirmed TB or unlikely TB:

Cohort 1 was collected from the Manhiça Health Research Center (CISM), Mozambique, as part of the ITACA study7,18. Children under three years with presumed TB and those in close contact with a sputum smear-positive TB patient were evaluated using international consensus definitions63. Recruiting was performed at different health units and patients were referred to CISM for diagnosis confirmation and follow-up. All the samples of patients were processed at CISM to ensure the required biosafety measures.Cohort 2 was collected from the multidisciplinary collaborative group Spanish Pediatric TB Research Network (pTBred), which included children and adolescents ≤18 years diagnosed with TB. This cohort also included TB-infected individuals (with no evidence of clinically manifest TB disease) and unlikely TB patients, including control patients—randomly selected children, matched for age and sex with the other groups within this cohort, who underwent CXR for non-infectious reasons. All data were collected from 31 centers across Spain. Standard practice in Spain for patients with presumed TB is referral, sample acquisition, and follow-up at tertiary hospitals with infectious disease and microbiology departments. In the case of pediatric TB, diagnosis and treatment is provided in referral hospitals with dedicated pediatricians. In this cohort, patients were referred from primary care and emergency care and all the diagnostic tests and confirmations were done at tertiary hospitals to ensure proper management and biosafety.

Reference standard

During training from scratch or fine-tuning, as well as evaluation, four pediatric TB cohorts were used, which underwent an evaluation performed by three experienced TB readers on a telemedicine platform. Reference TB standards were based on the clinical information and case definition as well as the radiological reading of three experienced TB readers and their corresponding TB-suggestive indication, following Gómez-Valverde et al.57 and Hernanz-Lobo et al.45. The CXR TB compatibility reference standard was defined as follows: TB-confirmed and TB-unconfirmed CXR cases with at least one positive reading were considered TB-compatible; in contrast, TB infection (TBI; with no evidence of clinically manifest TB disease) and unlikely TB patients (according to international consensus definitions43: patients where bacteriological confirmation is not obtained and the criteria for "unconfirmed tuberculosis" are not met) were considered non-TB-compatible. Further details by cohort are provided in Table 1. With this approach, we avoid training the model with positive patients lacking radiological evidence, and we do not introduce these cases as negative examples in the training, as they are not. Hence, our solution is able to detect TB-compatible CXRs with the potential of being incorporated in clinical practice through the already available standardized clinical algorithms14. During model pre-training, multiple large-scale publicly available multi-disease adult datasets were used, where all cases which presented an annotated lung disease were considered positive or diseased; else, negative or control.

Additionally, we used for evaluation some other reference standards, based on microbiological confirmation, expert reading (majority and minority voting), and TB case definition.

Deep learning framework

The proposed solution (see Fig. 1-B) consists of two main branches, one dedicated for the frontal (AP/PA) view, and another one for the LAT view. For each of the branches, a dedicated preprocessing and automatic feature extraction is implemented. This automatic feature extraction process is specific to pediatric TB CXR findings in the lung and mediastinal regions. Subsequently, these features are concatenated and fed into an MLP-based network that enables the fusion of information coming from both branches and outputs a TB prediction.

The first step of the pipeline consists of a preprocessing block in which we automatically crop the input image using an efficient YOLO model (YOLOv5s version, 7.2M parameters, 14 MB of size) trained on pediatric images, so that we are able to mask out undesired parts of the image and only consider our region of interest (ROI), i.e., lungs and mediastinal regions. More details on this model are included in Capellan-Martin et al.64. Secondly, we apply Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to the cropped image, a preprocessing image enhancing technique that has shown to improve TB detection in CXRs29,65,66. Some results and examples of this step on both TB-positive and TB-negative patients are included in the Supplementary Note 3 (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5).

The second step of the pipeline, a multi-view automatic feature extraction block, is divided in two branches, one for each view (AP/PA and LAT). For each branch, we trained a lightweight and efficient ResNet specifically tailored to pediatric TB findings in CXRs. It is an improved version of LightTBNet34, trained on pediatric data. The main improvements include adding normalization layers at the end of each convolutional block and an additional convolutional layer in each residual component, enhancing the combination after the concatenation of both pathways in the residual connection. The network depth remains unchanged. These modifications aim to improve generalizability and performance on out-of-domain data, such as pediatric data, while preserving computational efficiency. The full architecture is detailed in Fig. 1-B. The features extracted from each branch are linearly projected into a lower-dimensional vector and combined in an MLP-based network, which assigns importance to each feature. Finally, predictions are processed through a softmax layer, outputting a probability p, where p ∈ [0, 1], indicating the likelihood that the case is TB-compatible. Please, refer to Supplementary Note 4 (Supplementary Fig. 6) for further details on this process.

Training strategy

The proposed solution underwent a two-step training process: a pre-training phase on adult CXR data (pediatric representation was almost insignificant) and a fine-tuning step on pediatric data.

A first pre-training step was conducted on a large-scale adult CXR multi-disease database to enable our network to learn general patterns and representations from adult CXR data coming from different sources, which could help improve the performance of our method. All those models that underwent this pre-training phase were labeled as (P). In this first step, multiple public CXR datasets were used: CheXpert, ChestX-ray14, COVIDx CXR-3, Montgomery, Shenzhen and Belarus (see Datasets and Table 5 for further information about these datasets), labeling images as positive if any radiologic finding was present on the CXR (CheXpert, ChestX-ray14 and COVIDx CXR-3 datasets), or if that patient was classified as TB-positive (Shenzhen, Belarus and Montgomery datasets). All other cases were labeled as negative. During this initial pre-training phase, the data were randomly split into training and validation sets. Five percent of the data were reserved for validation, with no patient overlap, and stratifying by cohort, age, sex, CXR view and target label. The remaining 95% was used for training. Both AP/PA and LAT images were considered for this pre-training. LAT images were only available from the CheXpert and Belarus datasets, and represented 8.83% of the total dataset (14.5% of CheXpert and 0.99% of Belarus). However, positive LAT images (i.e., pathological) represented less than 0.01% of the total dataset (less than 0.01% of CheXpert, 0.99% of Belarus and 0.07% of all LAT images). As the representation of pathological LAT images was extremely low, leading to an extremely unbalanced training dataset, pre-training a LAT model with LAT images only was therefore pointless. Hence, for LAT model pre-training, both AP/PA and LAT images were used. On the other hand, for AP/PA model pre-training, only AP/PA images were used. Both pre-trainings were performed for 200 epochs. The weights which achieved the highest validation AUC were the ones used for further fine-tuning.

Secondly, a fine-tuning step was conducted to tackle pediatric TB classification. Those models that did not undergo the first pre-training step were instead trained from scratch, labeled as (S), i.e., with a random initialization of the parameters. We conducted experiments to determine whether freezing certain residual blocks of the model would result in improved performance. However, we found that not freezing any blocks was the better performing strategy. In this step, the datasets A (cohort 1, CISM) and B (cohort 2, pTBred) were used, and the data were split into training, validation, and testing sets, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The internal test subset (≈27%) had no patient overlap with the training data. The remaining training data (≈73%) were further split using a 5-fold cross-validation approach, employed for hyperparameter tuning and model selection. These splits were stratified taking into account variables such as TB type, sex, age, cohort, HIV and malnutrition. The reference TB standard used for these data was based on CXR TB compatibility (see Reference Standard).

We also performed 5-fold cross-validation. On each fold, we evaluated performance on the validation subset and chose the best model checkpoint based on the highest validation area under the curve (AUC) score.

During the testing of the models, we used the best algorithm trained on each fold and averaged their predictions across an ensemble of the five models (see Fig. 1). Previous experiments showed better performance when ensembling the five models extracted from each fold, ensuring a more robust final score.

To implement the networks, we used the PyTorch framework (v. 1.12) and kept track of the experiments using Weights and Biases (wandb.ai). For the experiments, we used a workstation with two GPUs: 1 × NVIDIA A30 24 GB and 1 × NVIDIA RTX A6000 48 GB, 256 GB RAM and 2 × Intel Xeon Silver 4216 @ 2.1 GHz CPUs. A cross-validation strategy (k = 5) was followed for all the experiments. The models obtained from all the folds were used for inference by ensembling the scores obtained from each of them. With this strategy, we are able to provide a more robust final prediction.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were chosen based on the available number of human data. Among the public data, no images were excluded from the analyses. Among the non-public data, all images meeting the inclusion criteria (as described in Fig. 2, Results, and Training strategy) were included in the evaluation, while studies were excluded if they did not meet the criteria described. Data used during pre-training were randomly split into training and validation. Non-public data were split as described in Fig. 2, stratified by variables such as TB type, sex, age, cohort, HIV co-infection, and malnutrition. Qualitative analysis and interpretation of the Grad-CAMs (Fig. 5) were conducted by an experienced radiologist blinded to the case definition and all clinical information associated to the participant. Performance of the proposed method was evaluated using standard metrics (ACC, F1 and AUC), with 95% confidence intervals calculated using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) for ACC and F1, and using DeLong’s method for AUC.
