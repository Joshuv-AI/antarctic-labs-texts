Multidrug-resistant bacterial infections in a tertiary hospitals: culture positivity, pathogen spectrum, and antibiotic susceptibility
Authors: Tahsin Salam, Tufael, Md Abu Bakar Siddique, Kaniz Fatima Bari, Mohd Hasan Mujahid, Asim Debnath, Nabil Deb Nath, Md Sefaut Ullah, Vijay Jagdish Upadhye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant health problem, especially in low- and middle-income countries. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and trends of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and multidrug resistance (MDR) in bacterial infections at a tertiary-care hospital in Dhaka, Bangladesh, and the prevalent pathogens and their resistance profiles.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on clinical specimens from patients admitted to tertiary-care hospitals, processed at Ibn Sina Diagnostic and Imaging Center in Dhaka, Bangladesh. A total of 3,996 clinical specimens were sampled by patients, with inclusion and exclusion criteria. The samples were blood, urine and wound swabs. Data regarding culture positivity, pathogen distribution and antimicrobial susceptibility were summarized using descriptive statistics.ResultOut of the 3,996 specimens, 72.5% were positive in terms of significant bacterial growth. The gram-negative bacteria were the dominant ones, with Escherichia coli (25.4%) and Klebsiella spp. (20.4%). The most common pathogens are Pseudomonas aeruginosa (15.8%), Acinetobacter spp. (11.9%), Staphylococcus aureus (10%), and Coagulase-negative Staphylococci (8.1%) were the most common gram-positive. In E. coli, the resistance was significantly high with a 42.5% ciprofloxacin resistance and a 47.0% ceftriaxone resistance. MDR was identified in 53.0% of E. coli isolates, and similar MDR patterns were observed in Klebsiella spp. and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.ConclusionThe study shows that there is high-AMR, especially amongst gram-negative pathogens, at a tertiary-care hospital. There is an urgent need to enhance antibiotic stewardship and infection control to deal with the increasing rate of MDR bacterial infections.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Multidrug-resistant, Bacterial infections, Pathogen spectrum, Antibiotic susceptibility

Background

The phenomena of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections have become a significant issue in tertiary-care environments, where the extensive use of antibiotics, prolong hospital stays, and numerous invasive procedures contribute to the selection and transmission of resistant pathogens. This is especially alarming due to the context of low- and middle-income countries, such as Bangladesh, where the lack of diagnostic capacity and the poor antimicrobial stewardship can delay the right treatment and exacerbate the clinical outcomes. Consequently, the purpose of this study was to identify culture positivity, define the range of bacterial pathogens, and evaluate the trends of antimicrobial susceptibility and multidrug resistance of clinical isolates in a tertiary-care facility [1–3].

Healthcare facilities with limited resources experience the most severe consequences of AMR, as it leads to increased mortality, higher medical costs, extended hospital stays, and limited treatment options [4, 5]. Bangladesh operates under similar conditions to other South Asian nations, contributing to its extreme AMR burden due to improper antibiotic consumption, unregulated drug sales, and weak diagnostic management systems [6]. Research conducted in this area indicates that more than 40% of hospital patients obtain antibiotics through empirical treatment methods, which lack microbiological confirmation, thus accelerating antibiotic resistance development [7]. These healthcare facilities manage many critically ill patients who have multiple health conditions, previous antibiotic treatments, and prolonged hospitalizations, making them particularly vulnerable to resistant bacterial infections [8, 9].

Hospitals can be strategic in providing real-time data on the spread and resistance to antibiotics, which is vitally crucial for informing clinical management, especially in treating infections such as urinary tract infections, wound infections, bloodstream infections, and respiratory illnesses [10]. Despite the availability of extensive clinical data, there remains a significant gap in understanding the local prevalence and resistance patterns of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) in tertiary-care units, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [11]. While some global trends in AMR have been reported, no local-level data from Bangladesh have been published that would inform empirical therapy or help develop effective antimicrobial stewardship strategies.

This gap will be addressed in the current report by examining the specific patterns and distribution of pathogens within a tertiary-care hospitals, providing valuable treatment outcomes and infection prevention systems across the region [12]. Surveillance studies have shown that multidrug-resistant enterobacterales have a major impact on patient outcome in hospitals in Bangladesh, as observed in other parts of the world, such as Tanzania. However, the high degree of the influence of multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales in the South Asian context, which this study aims to explore, requires more Bangladesh-specific data for validation [13, 14].

In Bangladeshi hospitals, Enterobacterales with multidrug resistance accounted for 50% to 60% of total patient admissions in these facilities [6]. Pseudomonas and Acinetobacter bacteria from non-fermenting species have developed resistance against most antibiotics commonly used in clinical practice [15]. This study was designed to thoroughly assess the culture positivity rate, identify the full spectrum of bacterial pathogens, and evaluate their antimicrobial susceptibility, including multidrug-resistance profiles, across a wide range of clinical samples processed at a tertiary-care hospitals. This comprehensive analysis aims to provide valuable insights into pathogen distribution and resistance patterns in the hospital setting.

The present work contributes useful information on the patterns of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in medical practice by examining the rates of the most prevalent bacterial pathogens and their resistance pattern. These findings are relevant to the development of specific antibiotic stewardship initiatives and the possibility to trace changes in resistance trends in healthcare facilities. Data-driven strategies based on research findings can ultimately help protect antibiotics from resistance, leading to better patient outcomes and more effective medical care in institutions with large patient populations.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional, laboratory-based study conducted in Ibn Sina Diagnostic and Imaging Center in Dhaka, Bangladesh. The study was carried out over a period from February 2023 to August 2025. During this period, clinical specimens from patients admitted to the different tertiary-care hospitals for routine diagnostic testing were analyzed in the laboratory at Ibn Sina Diagnostic and Imaging Center in Dhaka, Bangladesh. These patients were already receiving treatment for various health conditions at the tertiary-care hospitals, and their specimens were collected as a part of their standard diagnostic procedures. No new specimens were specifically collected for this research; all specimens were obtained as part of the patients’ routine medical care.

Sample collection and processing

The microbiology laboratory handled clinical specimens from 3,996 patients, including urine, wound swabs, blood, sputum, stool, and cerebrospinal fluid, all of which were received as part of routine diagnostic testing. No new samples were specifically taken for research. All specimens were processed according to standard aseptic practices widely followed in Bangladesh. They were promptly collected, labeled, and transported to the laboratory to preserve their diagnostic value. Patients admitted to the tertiary-care hospital with various complications and undergoing routine diagnostic testing between February 2023 and August 2025 were included in the study. Only clinical specimens collected as part of their regular care, focusing on bacterial pathogens and their resistance patterns, were used. Patients with incomplete clinical records or those who did not undergo diagnostic testing, as well as specimens collected for non-diagnostic purposes, were excluded from the study.

In the laboratory, the appropriate media were used to grow a wide variety of pathogens, including blood agar, chocolate agar and nutrient agar. The choice of these media was to isolate gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria in order to make certain that both common pathogens causing hospital-associated infections would be identified. Inoculated plates were incubated at 35–37 °C for 18 to 24 h.

The growth of the bacteria was estimated by registering the colony properties such as lactose fermentation, pigmentation, and hemolysis phenotypes. This was followed by Gram staining as a preliminary examination towards bacterial differentiation. These steps are common in the laboratory practice of hospitals in Bangladesh to achieve uniform and error-free sample processing during the research.

Identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Standard microbiological techniques, commonly used in Bangladesh, were employed to identify bacteria. Gram staining was initially performed, followed by standard biochemical tests such as Catalase, Coagulase, Oxidase, Indole, Citrate, Urease, and TSI. Antimicrobial susceptibility was tested using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller-Hinton agar, following CLSI guidelines. A total of ten antibiotics were tested, including Ciprofloxacin (CIP), Amoxicillin (AMX), Gentamicin (GEN), Ceftriaxone (CRO), Azithromycin (AZM), Meropenem (MEM), Cefotaxime (CTX), Ceftazidime (CAZ), Piperacillin (PIP), and Cefoxitin (FOX). The data analysis, presented in Figures and tables, shows resistance trends, and statistical analysis was performed for each antibiotic. Susceptibility (S), Intermediate (I), and Resistance (R) were classified based on CLSI M100-S29 standards [16].

Moreover, multidrug resistance (MDR) was considered as the resistance to at least three sets of antibiotics and was considered a key outcome of this study. The patterns of antibiotic resistance of the isolated bacteria formed the MDR profile. Quality control (QC) with the use of the standard QC strains of Escherichia coli, ATCC 25,922, and Staphylococcus aureus, ATCC 25,923, was carried out to guarantee accuracy of the susceptibility testing as per the CLSI guidelines. Each testing run contained such QC strains to confirm the performance of the antimicrobial disks and provide consistent, reliable results.

Multidrug Resistance (MDR) classification

In this study, MDR (Multidrug Resistance) is defined as resistance to at least three different classes of antibiotics (Table 1). The antibiotics tested for each bacterial species were selected based on their relevance and representation of commonly used antibiotic classes. For example, E. coli and Klebsiella spp. were tested with β-lactams, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones, whereas Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter spp. were tested with cephalosporins, penicillin, and carbapenems, as these antibiotics are more effective against these pathogens due to their resistance patterns.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics and cross-tabulation were used to compare demographic variables with specimen types, culture outcomes, and antimicrobial resistance. Frequency distributions determined the prevalence of MDR pathogens. Resistance rates for each bacterial species were compared using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Data are shown as mean ± SD from three independent replicates (n = 3); p ≤ 0.05 was considered significant, and “ns” indicates non-significant differences.

Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into bacterial infections and antibiotic resistance within a major Bangladeshi hospital, analyzing a total of 3,996 clinical specimens, of which 2,894 were culture-positive, revealing a high prevalence of bacterial infections (72.5%). The findings emphasize the widespread occurrence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, particularly among Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The findings reflect global concerns about the rising rates of antibiotic resistance and the increasing challenge of managing hospital-acquired infections that are resistant to first-line antibiotics. Given the increasing global burden of antibiotic resistance, the results of this study provide important information on local patterns that can help guide treatment strategies.

The study found that Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli (25.4%) and Klebsiella spp. (20.4%) were the most prevalent pathogens. The highest infection rates were seen in middle-aged adults (40–59 years), accounting for 35.6% of the infections, but the table only shows demographic distribution, not actual infection rates. The study also highlighted the spread of MDR pathogens, with E. coli showing 42.5% resistance to ciprofloxacin and 47% to cefotaxime, which reflects growing concerns about antimicrobial resistance in hospitals. While lower infection rates in children were observed, this cannot be attributed to viral infections or stronger immunity, as these factors were not measured in the study [17, 18].

While infections in elderly individuals (≥ 70 years) and children under five years were less frequent, these groups still accounted for 10.4% and 2.8% of infections, respectively. The elderly population, particularly those with chronic diseases or compromised immune systems, is known to be more susceptible to severe infections. Previous studies have shown that aging is associated with a gradual decline in immune function, referred to as immunosenescence, which may increase vulnerability to infections [19–21]. In contrast, children under five years exhibited a lower proportion of infections in our study. The nature of these infections (viral or bacterial), the immune status of the children, and their exposure to healthcare settings were not assessed. Therefore, the observed lower infection rate should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect differences in sampling practices, hospital admission patterns, or other unmeasured factors. No definitive conclusions regarding susceptibility can be drawn from this observation.

A proportion of the specimens did not yield bacterial growth under the culture conditions used in this study. The study did not investigate potential contributing factors, including prior antibiotic use or non-bacterial infections, and therefore no conclusions can be drawn about the underlying causes. These observations underscore the limitations of relying solely on culture-based diagnostic methods and suggest that complementary diagnostic approaches may be valuable for detecting pathogens that are not readily culturable. Reporting these findings provides a complete overview of the laboratory results while maintaining an evidence-based and neutral perspective [22–26].

One of the most concerning findings in this study was the high resistance of E. coli to commonly prescribed antibiotics, including ciprofloxacin, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, meropenem, and gentamicin. The 42.5% resistance to ciprofloxacin and 37.9% to ceftriaxone reflect the widespread use of these antibiotics in both hospital and community settings. Similarly, Klebsiella spp., a major pathogen in hospital-acquired infections, showed 45.3% resistance to ciprofloxacin, 39.7% to ceftriaxone, and 20.9% to meropenem. The emergence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing strains of Klebsiella spp. presents a significant challenge in the treatment of hospital-acquired infections, as these bacteria can hydrolyze third-generation cephalosporins and other beta-lactam antibiotics [27–30].

In addition to the Gram-negative pathogens, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Acinetobacter spp. exhibited high resistance rates, with Pseudomonas aeruginosa showing 63.4% resistance to ceftazidime and 51.3% resistance to piperacillin-tazobactam. Similarly, Acinetobacter spp. demonstrated 80.6% resistance to meropenem, which is concerning given the critical role of carbapenem antibiotics in treating multidrug-resistant pathogens. These organisms are known to form biofilms and possess efflux pumps, which contribute to their resistance to antibiotics [31–34]. In hospital settings, these pathogens often infect patients with prolonged hospital stays or those using invasive medical devices, making treatment more complex and requiring urgent attention to infection control measures.

The study results indicated that there were significant variations in antibiotic resistance pattern of different bacterial isolates as presented in Fig. 2(a-e). Resistance was found in all examined antibiotics confirming that several bacterial species are less susceptible to the most conventional drugs. Klebsiella spp. was consistently resistant to multiple important unrelated antibiotics. Treatment options may be restricted as there was similar resistance to all the antibiotic classes for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. For Acinetobacter spp., resistance patterns indicated a high adaptive potential, hindering treatment. The spread of MRSA, even with regard to front-line antibiotics, is highlighted here. These findings all illustrate the need for strong antimicrobial stewardship and infection control practices to help reduce the spread of resistant pathogens [35–37].

This study has several limitations as a cross-sectional study; it cannot establish causal relationships or assess changes in resistance patterns over time. Furthermore, the study focused on a limited set of pathogens, and the findings may not be representative of all infections in the population. Additionally, the study did not account for potential environmental or socioeconomic factors that might influence antibiotic resistance patterns. Future research should include a broader range of pathogens and investigate the role of external factors, such as socioeconomic status and geographic location, in shaping resistance profiles.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), with Gram-negative pathogens showing significant resistance rates. The prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens and the increasing resistance to commonly used antibiotics highlight the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship and enhanced infection control practices. While the development of new antibiotics may play a role, further research is needed to identify targeted therapies for resistant infections. Ongoing surveillance of antimicrobial resistance patterns is crucial to inform treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes. This study contributes to understanding local resistance trends and emphasizes the importance of continued monitoring to address the growing threat of AMR.
