Muscle spindles provide flexible sensory feedback for movement sequences
Authors: William P Olson, Varun B Chokshi, Jeong Jun Kim, Noah J Cowan, Daniel H O’Connor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYSensory feedback is essential for motor performance and must adapt to task demands. Muscle spindle afferents (MSAs) are a major primary source of feedback about movement. Their responses are readily modulated online by fusimotor neurons and, in the unique case of jaw MSAs whose cell bodies lie within the central nervous system, by central synaptic inputs. They are, therefore, a powerful site for implementing flexible sensorimotor control. We record from MSAs innervating the jaw musculature of mice performing a directed lick sequence task. Jaw MSAs encode complex jaw-tongue kinematics. However, kinematic encoding alone accounts for less than half of jaw MSA spiking variability. Jaw MSA coding of kinematics changes based on sequence progression (beginning, middle, or end of the sequence or reward consumption), suggesting that jaw MSAs are flexibly tuned across the task. Dynamic modulation of feedback signals from jaw MSAs may shape sensorimotor control during complex behavior.

INTRODUCTION

Motor control is implemented through sensorimotor loops that continuously integrate sensory feedback into ongoing motor commands. The relevance of sensory feedback, however, can change based on task demands.1–3 Movements that are kinematically similar can be associated with differing responsiveness to perturbation depending on behavioral context.4 Task-based control has been demonstrated at multiple levels of the sensorimotor hierarchy, including in the short-latency spinal reflexes mediated through direct feedback of stretch sensory responses onto spinal motor circuits.5–7 Such flexible tuning of reflexes in the periphery could allow strategic control over rapid feedback responses in line with higher-order goals.

Muscle spindles, innervated by sensory muscle spindle afferents (MSAs) and by efferent fusimotor neurons, are an important source of feedback about body position and movement. MSAs show complex and variable encoding of kinematics. MSAs comprise two major neuronal subtypes (primary, or type Ia, and secondary, or type II). Under passive stretch conditions (such as in the anesthetized animal), primary MSAs are co-tuned to muscle length and velocity, while secondary MSAs predominantly encode length.8,9 MSAs report the direction of passively applied10 or actively generated11 movements. However, during active movement, MSA activity is only partially coupled to kinematics.9,12,13

MSAs are mechanosensors driven by intra-spindle tension and its derivatives.14,15 Much of the complex activity seen during active movement can be understood in terms of the impact of fusimotor (γ and β motor neuron) drive on spindle tensions via activation of the spindle intrafusal muscle fibers.14 Fusimotor neurons comprise multiple subtypes, with “static” neurons controlling MSA activity offsets and “dynamic” neurons controlling MSA movement response gains. Fusimotor neurons are co-driven with the α motor neurons that cause muscle contraction (“α-γ co-activation”).16 However, γ motor neuron activity is only partially linked to α motor neurons.12,13,17–19 MSA outputs are also further modulated via axo-axonic synaptic contacts onto their central arbors.20–22 MSAs that innervate the jaw musculature are a specialized set of MSAs with cell bodies located in the midbrain mesencephalic trigeminal nucleus (abbreviated as MEV for the “mesencephalic nucleus of the fifth (V) cranial nerve”). MEV-MSAs receive axo-somatic inputs onto their cell bodies,23–28 representing an additional source of top-down modulation.

The role of complex modulation of MSA activity has been greatly debated, but is incompletely understood.16,29 Work in the cat hindlimb has shown both tonic (among static gamma) and phasic (among dynamic gamma) forms of active fusimotor modulation during locomotion.30,31 This drive has been hypothesized to help the nervous system disambiguate external perturbations from actively generated movement,18 to sensitize MSAs to specific points of the step cycle,18 and/or to set sensory feedback responses between behavioral contexts.17 Work in humans has shown that MSAs are modulated based on higher-order task goals,32,33 including during the preparation of upcoming movements19 (though other groups did not see these effects34–36). Recent models32 have generally proposed that MSAs may provide flexible movement representations that can adapt to task requirements. There are currently relatively few MSA recording datasets available from behaving subjects. Such datasets are greatly needed to resolve ongoing debates regarding MSA function during behavior.

Here, we sought to understand if MSAs may be flexibly modulated during the performance of a complex behavior. To this end, we studied the activity of jaw-innervating MEV-MSAs during the execution of flexible, learned orofacial motor sequences. MEV-MSAs are a powerful system for studying MSAs during behavior due to their recording tractability, and past work has shown complex encoding of jaw movement during licking and chewing.28,37–40 We used a recently developed task in which head-fixed mice perform complex sequences of directed licks to receive a water reward.41 Cortical recording and manipulation during this task reveal sensorimotor control operating on both single-lick and across-sequence timescales41 (i.e., “hierarchical” control42).

We asked (1) how orofacial kinematics are encoded in MEV-MSAs during these sequences, (2) what components of MEV-MSA activity are decoupled from orofacial kinematics, indicating top-down modulation of MEV-MSA activity, and (3) whether this modulation may be associated with higher-order task signals previously shown to be important for sequence performance. We found complex and diverse task-related responses from MEV-MSAs. A component of the MEV-MSA activity could be explained by jaw and tongue kinematics. However, much of the activity was instead linked to sequence progression and/or the pre-/post-reward context of licks. Our work suggests that MEV-MSA feedback representations are flexibly modulated during sequence performance to shape sensorimotor control in line with changing behavioral goals.

RESULTS

We trained head-fixed mice to perform the directed lick sequence task, as described previously.41 A horizontal water port tube was positioned in front of the mouse’s mouth. After an auditory cue, each mouse licked the end of the tube; an actuator moved the tube to the next of the seven positions in an arc from one side of the mouth to the other (Figure 1A and Video S1). Sequences started on either the left or the right and progressed to the opposite side, and each trial terminated with the delivery of a water reward through the port and a series of consummatory licks. The next trial started with a sequence in the opposite direction after a random intertrial interval. We tracked jaw and tongue kinematics from a high-speed (400 Hz) video of the animal’s mouth, extracting three jaw parameters (mandible tip positions: dorsoventral z, mediolateral x, and anteroposterior y), three tongue parameters (tongue length L, angle from the midline θ, and angle from the vertical φ), and the associated velocities (first derivatives: z˙, x˙, y˙, L˙, θ˙, and φ˙) (Figure 1B). We defined two additional task variables: lick cycle phase, the relative position of the jaw within a single lick cycle, and “progress,” the fraction of the trial that has been completed (relative to water delivery; Figures 1D and 1E; STAR Methods). The task features licks across orofacial kinematic space in two sequence directions as well as post-reward “drinking” licks at the end of each trial. The structure of the task, therefore, features sequence-progress linked behavior events (e.g., start, end, and reward) in parallel with the licking kinematics (e.g., left-to-right vs. right-to-left).

We isolated single units from chronic 32-channel tetrode recordings in MEV and identified units significantly modulated by task movement (n = 104 unique units from seven animals; Figure S1; STAR Methods). We probed the cheek musculature under isoflurane anesthesia and identified response fields on the cheek for some units (n = 20 units; Figures S2A–S2L). The jaw muscles and bony structures are irregularly shaped. Most muscles lack tendons and instead attach to the complex structures of the skull and mandible with long aponeuroses.43 We found five characteristic response fields. Four of these fields were associated with silent baseline activity and could be localized to specific regions of the jaw musculature43: temporalis, posterior masseter, anterior masseter, and ventral masseter-silent baseline. A fifth field comprised units showing tonic activity under anesthesia, with silencing/activating response fields localized to the ventral masseter (ventral masseter-tonic baseline). This type may represent units innervating the internal pterygoid, which sits inside the mandible and elevates the jaw.44 The slight jaw depression caused by anesthesia may cause tonic stretch of this muscle, explaining the tonic activity of the MEV-MSAs. We additionally found units with response fields in the tooth/whisker pad region. These may represent low-threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRs) for the tooth or whisker pad.23,45–47

Units differed based on their spike autocorrelograms (ACGs; Figures S1D and S2M). We segregated MEV units into subclusters based on their ACGs (Figures S2M–S2O; STAR Methods). Cluster 1 units show characteristics of type Ia MSAs (higher peak firing rates, coupling to jaw velocities, and higher interspike interval variability during resting bouts8,9,48 compared to clusters 2 and 3; Figures S2P–S2R). Cluster 3 contained all tooth region-response field neurons and showed low firing rates during the task (Figures S2P–S2R). Clusters 1 and 2 were selected as MEV-MSAs (n = 83 units); cluster 1 may represent type Ia MSAs, while cluster 2 may represent type II MSAs. Cluster 3 (n = 21 units) may represent MEV-LTMRs and low-firing MEV-MSAs and were excluded from subsequent analysis.

Many MEV-MSAs show responses to passively applied jaw movements in anesthetized animals (n = 45 units; Figures 1C and S3), indicating muscle stretch responses. Other MEV-MSAs (n = 59 units) showed movement modulation in awake animals but were silent or low-firing under anesthesia (Figure S3). This may result from direct anesthesia effects on these midbrain MEV-MSAs and/or result from changes in muscle tone or top-down modulatory drive under anesthesia. Moreover, prior work has reported peak firing rates during movement for cat hindlimb MSAs of 200 Hz and for human limb MSAs of 50 Hz.49,50 Peak movement firing of MEV-MSAs in monkeys, cats, and rabbits has been reported to approach 400–500 Hz.38–40 We found firing rates approached 1,000 Hz for mouse MEV-MSAs (Figure S1E). This may reflect species differences or strong central synaptic drives onto these midbrain mouse MEV-MSAs.

MEV-MSA responses were linked to lick- and sequence-level task dynamics

We aligned kinematics and MEV-MSA spike rates across trials using lick-cycle phase (Figure 2A). MEV-MSAs were strongly modulated during task performance, with highly diverse responses across the population. Many MEV-MSAs showed strong modulation on the order of single lick events (phase tuning; Figure 2A units 1–4), while others showed weaker response modulation to individual licks (“slowly modulated” units; Figure 2A units 5–6). We segregated units on the basis of the amount of mutual information51 single-unit spiking held with lick-cycle phase (STAR Methods) and identified 54/83 phase-coupled units (Figure 2E).

The patterning of hindlimb MSA responses with respect to locomotor step cycles is determined by the muscle of innervation, MSA subtype (Ia vs. II), and fusimotor modulation.12,49 To examine MEV-MSA activity in response to the lick cycle, we aligned data on the lick-cycle phase (Figures 2B and 2C). We observed that peak firing responses with respect to phase varied across the population (Figures 2C and 2D), with units showing peak activity during the opening swing (phase of −π to 0, just prior to tongue protrusion), around peak opening (phase of 0), and during the closing swing (phase of 0 to π, just after tongue retraction). Phase-tuning responses of MEV-MSAs are generally segregated based on their peripheral response fields. For example, while temporalis units fired around maximal opening, v-tonic baseline units fired during the closing swing (Figure 2D). Both clusters 1 and 2 MEV-MSA subpopulations have phase tunings distributed across the lick cycle, with some bias for fully open-closing swing responses among cluster 2 units (Figure 2D). Phase tuning in MEV-MSAs is likely to result from a combination of muscle of innervation, subtype, and top-down modulation (see Discussion).

In addition to lick cycle phase tuning, the MEV-MSA ensembles show patterned activity across the licking task sequences. While some MEV-MSAs responded consistently throughout the motor sequences (Figure 2A, units 1 and 2), most showed dynamic modulation of their response across the sequences. This included both phase-tuned and slowly modulated units (Figure 2E). Notably, we observed responses linked to kinematic changes over the sequence (i.e., tuning to left or right licking directions) (Figure 2A, units 3 and 5) as well as responses linked to progress regardless of sequence direction (Figure 2A, units 4 and 6). Activity across the population tiled the sequences, and many units showed stronger activity in one of the two sequence directions (Figure 2E). Together, these recordings indicate diverse encoding of kinematics across the population and additionally indicate that MEV-MSAs may be driven by task dynamics that are independent from the execution of specific movements.

Jaw-tongue kinematics partially explained MEV-MSA activity

We first sought to understand how orofacial kinematics are encoded in MEV-MSAs and estimate the fraction of total MEV-MSA activity that can be explained by kinematics. MSAs fire in relation to muscle lengths and the change in length.9 The features we extracted from video data (jaw z, etc.) are unlikely to be well matched to the underlying biomechanics (e.g., muscle lengths). To identify a coordinate frame that better approximates the underlying mechanics (see limitations of the study), we used a dimensionality reduction strategy to identify a set of axes describing the main coordinated components of the observed kinematics. While jaw and tongue movement during the task was highly coordinated, it was only partially correlated (Figure S4A). We identified kinematic coordinate axes from jaw, tongue, or jaw+tongue time-series data using singular value decomposition (SVD) (Figures 3A–3C; STAR Methods). We included position and velocity terms when identifying these axes, owing to the known combined position and velocity coding of MSAs.8,9

We fitted linear encoding models that predict the activity of single MEV-MSAs using these identified kinematic axes. We projected the time-series data onto the coordinate axes, calculated the weights, and then linearly regressed smoothed (Figure S4B; STAR Methods) single-MEV-MSA spike rates onto these weights. For each neuron, we chose the top three jaw, tongue, or jaw+tongue axes based on the correlation (absolute value of Pearson correlation) with the smoothed spike rates. For a minority of units, the linear encoding models strongly predicted MEV-MSA activity (11/83 units have mean cross-validated [c.v.] R2 values > 0.5 for jaw+tongue models) (Figure 3H). However, the major components of the MEV-MSA population activity were poorly explained by the linear encoding models. Clusters 1 and 2 MEV-MSAs showed similar model performance distributions (Figure S4E).

MSA’s coding of velocity terms is known to be nonlinear,9 and linear models may be too simple to capture complex MEV-MSA kinematic encoding. To estimate the upper bound on the amount of MEV-MSA activity that can be explained based on observed kinematics, we tested more flexible (nonlinear) decision-tree-based models (Boosted trees52). We fitted decision-tree models on the coordinate axes, and additionally fitted models on features extracted via SVD of the video data (Facemap53,54) (Figures S4C and S4D). These models outperformed the linear-encoding models, but left major components of the neural activity unexplained by kinematics (28/83, 33/83, and 40/83 units with mean c.v. R2 > 0.5 for models fitted on jaw axes, jaw+tongue axes, and video features, respectively). Similar distributions were found for clusters 1 and 2 MEV-MSAs (Figure S4F).

To better understand the kinematic tuning of MEV-MSAs, we examined the axes best correlated to individual MEV-MSAs. We were able to identify examples of individual MEV-MSAs whose activity was well explained by jaw position axes (Figure 3D) or by co-tuning to jaw position and velocity axes (Figure 3E), consistent with MSA activity in prior work. Notably, cluster 1 MEV-MSAs were more likely to be co-tuned to position and velocity (Figures 3E and S2R) compared to cluster 2 MEV-MSAs (Figures 3D and S2R).

We next asked if MEV-MSAs were only tuned to jaw kinematics or may be co-tuned to jaw and tongue joint movement. We asked if any MEV-MSAs had activity better explained by coordinated jaw+tongue axes compared to jaw axes alone. 23/83 units show significant outperformance of encoders using three jaw+tongue axes compared to three jaw axes (95% CI of jaw+tongue encoder—jaw encoder c.v. R2 > 0, difference taken within each resample for 1,000 bootstrap resamples) (Figure 3I). One subset of these units showed tuning to jaw opening and tongue protrusion conformations (Figures 3F, S5A, and S5B). A second subset was tuned to jaw opening but was silent during tongue protrusions (Figures 3G, S5A, and S5B). Jaw-tongue tuning subtypes were related to peripheral response field locations, with “jaw open–tongue out” responses seen in temporalis and anterior masseter units, and “jaw open–tongue in” responses seen in posterior/ventral masseter units (Figure S5C). These responses to coordinated jaw and tongue movements may reflect complex local changes in muscle stretch and/or changes in top-down modulation of MEV-MSAs. They could also reflect localized changes in intramuscular pressure for the jaw muscles caused by protrusion of the tongue.55 Notably, while unit kinematic co-tuning overlaps with orofacial conformations during lick port contacts, it was maintained in licks with no port contact (Figures S5D and S5E). This indicates that these responses were not likely to be driven by external contact forces.

Overall, we found encoding of complex orofacial kinematics in MEV-MSAs. We found individual units with jaw kinematic encoding properties consistent with prior work defining MSA type Ia and type II responses. We additionally found a small subset of units tuned to joint jaw and tongue kinematic configurations. While a small subset of MEV-MSAs strongly encoded movement (Figure 3H), large components of the responses across the population of MEV-MSAs (roughly half of the overall population activity) were poorly explained by observed kinematics alone.

MEV-MSAs were tuned to lick order within the sequence

Our kinematics encoding models failed to account for a large component of MEV-MSA activity. Much of the remaining activity may be due to top-down modulation, which could decouple MEV-MSA activity from the kinematics. Sequence progress is a parameter that is important for task control41 and is decorrelated from jaw kinematics (Figure S6A). Based on our observation of MEV-MSA responses linked to sequence progress (Figure 2A, units 4 and 6), we asked whether sequence progress may be a kinematic-independent task parameter that drives MEV-MSA modulation. We therefore asked if these neurons may show different responses to licks that are kinematically similar but differ based on sequence order. Put another way, we looked for patterns of activity that were consistent based on lick order (i.e., first vs. last lick), regardless of lick position (left or right), indicating progress-based patterns of modulation that are independent of kinematics.

We considered licks to the two outermost port positions (L3 and R3) obtained from sequences of both directions. These represent licks under two order conditions (“first” and “last”) at both locations (Figures 4A–4C). We then sub-selected the licks that were best matched on the kinematics within each position (i.e., “L3 first” vs. “L3 last”) (Figures S6D, S6G, and S6J), selecting equal numbers from each position–order combination (n = 40 total licks). We excluded one session in which the lick kinematics could distinguish lick order (indicating insufficient matching of lick kinematics; 78/83 units are included in the subsequent analysis based on this exclusion) (Figure S6B). For each unit, we determined whether spiking could be used to classify lick position, order, or both, indicating tuning to the relevant parameter. We pooled licks by position or by order (Figure 4C) and made linear classifiers using lick mean spike counts. We performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis on these classifiers, evaluating the performance using the ROC area under the curve (AUC, 0.5 indicates chance level performance) (Figures 4D, S6E, S6H, and S6K). Units responsive to position regardless of lick order (Figures 4B unit 5 and S6C–S6E) had strong position classifier performance and weak order classifier performance. Units that instead showed consistent activity based on sequence progress, regardless of sequence direction (Figures 4B unit 11 and S6F–S6H), had strong order classifier performance and weak position classifier performance. Some units could be used to classify both position and order (Figures S6I–S6K), indicating tuning to position in a manner that depended on sequence direction. This could be related to sensitivity to the direction of movement or to differences in top-down modulation between sequences.

Across the population, most units (67/78) showed significantly better than chance classification performance for position (29/78), order (18/78), or both (20/78) (95% CI of AUC > 0.5, estimated via bootstrap resampling on the licks for 1,000 resamples) (Figure 4E). Position and order tuning were weakly related to the unit peripheral response field (Figure S6L), and clusters 1 and 2 MEV-MSAs showed similar distributions of position and order tuning (Figure S6M). Repeating this analysis but using licks to the two positions on either side of the middle lick (L1 and R1) similarly showed order tuning of some MEV-MSAs (positiononly: 13/64; order-only: 5/64; and both: 28/64) (Figure S6N). In summary, we identified units tuned to a task variable that is independent from the kinematics (lick order with the sequence) as well as units tuned to kinematics in a sequence-dependent manner.

In summary, many MEV-MSAs showed tuning to sequence progress, such that they displayed significantly different firing during kinematically similar licks depending on whether they occurred at the beginning or the end of the sequence. This likely results from top-down modulation (fusimotor or central synaptic drive) patterned based on sequence progress. As progress is independent of orofacial kinematics, such progress-driven modulation can explain some of the non-kinematic components of MEV-MSA population activity.

Sequence task and drinking licks differentially activated MEV-MSAs

The sequence task featured two categories of lick events: licks that were directed to a series of specific port locations and drove movement of the port (“drive” licks) as well as a series of ∼5–10 water consummatory licks to a stationary port after reward delivery (“drink” licks) (Figure 5A). Sensorimotor cortex differentially encodes these two movement contexts, with a stronger encoding of drive compared to drink licks in the premotor cortex.41 We observed MEV-MSA units whose activity changed between these two contexts; we observed units with stronger responses to drive licks (Figure 5B) as well as units with stronger responses to drink licks (Figure 5D). We asked whether the reward context (drive vs. drink) of the lick represented an additional task parameter governing top-down MEV-MSA modulation in addition to sequence progress.

We therefore asked if MEV-MSA activity was more strongly coupled to kinematics in one of these two movement contexts. Because drive licks explore a larger movement space than drink licks, we first limited data to regions of the kinematic space well represented by drink licks (STAR Methods). 2D tuning histograms of jaw position and velocity showed dramatic changes in MEV-MSA kinematic tuning between contexts (Figures 5C and 5E).

To investigate this further, we fitted encoding models to predict MEV-MSA activity using jaw kinematics during drive or drink licks. 22/83 units showed a significant change in the strength of coupling to kinematics between conditions (95% CI of drink encoder performance—drive encoder performance did not include 0, comparison performed within resample for 1,000 bootstrap resamples) (Figure 5F). This included units with stronger coupling to drive licks (9/83 units) as well as units with stronger coupling to drink licks (13/83 units).

MEV-MSA ensembles encoded kinematic and task parameters

Tuning to kinematics (Figure 3) and task parameters (Figures 4 and 5) varied widely across the MEV-MSA population. This suggests sparse population coding for kinematic and task information, with individual units showing mixed encoding of specific kinematic/task parameters and highly diverse encoding across the population. To test this, we fitted decoder linear models that use spike rates from simultaneously recorded MEV-MSAs (“ensemble decoding models”) (n = 2–11 units per session) to predict kinematic and task parameters. We then tested the dependence of these models on the individual units in the ensemble.

Jaw and jaw+tongue coordinate axis weights could be decoded from MEV-MSA ensemble activity (Figure 6A). We used an iterative dropout strategy to test how this information was distributed across the ensembles (STAR Methods). Removal of 1–2 top-performer units caused a dramatic loss of decoder performance for many MEV-MSA ensembles (Figure 6B), indicating that kinematic information is largely non-redundant between ensemble units. Thus, while kinematics can be decoded from ensembles, decodability is largely reliant on a small subset of top-performing units.

Given that MEV-MSA phase tunings span the lick cycle, we asked if MEV-MSAs could be used to decode lick cycle phase. To decode phase (a circular variable) using linear models, we mapped phase to 2D Cartesian coordinates (cos[phase], sin [phase]) and linearly decoded these components using neural activity. The predicted terms were then converted back to phase (Figure 6C). Jaw ensembles could be used to decode lick cycle phase (Figure 6C), with performance relying strongly on high-information units (Figure 6F).

Finally, consistent with our observation on lick position and order information in MEV-MSA activity (Figure 4), MEV-MSA ensemble models could decode two parameters that together describe sequence performance: the jaw setpoint x position (representing the slow change in mediolateral center point of each lick) (STAR Methods) and sequence progress (Figures 6D–6F). Again, ensemble performance relied strongly on high-information units (Figure 6F).

Taken together, these ensemble decoding models support mixed kinematic and task information in MEV-MSA population activity. Our iterative dropout analysis supports a sparse population coding model; mixed representations of kinematic and task variables were highly diverse across the MEV-MSA population (Figure 7). Such a model may support diverse functional roles for MEV-MSAs, with subsets of units providing a stable representation of orofacial kinematics and others providing task-driven, flexible representations of movement.

Mixed encoding of kinematics and task variables of MEV-MSAs

Sensorimotor cortex hierarchically encodes cycle-by-cycle and sequence-level information during this task.41 We found a mixed representation of kinematic and sequence-task information in MEV-MSAs.

Jaw muscle activity is patterned by lick cycle and sequence progress

A large component of fusimotor modulation is coupled to α motor neuron drive.12,56 Finally, we asked if task-driven modulation of MEV-MSAs is correlated with jaw muscle activity. We recorded temporalis and masseter muscle activity (electromyography, EMG; STAR Methods) in a separate cohort of animals (n = 16 sites from four animals) performing the sequence licking task (Figures S7A and S7B). This revealed complex, muscle-specific activity during the task that was patterned by lick cycles and sequences (Figures S7A and S7E). Lick cycle muscle activity peaked at two points of the lick cycle: once during the opening swing around the time of tongue protrusion, and once during closing (Figures S7C and S7D). The first peak may stabilize the jaw for tongue protrusion, while the second likely drives jaw closure. MEV-MSAs show phase tuning around both peaks, with MEV-MSA peak activity leading peak muscle activity in both cases (Figures 2D and S7D). Phase tuning of MEV-MSAs may be partly shaped by α-linked fusimotor drives (see limitations of the study).

Jaw muscle EMG recordings showed diverse sequence responses, likely based upon the specific motor units sampled (Figure S7E). Similar to our analysis of order tuning in MEV-MSAs (Figure 4), we made linear classifiers that used activity from single recording sites to decode position or order of licks to the outermost positions (Figures S7F and S7G). We found components of muscle activity linked to lick port position as well as lick order independent of position (position-only: 7/16 recordings; order-only: 2/16; and both: 7/16). Therefore, task-driven changes in MEV-MSA responses (Figures 4 and S6) co-occur with changes in output motor drives across the movement sequences. Additionally, drive and drinks licks showed different muscle activity patterns for some EMG recordings, with more complex and variable activity seen during drive licks (Figures S7H–S7K).

In summary, we found task-driven patterns in motor output signals (jaw muscle activity) that paralleled task-driven modulation of MEV-MSAs (based on sequence progress as well as lick reward context). This may indicate that task-driven MEV-MSA modulation is, at least in part, coupled to α motor neuron drive. Top-down commands from higher-order motor control circuits may flexibly shape MEV-MSA and motor output signals in parallel to flexibly adjust sensorimotor control during this task (Figure S7L).

DISCUSSION

Kinematic encoding by MEV-MSAs

A large body of work has modeled MSA activity in relation to the length and change-of-length of the resident muscles.9 These models range from comparatively simple8 to highly complex,57 depending on their incorporation of fusimotor drives as well as multiple sources of nonlinearities in the responses. This work has notably identified a power-law relationship between Ia firing responses and muscle velocities (~velocity0.3–0.5); such models can account for nearly all hindlimb Ia activity during locomotion.58

Our work differs from and complements these prior studies in several ways. We describe MEV-MSA activity in relation to a body (orofacial) kinematic reference frame. While the relationship between externally observed kinematics and muscle lengths is highly complex due to the jaw and tongue mechanics,59,60 we note that this is very likely to be a behaviorally relevant reference frame for the nervous system. Using a machine learning approach that extracts movement features from the video data in an unbiased manner, we estimate an upper bound on the amount of population MEV-MSA activity explainable by the observed kinematics to be ∼50% (Figure S4D). This fraction is lower than the best-performing models from prior studies (which reported R2 values > 0.958). While this is likely, in part, due to differences between approaches (see limitations of the study), we note that we study MEV-MSA activity from animals exploring a large fraction of the orofacial movement space in the context of a trained, rewarded behavior. As our encoding models do not account for top-down modulation, our work is consistent with prior studies demonstrating the contribution of these modulatory drives to MSA activity and further characterizes the contribution of such drives in a behaviorally relevant context.

MSAs have long been proposed to be a source of kinesthesia, which is the body’s sense of its own position and movement.61 Based on the complex and variable coupling of MSAs to kinematics, the ability of MSAs to act as kinesthetic sensors has been questioned.14 While we found that only a small fraction of MEV-MSAs was strongly coupled to kinematics (Figure 3H; Figures S4C and S4D), MEV-MSA ensemble models could decode kinematics relying on the best-performing units (Figure 6B). This result contrasts with prior reports of MSA ensemble activity from the cat hindlimb,49 in which MSA ensembles provided largely redundant information. While this could reflect differences in body location or behavior context, it may also reflect the relative scarcity of units providing strong kinematic decodability (we may achieve greater coverage for this system due to the smaller size of the mouse). Overall, it is possible that a minority of MEV-MSAs are well-positioned to stably encode specific kinematic parameters based on their location and/or response properties. Moreover, we observed MEV-MSAs tuned to specific configurations of joint jaw-tongue movement (Figures 3F and 3G; Figure S5). Units with such tuning properties could provide a representation of kinematics in a multi-effector (jaw–tongue) space in a manner useful for downstream circuits.

MEV-MSA phase tuning

Hindlimb MSA tuning to step cycle phase is determined by MSA subtype (Ia vs. II), the muscle of innervation, and the activity of fusimotor units patterned to the step cycle.12,49 Our findings similarly suggest that multiple factors determine the lick cycle phase tuning of individual MEV-MSAs. Across the population, phase tuning peaks were associated with muscle of innervation as well as jaw-tongue co-tuning: “jaw open–tongue out” units (Figures 3F and S5D), located in temporalis or anterior masseter, were tuned to fully open phases, while “jaw open–tongue in” units (Figure 3G and S5E), located in posterior or ventral masseter, were tuned to jaw closing phases. Inhibition of the latter subset by tongue protrusion may set their cycle phase tuning, as they fired directly after the tongue was retracted into the mouth (Figure 3G). Notably, in the case of location-identified units, these two subtypes are positioned on opposite sides of the temporomandibular joint (temporalis/anterior masseter units innervate positions anterior to the joint and posterior masseter units innervate positions posterior to the joint).43 Phase tuning of these subsets may therefore be set based on complex stretch profiles of the musculature across the lick event (loading of the anterior musculature and unloading of the posterior musculature with tongue protrusion).

Lastly, MEV-MSA phase tuning is likely additionally set by top-down modulatory drives. Jaw opening and jaw closing phase tunings of MEV-MSAs were associated with muscle activity peaks (Figures 2D and S7C). MEV-MSA activity peaks preceded muscle activity peaks overall. This may result from MEV-MSA modulation linked to α motor neuron drive, since prior work has noted phase leading of static γ drives linked to muscle shortening.30 While cluster 1 and cluster 2 MEV-MSAs showed similar tuning distributions (Figure 2D), cluster 1 MEV-MSAs showed much stronger overall tuning (i.e., stronger modulation by phase) to lick cycle phase (Figure S2R), likely in part due to higher peak firing rates of cluster 1 neurons (Figure S2P). Prior work has shown that dynamic γ inputs sensitize type Ia MSAs to the onset of muscle lengthening at specific points of the step cycle (i.e., the swing-stance transition in the case of ankle dorsiflexors).18,30 Future work should seek to determine if similar mechanisms set sharp phase tuning of some type Ia MEV-MSAs.

Sensory feedback can coordinate movement timing by impacting central pattern generator activity62 and by demarcating important events.63 Our observation of MEV-MSA population phase tuning responses that span the lick cycle and are associated with relevant behavioral events (e.g., tongue protrusion/retraction) indicates that MEV-MSAs may be used to monitor (Figures 6C–6F) and/or coordinate progression through the stages of the lick cycle. For example, “jaw open–tongue out” and “jaw open–tongue in” MEV-MSAs could assist in appropriately timed reweighting of muscle activation by augmenting anterior muscle support when the tongue is protruded and then coordinating jaw closure activity when the tongue is safely in the mouth. Future work should address how phase-linked MEV-MSA feedback is used by lower- and higher-order motor control circuits to coordinate individual lick events.

Sequence task-driven modulation of MEV-MSAs

Following work in the cat hindlimb system showing fusimotor-driven modulation of MSAs patterned to individual step cycles,12,18 we identified MEV-MSA activity modulation patterned across entire action sequences. Prior work has defined how the “fusimotor set” can retune MSA responses across different movement contexts (such as movement directions or external loadings).13,17,19 Such changes are akin to the “gain scheduling” of engineered feedback control systems, in which feedback gains are strategically updated to meet changing operating conditions.64 Our work illustrates how MSA tuning can be flexibly shaped throughout the course of ongoing behavior. The flexible MEV-MSA tuning modulation we identified may come from alpha-linked fusimotor activity, alpha-independent fusimotor activity, central synaptic inputs, and/or other mechanisms. Interestingly, cluster 1 and cluster 2 MEV-MSAs showed similar distributions of lick position and order tuning (Figure S6M) and were not segregated between phase-coupled and slowly modulated subsets (Figure 2E). This may indicate that task-driven top-down modulation of these neurons similarly targets type Ia and type II units (through mechanisms common to both types, such as static γ and/or central synaptic drives). Finally, MSAs are sensitive to intramuscular pressure,55 and sequence-linked changes in muscle pressure may contribute to some component of MEV-MSA sequence tuning (e.g., due to increased muscle tone early in the sequence (Figure S7) or due to build-up of pressure over repeated contractions).

We speculate that sequence-tuning of MEV-MSAs shapes sensorimotor control for sequence task performance. For example, the early initiating licks of the sequence show landmarks of “reach-like” searching licks: they feature an increased duration as well as corrective submovements65 (Figure 1E). Subsets of MEV-MSAs show higher or lower activity during early licks (Figure 4). This could reflect flexible engagement of lower sensorimotor circuits to support expression of reach-like licks, for example, to override fast central pattern generator (CPG) rhythmic programs and/or to increase overall jaw stiffness. As the animal then performs consummatory post-reward licks, the changes in MEV-MSA responses we identified (Figure 5) may be related to a shift into a “fully CPG-driven” sensorimotor regime (i.e., disengagement of top-down control and engagement of fast lower-order reflexes). Future work should test how responsiveness to external perturbations (via short- and long-latency reflexes) is flexibly controlled across this task.

Licking is controlled by hierarchical somatomotor cortical circuits. Primary sensory (tongue–jaw S1 [S1TJ]) and motor (tongue–jaw M1 [M1TJ]) cortices encode sensory stimuli and drive lick movements, respectively.66–68 Tongue–jaw premotor (anterolateral motor [ALM]) cortex is involved with planning and initiating licking as well as online correction of ongoing lick movements.65,69,70 During the directed lick sequence task, S1TJ and M1TJ control cycle-by-cycle movements while ALM controls sequence-level changes.41 ALM engagement is flexible across lick behavioral contexts; ALM shows higher engagement with reach-like licks65 and lower engagement with drinking licks.41 In the motor output signals, we showed that jaw muscle activity is driven on both single-lick and sequence-level timescales (Figure S7). Our demonstration of lick- and sequence-level activity in the MEV-MSA population implies that MEV-MSAs are functionally coupled to multiple levels of the sensorimotor hierarchy. Such coupling may vary across the population; MEV-MSAs primarily driven by lick cycles may be preferentially coupled to M1TJ, while sequence-driven units may be preferentially coupled to ALM. Future work should investigate M1TJ and ALM activity correlations with MEV, including the evolution of coupling during complex behavior as well as the underlying neural mechanisms.

Limitations of the study

While we have strong confidence that we successfully isolated MEV-MSAs in our recordings, it is difficult for us to fully exclude the possibility that a small minority of units identified as MEV-MSAs are instead MEV-LTMRs or other neuron types. MEV comprises large-diameter sensory neurons sitting in the midbrain.23 The majority of these neurons are jaw musculature MSAs, while a minority are LTMRs. Prior reports have identified MEV-LTMRs innervating the tooth ligament as well as the whisker pad.23,46,47 We developed selection criteria for isolation of MEV-MSAs from our single-unit recordings (Figures S1 and S2) based on the location in MEV (identified by multi-unit passive jaw movement activity under anesthesia), significant movement modulation during the task, and unit autocorrelograms (reflecting intrinsic physiological properties). Our criteria isolate units with clear characteristics of MSAs (firing responses to muscle probing under anesthesia, passive jaw movement responses under anesthesia, and movement response properties consistent with classic MSA characterization). Our criteria also correctly segregate identified MEV-LTMRs (identified based on peripheral probing responses) from MSAs.

Notably, many units identified as MEV-MSAs do not show movement responses under anesthesia (Figure S3). We believe this reflects direct anesthesia effects on MEV-MSAs, as these anesthesia-silenced units can be isolated from the same tetrode as units showing robust passive movement responses (Figure S3). We do not believe this result indicates problems with recording stability between awake and anesthetized conditions, as unit waveforms are highly consistent between awake and anesthetized conditions (Figures 1C, S3A, and S3B).

Many prior models explained MSA activity based on the length of the resident muscle as well as its time derivatives, and some of these models can explain MSA activity almost fully.9 It is challenging for us to accurately estimate the relevant muscle lengths for our recorded MSAs, as muscle fascicle lengths across the jaw vary in a complex manner due to the irregular bony anatomy, and the peripheral location for many recorded MEV-MSAs is unknown. We additionally cannot accurately estimate localized muscle tensions, which are known to be a critical property explaining instantaneous MSA activity.14 We can therefore only make general comparisons between our kinematic models and much of the prior work. While it is possible that a meaningful fraction of MEV-MSA activity poorly explained by our models is related to unmeasured parameters (muscle fascicle lengths or localized tensions), we believe a significant portion of this unexplained activity reflects task-based MEV-MSA tuning. This is because of the following: (1) we expect fascicle lengths to have a stable (though likely complex) relationship between jaw kinematics, and models given a large amount of flexibility to explain MEV-MSA activity based on observed kinematics (video SVD-Boosted trees models) fail to account for large components of this activity; and (2) we identified MEV-MSA activity related to specific task parameters (progress and reward context) that are independent from the kinematics. Nevertheless, future work utilizing biomechanical models of the jaw may be able to better explain MEV-MSA activity fascicle lengths and/or tensions. This would allow a comparison of MEV-MSA activity to prior models and yield more precise insights into the biomechanical tuning of MSAs.

Lastly, while we identified a subset of units co-tuned to jaw and tongue movement, we can only account for tongue movement outside the mouth as the animal performs the licking task. Tongue movement inside the mouth is complex and dynamic71 and may account for some fraction of the activity poorly explained by our kinematic models. While challenging, future work that records tongue movement internal to the mouth could better estimate the contribution of tongue movement to MEV-MSA activity, further supporting the tuning of these units to multi-effector movement.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice

All procedures were in accordance with protocols approved by the Johns Hopkins University Animal Care and Use Committee (protocols MO018M187, MO21M195, and MO24M185). Mice were housed in a room on a reverse light-dark cycle, with each phase lasting 12 h, and maintained 20°–25°C and 30–70% humidity. Before surgery, mice were housed in groups up to five, but afterward were housed individually. All mice used in this study were obtained by mating Cre lines with wildtype (Jackson Labs 000664; C57BL/6J) or Ai32 (Jackson Labs 012569; B6;129S-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm32(CAG—COP4*H134R/EYFP)Hze/J) lines. Mice used in this study included two Advillin-Cre (Jackson Labs 032536; B6.129P2-Aviltm2(cre)Fawa/J) females, two Calb1-Cre; Pvalb-Flp (Jackson Labs 028532; B6; 129S-Calb1tm2.1(cre)Hze/J, Jackson Labs 022730; B6.Cg-Pvalbtm4.1(flpo)Hze/J) females, four Dbh-Cre (Jackson Labs 033951; B6.Cg-Dbhtm3.2(cre)Pjen/J) mice (two males and two females), one TH-Cre (Jackson Labs 008601; B6.Cg-7630403G23RikTg(Th—cre)1Tmd/J) male, and two wildtype mice (one male and one female). Mice were 2–8 months old at the start of behavior training, and training and testing sessions typically lasted 2–3 months.

METHOD DETAILS

Surgery

Mice underwent surgery for the implantation of a headpost and microdrive. For surgery, mice were anesthetized with 1–2% isoflurane and held on a heating blanket (Harvard Apparatus). Ketoprofen was injected i.p. to reduce inflammation, and lidocaine or bupivacaine as a local analgesic was injected under the scalp at the start of surgery. The skin and periosteum above the skull were removed. A small circular craniotomy was made with a dental drill over the anterior cortex for the implantation of a grounding pin. The ground pin was either a small stainless steel screw or a gold male pin soldered to a short length of silver wire fixed to the skull with dental cement. A thin layer of metabond (C & B Metabond) was applied to cover the surface of the skull to fix the headpost onto the skull. The custom-made metal or 3D printed headposts were positioned over the lambda suture and feature a recessed opening, allowing access to the skull posterior to the lambda suture.

A ∼2 mm diameter craniotomy was made centered at −1mm caudal and +1.1 mm lateral to lambda, near the border of the cerebellum and the inferior colliculus. All implantations occurred on the animal’s left side, to target MEV-MSA neurons innervating the left jaw musculature. To map the location of MEV within this craniotomy, tungsten recordings were performed to search for jaw movement related LFPs. The craniotomy was covered with sterile PBS, the dura was carefully removed using a tungsten needle, and a tungsten recording electrode (0.5 MΩ, WPI) was lowered into the brain using a micromanipulator (Sutter instruments). The differential signal between the recording electrode and a bath reference (AgCl pellet) was amplified (DAM80, WPI) and monitored on an oscilloscope (Tektronix) and through an audio monitor (A-M Systems). Characteristic LFP responses to ∼1 Hz manual jaw movement were typically found ∼ −2.5–4 mm below the surface of the brain.

Custom-built microdrives containing eight tetrodes (nichrome wire, 100–200 kΩ, Sandvik) and an optical fiber (0.39 NA, 200 μm core) were implanted at the identified location. The microdrive was slowly lowered to ∼0.5–1 mm above the identified dorsoventral position, then fixed to the skull with dental cement. The recording was grounded to the grounding pin before shielding the microdrive with plastic/aluminum foil housing. Mice were given a minimum of 1 week of recovery before water restriction.

Three mice received virus injection (200–750 nL, ∼5 nL/s injection speed) into the MEV or the nearby locus coeruleus. Viruses (Addgene) included AAV.EF1a.DIO.hChR2(H134R).eYFP, AAV.hSyn.Con/Fon.hChR2(H134R).EYFP, or AAV.mCherry.ChR2.

Sequence task behavior

The behavior apparatus, task, and training have been previously described.41 Briefly, the task was controlled by an Arduino-based system and custom MATLAB software. This system moved a two-axis motorized (LSM050B-T4 and LSM025B-T4, Zaber Technologies) lick port, monitored lick port contact registered via a conductive lick detector (Svoboda lab, HHMI Janelia Research Campus), delivered trial start auditory cues (a 0.1s long, 65 dB SPL and 15 kHz pure tone) and a constant masking auditory stimulus (white noise and previously recorded motor noise), delivered water rewards (∼2–3 μL) via the lick port, implemented user control of task parameters, and logged behavior control and performance.

During the task, the port moved through an arc of seven locations specified via a polar coordinate system. The origin of this system was referenced to the mouse’s incisors, so that these positions were symmetrical to the mouse’s midline with equal spacing in arc length. The radius and total arc distance of the positions were adjusted to control task difficulty, with smaller radii/lengths used for early training. Difficulty was gradually increased over 7–15 training sessions to reach performance of ∼100–400 trials in ∼1 h behavior testing sessions. Mice performed the task in the dark without visual cues about the port position. Ventral whiskers (macro and micro-vibrissae) were regularly trimmed to prevent any whisker-based localization of the lick port.

Behavior testing, unit recording, and unit characterization

Prior to behavioral training, the microdrive was slowly advanced while the presence of jaw movement-related LFPs was assessed to place the chronic recording in MEV. The mouse was water-deprived, and then received behavior task training. Toward the end of training, the mouse’s left cheek was dehaired using Nair depilatory cream, and the ventral whiskers were trimmed. After 1–2 further training sessions, mice underwent behavior testing and recording.

For a given recording session, high-speed video and tetrode electrical activity were recorded while the mouse performed the sequence task for ∼0.75–1 h. Extracellular voltages were amplified and digitized at 30 kHz via an RHD2164 amplifier board and acquired via an RHD20000 system (Intan Technologies) without filtering. The microdrive was typically advanced ∼100 μm per day across a set of testing sessions.

After behavior testing, the animal was induced under light isoflurane anesthesia (∼0.5–1%) for a panel of passive characterization stimuli. The depth of anesthesia was adjusted to maintain a breathing rate of ∼2 Hz, and the passive stimulus panel typically lasted ∼0.75 h. The jaw was first passively moved with a wooden stick. ∼1 Hz movement was applied in multiple directions (up-and-down and side-to-side in both directions). In some sessions, faster ‘lick-like’ movements were applied to the jaw. Extracellular recording and high-speed video were acquired during movement. Movement was timed to a 1 Hz auditory tone delivered by the Arduino. The Arduino delivered cues to the neural recording software synced to this tone, allowing movement to be registered to the neural and video recording.

To record unit cheek probing responses, a camera (Thorlabs CS165MU1, fitted with an 8 mm fixed focal length lens, Thorlabs MVL8M23) was positioned above the mouse to view the left cheek via a silver-coated mirror. Synchronous triggers, synced to an auditory tone, were sent to the recording system and the camera, which captured 3 frames (1 ms exposure, 10 frames per second). Cheek probe stimulation was manually timed to the tone. Inter-stimulus intervals were either 5 s or 2 s. Stimuli included a blunt wooden stick (∼2 mm diameter), a blunt metal piece sheathed in rubber (∼1.6 mm diameter), and von Frey hairs (0.6 g–2.0 g). Before acquiring data, the cheek was searched during audio monitoring of extracellular responses to localize unit responses. During mapping acquisition, the cheek was searched with multiple passes over the entire cheek within a session. During most sessions, high-speed video was acquired during the probing to localize the stimulus on the cheek and exclude stimuli that caused jaw movement.

Videography, tracking, and kinematic analysis

As previously described,41 high-speed (400 Hz, 0.6 ms exposure time, 32 μm per pixel, 800 × 320 pixels) dual-view video was acquired via Streampix 7 software (Norpix) and a single camera (PhotonFocus DR1-D1312–200-G2–8 camera) fixed with an ×0.25 telecentric lens (55–349, Edmund Optics). Side and bottom views were simultaneously acquired via a silver-coated mirror fixed under the mouth region. The mouth was illuminated via an 850 nm LED (LED850–66-60, Roithner Laser) passed through a condenser lens.

3D jaw and tongue keypoints were tracked from the dual view video using DeepLabCut.72 A network was trained to detect three points on the chin (forming a small triangle on the distal chin) and two points on the tongue (the base and tip of the visible tongue) from both views. Training data included 1070 manually labeled frames from 9 mice. The frames were selected from videos of mice performing the sequence licking task and mice receiving manual jaw movement under anesthesia.

We then mapped kinematic tracking data from the various animals into a common reference space. Resting frames were identified as those in which movement velocity did not exceed 3.2 mm/s for any jaw keypoint. A rigid transformation (rotation + translation) was calculated mapping the session average jaw rest position onto a common reference position, and this transformation was applied to all tracked keypoints.

The mean (centroid) of the three jaw-tracked keypoints was taken as the jaw position. Tongue length was taken as the 3D Euclidean distance between the visible tongue base and tip keypoints. The angle (in radians) of the base-tip vector from the vertical in the XY plane was taken as tongue θ, and the angle of the base-tip vector from the horizontal in the YZ plane was taken as tongue φ. Tracking data were smoothed with a 3rd-order Savitzky-Golay filter with a window size of 27.5 ms. The vector norm position ρ was calculated as the vector norm between the Euclidean position of the jaw and the resting position (previous paragraph). The vector norm velocity is the first time derivative of this term.

Jaw setpoint x was extracted by segregating the data into single lick events (using phase decomposition, see below), estimating setpoint x at phase = 0 rad. (maximal jaw opening) as the lick median jaw x value, and linearly interpolating for the other time points.73

Jaw–tongue coordinate kinematic axes were identified using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of jaw–tongue features extracted from the keypoints in each video frame (time bin size = 2.5 ms). For each session independently, data were first limited to licks passing a jaw z range of 0.5 mm (see Phase decomposition) and z-scored. SVD was performed (scipy.linalg.svd). The right singular vectors of the SVD were taken as the coordinate axes, the left singular vectors were taken as the weights onto these axes, and the singular values represented the relative variance explained by the axes.

Kinematic features were extracted from the video data by SVD using Facemap.53,54 We used a spatial binning parameter of 10 pixels. Weights for 500 features calculated via SVD of the raw frames and for 500 features calculated via SVD of the motion energy between frames were concatenated. Weights for all 1000 features were used for the prediction of neural activity (see below).

Electromyography

Temporalis and masseter muscle bipolar EMG recordings were taken using either custom-made fine-wire hook electrodes (PFA-coated tungsten wire, A-M Systems 795500) (n = 4 sessions from 2 animals) or high-density Myomatrix EMG arrays74 (n = 2 sessions from 2 animals). Recordings were grounded to implanted pins in the skull, digitized at 30 kHz via a 16 channel bipolar input headstage (RHD2216, Intan Technologies) and acquired via an RHD2000 system.

Histology

After the conclusion of testing sessions, electrolytic lesions were made under anesthesia in one tetrode channel. Mice were transcardially perfused with PBS, followed by fresh 4% PFA. The brain was dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight. The midbrain was sectioned on a vibratome (50–100 μm). Sections were then immunostained for Parvalbumin: sections were permeabilized in PBS +0.5% Triton X-100 (PBT), incubated in 5% donkey serum +1:1000 rabbit anti-parvalbumin (Abcam ab11427) + PBT (overnight at 4°C), washed in PBT, incubated in 1:1000 anti-rabbit IgG-488 or 594, washing in PBT, and mounted in Aqua-Poly/Mount (Polysciences). MEV was identified via location and characteristic PV+ large diameter cell bodies, and the lesion location was confirmed (Figure S1B).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

General data analysis and plotting

Analysis was performed using MATLAB (2023a), Python 3.10, and ImageJ. Large Language Models (LLMs; ChatGPT and GitHub Copilot) were used to suggest some analysis code, but all code was carefully reviewed by the authors. Boxplots show the median as a center line, quartiles 1 and 3 as box limits, and ±1.5x the interquartile range as whiskers (outliers are not shown). Error bars are defined in figure legends.

Spike sorting and unit inclusion criteria

We used Kilosort2 for initial spike sorting. Extracellular recording data were high-pass filtered at 500 Hz and across-channel-median subtracted (common average referencing) before passing into Kilosort. Spike sorting results were manually curated in Phy and MClust. We used three quality control metrics to select single units (Figures S1C–S1E): (1) interspike interval violation rate (ISI VR), (2) false positive rate (FPR), and (3) signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). We saw high-frequency bursts of MEV unit activity with inter-spike intervals of ~1 ms (Figure S1E). We chose a 1 ms refractory period size and excluded units with ISI VR > 2%. FPR was estimated using a published method,75 and we excluded units with FPR >15%. SNR was calculated as the unit waveform amplitude divided by the channel standard deviation for the channel with the largest waveform amplitude. Units with SNR <4 were excluded.

In anesthetized animals, the presence of robust jaw movement-related multi-unit activity is a hallmark of MEV in the local region (Figure S1A). For a given session, to select recordings from tetrodes located in MEV, we calculated the multi-unit autocorrelogram (ACG, ±5 s with 10 ms bin size) of each recording channel during 1 Hz manual jaw movement. Multi-unit spikes were detected using a threshold-crossing method, with the threshold set at 5.93 times the median absolute deviation of the channel. Channel recordings were classified as within MEV if a peak detection method (scipy.signal.find_peaks, rel_height threshold of 0.5, prominence threshold of 0.5) identified peaks in the ACG at both 1 and 2 s lags ( ±0.2s). Tetrodes with any channel passing this criterion were classified as MEV recordings for the session.

MEV units were further tested for movement-related modulation in the awake animal using the ZETA test76 (Figures S1F–S1H). This test references spike times against a behavioral event, collects cumulative referenced spike times within a test window, and compares the cumulative spike times array against a null distribution in which the event times are shuffled against the spike times. We referenced unit spike times against the time of lick contact at the central port, using a 1 s test window. Units with a significant (p < 0.05) ZETA (Zenith of Event-based Time-locked Anomalies) were classified as movement-modulated. Most within-MEV unit recordings are modulated by active movement (103/116 unique units, Figure S1H). Units passing the ZETA criterion sometimes appeared to show little task modulation when spikes were aligned in time, yet subtle movement-related activity was revealed when spikes were aligned on lick cycle phase (Figure S1G).

Across sessions, all single units found on the same tetrode were compared. Units with similar single-unit ACGs, waveforms, and activity during the sequence licking task were classified as putative duplicate units (seen across 2–4 sessions). For analyses of the single-unit dataset, only one session was kept for each putative duplicate recording. If a cheek response field was found for one session, that session was kept. Otherwise, the session with the highest SNR was chosen. When cheek response fields were found in multiple sessions, the response fields were consistent across sessions, and the highest SNR session was kept. Analyses using ensemble models (Figure 6) compare information across small populations of units rather than across individual units. Duplicates (representing a small fraction of the overall population) were not removed for ensemble analyses.

MEV unit clustering

Prior reports indicate a subset of MEV neurons are low-threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMRS) innervating the teeth or whisker pad.23,46,47 Units with tooth-region probe response fields showed low firing rates during the task. MEV units were clustered using their single-unit ACGs to isolate MEV-MSAs and to putatively classify MEV-MSAs as type Ia or type II MSAs (Figure S2). ACGs were normalized (0–1) by their maximum values, and PCA (sklearn.decomposition.PCA) analysis was run on the ACGs for time lags of 0–14 ms (this analysis window was chosen to maximize cluster separability). Spectral clustering (sklearn.cluster.SpectralClustering, 3 clusters, ‘nearest_neighbors’ affinity matrix) was performed using the first 2 PCs (Figure S2N). The coefficient of variation for interspike intervals during rest periods (Rest CVISI) was calculated by identifying rest periods based on the kinematics (periods in which jaw velocity did not exceed 8 mm/s in any direction for at least 0.5 s), finding inter-spike intervals, and calculating the session coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by the mean).

EMG signal processing

EMG recording signals were bandpass (400Hz - 3kHz) filtered with a 2nd-order Butterworth filter. Electrical lick port contact artifacts were removed by censoring data in 5 ms windows centered on contact times and linearly interpolating over the gaps. Signals were then rectified and smoothed with a 10 ms standard deviation Gaussian kernel.

Cheek probe response maps

Probe locations were manually identified in ImageJ. Image stacks for each session were registered to a common cheek image (Figures S2C and S2J) using manually selected keypoints (fitgeotrans in MATLAB, projective transformation). 0.25 s test windows were centered on the time of auditory tones. Single-unit spikes were binned (10 ms bin size), spike rates (spikes/s) were smoothed with a 20 ms std Gaussian kernel, and the maximum spike rate was taken as the unit test window response. Responses were combined across stimulus types (i.e., blunt probes and von Frey hairs). For sessions with simultaneous jaw video recording, responses from test windows with >0.32 mm movement were excluded.

Phase decomposition

Lick-cycle phase was decomposed from the jaw kinematics via methods previously used to decompose whisk cycle phase from whisker kinematics.73,77 Jaw z data were first smoothed with a 50 ms window moving median filter and then detrended with a Butterworth bandpass filter (2–20 Hz). Lick cycle phase was taken as the angle of the Hilbert transform of this signal. Rare short stretches in which the decomposed phase ‘doubled back’ (i.e., quickly decreased and increased) were identified and replaced with linearly interpolated values.

Sequence progress was identified by aligning sequence task data on the phase value of the start of the first lick with a port contact, unwrapping phase assignment across the trial, and dividing by the radian value of the time of water delivery. This assigns an arbitrary value of 0 to the start of the first contact lick and 1 to the time of water delivery. Phase assignments are unreliable for the stationary jaw and for low amplitude licks. Cycles in which the jaw z range did not pass a threshold of 0.5 mm were excluded from the analysis.

Lick and trial selection

For lick cycle analyses (kinematics, EMG, unit responses), licks within a session were ranked by the range of jaw z movement, and the top 25% of all session licks were kept (636–2174 licks per session).

For sequence trial analyses, a custom trial selection algorithm was used to choose the best matched trials across animals and sessions. For each trial, jaw z and x data were interpolated using unwrapped sequence phase (in rad.) onto a common array (the first through seventh licks, with 20 points per lick cycle) and concatenated into a single array. Within a sequence type (i.e., left-to-right), the median across-animal, across-session sequence array was found. Within a session, 50 left-to-right and 50 right-to-left trials with the smallest Euclidean distance from this median array were selected.

Averaging and bootstrapping

Kinematics and EMG signals were averaged in phase (scipy.stats.binned_statistic) with a bin size of pi/20 radians. Unit phase histograms were calculated by counting spikes in each pi/20 radian bin and dividing by the total real time spent in each phase bin. Phase-aligned trial histograms were calculated similarly, binning spikes on unwrapped phase assignments across the trial (for −1.5 to 9.5 cycles from the start of the first contact lick). Error estimates (standard deviation and 95% confidence intervals) were calculated via bootstrapping (1000 resamples). For within-session estimates, resampling with replacement was done on the trials. For across-animal, across-session averages, we employed hierarchical bootstrapping; on each pass, resampling with replacement was done at the level of the animal, then session, then trial (e.g., Figure 2A, z and x), or animal, then session, then lick (e.g., Figure 2B).

For 2D tuning surfaces and associated 1D tuning curves, we calculated 30 non-uniform bins for each parameter to more evenly distribute observations across the bins, as previously described.77 The sigmoid function parameter k was selected as the inverse of 0.3–0.4 times the maximum absolute value of the variable for each curve in order to ensure adequate coverage of the relevant kinematic space. For neuronal tuning surfaces, binned spike counts (scipy.stats.binned_statistic_2d) were divided by the total real time spent in each bin. For lick contact probability surfaces, the number of bin observations occurring with lick port contact was divided by the total number of bin observations. Surfaces were smoothed with a Gaussian 2D filter (kernel std. width of 1.5 bins). Bins with fewer than 10 observations were excluded (colored white on 2D surfaces).

Phase tuning analysis

We segregated units based on the strength of phase tuning. Some units showed bimodal phase tuning responses, which posed a challenge for tests that use vector strength (i.e., the Rayleigh test). We therefore identified units in which the spike counts in 2.5 ms bins had significant mutual information (MI) with lick cycle phase during task performance. Data were limited to task performance windows (0–16 lick cycles from the start of the first contact lick on each trial) because phase assignments are inaccurate when the jaw is not moving. Phase tuning peaks were identified for phase-coupled units and for EMG recordings. Unit phase histograms (or phase-averaged EMG signals) were concatenated on both ends with repeated copies (for smoothing over the ends), smoothed (3rd order Savitzky-Golay filter with a window size of 27.5 ms), and normalized by the maximum value. A peak detection algorithm (scipy.signal.find_ peaks with a prominence threshold of 0.1) was run, and the phase value of the peaks were kept. Phase-coupled units and EMG recordings showed either one or two peaks. These were ordered as primary and secondary peaks based on peak height. To calculate the probability of tongue protrusion out of the mouth as a function of lick cycle phase, a binary vector representing bins with a tongue length >0.5 was averaged based on phase across all animals and sessions.

Encoding and decoding linear regression

Encoding and decoding linear regression models were fitted on binned (2.5 ms bin size) kinematic and spike rate data. Unit spike counts were binned, and spike rates (spikes/s) were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel. The kernel width (std = 25 ms) was chosen to maximize overall encoder model performance (Figure S4B). Unit-coordinate axis correlations (absolute value of the Pearson correlation r) were calculated between axis projection weights and smoothed spike rates. The 3 best correlated axes were used for linear models.

Linear models were fitted using sklearn. Unless otherwise noted, model predictive performance was calculated using Monte-Carlo cross-validation (MCCV) on the trials. On each pass (resample number = 1000), 10% of trials were randomly chosen as the testing set, models were fitted on the training set (remaining 90% of trials), and performance was evaluated on the testing set. Reported cross-validated R2 or Pearson’s r values are the mean of the resample model performances. Pearson’s r is used for phase decoder performance (Figure 6F) since R2 is a poor goodness-of-fit measure for circular data.

For significance testing of the difference between two models (Figures 3I and 5F), performance was compared within each resample. For example, in Figure 3I, on each resample, jaw+tongue encoder performance was subtracted from jaw encoder performance. If the 95% CI of this comparison was greater than 0 (equivalent model performance), the performances were significantly different. For Figure 5F if the 95% CI did not include zero, the performances were significantly different.

For comparison of encoder model performance between drive and drink conditions (Figure 5), the drive licks explored a wider movement space than the drink licks. Therefore, we limited drive lick data to samples within the span of the drink lick jaw movement space. For each session, 100 uniform bins were found for the range of jaw z and x. We then identified z, x 2D bins with >10 observations during drink licks, and excluded samples in the drive lick data that did not match these bins. SVD was performed as above on the combined (drive and drink lick) restricted data and used for fitting encoding models.

Ensemble decoding models were fitted to linearly predict single kinematic or task parameters using smoothed (25 ms std. Gaussian kernel) spike rates from simultaneously recorded units. Model predictive performance was calculated using 5-fold cross-validation. Nested ensemble decoders missing subsets of these units were analyzed using an iterative unit dropping strategy: for each round, each unit was dropped in turn, and the 5-fold c.v. predictive performance was found for each nested ensemble. The nested ensemble with the largest performance loss (i.e., the ensemble lacking the top-performer unit) was used for the subsequent round.

Boosted trees models

Boosted trees encoding models using kinematic regressors to predict spike rates in 2.5 ms bins were fitted using XGBoost52 with a mean squared error objective. Hyperparameters were chosen to maximize cross-validated performance; we used 25 gradient-boosted trees and a 0.2 learning rate eta. We used a subsampling ratio of 0.2. Predictive performance was calculated using 10-fold cross-validation on the trials.

Binary decoder analysis

Binary decoders were fitted to classify lick position or order for licks to two selected port positions (L3 and R3, or L1 and R1). Within each port position, we sub-selected licks to match kinematics using a custom algorithm. An array containing the mean jaw z and y positions was found for each lick, and the pairwise Euclidean distances between all lick arrays were calculated. We then iteratively chose the best-matched pair, removed this pair, and chose another pair. 10 pairs were chosen for each position, yielding a total set of 40 licks.

For each unit, we assessed the ability of unit within-lick mean spike count to decode position (e.g., L3 vs. R3) or order (e.g., first vs. last) using the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of a Receiver Operating Characteristic analysis in sklearn (Figures S6E, S6H, and S6K). This analysis varies a test threshold and compares the True and False Positive Rates. An AUC of 0.5 indicates no better than random discriminability, whereas an AUC of 1 indicates perfect discriminability of the condition test distributions. 95% confidence intervals for the AUC scores were found using bootstrapping (1000 resamples) on the trials. If the 95% CI did not include 0.5, decoder performance was classified as significant. The same analysis was performed using mean jaw x, z, and y values (Figure S6B). One session in which jaw kinematics could significantly decode lick order was excluded, indicating insufficient performance of the lick selection algorithm. Binary decoders that used EMG activity (Figure S7F) were fitted similarly, except using within-lick mean rectified and smoothed EMG signals instead spike counts.
