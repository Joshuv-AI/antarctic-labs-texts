Ethical implications of high attrition in AI-based mental health interventions: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Hongze Yang, Jianfei Liu, Li Li, Ruiqing Jiang, Qing Lou, Weiming Sun, Chunxiao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe integration of AI-based conversational agents (CAs) in mental health aims to bridge the global “treatment gap.” However, high user attrition poses a critical ethical challenge, potentially compromising care continuity and distributive justice. This study quantifies attrition rates in AI-driven mental health interventions and evaluates their ethical implications for user autonomy and research integrity.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines and registered with PROSPERO, this systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from six databases through September 2025. Employing a convergent parallel mixed-methods design, we analyzed 39 independent studies. A random-effects model was used to calculate pooled attrition rates, while meta-regression explored the correlation between attrition and clinical efficacy (improvement in depressive/anxiety symptoms). Qualitative narrative synthesis was further applied to contextualize user experiences.ResultsThe meta-analysis revealed a pooled attrition rate of 17.04% (95% CI, 11.46%-23.38%) for AI interventions. Generative AI-based CAs exhibited significantly lower attrition rates (9.25%) compared to retrieval-based systems (23.12%, p = 0.044). Subgroup analysis identified intervention target, delivery platform, interaction mode, engagement reminders and safety measures as key moderators of engagement. Crucially, a significant inverse association between retention and treatment efficacy was observed in depression interventions, where lower attrition correlated with larger effect sizes (β = 0.97, p = 0.043). Furthermore, a pervasive “reporting gap” exists regarding the qualitative reasons for user withdrawal.ConclusionsHigh attrition rates in AI-based mental health interventions may represent more than technical disengagement, potentially implicating the ethical principles of beneficence and non-maleficence by disrupting the continuity of care. While generative AI shows promise in fostering a perceived therapeutic alliance and mitigating attrition, the observed association between disengagement and diminished efficacy suggests a risk of exacerbating structural health disparities. To address these challenges, we propose the “Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework”(EEF), which integrates transparent accountability, autonomy balance, safety-by-design, and inclusive beneficence. Background

The global mental health crisis has led to a profound “treatment gap,” marked by a chronic shortage of practitioners and systemic barriers to traditional psychotherapy [1, 2]. In response, AI-based conversational agents (CAs)—spanning from rule-based chatbots to Large Language Models (LLMs)—have been increasingly recognized as potential tools for expanding access to care [3, 4]. These AI-driven interventions are often presented as innovative solutions to increase the availability of personalized treatment, particularly for conditions such as depression and anxiety. From a bioethical perspective, these technologies are frequently framed as instruments of beneficence and distributive justice, offering the prospect of low-cost, 24/7, and stigma-free support to underserved populations [5, 6]. The recent paradigm shift toward generative AI has further heightened expectations; by simulating nuanced empathy and maintaining sophisticated dialogues, these agents appear to enhance user interaction and may foster a sense of connection previously associated primarily with human-to-human therapy [4, 7, 8].

However, the clinical integration of AI-based CAs appears to have progressed more rapidly than robust ethical and methodological scrutiny, particularly concerning user attrition. While existing scholarship has identified various ethical concerns regarding healthcare AI, including algorithmic bias and the potential erosion of professional autonomy [9], a critical lacuna remains in understanding how specific technical phenomena, such as the high attrition rates observed in digital interventions, may function as proxies for deeper ethical tensions regarding beneficence and justice. This gap is notably evident as AI begins to demonstrate potential in clinical settings, yet the absence of human oversight and the potential for over-reliance on automated systems for sensitive recommendations remain significant ethical considerations [10]. Within the broader digital health landscape, attrition (the premature disengagement of users) is still frequently relegated to a technical metric of usability or treated as a secondary endpoint [11, 12].

The ethical interpretation of attrition is inherently multifaceted and arguably transcends its classification as a monolithic “failure” [13, 14]. As a heterogeneous phenomenon, it may arise from benign drivers, such as “stable remission”, where symptom improvement may render further intervention unnecessary, or practical constraints like participant burden [15]. However, within the sensitive domain of mental health, attrition that is premature or unintended appears to intersect significantly with the principle of non-maleficence [7, 16]. When a distressed user ceases engagement, it often remains empirically unclear whether this reflects a successful resolution or a critical disruption in the continuity of care that might leave vulnerable individuals without adequate support. The limitations of automated, pre-programmed emotional expressions could potentially fragment therapeutic narratives, risk masking lapses in care continuity as mere user choice, and lead to ethically concerning inequities in retention [17, 18].

Beyond individual outcomes, the ethical trajectory of AI in mental healthcare is increasingly shaped by the tension between proactive and passive governance. High attrition may not merely be a technical byproduct but could potentially reflect a “passive” ethical positioning, such as opaque privacy practices or a lack of transparent boundaries, that may erode user trust and foster information asymmetry [7, 19, 20]. As suggested by existing research [21], proactive algorithmic ethics practices that anticipate and mitigate risks are often more effective in fostering patient trust, particularly in privacy-sensitive domains like psychiatry. Therefore, investigating the conditions under which attrition might compromise autonomy or justice is essential. A systematic evaluation of these dimensions appears imperative for developing more rigorous oversight frameworks that ensure AI-based interventions remain tools for empowerment rather than sources of unaccountable lapses in care.

A critical “reporting gap” further complicates these challenges. While a growing body of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) quantifies attrition rates, there remains a pervasive lack of qualitative documentation regarding withdrawal reasons [22, 23]. This is particularly notable as mental health experiences are inherently complex and culturally mediated, often resisting reduction to the fixed variables typical of quantitative paradigms. Without transparent reporting, the risk that digital interventions could inadvertently exacerbate vulnerabilities cannot be entirely dismissed; thus, the absence of data itself emerges as an ethical consideration. Addressing this gap suggests a need for standardized qualitative methodologies that incorporate reflexivity and trauma-informed ethics into research design. This transparency deficit not only potentially hinders scientific replicability but also raises questions regarding research integrity and health equity [19]. If specific demographic groups were to disproportionately disengage due to cultural insensitivity in AI training data, the promise of “democratizing care” would remain unfulfilled [24]. Consequently, there is a strong case for shifting the perception of attrition from a mere data point to a potentially essential indicator of the ethical safety of digital interventions.

This systematic review and meta-analysis endeavors to bridge the current gap between empirical quantitative evidence and normative ethical analysis. By synthesizing data from 39 independent studies, we seek to provide a systematic evaluation of user engagement through an ethical lens. Specifically, this study addresses three primary objectives:

First, to quantify pooled attrition rates across diverse AI-based mental health interventions and identify key technological and methodological moderators.

Second, to examine the extent to which different AI architectures (specifically generative VS. retrieval-based systems) associate with user retention, and to explore the subsequent ethical considerations regarding user autonomy and beneficence.

Third, to evaluate the transparency and rigor of attrition reporting in the current literature, assessing its potential implications for clinical safety and health equity.

Through this integrative approach, we outline a potential foundation for an “Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework” [4, 19]. This framework aligns with emerging calls for responsible AI integration that emphasizes clinical oversight, transparency, and the prioritization of patient well-being. By transitioning from narrow retention metrics toward a more holistic assessment of user welfare, this study aims to contribute to ensuring that the digital evolution of mental healthcare remains grounded in the fundamental commitments of non-maleficence and the preservation of user dignity.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [25]. The review protocol was documented internally in August 2025, and the study was registered on PROSPERO (CRD420251275051) on December 29, 2025. To mitigate potential reporting bias, we maintained a strict adherence to the pre-specified primary outcome (pooled attrition rate). All subgroup analyses (e.g., AI architecture, target condition) were pre-defined in the internal protocol prior to data extraction.

Search strategy and selection process

Databases searched included PubMed, PsycINFO, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and CINAHL. To ensure comprehensiveness, we refined previously employed search strategies in systematic reviews [26–28] for each database. These strategies included index terms and keywords describing CAs, such as “intelligent conversational systems”, “embodied CAs”, and “conversational artificial intelligence”. The search scope encompassed all records from each database’s inception to September 2025 and was restricted to English-language literature. Additionally, we manually searched for studies meeting the research criteria, cross-referenced prior reviews’ reference lists, and included articles’ reference lists to identify additional eligible articles. The complete list of databases and the detailed search strategy are provided in “Supplementary File 1 Search strategies”.

After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the records. They then screened the full texts of the records meeting the inclusion criteria. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in “Supplementary File 2 inclusion and exclusion criteria”. In summary, we formulated the following inclusion and exclusion criteria based on the PICOS framework: (1) Population: Participants of any age and health status; (2) Intervention: AI-based CAs, defined as software programs or bots using natural language processing, machine learning, and large language models to simulate human-like conversations. Unlike rule-based agents, which generate responses from predefined rules [29], AI-based CAs can understand user intent, analyze context, and retrieve or generate suitable responses. This definition includes CAs in virtual and augmented reality, as well as robotics. Interventions must utilize such agents to facilitate real-time, two-way dialog to promote psychological well-being or alleviate distress. (3) Control: Any control group type, e.g., waiting lists, routine care, other CAs. (4) Outcomes: Primary findings report attrition rates, including within the intervention group and differential rates between intervention and control. Secondary findings include user experience, open-ended feedback, and a narrative synthesis of churn prediction factors. (5) Studies were English-language RCTs of any form.

Data extraction

We utilized an Excel-based data extraction method to identify relevant characteristics for inclusion in the study. Two assessors pilot tested the method on a subset of included articles (n = 10/38), refining the process through discussion to ensure reliability and reproducibility. The extracted study characteristics included: (1) study and sample characteristics (authors, publication year and country, intervention length, follow-up length, participant age, and health status); (2) relevant characteristics of the AI-based CAs (delivery platform, response generation mode, automation, interaction mode, cognitive behavioral therapy, embodiment, emotional and empathetic responses, personalization); and (3) intervention-related characteristics (incentive measures, safety measures, engagement reminders, control group type, and protocol/registration).

We also used a mixed-methods approach to analyze secondary outcome measures. For data on engagement and user experience, which included both interaction behaviors and subjective evaluations, we conducted a narrative synthesis. Two reviewers performed a thematic analysis of the included studies to capture variations in user feedback and identify success factors for CA interventions. Finally, we extracted factors significantly associated with attrition rates (P < 0.05) from quantitative trials, providing an overview of predictors of discontinuation.

Two assessors independently evaluated the risk of bias in the included studies using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool 2.0 [30]. Discrepancies between the assessors were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

These categories, particularly concerning AI technical architectures and clinical risk-management protocols, were grounded in established digital health and ethical frameworks [2, 15, 26, 27]. Data analysis and synthesis

This study employs a convergent parallel design within a mixed-methods framework to provide a holistic evaluation of attrition [31]. Quantitative data (pooled attrition rates) and qualitative data (narrative synthesis of user experiences) were collected and analyzed concurrently to facilitate methodological triangulation.

Regarding the quantitative strand, statistical analyses were performed using the meta and metafor packages in R (version 4.5.1), adhering to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (v6.4) [32]. To estimate pooled attrition rates, we employed a random-effects model with the Freeman–Tukey double arcsine transformation [5].

We utilized a standard two-level random-effects model as our primary framework. While Cochrane guidelines often suggest collapsing groups, we purposefully treated each intervention-control pair separately to preserve the distinct technical characteristics of CAs (e.g., generative vs. retrieval-based), essential for our nuanced ethical analysis. A three-level meta-analytic model was initially fitted to account for nested dependency [33], but variance partitioning justified the more parsimonious two-level model. Detailed formulas and integrated narrative syntheses are provided in “Supplementary File 4 The three-level meta-analysis and effect size calculations”. To definitively rule out unit-of-analysis errors, comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted (e.g., extracting only a single effect size per study), which confirmed that our methodological choice did not significantly alter the pooled effect sizes or the robustness of our conclusions.

To ensure the validity of this approach, we conducted further robustness checks: (1) influence diagnostics using Cook’s distance to identify disproportionate impacts [34]; and (2) sensitivity analyses selecting only the largest or smallest effect sizes per study to bound the potential distortion. Comparative attrition was synthesized via Odds Ratios using inverse-variance weighting [35]. Heterogeneity was explored through mixed-effects subgroup analyses and the Q-statistic [36], stratified by intervention duration (≤ 8 weeks vs. >8 weeks).

We addressed publication bias through a multi-method framework tailored to study volume (k). For k ≥ 10, we supplemented funnel plots and Egger’s test with the Henmi–Copas method, PET-PEESE, and p-value-based selection models (3PSM and Vevea–Hedges). For k < 10, the Doi plot and Luis Furuya–Kanamori index were utilized. The three-level model was also employed to examine the “Efficacy-Attrition Association” by synthesizing Hedges’ g effect sizes while managing data nesting.

In parallel, the qualitative strand employed a systematic narrative synthesis to contextualize the “why” behind the identified statistical patterns. We conducted a thematic analysis of open-ended user feedback and secondary engagement outcomes reported across the included studies. This process involved iterative coding to identify core themes related to user burden, perceived empathy, and technical friction. To ensure rigorous integration, the qualitative themes were systematically aligned with the quantitative moderators (e.g., interaction mode, safety measures).

Finally, these two strands were merged during the interpretation phase using a joint display approach. By mapping qualitative drivers onto statistical trends and identified moderators, we contextualized attrition beyond mere numbers. This synthesis facilitated the development of the “Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework”, reconciling the tension between proactive engagement and the ethical mandate of non-maleficence.

Results

The selection process details are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram (see Fig. 1). Of the 7,305 records identified across six databases, 6,904 remained after removing 401 duplicates. Screening of titles and abstracts led to the exclusion of 6,620 records, leaving 284 reports for full-text assessment. Following the exclusion of 12 single-session interventions, 39 independent studies (from 38 articles, including one article reporting two trials [37]) were finalized for meta-analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the 39 included RCTs was rigorously evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, with the comprehensive domain-level assessments visualized in Fig. 2. Overall results indicate a significant quality challenge within the current evidentiary base: only 28% of the studies were rated as having a low overall risk of bias, while more than 50% were categorized as high risk. This prevalence of bias was primarily driven by two critical methodological lapses. First, the measurement of outcomes was compromised in 35% of trials, largely due to the absence of blinded outcome adjudication for subjective, patient-reported measures. Second, regarding deviations from intended interventions, while 41% of studies utilized rigorous intention-to-treat (ITT) analyses, the majority (59%) relied on per-protocol (PP) assessments, thereby introducing substantial risks related to selection and attrition biases.

Risk of bias of RCTs

In the realm of randomization processes, although approximately 55% of studies demonstrated robust protocols, 35% raised some concerns due to opaque allocation concealment or retrospective trial registration—a trend that corroborates the findings of our PROSPERO database audit. Notably, the missing outcome data domain emerged as the most resilient, with 75% of studies maintaining a low-risk rating, suggesting that most trials preserved reasonable data completeness despite other systemic analytical limitations.

Characteristics of included studies

The 39 included studies (38 articles) exhibit an explosive growth in research interest, with 92% (n = 36) published since 2020 and a peak in 2024–2025. This temporal surge mirrors the rapid advancement of AI technologies in mental health. Geographically, the research shows global coverage; while the United States (n = 7) and China (n = 6) are the primary contributors, a notable expansion into underrepresented regions—including Africa (Malawi, Egypt), South America (Argentina), and Eastern Europe (Romania, Ukraine, Poland)—reflects an increasing global footprint (see “Supplementary File 5 Characteristics of included studies”).

Participant demographics are highly diverse. University students comprise the largest subgroup (37%, n = 14). Ten studies focused on clinically diagnosed populations, covering not only mental disorders (e.g., MDD, ADHD) but also somatic conditions with psychological distress (e.g., cancer, Parkinson’s, diabetes). Other targets include healthcare workers, corporate employees, and populations with specific behavioral risks (e.g., substance abuse). Sample sizes vary significantly, ranging from small-scale feasibility pilots (N < 50) to large-scale RCTs exceeding 1,000 participants.

Regarding intervention design, most studies (n = 25) adopted short-term cycles of ≤ 4 weeks. Evidence on long-term effects remains critically scarce, as only seven studies conducted follow-ups beyond the post-test, with the longest observation reaching six months [38]. Control conditions ranged from waitlists (n = 12) to rigorous active controls—such as treatment as usual (TAU), educational e-books, and human-led counseling—allowing for a nuanced distinction between the specific effects of AI interaction and general digital intervention effects.

Characteristics of the CAs

As detailed in Table 2, the technological landscape of the included CAs reflects a transition from traditional rule-based systems to advanced generative models. Smartphone or tablet applications remained the primary delivery platform (n = 23), followed by instant messaging platforms (n = 11), with minimal utilization of web-based interfaces or physical robots. While text-based interaction was the predominant mode (74%), ten studies implemented multimodal or voice-based interfaces.

Regarding technical architectures, the majority (n = 27) utilized retrieval-based approaches. These systems often integrated classical machine learning or specialized emotion-processing algorithms to enhance contextual responsiveness. In contrast, 12 studies employed generative CAs, predominantly leveraging Transformer-based architectures such as the GPT series, BERT, and other Large Language Models (LLMs) like LLaMA-2. Notably, these emerging agents have also begun incorporating advanced alignment methodologies, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) [39]. Despite these rapid advancements, the application of generative AI in psychotherapy remains in its nascent stages, with varied developmental frameworks ranging from open-source platforms (e.g., Rasa) to proprietary LLMs.

Attrition rates of the included studies

As shown in Fig. 3, the meta-analysis of 39 independent studies revealed a substantial degree of user disengagement across AI-based mental health interventions. The overall pooled attrition rate for the intervention groups was 17.04% (95% CI, 11.46%-23.38%), characterized by significant heterogeneity (I2 = 95.7%; p < 0.001). When stratified by intervention duration, short-term interventions (≤ 8 weeks) exhibited a higher pooled attrition rate of 19.13% (95% CI, 12.85%-26.25%) compared to long-term interventions (> 8 weeks), which showed a rate of 7.54% (95% CI, 0.19%-20.98%). To further elucidate the sources of heterogeneity and the impact of intervention characteristics on user engagement, we conducted a series of subgroup analyses integrating absolute attrition rates within intervention groups (Table 3) and relative attrition risks compared to control conditions (Table 4).

The technological architecture of the AI agent significantly moderated absolute engagement. Specifically, AI agents utilizing generative models achieved a significantly lower absolute attrition rate (9.25%, 95% CI, 3.70%-16.74%) than traditional retrieval-based systems (23.12%; 95% CI, 13.96%-33.66%; p = 0.044). Although the differential risk in Table 4 did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.38), the disparity in absolute rates underscores the inherent advantage of generative AI in fostering user persistence. Parallel to technical design, the clinical focus played a decisive role; CAs designed for education and disease management yielded a notably low attrition rate of 6.49% (95% CI, 2.80%-11.25%), whereas therapeutic tools suffered from significantly higher disengagement (21.52%; 95% CI, 24.34%-29.63%; p = 0.007).

The delivery platform was a further critical predictor of both absolute and relative attrition. Smartphone-based applications experienced the highest internal attrition (25.55%; 95% CI, 17.08%-34.12%) and a significantly elevated risk relative to controls (OR = 1.61; 95% CI, 1.20–2.17),, highlighting a potential “app fatigue” in mobile-based mental health support. Interestingly, the use of engagement reminders appeared to be counterproductive in terms of absolute retention, with reminder-based protocols showing higher attrition (21.85%; 95% CI, 14.44%-30.23%) than those without (8.25%; 95% CI, 3.33%-14.70%; p = 0.008). While Table 4 reveals no significant difference in the ORs between these two groups (p = 0.67).

The implementation of safety measures proved to be another vital moderator, particularly for differential risk. While absolute attrition rates remained similar regardless of safety protocols (p = 0.70), studies lacking crisis measures showed a 70% increase in the odds of dropout relative to controls (OR = 1.70; 95% CI, 1.32–2.20), whereas those with integrated safety frameworks achieved parity (OR = 0.97; 95% CI, 0.65–1.46; p = 0.022). Similarly, interaction modes significantly influenced adherence; multimodal or voice-based interfaces yielded lower internal attrition (9.35%; 95% CI, 3.42%-17.28%) than text-based agents (21.95%; 95% CI, 14.35%-30.58%; p = 0.03). Crucially, text-based interactions significantly increased the odds of attrition relative to controls (OR = 1.47; 95% CI, 1.12–1.92), while multimodal interfaces exerted a protective effect (OR = 0.39; 95% CI, 0.21–0.72; p = 0.001).

Finally, the choice of control group significantly influenced the comparative findings (p < 0.001). AI interventions showed a substantially higher risk of attrition when compared to information or attentional controls (OR = 2.05; 95% CI, 1.38–3.04). In contrast, they exhibited a significantly lower risk of dropout when compared to other CA controls (OR = 0.18; 95% CI, 0.10–0.32). This suggests that the perceived efficacy of AI-based interventions is highly sensitive to the nature of the comparator, emphasizing the need for standardized control conditions in future digital health trials.

Additional analysis

To ensure the reliability of our findings, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of publication bias and small-study effects (see “Supplementary File 6: Supplementary analyses”). For the outcome of attrition rates (≤ 8 weeks), the LFK index (-2.93) and Doi plot indicated only moderate asymmetry. The Egger’s test yielded (t = -3.95, p < 0.001), suggesting significant publication bias in the intervention group effect estimates. A total of 14 studies were imputed via the trim-and-fill method, with the adjusted pooled result being 36.49% (95% CI, 26.49%–47.07%). For the differential loss to follow-up rate (OR, ≤ 8 weeks), neither the funnel plot nor Egger’s test (t = -0.06, p = 0.95) provided evidence of significant publication bias. In the long-term outcomes (> 8 weeks), the LFK index for overall attrition and differential attrition in the intervention group was − 2.62 and − 0.99, respectively, indicating bias in the effect size estimates of the intervention group. Advanced sensitivity analyses, including the Henmi-Copas method, PET-PEESE, and p-value-based selection models (3PSM and Vevea–Hedges), consistently yielded adjusted estimates aligned with our primary findings. This indicates that in short-term and long-term studies, the intervention group effect estimates may be biased and underestimate the actual loss to follow-up, whereas the OR results are robust to selective reporting.

Critically, to address potential unit-of-analysis errors from multi-arm trials, our multiple sensitivity analyses confirmed that accounting for the nested data structure did not significantly alter the pooled effect sizes. Influence diagnostics using Cook’s distance and leave-one-out analysis revealed that no single study could significantly affect the p-value and heterogeneity of the pooled estimates, ensuring that the identified moderators and the “Efficacy-Attrition Association” were representative of the broader evidence base.

Meta-regression

The results of the meta-regression analysis indicated that participant mean age did not significantly moderate either the pooled attrition rates (β = 0.0094, p = 0.481) or the differential attrition risk (β = -0.0031, p = 0.296). Regarding clinical efficacy, AI-based CAs yielded a significant moderate effect in reducing anxiety and depressive symptoms (Hedges’ g = -0.464; 95% CI, -0.696- -0.232; p < 0.001). Additional factors associated with attrition

Beyond quantitative pooling, eight studies provided qualitative insights into the mechanisms underlying participant withdrawal. These factors are categorized into demographic profiles, baseline psychological characteristics, and technical-procedural barriers.

Regarding demographic and psychological predictors, male gender [39–41] and advanced age were identified as risk factors for higher attrition, while higher educational attainment (bachelor’s degree or above) appeared to foster better retention [42]. Interestingly, baseline psychological distress yielded divergent effects: while pandemic-related distress motivated continued participation [42], individuals with severe baseline depression or intense negative affect were significantly more prone to withdrawal [39].

The most prominent drivers, however, centered on technical limitations and procedural burdens. Multiple studies highlighted that repetitive AI responses, flawed content, and frequent misinterpretations, collectively perceived as a “lack of genuine interpersonal warmth”, led users to dismiss the intervention as “time-consuming and insufficiently beneficial” [41, 43]. Furthermore, demanding study protocols, such as mandatory daily five-minute interactions [39] or restrictive usage norms (e.g., requiring dedicated hardware in private spaces), imposed impractical burdens that alienated participants [44]. These findings suggest that attrition is rarely a random event but a calculated response to a mismatch between user expectations and the functional or logistical reality of the AI intervention.

Narrative synthesis of user engagement and experience

Of the 39 included studies, 30 reported user engagement metrics across three primary dimensions: frequency of use (n = 18), interaction intensity (n = 15), and longitudinal commitment or module completion (n = 13). However, the lack of standardized operational definitions for “engagement” across these studies presents a significant methodological challenge for cross-study comparisons. Regarding user experience, quantitative assessments of satisfaction and usability dominated the literature (n = 16). Notably, several studies (n = 5) found that user satisfaction was markedly higher when AI chatbots functioned as adjunct tools integrated with human therapists, whereas standalone CAs often yielded lower perceived value (n = 4). This trend underscores the importance of the “human-in-the-loop” model in maintaining the therapeutic appeal and ethical reliability of digital interventions, as further detailed in the qualitative synthesis in “Supplementary File 7 Summary of user engagement and experiences”.

Qualitative feedback from 24 studies provided deeper insights into the socio-ethical experiences of users, revealing a distinct divergence between retrieval-based and generative AI frameworks (see Table 5 and Supplementary File 7). On the positive side, users of retrieval-based systems highly valued the “non-judgmental” nature of the interaction (n = 7). By providing a space free from the social stigma often felt in human-to-human therapy, these tools appeared to protect the user’s sense of safety and privacy during self-disclosure. In contrast, generative AI fostered a more profound sense of emotional connection and companionship (n = 3). While the “empathy” provided by these models is a simulated outcome of natural language processing, it effectively reduced feelings of loneliness and encouraged user disinhibition. Furthermore, the structured skill-building provided by retrieval systems (n = 8) and the practical utility of reminder functions (n = 2) were recognized as essential supports for user autonomy and self-management.

Discussion

Ethical implications of attrition in AI-mediated mental healthcare

Using a mixed-methods framework, this study establishes a comprehensive evidence base for user attrition in AI-mediated mental healthcare, integrating a pooled quantitative attrition rate of 17.04% (95% CI: 11.46%-23.38%) with a narrative synthesis of user experiences. Beyond its utility as a technical metric, attrition serves as a critical interface between empirical performance and normative bioethics, particularly regarding the “continuity of care” [52, 53]. When users (especially those in acute distress) disengage before reaching clinical goals, the absence of human oversight or effective referral mechanisms may manifest as “therapeutic vacuum”. This rupture in the care continuum prompts an ethical re-examination of the principle of non-maleficence, as silent withdrawal could potentially signal a structural failure to sustain the therapeutic alliance rather than a benign conclusion to treatment [54]. Consequently, the ethical implications of attrition are highly contextual and contingent upon its drivers: while autonomous “benign withdrawal” reflects user agency, disengagement precipitated by a lack of safety protocols or structural inequities presents a risk to distributive justice [48, 55, 56]. Our findings necessitated a shift from viewing dropout as mere data loss to recognizing it as a conduit for evaluating the foundational moral obligations of beneficence and justice [19, 57]. Ultimately, the convergence of our quantitative benchmarks and qualitative insights underscores that attrition is not merely a technical metric but a proximal indicator of the ethical safety and clinical integrity of AI-mediated care, as evidenced by the following key findings.

A pivotal finding of this study is the significant difference in attrition between generative and retrieval-based AI (9.25% vs. 23.12%, p = 0.044). From an ethical standpoint, the lower attrition in generative systems suggests that naturalistic, empathetic and personalized interaction fosters a “perceived alliance” that enhances user retention. Compared to the mechanical and scripted nature of retrieval-based system, which often triggers early disengagement, generative AI provides a sense of emotional resonance that appears to align with users’ psychological needs for connection. However, this heightened engagement introduces a profound transparency-autonomy paradox: while such systems are more immersive, their capacity for authentic emotional support in complex clinical scenarios remains limited [58]. The anthropomorphic nature of generative AI may inadvertently obscure these functional boundaries [59], potentially leading users to attribute authentic clinical empathy to algorithmic responses. This opacity regarding the agent’s limitations could lead users to bypass traditional care pathways, potentially delaying essential human-led clinical intervention [7, 57]. Ethical deployment must, therefore, balance naturalistic engagement with explicit disclosure of the agent’s artificial nature to preserve the integrity of informed consent [8]. Furthermore, the higher attrition observed in therapeutic tools compared to educational or screening tools reflects a heightened “trust threshold” in digital psychiatry. Users’ experiences of technical friction and privacy concerns during intensive therapeutic tasks often lead to psychological fatigue, suggesting that current AI capabilities may be more ethically aligned with supportive or adjunct roles [60]. This reinforces that engagement strategies must be context-sensitive, acknowledging that a “one-size-fits-all” approach may fail to respect the unique clinical and ethical requirements of diverse populations [61].

Our meta-regression revealed a significant “efficacy-retention association” in depression interventions, suggesting that attrition and therapeutic realization are not merely correlated but may form a mutually reinforcing cycle. This statistical link is further elucidated by our qualitative synthesis, which identifies a “positive feedback loop”: consistent clinical exposure fosters the development of a “digital therapeutic alliance”, while early symptom relief reinforces user trust and motivation [48]. However, we caution that this observed link is primarily observational and remains susceptible to potential confounding factors, such as survivor bias. Crucially, reducing attrition does not per se guarantee superior clinical potency; rather, it underscores the intricate interplay between engagement dynamics and qualitative determinants, such as perceived user burden and technical friction. Nevertheless, mitigating preventable attrition remains essential to upholding distributive justice, ensuring that digital interventions overcome the structural and technical barriers identified in user feedback to effectively reach and sustain care for vulnerable populations [62].

Intriguingly, our meta-analysis revealed a “reminder paradox”, where interventions utilizing automated reminders experienced substantially higher attrition. Methodologically, this may reflect reverse causality or confounding by intervention intensity. However, a more nuanced explanation emerges from the qualitative feedback, where users often characterize repetitive, fixed-schedule prompts as “mechanical” and “impersonal”, particularly when they feel forced to follow rigid procedures rather than accessing support on-demand. From a bioethical perspective, while these “digital nudges” are conceptually rooted in beneficence, they are often subjectively experienced as “digital nagging” or intrusive steering. The qualitative evidence suggests that such reminders can precipitate “technical friction”, triggering psychological reactance when users perceive their need for flexibility and customization as being overlooked [49]. This overly prescriptive strategy may inadvertently undermine both user autonomy and the therapeutic alliance by transforming a supportive resource into a perceived burdensome imposition. This paradox underscores the necessity of prioritizing “respect for autonomy” by transitioning toward agentic, user-centric notification strategies [19, 63]. By empowering users to calibrate prompt frequency and timing, ethical design ensures that engagement remains a deliberate, voluntary choice rather than a consequence of technological coercion.

Notably, our findings suggest that the implementation of safety protocols is associated with a reduction in attrition risk. This quantitative evidence is reinforced by our qualitative synthesis, which indicates that a lack of clinical safeguards may be a significant factor contributing to user disengagement. Integrated clinical safeguards, such as real-time crisis monitoring, appear to function as “trust-building anchors” [50]. By addressing potential user concerns regarding “inappropriate clinical advice” or the “unpredictable responses” inherent in automated systems, these protocols may provide a “psychological safety net”. Such mechanisms may mitigate the sense of “vulnerability” or “impersonality” often associated with digital environments, potentially fostering a sense of clinical support that discourages silent withdrawal during symptom fluctuations [21, 48]. This integration highlights that safety features extend beyond administrative requirements; they appear fundamental to sustaining the “digital therapeutic alliance”.

Finally, the observation that only 38.4% of studies reported qualitative reasons for attrition suggests a pervasive “reporting gap” that warrants closer alignment with CONSORT guidelines. This gap creates a conceptual “black box,” potentially obscuring the distinction between “benign attrition” (e.g., symptom resolution) and “adverse attrition” (e.g., systemic barriers). To uphold the principles of research integrity and professional accountability, it is imperative that future trials adopt standardized, qualitative reporting frameworks. Enhanced transparency is essential to ascertain whether structural inequities may disproportionately affect vulnerable populations, thereby ensuring that the risk of harm is systematically identified and mitigated [8, 51].

In conclusion, while AI-based CAs demonstrate transformative potential for mental health, the heterogeneity in attrition rates underscores the necessity of a robust “Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework”. We advocate for an approach that prioritizes ethical commitments, such as ensuring equitable, safe, and sustained well-being, over the singular goal of reducing attrition. By integrating “human-in-the-loop” mechanisms and respecting the dignity of users, digital interventions can move beyond technical efficiency toward a more human-centric and ethically grounded model of care [57].

An ethics-oriented engagement framework

To align our meta-analytic findings with the evolving governance of digital psychiatry, we propose the Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework (EEF). This framework is theoretically grounded in Value Sensitive Design (VSD), a methodology which posits that core human values—such as trust, autonomy, and justice—should be proactively integrated into the technical specifications and procedural protocols of AI systems throughout their lifecycle [64, 65]. By applying VSD principles, the EEF reconceptualizes attrition from a technical data loss into a critical juncture for ethical oversight, ensuring that AI-driven mental healthcare remains anchored in fundamental bioethical commitments. III. Safety-by-

design trust

The conceptual coherence of this framework underscores a reciprocal relationship between technical efficacy and moral accountability. Within the pillar of “I. Transparent accountability”, we address the pervasive “reporting gap” by advocating for a transition from purely quantitative metrics to a qualitative responsibility model. A significant ethical consideration arises from the enhanced capacity of generative AI to mitigate attrition. While naturalistic interactions may foster a “perceived alliance”, they simultaneously necessitate rigorous safeguards for integrity. The EEF posits that reduced attrition should not be achieved through digital opacity; rather, systems must maintain transparent disclosure of their artificial nature to ensure that user engagement reflects an informed therapeutic bond rather than an artifact of emotional mimicry. Furthermore, the framework addresses the “Reminder Paradox” by proposing “II. Dynamic autonomy balance”. By transitioning from system-led intrusive prompts to user-calibrated nudging, the framework seeks to respect individual digital boundaries and mitigate the potential for overly paternalistic technological interventions [66].

The significant moderating effect of safety features on user retention, alongside the marked discrepancy in attrition between therapeutic (21.52%) and educational tools (6.49%), positions clinical rigor as the foundational pillar of the “III. Safety-by-design trust” architecture. We contend that higher attrition in therapeutic interventions may reflect a “trust threshold”, wherein the increased depth of clinical engagement necessitates a commensurately robust safety framework to alleviate user apprehension. The EEF operationalizes this through an integrated Human-in-the-Loop system, facilitated by automated “ethical triggers”: (1) Clinical Triggers, involving AI detection of acute risk keywords (e.g., self-harm) or clinical scale threshold breaches; (2) Engagement Triggers, identifying “churn signals” such as prolonged inactivity (> 72 h); and (3) Relational Triggers, monitoring for maladaptive over-reliance or boundary violations. By ensuring that safety protocols are not merely administrative but are proactively woven into the user experience, the framework mitigates “silent withdrawal” and upholds non-maleficence through timely human escalation.

Furthermore, the framework frames sustained engagement as a moral imperative within the dimension of “IV. Inclusive beneficence”. Given the observed “Efficacy-attrition Association”, maximizing retention is essential for the delivery of the intended clinical “dose”. By leveraging low-friction delivery platforms (e.g., social media integration) to enhance distributive justice, the EEF aims to democratize access to care while ensuring that increased accessibility is balanced with rigorous data sovereignty.

The significance of the EEF lies in its capacity to shift the discourse in digital psychiatry from “efficiency-centric retention” to “value-aligned participation”. It provides a normative roadmap for developers and clinicians to ensure that as AI systems become increasingly autonomous, their deployment remains tethered to the dignity and specific needs of the user. By addressing identified reporting deficiencies, the framework also establishes a foundation for standardized accountability, requiring that attrition be treated as a clinical outcome necessitating ethical justification. Ultimately, the EEF serves to ensure that AI-based mental health interventions function as instruments of human empowerment, anchored in the bioethical commitment to protect vulnerable populations and promote equitable well-being [61].

Strength and limitations

This study provides a systematic synthesis of user attrition in AI-based mental health interventions, integrating quantitative meta-analytic evidence with a normative bioethical analysis. By identifying a significant “retention–efficacy” correlation and proposing the Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework, we bridge the critical nexus between technical performance and clinical ethics. Furthermore, our comparative analysis of generative versus retrieval-based AI architectures offers a timely, evidence-based foundation for the development of both engaging and ethically responsible digital psychiatric tools.

Notwithstanding these contributions, several limitations warrant consideration. First, First, our reliance on study-level meta-regression precludes definitive causal inferences; the observed association between attrition and efficacy likely reflects a complex interplay of user engagement and potential confounders. Second, treating multi-arm trials as independent comparisons to preserve technical granularity risks “double-counting” shared control groups. Although we employed three-level modeling to mitigate this, a residual risk of bias remains, as statistical dependence may overstate precision and inflate the significance of subgroup differences. Consequently, our results should be interpreted as a nuanced exploration of AI architectures rather than definitive effect quantifications. Third, the current evidence base lacks sufficient longitudinal data, leaving the cumulative ethical impacts of attrition on care continuity largely unaddressed. Fourth, a pervasive “reporting gap” persists; the scarcity of qualitative data regarding dropout motives, such as algorithmic distrust, necessitates that our equity-related arguments remain partially inferential. Furthermore, the presence of substantial statistical heterogeneity and the exclusion of non-English publications suggest that our pooled estimates represent a localized global snapshot rather than a universal standard. Finally, given that the rapid evolution of AI technology renders any classification a “moving target”, we advocate for more granular technical and qualitative reporting in future digital psychiatry research.

Conclusions

Our meta-analysis underscores a critical “retention-efficacy” nexus that carries profound ethical implications for AI-driven mental health care. User attrition, therefore, should be conceptualized not merely as a technical metric of disengagement, but as a substantive challenge to the core bioethical principles of beneficence and non-maleficence. While the emergence of generative AI appears to enhance retention by mitigating the limitations of rigid, monotonous interaction patterns, the persistent “reporting gap” regarding withdrawal motives remains a significant concern; such opacity may obscure potential disruptions in care continuity and pose latent risks to vulnerable populations. The observed linear association between attrition and diminished efficacy in depression interventions underscores that high dropout rates could inadvertently exacerbate structural inequities, potentially undermining distributive justice. Given that current deficiencies in qualitative transparency preclude the systematic exclusion of systemic biases, we propose an “Ethics-oriented Engagement Framework”. This framework seeks to address the identified reporting gap, facilitating the transition of user engagement from a purely computational proxy toward a trust-based therapeutic alliance. By prioritizing transparency, user autonomy, and safety-by-design mechanisms, the EEF aims to ensure that the digital evolution of psychiatry is anchored in an equitable commitment to patient well-being, rather than being propelled solely by technological momentum.
