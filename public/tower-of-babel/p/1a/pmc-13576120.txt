The influence of anti-involution training on the critical thinking of young healthcare professionals in dental outpatient clinics: a pre-post quasi-experimental study
Authors: Yuxiang Chen, Anna Zhao, Haoran Yang, Xia Yang, Tingting Cheng, Xianqi Rao, Jianzhong Zhou, Lin Li, Ziliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study evaluates changes in critical thinking among dental healthcare workers after anti-involution training (AIT) and identifies key predictors of training effectiveness.MethodsA pre-post quasi-experimental design was conducted with 91 participants. Critical thinking was assessed using the CTDI-CV scale. Spearman correlation, multiple linear regression, and LASSO regression were employed to identify predictors, with machine learning models (Random Forest) used for nonlinear exploration and validation.ResultsAIT significantly improved critical thinking disposition, with a mean total score increase of 11.813 ± 6.752 (P = 0.001). Cognitive maturity showed the greatest improvement (3.978 ± 6.645, P = 0.004). Multiple linear regression revealed that truth-seeking (β=-1.738, P = 0.019) negatively predicted improvement, while cognitive maturity (β = 1.467, P = 0.016) positively predicted improvement. Random Forest validated these findings (AUC = 0.889).ConclusionsAIT effectively enhances critical thinking among dental professionals. Baseline truth-seeking and cognitive maturity are key predictors of training outcomes, informing personalized educational strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09793-5.Keywords: Anti-involution training, Critical thinking, Multiple linear regression, Machine learning

Introduction

Critical thinking, defined as the disciplined process of actively analyzing, synthesizing, and evaluating information to guide decision-making, is a foundational competency for healthcare professionals [1]. In clinical practice, especially in fast-paced environments like dental outpatient clinics, practitioners must integrate evidence, patient preferences, and contextual constraints to deliver optimal care [2]. Studies consistently link critical thinking to reduced medical errors and improved diagnostic accuracy, yet its development remains challenging in high-pressure, resource-limited settings where systemic barriers may hinder cognitive growth.

One such barrier is the phenomenon of involution - a term originating from Chinese discourse to describe counterproductive competition and excessive work intensity that leads to diminishing returns in personal development and well-being [3]. While initially discussed in socioeconomic contexts, involution manifests distinctly in healthcare, particularly among early-career professionals [4]. In academic medicine, it may drive redundant or incremental research at the expense of transformative innovation, while in clinical practice, it can lead to excessive workloads without commensurate improvements in service quality or professional satisfaction. Although the term “involution” is most prevalent in Chinese discourse, analogous phenomena like burnout and productivity paradox are well-documented globally, suggesting the need for targeted interventions to mitigate its effects [5, 6]. Anti-involution training (AIT) has emerged as a potential solution, aiming to counteract these dynamics by fostering efficient work strategies, collaborative mindsets, and goal-oriented behaviors [7–9]. While AIT shares some features with burnout prevention, resilience training, and reflective practice interventions, it is conceptually distinct in its primary focus on optimizing cognitive efficiency and reducing ineffective effort rather than merely managing stress or promoting well-being. A detailed comparison of AIT with related professional development approaches is provided in Table S1. While AIT’s theoretical foundations draw from established frameworks like resilience training and cognitive debiasing, its specific application to healthcare education remains underexplored [7–9]. Notably, despite shared cognitive components between AIT and critical thinking - such as metacognition and open-mindedness - no prior studies have empirically examined whether AIT enhances critical thinking in clinical professionals [7–12]. This gap is particularly salient given evidence that learner performance is influenced by both static factors and dynamic factors, yet the moderators of AIT’s effectiveness remain unknown [10–12].

AIT is grounded in self-efficacy theory and critical thinking models [13, 14]. According to Bandura, AIT may enhance perceived job control and self-efficacy by optimizing goal-setting strategies and reducing ineffective effort [13]. Facione’s model further suggests that AIT could improve clinical decision-making by cultivating core cognitive skills such as analysis and systematicity [14]. However, existing research on AIT has notable limitations: most studies have focused on corporate or educational settings, with limited exploration in healthcare, particularly among dental professionals in high-pressure environments [7–10]. Moreover, existing interventions often overlook heterogeneity in learners’ baseline cognitive characteristics, limiting the generalizability of training outcomes [15]. In resource-constrained clinical settings, overly standardized AIT may fail to accommodate individuals with diverse cognitive styles [15].

This study aims to address the following core issues by integrating cognitive load theory with a layered training design: (1) the differential impact of AIT on different dimensions of critical thinking; (2) How to optimize training efficiency based on individual cognitive traits [16]. Firstly, we evaluate whether AIT participation will improve critical thinking scores and consider potential confounding variables. Secondly, we use multiple linear regression and machine learning methods to identify which static or dynamic factors are most likely to predict the success of AIT, and elucidate the mechanism of the relationship between AIT and the development of critical thinking. This study provides evidence that may inform medical education practices and could be useful for designing interventions for learners requiring additional support.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This pre-post quasi-experimental design was conducted in Kunming, China, from January to December 2020. The study participants included young medical staff under the age of 40 from five outpatient departments of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Kunming Medical University. A total of 114 people participated in AIT and were surveyed before and after the training. A total of 205 questionnaires were collected, and after excluding cases with missing basic information and obvious logical errors, 182 questionnaires were considered suitable for analysis, with an effective response rate of 88.78%. All study participants have obtained informed consent. Due to practical constraints within the clinical setting and the educational nature of the intervention, a control group was not included in this study. All eligible young healthcare professionals in the participating outpatient departments received the AIT as part of their continuing professional development.

AIT implementation plan

The AIT implemented in this study lasted for one year and included the following core modules (Table 1), each designed based on cognitive load theory and adult learning principles [16, 17]. AIT adopts a modular design, including three core modules: deviation in clinical decision-making (4 months), target optimization training (3 months), and pressure efficiency regulation (5 months). The training adopts active learning forms such as case video analysis (60%) and role-playing (40%), with 40 min courses twice a month, as well as quality assurance mechanisms such as Standardized instructor training, Attendance tracking, and reflection logs. The course design is based on the cognitive load theory and reinforced through interval learning and clinical scenario transfer training [16].

Integrity and participation in intervention delivery

To ensure intervention Integrity and participant engagement across the five outpatient departments, several strategies were implemented. First, all instructors underwent standardized training prior to the intervention, which covered module objectives, teaching protocols, and quality assurance procedures. Second, the curriculum was manualized using uniform teaching materials, including case videos, role-play scenarios, and discussion guides. Third, participant attendance was systematically recorded for each session. Finally, engagement was monitored through monthly reflection journals, which were reviewed by facilitators to track active involvement.

Data collection

The critical thinking assessment was performed using the Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory-Chinese Version (CTDI-CV) [18], a culturally adapted and validated tool based on Facione’s California Critical Thinking Disposition Inventory (CCTDI) [19]. The CTDI-CV retains the original 7-domain structure with modifications for linguistic and cultural appropriateness in Chinese populations.The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the CTDI-CV was 0.90. The inventory consists of positive and negative items, rated on a 6-point Likert scale. The scoring for positive items ranges from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree), while the scoring for negative items is the reverse [20]. The CTDI-CV encompasses seven traits comprising 70 items: truth-seeking, analyticity, open-mindedness, systematicity, inquisitiveness, self-confidence in critical thinking, and cognitive maturity. Each trait includes ten items, resulting in a total score range of 70–420 [21].The CTDI-CV was selected for several reasons. First, as this study represents the initial evaluation of AIT in dental professionals, understanding changes in dispositional tendencies, the affective and motivational components of critical thinking, was considered a necessary first step before assessing higher-level clinical reasoning skills [19]. Second, the CTDI-CV has established reliability and validity in Chinese healthcare populations, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.90, making it suitable for this context [18, 21, 22]. Third, practical constraints within the clinical setting (limited time for assessment, lack of validated performance-based measures in Chinese) favored the use of a self-report instrument [19, 23]. However, we acknowledge that disposition does not equate to ability, and this limitation is addressed in the discussion.

Before completing the questionnaires, the research team provided detailed explanations to the participants regarding the purpose, significance, instructions, precautions, and deadline for questionnaire submission. Before the AIT, baseline measurements were taken from young healthcare professionals under 40 in five outpatient departments of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Kunming Medical University. The training, led by the department directors, lasted for one year and included a series of lectures on involution-related theories. Regular assessments on involution-related knowledge points and measurements of critical thinking disposition were conducted every three months.The data collected in this study encompassed two groups of factors: (1) participants’ baseline predictors, including initial knowledge and skills and inherent indicators. Initial knowledge and skills involved the pre-training total score of CTDI-CV, as well as the total scores of the seven traits of CTDI-CV before the training, including truth-seeking, analyticity, open-mindedness, systematicity, inquisitiveness, self-confidence in critical thinking, and cognitive maturity. Inherent indicators included gender and profession. (2) Outcome indicators: The effects of AIT on enhancing critical thinking among young healthcare professionals in dental outpatient clinics. The difference between the total scores of CTDI-CV before and after training is used to measure the impact of AIT on improving critical thinking among young dental outpatient clinic staff. For clinical interpretability, we initially used a 25 point threshold (based on previous estimates of MCID from scales to divide the CTDI-CV score changes into two points) [18, 24]. Based on this criterion, the participants were divided into the significantly improved and non-significantly improved groups. However, in order to minimize information loss, all analyses were repeated using continuous score differences as the primary outcome, and binary results were presented only as secondary outcomes [25].

Statistical analysis

Analytical methods

Descriptive analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0), with all other statistical analyses conducted in R (version 3.6.1) and Python (version 3.4.3). Between-group differences (significantly improved vs. non-significantly improved) were examined using Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables (presented as median with interquartile range) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (presented as counts and percentages). Paired-sample t-tests were used to assess the overall effect of AIT on critical thinking disposition. Spearman correlation analysis and multivariable logistic regression were employed to evaluate relationships between baseline predispositional indicators and training outcomes. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Given the exploratory nature of this study, a dual analytical approach was adopted. First, a multivariable linear regression model was fitted, with the change in CTDI-CV scores (post-training minus baseline) as the dependent variable and all baseline predictors as independent variables, providing interpretable effect sizes (β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals). Second, to complement regression analysis and explore potential nonlinear relationships, machine learning algorithms with five-fold cross-validation were applied. Due to the moderate sample size (N = 91), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) was employed for feature selection to minimize overfitting. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) for classification tasks and R² for regression tasks.

Fit multiple linear regression model

To evaluate the association between AIT and critical thinking improvement, we used multiple linear regression analysis with continuous changes in CTDI-CV scores (after training minus before training) as the dependent variable. The model uses the baseline critical thinking disposition subscale (including 7 dimensions including truth seeking and analysis) as the primary predictor, while adjusting for demographic features such as gender and profession. The final model provides standardized regression coefficient estimates and a 95% confidence interval, with a p-value < 0.05 (two-sided) considered significant. After fitting the model with the lm() function in R, the explanatory power was evaluated by adjusting R², the model parsimony was measured by AIC, the prediction error was quantified by RMSE, and the system performed residual analysis to verify the model hypothesis. Before fitting the model, first draw a scatter plot of the predicted student residuals to check the homogeneity of variance and outliers. Then, use the QQ normal probability plot to evaluate the normality assumption of the residual distribution. Graph diagnosis is generated using ggplot2, while statistical testing is implemented using the car package. For cases of non constant variance or non normal distribution, use robust standard error for correction. This method maintains the information integrity of continuous variables while controlling for confounding factors, and combines graphical diagnosis and statistical testing for double validation to ensure the clinical interpretability and robustness of effect size estimation.

Variable selection and establishment of ML prediction models

In order to mitigate the influence of indicator dimensions and magnitudes on the study results and to facilitate comparability of data across each test index, the test indicators were subjected to normalization. The normalization process employed in this study was “MinMaxScaler” which linearly transformed the original data to ensure that the resulting values fell within the range of [0,1]. Additionally, data balancing was performed using the “SMOTE” technique, which equalized the proportion of minority and majority classes to a ratio of 1:1 [26].We employed LASSO regression for feature selection to select the most predictive and relevant features to improve model performance and reduce computational burden [27]. Lasso regression compresses the coefficients of variables in the regression model by generating a penalty function to prevent overfitting and address severe collinearity issues [28–30]. Lasso regression was performed with 10-fold cross-validation using the “glmnet 4.1.2” R package. The study participants were divided into training and validation sets at 7:3.On the training set, we fitted nine machine learning classifiers: logistic regression, XGBoost, LightGBM, random forest, AdaBoost, Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), Support Vector Machine (SVM), multilayer perceptron (MLP), and K-nearest neighbors (KNN). XGBoost was built using the “xgBoost 1.2.1” Python package, while LightGBM was built using the “lightgbm 3.2.1” Python package. The other machine-learning algorithms were built using the “sklearn 0.22.1” Python package.

ML prediction models performance evaluation

Model performance was evaluated based on its discriminative, calibration, clinical applicability, and generalizability. Resampling was employed to ensure consistency of training samples across multiple models, enabling comparison of the results. The best model selection was based on analyzing the importance of metrics across models on both training and testing sets. The area under the curve (AUC), which is commonly used to characterize the diagnostic accuracy of a test or predictive model, was constructed using the “sklearn 0.22.1” Python package [30]. Decision curve analysis (DCA) plots, representing a decision analysis technique with substantial advantages in assessing the clinical utility of models, were generated using the “rmda 1.6” R software [31]. Calibration curves, which assess the predictive ability of models, were constructed using the “sklearn 0.22.1” Python package [32]. Precision-recall (PR) curves, widely used in the field, were plotted using the “scikit 0.22.1” Python package to evaluate model performance [33]. Once the most helpful model was determined through comprehensive comparison, all eligible participants were reclassified into training, validation, and testing sets at 6:2:2. In order to maximize statistical power in a limited sample (n = 91), this study used 5-fold cross-validation for all models, instead of testing segmentation for individual trains, and reported cross fold summary performance indicators.The best model underwent 5-fold cross-validation on the training set and was evaluated on the validation and testing sets. Learning curves were generated using the “scikit 0.22.1” Python package to assess the adaptability and stability of the model on the training and validation sets .

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

Among the 91 participants analyzed, 82 (90.1%) completed all AIT modules, with an average attendance rate of 89.7% and a reflection log submission rate of 85.7%, indicating satisfactory intervention fidelity and sustained participant engagement. The demographic characteristics of the participants are presented (Table 2). In this study, we excluded a total of 23 participants with incomplete data, a total of 91 participants were enrolled in this study, with 62 participants who underwent AIT showing no significant improvement in critical thinking skills and 29 participants showing significant improvement. Detailed data for 91 participants can be found in Data S1. When comparing the non-significant improvement group to the significant improvement group, significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in several critical thinking aspects, including CTDI-CV total score before training (p = 0.002), truth-seeking (p = 0.007), analyticity (p = 0.008), systematicity (p = 0.015), self-confidence in critical thinking (p = 0.047), inquisitiveness (p = 0.027), and cognitive maturity (p = 0.047). However, there were no significant differences (p > 0.05) in terms of profession (p = 0.378), gender (p = 0.953), and open-mindedness (p = 0.185).

Fitting results of multiple linear regression model

Multiple linear regression analysis evaluated the effects of baseline critical thinking tendencies and demographic factors on the overall score changes after AIT. The model showed moderate explanatory power, with an adjusted R²of 0.219 (RMSE = 30.555, AIC = 904.599), indicating that the included predictive factors can explain approximately 22% of the variance in training effectiveness. Figure 2a is a line graph depicting the target and predicted values of data samples. According to the visualization results, the predicted results of multiple linear regression are basically close to the true results, although there are some differences. Table 5 shows the regression coefficients and their P-values for each variable in the multiple linear regression analysis. Two cognitive features became statistically significant predictive factors (p < 0.05): seeking truth showed a negative correlation (β=-1.738, 95% CI: -3.156 to -0.321, p = 0.019),Participants with lower baseline scores for seeking truth showed greater improvement, while cognitive maturity showed a positive correlation (β = 1.467, 95% CI: 0.298 to 2.635, p = 0.016༉,It indicates that individuals with higher initial cognitive maturity benefit more from the intervention. It is worth noting that other critical thinking subscales, including openness (β = 0.428, p = 0.635), analytical (β = 0.430, p = 0.717), and systematic (β=-1.105, p = 0.180), did not reach statistical significance. Population factors also had no significant impact (gender: p > 0.05; profession: p > 0.05). The model diagnosis confirmed the appropriate specifications: the residual plot showed homoscedasticity, and the QQ plot showed the error of normal distribution (Fig. 2b, c). These results indicate that although multiple cognitive factors were considered, only specific dimensions have clinical significance and statistical reliability in relation to training outcomes in this cohort of dental professionals.

Comprehensive analysis of classified multi-mode

Multiple ML models were utilized for data sample classification: XGBoost, logistic regression, LightGBM, Random Forest, AdaBoost, KNN, SVM, GNB, and MLP. A 5-fold cross-validation approach was performed to validate all models. The AUC value was employed to evaluate model predictions. Results indicated that Random Forest, XGBoost, and AdaBoost exhibited superior performance on the training set, whereas Random Forest, KNN, and LightGBM achieved the highest performance on the validation set (Fig. 4a, b); see more details in Supplemental Table S2 and Table S3. The AUC value primarily assesses the predictive accuracy of the models without providing information on their clinical utility or preferences [35]. Therefore, we analyzed the PR curve, calibration curve, and DCA. The calibration curve suggested that the Random Forest and KNN models provided more accurate predictions (Fig. 4c). The DCA curve illustrated the favorable clinical applicability of the Random Forest model (Fig. 4d). The Random Forest model displayed exceptional performance in the training and validation sets, generating the highest average precision value on the validation set (Fig. 4e, f). Considering all factors, the comprehensive analysis suggests that Random Forest can be considered the optimal model.

Discussion

Critical thinking is a foundational competency for healthcare professionals, integrating cognitive skills and reflective dispositions necessary for navigating complex clinical environments [36, 37]. Our findings indicate that young dental professionals exhibit moderate-to-low critical thinking levels at baseline, consistent with prior evidence of cognitive skill gaps among early-career clinicians [2]. This baseline deficit underscores the need for targeted educational interventions in high-volume clinical settings, where time constraints and workflow pressures may impede reflective practice.The observed improvements in six of seven critical thinking dimensions following AIT suggest that the intervention may help mitigate systemic barriers to cognitive development. Notably, the only dimension showing a decline—self-confidence—may reflect a constructive “unfreezing” consistent with the Dunning-Kruger effect, wherein trainees become more aware of their initial limitations [38]. This pattern aligns with cognitive debiasing literature, which suggests that metacognitive training often temporarily reduces confidence while enhancing actual performance [38–40]. The positive changes in analytical ability, truth-seeking, and systematic thinking are particularly encouraging, as these dimensions directly support evidence-based clinical decision-making.

The observed decline in self-confidence in critical thinking (mean decrease of 2.011 points, p = 0.075), while not reaching statistical significance, represents an intriguing pattern that warrants theoretical consideration. This finding may reflect a constructive ‘unfreezing’ process consistent with the Dunning-Kruger effect, wherein individuals with lower competence in a domain tend to overestimate their ability, while those with greater competence develop more accurate—and often more modest—self-assessments [38]. In the context of AIT, participants may have gained metacognitive awareness of the complexity of clinical reasoning, recognizing limitations in their thinking processes that were previously unrecognized. This interpretation aligns with cognitive debiasing literature, which suggests that metacognitive training often temporarily reduces confidence while improving actual performance, as learners become more attuned to cognitive pitfalls [39, 40]. From an educational perspective, this transient decline in self-confidence may be a necessary precursor to genuine cognitive growth. According to Mezirow’s transformative learning theory, critical reflection on one’s assumptions—often triggered by disorienting dilemmas—is essential for perspective transformation. The case analyses and role-playing exercises in AIT may have served as such dilemmas, challenging participants’ existing cognitive habits and prompting reappraisal of their thinking processes. The reflection logs, in particular, required participants to articulate their reasoning and identify potential biases, potentially heightening their awareness of cognitive limitations.Notably, this decline in self-confidence did not impede improvements in other dimensions, such as cognitive maturity (p = 0.004) and truth-seeking (p = 0.287). This dissociation suggests that AIT may foster more accurate self-calibration of critical thinking abilities—a valuable educational outcome in itself. Future research should examine whether this initial decline in confidence is followed by subsequent recovery and growth with continued practice, and whether it correlates with objective measures of clinical reasoning performance.

Beyond these cognitive and theoretical considerations, we also examined the role of demographic characteristics in training outcomes. Consistent with our correlation analysis, gender showed no meaningful association with training outcomes (r=-0.006, p = 0.954). Gender was included as a demographic variable in the analysis because prior literature has reported mixed findings regarding gender differences in critical thinking dispositions [21, 36]. Some studies have suggested potential differences in cognitive styles or self-assessment patterns between male and female healthcare professionals, warranting examination in the context of AIT. The negligible correlation observed in this study suggests that AIT may be equally effective across genders, which has practical implications for training design—namely, that personalized strategies need not be differentiated by gender. However, given the imbalanced gender distribution in our sample (82.4% female), which reflects the broader demographic profile of nursing and dental hygiene professions in China, this finding should be interpreted with caution and validated in more gender-balanced samples.

Turning to the primary cognitive predictors identified in this study, the dimensions of truth-seeking and cognitive maturity demonstrated significant predictive effects on training effectiveness, which aligns meaningfully with self-efficacy theory and cognitive load theory [13, 16]. These theoretical frameworks were operationalized through specific AIT components.Firstly, the negative correlation presented by truth-seeking (β=-1.738, p = 0.019) indicates that participants with lower initial truth-seeking tendencies benefit more from training. This may reflect the effectiveness of case video analysis with structured debriefing—grounded in cognitive load theory—which provided clear frameworks for questioning clinical evidence, particularly benefiting those who initially lacked this tendency [16]. This likely reflects a key mechanism whereby individuals with initially low truth-seeking levels (and consequently lower self-efficacy in challenging clinical norms) show greater improvement through structured training - consistent with Bandura’s “efficacy expectation-behavior change” model [13]. However, causal confirmation of this underlying mechanism requires further controlled designs. Based on the results of this study, AIT may be particularly helpful in improving the thinking habits of clinical workers who are not good at actively questioning and exploring truth, which can also be explained by cognitive development theory. In addition, the positive predictive effect of cognitive maturity (β = 1.467, p = 0.016) supports cognitive load theory’s premise that individuals with higher cognitive maturity are more adept at managing working memory resources, thereby more effectively integrating complex AIT strategies (such as decision-making optimization under pressure) [16]. The reflection logs promoted metacognitive development by externalizing internal thought processes for conscious examination [12], while role-playing exercises provided enactive mastery experiences aligned with self-efficacy theory [13], potentially explaining the recalibration of self-confidence. This may reflect that individuals with high cognitive maturity are better at integrating new knowledge and transforming it into practical application abilities. This discovery provides important reference for talent selection and cultivation.It is worth noting that other dimensions of critical thinking, such as analyticity and systematicity, did not show significant effects (p > 0.05), which may suggest insufficient targeting of analytical thinking in the current training. A detailed summary of the theoretical mechanisms linking all AIT components to learning outcomes is provided in Table S5. This highlights the need to more systematically incorporate multidimensional skill training from Facione’s critical thinking model in future AIT design, rather than focusing solely on efficiency improvement [14]. Furthermore, the use of CTDI-CV alone limited the validity of our measurement results. Although CTDI-CV captures individuals’ tendency towards critical thinking, it may not fully reflect actual clinical reasoning or performance. The moderate explanatory power of our model (adjusted R²=0.219) may partially reflect this conceptual gap. Future research should incorporate multimodal assessments to capture different dimensions of critical thinking abilities, including performance-based measures, direct observation of clinical reasoning, behavioral indicators, and comprehensive management tools.

This study systematically evaluated the impact of anti involution training on critical thinking by integrating multiple linear regression analysis and machine learning methods. Both methods have their own advantages and the results confirm each other. Multiple linear regression analysis clarified the linear predictive role of truth seeking (β=-1.738) and cognitive maturity (β = 1.467), providing clear clinical explanatory estimates of effect sizes and statistical inferences. The machine learning method (Random Forest AUC = 0.889) further revealed the nonlinear features and interactions in these relationships. The advantage of this hybrid approach framework is that traditional regression ensures the robustness and interpretability of the results, while machine learning captures complex underlying patterns in the data. The combination of the two satisfies the needs of hypothesis testing and realizes the value of exploratory analysis. A practical overview of how these machine learning findings can support faculty development and individualized learning pathways is provided in Table S6. Based on the explanatory power of the model constructed in this study, there are other important influencing factors that need to be explored, such as work environment, incentive mechanisms. This study provide important insights for medical continuing education: (1) Attention should be paid to the assessment of students’ baseline cognitive characteristics; (2) The training plan needs to be personalized and adjusted, potentially through differentiated content and tiered complexity modules; (3) Further research is needed to optimize the intervention effects on other dimensions of thinking through more comprehensive training approaches. A practical guide for operationalizing personalized AIT strategies based on truth-seeking and cognitive maturity profiles is provided in Table S7.

The high attendance and participation rates in this study indicate that the intervention measures have been well received and implemented with reasonable loyalty. However, this study has several limitations: firstly, the sample size limits the detection effectiveness of machine learning models for high-dimensional interactions; Secondly, the absence of a control group poses a significant threat to internal validity. Without a comparator, the observed improvements in critical thinking cannot be definitively attributed to the AIT intervention, as alternative explanations—such as natural cognitive maturation, concurrent educational activities, or regression to the mean—cannot be ruled out. This limitation is inherent to the quasi-experimental design and tempers causal inferences; Thirdly, relying on self-assessment scales may introduce reporting bias; in particular, the exclusive use of the CTDI-CV means that improvements in disposition may not necessarily reflect gains in clinical reasoning performance—a critical distinction summarized in Table S8. Fourthly, the single center design affects the universality of the results. Additionally, the concept of “involution” and the design of AIT are rooted in the Chinese cultural and educational context; the generalizability of these findings to other healthcare systems with different professional pressures and training cultures remains to be established. Although the stability of the model has been improved through LASSO, these methodological limitations still need to be overcome in future multicenter large sample studies. Subsequent studies should adopt multicenter, large-sample, and longitudinal designs, incorporate randomized controlled or wait-list control groups to strengthen causal evidence, and include multimodal assessments (self-report + physiological + behavioral measures) to more comprehensively capture training effects.

Conclusions

This study confirms that AIT effectively enhances critical thinking disposition among dental healthcare workers, with truth-seeking and cognitive maturity emerging as key predictors of training outcomes. Individuals with lower baseline truth-seeking benefited more from the intervention, whereas those with higher cognitive maturity showed greater improvements. These findings provide empirical support for designing personalized critical thinking training programs tailored to learners’ cognitive profiles.
