Evaluating the implementation of a clinical decision support system for substance use disorder screening in maternal healthcare
Authors: Melanie Besculides, Nicklas Klepser, Roland C Merchant, Pritika Paramasivam, Yasmin Hurd, Karen Hicklin, Leah Habersham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeScreening for substance use disorders (SUDs) during pregnancy is essential but is hindered by barriers, including limited leadership engagement, financial sustainability concerns, and insufficient infrastructure. This study evaluated the implementation in a maternal health clinic of an electronic health record (EHR)-integrated Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment clinical decision support system (SBIRT-CDSS) that incorporated the 5Ps screening tool. We aimed to assess social workers’ perceptions of usability and integration, and to compare screening rates, missed referrals, and revenue generation of the SBIRT-CDSS to SBIRT without CDSS.MethodsOur mixed-methods assessments encompassed four phases: (1) pre-implementation acceptability assessment using Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) surveys and qualitative input from social workers; (2) cognitive walkthroughs with mock patients; (3) systems modeling comparing SBIRT-CDSS to SBIRT without CDSS; (4a) post-implementation feedback from social workers at 1 month and (4b) quantitative analysis of SBIRT-CDSS usage, CPT billing, and screen-positive rates after 18 months of deployment.ResultsSocial workers rated SBIRT-CDSS as easy to use and useful. Systems modeling projected SBIRT-CDSS adoption would increase screening rates, referral rates, and revenue—depending upon extent of CDSS usage. Among 1967 patients with an initial prenatal visit during the 18-month post-implementation period, 1721 (87.5%) had SBIRT screenings documented at that encounter. Among screened patients, 1331 (77.3%) had CPT codes entered and 422 (24.5%) screened positive.ConclusionsSBIRT-CDSS implementation facilitated screening, billing code entry, and substance use identification. Social workers reported high usability and workflow integration. Utilization is expected to enhance referral rates and financial sustainability.Keywords: Substance use, Addiction, Maternal health, Pregnancy, CDSS, Systems modeling

Introduction

Maternal mortality remains a public health crisis in the United States, with 18.6 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2023 (Hoyert, 2023). In 2022, substance use disorders (SUDs) contributed to 25% of pregnancy-related deaths (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). Among pregnancy-related mental health deaths, 51% had SUD as the underlying cause (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2026). These deaths are largely preventable, highlighting the need for effective screening and timely intervention (Hawk et al., 2015).

Maternal health providers play a critical role in identifying SUDs and facilitating access to treatment and support. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends universal screening for substance use during pregnancy as part of comprehensive obstetric care, to be conducted in partnership with the patient and in a supportive, nonpunitive manner (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, 2017). However, consistent use of evidence-based practices in routine care is often hindered by systemic obstacles, including lack of leadership engagement, limited adaptation of SUD screening to local needs, concerns about long-term financial viability, and insufficient organizational infrastructure (Adeniran et al., 2023, Vendetti et al., 2017).

Our previous work evaluated a universal screening tool and maternal health policy initiative to support equitable SUD identification among prenatal patients (Besculides et al., 2026). Screening was feasible, well-integrated into workflows, and accepted by providers, achieving near-universal uptake and improved equity. However, screen-positive rates were under 1%, lower than 13–42% reported in the literature using similar tools (Chasnoff et al., 2007, Reese et al., 2023, Ulrich et al., 2021).

Qualitative inquiry of our SUD screening initiative revealed that patients often remained reluctant to disclose substance use due to fear and stigma, the screening tool may not have supported open disclosure, and provider communication and relationships were critical to honest dialogue (Besculides et al., 2026). Prior research has consistently demonstrated that fear of anticipated legal repercussions and provider stigma are major barriers to disclosure of substance use during pregnancy, and that patient-centered, nonjudgmental screening approaches are recommended to improve trust and reporting (Weber et al., 2021, Oni et al., 2022).

Our findings coupled with previous research led us to explore other screening tools and ultimately to develop and implement a clinical decision support system (CDSS) to facilitate tool adoption. This paper evaluates the implementation and scalability of the CDSS.

Methods

Screening tool selection and CDSS development

To guide screening tool selection and workflow updates, we reviewed tools used in other settings and conducted a needs assessment with five informants (a hospital finance leader, two medical informaticists, and two maternal health clinical champions). Key insights emphasized adoption of the SBIRT (Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment) model, use of a validated screening tool to enable reimbursement and generate revenue, and integration of a CDSS to support workflow and consistent use (New York State Office of Addiction Services and Supports, 2025).

We selected the 5Ps (parents, peers, partner, past, present), a New York State–approved pregnancy-specific tool, for its comprehensive assessment of substance use and related risks (New York State Office of Addiction Services and Supports, 2025, nstitute for Health and Recovery, 2026). The 5Ps has been successfully used within SBIRT for perinatal populations and is rated more favorably by patients than alternatives (DiCarlo and Whiffen, 2024, Trocin et al., 2022). It has high sensitivity (0.80–0.88) but low specificity (0.35–0.37) (Ondersma et al., 2019); we prioritized sensitivity given institutional policy specifying that patients should not be reported to Child Protective Services (CPS) for substance use alone, with prior work demonstrating adherence to this policy (Besculides et al., 2026).

In collaboration with a clinical informaticist, we developed a CDSS incorporating the 5Ps to support SBIRT adoption among social workers at a single ambulatory Obstetrics and Gynecology (OBGYN) clinic in New York City. We focused on social workers because they are responsible for conducting the initial substance use screening at this clinic.

Implementation evaluation

To evaluate SBIRT-CDSS implementation we used a four phase, mixed-methods approach (Fig. 1). The evaluation was guided by two established frameworks: the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which posits that technology adoption is driven by users’ perceptions of usefulness and ease of use, and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), which facilitates exploration of multilevel determinants of implementation success (Tong et al., 2007). This article follows COREQ guidelines for reporting qualitative research (Appendix A).

Four-phase study design for SBIRT-CDSS implementation and evaluation. The diagram illustrates sequential study phases, including pre-implementation assessment, cognitive-based usability testing, systems modeling, and post-implementation evaluation of feasibility, utilization, and effectiveness.

Phase 1: pre-implementation perceived feasibility and utility (April–May 2024)

In Phase 1, we assessed the initial acceptability and usability of the SBIRT-CDSS. All clinic social workers responsible for prenatal substance use screenings, along with their supervisor, were invited by email to participate in the evaluation; all agreed to participate and provided informed consent.

Focus groups with the four social worker end-users and a semi-structured interview with the social worker supervisor (who does not conduct screenings) from the maternal health clinic were conducted via HIPAA-compliant Zoom™ and recorded with permission. Focus groups and interview were conducted in English using a semi-structured guide (Appendix B). Focus groups lasted approximately 90 min and the interview approximately 45 min. All participants received a gift card for their time. A qualitative research consultant (female licensed therapist without hierarchical power over participants) conducted focus groups with social worker end-users. The lead study author (a female qualitative expert with doctoral level training in behavioral science and no hierarchical power over participants) conducted the interview with the supervisor. The purpose of data collection was explained in the invitation and reiterated during interviews. Participants first completed the 12-item TAM survey, which includes six items each for perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU), scored on a 5-item Likert scale (Holden and Karsh, 2010). Qualitative data collection explored key adoption domains: integration and usability, training and support, privacy and data security, cultural/linguistic and stigma-related concerns, and anticipated impact on patient care. Rapid qualitative analysis, a widely used method for time-sensitive evaluations that emphasizes summarizing feedback over detailed coding or quote extraction, was employed (Kowalski et al., 2024). Findings were documented by the lead study author using a customized rapid analysis template, and meeting recordings were reviewed by a separate study author to ensure data accuracy. They independently synthesized responses by domain in a matrix across participants, meeting to establish consensus. Rapid analysis allowed for iterative refinement of the CDSS between phases.

Phase 2: cognitive-based assessments of the SBIRT-CDSS with mock patients (July 2024)

In Phase 2, four maternal health clinic social workers completed a facilitated cognitive walkthrough of the SBIRT-CDSS using mock patients; one participant differed from those in Phase 1 due to staff turnover. The social worker supervisor was excluded from the walkthrough and post-TAM survey because they do not conduct substance use screening. Another study author (female physician with clinical expertise, knowledge of SBIRT and with no hierarchical authority) facilitated these sessions. Social workers provided real-time feedback on usability, interface design, and training/support needs. Feedback was documented immediately after the session using the analysis template to inform system refinements. These sessions enabled iterative improvement before real-world SBIRT-CDSS deployment.

Phase 3: systems modeling (July 2024)

To quantify the impact of SBIRT-CDSS on workflow and projected revenue under varying levels of utilization, we conducted a systems modeling analysis using discrete event simulation (DES) (Cai and Jia, 2019). DES offers a virtual, low-risk environment for examining real-world processes, enabling researchers to test alternative implementation strategies, predict outcomes, and support the scalable deployment of interventions. This method aligns with CFIR domains, specifically Individuals (Innovation Recipients) and Intervention Characteristics (Innovation Relative Advantage and Cost) (Damschroder et al., 2022).

The DES model simulated the population of pregnant patients expected to receive care over a one-year period at the clinic. Two workflows were modeled for comparison: the standard SBIRT process without CDSS and the enhanced SBIRT-CDSS process. The simulation began with the patient’s initial clinical assessment and ended with referral to treatment or additional counseling, depending on the screening outcome.

In the model, patients were represented as dynamic entities moving through the SBIRT process, while social workers were modeled as resources responsible for completing necessary tasks and interventions. This patient-centered modeling approach reflected the usual practice within this setting wherein social workers conduct SUD screenings and facilitate appropriate follow-up care. The process flow diagram shown in Fig. 2 illustrates the full system architecture and the sequence of interactions between patients and social workers within the SBIRT-CDSS workflow.

Process flow diagram for SBIRT and SBIRT-CDSS. The diagram compares standard SBIRT and SBIRT-CDSS workflows, illustrating screening, brief intervention, referral processes, and patient flow, including decision points and billing integration in the CDSS pathway.

All patients entered the system through an initial assessment including 5Ps screening as part of routine prenatal intake. Patients were probabilistically assigned to one of two pathways: (i) the traditional SBIRT model without CDSS or (ii) the SBIRT-CDSS model. As part of the initial assessment, the 5Ps screening result determined whether a brief intervention should be initiated. If a brief intervention was not needed, the patient exited the system and the simulation ended.

Traditional SBIRT workflow

For patients following the traditional SBIRT without CDSS pathway, the first step was a screen for substance use and SUD-risk. If there was a positive screen, the next step involved was a brief intervention, followed by a referral to treatment, if clinically indicated. If no referral was needed, the patient exited the system. However, when a referral was warranted, the social worker must notify the physician or nurse practitioner, who was then responsible for initiating the referral. The simulation model included a potential delay or breakdown at this step, which led to the incorporation of a probability of missed or delayed referrals due to reliance on the provider’s subsequent action. Patients in need of treatment remained in the system until the referral was successfully made.

SBIRT-CDSS workflow

The SBIRT-CDSS pathway mirrored the traditional workflow but included several critical enhancements. In the initial assessment, patients underwent a structured screening for substance use and SUD-risk facilitated by the CDSS. For patients who screened positive, a brief intervention was administered. If further care was required, the referral was initiated directly by the social worker via the CDSS, bypassing the need for provider involvement and thereby reducing delays.

An additional feature of the SBIRT-CDSS model was billing integration. Diagnostic and procedure codes were entered into the system as part of the workflow, enabling real-time reimbursement for eligible services. This capability, identified as a major missed opportunity in the pre-implementation needs assessment, streamlined administrative processes and enhanced revenue capture. After a referral was made, patients received up to five additional counseling sessions, depending on clinical need. Billing occurred after the initial screening and brief intervention, as well as after any subsequent counseling sessions. Once all relevant services were completed and billing was finalized, the patient exited the SBIRT-CDSS simulation.

Outcomes tracked in the simulation included: the number of patients completing the SBIRT-CDSS process, total projected revenue, and number of missed or delayed referrals. Each outcome was assessed across multiple levels of SBIRT-CDSS utilization.

Simulation assumptions and parameters

All time estimates for process steps in the simulation were informed by expert opinion from a study author who has first-hand knowledge of the time needed for each step of the SBIRT process in prenatal patients and is the only practicing OB-GYN specializing in addiction medicine at the institution (see Table 1). Reimbursement estimates were based on SAMHSA Coding for Screening and Brief Intervention, which specifies billable services including CDSS-guided screening, brief interventions, referrals to treatment, and counseling sessions (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2024). For the traditional SBIRT model without CDSS, referral time was estimated at 2.5 min. However, the delay between social worker notification and provider-initiated referral might extend up to 7 days. We modeled a 5% probability of referrals being missed entirely in this workflow. The simulation assumed a population of 1873 patients, corresponding to unique patients with completed prenatal encounters at the clinic in 2023, and an average arrival rate of one patient per hour during clinic hours (9:00 AM–5:00 PM). The model was executed over a one-year period, with 10 replications conducted using Simio (version 17.261.39190) to ensure robustness of results (Simio, 2024).

Phase 4a: real-world feasibility and utility (August–September 2024)

After August 1, 2024, maternal health clinic social workers used the revised SBIRT‑CDSS during clinical encounters with patients. After one month of use, we administered a follow‑up survey in September 2024 to the four end‑users, capturing TAM post-use perceptions, integration of the tool into workflow, usability, remaining training/support needs, and open-ended feedback on their experience in general. Survey items were adapted and expanded using insights from earlier phases. For TAM-related questions, each respondent’s domain score was calculated by taking the median of their six responses per domain (PU and PEOU). Overall domain scores were calculated by aggregating medians across participants, as the median is the appropriate central tendency measure for ordinal Likert-scale data. Social workers received gift cards only after completing both the cognitive walkthrough (Phase 2) and the post-survey (Phase 4a).

Phase 4b: real-world utilization and effectiveness (August 2024–January 2026)

To evaluate system utilization and effectiveness over time, we retrospectively extracted encounter‑level data from the hospital’s electronic health record (EHR) (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI). We utilized a predefined Epic Hyperspace operational report to obtain item‑level SBIRT‑CDSS responses and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code entries. The analytic dataset included only prenatal social work encounters utilizing the SBIRT-CDSS at the maternal health clinic between August 1, 2024, and January 31, 2026, which was the period from CDSS launch through the most recent available data.

To determine monthly volumes of patients eligible for screening, we extracted all initial prenatal visits and aggregated them by date of service. Although the clinical workflow during the study period targeted SBIRT-CDSS administration during the initial prenatal social work encounter, screening could be completed at a follow-up prenatal social work assessment if time constraints prevented completion at the initial social work assessment. There was no EHR prompt or hard stop for incomplete SBIRT screening; however, the intake social work assessment (including SBIRT) is required for reimbursement, so social workers should determine screening status from documentation and complete screens at follow-up visits as needed.

The primary outcomes were SBIRT‑CDSS utilization, defined as the proportion of patients with an initial prenatal visit for whom CDSS tool data was recorded during their initial prenatal social work assessment, and screening effectiveness, defined as the proportion of screened patients with a positive substance use screen. Another outcome was CPT code entry, used as an indicator of complete SBIRT documentation and reimbursement‑eligible activity. We calculated average outcome rates for the full 18‑month intervention and for each 6‑month interval to evaluate how utilization evolved over time. Finally, we calculated screening rates for patients with an initial prenatal visit who were not screened at their initial social work assessment. These outcomes align with the CFIR’s Individuals (Innovation Recipients) and Process (Implementation) domains. All extracted variables were complete, with no missing data.

The medical school’s institutional review board approved the study (25–00133).

Results

Qualitative results

Social workers experience ranged from 1 to 7 years and all identified as female. Table 2 summarizes qualitative findings across phases of the SBIRT-CDSS evaluation and CFIR domains. SBIRT-CDSS was well-received by social workers, who described it as simple, intuitive, and well-integrated into their existing clinical workflow. When completing the survey after one month of use, one user remarked “It is easy to use, love the drop downs, culturally sensitive, and easy to engage patients.” Despite this overall enthusiasm, social workers identified potential barriers to implementation—particularly staff resistance to added responsibilities. To mitigate this barrier, they encouraged visible support from hospital leadership. To reduce user burden, social workers recommended aligning and streamlining questions across SBIRT and other routine assessments. One social worker noted “While I see the clinical importance of using SBIRT, it can sometimes be redundant since several of the SBIRT questions are already in our general new OB screening.” Social workers also stressed the importance of having a private space to conduct SBIRT, given the sensitivity of the topics. Ongoing training and a designated point person or system champion to answer questions and troubleshoot issues were cited as critical for sustained integration.

Although social workers expressed confidence in their ability to gather sensitive information and did not raise concerns about managing data security, they noted that patients may require reassurance about how their data will be used and protected, regardless of the screening tool. They also emphasized the importance of non-judgmental interpreters and culturally sensitive care to reduce stigma, ensuring patients feel safe and respected during screening. One social worker noted “Patients are largely understanding as to why this survey is important, but I feel that it could make some feel targeted or alienated somehow.” Finally, social workers appreciated the structured format of the SBIRT-CDSS, noting that it helped facilitate supportive conversations with patients while improving the quality and consistency of the screening.

Quantitative results

Systems modeling

The simulation model provided insight into how changes in SBIRT-CDSS use impact the number of patients guided through the process, missed referrals, and total revenue. (Table 3). A CDSS probability of 0 indicates that SBIRT without CDSS was used in 100% of cases, while a probability of 1 indicates SBIRT-CDSS was used in 100% of cases. As the probability of SBIRT-CDSS use increased relative to SBIRT without CDSS use, so did the number of patients who underwent SBIRT-CDSS screening. Total revenue also increased. As expected, the number of missed referrals decreased as more patients underwent SBIRT-CDSS screening. Without SBIRT-CDSS screening (and the resulting changes to treatment referrals), an estimated 9.4 cases would have been missed. Under a best‑case scenario of 100% SBIRT‑CDSS utilization, no treatment referrals would be missed, and revenue would be maximized through comprehensive billing capture.

TAM survey

Five social workers completed the TAM survey before deployment of SBIRT-CDSS at the maternal health clinic, and four completed it after one month of deployment. Before deployment, the median perceived usefulness score was 6.0 (interquartile range [IQR]: 5.0–7.0). After deployment, the median score remained 6.0 (IQR: 4.9–7.0). The median perceived ease of use score before deployment was 6.0 (IQR: 6.0–7.0). Following deployment, the median score increased to 6.5 (IQR: 6.0–7.0).

SBIRT-CDSS utilization

Over the 18-month study period after implementation of SBIRT-CDSS, the clinic recorded 1967 initial prenatal encounters. Longitudinal trends for screening, positivity, and CPT code documentation are illustrated in Fig. 3. Overall, 1721 (87.5%) SBIRT-CDSS screenings were documented for these patients during their initial prenatal social work assessments. This high utilization remained consistent across the six-month intervals (89.2%, 85.8%, and 87.5%). Among the 1721 screened at their initial prenatal social work assessment, 422 (24.5%) screened positive, with the positivity rate increasing over time (21.8% for the first 6 months, 22.7% over the second 6 months, and 28.9% for the final 6 months). CPT codes were documented for 1331 (77.3%) screens, with a higher rate in the final six-month period (94.0%) compared to earlier intervals (72.3% for the first 6 months and 64.9% over the second 6 months). All records with CPT codes also included ICD−10 codes.

Discussion

We report on the 18-month implementation of SBIRT-CDSS at a maternal health clinic. Prior to deployment, social workers shared their perspectives on workflow integration and engaged with a prototype to assess functionality and recommend improvements. This participatory design approach, shown to enhance healthcare delivery by ensuring technological tools are acceptable and usable, was a key component of our development (Bødker and Kyng, 2018, Boyd et al., 2012, IxDF - Interaction Design Foundation, 2023, Trischler et al., 2018). Consistent with this approach, social workers viewed SBIRT-CDSS as simple, intuitive, and well aligned with existing workflows, rating it highly on the TAM survey for ease of use and usefulness.

The results from the simulation model are consistent with those observed during the 18-month implementation period. The simulation framework enables evaluation of sustained SBIRT-CDSS utilization and quantifies the benefits of maintaining high levels of use.

Over the 18-month implementation, SBIRT-CDSS was used for 87.5% of initial prenatal visits, with high utilization across the entire study period. Previous studies support the acceptability of SBIRT and the 5Ps screening tool in perinatal care (Latiolais et al., 2022, Reese et al., 2023, Wouldes et al., 2021). SBIRT models for alcohol and drug use screening among pregnant women have shown acceptability and effectiveness (Wouldes et al., 2021). To our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the development and implementation of a CDSS-based SBIRT tool for pregnant populations while exploring how the tool impacts billing and reimbursement. This 18‑month observation period is valuable, as CDSS engagement is rarely examined beyond one year (Newton et al., 2025). A recent systematic review found that CDSS use barriers and facilitators evolve after initial rollout, with staffing turnover and onboarding of new users identified as factors that can weaken long‑term uptake (Newton et al., 2025). Although screening rates varied month to month, they remained consistently high throughout the follow‑up period.

CPT code entry occurred for 77% of SBIRT‑CDSS screenings overall, reaching 94% in the final six months of follow‑up. Higher CPT capture rate in the final six months may reflect increasing comfort with billing code entry. Maintaining this high rate of accurate CPT documentation is important for maximizing referral completion and financial sustainability.

Over the study period, 25% of those screened using the SBIRT-CDSS at their initial social work assessment screened positive. In contrast, a previous study using the 5Ps at multiple points in pregnancy reported a 42% positive rate, likely influenced by repeated screenings across gestation (Reese et al., 2023). Our goal was to improve detection over the prior screening tool (BOAS) used in our clinic, which was developed based on existing validated tools but was not re-validated (Besculides et al., 2026). BOAS produced a screen‑positive rate below 1%, suggesting that using the 5Ps within the SBIRT-CDSS substantially improved substance use identification. The higher positivity rate in the last six months may indicate increased substance use among patients, improved ability among social workers to elicit honest responses as their proficiency with the screening tool increased, or random variation.

Strengths of this study include use of multiple methods across timepoints to provide a comprehensive understanding of implementation and areas for improvement. Also, our systems modeling approach provides the opportunity to test different implementation strategies in a controlled environment at low cost. Systems modeling can be used to tailor implementation strategies for different clinical environments. Further, it allows for optimization of resources, services, and processes associated with CDSS implementation.

This study was limited by its single-clinic setting and focus on social workers conducting substance use screening during prenatal assessments; therefore, findings may not be fully generalizable to other clinical settings or maternal care teams with dissimilar screening processes and staff compositions. Finally, treatment engagement and referral outcomes were not assessed and will be examined in future phases as the SBIRT‑CDSS is scaled.

Future research will build on this early implementation to examine determinants of SBIRT-CDSS uptake and sustained use, including validated reimbursable screening tools and EHR-embedded workflows. Systems modeling can test implementation strategies under varying clinical conditions and identify the workflow adaptations, training needs, and operational investments required to achieve and sustain high SBIRT-CDSS use before broader real-world rollout. Additional studies should evaluate training approaches, financial sustainability, reasons for non-use during visits, and outcomes when expanding CDSS to other staff (e.g., nurses) and clinics.

Conclusion

The SBIRT-CDSS was implemented into an outpatient maternal health clinic and demonstrated usability, workflow integration, and acceptability amongst social workers. Data and systems modeling suggest it may improve substance use identification, increase referrals, and enhance financial sustainability. By enabling reimbursement, it also addressed gaps in leadership and organizational support. Continued refinement and broader implementation may support scalable, sustainable approaches to substance use identification and management during pregnancy.
