Video-assisted thoracoscopic sublobar versus lobar resection for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis: a 15-year single-center cohort study
Authors: Libing Yang, Chao Guo, Xuehan Gao, Lei Liu, Yeye Chen, Shanqing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is a progressive fungal lung disease that often requires surgical intervention. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has emerged as a minimally invasive approach, and sublobar resection may offer potential benefits in selected patients by preserving lung parenchyma. This study aimed to describe the perioperative and long-term outcomes of VATS for CPA, with exploratory comparisons between sublobar and lobar resections.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 151 patients with CPA who underwent VATS lung resection, categorized into simple aspergilloma (SA), chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA), and chronic necrotizing pulmonary aspergillosis (CNPA). Perioperative and long-term outcomes were analyzed across CPA subtypes and resection extents, and compared with a propensity score-matched lung cancer cohort. To account for treatment-selection imbalance in the sublobar-versus-lobectomy comparison, propensity score analyses were additionally performed within the CPA cohort.ResultsOf 151 patients, 90 had SA, 57 had CCPA, and 4 had CNPA. VATS was completed without conversion to open thoracotomy in all cases. Compared with the matched lung cancer cohort, CPA surgeries had longer operative times, more severe pleural adhesions, and higher postoperative complication rates. Among CPA patients, sublobar resection (n = 49) was associated with lower chest drainage volumes compared with lobectomy (n = 102), with similar overall complication rates (10.2% vs. 11.7%). Among patients with available paired spirometry, pulmonary function decline was smaller after sublobar resection, and this difference persisted in exploratory propensity-adjusted analyses. Long-term follow-up was available for 136 of 151 (90.1%) patients at a median of 27 months; recurrence was documented in 15 (11.0%) patients, with numerically similar rates between resection groups. Lesion diameter > 3 cm and severe pleural adhesions were independent risk factors for postoperative complications; SA subtype was independently protective.ConclusionsIn selected patients with CPA, VATS appears feasible and safe. Sublobar resection could be a plausible lung-sparing strategy for patients with localized disease; however, comparisons with lobectomy remain exploratory because of the retrospective design, potential selection bias and limited recurrence events. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized selection criteria and longer follow-up are needed to define the role of limited resection in CPA management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13019-026-04534-7.

Introduction

Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis (CPA) is a progressive fungal lung disease associated with substantial morbidity that often requires long-term management. It commonly presents with chronic cough, hemoptysis, and constitutional symptoms — such as fatigue, fever, and weight loss [1]. In particular, hemoptysis can be life-threatening in a subset of patients and often prompts clinical intervention [2].

CPA typically arises in individuals with preexisting structural lung abnormalities, such as prior pulmonary tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. The incidence of CPA is increasing, likely due to aging populations, an increased prevalence of chronic lung diseases, and prolonged survival among immunocompromised individuals [3]. According to the 2017 ESCMID-ECMM-ERS guidelines, CPA encompasses four subtypes: simple aspergilloma (SA), chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA), chronic fibrosing pulmonary aspergillosis (CFPA), and chronic necrotizing pulmonary aspergillosis (CNPA) [4].

Antifungal therapy remains the cornerstone of CPA management, with oral triazoles — such as voriconazole — serving as first-line agents. However, antifungal treatments are sometimes limited by adverse drug reactions, interactions with other medications, or the emergence of drug resistance. In such cases, particularly in patients with localized lesions or recurrent hemoptysis, surgical resection may be required to achieve sustained clinical improvement and reduce the fungal burden [5].

While surgical resection can be an effective intervention in select patients [6–8], it carries greater procedural risks than medical therapy. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has emerged as a less invasive alternative to thoracotomy, offering advantages such as reduced postoperative pain and faster recovery [8, 9]. However, VATS for CPA is often technically more challenging than routine lung cancer surgery due to dense pleural adhesions and anatomical distortion from chronic infection and inflammation, which can increase the risk of intraoperative complications and conversion to open thoracotomy.

The extent of the pulmonary resection of CPA remains a subject of ongoing debate. While lobectomy is frequently performed, sublobar resection may preserve lung function while providing acceptable outcomes in selected patients, particularly in patients with localized lesions or reduced pulmonary function. However, evidence directly comparing the lobar and sublobar approaches in CPA remains limited.

To address these questions, we analyzed perioperative and long-term outcomes of VATS for CPA over a 15-year period. Our primary aim was to describe surgical outcomes across CPA subtypes and to explore whether sublobar resection can provide acceptable results in carefully selected patients. We additionally investigated risk factors for postoperative complications to inform individualized surgical decision-making.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital; informed consent was obtained from all participants. We retrospectively reviewed patients diagnosed with CPA who underwent lung resection via VATS in the Department of Thoracic Surgery between January 2010 and December 2024. Those who underwent primary thoracotomy (n = 17) were not included in this study. All included patients completed the planned VATS procedure without intraoperative conversion to thoracotomy. Therefore, the cohort represents selected patients in whom a VATS approach was attempted and successfully completed, rather than the full surgical population with CPA.

The clinical data were extracted from an institutional Artificial Intelligence-Enhanced Lung Disease Database. CPA was diagnosed based on clinical symptoms, laboratory findings, sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid culture results, and computed tomography (CT). Final diagnoses were confirmed through postoperative pathological examination. For pathological assessment, complete (R0) resection was defined as macroscopically complete resection with no fungal involvement at the parenchymal or bronchial margin on final pathology. Pulmonary function outcomes were assessed using paired spirometry (FEV1 and FEV1% predicted) obtained preoperatively and at the first postoperative reassessment approximately 3–6 months after surgery, when available.

Surgical procedure and perioperative management

All patients were initially evaluated by respiratory specialists, and antifungal therapy was initiated when appropriate. After medical optimization, thoracic surgeons conducted surgical assessments to determine resectability. Preoperative optimization strategies included pulmonary rehabilitation, cessation of smoking and alcohol use, and nutritional support when necessary. As this was a retrospective study, no prospectively standardized surgical selection algorithm was applied. The choice between sublobar resection and lobectomy was made on a case-by-case basis, guided by disease extent, lesion location and size, anatomic feasibility, anticipated completeness of macroscopic resection, and the patient’s cardiopulmonary reserve. Sublobar resection was more often considered for peripheral and localized lesions, whereas lobectomy was generally preferred for more extensive parenchymal destruction, centrally located disease, or when limited resection was unlikely to achieve adequate source control.

Early postoperative ambulation was encouraged. Chest radiography was routinely performed 1 day postoperatively to evaluate the lung re-expansion and drainage status. Chest tubes were removed when drainage was < 200 mL/day and no air leak or chylothorax was present.

Postoperative antifungal therapy was prescribed by respiratory physicians based on the CPA subtypes and clinical condition. Patients were scheduled for clinic or telephone follow-up at regular intervals after surgery. Loss to follow-up was defined a priori as no documented clinic/telephone contact beyond 6 months after surgery despite repeated contact attempts.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) and Prism (version 9.2.0) software. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means ± standard deviations (SDs) based on distribution and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s t-test as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.

To compare CPA surgeries with routine surgeries of lung cancer, a cohort of patients with lung cancer was selected using propensity score matching (PSM). Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression based on age, sex, Charlson Comorbidity Index, Forced Expiratory Volume in one second (FEV1), smoking history, lesion size, and resection extent. We performed 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement using a caliper width 0.2 times the SD of the logit of the propensity score. The covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 indicating adequate balance.

To address confounding by indication in the comparison between sublobar resection and lobectomy, we additionally performed propensity score analyses within the CPA cohort. Because the matched cohort was already small and the number of recurrence events was limited, further expansion of the propensity score model to include more granular variables would likely have reduced matching feasibility, substantially decreased the number of matched pairs, and increased the risk of model instability and overfitting. Therefore, propensity scores for receiving sublobar resection were estimated by multivariable logistic regression using age, sex, CPA subtype, lesion diameter, history of tuberculosis, severe pleural adhesions, and preoperative FEV1% predicted. We then performed 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement with a caliper of 0.2 SD of the logit of the propensity score. Covariate balance after matching was assessed by standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 considered acceptable. In the matched cohort, we compared postoperative complications, chest drainage, R0 resection, recurrence, and pulmonary function change. Therefore, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory sensitivity analyses rather than causal estimates.

Logistic regression was used to identify the predictors of postoperative complications. We fitted two multivariable logistic models: a full model including all clinically relevant covariates (sex, age ≥ 60, bronchiectasis, tuberculosis, emphysema, CPA subtype, diameter > 3 cm, severe adhesions, and resection extent), and a parsimonious model including predictors with p < 0.10 on univariate screening.

For recurrence analyses, patients without a documented event were censored at their last recorded contact. We did not perform imputation given the retrospective design. To assess potential bias from attrition, we compared baseline and key surgical characteristics between followed and lost-to-follow-up patients and found no significant measured differences. However, informative censoring, differential recurrence detection, and underestimation of recurrence remain possible. Given the small number of recurrence events, comparisons of recurrence should be interpreted as exploratory tests for statistically detectable differences, not as evidence of equivalent recurrence risk.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 151 patients who underwent VATS lung resection for CPA were included in the study. Patients were classified into three CPA subtypes — SA (n = 90), CCPA (n = 57), and CNPA (n = 4) according to the 2017 ESCMID-ECMM-ERS guidelines [4]. The demographic characteristics of all patients are summarized in Table 1. Patients with SA and CCPA both presented with a median age of 47 years, whereas the small CNPA cohort was significantly younger (median age, 39 years). Notably, women were predominant across all subtypes. The most common symptoms were cough and hemoptysis, with approximately half of the patients exhibiting these symptoms at disease onset. Other symptoms — including fever, chest pain, and dyspnea — were less common, with no significant differences between the subtypes. Comorbidities including bronchiectasis, emphysema, history of tuberculosis, and immune diseases were not significantly different between the SA and CCPA groups; however, diabetes occurred more frequently in the CCPA group (17.5% vs. 4.4%; p < 0.05). The lesions were most commonly located in the lower lobes, with no significant difference in distribution between the groups.

Comparisons between patients with CPA and lung cancer

Using 1:2 PSM, 151 patients with CPA and 302 patients with lung cancer were included for comparison. Compared with lung cancer surgeries, CPA procedures had a longer operative time and a higher incidence of severe pleural adhesions. Total hospital stay was significantly longer in the CPA group, whereas postoperative stay was similar. Patients who underwent VATS for CPA also had a higher rate of postoperative complications than those who underwent VATS for lung cancer (11.2% vs. 4.3%; p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Comparisons between lobectomy and sublobar resection in patients with CPA

Among the 151 patients with CPA, 102 and 49 underwent lobectomy and sublobar resection, respectively. The lesion diameter was numerically smaller in the sublobar group (p = 0.05); additionally, the distribution of CPA subtypes differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.01), with SA being more common in the sublobar group, while CCPA and CNPA were more common in the lobectomy group. Regarding perioperative outcomes, the average daily chest drainage volume was significantly lower in the sublobar group, and other perioperative parameters were comparable between the groups (Table 3). The overall incidence of postoperative complications was similar between the two groups (11.7% vs. 10.2%; p = 0.44). Twelve complications were observed in the lobectomy group, including prolonged air leakage (n = 4), poor wound healing (n = 2), atrial fibrillation (n = 2), lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis (DVT; n = 3), and pulmonary infection (n = 1). Five complications were observed in the sublobar group, including prolonged air leak (n = 3), DVT (n = 1), and poor wound healing (n = 1). No perioperative mortality occurred in either group. R0 resection rates were high in both groups without a statistically significant difference. Among patients with paired pulmonary function data, the postoperative decline in FEV1 and FEV1% predicted was smaller after sublobar resection (Table 3).

Given the baseline imbalance between the two groups, we further performed an exploratory propensity score-matched analysis within the CPA cohort. The matched cohort included 39 pairs. After matching, postoperative complications remained numerically similar between the two procedures, while the lower drainage volume and smaller decline in pulmonary function after sublobar resection persisted (Table 4).

Risk factors for postoperative complications in patients with CPA

Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed a history of tuberculosis, lesion diameter > 3 cm, and severe pleural adhesions to be significantly associated with increased postoperative complications. By contrast, SA was associated with a significantly lower risk of complications (Fig. 1).

Considering the potential correlations among these clinical variables, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. In both parsimonious and full multivariable model, a lesion diameter > 3 cm and severe pleural adhesions remained independent risk factors, whereas SA remained an independent protective factor against postoperative complications (Table 5).

Long-term therapy and prognosis in patients with CPA

Among the 151 patients with CPA, long-term follow-up data were available for 136/151 (90.1%) patients, including 91/102 (89.2%) in the lobectomy group and 45/49 (91.8%) in the sublobar resection group. The median follow-up duration was 27 (IQR, 14–44) months. Postoperative antifungal therapy, primarily with oral triazoles, was administered numerically more frequently after lobectomy than after sublobar resection, consistent with the higher proportion of more advanced CPA subtypes in the lobectomy group. The median duration of postoperative antifungal therapy was 4.5 (IQR, 1.0–8.0) months after lobectomy and 2.0 (IQR, 0.5–6.0) months after sublobar resection.

During follow-up, recurrence was documented in 15/136 (11.0%) patients, including 11/91 (12.1%) in the lobectomy group and 4/45 (8.9%) in the sublobar resection group (Table 6). Among the recurrent cases, 5 occurred in patients with SA, 8 in patients with CCPA, and 2 in patients with CNPA. In the lobectomy group, recurrence developed in 4 SA, 5 CCPA, and 2 CNPA cases, whereas in the sublobar resection group, recurrence occurred in 1 SA and 3 CCPA cases. The median time to recurrence was 18 (IQR, 10–27) months. All recurrent cases were managed conservatively with prolonged antifungal therapy; no patient underwent reoperation for recurrence, and no CPA-related death was observed during follow-up.

To further assess the robustness of the long-term findings, we performed an exploratory propensity score-matched analysis restricted to patients with available long-term follow-up. In this matched analysis, recurrence outcomes remained numerically similar between lobectomy and sublobar resection (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

This study provided a comprehensive analysis of the short- and long-term surgical outcomes of CPA managed using VATS. Our findings suggest that VATS is feasible and safe among selected patients, and that sublobar resection may be feasible as a parenchyma-sparing approach in patients with localized disease. We also identified a lesion diameter > 3 cm and severe pleural adhesions to be significant predictors of postoperative complications, whereas SA appeared to be protective against complications.

The clinical characteristics observed among patients with CPA in our cohort — including common symptoms such as cough and hemoptysis, and a predominance of female patients — are consistent with those of previous reports, particularly among patients with SA and CNPA [7, 10]. This sex distribution warrants further exploration, as it may provide insights into susceptibility and disease progression mechanisms.

Using a matched lung cancer cohort as a contextual benchmark, we found that VATS for CPA was associated with greater perioperative complexity than routine lung cancer resection, including more severe pleural adhesions, longer operative time, and a higher complication rate. This is clinically plausible given the chronic inflammation, prior pulmonary infection, and dense adhesions commonly seen in CPA, which may increase operative difficulty and contribute to postoperative morbidity such as prolonged air leak [9, 11–14]. Nevertheless, perioperative mortality was not observed in our cohort. These findings are broadly consistent with those of Patel et al., who also supported the feasibility of a VATS approach for pulmonary aspergillosis in a large Western cohort [8]. Importantly, our study excluded 17 patients who underwent primary thoracotomy, and no conversion occurred among included cases; thus, the results describe a highly selected institutional VATS experience rather than the outcomes of all surgically treated patients with CPA. Together, these data suggest that VATS is feasible for selected patients with CPA, although it is technically more demanding than routine lung cancer surgery and requires careful preoperative selection.

The optimal extent of lung resection for pulmonary aspergillosis remains debated, particularly in patients with compromised pulmonary reserve. Balancing source control against preservation of functional lung parenchyma is especially important in patients with underlying lung disease. Notably, in the context of the existing literature, our cohort represents one of the larger published experiences of sublobar resection for CPA, allowing a more systematic assessment of parenchyma-sparing surgery in real-world practice [7, 13, 15]. In our cohort, patients selected for sublobar resection had lower chest drainage volumes, which may reflect less extensive adhesiolysis, a shorter staple-line burden, more limited hilar manipulation, and the fact that these patients more often had localized SA. At the same time, baseline differences between the resection groups were substantial: the sublobar cohort had smaller lesions, a higher proportion of SA, and no CNPA, representing a fundamentally less severe disease spectrum than the lobectomy cohort. Accordingly, the similar crude complication rates between procedures are best interpreted as real-world outcomes arising from different clinical indications, rather than as evidence of equivalent treatment effect.

With respect to long-term outcomes, the high follow-up completeness in our cohort provides a relatively robust assessment of postoperative disease control after surgery for CPA. Recurrence was uncommon in both the lobectomy and sublobar groups, and the observed recurrence rates were numerically similar between the two procedures. In parallel, among patients with available paired spirometry, sublobar resection was associated with a smaller postoperative decline in pulmonary function, which is consistent with the expected physiologic benefit of parenchymal preservation. These findings support the potential value of sublobar resection in carefully selected patients in whom complete lesion removal is achievable. However, because only 15 recurrence events were observed and postoperative pulmonary function data were available only in a subset of patients, the recurrence and functional findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered definitive evidence of equivalent recurrence risk or confirmed functional superiority.

The interpretation of R0 resection in CPA also differs from that in malignant disease. A positive fungal margin does not imply microscopic tumor persistence, but may indicate residual infected cavity wall or fungal involvement at the bronchial or parenchymal margin. In this cohort, non-R0 resections occurred only after lobectomy and were generally associated with more extensive disease; these patients received continued postoperative antifungal therapy. The clinical implications of fungal margin positivity, including its relationship with recurrence and antifungal duration, remain uncertain and require further study.

Given the marked baseline imbalance between the two groups, particularly with respect to SA proportion, lesion size, and overall disease severity, we further performed exploratory propensity-adjusted analyses within the CPA cohort. In both the perioperative and long-term comparisons, the adjusted results were broadly consistent with the unadjusted analyses; after partially accounting for major observable differences, sublobar resection showed no evident increase in perioperative morbidity or recurrence (Tables 4 and 6, and Supplementary Table S1). In addition, the smaller postoperative decline in pulmonary function after sublobar resection suggests a potential functional advantage. Although these analyses remain exploratory and cannot substitute for prospective or multicenter comparative data, their overall consistency lends further support to the interpretation that sublobar resection may be a reasonable individualized lung-sparing option in appropriately selected patients.

Previous studies have identified a range of clinical factors associated with postoperative CPA complications. Diabetes has been consistently reported as a significant risk factor and is often associated with a higher incidence of prolonged air leaks and pulmonary infections in patients with advanced CPA [14]. Additionally, comorbidities such as cancer and immunosuppression have been shown to increase the risk of postoperative infections, while a history of tuberculosis and impaired pulmonary function has been associated with an increased risk of respiratory failure, prolonged air leaks, and postoperative infections [15]. In our study, severe pleural adhesions and lesion diameters > 3 cm were independent risk factors for postoperative complications, whereas SA emerged as an independent protective factor. These findings align with and extend previous research by integrating anatomical and radiographic characteristics into risk stratification, thereby contributing additional insights to guide surgical decision-making and perioperative management.

Only four patients with CNPA were included in this cohort, so no meaningful subgroup conclusion can be drawn. Recurrence occurred in two of the three CNPA patients with long-term follow-up, consistent with the concern that diffuse and progressive disease may be less amenable to complete surgical source control [4, 13]. Decisions regarding surgery for CNPA should be individualized within multidisciplinary discussion.

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design over a 15-year period introduces selection bias and potential temporal confounding related to evolving surgical techniques, perioperative care, and antifungal strategies. Second, the study evaluated patients in whom VATS was attempted and completed successfully; the 17 patients who underwent primary thoracotomy were excluded, and the absence of conversion likely reflects a highly selective institutional strategy. Therefore, the findings should not be generalized to all surgically treated patients with CPA. Third, and most importantly, the comparison between sublobar resection and lobectomy is subject to substantial confounding by indication: patients selected for sublobar resection had smaller lesions, a markedly higher proportion of SA, and no CNPA, indicating a fundamentally different disease severity profile. Although exploratory propensity score analyses were performed to reduce observable imbalance, the matched cohort included only 39 pairs and several potentially important confounders were not included. Fourth, while long-term follow-up was available for a high proportion of patients, only 15 recurrence events were observed; therefore, the recurrence analysis was underpowered, the possibility of a type II error cannot be excluded, and recurrence ascertainment may still have been incomplete. Fifth, because this was a retrospective database study, standardized criteria for choosing VATS versus thoracotomy and sublobar resection versus lobectomy were not prospectively defined. Sixth, paired pulmonary function data were available in only part of the cohort.

To assess potential selection bias in the pulmonary function analysis, we compared baseline characteristics between patients with and without paired pulmonary function data (Supplementary Table S2). The two groups were broadly comparable in age, lesion diameter, and CPA subtype distribution, although patients with paired PFT data showed numerically lower baseline FEV1 and FEV1% predicted. Therefore, the pulmonary function findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory evidence rather than confirmatory proof of functional superiority. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized selection criteria, complete pathology verification, comprehensive functional assessment, and longer follow-up are needed to define the role of sublobar resection in CPA management.

Conclusions

In selected patients with CPA, VATS appears to be a feasible and safe surgical approach. For localized disease, particularly simple aspergilloma, sublobar resection may serve as a reasonable parenchyma-sparing strategy when complete macroscopic resection is achievable, with favorable perioperative and functional outcomes observed in this cohort. However, the comparison with lobectomy remains exploratory and hypothesis-generating because of retrospective selection bias, the small propensity-matched cohort, limited recurrence events, incomplete pulmonary function data, and unmeasured confounding. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized selection criteria, comprehensive functional assessment, and longer follow-up are needed to better define the role of limited resection in CPA management.
