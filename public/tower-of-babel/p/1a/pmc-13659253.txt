Structural Variation Sequencing of 26 Amniotic Fluid Samples With Partial Gene Duplications and Postnatal Follow‐Up of the Fetuses
Authors: Shengfang Qin, Xueyan Wang, Mengjia Yan, Yuxia He, Zhuo Zhang, Yan Yin, Jin Wang, Beibei Guo, Jing Wang, Na Xi, Zhaokai Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPartial gene duplications (PGDups) are a significant contributor to genetic disease. The precise genomic location and structure of PGDups are often unresolved using conventional methods, so prenatal diagnosis for PGDups is challenging, especially without ultrasound abnormalities.MethodsWe retrospectively applied structural variation sequencing (SVseq) to 26 amniotic fluid samples with PGDups initially identified by chromosomal microarray or sequencing. SVseq utilized mate‐pair library construction and high‐throughput sequencing to map PGDup structures. Pathogenicity was classified using ACMG guidelines, followed by postnatal phenotypic follow‐up.ResultsSVseq deciphered the PGDup structure for all twenty‐six cases. Twenty‐two (84.62%) were tandem duplications (TDs), three (11.54%) chromosomal complex rearrangements (CCRs), and one (3.85%) had no duplication. Among twenty‐two TDs, thirteen were extragenic (TDEG), preserving gene integrity, and were classified as benign or variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Nine were intragenic (TDIG), disrupting gene structure, and were rated pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) or VUS. Postnatal follow‐up revealed obvious abnormal phenotypes in only two TDIG cases (one inherited and one de novo).ConclusionSVseq effectively resolves PGDup location and structure, allowing confident pathogenicity assessment and clear genotype‐phenotype correlation. SVseq is a robust method for prenatal PGDup evaluation that could be adopted in diagnostic protocols to improve clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Partial gene duplications (PGDups), which involve small regions of DNA, are considered a significant component of the mutation spectrum responsible for genetic diseases. In the 1990s, PGDup was first recognized as a potential cause of human diseases [1]. The first documented case of PGDup involved a male patient with Lesch‐Nyhan syndrome (#300322), resulting from a deficiency in hypoxanthine‐guanine phosphoryltransferase (HPRT) caused by a duplication of two exons in the HPRT gene [2]. Currently, PGDup has been established as being associated with several genetic disorders [3, 4, 5, 6].

Based on the position relation to genes, PGDup can be classified as intragenic or extragenic duplication (including regulatory elements). Intragenic duplications involve the 5′ or 3′ end or partial exons. Compared with gene deletions that directly disrupt gene structure, the impact of duplication on gene function can vary significantly, influenced by factors such as location, breakpoints, and reading frame. Different types of gene duplication can have varying effects on mRNA expression levels, which may result in different clinical outcomes [7]. Therefore, the interpretation of partial gene duplications can be challenging and complicated [8]. To evaluate their pathogenicity, several factors must be considered: (1) Whether it affects the duplication of amino acids in the protein or alters the protein's conformation, leading to instability or dysfunction. (2) Whether it causes a frameshift in the mRNA reading frame, resulting in truncated and dysfunctional proteins. (3) Whether fusion genes are created by merging genes at breakpoints, which can produce abnormal proteins that contribute to diseases. (4) Whether it forms compound heterozygous variations alongside defective alleles [9]. (5) Whether it induces complex chromosomal rearrangements (CCR) (such as triplications and/or inversions) that have multifaceted effects. Thus, determining the direction and location of PGDup is a crucial prerequisite for understanding its potential adverse effects on gene expression and its association with disease phenotypes [8, 10].

PGDup usually involves small fragments that cannot be identified using traditional cytogenetic methods. With the application of chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) technology and low‐pass copy number variation sequencing (CNVseq) in clinical practice, it is now possible to routinely detect small copy number variations (CNVs) (with a size ranging from 50 to 100 kilobases) throughout the entire genome. Most diseases associated with PGDup are non‐recurrent, random, and rare [11], highlighting the importance of routine PGDup analysis in genetic testing. Moreover, although the frequency of gene duplications is lower than that of partial gene deletions, detecting and quantifying PGDup remains challenging. Methods such as CMA and CNVseq can identify PGDup by detecting copy‐number changes. However, these approaches generally provide limited information about the genomic architecture of the duplicated segment, including breakpoint locations, orientations, and insertion sites [8]. Consequently, for PGDup identified through the above methods, it is essential to ascertain gene breakpoints, locations, and orientations using available methods. Currently, Optical Genome Mapping (OGM) and Electronic Genome Mapping (EGM) are emerging techniques to resolve large structural variants and may facilitate breakpoint delineation and structural characterization of PGDups. Specifically, OGM can detect structural variations of 500 bp or more, while EGM can detect those of 300 bp or more [12, 13]. However, their high instrument and detection costs, coupled with technical complexity, limit their clinical application and can delay timely molecular diagnoses for some patients.

Structural variation sequencing (SVseq) is a high‐throughput technique for detecting chromosomal structural variations (SVs) [14, 15]. By utilizing mate‐pair libraries, SVseq identifies chromosomal changes, enabling precise analysis of SVs. This Mate‐pair sequencing strategy allows SVseq to achieve long‐read effects similar to those seen in third‐generation single‐molecule sequencing, yet it operates on short‐read NGS platforms. Clinically, SVseq outperforms traditional karyotyping, increasing the detection rate of chromosomal abnormalities by 11.7% [16]. It has identified clinically significant SVs in 17.8% of infertile men [14], pinpointing precise genetic breakpoints involved in sperm production. Furthermore, SVseq has guided preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) [17], improved the molecular diagnosis of acute myeloid leukemia by 11% [18], and revealed cryptic genomic complexity in various prenatal and de novo chromosomal rearrangements [19].

To evaluate the clinical value of the SVseq for detecting PGDup location in prenatal diagnosis, we retrospectively analyze 26 cases of PGDup previously classified as Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS) by CMA or CNVseq. Additionally, we carried out comprehensive follow‐up evaluations for these children with PGDup during the early postnatal period.

Materials and Methods

Sample Screening

We conducted a retrospective study of 26 amniotic fluid samples that showed genetic VUS detected by CMA or CNVseq during prenatal diagnosis. This study will focus on incomplete gene region duplications, including the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) plus the beginning or middle part of gene exons that involve haploinsufficient genes.

SVseq Analysis and Breakpoints Validation

The SVseq detection technology, based on the MP Library Prep Kit (GeneTech Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China), detects large‐span genomic fragments. First, genomic DNA is randomly fragmented to a specific size (2–8 kb, mostly around 5 kb). Next, after end repair, biotin labeling, and DNA strand ligation, both ends of the DNA fragments were sequenced in PE150 mode on the DNBSEQ‐T7 platform. Sequencing is performed to achieve an average coverage greater than 10×. After filtering out sequencing headers and low‐quality reads, the high‐quality paired reads are aligned to the NCBI Human Reference Genome (GRCh37) using BWA, retaining only those with unique mappings for further analysis [16, 20, 21]. All CNVs and SVs were analyzed using the paired‐read approach.

To accurately locate the breakpoints in these TDIG cases, Sanger sequencing was used for validation, as in our previous research [19]. In the case of homologous pseudogenes and high GC content interference in the PKD1 gene, targeted long‐read sequencing was employed for verification, following the method reported by others [22, 23].

Pathogenicity Assessment and Clinical Outcome Follow‐Up

The pathogenicity of PGDup was evaluated according to the ACMG guidelines [23, 24]. This evaluation considered factors such as the dose‐effect relationship of PGDup‐related genes, gene structure, sequencing results, gene position and arrangement, effects on reading frames, parental comparisons, family history, and clinical phenotypes from follow‐up. Following this, clinical follow‐up for carriers of 26 PGDup included assessments of child health indicators, clinical evaluations, laboratory results, and telephone interviews. The shortest follow‐up period was 100 days; the longest was 6 years 2 months.

Result

SVseq analysis determined the location and arrangement of the PGDup gene in 26 cases. After pathogenicity analysis, we provided detailed genetic counseling and obtained informed consent from the participants. Clinical follow‐ups were performed simultaneously. Of 26 PGDup cases, 19 were inherited from their parents, and 7 were de novo.

Among the 26 cases, 13 were TDEG of the target gene. TDEG carriers were inherited from parents without abnormal traits and did not develop clinical phenotypes during follow‐up. These carriers were rated as benign (B). De novo TDEG, even without abnormal phenotypes during follow‐up, could not rule out pathogenicity and was classified as a VUS (Table 1). Nine cases involved TDIG, which lacked a complete gene structure and had partial exon duplications in TRPS1, CDKL5, DOCK8, GLI2, NEXMIF, KMT2C, and DMD genes, and were assigned pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) or VUS; two TDIG cases (Cases 5 and 10) exhibited abnormal phenotypes, one inherited from her mother (Case 5) and one de novo (Case 10) (Table 2).

Discussion

Currently, there is a significant lack of understanding of PGDup across the vast variations of the human genome, particularly during the prenatal stage. Most existing research has focused on specific genetic diseases, such as DMD (#310200) [25, 26]. The pathogenicity of PGDup, identified by conventional methods, remains uncertain because we cannot ascertain the specific physical location, the pattern of these duplications within the gene, or their impact on the gene's reading frame. While most duplications are end‐to‐end and near the original site [10], a few PGDups can lead to reading‐frame displacement and truncation, resulting in nonfunctional proteins. Therefore, understanding the clinical implications of PGDup and analyzing it at the breakpoint level is essential. To achieve this, we utilized the SVseq technology to analyze 26 cases of PGDup detected by CMA or CNVseq. The results revealed 22 occurrences of TD (84.62%), approximately the same proportion as the 83% of TDs reported by Newman Scott et al. [10] through next‐generation sequencing and WGS.

Different types of PGDup have varying effects on diseases and clinical outcomes. Pathogenicity analysis and clinical follow‐up of 26 cases revealed that 13 TDEG patients had complete gene copies on both alleles, and no clinical phenotypes were observed during follow‐up, rated as B or VUS. Additionally, nine cases involving TDIG (lacking complete gene structure) resulted in partial exon duplications of the TRPS1, CDKL5, DOCK8, RPS6KA3, DMD, GLI2, AEXMIF, and KMT2C genes, which were assigned pathogenicity ratings of P, LP, or VUS (Table 2). These TDIG cases can disrupt the gene's reading frame, potentially resulting in truncated or dysfunctional proteins [27]. They may also lead to increased gene expression via intra‐gene cis‐regulatory elements or to proteins that elongate and acquire abnormal cellular localization. These can produce effects similar to those seen with haploinsufficiency or result in a gain of function [28]. The literature indicates that most TDIGs are considered pathogenic variants [29, 30, 31].

In this study, Case 5 describes an E2‐5 duplication of the TRPS1 gene (AD). The child exhibited a typical clinical phenotype after birth. Related literature indicates that tandem duplications of exons 2‐4 of the TRPS1 gene can lead to abnormal transcripts, including altered splicing patterns in exons 4–2 and 4–3. Patients often present with characteristic ectodermal, facial, and skeletal abnormalities associated with TRPS1 [32, 33]. Case 10 involves a duplication of the CDKL5 gene (AD) from exons 2 to 18, including the entire coding sequence, resulting in structural damage to the gene. The child also presented a typical clinical phenotype after birth. Hiatt et al. [34] reported clinical cases resulting from a duplication of exon 3 of this gene. Currently, this gene is not known to be triplosensitive. However, the literature reports that seven cases of this gene duplication across three families all exhibited CDLK5‐related phenotypes. It is speculated that this gene may exhibit a triplosensitivity effect [35]. Additionally, numerous studies have shown that 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) variants play a significant role in regulating the gene, causing changes in gene expression and human phenotype, which are important factors in human evolution and diseases [36, 37]. The second breakpoint of this tandem duplication is in the 3′UTR region. An incomplete 3′ UTR may also contribute to the functional regulation of this gene. Based on the available evidence, this case is only rated as VUS. After conducting relevant functional research, it may be possible to upgrade it to LP or P.

Case 23 is a female carrier with a DMD gene E10‐44 duplication and no clinical abnormalities. This region's duplication is associated with Becker Muscular Dystrophy (BMD) (#300376) [38, 39, 40, 41]. Case 24, a female carrier with a DMD E38‐44 duplication, also has no clinical symptoms. E38‐44 and E39‐40 duplications have been reported in Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) [42, 43]. Both cases inherited the variants from their mother, who presented no abnormal traits, and their male relatives did not have DMD/BMD. Case 25 presents a de novo male hemizygote with a DMD gene duplication spanning E28‐33. From the prenatal stage until 7 months after birth, no noticeable abnormalities were found. Several studies have documented related cases of BMD and DMD in this genomic region duplication [44, 45, 46]. Among the 22 cases in this study, 16 cases showed PGDup on the X chromosome. In female carriers with a pathogenic X‐chromosome duplication CNV, most may not have a relevant phenotype, or the phenotype may be very mild due to random or differential X‐chromosome inactivation [47]. However, their male offspring have a 50% chance of inheriting this duplication CNV, and hemizygotes are usually fully expressed. Therefore, when consulting, it is necessary to be vigilant about whether the duplicated CNV is a definite pathogenic factor, the possibility of incomplete phenotypic expression in female carriers, and the higher risk of recurrence in male offspring.

We conducted a detailed analysis to understand why abnormal phenotypes are absent in these cases, including seven TDIGs, two inversions, and three CCRs. The potential reasons are as follows: 1. The recessive inheritance pattern of genes (e.g., Case 9). 2. Female carriers of X‐linked disorders (Cases 23 and 24). 3. The duplication region does not include the ATG start codon necessary for protein translation and has a complete gene coding sequence (Cases 16 and 18). 4. Differences in the penetrance and expressivity of a gene (Case 20). 5. Clinical phenotypes that cannot be observed or manifested during the fetal or early postnatal period (Case 25). 6. The non‐OMIM morbid genes in inversion (Cases 21 and 24). 7. PGDup is located outside the target gene in CCR (Cases 7 and 26). 8. The target gene itself is not involved in CCR (Case 19). 9. The duplication does not involve the exons of the target gene (Case 11). In addition, the aforementioned factors along with 3D genomic alterations of duplicated CNVs, are important influences. Duplicated sequences can regulate the patient's phenotype by reshaping the architecture of topologically associated domains (TADs). Duplications can be intra‐TAD, having no phenotypic effect, or inter‐TAD, creating neo‐TADs that rewire enhancer‐promoter contacts and induce ectopic gene expression [48]. So far, a few studies have clearly demonstrated that duplicative CNVs affect gene function by altering gene TADs, leading to clinical phenotypes [49, 50, 51]. Currently, no TAD‐related research has been conducted in this study. We will employ techniques such as Hi‐C to explore these structural changes and their potential pathogenic roles for further investigation.

SVseq results generally provide clear genomic locations and structures of PGDups and do not rely on parental genetic information, which is a significant advantage in prenatal diagnosis. However, the above factors remind us that in some TDIG cases, a comprehensive analysis is required, including gene penetrance, X‐inactivation status, and age at onset. A detailed assessment of the patient's possible phenotypes and influencing factors should be conducted, and genetic counseling should be provided, especially when abnormal findings cannot be observed through imaging during the prenatal stage.

In a single analysis, the SVseq method pinpoints CNVs (deletions and duplications) and SVs (translocations, inversions, insertions, complex structural variations) including breakpoint identification. In this study, SVseq addresses the challenges posed by low‐depth sequencing in CNVseq and the restricted probe coverage in CMA, which impede the detection of PGDup or CCR. For example, SVseq surpasses specific limitations of the CMA chip (CGX SNP V1.1‐4‐plex ‐CGH/SNP), such as uncovered probes in the E38‐41 region of the DMD gene in Case 24 and interference from the PKD1 pseudogene in Case 11. However, neither SVseq nor targeted long‐read sequencing identified the PGDup duplication in PKD1 in Case 11, as detected by CMA/CNVseq. This discrepancy may be explained by two factors: first, the duplication region identified by Agilent CMA is only 14kb—below average resolution—so probe coverage is limited; second, PKD1 contains highly homologous sequences [52], suggesting that the duplication detected by CMA may not actually be within the PKD1 gene.

In this study, SVseq detects only the imbalances found by CMA or CNVSeq. Smaller CNVs, below the resolution thresholds of Agilent CMA (25–40kb), Affymetrix CMA (50kb), and CNV‐seq (100kb), or those in non‐coding regions (98%–98.5% of the genome), may be missed. These missed CNVs can alter disease presentation or severity in the patients by disrupting normal gene regulation or expression. Additionally, SVseq initially utilized long DNA fragments for library construction; the DNA was subsequently cyclized and fragmented into shorter pieces. Short‐read sequencing may not be well mapped to the original location in highly complex genomic regions, resulting in uncertain positions, as shown in Case 26. Conversely, long‐read fragments from “third‐generation” sequencing have a significant advantage as they can span intricate regions more effectively [53, 54]. Compared with the third‐generation WGS, OGM, and EGM, SVseq is an economical and efficient tool for most SV cases in clinical settings, generating less data and facilitating easier analysis. Information about SVs and breakpoints, along with further RNA and protein functions and the 3D genome, is important for elucidating the pathogenic mechanism of PGDup.

Newman Scott reported that nearly all duplicated CNVs identified in their study (93%) were inherited from parents [10]. In our study, 19 cases (73.08%) were of parental origin, while the remaining 7 (26.92%) were de novo. Among the 13 cases with TDEG, 3 were de novo; in the 14 cases with TDIG, CCR, and inversions, 4 were de novo. Among the 7 de novo cases, 1 had an incomplete gene structure in TDIG and abnormal phenotypes after birth; the remaining 6 cases showed no abnormal clinical manifestations, suggesting that parental inheritance or de novo is not a basis criterion for determining the pathogenicity of PGDup. The SVseq results showed that, in all cases with complete gene structures, whether of genetic or de novo origin, no abnormal phenotypes were observed in the child or parents during follow‐up. Of the 3 patients with incomplete gene structure in TDIG and no normal gene structure, 2 had abnormal phenotypes after birth, one of which originated from a mother with the same abnormal phenotype, and one was de novo; the third, for which no abnormal phenotypes were found during follow‐up, was a de novo hemizygote involving the DMD gene's TDIG. We predicted that he had late‐onset BMD/DMD, requiring an extended follow‐up period and enhanced monitoring. The positions and structures of PGDup in this study can accurately predict whether they will affect gene function, independent of parental origin, and consistent with clinical follow‐up results, except for 7 TDIG cases (reasonable explanations are described in Table 2). In special situations, such as prenatal diagnosis or when obtaining samples from family members is not possible, using a single sample for SVseq analysis is an option.

Overall, the penetrance of duplication CNVs appears to be lower than that of deletion or missing CNVs [55, 56, 57]. Due to the limited time in our clinical follow‐up, for individuals with abnormal phenotypes or atypical features not observed in our study, it is essential to extend the follow‐up period or conduct further functional studies. These efforts will help clarify the impact of PGDup variations and their potential pathogenic mechanisms, a primary focus of our future research.

Understanding the pathogenicity of PGDup is crucial for effective counseling and decision‐making. For fetuses with PGDup identified as VUS by CMA or CNVseq, advise parents to follow these steps: verify the duplication location and direction, assess gene pathogenicity, seek clinical correlation, and consider parental testing if uncertainty persists. Clearly guide parents through each step to resolve uncertainty about VUS. For P/LP variants, especially TDIG, parents should be informed about the possible clinical phenotype, treatment options, recurrence risks, and fertility issues. Life‐long follow‐ups and comprehensive clinical assessments are recommended. SVseq shows promise for prenatal diagnosis and should be integrated into the prenatal protocol.
