Blue LED–assisted bioreactor cultivation enhances carotenoid accumulation in Rhodotorula mucilaginosa LB-01
Authors: Aldana L Delgado Cuadros, Nayeli Ortiz Silos, Kiara Garcia Bustos, Kevin Tejada Meza, Gilberto Colina Andrade, Ruly Terán Hilares
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractMicrobial production of carotenoids provides a sustainable alternative to plant-derived sources. In this study, a yeast strain, Rhodotorula mucilaginosa LB-01, was isolated and its carotenoid biosynthetic potential was evaluated. Medium optimization (10 g/L glucose and 3 g/L yeast extract) improved relative carotenoid accumulation, reaching 10.13 Abs/g dry biomass. The influence of light quality was subsequently assessed, showing that blue LED illumination was associated with higher relative carotenoid accumulation (12.4 Abs/g) compared with red, orange, white, and green light conditions. Cultivation in a stirred-tank bioreactor (2.5 L working volume) under blue LED illumination resulted in further increases in carotenoid accumulation, reaching 16 Abs/g and 311 µg/g dry biomass, corresponding to an approximately 20% increase compared with control conditions operated without direct light exposure. Purification and quantification of individual pigments identified torularhodin (173 µg/g), torulene (71 µg/g), and β-carotene (67 µg/g), all present at higher levels than in the control. Overall, these results indicate that R. mucilaginosa LB-01, combined with controlled LED illumination, represents a promising microbial system for carotenoid accumulation.Key points• Rhodotorula mucilaginosa LB-01 was identified as a carotenoid-producing yeast.• Blue LED illumination yielded the highest relative carotenoid accumulation (12.4 Abs/g).• Blue LED bioreactor cultivation increased accumulation to 16 Abs/g dry biomass (+ 20%).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13760-x.Keywords: Carotenoids, Blue LED illumination, R. mucilaginosa, Stirred-tank bioreactor, Torularhodin

Introduction

Carotenoids have garnered increasing attention in recent years, primarily driven by rising consumer demand for natural and health-promoting compounds. This growing interest stems from their high bioavailability and extensive range of health benefits, including provitamin A activity, as well as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, anti-diabetic, anti-macular degeneration, and cardiovascular protective properties (Zhao et al. 2025). Due to their vivid pigmentation and functional bioactivities, carotenoids enhance the visual appeal, nutritional value, and shelf life of a wide variety of food products (Atencio et al. 2022; Cheng and Wu 2024; de Lima et al. 2025). Reflecting this surge in demand, the global carotenoid market has expanded from USD 1.5 billion in 2019 to a projected USD 2.0 billion by 2026, with an estimated annual growth rate of 4.2% (Papapostolou et al. 2023).

Microbial carotenoid production has emerged as a promising alternative to plant-based extraction, offering several advantages such as scalability, lower production costs, and the use of inexpensive feedstocks. A key focus in this area of biotechnology is the optimization of fermentation conditions to maximize carotenoid yield (Mussagy et al. 2019). Among microbial producers, many yeast species within the Basidiomycota phylum commonly referred to as red yeasts are particularly notable for their ability to accumulate carotenoids intracellularly, resulting in pigmented colonies ranging in shades from orange and pink to deep red (Kot et al. 2021). Within this group, species of the Rhodotorula genus stand out due to their unicellular morphology, rapid growth, and high carotenoid productivity (Sharma and Ghoshal 2020). These yeasts can synthesize a diverse range of commercially valuable carotenoids, including β-carotene, torulene, torularhodin, γ-carotene, and lycopene (Ochoa-Viñals et al. 2024). Among these, β-carotene is often the predominant pigment, comprising up to 70% of the total carotenoid content (Ochoa-Viñals et al. 2024). R. mucilaginosa has emerged as a highly promising natural carotenoid producer, owing to its adaptability to various substrates and its suitability for large-scale fermentation systems (Gualberto et al. 2022).

Carotenogenesis in microorganisms can be further enhanced by the application of specific stress conditions, such as elevated light intensity, salinity, or nutrient limitation strategies that have attracted considerable interest in industrial biotechnology (Mapelli-Brahm et al. 2020). Light exposure is a well-known inducer of carotenoid biosynthesis, as it activates photoprotective responses aimed at counteracting oxidative damage caused by irradiation (Pham et al. 2020). Light quality and wavelength have been shown to significantly influence both the yield and composition of carotenoids, with strain-specific responses reported under various lighting conditions (Losinska-Sičiūnienė et al. 2025). However, most of these studies have been conducted in Erlenmeyer flasks rather than in controlled bioreactor systems.

Accordingly, an initial optimization of the fermentation medium was conducted using shake flasks. Under optimized conditions, the impact of different LED light sources (red, green, blue, and white) on total relative carotenoid accumulation was evaluated. The LED treatment that yielded the highest carotenoid accumulation was subsequently scaled up in a 5-L stirred-tank bioreactor containing 2.5 L of fermentation medium. Finally, the produced carotenoids were extracted, fractionated by column chromatography, and preliminarily identified using UV–VIS spectroscopy.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of yeast

For yeast isolation, an Erlenmeyer flask containing a glucose-based culture medium was exposed to the Bioprocess Laboratory environment in Arequipa, Peru (16.4040° S, 71.5590° W), for 48 h. The resulting culture was streaked onto fresh potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates and incubated at 28 °C for 72 h. Colonies exhibiting orange pigmentation were successively subcultured until a pure strain was obtained, which was subsequently identified.

Molecular identification began with the extraction of genomic DNA (gDNA), using either a resin-based method or the boiling method with PrepMan™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by PCR amplification. The 18S rRNA gene was selected as the target region for molecular identification. PCR amplification targeted a partial region of the 18S rRNA gene using the universal primers NS1 (5′-GTAGTCATATGCTTGTCTC-3′, forward) and NS8 (5′-TCCGCAGGTTCACCTACGGA-3′, reverse). Sequencing was conducted in both forward and reverse directions using the BigDye™ Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit and a 3730XL automated sequencer (Applied Biosystems). The results were analyzed by comparing sequence homology with the NCBI rRNA database, and a standard report was generated showing the most similar strains.

The PCR reaction mixture had a total volume of 20 µL and contained: 10 µL of Axen™ H Taq PCR Master Mix (2X), 1 µL of each primer (forward and reverse, 10 pmol/µL), 1–2 µL of template DNA (20 ng/µL), and 7 µL of HPLC-grade water. gDNA extraction included an initial incubation at 56 °C for 15 min, followed by heating at 99 °C for 15 min, and a final cooling step at 12 °C. The samples were then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 5 min, and 1.5 µL of the supernatant was used as the DNA template for PCR.

The PCR cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 31 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 57 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1.4 min. A final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min was applied, and the samples were stored at 4 °C until further processing.

The morphological and fluorescence characteristics of the yeast strain were examined using an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope. The sample was prepared from a solid agar plate by resuspending a small amount of biomass in ultrapure water. The suspension was gently homogenized to ensure even distribution of cells. A small aliquot of the yeast suspension was mounted on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip. Fluorescence was visualized using the U-FUW filter set. The excitation wavelength range was set between 300–400 nm, and the emission was detected at wavelengths above 410 nm. Observations were performed under consistent optical settings to detect the natural autofluorescence of the yeast cells. Images were captured using the microscope’s integrated imaging system.

R. mucilaginosa LB-01 has been deposited in the NCBI BioProject database (accession number [PRJNA1347446]) and is preserved in the culture collection of the Bioprocess Laboratory, Catholic University of Santa Maria, Arequipa, Peru. The strain is available to other researchers upon reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Inoculum preparation

The microorganism was stored at 4 °C on PDA agar until use. The inoculum was cultivated in a rotary shaker at 28 °C and 180 rpm for 48 h in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of culture medium composed of 20 g/L glucose, 5 g/L yeast extract, 0.52 g/L KH₂PO₄, 4 g/L (NH₄)₂SO₄, and 0.52 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O. After incubation, the cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in sterile distilled water. The inoculum was then added to the fermentation medium to achieve an initial cell concentration of approximately 0.2 g/L, as determined using a previously established calibration curve correlating optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) with dry cell weight.

Optimization of relative carotenoids accumulation in biomass

Experiments were conducted using a Central Composite Design to evaluate the effects of glucose concentration (5.86–34.14 g/L) and yeast extract (YE) concentration (0.17–5.83 g/L) on relative carotenoid accumulation in the biomass. The response variables considered were normalized carotenoid content (Abs/g dry cell weight), used as a relative indicator of carotenoid accumulation, and biomass production (g/L). Fermentations were carried out in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of medium, prepared according to the experimental design with varying concentrations of glucose and yeast extract, and supplemented with 0.52 g/L KH₂PO₄, 4 g/L (NH₄)₂SO₄, and 0.52 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O. The fermentation processes were performed at 25 °C and 180 rpm for 96 h.

Design-Expert software version 12.0.1.0 (Stat-Ease, Inc., USA) was used to develop and evaluate an empirical model describing the response variable (carotenoid accumulation in biomass) as a function of glucose and yeast extract concentrations. Process optimization was conducted using the software’s numerical optimization function, based on a desirability function. Maximizing carotenoid accumulation was established as a key objective in the optimization process.

Experiments were carried out in triplicate under the optimized conditions to validate the predictive model, maintaining identical temperature, agitation, and light parameters. Samples were collected at regular intervals to determine biomass concentration, carotenoid content, and sugar consumption.

Evaluation of different LED-light wavelengths on carotenoids accumulation

Fermentation was performed in 100 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing (per liter): 10 g glucose, 1 g yeast extract, 0.52 g KH₂PO₄, 4 g (NH₄)₂SO₄, and 0.52 g MgSO₄·7H₂O. The cultures were exposed directly to LED light from both the bottom and side walls of the flasks. LEDs with peak wavelengths of 620–630 nm (red), 515–535 nm (green), and 450—470 nm (blue) were tested. Control experiments were conducted in darkness and under white light. Light intensity was maintained at 390—400 lx in all treatments.

All fermentations were conducted in triplicate in a rotary shaker at 25 °C and 180 rpm for 96 h. At the end of the fermentation period, a final sample was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the biomass. The recovered cells were analyzed for growth and relative carotenoid accumulation, following the procedures described in the analytical methods section.

Carotenoids accumulation in biomass cultivated under blue LED-light in bioreactor

Fermentation was carried out in a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor BIO-TEC-PRO-II (TECNAL, Brazil) under blue LED light exposure. A total of 2.5 L of culture medium, prepared according to the optimized conditions, was used. The bioreactor containing the medium was sterilized and subsequently inoculated to achieve an initial cell concentration of 0.2 g/L. The fermentation was conducted in triplicate under controlled conditions: temperature at 25 °C, pH maintained at 5.5, agitation between 100–250 rpm, and airflow ranging from 1.0 to 3.5 L/min, ensuring that the dissolved oxygen level remained at approximately 40% throughout the process. The illumination was achieved by externally mounting a blue LED strip around the glass vessel, providing an average light intensity of 398 lx (Fig. 1). Samples were collected every 12 h to monitor substrate consumption, cell growth, and carotenoid accumulation in biomass.

Analytical methods

Carotenoid extraction was performed according to the methodologies reported by Díaz-Ruiz et al. (2024) and Kanzy et al. (2015). Briefly, the harvested biomass was normalized to the same concentration across samples prior to extraction, suspended in 1 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) preheated to 55 °C, centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, 5 mL of a methanol: acetone mixture (7:3, v/v) was added, and the suspension was incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and total carotenoids were analyzed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Genesys 150, Thermo Scientific, USA) with 1 cm path-length cuvettes. Spectral scanning was performed over the wavelength range of 300–800 nm, revealing characteristic absorption peaks in the carotenoid fingerprint region, with maximum absorbance observed at 490 nm. The solvent corresponding used was used as the blank. For all samples, extraction, dilution, and measurement were conducted under identical conditions to enable comparison of results, which were expressed as normalized carotenoid content (Abs/g dry biomass) (Liu et al. 2021; Mavria et al. 2022).

The same procedure was used to extract carotenoids from biomass cultivated in a stirred-tank bioreactor. After extraction, the carotenoid extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 40 °C. A silica gel glass column was first packed and compacted by gradually adding ethanol. After complete drying, the carotenoid concentrate was loaded onto the column, and elution was performed sequentially with hexane for β-carotene and torulene, followed by acetone for torularhodin. The absorbance spectra of each collected fraction were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer in the same solvent used for elution (hexane or acetone), with the corresponding solvent used as the blank. Each individual pigment fraction was subjected to a full spectrophotometric scan, and the resulting spectra were compared with literature data for tentative identification. Quantification of purified carotenoid fractions was performed using literature-reported extinction coefficients, and concentrations were calculated using Eq. (1) as described by Moliné et al. (2012) and Zeng et al. (2023), with results expressed as µg/g dry cell weight.

Sugar content was analyzed according to the dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method described by Miller (1959). The centrifuged samples were diluted to an appropriate concentration. To each 0.5 mL of diluted sample, 0.5 mL of DNS reagent was added. The mixture was heated at 100 °C for 10 min to develop the color. The reaction was then stopped by adding 1.75 mL of distilled water. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm. A calibration curve was prepared using glucose standards ranging from 0.1 to 1 g/L to determine the reducing sugar concentration in the samples.

Biomass concentration was determined gravimetrically. A 10 mL aliquot of culture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was washed three times with distilled water to remove residual medium components. Pre-weighed aluminum weighing dishes were used; these were previously labeled, dried at 60 °C for 4 h, cooled in a desiccator, and weighed. The washed pellet was resuspended in distilled water and transferred to the pre-weighed dishes, which were then dried at 60 °C for 24 h. After cooling in a desiccator, the dishes were reweighed. The difference between the final and initial weights corresponded to the dry biomass contained in the 10 mL sample.

Lipid extraction was performed using a modified Folch method adapted from Nagaraj et al. (2022). Briefly, 100 mg of freeze-dried biomass was incubated with 2 mL of 1 M HCl and heated at 75 °C for 1 h in a heating block. Then, 2 mL of 0.8% KCl and 6 mL of Folch solution (chloroform: methanol, 2:1 v/v) were added, followed by vigorous mixing and centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min to separate the phases. The lower lipid-containing phase was collected into a pre-weighed glass tube. Extraction was repeated with 4 mL of chloroform until the phase became clear. Finally, the solvent was evaporated in an oven at 40 °C for 2 h.

For elemental analysis, approximately 3 mg of freeze-dried biomass were weighed into tin capsules, alongside blanks and certified standards for calibration. Samples were then combusted completely in an organic elemental analyzer (Thermo Fisher, Germany) under the manufacturer’s recommended operating conditions.

Results

Identification of isolated strain

The carotenoid-producing yeast was isolated and designated as R. mucilaginosa LB-01. The strain was cultivated on PDA medium, exhibiting a characteristic orange pigmentation typical of Rhodotorula species, with smooth and shiny colonies (Fig. 2A).

Observation of R. mucilaginosa LB-01 isolated and used in this study. A) Visual appearance on PDA medium; B) Gram-stained yeast cells (magnification: 100 ×); and C) Unstained fluorescence optical microscopy image at a scale of 10 μm

Gram staining revealed globose to elongated yeast cells undergoing budding, with no evidence of pseudohyphae formation (Fig. 2B). Figure 2C presents a light microscopy image (scale bar: 10 μm) of strain R. mucilaginosa LB-01, showing yeast-like cells with ellipsoidal to ovoid morphology, which is characteristic of the genus Rhodotorula. For molecular identification, partial sequencing of the 18S ribosomal RNA gene was performed, and the resulting sequence was compared against the GenBank database using the BLASTn algorithm. The highest similarity was obtained with Rhodotorula mucilaginosa strain JGTA-R1 (GenBank accession no. KU051691.1), showing 97% sequence identity over 1,243 aligned nucleotides, including 1,200 exact matches, with a bit score of 2,047 and an E-value of 0.0. The alignment was detected in reverse orientation (Plus/Minus), which is a common outcome in nucleotide sequence comparisons and does not affect taxonomic interpretation.

Culture medium optimization for carotenoid accumulation

An optimization of the fermentation medium was performed to enhance carotenoid accumulation in R. mucilaginosa LB-01 cultivated in Erlenmeyer flasks, with results summarized in Table 1. The highest carotenoid accumulation was obtained in run 2, which contained 20 g/L glucose and 0.17 g/L yeast extract, representing a 72% increase compared to run 10, which used 10 g/L glucose and 5 g/L yeast extract. These runs correspond to carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratios of 4.68 (run 2) and 3.11 (run 10), indicating that a higher C/N ratio favored carotenoid production in this strain. Although some studies report the use of elevated C/N ratios to maximize carotenoid synthesis, a relatively low C/N ratio was employed here based on preliminary results (data not showed), which showed improved production under these conditions. Moreover, maintaining a low C/N ratio allowed for more efficient nitrogen utilization and favored carotenoid synthesis without limiting cell growth, as confirmed experimentally.

Evaluation of different LED light wavelengths on carotenoid accumulation

The relative total carotenoids production in Erlenmeyer flasks were evaluated under exposure to different LED light sources, and the results are presented in Fig. 5. As shown in Fig. 5A, the highest relative total carotenoid content was obtained under blue LED light (12.40 ± 0.23 Abs/g of biomass), which was significantly higher than that observed under red, green, white, and black light conditions. This corresponds to an increase of 9.82% compared to the white light control (11.20 ± 0.15 Abs/g biomass) and 14.0% relative to the condition without light (10.70 ± 0.34 Abs/g biomass), according to Tukey’s HSD test (p < 0.05). Moreover, Fig. 5B shows the coloration of the medium after fermentation (96 h), where a more intense pigmentation was observed under blue light (Fig. 5B, flask “F1”) compared to white light (flask “F2”) and the condition without light (flask “F3”). Regarding biomass production, a concentration of 5.4 g/L was achieved under white LED light, which was higher than that obtained under red (4.6 g/L), green (5 g/L) LED light and dark condition (5.1 g/L).

Carotenoid accumulation in biomass cultivated under blue LED illumination in a bioreactor

Figure 6 shows the profiles of sugar consumption, biomass concentration, and relative carotenoid accumulation in biomass cultivated in a stirred-tank bioreactor operated under blue LED illumination and under a control condition without direct light exposure, with both experiments conducted under identical operating conditions. As shown in Fig. 6A, blue LED light enhanced relative carotenoid accumulation, reaching 16 Abs/g, which was notably higher than the 12.4 Abs/g observed in the control (Fig. 6B). Regarding sugar consumption, a slower rate was observed under blue LED illumination, with complete glucose depletion occurring around 40 h, compared to approximately 25 h in the control condition. Regarding lipid production (8.13 ± 0.40% of dry cell weight), exposure to blue LED light showed no significant difference compared to the control. Likewise, no differences were detected in elemental biomass composition for nitrogen (N, 6.99%), carbon (C, 45.59%), hydrogen (H, 6.77%), or sulfur (S, 0.29%).

Separation and identification of carotenoids

Carotenoids were extracted from the recovered biomass, and their composition was analyzed, with the results presented in Fig. 7. As shown in Fig. 7A, thin-layer chromatography (TLC) revealed three distinct colored fractions (red, orange, and yellow) using hexane as the mobile phase. Furthermore, the crude carotenoid extract was fractionated and separated by column chromatography, using silica gel as the stationary phase and hexane (for yellow and orange pigment) and acetone (for red pigment) as eluent (Fig. 7B). In Fig. 7C, are shown the spectrophotometric analysis of each separate fractions which were identified as torularhodin (red fraction) with a maximum absorbance at 498 nm, torulene (orange fraction) at 485 nm, and β-carotene (yellow fraction) at 451 nm. Carotenoid identification was based on UV–Vis absorption spectra and comparison with reported literature values for Rhodotorula carotenoids obtained using the same solvent in each case; therefore, the assignments should be considered tentative rather than definitive.

Discussion

Identification of isolated strain and carotenoids production

The results indicate that the isolated strain exhibits high sequence similarity to Rhodotorula mucilaginosa and was therefore designated R. mucilaginosa LB-01. Partial 18S rRNA gene sequencing provided sufficient resolution for reliable genus-level identification; however, it is acknowledged that higher-resolution markers, such as the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and large subunit (LSU) rRNA regions, are required for definitive species- or strain-level discrimination within the genus Rhodotorula.

BLASTn analysis revealed multiple independent R. mucilaginosa 18S rRNA gene sequences deposited in the NCBI database with identical alignment statistics, including a bit score of 2,047 and an E-value of 0.0. Representative accessions include KP233783.1, KJ806315.1 (R. mucilaginosa isolate H-4), DQ832199.1 (R. mucilaginosa AFTOL-ID 1548), and AB042787.1. In addition, uncultured Basidiomycota clones (e.g., HQ438118.1) exhibited equivalent alignment metrics, reflecting the highly conserved nature of the 18S rRNA gene within this fungal lineage. The recurrence of multiple high-scoring, independent matches consistently support assignment of the isolate to the genus Rhodotorula (Paul et al., 2019).

The highest sequence similarity was observed with R. mucilaginosa strain JGTA-R1. While detailed information on this reference strain is limited, it was originally isolated from Typha angustifolia (Paul et al. 2020). In contrast, strain JGTA-S1 closely related to JGTA-R1 has been extensively studied (Nag et al. 2024; Paul et al. 2020). JGTA-S1 is an endophytic strain known to promote the growth of Typha and other plants such as rice, particularly by enhancing nitrogen uptake (Saha and Seal 2015; Nag et al. 2024). It has also been identified as a promising producer of carotenoids, lipids, enzymes, and other high value bioproducts. Given its genetic similarity to JGTA-R1, the isolated strain LB-01 may also possess plant growth–promoting properties and significant biotechnological potential, particularly for carotenoid production. Nevertheless, further studies specifically focused on these applications are necessary to confirm and fully exploit its capabilities.

With respect to the fermentation process for carotenoid production, the results show similar behavior to that reported in previous studies. For example, Juyal et al. (2025) observed an increase in pigment productivity from 40.13 μg/g/h at a C/N ratio of approximately 20 to 61.75 μg/g/h at a C/N ratio of approximately 80 in R. mucilaginosa IIPL32 (MTCC 25056). In that study, the glucose concentration was fixed at 20 g/L, while the C/N ratio was increased by reducing the (NH₄)₂SO₄ concentration. Similarly, Braunwald et al. (2013) reported that increasing the C/N ratio from 70 to 120 did not enhance lipid production but positively affected carotenoid synthesis in R. glutinis. Overall, nitrogen limitation redirects cellular metabolism in Rhodotorula from biomass formation toward secondary metabolism (e.g., carotenoid biosynthesis), increasing carbon flux through the mevalonate pathway and promoting carotenoid accumulation as part of a stress-adaptive response (Braunwald et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2014).

Regarding biomass production, the highest value of 9 g/L was obtained in experiment 8, which used the highest concentration of yeast extract. In contrast, a lower biomass yield of 4.6 g/L was observed in run 2, where yeast extract concentration was minimal. This trend is consistent with the findings reported by Juyal et al. (2025) for R. mucilaginosa IIPL32 (MTCC 25056).

The extracted relative total carotenoids in fermentation process were analyzed at 490 nm, corresponding to the wavelength of maximum absorbance for the carotenoid mixture. This result is consistent with previous reports by Sharma and Ghoshal (2020), who observed a maximum absorbance at 490 nm for carotenoids extracted from R. mucilaginosa (MTCC-1403), as well as the findings of Cheng and Yang (2016), who reported the same absorbance peak for carotenoids obtained from R. mucilaginosa F-1 using acetone as the extraction solvent. These results confirm that the extracted pigments correspond to carotenoid compounds typically produced by Rhodotorula species under fermentative conditions.

Improving the carotenoids accumulation in biomass by effect of LED-light

The effect of LED light on microbial pigment expression in Rhodotorula strains has been previously reported. For instance, Rodrigues et al. (2024) evaluated the effect of LED light on carotenoid production by R. mucilaginosa CCT 7688 in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 225 mL of culture medium and found that red LED light resulted in the highest pigment yield. However, the light intensity used in their study was 50 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1, which is substantially higher than the intensity applied in our study (400 lx, approximately 7 µmol m⁻2 s⁻1). Moreover, a recent study by de Lima et al. (2025) reported the impact of blue LED light (100W RGB reflector) on torularhodin production, showing slightly higher yields under blue LED compared to red and green light. Nonetheless, pigment production under blue light was still lower than that obtained under dark and white light conditions.

The higher relative total carotenoid production observed under blue LED illumination in the stirred-tank bioreactor, beyond the effect of light wavelength alone, may be associated with the precise control of operational parameters. For instance, the efficient pH and oxygen supply may favor the carotenoids accumulation (Mussagy et al. 2021), compared to flask-scale systems where conditions are not easily controlled. For example, Kong et al. (2019) reported that in a non-controlled system (Erlenmeyer flask), the pH of the medium decreased from 5.5 to 3.0 within 24 h and dropped below 2.0 after 48 h. Within each cultivation scale, the application of LED illumination was evaluated under otherwise comparable fermentation conditions, allowing the effect of light wavelength to be assessed relative to a corresponding control. In this context, light exposure may act as a cellular stressor, potentially stimulating pigment synthesis as part of a protective mechanism. In addition, light exposure may influence the activity of enzymes involved in carotenoid and lipid biosynthesis, with is consistence with observations reported in previous studies (Gong et al. 2019; Hernandez-Almanza et al. 2014; Rodrigues et al. 2024). Nevertheless, these relationships should be interpreted as correlative, as multiple cultivation parameters differ between scales and no direct physiological or molecular analyses were performed in the present study.

The slower glucose consumption observed under blue LED illumination may be associated with light-induced cellular stress, which could lead to increased intracellular generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), as reported in previous studies (Luo et al., 2021). Under these conditions, carbon flux may be partially redirected from rapid substrate assimilation and biomass formation toward stress-response and photoprotective pathways, including carotenoid biosynthesis, which is known to play a role in quenching singlet oxygen and mitigating oxidative stress (Mano et al. 2016; Luo et al., 2021). Consequently, substrate consumption kinetics may be reduced even as carotenoid accumulation increases, a phenomenon previously reported for carotenoid-producing yeasts and filamentous fungi (Mano et al. 2016; Saejung and Apaiwong 2015).

The lipid content observed in this study at low C/N ratio aligns with previous findings; for instance, Zhang et al. (2014) reported low lipid synthesis in R. glutinis under low C/N ratios, achieving 12% at a C/N ratio of 30 and exceeding 25% at a ratio of 90. Furthermore, some R. glutinis strains have been classified as non-oleaginous when lipid accumulation is below 20%. For example, Maza et al. (2020) reported 9.35% lipid content for R. glutinis Y-34 cultivated in a nitrogen-limited, glucose-based medium at a C/N ratio of 40%. These observations highlight the critical role of the C/N in triggering lipid accumulation in Rhodotorula strains, although other factors such as carbon source, medium composition, and cultivation conditions also influence lipid biosynthesis (Lopes et al. 2020; Ochoa-Viñals et al. 2024). Similarly, Mapelli-Brahm et al. (2020) reported that R. mucilaginosa R2 increased lipid content from 7.08 ± 0.1% at a C/N ratio of 20 to 20.67% at 40, with higher ratios reducing yields. These results indicate that the low lipid content observed in the present study (8.13%) is consistent with cultivation at a suboptimal C/N ratio, highlighting the importance of optimizing this parameter to enhance lipid biosynthesis.

Separation and identification of carotenoids

The maximal absorbance wavelengths obtained for each carotenoid (Fig. 7C) are consistent with previous reports for R. mucilaginosa strains. For example, Zeng et al. (2023) reported a maximum absorbance for torularhodin at 499 nm, while Moliné et al. (2012) observed absorbance maxima at 484 nm for torulene and 450 nm for β-carotene extracted from the mutant strain R. mucilaginosa CBS 316 T. Moreover, in the study by Juyal et al. (2025), the maximum absorbance of standard β-carotene was reported at 450 nm, which is consistent with the value obtained for the separated yellow pigment in this study. However, they also reported a peak at 480 nm as β-carotene, which does not correspond specifically to β-carotene but likely reflects the absorbance of a mixture of carotenoids like what was observed in the present study.

The total carotenoid yield reached 311 µg/g of dry biomass, which surpasses the 280 µg/g reported by Ambrico et al. (2024) using Rhodotorula spp. (strain ELP2022) in a 5 L bioreactor with 20 g/L glucose. It also exceeded the 252.99 µg/g obtained by Machado et al. (2019) using R. mucilaginosa URM 7409 cultivated in flasks with 30 g/L glucose, as well as the 121.3 µg/g reported by Zeng et al. (2023) using R. mucilaginosa I2002 with 20 g/L glucose. However, the achieved value was lower than the 601 µg/g reported by Fonseca et al. (2025) for R. mucilaginosa RGM42 isolated from Amazonian substrates. Notably, in that study carotenoids were quantified as total carotenoids based on absorbance at 450 nm, which may limit direct comparability with the present results. Future work will focus on further optimization of bioreactor cultivation conditions to enhance carotenoid accumulation.

Table 3 was constructed to compare the individual carotenoids identified in this study with those reported in previous work. Although UV–Vis spectroscopy is suitable for preliminary carotenoid profiling, unambiguous identification and purity assessment require more advanced analytical techniques. Accordingly, future studies will employ LC–MS–based approaches to confirm compound identity and structural integrity. As observed, the torularhodin yield obtained in this study under incidence of blue LED light was 173.45 µg/g dry cell weight, which is notably higher than the 50.5 µg/g reported by Zeng et al. (2023) after purification from R. mucilaginosa I2002. It also exceeds the values reported by Ambrico et al. (2024) who obtained 152 µg/g of torularhodin, 21.9 µg/g of torulene, 78.6 µg/g of β-carotene, and 27.2 µg/g of γ-carotene. Finally, the separation of individual carotenoids is important due to their distinct properties and functionalities, which enable their application across various industries. For instance, they can be used as food additives, serving as natural colorants and antioxidants (de Lima et al. 2025). Carotenoids also have significant value in cosmetic formulations, such as for skin protection and anti-aging, owing to their ability to scavenge free radicals and mitigate oxidative damage. Additionally, they exhibit promising potential in pharmaceutical applications, due to their reported antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and possibly anticancer (Jin et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025; Ribeiro et al. 2023).

Conclusion

A novel strain, R. mucilaginosa LB-01, was isolated and identified as a promising carotenoid-producing yeast. Blue LED lighting was associated with a greater relative total accumulation of carotenoids at each culture scale, and culture in a stirred-tank bioreactor showed a further increase in carotenoid levels compared with illuminated flask cultures, likely reflecting improved control of operational parameters. In particular, the accumulation of torularhodin, torulene, and β-carotene was enhanced. In contrast, lipid production and elemental composition remained unchanged under the conditions tested, indicating that further studies are required to evaluate additional fermentation parameters to promote lipid accumulation. Notably, this study successfully separated and quantified individual carotenoids, providing detailed insights into the production profile of the strain. Overall, R. mucilaginosa LB-01 demonstrates strong potential as a carotenoid-producing microorganism, while blue LED illumination appears to be a promising factor associated with increased carotenoid accumulation under the evaluated conditions.
