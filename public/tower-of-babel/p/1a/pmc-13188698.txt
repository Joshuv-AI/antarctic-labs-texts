A novel algorithm for the continuous determination of individualized intracranial pressure (iICP) thresholds using a multi-window weighted approach
Authors: Kevin Y Stein, Logan Froese, Amanjyot Singh Sainbhi, Nuray Vakitbilir, Tobias Bergmann, Noah Silvaggio, Rakibul Hasan, Isuru Herath, Mansoor Hayat, Jaewoong Moon, Frederick A Zeiler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividualized intracranial pressure (iICP) thresholds, defined as the function intersectionality between intracranial pressure (ICP) and cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR), represent a promising potential approach to personalized medicine in neurocritical care. However, current iICP derivation methods fail to account for the variable quality of iICP estimates and rely on entire recording periods, limiting their use to retrospective, post-hoc calculations and thereby limiting clinical applicability. Therefore, the goal of this study was to develop an automated, continuous iICP derivation algorithm that provides accompanying quality metrics, evaluate the algorithm’s performance, and identify patient-related factors influencing derivation yields.MethodsA custom algorithm employing a multi-window weighted approach was developed for the continuously updating derivation of iICP. The algorithm was designed to concurrently generate quality metrics alongside iICP outputs, which grade key characteristics of the underlying ICP-CVR curves. The algorithm was tested on a cohort of 131 moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients from the Winnipeg Acute TBI Database. Multiple iterations of the algorithm, with varying parameter settings, were tested to assess algorithm performance. Subgroup analyses were performed to identify demographic-, admission-, and treatment-related factors that may influence derivation yields.ResultsAlgorithm performance varied significantly with parameter selection, including modeling method, CVR index-threshold combination, and update frequency. The best performing iteration achieved a median derivation yield of 66.1%. The use of modeling methods in conjunction, the use of the pressure reactivity index and thresholds of + 0.20 and + 0.25, and the use of higher update frequencies were shown to be associated with superior algorithm performance. Time-based subgroup analyses revealed lower yields during the early phases post-injury, while demographic-, admission-, and treatment-related factors showed minimal influence on algorithm performance.ConclusionThis study demonstrates the feasibility of deriving iICP thresholds in a continuously updating fashion. While promising, further optimization is needed to improve derivation yields and enable bedside implementation. Moreover, comprehensive outcome and physiologic analyses are needed to clarify the relationships of iICP with long-term outcomes and cerebral physiologic insult burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03472-7.Keywords: Intracranial pressure, Personalized medicine, Cerebrovascular reactivity, Individualized intracranial pressure thresholds

Introduction

Intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring has become a cornerstone in the management of numerous critical neurological conditions, particularly those involving brain bleeding or swelling such as traumatic brain injury (TBI). Since little can typically be done to reverse the immediate injury inflicted on the brain at the time of the initial insult, the primary management goal with such conditions is to minimize secondary neural injury resulting from downstream derangements in cerebral physiology. This includes preventing elevated pressure within the intracranial cavity, as this can contribute to reduced cerebral blood flow (CBF) and subsequent ischemia since ICP is intricately related to cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP): CPP = mean arterial pressure (MAP) – ICP.

However, ICP-lowering therapeutics are associated with inherent risks. For example, induced hyperventilation may cause hypocapnia-mediated cerebral vasoconstriction and cerebral ischemia; hyperosmolar therapies can compromise systemic hemodynamics and electrolyte balance; sedative and analgesic agents may depress respiratory drive and obscure neurologic assessment; and barbiturates can reduce cardiac output [1, 2]. Furthermore, reductions in ICP do not necessarily translate into improved cerebral perfusion or oxygen delivery, particularly in the setting of impaired autoregulation. Clinicians must therefore balance the dangers of intracranial hypertension with the potential iatrogenic risks of treatment.

Current neurocritical care guidelines laid out by the Brain Trauma Foundation recommend therapeutically maintaining ICP below a threshold of 20mmHg or 22 mmHg [1, 3]. Over the past several decades, there have been significant therapeutic advancements that have radically improved our ability to achieve this target. However, despite this, there has been limited improvement in the outcomes associated with critical neurologic conditions [4–6]. This divergence has been attributed to the fact that these guideline-based thresholds were identified through population-based analyses on the association between grand-averaged ICP and long-term outcomes [7], and thus fail to account for individual-phenotype, injury heterogeneity, and the dynamic nature of cerebral physiology [8].

Furthermore, recent literature has shown that a considerable portion of cerebral physiologic insult burden is resistant to guideline-based care, and that the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions differs highly among various patient subgroups [6, 9–15]. Studies have also suggested that existing population-based prognostic models account for less than half of outcome variance [16–18]. The high degree of variability in injury-response, heterogeneity of critical neurologic conditions, and dynamic nature of cerebral physiology demands the replacement of the “one treatment fits all” dogma of current guideline-based management with a new personalized approach, that can be tailored to the individual patient.

Cerebral autoregulation is an important cerebral physiologic concept that describes the inherent ability of the cerebral vasculature to self-regulate tone in order to maintain constant CBF despite fluctuating systemic blood pressure. This mechanism serves as a key defense against secondary neural injury as cerebral tissues are incredibly sensitive to both ischemic and hypervolemic damage. However, this mechanism is not infallible, as it is unable to compensate for extremes in systemic blood pressure, such as when MAP falls below 60 mmHg or rises above 160 mmHg [19, 20]. Further, various critical neurologic conditions are associated with cerebrovascular derangements that reduce the range of systemic pressures that this cerebral autoregulatory mechanism is able to compensate for. This can lead to a pressure-passive state where CBF becomes directly dependent on systemic pressure, increasing the risk of both hypo- and hyper-perfusion related injury [19, 21, 22].

Recent advancements in bedside biomedical signal processing have made real-time, continuous assessment of cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR), a surrogate measure of cerebral autoregulatory function, feasible [23–26]. Over the past decade, impaired CVR has been identified as a key driver of secondary brain injury, with a substantial portion of a TBI patient’s stay in the ICU marked by such impairments [6, 25–29]. Numerous studies have established a robust association between impaired CVR and poor long-term outcomes following TBI [6, 26, 27], with both mean values of CVR metrics and duration of impairment during the acute phase being associated with 6-month outcomes [25, 26, 29–31]. This notion has made CVR a popular target in the development of recent personalized medicine techniques.

Cerebral perfusion pressure optimum (CPPopt) has emerged as a promising individualized therapeutic target. The concept of CPPopt was first introduced by Steiner and colleagues in 2002, when they demonstrated the feasibility of identifying a personalized optimal CPP target using the relationship between CPP and CVR [32]. The authors achieved this by plotting CPP against the pressure reactivity index (PRx; correlation between ICP and MAP), revealing a U-shaped curve, and finding the CPP value at which PRx was at its minimum. Therefore, this target theoretically represents the CPP at which CVR is most preserved. These patient-specific CPP targets have been found to be more strongly associated with patient outcomes than the current guideline-based CPP targets [33–37]. Additionally, subsequent work has enabled the continuous calculation of CPPopt at the patient bedside, making its clinical application a possibility [33, 38].

Interestingly, ICP also demonstrates a relationship with CVR. However, unlike the U-shaped relationship seen with CPP, ICP generally displays a non-linear higher order polynomial or exponential relationship with CVR, with increasing ICP corresponding with more impaired CVR [39]. Following the same fundamental principles underlying CPPopt, a personalized ICP threshold, past which CVR becomes persistently deranged, should also be possible to derive. However, despite the promising results of CPPopt, there is currently limited literature pertaining to such individualized ICP (iICP) thresholds.

The concept of iICP thresholds was first described by Lazaridis and colleagues in 2014, when the authors plotted ICP against PRx and, through manual inspection, identified the ICP at which PRx surpassed a value of 0.20 a.u., a threshold chosen by the authors to represent the transition point between intact and impaired CVR [40]. The authors were able to identify an iICP in approximately 68% of patients and found that these personalized thresholds demonstrated a more robust association with long-term outcomes when compared to guideline-based ICP thresholds. A few years later, Zeiler and colleagues were able to semi-automate iICP identification by developing an algorithm with an accuracy of approximately 83.2% [41]. Similar yields and outcome associations were observed in this study.

Despite the promising results of these two studies, their reliance on manual inspection for the identification of iICP greatly hindered the future applicability of iICP clinically [40, 41]. Additionally, both studies exclusively used PRx, ignoring other ICP-based CVR indices, and an arbitrarily selected threshold of + 0.20. In response to these shortcomings, our lab developed a fully automated algorithm that requires no manual inspection and, when tested on the entire recording periods of a local dataset with over 100 patients, demonstrated an accuracy of > 99% [42]. PRx, the pulse amplitude index (PAx – correlation between the pulse amplitude of ICP [AMP] and MAP), and RAC (the correlation [R] between AMP [A] and CPP [C]) were also compared for their utility in deriving iICP, with results suggesting that PRx may provide the greatest utility when considering both outcome prediction capability and derivation yield. In a later analysis, using the same custom algorithm, we conducted a thorough thresholding analysis comparing a comprehensive list of thresholds for PRx, PAx, and RAC in deriving iICP [43]. We were unable to identify an ideal threshold for PAx- and RAC-based iICP; however, an optimal threshold of + 0.05 was identified for PRx-based iICP.

iICP thresholds represent a promising potential personalized medicine approach in neurocritical care. However, despite the promising developments described above, the current iICP derivation methods rely on the use of entire recording periods, thereby restricting their use to retrospective, post hoc calculations. This inability to allow for real-time calculations of iICP at the patient bedside significantly limits any potential clinical applicability. Furthermore, existing methods do not account for the variable quality of their outputs. The reliability of an iICP estimate depends heavily on the characteristics of its underlying ICP-CVR curve, such as goodness-of-fit and overall shape. This means that not all iICP estimates are equally reliable and, therefore, would create uncertainty for clinical end-users attempting to interpret these values in real-time. To address these limitations, a continuously updating iICP derivation algorithm, analogous to those that exist for CPPopt [44–46], that also provides accompanying quality measures is needed. In this study, we sought to develop such an algorithm, evaluate its derivation yields, and identify patient-related factors that may influence yields.

Methods

Patient population

For the purposes of this study, all available archived patient datasets from the Winnipeg Acute TBI database, managed by the Multi-omic Analytics and Integrative Neuroinformatics in the HUman Brain (MAIN-HUB) Lab at the University of Manitoba, were utilized [47]. This repository includes all adult (≥ 18 years of age) moderate-to-severe (Glasgow Coma Score [GCS] ≤ 12) TBI patients who were admitted for invasive cerebral physiologic monitoring to the intensive care unit (ICU) at the Health Sciences Centre in Winnipeg, Canada (Shared Health Manitoba) since January of 2019. All patients received standard care management in line with the Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines and contemporary consensus recommendations for ICP-monitored patients [1, 3]. This includes maintenance of ICP below 22 mmHg and CPP between 60 and 70 mmHg. Elevated CPP was not routinely treated unless clinically indicated, in accordance with local practice.

All patients were intubated, sedated, and mechanically ventilated with positive pressure ventilation, initially targeting normocapnia and normoxia based on end-tidal and arterial blood gas monitoring. General supportive measures included head-of-bed elevation (30–45°), maintenance of euthermia, avoidance of hypoxia and systemic hypotension, and correction of anemia and electrolyte abnormalities. Intracranial hypertension was managed using a tiered escalation strategy, including optimization of sedation and analgesia, intermittent CSF drainage when an EVD was present, and hyperosmolar therapy. Prophylactic or prolonged hyperventilation was avoided. Vasopressor support was titrated according to ICP and hemodynamic response. Central venous pressure was intermittently assessed, with fluid resuscitation guided by low values in conjunction with clinical and laboratory indicators of hypovolemia, at the discretion of the treating critical care physician. Escalation to higher-tier therapies (e.g., short-duration mild hyperventilation, neuromuscular blockade, barbiturate therapy, induced hypothermia, or decompressive craniectomy) was reserved for refractory intracranial hypertension.

ICP monitoring was conducted using intra-parenchymal strain gauge probes (Codman ICP MicroSensor; Codman & Shurtleff Inc., Raynham, MA, USA), placed in the frontal lobe, or using external ventricular drains (EVD; n = 4). ABP monitoring was performed using radial arterial lines connected to pressure transducers (Baxter Healthcare Corp. CardioVascular Group, Irvine, CA, USA), zeroed at the level of the tragus [48, 49].

Data collection

As part of the Winnipeg Acute TBI Database, all available high-frequency physiologic signals are recorded in real-time from patient bedside monitors using Intensive Care Monitoring “Plus” (ICM+; Cambridge Enterprise Ltd, Cambridge, UK, http://icmplus.neurosurg.cam.ac.uk), at sampling frequencies of 100 Hz or higher. Physiologic data capture is initiated within 24 h of the time of injury, following admission to the ICU and placement of invasive monitoring devices, and is performed using either direct digital data transfer or analog-to-digital signal conversion (DT9804/DT9826, Data Translations, Marlboro, MA, USA). Data collection continued until invasive physiologic monitoring was discontinued by the treating clinical team.

Additionally, demographic details, admission characteristics, imaging results, and daily therapeutic intensity level (TIL) scores are collected from patient bedside records. Following discharge from the ICU, patients undergo routine follow-ups at 1, 3, and 6 months, during which their outcome status is assessed and recorded using the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended [50]. These assessments are conducted by experienced specialist surgeons through structured interviews with the patient and, when applicable, their designated proxies. All data is thoroughly de-identified and stored securely in an encrypted digital repository.

Signal processing

To ensure data quality, signal artifacts were removed in ICM+ by trained professionals, who were blinded to the study objectives, using a combination of automated and manual techniques. Data segments with implausible values (defined as outside the range of 0–100 mmHg for ICP and 0–300 mmHg for ABP) or without discernible waveform morphology (i.e., static signals) were excluded, as these reflect non-physiologic events such as monitor disconnection, arterial line flushing, or patient movement. In cases where EVDs were used, drain-opening artifacts were additionally identified and removed.

Following artifact detection and removal, the signals were processed using ICM+. Fourier analysis of the ICP pulse waveform was performed on contiguous 10-second windows of data to derive AMP [51–53]. A 10-second non-overlapping moving average filter was then applied to both ICP and ABP (yielding MAP) to isolate the frequency range most pertinent to CVR [54, 55], as well as minimize respiratory-related oscillations [6]. CPP was computed as the difference between MAP and ICP (CPP = MAP – ICP). CVR was quantified using three ICP-based surrogate indices: PRx, PAx, and RAC. PRx was derived as the Pearson correlation coefficient between 30 consecutive 10-second averages of ICP and MAP, updated every minute [28, 29, 56]. PAx and RAC were calculated in a similar manner, correlating AMP with MAP and AMP with CPP, respectively [52, 57].

Finally, all data was down-sampled to minute-by-minute resolution and exported as comma-separated values (CSV) files for further processing and analysis in R (Version 4.5.0; R Core Team, 2025. R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/).

Continuous iICP derivation algorithm

Building upon our previously described iICP framework [42], we developed a custom algorithm in R (with packages dplyr and ggplot2) capable of continuously calculating an updating iICP threshold. The algorithm sequentially processes a time-series dataset and recalculates iICP values at user-defined intervals (e.g., every minute). We leveraged a sliding multi-window weighted approach, similar to that seen with recent renditions of CPPopt [44–46], allowing for integration of information across multiple temporal scales while prioritizing data segments that are more recent and yield more stable, physiologically plausible estimates. Manual validation of our algorithm was deemed unnecessary, as it was developed based on a previously validated framework that had undergone manual verification [42]. Algorithm iterations tested

To evaluate how different parameter settings influence algorithmic derivation yields, we conducted multiple iterations of our algorithm on the Winnipeg Acute TBI Database cohort. Firstly, the algorithm was run using three ICP-based CVR indices: PRx, PAx, and RAC. Example ICP-CVR plots and iICP identification using these indices can be found in Supplemental Appendix B. Thresholds tested included those previously reported in the literature to best discriminate between outcomes: 0, + 0.25, and + 0.35 for PRx [25, 29, 58]; 0 and + 0.25 for PAx [25, 58]; and 0 for RAC [25, 58]. In addition, a threshold of + 0.20 was tested for all three indices, as this value was used exclusively in the original iICP studies [40, 41]. The + 0.05 threshold for PRx, identified in our earlier thresholding analysis [43], was also evaluated.

Next, to assess the benefits of employing a multi-window weighted approach, we performed five fixed-window iterations of the algorithm. While the standard multi-window configuration applies window widths of 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h concurrently; these iterations utilized a single window width each. Accordingly, 1-, 2-, 4-, 8-, and 12-hour fixed-window runs were conducted. Additionally, the influence of update frequency on iICP yields was also assessed by testing update intervals of 1, 15, 30, 60, and 240 min. Lastly, the algorithm was implemented in three modeling configurations: (1) the standard configuration using both LOESS and cubic spline modeling; (2) a LOESS-only version; and (3) a cubic-spline-only version. This comparison aimed to determine whether combining modeling methods enhances derivation yields or if one method performs better than the other.

For each combination of algorithmic variations, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were calculated for the following variables across the entire study cohort: derivation yield percentage, mean iICP, mean total score, mean curve shape score, mean CI score, and mean data range score. The resulting summary metrics were then organized into tables to allow for comparison of the different algorithm iterations.

Subgroup analysis

To identify patient-related factors that influence iICP derivation yields, a series of subgroup analyses were conducted. First, time-based analyses were conducted to evaluate whether iICP yields differ across different phases of the ICU stay. These included comparisons between the first 24 h versus the remainder of the recording period, and between the first 72 h versus the remainder of the recording period.

Next, demographic- and admission-related factors were assessed. Subgroups were defined based on sex (male vs. female), age (< 40 years, 40–60 years, > 60 years), admission GCS-motor score, moderate (GCS 9–12) vs. severe (GCS 3–8) TBI, pupillary response (bilaterally reactive, unilaterally unreactive, bilaterally unreactive), and admission Marshall CT score (II, III, IV, V). Additional admission characteristics were also analyzed, including the presence of hypoxia, hypotension, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage (tSAH), and epidural hematoma.

To assess the impact of therapeutic intensity, days of monitoring were grouped based on daily TIL scores. Unlike the other subgroup analyses, which grouped patients, this analysis was performed using days of data, compiled from across the entire cohort. For all subgroup analyses, between-group comparisons were conducted using Mann–Whitney U tests for dichotomous variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for variables with more than two groups. The threshold for statistical significance was set to p < 0.05.

Lastly, to evaluate how iICP behaves during periods of cerebral physiologic insult compared with more stable conditions, minute-by-minute physiologic data were stratified for each patient by ICP (< 20 mmHg, 20–25 mmHg, > 25 mmHg), CPP (< 60 mmHg, 60–70 mmHg, > 70 mmHg), and cerebrovascular reactivity (PRx, PAx, and RAC; < 0 a.u., 0–0.20 a.u., and > 0.20 a.u.). The listed thresholds used for stratification were selected based on a previously published methodology [59]. For each stratum, patient-level summaries of mean iICP, derivation yield, and mean quality metrics were calculated and tabulated. Group differences across strata were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis testing.

Results

Study cohort

A total of 131 patient datasets from the Winnipeg Acute TBI Database were included in this analysis. The cohort had a median age of 42 years (IQR: 27–57), a median admission GCS score of 6 (IQR: 4–8), and consisted of predominantly males (83%). Approximately 64% of patients were alive at 6 months post-injury. Duration of physiologic data recording varied across the cohort, ranging from as low as 14 h to approximately 10 days, with a median duration of 79.2 h (IQR: 37.4–133.9). Algorithmic yields

The performance summaries of the various multi-window weighted algorithm iterations are presented in Supplemental Appendix C. Algorithm performance varied significantly depending on parameter settings used for derivation. Between the three modeling variations, the combined modeling approach (using both LOESS and cubic spline) consistently produced higher yields than either model alone. The combined model iterations generally achieved 2–5% higher yields than their corresponding LOESS-only iterations, and 4–8% higher yields than their corresponding cubic spline-only iterations. iICP quality metrics were comparable across modeling approaches. Regarding update frequency, longer update intervals tended to produce slightly lower derivation yields, generally decreasing by approximately 4–6% between the shortest (1-minute) and longest (240-minute) update intervals.

Between the various CVR index-threshold combinations tested, stark differences in performance were observed. Overall, the PRx-based iterations consistently produced the highest derivation yields and quality scores across all algorithmic variations. RAC-based iterations performed the poorest, regardless of the modeling approach or update frequency used, with yields frequently falling below 40%. PAx outperformed RAC but remained substantially inferior to PRx, with yields approximately 10–20% lower for corresponding iterations. For both PAx and RAC, a threshold of 0 yielded the best performances, though still considerably worse than the poorest-performing PRx configurations. Among the PRx thresholds, + 0.20 and + 0.25 were found to perform best, with yields consistently exceeding 60% for the combined modeling approach. Overall, the 1-minute updating, combined modeling approach iteration using PRx > + 0.25 achieved the highest median yield of 66.09%.

The performance results of the fixed-window iterations are presented in Supplemental Appendices D.1–D.5. The same general trends seen with the multi-window weighted iterations were observed here, with PRx-based iterations, the combined modeling approach, and shorter update intervals consistently performing best. However, compared with the multi-window weighted method, the fixed-window approach produced substantially lower derivation yields, often reduced by more than half. Interestingly, increasing the fixed-window width was associated with progressively poorer yields, with the 12-hour fixed-window iterations achieving yields of no more than 18.4% while using a 1-hour window allowed for yields up to 33.6%. Though seemingly counterintuitive, this likely results from instability of the ICP–CVR relationship over extended periods, due to more frequent changes in treatment and physiologic state, which prevent proper curve fitting. Conversely, larger window widths were associated with higher quality scores, with fixed-window sizes of 4 h or longer even achieving mean total scores exceeding those of the multi-window weighted approach; however, this was mainly driven by greater data range scores.

Subgroup analysis

Derivation yields varied considerably across the patient cohort, ranging from near 0% in some patients to over 90% in others. Histograms illustrating the distributions of iICP derivation yields are shown in Supplemental Appendices E.1 (for PRx-based iICP) and E.2 (for PAx- and RAC-based iICP). Mean iICP values also demonstrated substantial variation across the patient cohort. Histograms illustrating the distribution of mean iICP values can be found in Supplemental Appendices F.1 (for PRx-based iICP) and F.2 (for PAx- and RAC-based iICP). Subgroup analyses were subsequently performed to shed light on potential drivers of this significant variation.

The results of the time-based subgroup analyses are presented in Supplemental Appendices G.1-G.2. iICP derivation yields and quality scores were significantly lower during the first 24 h of ICU monitoring compared to the remainder of the recording period. However, this difference became substantially less pronounced and fell out of statistical significance when comparing the first 72 h with the rest of the monitoring period.

The results of the demographic- and admission-related subgroup analyses are summarized in Supplemental Appendices H.1-H.10, respectively. Generally, no statistically significant differences were observed for sex, admission GCS-motor score, pupillary response, admission hypotension, admission tSAH, or admission epidural hematoma. Younger patients (< 40 years) demonstrated lower mean iICP values and higher mean curve-shape scores. Having severe TBI (GCS 3–8) was associated with greater derivation yields and greater mean iICP values for PRx-based iICP (with the exception of PRx > + 0.35). The Marshall CT score analysis showed that intermediate scores (III–IV) were associated with greater mean iICP values, total scores, and data-range scores. Presence of hypoxia on admission was found to be associated with greater yields, but for PRx-based iterations only.

The treatment-based subgroup analysis can be found in Supplemental Appendix I. Statistically significant differences were observed across TIL groups for yield, mean iICP, and mean quality scores; however, overall directional trends were generally inconsistent and difficult to discern. Yields were generally comparable among TIL 1–3 groups but were noticeably lower in the TIL 4 group. Mean iICP values showed a clear upward trend with increasing therapeutic intensity, whereas quality scores demonstrated no consistent directional pattern.

Lastly, the results of the physiologic stratification analyses are presented in Supplemental Appendix J. For the most part, statistically significant differences were observed only with ICP-based stratification. Periods of elevated ICP were associated with higher iICP values, greater derivation yield, and improved data range scores. In contrast, mean iICP values, derivation yields, and mean quality metrics were largely comparable across CPP and CVR strata.

Discussion

We were able to develop the first automated algorithm capable of deriving iICP in a continuously updating fashion. Although applied retrospectively in this study, the algorithm processes physiologic data sequentially, effectively mimicking the temporal dynamics of real-time monitoring. This design allows iICP to be recalculated at user-defined intervals as new data becomes available, enabling fully automated, real-time calculations of iICP that are compatible with clinical bedside use. Furthermore, the inclusion of quality metrics alongside iICP outputs allows potential clinical end-users to assess the confidence and reliability of each iICP estimate, thereby supporting more informed bedside decision-making.

In addition to laying out the computational workflow of our custom-made algorithm, we also evaluated its performance across a range of parameter configurations and examined various patient-related factors that may influence derivation yields.

Impact of parameter selection on algorithmic performance

The results of our yield analysis demonstrate that algorithm performance is highly variable depending on various parameter settings. Firstly, as expected, using both LOESS and cubic spline modeling in conjunction produced greater derivation yields than using either modeling method in isolation. The 2–8% improvement in yields relative to the single-model approaches indicates that integrating multiple curve-fitting techniques enhances overall algorithm stability. Interestingly, all three modeling configurations demonstrated comparable quality metrics, further supporting the use of the two modeling methods in conjunction. Additionally, this suggests that the benefit of the dual-model approach likely lies exclusively in improved algorithmic stability, rather than greater reliability of iICP outputs.

Update frequency was also found to influence algorithm performance. Shorter update intervals (e.g., 1 min) were found to result in slightly greater yields than longer intervals. This is likely because longer update intervals have a greater probability that a recalculation occurs during periods when no valid iICP can be identified. Conversely, shorter update intervals provide more frequent opportunities for recalculation, increasing the chance of capturing physiologically favorable moments which permit derivation of an iICP threshold. Although shorter update intervals appear to produce greater derivation yields, excessively high update frequencies would be impractical in a clinical setting, as they would necessitate overly frequent and rapid adjustments in management. Therefore, the optimal update frequency for iICP derivation will likely represent a balance between maximizing derivation yield and maintaining clinical feasibility.

Next, the choice of CVR index and threshold had a substantial impact on algorithm performance. Consistent with prior literature [42], PRx-based iterations produced the highest derivation yields, while RAC-based iterations consistently yielded the lowest. The blatant superiority of PRx, with regard to both iICP yields and quality scores, strongly supports its preferential use over PAx and RAC. However, it is currently unclear whether PRx-derived iICP has superior associations with cerebral physiologic insult burden and long-term outcomes. Although one study has suggested this [42], it would be premature to exclude PAx and RAC from future investigations until their inferiority to PRx, with regard to all aspects of iICP derivation, has been clearly demonstrated. Among the thresholds tested, PRx thresholds of + 0.20 and + 0.25 were associated with the greatest yields and quality scores. This contrasts with earlier findings that identified a PRx threshold of + 0.35 as producing the greatest yields [42].

Lastly, our analysis clearly demonstrates the superiority of the multi-window weighted approach over fixed-window methods. Although the sliding fixed-window approach produced similar overall performance patterns across algorithmic iterations, its derivation yields were markedly lower, often by more than half. This highlights the significant benefit of integrating multiple overlapping windows of data rather than relying on a single window width. This observation is consistent with prior CPPopt modeling studies demonstrating improved performance with multi-window approaches, which the authors attributed to more effective capture of both local and global ICP-CVR relationships and enhanced robustness to transient signal irregularities [45, 46].

Patient-related factors that may influence yields

Our subgroup analyses provided valuable insight into how demographic-, admission-, and treatment-related factors may influence iICP derivation yields. The time-based analyses revealed that iICP derivation yields and quality scores were significantly lower during the first 24 h of ICU monitoring compared to later phases of monitoring. This is likely a consequence of the physiologic instability often observed early after injury, when cerebrovascular dynamics are rapidly evolving and the ICP-CVR relationship is more variable, thereby hindering the algorithm’s ability to identify stable iICP values. The fact that these differences diminished and fell out of statistical significance by 72 h suggests that as patients stabilize, autoregulatory relationships become more consistent and the algorithm’s performance correspondingly improves. Overall, these findings suggest improved signal stability and physiologic consistency over time.

Demographic- and admission-related factors demonstrated minimal influence, with only a few significant associations observed. The absence of differences for pupillary response and admission GCS-motor score suggests that iICP derivation yield is likely independent of overall injury severity or global neurological status. The observation that younger patients exhibited lower mean iICP values may indicate that older patients require stricter ICP control or maintenance of lower ICP levels to preserve autoregulatory capacity. The observation that more severe TBI is associated with greater derivation yields and greater mean iICP values (for PRx-based iICP) suggests that increased physiologic variability, seen with physiologic stress, may enhance the detectability of iICP by providing a broader and more dynamic range of data for model fitting. This conclusion is further supported by the observation that admission hypoxia was associated with higher yields in PRx-based iterations.

The treatment intensity subgroup analysis revealed that days with higher TIL scores tended to have higher mean iICPs and lower derivation yields. This is likely attributable to the combined physiologic effects of more frequent intracranial hypertension and the use of aggressive ICP-directed therapy. Patients requiring greater therapeutic intensity often exhibit persistently elevated ICP, which may distort the normal ICP-CVR relationship and shift identified iICPs toward higher values. At the same time, ICP-directed interventions reduce physiologic variability and spontaneous cerebrovascular fluctuations, which are essential for iICP derivation, thereby leading to lower yields. This suggests that iICP derivation may be constrained under conditions of suppressed physiologic variability.

The physiologic stratification analyses demonstrated that periods of elevated ICP were associated with higher iICP values, greater derivation yield, and improved data-range scores. This suggests that (1) under conditions of increased intracranial stress, the ICP level at which CVR becomes impaired may shift upward, and (2) greater ICP variability, which is more commonly observed during intracranial hypertension, facilitates improved model fitting, thereby resulting in higher derivation yield and more stable iICP estimation. In contrast, no statistically significant differences in mean iICP or derivation performance were observed across CVR strata. This indicates that continuously derived iICP is relatively robust to short-term fluctuations in CVR state within a given window and that reliable iICP estimation depends more heavily on sufficient ICP variability to support robust curve fitting.

Clinical potential of the iICP concept

Although established guideline-based ICP thresholds (e.g., 20 and 22 mmHg) are associated with cerebral physiologic insult and poor outcomes, these cutoffs were derived from population-based analyses linking grand-averaged ICP values to long-term outcomes [7]. As such, these static thresholds do not account for inter-individual variability in autoregulatory capacity, injury heterogeneity, or the dynamic and evolving nature of cerebral physiology following TBI [8]. Continuously derived iICP represents a potential personalized physiologic solution to this limitation by estimating a dynamic ICP boundary based on the individual’s cerebrovascular response profile.

If prospectively validated, continuously derived iICP is not intended to replace established guideline-based ICP thresholds, but rather to complement them by providing a dynamic, patient-specific upper ICP boundary that reflects individualized cerebrovascular physiology. Such a personalized target may help identify periods during which there is greater physiologic tolerance to transient ICP elevations (i.e. when iICP exceeds conventional guideline-based thresholds), as well as periods during which more stringent ICP control may be warranted than suggested by population-based cutoffs (i.e. when iICP falls below conventional guideline-based thresholds). In this way, iICP may provide contextual information regarding the physiologic significance of a given ICP value rather than treating all threshold crossings as equivalent. Within our cohort, patients spent a median of 24.3% of identifiable monitoring time with iICP within ± 5 mmHg of 22 mmHg, 4.9% of time with iICP > 27 mmHg, and 70.8% of time with iICP < 17 mmHg. This suggests that substantial portions of the ICU stay may require stricter ICP control than a fixed 22 mmHg threshold would imply. A similar pattern can be seen in Fig. 4, where the iICP is below 20 mmHg for most of the time trend.

From a clinical workflow perspective, iICP could be integrated into existing neurocritical care monitoring platforms and displayed alongside ICP, CPP, and CVR indices as an additional real-time parameter. Conceptually, it could function within the existing tiered management frameworks outlined by the Brain Trauma Foundation and SIBICC guidelines. For example, when ICP exceeds the patient-specific iICP threshold, this may signal a higher likelihood of autoregulatory impairment and prompt escalation within the established treatment algorithm. Conversely, when ICP transiently exceeds conventional population-based cutoffs but remains below iICP, clinicians may interpret this in the context of relatively preserved autoregulatory tolerance, potentially avoiding unnecessary therapeutic escalation.

However, it is important to note that this study merely introduces an algorithmic framework for continuously deriving iICP thresholds, and as a methodology-focused investigation, does not demonstrate clinical efficacy. Therefore, these proposed applications of iICP remain conceptual. To determine whether iICP-guided management meaningfully alters therapeutic decision-making or improves outcomes beyond conventional static threshold-based care, prospective validation and randomized clinical trials will be required. We are therefore not advocating for the clinical implementation of the iICP concept at this time. Rather, we wish to only provide a continuous derivation framework for future development and validation of the iICP concept. Nonetheless, continuously derived iICP thresholds represent a potential step toward more individualized, physiology-informed neurocritical care, aligning ICP management with the broader movement toward personalized physiologic targeting in TBI management.

Limitations

Despite the promising results of our study, several important limitations must be acknowledged. Although our algorithm was designed to be compatible with real-time implementation at the patient bedside, the algorithm was only tested retrospectively on previously recorded physiologic data. Consequently, the performance of the algorithm in a real clinical setting remains to be validated. Moreover, the algorithm was only tested on a single-center TBI cohort, which may restrict the generalizability of these findings to other patient populations.

Next, the overall derivation yields of our algorithm remain modest. The best-performing iteration (the 1-minute updating, LOESS-cubic spline combined modeling approach using PRx > + 0.25) achieved a median yield of 66.1%. Although this yield is comparable to those reported in previous non-continuous iICP studies [40–42], it remains notably lower than that achieved by recent CPPopt renditions, which have demonstrated yields exceeding 90% [45, 60].

We also acknowledge that only a limited number of CVR thresholds were evaluated in this study and that thresholds identified in prior critical thresholding analyses may not directly translate to optimal values for continuous iICP derivation. A comprehensive comparison of a broader range of CVR thresholds in deriving continuous iICP specifically may further refine algorithm performance and will require dedicated investigation in future work.

Another noteworthy limitation is that several components of the curve-scoring framework reflect informed but ultimately subjective methodological choices, as no universally accepted standards for grading ICP-CVR curve morphology or defining optimal qualification thresholds exist. Although these criteria were selected to balance physiologic interpretability, effective discrimination between high- and low-quality curves, and algorithmic stability, and although many parameters are end-user configurable, the criteria used here will require formal validation, refinement, and potential reweighting in future investigations.

Another important limitation of our study is that derivation yields were used as the primary measure of algorithm performance. A high yield does not necessarily translate to clinical utility, and clinically important parameters such as prognostic associations with long-term outcomes or cerebral physiologic insult burden were not assessed. Future dedicated studies evaluating the association between continuously derived iICP and these key parameters will be needed.

Lastly, although the computational workflow has been described in detail to permit conceptual replication, the full implementation of the algorithm is not yet publicly available due to institutional intellectual property constraints. This may limit immediate independent validation and external application by groups outside our current collaborations. Future controlled dissemination of the algorithm (i.e. through institutional licensing frameworks) will be pursued once intellectual property considerations are resolved. We also acknowledge that our current algorithm is a custom-coded entity, limiting its utility for bedside monitoring and clinical application. Future work will be required to integrate this automated pipeline into existing data acquisition platforms, such as the Moberg CNS Monitor system or ICM+, to improve accessibility and facilitate broader implementation.

Future directions

Although the development of our custom algorithm represents a substantial step forward in advancing iICP research, considerable further work is required before clinical implementation of iICP thresholds can become feasible. Firstly, our continuously updating iICP derivation algorithm requires external validation using a large multi-center TBI cohort to demonstrate generalizability of its performance. Such work is already currently underway through the CAnadian High Resolution (CAHR) TBI Research Collaborative, which maintains a repository of high-frequency cerebral physiologic data from approximately 400 moderate-to-severe TBI patients across four university-affiliated hospitals. Additionally, further validation in non-TBI neurocritical care populations will be needed to demonstrate the existence and relevance of iICP in these populations.

Second, further refinement and methodological advancement of the continuous iICP framework will be needed, particularly with respect to improving algorithmic yield. Yields below 70% present a major obstacle for clinical application, as they limit the reliability and continuity of potential iICP-directed care. Inconsistent output may reduce clinician confidence and undermine the practicality of such personalized care in real-world settings. Efforts to enhance yields and overall algorithmic performance should focus on refining curve fitting, optimizing parameter selection, and improving window-weighting strategies. Exploration of more sophisticated modeling approaches, such as symbolic regression or the advanced techniques recently implemented for CPPopt derivation [45, 60], is also warranted. Additionally, further investigations into determinants of derivation yields are needed to better understand the substantial inter-patient variability observed. Future iterations should also evaluate window-level outlier-handling and enhanced data-quality control strategies to further improve derivation stability and robustness. Also, once optimal CVR thresholds for iICP derivation have been more clearly defined, investigation of multi-index aggregation strategies that jointly leverage PRx, PAx, and RAC may represent a logical next step in improving the robustness of continuous iICP derivation.

Next, thorough outcome analyses will be needed to determine whether iICP offers meaningful associations with long-term outcomes. Such prognostic validation analyses are currently underway in a separate multi-center cohort study and will be reported in a dedicated manuscript to allow rigorous evaluation independent of the methodological focus of the present work. However, ultimately, to determine whether iICP-guided management offers genuine clinical benefit, a prospective randomized controlled trial will be required to evaluate whether it leads to improved long-term outcomes compared with current guideline-based management.

In parallel, detailed characterization of the temporal dynamics of continuously derived iICP across the recovery phase will be needed. Application of multivariate pattern analyses (e.g. hierarchical clustering and principal component analysis) alongside time-series approaches, including cross-correlation analysis, Granger testing, and vector autoregressive modeling, will allow identification of physiologic states associated with shifts in iICP and quantification of time-resolved, directionally informative relationships between iICP and other multimodal cerebral physiologic parameters, including systemic hemodynamic variables such as MAP and CPP. Such work is important for refining the iICP concept, as it will help determine the physiologic drivers and temporal stability of iICP, thereby informing how iICP can be optimally interpreted and integrated into dynamic bedside monitoring frameworks.

Continuous, invasive CBF monitoring was not available within our clinical setting, as there is currently no Health Canada-approved device that provides reliable continuous bedside CBF measurement for routine TBI care. However, future investigations incorporating direct CBF-based approaches to iICP and CPPopt derivation, rather than surrogate cerebrovascular reactivity measures, would be of significant interest and may further refine physiologic targeting strategies. Additionally, integration of advanced systemic hemodynamic monitoring with high-frequency cerebral physiologic data in future investigations could help clarify cardiocerebral interactions and their influence on iICP behavior.

Finally, if iICP is to ever be implemented clinically, substantial technical work will be needed to tailor the algorithm for bedside integration. This will involve developing an intuitive user interface that allows clinicians to easily monitor and interpret iICP outputs in real-time. Additionally, the algorithm will need to be adapted to utilize raw physiologic data streams in real-time. All existing work on iICP has utilized highly curated datasets that differ substantially from what is typically available at the patient bedside in real-world clinical settings. In reality, the raw physiologic signals used for iICP derivation, ICP and ABP waveforms, are frequently affected by signal artifacts that can significantly distort the ICP-CVR relationship and reduce algorithm reliability. Consequently, reliable continuous derivation of iICP, from raw bedside data, is currently not feasible without extensive manual artifact clearing. To bridge this gap, future work should prioritize the development of an automated artifact management system using a combination of various computational techniques, such as basic thresholding, Fourier transform, and machine learning. Such a system would allow for artifact free signals in real-time and would represent a critical step toward enabling iICP derivation at the patient bedside.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated the feasibility of deriving iICP in a continuously updating fashion by developing a custom, automated algorithm that employs a multi-window weighted approach and incorporates complementary quality metrics for reliability assessment. We found that algorithm performance varies significantly with parameter selection, including modeling method, CVR index-threshold combination, and update frequency. Combined use of LOESS and cubic spline modeling, use of PRx and a threshold of + 0.20 or + 0.25, and a high update frequency were found to consistently produce the greatest yields. Additionally, the multi-window weighted approach was shown to substantially outperform fixed-window configurations. Although the development of our custom algorithm represents an important milestone in iICP research, further work will be needed to validate our algorithm, improve derivation yields, confirm prognostic and physiologic associations, and enable bedside implementation.
