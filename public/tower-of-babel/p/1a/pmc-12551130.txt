Prediction of postoperative haemorrhage after cerebral tumour surgery using machine learning algorithms
Authors: Yasin Göktürk, Seyit Kağan Başarslan, Şule Göktürk, Hikmet Kocaman, Hasan Yıldırım
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional diagnostic methods used by neurosurgeons are limited in their ability to address complex interactions. These limitations have necessitated the use of advanced artificial intelligence approaches capable of analyzing multidimensional data with greater precision in neurosurgical clinics. Postoperative intracranial hemorrhage is a critical complication following cerebral tumor surgery, often associated with increased morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to predict the risk of postoperative intracerebral hemorrhage in patients undergoing intracranial tumor surgery by employing machine learning (ML) algorithms for risk stratification and identifying key contributing factors.MethodsThis retrospective study included 118 patients monitored in the neurosurgical intensive care unit between January 2024 and January 2025. The primary outcome was postoperative hemorrhage, defined as a radiologically confirmed hematoma ≥ 5 ml on brain CT within 24 h. Using a predefined set of clinical and biochemical parameters analyzed with SPSS and R, multiple ML algorithms were developed. To address class imbalance in the training data, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied. Models were evaluated using metrics including Area Under the Curve (AUC), accuracy, and F1-score, with further assessment via calibration plots and Decision Curve Analysis (DCA).ResultsThe LightGBM model demonstrated a robust and balanced predictive performance, achieving a test AUC of 0.7451, an accuracy of 76.9%, a sensitivity of 77.8%, and an F1-score of 0.700. Platelet count (PLT), serum chloride (Cl), and the change in C-reactive protein from pre- to postoperative state (delta-CRP) emerged as the most influential predictors of hemorrhage. Model explainability was enhanced using SHAP and LIME analyses, and the model showed good calibration with potential clinical net benefit.ConclusionOur study suggests that ML algorithms, particularly LightGBM, show promise for predicting postoperative hemorrhage following brain tumor surgery. Biomarkers such as platelet count, chloride, and delta-CRP offer clinically meaningful insights for early risk detection. Once externally validated, the integration of such models into clinical decision support systems could potentially improve postoperative monitoring and patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-025-03245-8.

Introduction

In recent years, the use of machine learning programs in medicine has increased significantly and has shown great potential in various fields, particularly in the management of brain disorders [1]. Traditional diagnostic methods used by neurosurgeons are limited in their ability to address complex interactions. These limitations have necessitated the use of advanced artificial intelligence approaches capable of analyzing multidimensional data with greater precision in neurosurgical clinics. While these algorithms have not been used clinically, the results suggest promising avenues for their adoption to guide clinicians in selecting the appropriate treatment option for each patient. Machine learning (ML) has gained significant global attention due to its revolutionary capabilities in medical research by providing robust tools to examine complex datasets, identify subtle patterns, and produce accurate predictions [2]. Its capacity to process comprehensive clinical and biological data, often beyond the limitations of traditional methods, has also enabled rapid advances in the field of neurosurgery, such as identifying correlations between multiple biomarkers.

AI has emerged as a transformative tool in medical diagnostics, providing significant advances in the detection and management of complex and critical conditions [3]. AI algorithms using machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) models have demonstrated remarkable accuracy in analyzing medical imaging data such as MRI and CT scans, facilitating early and precise diagnosis. This is critical because while traditional diagnostic methods struggle to predict postoperative bleeding in operated cranial masses, AI programs can predict bleeding risk by integrating biomarkers and clinical data.”

Neurosurgical surgery means “craniotomy”. It includes basic techniques used to reach the surgical area in intracranial pathologies. It consists of a series of technical methods used in cerebral tumors. Management of bleeding-coagulation is difficult in intracerebral tumor surgery (ICTS) and subsequent intensive care follow-ups. In this struggle, avoiding intracranial bleeding is mandatory and has prognostic value. Perioperative bleeding can be associated with a number of factors including anticoagulant drugs and coagulation status. In addition, it is also related to the characteristic-morphological features of the intracranial mass and its intracerebral location [4].

After craniotomy performed in ICTS, infections and bleeding in the surgical cavity can be a common and potentially fatal complication in neurosurgical intensive care [5]. Regular neurological assessment in the intensive care unit or recovery room is extremely important. Unexpected onset or worsening of neurological signs and any deterioration in the patient’s state of consciousness should prompt immediate neuroradiological investigation. Urgent computed tomography of the brain is the method of choice if intracranial complications are suspected after brain tumor removal [6]. Although it is controlled with computed tomography (CT) within the first 24 h postoperatively [7], when bleeding occurs, it causes a decrease in the patient’s Glasgow coma score and may require reoperation. It is vital to be able to foresee this situation and take precautions.

Perioperative bleeding can be associated with a number of factors, including antihemostatic and anticoagulant medications [8]. On the other hand, the risk of venous thromboembolism is also increased after elective and emergency neurosurgery, and a hypercoagulable state has been described in a few patients, particularly after surgery for ICTS [9]. It is important to know what changes occur in coagulation in the perioperative period to balance the risk of thrombosis against the risk of bleeding. Routine coagulation tests include activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), prothrombin time (PT), platelet count (Plt) and fibrinogen levels. A study conducted to determine the incidence of postoperative hematoma and to identify risk factors for perioperative bleeding disorders during the 5-year period from 1989 to 1993 found that two-thirds of patients had risk factors for perioperative bleeding disorders. Administration of antiplatelet agents (aspirin and nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs) was the most frequently associated risk factor. At least 75% of these identified risk factors were potentially preventable or amenable to correction [10]. In patients who develop bleeding at the surgical site, blood transfusions and blood component therapy can be life-saving in life-threatening bleeding situations.

The increasing availability of complex clinical data has driven the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) as a powerful analytical paradigm in modern medicine. A key subfield, machine learning (ML), encompasses a suite of algorithms designed to identify subtle, non-linear patterns within large datasets—relationships often missed by traditional statistical methods. Within neurosurgery, these technologies are already enhancing diagnostic accuracy in tumor classification, optimizing intraoperative decision-making, and improving neurovascular risk prediction [11–13]. However, despite this promising potential, their application in predicting postoperative intracerebral hemorrhage remains limited, highlighting the need for targeted studies such as the present research. To our knowledge, there is no study on machine learning that can specifically predict postoperative hemorrhage in patients who have undergone intracerebral tumor surgery. Therefore, the aim of the current study was to predict postoperative bleeding using machine learning algorithms based on clinical and laboratory results from patients after brain tumor surgery.

Material and method

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a single tertiary care center. The study protocol was approved by the Kayseri City Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: 346/25.02.2025) and performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised 1983). Owing to the retrospective design and use of fully anonymized data, the requirement for individual patient informed consent was formally waived by the “Kayseri City Hospital Ethics Committee”, in line with the Regulation on Clinical Researches of the Ministry of Health of Turkey (Official Gazette No. 28617, 13 April 2013). This study is reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement (The completed TRIPOD checklist is available in Supplementary Table S1).

We retrospectively analyzed the records of 118 patients who underwent intracranial tumor surgery and were subsequently monitored in the neurosurgical intensive care unit between January 2024 and January 2025.

Outcome and predictor variables

The primary outcome was postoperative hemorrhage, defined as a radiologically confirmed new or expanded hematoma with a volume of ≥ 5 ml on the routine postoperative head CT scan performed in 24 h after surgery. This threshold was selected based on prior neurosurgical literature where such volumes are considered clinically significant and associated with an increased risk of neurological deterioration. Wang et al. combined the modified Graeb score and the intracerebral hemorrhage score to predict poor outcome in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage undergoing surgery. In this study, they defined hematoma growth as an increase in hematoma volume of >33% or >6 ml on follow-up CT scans [14]. Demchuck et al. used hematoma expansion, defined as absolute growth greater than 6 ml or relative growth greater than 33% from the initial to the follow-up CT scan, to predict hematoma growth and outcome [15].

Under normal circumstances, minimal bleeding from the surgical site cannot be considered a hematoma. However, it can be recognized if it exceeds volume of ≥ 5 ml. All hematoma measurements in our study were performed individually using CT scans by experienced surgeons. A volume of 5 ml or with a volume of ≥ 5 ml was considered visible bleeding. We hope this study serves as an example for the literature and researchers. Patients with intracerebral hemorrhage who had a GCS between 6 and 12, a hematoma volume greater than 30 ml, and a midline shift > 0.5 cm (subfalcine herniation) underwent surgery. In our study, only one patient underwent reoperation due to postoperative bleeding greater than 30 ml.

The model was developed using a predefined set of predictor variables extracted from demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters. To ensure the model’s applicability for preoperative or early postoperative risk assessment, all predictors were based on measurements taken within the 24 h preceding surgery. To prevent data leakage, postoperative outcomes—such as length of ICU stay and radiographic bleeding measurements—were explicitly excluded from the model. In line with this principle, rather than using the absolute postoperative CRP value, we engineered a methodologically sound predictor by calculating the early change in C-reactive protein (delta-CRP; ΔCRP). This derived variable, calculated by subtracting the preoperative CRP value from the first postoperative measurement within 24 h, captures the dynamic inflammatory response to surgery without violating the prediction timepoint. Although 37 variables were initially collected, the final machine learning models were developed using a curated set of 28 predictors deemed most relevant for the prediction task.

To prevent any data leakage, all preprocessing steps were defined and fitted exclusively on the training data and then applied to both the training and test sets.

Machine learning model development and validation

Model development and hyperparameter optimization were conducted within the tidymodels framework. To identify the most effective predictive algorithm, a wide array of classification models was systematically developed and evaluated. This included traditional statistical models such as Logistic Regression and Elastic Net; instance-based learners like K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN); tree-based ensembles including Random Forests and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM); Support Vector Machines (SVM) with both Polynomial and Radial Basis Function kernels; and Bagged Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (Bagging MARS).

Internal Validation and Hyperparameter Tuning: A robust internal validation strategy consisting of 5-repeated 10-fold cross-validation was applied to the training data. This resampling technique was used for hyperparameter tuning across sixty hyperparameter combinations for each model, with the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC AUC) as the primary metric for selecting the optimal model.Final Model Evaluation: After identifying the optimal hyperparameters, the final model was fitted to the entire training dataset and subsequently evaluated on the completely unseen test set to assess its generalization ability.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were performed using both SPSS (version 26.0) and R. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent samples t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (n) and percentages (%) and compared using Pearson’s Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. To quantify the strength of association between potential risk factors and postoperative bleeding, odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The results of the univariate analysis for predictors of postoperative bleeding are detailed in Table 2. The analysis of surgical site, with the parietal lobe serving as the reference category, did not identify any location with a statistically significant association with bleeding. However, the point estimates suggested potential variations in risk. Notably, the occipital lobe was associated with a 42% lower odds of bleeding (OR = 0.58, 95% CI [0.17, 1.98]), while the pterional approach trended towards a 29% higher odds (OR = 1.29, 95% CI [0.33, 5.06]). A similar pattern of non-significant trends was observed in the analysis of tumor pathology, using glial tumors as the reference. The most clinically notable trend was seen in patients with meningioma, who demonstrated nearly half the odds of experiencing postoperative bleeding (OR = 0.53, 95% CI [0.17, 1.61]). Conversely, metastatic tumors were associated with a slight, non-significant increase in bleeding odds (OR = 1.15, 95% CI [0.42, 3.11]). While these findings highlight potential risk variations—particularly a reduced risk for meningiomas and an elevated risk for pterional procedures—the wide confidence intervals spanning unity for all comparisons indicate that the study was not sufficiently powered to confirm these associations. Consequently, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, warranting further investigation in larger patient cohorts.

Discussion

Postoperative intracerebral hemorrhage remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality following intracranial tumor surgery, despite advancements in surgical and perioperative care. Identifying patients at high risk for postoperative bleeding is crucial for optimizing intensive care management and reducing adverse outcomes. In this context, the present study demonstrates that machine learning algorithms—particularly LightGBM—show promise for predicting hemorrhage risk using routinely collected clinical and laboratory data. After a comprehensive re-analysis addressing class imbalance, our final model’s strong performance metrics, including a test AUC of 0.7451 and a sensitivity of 77.8%, underscore its potential as a clinical decision support tool for individualized risk stratification. In this model, Platelet count (PLT/µL) was identified as the most influential predictor, followed by serum Chloride (Cl) mmol/L, and the early change in C-reactive protein (delta-CRP) mg/L. These biomarkers, readily calculated in the early postoperative period, offer clinically relevant insights for early risk detection.

CRP is one of the key factors contributing to the pathophysiology of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH). One of the key factors contributing to the pathophysiology of ICH is the presence of mCRP, a structural isoform of native pCRP. Recent studies have provided evidence that CRP plays a significant role in neuroinflammation, exacerbating tissue damage and contributing to the progression of neurodegenerative processes. Şalari et al. reported in their research that mCRP is also an active driver of post-hemorrhage neuroinflammation and tissue damage, directly aggravating the severity and spread of ICH pathology [16].

In our research, machine learning programs provided us with the most important parameters that have an impact on postoperative hematoma formation. Serum Na (sodium) level and BUN (Blood Urea Nitrogen) are some of these. When we examined the literature, we found significant studies addressing these two parameters. Hyponatremia is a common electrolyte disorder in patients with neurological diseases; however, its predictive role for outcome in patients with supratentorial spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage is controversial. In their study conducted in 2024, Qian et al. reported that hyponatremia occurring in the first 7 days after hemorrhage in patients with supratentorial spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage was an independent predictor of 90-day mortality and adverse outcomes [17]. They reiterated the need for meticulous electrolyte monitoring in patients treated surgically. In our study, low sodium levels were also associated with poor prognosis. The impact of chronic kidney disease (CKD) on the severity and prognosis of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) has rarely been studied. Chronic kidey disease and increased BUN levels were associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality, nonroutine discharge, hemorrhagic stroke severity, and in-hospital complications such as pneumonia, hydrocephalus, and hematoma evacuation in patients with acute intracerebral hemorrhage [18].

Postoperative bleeding rates after cranial surgery vary greatly in the literature, ranging from 0.77% to 50% [5, 19–21]. However, the definition of postoperative bleeding varies. Series that include patients with radiographic evidence of bleeding have reported rates ranging from 10.8% to 50% [4, 8, 9]. In fact, the location of postoperative hematoma depends largely on preoperative pathology. Intraparenchymal hematomas have been frequently observed as locations after cranial operations. In routine intensive care follow-up, blood coagulation cascades in particular are routinely examined. In our study, Platelet count (PLT) was determined as the most effective predictor among the three most important predictive parameters for postoperative bleeding in Machine Learning programs, followed by serum Chloride (Cl) and delta-CRP.

Gerlach et al. retrospectively analyzed the data of 296 patients with and without hematoma who underwent surgery for meningioma to determine the risk factors associated with postoperative hematoma [22]. Meningioma surgery carries a higher risk for postoperative hematoma in the elderly. Thrombocytopenia and other hemostatic abnormalities are frequently associated with postoperative bleeding after meningioma surgery. Expansion of coagulation tests and specific replacement therapy may prevent hematoma formation and improve patient outcomes. Literature suggests that most hematomas are discovered within three days after the initial operation [22]. In the planning of our study, we focused on the patients’ brain CT scans and laboratory values ​​taken at the 48th postoperative hour. Kalfas reported that 35% of ICH cases occurred within 12 h [23], while Taylor reported that 44 out of 50 of 2,305 patients developed a hematoma within six hours after surgery [24]. Of course, it should not be forgotten that longer periods of observation in the intensive care unit may be required after intracerebral tumor surgery. It has been previously reported that a perioperative platelet count of less than 100,000/µL is associated with a higher risk of postoperative hematoma formation [19]. Our goal should be to reach a platelet count of at least 100,000/µL, or even 150,000/µL, before tumor surgery to prevent any possible postoperative bleeding. Again, Ziai et al. stated in their study that platelet dysfunction is common in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage and that low platelet count and platelet dysfunction are effective factors in the expansion of hematoma volüme [25].

Various risk factors for hematoma development have been analyzed, and hypertension is usually the most important comorbidity in most studies [5, 23]. However, in our study, no statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of age, gender, hypertension (HT), diabetes mellitus (DM) and diet method. A statistically significant difference was observed between the groups in other parameters (length of stay in the intensive care unit, progression, nutrition, comorbidity, presence of cancer). Other risk factors include use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, use of anticoagulants, coagulopathies, thrombocytopenia, factor XIII deficiencies, and decreased fibrinogen levels.

Anionic Gap (AG) is a marker that defines metabolic acidosis. The anion gap is calculated by subtracting the serum chloride and bicarbonate (anions) concentrations from the sodium and potassium (cations) concentrations. Anionic Gap = ([Na + ] + [K + ]) − ([Cl − ] + [HCO − 3]) = 20 mEq/L (Normal value). It is important to note that subarachnoid hemorrhage leads to acidosis and AG formation through various mechanisms, including cerebral tissue hypoxia, accumulation of acidic metabolites, and other electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia and hypocalcemia [26]. Therefore, increased AG after subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) may comprehensively reflect abnormal metabolic status and renal dysfunction, thereby contributing to a higher mortality risk. The negative impact of hypernatremia on the prognosis of patients with intracerebral hemorrhage has been demonstrated in several studies [27]. Finally, high serum AG levels have been found to be associated with elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers, including white blood cells and C-reactive protein.

Our study undertook a comprehensive evaluation of multiple machine learning algorithms to accurately classify the risk of postoperative cerebral hemorrhage. Following the implementation of SMOTE to address class imbalance, the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) model emerged as the superior performer among the tested algorithms. As detailed in Table 4, the LightGBM model demonstrated a robust and balanced performance on the independent test set, achieving an AUC of 0.7451, an accuracy of 76.9%, and a sensitivity of 77.8%. A detailed examination of this model was conducted to understand its predictive mechanisms and identify the primary determinants of hemorrhage risk. Platelet count (PLT) was identified as the most effective predictor of postoperative intracerebral hemorrhage, followed by serum Chloride (Cl) and delta-CRP. These findings highlight the model’s ability to capture the complex relationships between routine laboratory parameters and postoperative bleeding risk, demonstrating its potential as a tool to distinguish between patients at high and low risk of developing this critical complication.

In a similar study, Yang et al. used radiomics models based on machine learning algorithms and effective computer-aided tools to predict ruptured intracranial aneurysms in 2023. The AdaBoost algorithm was found to be superior in the application of radiomics combined with machine learning algorithm to predict aneurysm ruptures [23]. Mia et al. In their study published in 2024, they show the high efficiency of the ADASYN_RF algorithm on the cerebral stroke prediction dataset. Also, the AUC channels help to determine which type of categorization is best. Following this procedure, cerebral stroke can be predicted more accurately using ADASYN_RF methods [27].

Limitations of study

Several key limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First and foremost, the single-center, retrospective design inherently limits the generalizability of our findings. The patient demographics, specific surgical techniques, and postoperative management protocols at our institution may not be fully representative of other healthcare centers, potentially introducing a selection bias. Second, the relatively modest sample size (n = 118), while partially addressed for model training through the use of SMOTE, still impacts the statistical power of our test set evaluation and the overall robustness of the predictive model.

These factors collectively underscore that while our results are promising, the model currently serves as a proof-of-concept and requires rigorous external validation. Therefore, future large-scale, prospective, and multicenter studies are an essential and non-negotiable next step to confirm the model’s performance and ensure its applicability across diverse patient populations before it can be considered for routine clinical use.

Our study offers potential for predicting and taking precautions against bleeding that may occur after intracerebral tumor surgery. It provides a promising basis for new research in the future. Finally, while we employed SHAP and LIME to enhance the interpretability of the LightGBM model, gradient boosting models are inherently more complex than traditional regression models. Their “black-box” nature means that understanding the precise mechanisms behind predictions relies on these post-hoc approximation techniques, which may not capture every nuance of the model’s internal logic.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that machine learning algorithms, particularly LightGBM, show promise in predicting the risk of postoperative hemorrhage following cerebral tumor surgery. Our findings, strengthened by the use of SMOTE and evaluated with robust metrics, suggest that key predictors such as platelet count, serum chloride, and delta-CRP can offer actionable insights for clinical management.

However, while these results are encouraging, it is imperative to recognize that our model currently serves as a proof-of-concept. Before any consideration for clinical implementation, rigorous external validation through prospective, large-scale, and multicenter studies is an essential and non-negotiable next step. This further investigation is required to confirm the model’s generalizability and robustness across diverse patient populations. Once externally validated, such predictive tools could potentially support clinicians in early risk identification and the delivery of tailored intensive care, ultimately aiming to improve patient outcomes.
