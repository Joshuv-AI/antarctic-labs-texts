Depression, anxiety, and psychiatric morbidity in Ethiopian immigrants living with HIV
Authors: Dor Gladstone, Hila Elinav, Matan-Joel Cohen, Keren Olshtain-Pops, Sarah Israel, Maya Korem, Meir Cherniak, Efrat Orenbuch-Harroch, Jacob Strahilevitz, Aryeh Dienstag, Yonatan Oster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of mental health morbidity among Ethiopian immigrants in Israel is higher than in the general population, with diagnosis complicated by differing clinical presentations and unfamiliarity with the concept of depression. Among Ethiopian immigrants living with HIV, mental health challenges may further be exacerbated by HIV related stigma, anti-retroviral treatment, and HIV-associated neurotoxicity.AimsAssess the rates of depression and identify risk factors among Ethiopians with HIV living in Israel.MethodsTwo culturally adapted questionnaires in Amharic (SRQ-F and HADS) were administered to HIV + patients of Ethiopian origin in the Hadassah AIDS center between April 2021 and December 2022. Demographic and clinical data were collected and compared to the questionnaire's outcomes. Relationships with questionnaire outcomes were analyzed using chi-square tests, ANOVA, T-tests, and Wilcoxon-rank test.ResultsNinety patients were recruited to the study, 60% of them were identified by at least one of the questionnaires as suffering from depression, anxiety, or psychopathology, with both questionnaires yielding consistent results. A significant correlation was found between depression or psychopathology and employment status, immigration dates 2000–2009, CD4 at diagnosis, current viral load, and the number of antiretroviral pills taken.ConclusionsWe found a higher-than-expected rate of psychiatric morbidity among Ethiopian immigrants living with HIV in Israel, exceeding global rates reported for PLWH. The strong correlation between mental health issues and social factors (unemployment, immigration year) and HIV-related factors (CD4, pill burden, viral load) underscores the role of social difficulties experienced by this community in the community's well-being. A detectable viral load should alert healthcare providers to the possibility of undiagnosed depression and/or anxiety. As untreated depression and anxiety can lead to poor treatment adherence, there is a pressing need for a structured, culturally adapted program for the detection and treatment of mental illness in this population.

Introduction

Between 1980 and 2021, approximately 90,000 Jewish-Ethiopian immigrants arrived in Israel through government-facilitated immigration. Around 30,000 of them came in two major waves, in 1984 and 1991. Ever since. migration continued in steady numbers over the years (Israel Central Bureau of Statistics, 2024). Since the early 1980′s, Jewish Ethiopian immigrants in Israel underwent HIV screening upon arrival, with those testing positive referred to HIV centers (Mor et al., 2013). The incidence of HIV among Ethiopian immigrants in Israel was reported at 2.41 to 5.8% (Mor et al., 2013; Olshtain-Pops et al., 2014).

Ethiopian immigrants in Israel have a 2–3 times higher rate of psychiatric hospitalizations (Israeli Health Ministry, 2018) and a threefold higher prevalence of suicide and domestic violence compared to the general Israeli population (Youngmann and Barasch, 1999). Contributing factors include cultural crises, economic disparities, and difficulties adapting to modern life. Depression is often somaticized, leading to underdiagnosis and inadequate treatment due to cultural and communication barriers in the healthcare system (Lubotzky-Gete et al., 2021)

Depression affects 15–40% of people living with HIV (PLWH) (Angelino and Treisman, 2001). HIV-positive Ethiopian immigrants in Israel experience compounded challenges, including stigma and the psychological burden of concealing their condition. Although they represent a significant portion of the patients in HIV clinics throughout Israel, depression in this group remains under-assessed, and treatment is inadequate. The absence of data on this population underscores the need for further research to assess depression rates, identify risk factors in a quest to develop a program for early detection and treatment.

Methods

We conducted an observational cross-sectional study from April 2021 to December 2022, among patients cared for at the Hadassah AIDS center. Patients included in the study were all adults born in Ethiopia who were willing to participate and signed informed consent forms. We excluded individuals who were congenitally infected with HIV and individuals without decision-making capacity. Patients were screened for depression, anxiety, and psychological distress by the Hospital Anxiety Depression screening tool (Reda, 2011) and the SRQ-F questionnaire (Youngmann et al., 2008) in Amharic. In cases of illiteracy, the screening was conducted in Amharic with the assistance of two staff members -Ethiopian cultural mediators who explained the study and read the questionnaire aloud to the participants. Patients who could read the questionnaires in Amharic filled them out without assistance. The study was approved by the Hadassah Medical Center Ethics Review Board (HMO-0410-19) and was performed according to the GCP guidelines.

Participants provided demographic information, including sex, age, marital status, number of children, place of residence, employment, income, household size, and year of immigration to Israel. Data was also collected from the medical records as well, including comorbidities, psychiatric history, hospitalizations unrelated to HIV, alcohol consumption, smoking habits, and HIV-related factors including year of HIV diagnosis, ART initiation, CD4 counts (at diagnosis, nadir and current CD4), viral load (current and peak), HIV medications, hospitalizations related to HIV, HIV complications, and information on HIV-positive partners and children.

Depression and/or anxiety were evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety Depression screening tool (HADS), consisting of 14-questions, developed to screen and evaluate the presence and progression of clinically significant depression and anxiety in patients presenting at a general medical clinic (Zigmond and Snaith, 1983), with responses ranging from 0 (no depression) to 3 (severe depression). The HADS had been previously validated in Ethiopia with 302 individuals living with HIV (Reda, 2011). Scores of 0–7 were considered normal, 8–10 suggested possible depression or anxiety, and 11–21 indicated a high likelihood of depression or anxiety.

Additionally, the SRQ-F questionnaire, a modified version of the WHO Self-Reporting Questionnaire (SRQ), was used to screen for overall poor mental health, defined as a composite of depression, anxiety, and/or somatic symptoms. Translated into Amharic and validated in Ethiopia and Israel (Youngmann et al., 2008). The SRQ-F has previously demonstrated 85% sensitivity and 72% specificity for detecting psychopathology among individuals without a psychiatric diagnosis, and 91% sensitivity and 85% specificity among psychiatric patients. A score of 8 or higher on the SRQ-F indicates psychopathology.

For participants who did not complete all the questions of a questionnaire, a weighted score was calculated based on the percentage of questions answered. Participants answering fewer than half of the questions were excluded from the analysis.

Patients identified with depression, anxiety, or psychological distress were offered additional psychiatric evaluation and treatment in the psychiatric clinic.

Question consistency was evaluated with the Cronbach alpha test and with estimates of the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). A comparison of the results from both questionnaires was made using the Fisher exact test to evaluate the correlation between the two tools. Descriptive statistics were also applied to characterize the population and identify factors potentially influencing depression and anxiety. The relationship between these factors and the questionnaire results was examined using the Fisher exact test, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the Kruskal-Wallis Test. A significance level of 0.05 was predefined for all statistical analyses, with two-tailed testing. The data in this study were analyzed using SPSS 30 software.

All the participants in the study filled out the HADS questionnaire. Four individuals answered only half or fewer of the questions and were excluded. In total, 86 participants were included in the final analysis. Based on the HADS questionnaire, 37 (43%) of participants suffered from depression and/or anxiety, and 18 (20.9%) of the participants were suspected of suffering from depression and/or anxiety.

All participants in the study filled out the SRQ-F questionnaire, which screens for overall psychiatric morbidity. Three individuals were excluded as they answered only half or fewer of the questions. According to the SRQ-F, 31 (35.6%) of participants suffer from some level of psychiatric morbidity.

The HADS items had good consistency - Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85 and the ICC 0.847 (95%CI 0.79 to 0.89). The SRQ-F questionnaire, similarly, scored 0.84 in the Cronbach’s alpha estimated, and the ICC was 0.843 (95%CI 0.78 to 0.89). Overall, there was a 0.59 correlation between the results of the SRQ-F and the HADS questionnaires (Fig. 1). Seventy-one percent (24/34) of those identified by the HADS as suffering from depression and/or anxiety were also identified by the SRQ-F as suffering from psychiatric morbidity. Moreover, 93.3% (28/30) of those who did not suffer from depression and/or anxiety as identified by the HADS were not identified as suffering from psychiatric morbidity on the SRQ-F. Thus, a significant correlation was found between the two questionnaires (P < 0.001). Discussion

Depression affects 15–40% of people living with HIV, which may develop precede infection or develop later, before or after HIV diagnosis, and is often linked to risky behaviors. Effective treatment of depression is vital for antiretroviral therapy adherence, quality of life, and reducing mortality (Angelino and Treisman, 2001).

Our study is the first to assess rates of depression and anxiety among Ethiopian immigrants who are living with HIV in Israel. Diagnosing depression in PLWH can significantly improve their health by increasing adherence to HIV treatment. In our cohort, 43% scored as having depression and/or anxiety, and 20.9% scored as having borderline depression/anxiety according to the HADS questionnaire. Similarly, on the SRQ-F questionnaire, 34.8% scored in the psychiatric morbidity range. In total, 60.87% of participants either suffer from or are suspected of suffering from depression/anxiety. Notably, the rate of depression in our study was higher than the rate of depression reported in a 2019 meta-analysis, which reported the worldwide prevalence rate of depression in patients with HIV/AIDS to be 31% (Rezaei et al., 2019) as well as a 2017 meta-analysis, which reported the prevalence estimates of depression to be between 9 and 32% in people living with HIV in sub-Saharan Africa (Bernard et al., 2017). Notably, in our study, 70.6% of those with depression had overall poor mental health (P = 0.001), confirming a strong correlation between assessment tools. Although the setting of our survey is different, the rate of depression and anxiety in our cohort is similar to rates reported by a study at Ayder Hospital in Mekelle, which found that 9.8% of PLWH had neurocognitive impairment, with 55.6% experiencing anxiety and 67.1% depression (Belete et al., 2017). A 2017 study at Zewditu Hospital in Addis Ababa found that 22.5% of HIV patients reported suicidal thoughts and 13.9% had attempted suicide (Gebremariam et al., 2017). The differences in the rates reported between the different studies, including ours, could stem from different endpoints and assessment tools, as well as specific situations and local culture in different locations, the additional stress related to immigration, and the integration difficulties in a completely different community in Israel.

Our study identified only two non-HIV-related factors that were associated with depression/anxiety: unemployment (P = 0.02) and recent immigration (2000–2009 vs. pre-2000; P = 0.03). The negative effect of unemployment on mental health is not surprising and was described by Paul (Paul, 2009). Additionally, Foo et al. described that longer-term immigrants report lower depression rates than recent arrivals (Foo et al., 2018).

Regarding HIV related factors, a significant association was found between the number of daily antiretroviral pills and depression/anxiety (P = 0.014). Viral load correlated with psychopathology when analyzed numerically (p = 0.04) but only trended when analyzed as a categorical parameter (detectable/undetectable viral load p = 0.09). Surprisingly, no association was found with medications that are suspected to cause neurotoxicity, such as second-line integrase inhibitors. This aligns with research by Tareke et al., who found an association between taking two or more pills and second-line treatment for depression in Ethiopian HIV patients (Tareke et al., 2018). Furthermore, Gonzalez et al. (2011) demonstrated a consistent correlation between poor ART adherence and depression across various populations, while Ironson et al. (2015) identified a connection between neurohormonal changes, depression, and viral load, independent of adherence. Depression can impair treatment adherence, leading to increased viral resistance and the need for treatment changes, often necessitating more complicated treatment regimens, which may further impact adherence due to a larger pill burden. This aligns with the correlation we noted between current viral load and psychopathology, possibly suggesting either mental health effects on adherence or neurological impacts of the virus.

Screening for depression, anxiety, or other mental illness by the tools used in our study is not routinely conducted in most HIV centers in Israel and, according to our experience, may even cause suspicion and resistance. Filling out the questionnaires posted challenges for some participants in our study, whether due to technical problems such as limited literacy or emotional distress arising from stigma and perception of depression. Consequently, antiretroviral treatment failure, especially among unemployed individuals or recent immigrants from Ethiopia, could serve as a marker for the possibility of depression. Subsequently, use of the HADS and the SRQ-F questionnaires would then validate the diagnosis, enabling appropriate culturally adapted mental health intervention and support.

Previous studies indicated that antidepressants not only treat depression in HIV patients but also improve ART adherence (Thompson et al., 2006; Basu et al., 2005; Medeiros and WEBB, 2022). Unfortunately, there is a high level of hesitancy toward psychiatric medications in the Ethiopian community – highlighting the need for a structured program to identify and address mental illness in this population. Such a program should focus on raising awareness among Ethiopian immigrants with HIV and healthcare providers, training medical staff, utilizing validated screening tools, and establishing a multidisciplinary team—including a psychiatrist and cultural mediators at HIV centers. Given cultural gaps and the shortage of Ethiopian psychiatrists in Israel, recruitment of Ethiopian psychiatrists from Ethiopia or the U.S.A (where there is a large Ethiopian community) should be explored. This may include developing a culturally appropriate telemedicine program or training Israeli healthcare teams with the guidance of Ethiopian psychiatrists. Telepsychiatry for refugees and immigrants has already been shown to be effective, leading to high patient satisfaction and willingness to continue treatment (Mucic, 2008). Furthermore, with patient consent, social services should be integrated to provide further support.

Our study has several limitations. A primary one is the lack of a control group of Ethiopian immigrants without HIV, with similar sociodemographic characteristics. Our recurrent attempts to recruit a comparable control group in the community failed, potentially due to the suspiciousness of the general Ethiopian community towards the medical profession. Even in our clinic, where patients are followed closely for many years, <50% of patients participated in the study. Consequently, we could not evaluate the role of HIV diagnosis and stigma in our findings. Examined associations were not identified, though our sample size was certainly not sizable. Additionally, recruiting patients with depression for research is challenging, potentially leading to a selection bias and an underestimation of the depression rate. Lastly, our reliance on self-report questionnaires, which for some participants who are illiterate required interviews conducted by Ethiopian cultural mediators, introduces potential for reporting bias.

In summary, this is the first study to assess the rate of depression, anxiety, and psychopathology among Ethiopian immigrants living with HIV in Israel, since the 1990s. Using two culturally adapted questionnaires proved both valid and reliable. We revealed a higher-than-expected rate of depression, anxiety, and overall psychiatric morbidity. Social factors, including unemployment and immigration year (2000–2009), were linked to depression and/or anxiety, while HIV-related factors, such as a higher pill burden and detectable viral load, were connected to psychiatric morbidity.

The very high rates of depression and its effects on mental health and ART adherence indicate the need for a structured program to identify depression and psychopathology in this population. The program should focus on increasing awareness among Ethiopian immigrants with HIV and healthcare providers, training staff, using validated questionnaires, and forming a multidisciplinary team that includes a psychiatrist. Exploring telemedicine options could help address cultural and professional gaps.
