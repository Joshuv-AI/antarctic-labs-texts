Choroid plexus carcinoma: state of the field and emerging directions
Authors: Ashley Thompson, Hannah Pescaru, Briana Griffin, Ryann M Fame
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChoroid plexus carcinoma (CPC) is a class of rare malignant life-threatening pediatric brain tumors, with a median survival of ~2.5–3 years [1, 2]. Recent advances in human genomics, cellular studies, and animal models have improved our understanding of the pathophysiology and molecular characteristics of CPC. However, because this cancer is rare, it remains understudied. This limited research has led to a limited understanding of the tumor’s heterogeneity and molecular pathogenesis, resulting in fewer treatment options. Surgical resection remains the most significant contributor to patient survival and is often combined with chemotherapy, but tumors can still recur and metastasize. The post-surgical 5-year survival rate for CPC is only 60–65% and the adjuvant anti-mitotic radiation or chemotherapy included in standard treatment results in lifelong side effects for these children. Here we conduct a systematic review of existing literature and analyze the current body of research on CPC with special focus on preclinical models to highlight progress and barriers while emphasizing important open questions for improving patient treatment and longevity outcomes. This analysis underscores the importance of scholarly discussion and further exploration into the carcinogenesis of CPC.Subject terms: Paediatric cancer, Head and neck cancer

Introduction

The choroid plexus is a neural tissue that extends into each brain ventricle, produces cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and forms the blood-CSF-barrier (B-CSF-B). The choroid plexus structure comprises multiple cell types including a vascularized core of fenestrated capillaries surrounded by mesenchymal, immune, neuronal, and epithelial cells [3, 4]. There are 4 distinct choroid plexus tissues: one in each of the lateral ventricles, one in the third ventricle, and one in the fourth ventricle. Each choroid plexus tissue has unique features [5, 6], but each have generally similar cell types and substructure. Choroid plexus epithelial cells are the predominant cell type and are distinct from other cell-types that touch the CSF-filled ventricle including ependymal cells and circumventricular organs. Choroid plexus epithelial cells have microvilli specialized to interact with the CSF, high metabolism, and a unique combination of ion pumps, transporters, and channels that operate in concert to produce CSF [7]. The tight junctions at the apico-lateral surface of the choroid plexus epithelium form the B-CSF-B. These cells are largely terminally differentiated; however, there is slow turnover that decreases as the tissue matures. While damage or surgical removal are not risk factors for choroid plexus tumors, choroid plexus proliferation can ramp up after damage or surgical removal to allow regeneration of the tissue [8–10] - thus unlike neurons, choroid plexus is proliferation-competent, rendering the tissue sensitive to transformation.

Choroid plexus carcinoma (CPC) is a malignant subclass of choroid plexus tumors, which also include the benign choroid plexus papilloma (CPP) that affect this tissue [11]. Since CPC occurs largely in young children, it has overlapping features with some embryonal tumors that differentiate from persisting neural progenitors. Thus, considering the developmental origin of the choroid plexus is relevant to both the disease and preclinical model development. The choroid plexus is derived from the dorsal midline and rhombic lip lineages of neural (neuroepithelial) origin [12, 13]. In humans, choroid plexus progenitors first arise during gestational week (GW) 8, with the fourth ventricle and lateral ventricle choroid plexus expanding first between GW 9–10, followed by expansion of third ventricle choroid plexus in the diencephalon [14]. These tissues all continue to mature into GW29 [15]. This timeline is likely relevant to the presentation of CPC, which is largely found in the early expanding fourth and caudal lateral ventricle choroid plexus tissues.

Epidemiology: incidence and mortality

Pediatric CPC

CPC is predominantly pediatric, with more than 75% of the CPC patient population diagnosed before the age of five [1, 2, 16–18] and CPC present at the median age of 3 years [19]. While rare overall in the pediatric population (~1% of all pediatric CNS tumors) CPCs represent 20% of all brain tumors in children less than 1 year old [11] and up to 40% of all choroid plexus tumors among children [16, 17]. A sex bias has been reported with 65% of CPCs occurring in males [18]. Pediatric CPC are reported with a median survival expectancy of ~2.5–3 years [1, 2, 16, 17], with one-year survival rates of 71%, 5-year survival of 41%, and 10-year survival of 31% [20, 21]. Pediatric CPC are predominantly localized to lateral ventricles with some presentation in fourth ventricles and only very rare presentation in third ventricles [22, 23]. Specific histological criteria are predictive of survival and totality of surgical resection contributes to overall survival [19, 20]. Therefore, the effect of CPCs on very young patients remains high.

Adult CPC

In the adult patient population, choroid plexus tumors are particularly rare intraventricular papillary neoplasms (usually CPP) that account for 0.5% of intracranial tumors [16]. Adult CPC is extremely rare, with a reported average annual incidence rate of only 0.3 per 1,000,000 people [16]. Therefore, most of the literature and this review focus on pediatric CPC.

Diagnosis, screening, and risk factors

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) grades choroid plexus tumors according to their characteristics. Compared to CPP, a World Health Organization (WHO) grade I neoplasm or atypical CPP (aCPP) a WHO grade II neoplasm, CPC is categorized as a WHO grade III tumor [24, 25] with higher tendencies to invade the brain parenchyma and metastasize to other areas of the central nervous system due to its fast growth [26, 27]. When CSF is shunted from CPC patients with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, tumor cells have been reported to metastasize through the shunt into the abdomen, the lung, and the tibia, although these cases are rare [26]. Metastasis is much more likely to occur in CPC than CPP, which is classified as benign [26], although rare CPP metastases have been observed [28, 29].

Symptoms

Approximately 90% of CPC patients also present with symptoms of hydrocephalus. These may manifest in infants as increased head circumference, bulging fontanelles, and splayed sutures, strabismus, vomiting, and developmental delay. In older children and adults these hydrocephalus-associated symptoms often present as vomiting, headaches, neurological deficits, seizures, lethargy, visual changes, and altered mental status [26].

Specific symptoms may occur depending on the location of the CPC. For example, a CPC that is more prominent in the posterior fossa may lead to brain stem compression and cranial nerve paresis, as compared to endocrine disturbances and precocious puberty potentially caused by tumors in the third ventricle [26].

Imaging

CPCs typically manifest as large intraventricular lesions with irregular borders [26, 27]. As CPC is categorized as a WHO grade III tumor [24, 25], the higher tendencies to invade brain parenchyma are typically indicated on imaging by peritumoral vasogenic edema in surrounding white matter [4, 26, 30]. On T1- and T2-weighted images they may appear slightly hyperintense, but gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted images enhance these tumors significantly [26]. Serpentine signals that represent enlarged blood vessels that grow into the tumor to supply blood or signal voids that indicate increased blood vessel patency may also be present [26].

There may be some similarities between CPC and other choroid plexus tumors when it comes to diagnostic markers or imaging characteristics. The above characteristics can also apply to CPP, choroid plexus cysts, ependymoma, primitive neuroectodermal tumor, astrocytoma, germinoma, teratoma, meningioma, choroid plexus metastases, and xanthogranulomas [26]. Therefore, molecular markers from histological analyses areneeded for differential diagnoses.

Histological and molecular diagnostic markers for CPC

CPC has distinguishing features that enable its diagnosis following the WHO criteria for choroid plexus tumors. These criteria include increased mitotic activity, nuclear atypia, loss of papillary differentiation, necrosis, and immunoreactivity for the epithelial marker cytokeratin [31] (Fig. 1). In rare cases, it has been reported that CPCs contain a solid pattern of atypical clear cells, which can sometimes confound diagnosis, if unrecognized [32]. Taken together, severity of these histological features corresponds with survival [19, 20].

CPCs also often lose their papillary configuration since the typical monolayer of choroid plexus epithelium is obscured by epithelial tumor cells. In contrast, low-grade CPPs retain the typical choroid plexus papillary structure [30]. Proliferation indices from H&E mitotic figures or molecular stains such as Ki-67/MIB-1 can also be used to distinguish between CPP, with a reported mean nuclear labeling rate of 1.9%, contrasted to the higher rate in CPC of 13.8% [33].

For differential diagnosis of CPC vs. carcinomas with metastases to the brain, immunohistochemical markers show that CPCs co-express cytokeratins, vimentin, and S-100. Further, CPC generally have low immunoreactivity for the antibodies HEA125 and BerEp4, which recognize an epithelial surface/membrane antigen EpCAM. Together, HEA125 and BerEp4 mark over 95% of all cerebral metastatic carcinomas, but mark substantially fewer (only ~10%) CPPs or CPCs [32]. However, metastatic carcinomas to the brain are highly unlikely in pediatric cases, which represent the primary demographic of patients with CPC [32]. Choroid plexus villous hypertrophy is sometimes considered in a differential diagnosis. In contrast to CPC, however, choroid plexus villous hypertrophy retains typical choroid plexus histology and has lower proliferation than CPC [30].

Atypical teratoid/ rhabdoid tumor (AT/ RT) are primitive neuroectodermal tumors that can occur in similar brain regions as CPC in infants. It can be difficult to distinguish between the two poorly differentiated tumors with predominant epithelial differentiation (CPC and AT/ RT), but immunohistochemical staining may prove useful [30]. One such stain is the BAF47 clone of the INI1 antigen, a tumor suppressor gene that is often mutated or deleted in AT/ RTs [30, 34]. Most CPCs (75%) stained positively for INI1. In contrast, most AT/ RTs failed to stain positively for INI1 [30, 34]. While these differentiations have proven useful in diagnosing CPC [24, 25], some difficulties remain. For example, commonly used markers can lack specificity as CPC is histologically similar to extracranial papillary epithelial neoplasms. To address this limitation, new CPC diagnostic markers, such as Stanniocalcin-1 (STC1) and Kir7.1, were integrated into a microarray-based approach, which improves diagnostic accuracy between other brain tissues or other primary brain tumors and cerebral metastases versus those of choroid plexus origin like CPC, CPP, and normal choroid plexus [30, 35]. They are now accepted markers of CPC [24, 25]. Interestingly, CPP frequently maintain expression of the canonical choroid plexus gene Transthyretin (TTR), while CPC often lose this expression, indicating more complete loss of healthy tissue function [30, 35].

Risk factors

The most notable risk factors for CPC are genetic and have been characterized in pediatric populations, which we discuss in more detail below. However, genetic diversity and carcinogen exposure are still being characterized for these rare tumors. Risk factors for the exceedingly rare CPC in the adult population remain unidentified [16, 36].

Molecular and genomic risk factors

Genetic analysis of patient tumors has identified a suite of molecular and genetic risk factors for CPC. The most common of which by far is dysfunction of the tumor suppressor gene TP53 (known as p53 in mice). Mutated TP53 is the only identified common driver in CPC, representing anywhere from 36 to 60% of CPC cases [24, 37–40]. About 40% of CPC cases result from Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS), a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder that increases the risk of multiple primary neoplasms due to mutations in the TP53 gene. TP53 acts as a tumor suppressor gene by encoding a transcription factor that arrests the cell cycle if DNA anomalies are detected and potentially inducing apoptosis, thus protecting cells from DNA instability. Consistent with this function, single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array analysis, found that CPCs with mutated TP53 have larger genetic instability compared to CPCs that express unmutated copies of TP53 [41]. Such genomic instability contributes to choroid plexus tumors. Tumors with higher genetic instability, as calculated based on the average size and number of copy number alterations generating the metric of “total structural variation (TSV)”, tend to have worse prognoses. In a study involving 64 patients with choroid plexus tumors, individuals with a low TSV were more likely to present with favorable prognoses that respond to treatment without radiation [38]. Strikingly, patients with TP53 dysfunction are reported to have a 5-year survival rate of somewhere between 0% and 50% [37–39, 41–43] (Table 1), whereas those with normal TP53 function had an 82% survival rate. The wide range of survival may be due to discrete populations or variations in numbers of enrolled patients in the studies, but survival in CPC patients with non-functioning TP53 remains below that of those without. Conversely, 14 out of 16 patients with tumors immunonegative for TP53 mutations (i.e., with functional TP53) survived without requiring radiation therapy [38], potentially informing treatment decisions.

Unlike the tumor suppressor gene TP53, other genetic changes, such as MYC, SOX2, and Notch3 expression, increase cell differentiation and proliferation. A small study of pediatric CPC cases found that CPCs with higher proliferation rates also had higher levels of protooncogene cMYC [33]. However, while copy number gains in chromosome 8, which contains the MYC locus, are associated with human choroid plexus tumors, MYC alone is not widely appreciated as a CPC inducer in patients [44], suggesting it might be upregulated secondarily. SOX2 expression was observed across multiple grades of choroid plexus tumors [45]. Increased Notch3 expression was found in a small cohort of choroid plexus tumors including six of seven CPPs and two of two CPCs [46] as was NOTCH1-3 in a subset of cells in a single CPC sample [45]. This involvement of Notch implicates the traditional WNT/ β-catenin pathway, as canonical Notch limits WNT signaling. Notch1 can antagonize β-catenin activity through an endocytic mechanism that sequesters β-catenin to the membrane. Within the nucleus, the intracellular domain of Notch1 can limit β-catenin-induced transcription [47]. Indeed, WNT/β-catenin signaling is activated in human choroid plexus tumors including CPC and at least some of these tumors (HIBCPP cells) depend on WNT/ β-catenin signaling to survive. This dependance on WNT signaling is consistent with developmental studies of the choroid plexus and in vivo constitutive activation of canonical WNT signaling disrupts choroid plexus epithelial fate and shifts these progenitors to a more neuronal identity [48] linking basic developmental biology of the choroid plexus to relevant choroid plexus tumor pathways.

Finally, new studies are using widespread sequencing of tumors to investigate commonalities between and diversity among CPC to identify both genetic and genomic risk factors. For example, single-cell sequencing of 4 CPP samples, 2 atypical CPP samples, and 5 CPC samples, identified genetic signatures of NOTCH1, 2, and 3, and RAD54L as emergent in choroid plexus tumors [49]. Further, single-cell sequencing of CPP and CPC alongside healthy choroid plexus identified signatures of disease including cell-cell interactomes of epithelial cells with the stroma and tumor-wide changes in genome epigenetic marks like histone methylation [49]. Evidence suggests that epigenetic dysregulation, particularly through DNA methylation, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and biological behavior of brain tumors like choroid plexus tumors including CPC. DNA methylation normally functions as a gene-silencing mechanism, and in CPC, alterations in the methylation of tumor suppressor genes can contribute to unchecked cellular proliferation and drawbacks in treatment [50]. Using methylation-based profiling, distinct epigenetic subgroups have been identified among choroid plexus tumors in a way that enables useful segregation of CPC. Most CPC cases fall within the PED_B group, which is a subgroup associated with aggressive behavior, supratentorial location, and worse prognoses than the other choroid plexus tumors [50]. This subgroup also overlaps with TP53 mutations, suggesting a synergistic effect of genetic and epigenetic disruption [51]. In contrast to the more indolent methylation seen in other choroid plexus tumors, the CPC-specific methylation characteristic could be useful in diagnostics and as a potential target for epigenetic therapies in the future [50]. In sum, molecular and genomic risk factors are key to subset CPC and understand their and progression and have much to contribute to future diagnostics. Further, applying modern artificial intelligence technologies to these large datasets has the potential to help predict efficacy of combination therapies [52] and ushers in an exciting era of research into rare tumors like CPC.

CPP as a risk factor for CPC

Since choroid plexus tumors are rare CNS neoplasms with a wide range from benign low-grade CPPs to malignant CPCs, understanding how they are related and their potential ability to progress between these states is critical for patient monitoring. Atypical CPP (Grade II) presents with an increased mitotic rate, but is still easily distinguishable from CPC. While it is rare for CPP to transform into the more malignant CPC, two case studies (one pediatric and one adult) illustrate that this progression is possible. In a pediatric setting one 9-year-old child’s CPP was found to have progressed into carcinoma 7 years after the initial presentation [17]. While this is possibly a separate tumor instance separated by 7 years, comprehensive molecular sequencing analyses of samples from both the initial presenting CPP and the transformed CPC found chromosomal aneuploidy in both. The CPP was associated with more chromosomal gains and the CPC was associated with chromosomal losses. For example, the CPC lost chromosome 13, which includes tumor suppressor genes RB1 and BRCA2. This loss may have played a role in the malignant transformation to CPC. A second case report of a CPP-to-CPC transition in an adult patient was reported to occur over a much shorter timeframe (7 months) and therefore represents a compelling case of such a transition. The 67-year-old patient presented with headaches, slurred speech, and lower-extremity weakness [53]. The patient underwent a left parietal craniotomy to remove a cystic mass in the left parietal lobe that was revealed on initial CT and MRI, and pathology confirmed the diagnosis of a CPP, WHO Grade I. Unfortunately, imaging conducted 7 months later showed tumor regrowth and new cysts in the brain. Subsequent pathology revealed that the tumor was a CPC, WHO Grade III, including features like increases mitotic frequency (high Ki67), increased cellular density, nuclear atypia, loss of papillary structure, and necrosis, confirming the diagnosis of CPC [53]. This case strongly suggests progression from CPP to CPC, although original mixed histology cannot be ruled out with only limited sampling used for the original diagnosis. Confirming whether these cases indicate true malignant transformation or coexisting lesions of CPP and CPC remains challenging, as mixed histologic features and sampling limitations can complicate interpretation.

Management and treatment

Current treatment for CPC involves a combinatorial approach including surgical resection, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy [54]. Near total or gross total resection is the most significant contributor to positive patient outcomes [17, 20, 26]. Management includes emergency reduction of increased intracranial pressure for those CPC patients presenting with hydrocephalus.

Surgical resection

There are two approaches to resection: incomplete and complete [21] and the extent of the resection is linked to the survival rate [21]. When comparing the two types of resections, complete resection resulted in a 2-year survival rate of 72%, while incomplete resection had a 2-year survival rate of 34% [21]. When combined with adjuvant chemotherapy, the differences in survival between total and subtotal resection are no longer significant [20]. Regardless of the extent of the resection, adjuvant therapy such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy is often recommended to follow surgical resection [21]. While radiotherapy has been found to improve patient survival rates, it is not recommended for young children due to potential lifelong effects [21]. Therefore, for most patients, chemotherapy, which can shrink CPC size, is the preferred choice [21].

Therapies

According to Thariat et al. [55], radiotherapy should be avoided when treating LFS patients or those with other inheritable TP53 cancer-related syndromes. Their review on LFS management shows that patients with LFS have an increased incidence of radiation-induced secondary cancers because of their decreased ability to repair damage caused by radiotherapy due to their TP53 mutation. This decreased ability to repair DNA coupled with their likelihood to bypass the p53 checkpoint and proliferate regardless of this DNA damage makes them more prone to developing additional cancers after radiation exposure. Since CPC is a tumor commonly associated with LFS, it is important to modify or completely avoid radiation when considering treatment options, especially in children under the age of 3 with CPC. Radiotherapy has the potential to cause damage to a child’s developing nervous system. This can also lead to cognitive impairment, endocrinopathy, and an increased risk of secondary malignancy [56]. Recent trials have mixed results for efficacy of adjuvant chemotherapy. Analysis of results from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Result (SEER) database failed to find additional survival associated with adjuvant chemotherapy broadly [20]. However, some specific chemotherapies, when accompanied by total resection showed benefit, like patients in the multi-center SJYC07 trial receiving therapies containing high-dose methotrexate [57]. Further, in the CPT-SIOP-2000 registry, chemotherapy such as Carboplatin/Etoposide/Vincristine (CarbEV) has been shown feasible and effective for CPC and may be recommended to patients under the age of 3 years [58], because it shows benefits when joined with resection, does not require radiation, and is less likely to cause life-long side effects in these young patients. More research remains to be done on the long-term survival benefits of chemotherapy [21]. Together, these mixed results demonstrate the need for a personalized approach to managing this type of cancer. A key consideration for these interventions is to minimize long-term damage caused by radiation or antimitotic therapies while also addressing the immediate risks of the tumor.

Preclinical research models

Preclinical research models are critical for advancing our mechanistic understanding of CPC and the molecular and pathophysiological diversity among CPC subtypes. This is especially true because these tumors are so rare that resections do not provide enough samples of similar genetic changes to enable large-scale mechanistic investigation from patient samples. Additionally, models that represent the molecular and genetic susceptibilities of these tumors that are adaptable to testing treatments or more high-throughput therapeutic screening have the potential to advance treatments and even generate personalized screens for tumors in individualized patient populations, a key to treating rare diseases [59].

Animal models

Preclinical animal research tools and models have facilitated the investigation of choroid plexus carcinomas by allowing detailed analyses of the tumor microenvironment, mechanisms, and metabolic processes. Small vertebrates can be used to screen sufficiency of mutations to induce tumors and are used as models to study CPC tumorigenesis, providing insights into the complex interactions that drive tumor development and progression, which would be otherwise unethical and impractical to observe in human patients [33, 60–64].

Cross-species genomic analyses between mouse models and human CPC samples have identified several functional oncogenes linked to CPC. Tong et al. [44] found that among 21 candidate genes from genomic studies, only three—RAD54L, TAF12, and NFYC—induced dysplasia and proliferation of choroid plexus epithelial cells in mouse models. TAF12 and NFYC are transcription factors that contain histone-fold domains; TAF12 recognizes core promoter elements, and NFYC regulates gene expression through CCAAT promoter binding, while RAD54L functions in DNA repair. In vitro knockouts of Rad54l and Taf12 triggered apoptosis and markedly reduced CPC cell viability, whereas Nfyc exerted a lesser effect. In vivo deletion of these genes in embryonic mice prevented CPC formation, confirming their essential role in tumor initiation. Moreover, in mice predisposed to CPC, knockout of Rad54l, Taf12, and Nfyc significantly suppressed tumor development, whereas their overexpression—when combined with loss of TP53, Rb, or Pten—accelerated CPC progression. These findings demonstrate that RAD54L, TAF12, and NFYC are critical oncogenic drivers that cooperate with tumor suppressor loss to promote CPC formation.

Animal models traditionally involve manipulating tumor suppressor genes that are associated with CPC in patients, including gene knockouts and oncogene activation. Some studies have employed the mouse Cre-LoxP system to facilitate site-specific recombination in neural progenitor cells, thereby generating the desired CPC phenotype [64]. Multiple cell-type-specific conditional knockouts have generated choroid plexus tumors. Breeding Cre lines with either broad expression in CNS precursors (GFAP-Cre in radial glia, astrocytes, oligodendroglia, and ependyma) or (Blbp-Cre; aka Fabp7-Cre in radial glia), or more restricted expression in precursors of the cerebellum and dorsal hindbrain (Atoh1-Cre; aka Math-Cre) with lox-stop-lox-MycT58A (a stabilized form of MYC) to overexpress MYC long-term resulted in the development of CPPs [64]. Subsequently, further crossbreeding of these Cre-induced CPP lines with p53fl/fl mice has led to the targeted deletion of p53, promoting the expression of CPCs [65]. Specifically, Atoh1-Cre and GFAP-Cre both induced choroid plexus tumors in the fourth ventricle choroid plexus exclusively, while Blbp-Cre induced tumors in the choroid plexus tissues of all four ventricles including lateral ventricle choroid plexus, third ventricle choroid plexus, and fourth ventricle choroid plexus [64]. Another research team developed a murine model using a Cre line with expression in neuroectoderm that refines expression by E16.5 to retina, forebrain, ventral midbrain, and fourth ventricle choroid plexus (Otx2-Cre) crossed with Myc; p53fl/fl progeny. This line resulted in tumor development with 100% penetrance predominantly in the lateral and fourth ventricle choroid plexus tissues [62]. A Cre line with recombination in roof plate and some rhombic lip progenitors (Lmx1a-Cre) used to induce p53 and Rb loss of function, generating Lmx1a-Cre; p53fl/fl;Rbfl/fl progeny, demonstrates the pivotal role of the GMNC-MIDAS transcriptional network in regulating multi-ciliated cell expression within the human and mouse choroid plexus [63]. When defunct p53 and Rb functions disrupts this GMNC-MIDAS ciliogenesis network, it correlates with increased proliferation of CPC tumor cells in the mouse model [44, 62, 63]. Collectively, these findings highlight that the combination of disrupting the p53–Rb tumor suppressor axis and sustained MYC activation drive CPC formation by destabilizing normal choroid plexus progenitor differentiation. This convergence across multiple models underscores a shared oncogenic mechanism despite variations in Cre drivers and anatomical sites.

Conversely, overactivation of NOTCH or MYC are alternative methods for inducing choroid plexus tumorigenesis that differ from models that rely on tumor suppressor gene deletion. CPPs are induced by NOTCH overactivation. Notch3 acts as an oncogene in mice that promotes development of CPPs by increasing proliferation in the choroid plexus anlagen [46]. Similarly, using a constitutively active NOTCH1 intracellular domain (NICD1) in the LMX1a lineage induced CPP [45] supporting a role for Sox2 acts as a main effector mediating these tumorigenic effects [45]. Mouse models of CPP that are generated by constitutive activation of NOTCH signaling also upregulate the canonical WNT pathway [66], consistent with implication of WNT in patient samples [46]. MYC overexpression has been achieved using the Nestin-Cre; Myc model to induce CPC. This CPC model was developed by introducing a human MYC transgene into flox-stop-flox MYC mice, which are subsequently crossbred with Nestin-Cre mice, resulting in Nestin-Cre-driven MYC overexpression in Nestin-expressing cells in the CNS and PNS, including neuronal and glial cell precursors [33]. Mice that report for Nestin lineage (Nestin-Cre/RosamTmG) showed recombination in each choroid plexus by postnatal day 8, showing that the choroid plexus arises from Nestin-expressing cells [33]. Notably, in this model, tumor progression exhibits a specific location pattern in the posterior lateral and fourth ventricles that recapitulates the tissue distribution for CPC in a majority of patients [22, 23, 33]. This ability of MYC to drive CPC in mice under the Nestin promoter [33], but only CPP under Atoh1-Cre [64], strongly indicates that cell type and cell developmental stage is critical for this and likely other CPC driver events.

Similarly, Cre-recombinase systems have been employed in rodents for transplant into zebrafish models to investigate CPCs. For instance, xenografts derived from a mouse CPC model engineered to express p53fl/fl, Rb fl/fl, and PTEN fl/fl alleles were implanted into zebrafish [61]. These xenografts produce the parent brain tumors; however, the zebrafish vasculature was recruited to support tumor growth [61]. This xenograft model is a cost-effective approach for generating large quantities of CPCs, which is otherwise challenging to produce in larger animals like mice. Additionally, it provides a valuable platform for testing the drug efficacy of target therapies like tyrosine-kinase inhibitors [61]. Non-human tumor xenografts may yield insights into tumor transcriptomes and metabolic processes that are not fully representative of the human populations affected by CPCs. Additionally, while the natural incidence of CPC has been documented in rats and dogs, literature on the use of these species as preclinical research models in relation to human pathogenesis remains limited [60, 67]. Together, these findings underscore that MYC-driven and tumor-suppressor–deficient models each capture distinct facets of CPC biology, emphasizing that multiple cellular contexts can give rise to similar malignant phenotypes.

Cell line models

Cell lines are powerful tools for understanding tumors and for in vitro high-throughput screening of potential pharmaceutical treatments [68]. For CPC, a variety of cell lines exist as in vitro models of the choroid plexus and of CPC more specifically. Each of the types of cellular models has its own unique benefits and limitations, summarized below.

Human-derived cell lines have been used to examine tumor metabolism and gene expression in CPC. The CPC-2 cell line, derived from a from a pediatric patient of Japanese origin, has been used in comparative studies analyzing aberrant junctional protein expression in these tumor cells [69]. More specifically, CPC-2 cells exhibit aberrant adherens junction (AJ) proteins, including mislocalized E-cadherin or complete absence of α-catenin, resulting in irregular choroid plexus epithelial monolayers [69]. Such AJ abnormalities are characteristic features observed in various carcinoma cell lines [69] and in this case render the cell line less appropriate to study normal choroid plexus barrier function. However, this junction anomaly may reflect true CPC pathology, although no detailed analysis has been reported in patient or preclinical tumor samples. This observation in cell lines may represent a key opportunity for further study into disruption of the key choroid plexus B-CSF-B in CPC. Cell line CCHE-45, from a pediatric CPC patient in Egypt, showcases deviation from normal cell proliferation and differentiation pathways, the loss of tumor suppression via deletion of TP53, along with the formation of aggresomes, which induce misfolded protein formation and their subsequent removal through autophagy [70, 71]. The NGT131 cell line, from a 4-year-old boy, has also been used to study aberrant cell proliferation and differentiation pathways similar to those observed in CCHE-45 [71]. All of these cell lines were established from surgical tumor resections [69–71]. Using CCHE-45, a high-throughput compound screen identified potential chemotherapeutic agents that, in turn, guided a preclinical study to test melphalan/elimusertib both in this line and in vivo with intra-arterial delivery to the flox-stop-flox MYC x Nestin-Cre CPC mouse model [33, 72], highlighting the utility of combining cell models with animal models. Because CPCs are rare and because the development of human-derived cell lines demands considerable time and resources, human-derived CPC cell lines are sparse [73, 74]. The scarcity of CPC lines significantly contributes to the limitation of fully understanding and addressing the complexities of tumor oncogenesis. Additionally, the existing CPC cell lines do not represent the full spectrum of human genetic ancestry. This barrier diminishes the genetic and epigenetic components of CPC tumors, thereby limiting the generalizability of findings [73, 74], revealing additional opportunity for generating cell lines that more fully represent the patient population.

Directed differentiation of human pluripotent cells has generated 2D [75] and 3D (organoid) [76] choroid plexus epithelial cells that have become useful modes to investigate human-specific genetic necessity for transforming healthy choroid plexus into tumors. For example, constitutive activation of the WNT/ β-catenin pathway was able to induce tumors in 2D in vitro models of the choroid plexus either through knockout of the negative regulator APC or overexpression of the ligand WNT3A. Further, increased Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation in choroid plexus organoids by treating with a GSK3β inhibitor, reverted mature choroid plexus epithelial cells to a more immature state and depleting APC induced the transformation of choroid plexus organoids [77]. Thus, these human cellular models hold great promise to bridge the key work in animal models with human genetic studies. However, while powerful to test cell-autonomous transformation, models that only incorporate one cell type, choroid plexus epithelial cells, will fall short in modeling the full tumor environment.

Outlook: future directions and open questions

CPCs are aggressive malignant tumors largely affecting young children [17, 26]. Currently, the intervention that results in the most significantly improved overall survival is gross surgical resection [17, 26]. Although surgical-related morbidity remains a significant complication those patients whose CPC resection surgeries resulted in near-total or gross total CPC tumor resection demonstrated a 5-year survival rate of over 85% [31, 32]. Radiation is largely recommended for patients after a gross total CPC resection, but this is not useful for LFS patients and is often not feasible for very young pediatric CPC patients given the potential long-term neurological effects on the developing brain [26]. Further, radiation has not been shown to be curative in CPC patients [31]. One possible alternative is the use of intensive chemotherapy without irradiation, especially for those with TP53 mutations, which are found in 50% of CPC cases [17, 55]. “Head Start”-like chemotherapy (e.g., vincristine, cyclophosphamide, etoposide, cisplatin, and high-doses of systemic methotrexate), which are designed for young children to decrease the risk of long-term side effects, and I.C.E. chemotherapy (ifosfamide, carboplatin, and etoposide) have also been shown to be effective and appropriate for CPC [17]. Depending on CPC presentation and development, a patient may also differ in terms of needed treatment combinations; prognostic factors need to be more clearly determined [31]. Therefore, precision oncology may offer significant advantages in refractory CPC cases.

Preclinical animal models, cell lines, human pluripotent cell-derived choroid plexus models, and human genetics have all proven to be exceptional tools in the arsenal of scientists and clinicians to continue learning about and targeting these devastating tumors. Overall, opportunities remain for further experimental investigations into causative effects, cell-type of origin, and molecular genomic data on adjuvant therapies to identify safe, comprehensive treatment plans, especially for young children with incompletely resected tumors [17]. Further, by determining the molecular profile of CPC in individual patients, including identifying biomarkers that define specific molecular subgroups, integrating this information with a robust bioinformatics system capable of linking targeted chemotherapy to the appropriate therapeutic targets, and generating personalized preclinical or cell line models, there are ample opportunities to continue to improve survival and health for these vulnerable patients [17, 78].

Eligibility criteria

A systematic literature review was conducted to identify current information on CPC pathology, diagnosis, and treatment. Articles were limited to those peer-reviewed and written in English during or after 1994, excluding those that had an exclusive focus on CPP or were classified as editorials, letters, and/or commentaries (Fig. 2). Articles classified as literature reviews that did not contain the presence of novel case reports and literature that exclusively focused on the adult patient population were also excluded. Seventy-five were selected for final synthesis to create a comprehensive overview of CPC. Other references outside of these selection criteria were included if they served as key foundational knowledge.
