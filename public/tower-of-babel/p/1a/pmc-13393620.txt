A methodological framework for establishing regional disease-specific health research priorities (the RDHRP Framework)
Authors: Xiayan Chen, Xianghui Zhang, Qianqian Li, Yanjun Ma, Siyu Chen, Yifang Yuan, Yangfeng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSetting regional disease-specific health research priorities is essential for guiding the development of the local health system and even the society through research. However, existing priority-setting approaches either rely heavily on opinions and lack transparent and reproducible procedures or demand intensive technical resources and are time-consuming. This study aimed to develop a structured, disease-specific, regionally adaptable methodological framework to address these gaps.MethodsA multidisciplinary working group with expertise in public health and clinical research methodology was established to develop the framework, with consultation from an expert panel in clinical medicine and health research administration. Through iterative discussions, the group defined five guiding principles: gap-oriented, evidence-based, disease-life-cycle-covered, aligned with clinical practice realities and reproducible and transparent. Based on these principles, the analytical steps of the framework were proposed, refined through expert review and pilot tested using gastric cancer and other diseases.ResultsA framework consisting of seven sequential steps and a generic health matrix template was developed and named the Regional Disease-Specific Health Research Priorities (RDHRP) Framework: (1) defining the research scope; (2) developing a disease-specific matrix for data search; (3) retrieving and processing indicator-based evidence; (4) synthesizing evidence and linking performance gaps to generate actionable research priorities; (5) drafting the preliminary report; (6) consulting experts and stakeholders; and (7) reporting, dissemination and monitoring. Future work is warranted to evaluate its utility in diverse global settings, recognizing that resulting priorities may vary depending on disease, regional context and evolving evidence.Keywords: Health research priorities, Regional priorities, Research gap identification, Regional benchmarking, Evidence-informed policy, Methodological framework

Background

Research priorities refer to research topics or questions identified and ranked as most important for investigation through a systematic priority-setting process, typically to guide the allocation of limited research resources and address key knowledge gaps [1, 2]. Establishing regional (that is, referring to a particular area of a country or the world) disease-specific health research priorities is critical for policy-makers, research funders and academic institutions to guide policy development, strategic planning and resource allocation aimed at improving population health through scientific research. Priority-setting also helps orient researchers toward questions that address pressing societal and health system needs [3, 4].

Although the concept of research priority-setting is widely recognized, both the levels at which priorities are defined and the methods used to determine them vary substantially. Priority-setting may occur at global, national or regional, institutional or project levels, each reflecting different objectives and decision contexts. Correspondingly, diverse methodological approaches have been used, including burden-of-disease analyses, evidence-gap assessments, Delphi consensus methods, stakeholder consultations, scoping reviews and strategic foresight exercises [5–8].

In practice, however, priority-setting is often influenced by expert opinion or institutional preferences rather than being fully evidence-informed, transparent and aligned with societal needs [1, 5]. This can result in fragmented or duplicative efforts that diverge from pressing health challenges and lead to inefficient use of limited resources [9]. To address these concerns, several structured and systematic approaches have been developed, including the James Lind Alliance Priority Setting Partnerships(JLA PSP), the Child Health and Nutrition Research Initiative (CHNRI) and the Essential National Health Research (ENHR) strategy [10–12], as well as burden-based prioritization frameworks proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO) [13]. Despite their methodological rigor, these approaches are relatively underused because they are resource-intensive and technically demanding, and many regions lack the expertise required for their implementation [14]. Furthermore, reliance on expert judgment may introduce bias, while burden-based frameworks may overlook systemic drivers of knowledge gaps and research needs [14].

Importantly, few existing approaches are designed for disease-specific research priority-setting, and most do not examine research gaps across the full disease life cycle, from prevention and diagnosis to treatment and rehabilitation [15]. Yet, disease-level priorities are often more practical for implementation, as most researchers work within defined disease domains. Meanwhile, advances in artificial intelligence (AI) offer opportunities to accelerate evidence synthesis and literature analysis, although standards for responsible and rigorous use are still evolving [16, 17].

To address these gaps, the present study aims to develop a structured, regionally adaptable methodological framework that integrates systematic evidence synthesis and expert input to identify disease/health-condition-specific regional health research priorities across the full disease life cycle, aligned with real-world clinical and public health practice. The focus of the paper is on priority-setting in health research, rather than on healthcare interventions or service provision.

Methods

The working group was established to take responsibility for developing the methodology for setting regional health research priorities, and its members included academic scholars with backgrounds in research methodology, public health, clinical medicine, health policy and health research management. In addition, external expert inputs were obtained through consultations with a multidisciplinary panel, including health administrators, clinicians, epidemiologists and clinical research methodologists.

Definition of terminologies

To guide framework development, we first established working definitions of regional gap in population health, regional health research needs and corresponding research priorities through expert consultation and iterative discussions:

Regional gap in population health: a measurable deficiency, disparity or uncertainty in the population health status, health service performance or health system outcomes of a specific region, relative to internal goals or external benchmarks.

Regional health research need: arises when an identified gap in population health requires new knowledge, interventions or system improvements to address the underlying causes of the gap.

Regional health research priority: represents a subset of research needs considered most important on the basis of their expected clinical significance, innovation potential, feasibility of implementation, economic implications and acceptability to stakeholders.

These priorities are not tied to individual studies but define strategic directions that can inform medium- to long-term research planning and guide investigators in selecting research ideas aligned with regional health needs.

Establishing methodological principles

We performed a literature review to assess the advantages and disadvantages of existing priority-setting methodologies. The identified literature [2, 5, 10–12] together with informal exploratory efforts in evidence searching and evaluation informed the development of five methodological principles, which were then established through iterative discussions within the working group and in consultation with the key stakeholders: (1) gap-oriented: focus on identifying performance deficiencies; (2) evidence-based: rely on verifiable, high-quality and traceable evidence; (3) disease-life-cycle-covered: assess gaps across all stages of care, that is, prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation; (4) aligned with clinical practice realities: maintain alignment with real unmet needs in clinical and public health practice; (5) reproducible and transparent: ensure that procedures are consistently repeatable and clearly documented.

Construction and refinement of methodological framework

To operationalize these methodological principles, we developed a structured workflow consisting of seven steps (refer to Fig. 1 and the “Results” section for details) through iterative internal discussions. We also developed a generic health matrix template based on disease burden and the disease life cycle. It contains five domains, with the Burden of Disease as the leading domain and the other four domains as explanatory domains, including Prevention, Diagnosis, Treatment and Rehabilitation (refer to Fig. 2 and the “Results” section for details). The leading domain will guide the gap identification at the population health level, and explanatory domains will guide gaps in each key disease life cycle stage where disease burden arises. Indicators within each domain were selected from commonly used clinical and public health measures and refined through multidisciplinary discussion.

These steps and the generic health matrix template were jointly reviewed by clinical experts selected by the Beijing Medical Association and representatives from the health authority because they are the primary end users responsible for implementing research prioritization and translating priorities into funding and policy decisions. They agreed that the overall approach was feasible and appropriate; their feedback centered on three points. First, while AI tools may support evidence searching in practice, the formal methodology should emphasize evaluating the quality and reliability of retrieved evidence rather than highlighting AI use. Second, the workflow should be driven by disease burden and grounded in real clinical needs, with final priorities validated by expert panels designated by the relevant regional professional associations. Third, a standardized reporting format should be established to ensure clarity and consistency across disease areas.

Pilot testing and finalization of methodological framework

The draft framework was pilot tested using gastric cancer and cardiovascular disease as case studies in the Beijing regional project. The pilot assessed the usability of the generic health matrix template, the feasibility of evidence retrieval procedures, the clarity and consistency of gap identification, the interpretability of underlying causes mapping, the practicality of the reporting structure, and overall workload and data demands across a multidisciplinary analyst team. Based on these findings, we refined the framework by clarifying data sources and recommended literature types, formalizing gap categories, defining dimensions for identification of underlying causes and strengthening the distinction between research needs and priorities. The revised framework was then applied to additional disease areas, demonstrating broader applicability. For conditions with multiple subdomains, specifying the exact disease focus prior to analysis proved essential.

Results – the methodological framework

The framework is named the Regional Disease-Specific Health Research Priorities (RDHRP) Framework. It consists of seven sequential steps plus a generic health matrix template. The seven steps include (Fig. 1): (1) defining the research scope; (2) developing a disease-specific matrix for data search; (3) retrieving and processing indicator-based evidence; (4) synthesizing evidence and linking performance gaps to generate actionable research priorities; (5) drafting the preliminary report; (6) consulting experts and stakeholders; and (7) reporting, dissemination and monitoring. These steps are preceded by the development of a generic health matrix template (Fig. 2), which provides a standardized foundation for evaluating healthcare system performance across prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. As the identification and formulation of research gaps, needs and priorities is a scientific process, users of this framework are expected to be at the master’s level or above in health sciences or medicine and possess foundational skills in evidence-based medicine, literature search and synthesis, and interdisciplinary analysis, without requiring specialized clinical expertise.

A generic health matrix template provides a standardized structure for developing disease-specific health matrices and for systematically identifying gaps in a region’s clinical and public health practices, ensuring compatibility across different diseases and health conditions (Fig. 2). It begins with key indicators of global and local burden of disease – such as mortality, incidence, prevalence and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) – to highlight macro-level gaps and guide subsequent gap identification across prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation.

In the Prevention domain, indicators typically include the prevalence, awareness, treatment and control of major risk factors, as well as the availability and coverage of effective population-level preventive interventions.

In the Diagnosis domain, indicators focus on underdiagnosis, misdiagnosis, early detection, adoption of advanced diagnostics and new diagnostic technologies in research and development.

In the Treatment domain, indicators assess short- and long-term outcomes such as 7-day or 30-day mortality, overall survival and disease-free survival, along with the adoption of advanced therapies and new treatment options in research and development.

In the Rehabilitation domain, indicators cover recovery, recurrence and quality of life.

Overall, the template enables comprehensive and comparable assessment across diseases while remaining adaptable to disease-specific characteristics and regional contexts.

Step 1: Defining scope for research

This step defines the specific disease/health conditions to be reviewed and analyzed, the target region and comparator regions. The disease/health conditions are selected by the working group, according to the regional burden of disease on the basis of global and regional health statistics, in consultation with the key stakeholders, the Beijing Municipal Health Commission in the present study. For example, cardiovascular diseases represent a major global health burden, while gastric cancer has a particularly high burden in China, and both were used as pilot cases. In practice, it may be selected by the researcher’s own interest, particularly when rare diseases are to be addressed.

The target region may range from the city to the subnational or national level. Comparators are selected on the basis of similarity in demography, disease burden and epidemiological characteristics, while possessing more advanced health system capacity. These choices provide the foundation for disease-specific health matrix development and subsequent analysis.

Step 2: Developing a disease-specific matrix

While the generic health matrix template (Fig. 2), based on the disease-lifecycle approach, provides a consistent foundation for assessment, disease-specific adjustments are often necessary to ensure clinical relevance and interpretability. This step involves customizing the health matrix on the basis of the clinical, epidemiological and policy characteristics of each disease, following an initial review of disease-specific knowledge. We suggest checking the most up-to-date clinical guidelines to obtain disease-specific knowledge in prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. Adaptation ensures that selected indicators reflect both the natural history of the disease and the decision-making needs of stakeholders. For example, early diagnosis may be defined by cancer stage distribution in oncology but by community-based cognitive screening rates among older adults for Alzheimer’s disease. Similarly, treatment effectiveness may rely on survival outcomes in oncology but on time-to-intervention metrics (for example, thrombolysis and thrombectomy rates) and in-hospital mortality in stroke.

Step 3: Retrieving and processing indicator-based evidence

For evidence search and retrieval, a targeted, indicator-driven rather than comprehensive systematic review is adopted. There are broadly four categories of data sources to be considered: (1) global databases (for example, Global Burden of Disease, Global Cancer Observatory, Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development Health Statistics, World Health Organization Global Health Observatory), which provide comparable metrics on disease burden and health system capacity; (2) clinical guidelines and knowledge platforms (for example, UpToDate, Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology, National Comprehensive Cancer Network), which reflect current knowledge of the disease, expert consensus and current standards of care; (3) peer-reviewed scientific literature, retrieved from PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus and Google Scholar, with a focus on high-quality evidence including systematic reviews, randomized controlled trials and nationally representative studies; and (4) clinical trial registries (for example, ClinicalTrials.gov, the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform), which provide insights into ongoing and upcoming studies, emerging technologies and gaps not yet addressed in published literature.

Direct keyword-based searches combining indicator, disease and regional terms were used as the primary searching strategy, with AI tools applied only to speed background scanning and all outputs verified by human reviewers. Evidence selection prioritizes timely, credible and representative sources with transparent methods, favoring population-level estimates from national datasets or high-impact studies when multiple sources exist. To guide indicator-based evidence retrieval, literature types are categorized into disease registries and surveillance data, international/national/regional health reports, cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and health economic evaluations. Reference management and review platforms such as EndNote, Rayyan or Covidence are recommended to facilitate workflow tracking, reviewer collaboration and auditability.

Step 4: Synthesizing evidence and linking performance gaps to generate actionable research priorities

This step integrates the evidence retrieved to identify performance gaps across the disease burden, prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation domains. Indicators are systematically compared against benchmarks to detect areas of significant underperformance, and potential gaps are retained only when supported by reliable, consistent and traceable evidence. This framework adopts a gap → cause → need → priority logic, operationalized through structured discussions within the working group. First, after mapping all domain-level gaps, we identify a set of critical gaps, those marked by large or persistent shortfalls, unfavorable temporal trends or strong associations with downstream outcomes. Key contradictions, such as low incidence but high mortality, improving treatment availability without corresponding gains in survival, or striking discrepancies between data sources, serve as diagnostic entry points in the gap analysis. These inconsistencies prompt researchers to determine whether the observed gaps reflect true system underperformance or arise from data-quality issues, inconsistent indicator definitions or fragmented surveillance systems. Such contradictions guide deeper inquiry. For example, a pattern of stable incidence but persistently high mortality may indicate inadequate early detection, suboptimal risk-factor control, limited treatment capacity or poor access to effective technologies. Conversely, improvements in diagnostic or therapeutic capacity without parallel outcome gains may suggest weak guideline implementation, affordability barriers or organizational inefficiencies. When population-level indicators conflict with hospital-based data, researchers must assess the completeness, representativeness and methodological consistency of each source.

Second, for each critical gap, identification of underlying causes is conducted using the WHO Health System Building Blocks, which includes leadership and governance, service delivery, health financing, health workforce, medical products and technologies and health information systems [18]. For each identified gap, researchers examine plausible systemic drivers according to these building blocks using evidence from multiple data sources and assessed for internal consistency and logical coherence. For example, difficulties in scaling national gastric cancer screening may derive from constrained endoscopy capacity, uneven provider skills, low public acceptability of invasive tests, inadequate financing mechanisms or the absence of validated non-invasive technologies.

Third, each gap-cause pattern is translated into one or more research needs, framed as answerable questions about which interventions, models of care, technologies or policy instruments are required to address the underlying causes.

Finally, identified needs are then prioritized on the basis of five predefined criteria, including clinical or public health significance, innovation potential, feasibility of implementation, economic implications and acceptability to stakeholders [19]. Using these criteria as a structured guide, the working group conducts iterative discussions to reach consensus and select a limited number of key research priorities, typically 3–5 per disease.

Step 6: Consulting experts and stakeholders

Step 6 engages a designated expert focus group review organized through the regional professional medical associations relevant to the specific disease or health condition. To do this, we suggest that the draft health research priorities report should be distributed to the panel members prior to a face-to-face meeting. The meeting will give opportunities for the medical experts and working group to discuss and reach a consensus. In addition to reviewing the draft priorities, they may propose other research needs they consider important but are missing in the draft report. Where appropriate, this consultation process may also include patient representatives, caregivers and other relevant stakeholders to incorporate lived experiences and population perspectives alongside system-level evidence.

Step 7: Reporting, dissemination and monitoring

The final step integrates expert feedback and revises the analyzes as needed to produce the final report. The finalized report is then prepared for dissemination to regional health authorities, funding bodies, professional associations and research institutions to inform planning and guide future regional research agenda development.

Priorities implementation could be monitored by examining the extent to which priorities are taken up by funding bodies and policy-makers (for example, incorporation into funding calls, strategic plans or policy documents), as well as by tracking research responses from investigators (for example, registration of related studies in trial registries). Given the inherent time lag between priority-setting and research outputs, impact evaluation should be conducted over defined follow-up periods (for example, 3–5 years) using publication and patent tracking and bibliometric analysis, while acknowledging challenges in attribution. Findings from such monitoring can inform periodic reassessment and adaptive refinement of priorities as contextual conditions evolve.

Cases – gastric cancer

This pilot application was conducted under an initiative led by Beijing Municipal Health Commission and the Beijing Medical Association to identify regional health research priorities. Gastric cancer was selected as a test condition due to its substantial disease burden in China.

Defining the research scope

China was defined as the target region for analysis. Japan and South Korea were selected as primary comparator regions due to their demography and disease burden similarity, while the United Kingdom (UK) and the United States of America (USA) were included to provide additional international comparisons where high-quality population-level data and mature health system performance indicators are available.

Developing a disease-specific matrix for data search

Retrieving and processing indicator-based evidence

Disease burden

Gastric cancer remains a major health burden in China, with age-standardized mortality (21.51 per 100,000), incidence (29.05 per 100,000) and DALY rates (501.26 per 100,000) markedly higher than those in comparator countries [21].

Prevention

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) remains the dominant preventable risk factor for gastric cancer [22]. H. pylori prevalence in China is estimated at 55.8%, comparable to Japan (51.7%) and South Korea (54.0%) [23]. In Beijing, prevalence is estimated at 35–40% [24], comparable to the UK (35.5%) and the USA (35.6%). Treatment uptake for H. pylori is low in China, despite high eradication efficacy (89.4%) and the availability of effective regimens such as rifasutenizol-based triple therapy [25, 26]. Japan and South Korea operate national population-based gastric cancer screening programs, whereas China adopts a modified high-risk strategy [27–29]. Key challenges in China include low endoscopic detection rates, the absence of efficient prescreening tools and limited health economic research [30].

Diagnosis

Endoscopic non-detection rates for gastric cancer in Japan and major Chinese centers range from approximately 5–28%, mainly reflecting variability in endoscopist expertise [31, 32]. AI-assisted systems substantially reduce the non-detection rate, with reported sensitivities of 92.2% in Japan and 94.2% in China [31].

Early-stage gastric cancer detection in China remains low: only 21% were stage I. In contrast, early-stage detection reaches 63% in Japan and 61% in South Korea [33–36].

Recent advances in gastric cancer diagnostics include a mobile capsule endoscopy system with 99.4% diagnostic accuracy and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) assays with 92.07% specificity and 80.77% sensitivity, both of which have obtained marketing authorization in China. Other emerging non-invasive technologies, such as Raman spectroscopy and plasma metabolic fingerprinting, are under active investigation [37–44].

Treatment

Five-year survival for gastric cancer is 35.9% in China, 60.3% in Japan, 68.9% in South Korea, 20.7% in the UK and 33.1% in the USA [45].

For patients with T2–T4 disease undergoing laparoscopy, China reported a 3-year disease-free survival of 76.5% and a 5-year overall survival of 72.6%, similar to Japan and South Korea [46–49]. Robotic gastrectomy in China, Japan and South Korea shows a 0% mortality rate and a 1.0–5.3% morbidity rate; however, most Chinese evidence comes from single-center retrospective studies without multicenter prospective data [50].

Most targeted therapies and immunotherapies available in the USA are also approved in China, with most approved after 2016; trastuzumab, vidicitumumab and apatinib have been included in national insurance coverage. From 2012 to 2021, 114 gastric cancer clinical trials were registered in China (3.7% of all oncology trials), with a rapid increase after 2016 and a peak in 2020; globally, major investigational targets include human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), claudin-18 isoform 2 (CLDN18.2), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR)[51].

Synthesizing evidence and linking performance gaps to generate actionable research priorities

Gap summary

China’s major gaps compared with leading countries lie in its higher incidence and lower 5-year overall survival of gastric cancer, while the lack of effective therapies for advanced disease remains a major challenge globally.

Identification of underlying causes

Based on the identified performance gaps and comparative evidence, three explanatory pathways were identified through structured discussion within the working group.

First, high incidence appears to be associated with insufficient population-level H. pylori detection and eradication coverage, ongoing household transmission, low public awareness and reliance on opportunistic screening rather than organized population-based screening, in contrast to Japan’s national H. pylori control program.

Second, low overall survival is primarily attributable to insufficient early-stage diagnosis rather than inferior treatment quality. Constraining factors include limited endoscopic capacity, inadequate primary-care endoscopy services, the absence of robust prescreening tools and low public acceptability.

Third, therapeutic progress for advanced gastric cancer is restricted by biological heterogeneity, low prevalence of actionable targets, inconsistent biomarker concordance and modest efficacy of current molecular and immunotherapies.

The preliminary analysis identified three research needs through structured discussion within the working group:

developing simplified, cost-efficient screening technologies and validated early-warning biomarkers to expand early detection;

advancing therapeutic innovation for advanced and metastatic gastric cancer through molecular subtype-oriented target discovery and prospective clinical evaluation.

the hospital-based and incomplete nature of existing data systems, which fail to capture reliable data on community-level incidence, early cases and risk-factor distributions;

Discussion

This study presents the development and initial application of a structured methodological framework for establishing region-specific health research priorities according to regional gaps in clinical and public health practices. The tool is organized into seven steps and supported by a detailed reporting checklist. Each step is adaptable, allowing users to tailor the framework to diverse regional or institutional decision contexts. From a policy perspective, the framework supports evidence-informed decision-making by linking research investment to identified health system needs. Its applications include guiding funding allocation, informing the development of new technologies or therapeutics and supporting the design of real-world implementation studies tailored to local contexts. It also fosters cross-sectoral collaboration by aligning research planning with measurable system-level deficiencies.

Unlike prior approaches that often rely on early expert involvement or predefined agendas, this framework emphasizes systematic search and synthesis of evidence before engaging professionals and stakeholders [10, 11, 52]. This workflow minimized subjective bias, reduced the time and effort required from medical experts and ensured adherence to an evidence-based approach.

Compared with the existing approaches for establishing research priorities such as CHNRI, JLA, ENHR and burden-based prioritization frameworks proposed by the WHO, our method brings forth the following new ideas [10–13]. First, we focus on regional or local special research needs instead of global general research needs. To achieve that, we proposed criteria for setting comparator regions so that key gaps in clinical and public health practices can be precisely identified and used as the fundamental goals directing the activities of prioritizing research questions. Second, the framework adopts a full-life-cycle analytical structure supported by a generic health matrix template, which enables systematic identification of gaps across disease burden, prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. This structured approach replaces ad hoc or keyword-based topic selection with an evidence-driven process that links observed gaps with their underlying causes and corresponding research needs. Third, the framework is designed to be operationally feasible with limited technical resources. In the pilot application, the initial evidence retrieval and synthesis were conducted by one to two trained researchers over approximately three weeks, followed by several working-group discussions and one to two rounds of expert consultation. Fourth, the integration of AI tools for literature retrieval may enhance operational efficiency and lower methodological barriers, where methodological expertise is limited. However, these tools serve only as supportive aids and cannot be applied without human monitoring and verification.

Nonetheless, this framework has several limitations. First, it relies primarily on publicly available literature and datasets, which may not fully capture unreported system-level deficiencies in frontline care or health governance. While expert and stakeholder consultations were incorporated to mitigate this disadvantage to some extent, obtaining comparable data from comparator regions remains challenging. Second, although the generic health matrix template enhances applicability, it may require further customization for rare or atypical conditions. Third, the reproducibility of the framework’s outputs has not yet been formally tested through application by independent teams. Finally, the current gastric cancer pilot did not incorporate patient or caregiver perspectives, as the initial application focused on testing the methodological framework. However, the framework supports inclusion of patient and public stakeholders during consultation, and future applications are encouraged to integrate patient input to enhance real-world relevance [53]. In fact, the framework is currently being piloted in 10 major disease areas in Beijing. Future applications in diverse regional and disease contexts are essential to refine the framework and enhance its robustness as a tool for informing evidence-based health research prioritization.
