Local excision after regrowth in rectal sparing: a review on iterative rectal preservation after neoadjuvant treatment
Authors: S Picotto, D Rega, A La Terra, A Muratore, P Delrio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRectal-sparing strategies for locally advanced rectal cancer are gaining interest owing to favorable oncological results and reduced impact on functional outcomes. In patients managed with watch-and-wait or local excision after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT), local regrowth occurs in approximately 15–30% of cases. Total mesorectal excision (TME) is the standard treatment for regrowth; however, local excision (LE) may be considered in selected cases to preserve rectal function. This narrative review evaluates clinical and oncological outcomes of patients undergoing LE for suspected regrowth.MethodsA narrative review of the literature was conducted using databases and search terms including “rectal cancer,” “rectal sparing,” and “local regrowth.”ResultsFive retrospective studies were identified, encompassing a total of 159 patients. Treatment protocols, neoadjuvant regimens, and follow-up strategies varied across the studies. Histopathological findings revealed ypT ≥ 2 in 45.3% of cases. Reported overall survival (OS) was consistently above 94.5%, while 2-year locoregional recurrence-free survival ranged from 74% to 85%. Systemic recurrence occurred in 9.1% of patients. LE was associated with shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, and lower rates of Clavien–Dindo ≥ 3 complications.ConclusionsLocal excision for regrowth may represent a feasible alternative to radical surgery in selected patients, particularly within specialized centers and under strict surveillance protocols. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate its long-term oncologic safety and functional outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-025-03228-x.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Rectal sparing, Local excision, Regrowth, Organ preservation

Background

Neoadjuvant treatment followed by total mesorectal excision (TME) has become the standard approach to locally advanced rectal cancer. In 1998, Habr-Gama introduced the idea of rectal sparing (RS), a multimodality strategy aimed at avoiding rectal resection in selected patients achieving a clinical complete response (cCR) or near-complete response (ncCR) after neoadjuvant treatment [1].

RS strategies have demonstrated a lower impact on patient functional outcomes, with fewer cases of low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), a reduced risk of urinary and sexual dysfunction, improved overall quality of life, and the avoidance of temporary or permanent stomas [2, 3]. Disease-free survival, local recurrence, metastatic disease, and overall survival have been found to be comparable to outcomes following TME, enabling less aggressive and tailored treatment even for patients who are either unfit for TME or unwilling to undergo major surgery [4].

However, the major drawback of RS strategies is local regrowth, which can occur in 15–30% of patients [5], usually within the first year [6, 7].

Owing to the high rate of tumor regrowth and the observation of complete pathological responses even in patients without a clinical complete response, the concept of “good responders” to neoadjuvant therapy has been introduced, and local excision (LE) has become the gold standard treatment for this subset of patients [8]. Furthermore, local excision is considered a viable option to continue preserving the rectum in case of regrowth, minimizing complications, and maintaining a good quality of life. This literature review aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes, survival rates, and postoperative morbidity of patients undergoing iterative rectal preservation following local regrowth.

Methods

This review was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) and is presented as a narrative review. Our analysis focused on the literature addressing rectal-sparing strategies in rectal cancer, with particular emphasis on local excision following local regrowth. We examined articles reporting various outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), in patients with suspected local regrowth. Additionally, we analyzed complication rates and postoperative outcomes, comparing LE for local regrowth with TME.

We searched multiple electronic databases—including PubMed, Embase, and Scopus—for studies published between 2020 and March 2025. Search terms included combinations of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), such as “rectal carcinoma,” “local excision,” “local regrowth,” and “rectal sparing.” The search was restricted to studies published in English.

Studies were included if they met the following PICO criteria:

We excluded studies that did not include local excision of the regrowth or failed to report relevant outcome data. The initial search yielded 136 potentially eligible studies, of which 5 met the inclusion criteria. To minimize bias from potential patient duplication, we assessed overlap based on study centers, periods, and author affiliations. In particular, Fernandez et al. [9] and Jehaes et al. [10], as well as Van der Sande et al. [11] and Geubels et al. [12], likely report data from overlapping cohorts. We conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding the studies most at risk of duplication to evaluate the robustness of the outcomes.

Rectal-sparing strategy

Patients with clinical complete response were offered to defer surgery and enter a rectal-sparing protocol, irrespective of baseline staging features. According to Habr-Gama et al. [13], cCR was defined as a combination of no palpable tumor on digital rectal examination, a white scar and/or telangiectasia of the mucosa at endoscopy, and a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) tumor regression grade 1. When a minor soft mucosa irregularity was felt on digital rectal examination (DRE), or a small superficial ulceration persisted at endoscopy, or MRI showed residual signal or remaining node(s), the response was reported as near complete [14, 15]. Patients with ncCR could either enter the RS protocol or undergo surgical removal.

Several surveillance protocols are described across the included studies, each with varying frequencies for testing and monitoring over different time periods. All protocols emphasize the need for regular monitoring with a combination of imaging, endoscopy, clinical and rectal examinations, and biochemical markers, generally performed by experienced colorectal teams and in highly dedicated centers with large experience in watch-and-wait (WW) strategies and RS. DRE, endoscopy, high-resolution pelvic MRI, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) testing are performed at intervals of 8–12 weeks for the first 2–3 years. The frequency is reduced to biannual checks in the third to fifth year. Computed tomography (CT) scans are done every 6 months for the first 3 years, then yearly thereafter. Overall, a minimum of 5-year follow-up is maintained.

Local regrowth

Local regrowth is generally defined as clinical or radiologic re-occurrence of the disease during follow-up in patients with initial cCR or ncCR treated with nonoperative management [16]. Local regrowth is preferably confirmed by biopsy but sometimes is only suspected on endoscopy or MRI and confirmed through local excision. Signs of luminal regrowth included new mucosal abnormalities on endoscopy, and/or mass or wall thickening of the fibrotic scar on T2W-MRI, or new focal high signal spots on DW-MRI. Lymph node enlargement or change in contour features on consecutive MRI could indicate lymphatic metastases.

In cases where local excision (LE) was performed for suspected local regrowth but the final histopathology revealed ypT0, patients were still included in the analysis. Although these cases do not represent confirmed tumor regrowth, LE in this context serves an essential diagnostic and therapeutic function. Given the difficulty in reliably distinguishing true regrowth from post-treatment changes on the basis of solely clinical, endoscopic, or radiological assessment, LE is often employed to obtain definitive histological confirmation. Therefore, the inclusion of these patients was deemed appropriate to accurately reflect the real-world clinical use of LE in the watch-and-wait setting.

Salvage surgery

Salvage surgery is defined as total mesorectal excision performed after local excision when high-risk histopathological features are present (such as ypT2–T3 stage or positive margins), or as definitive surgical treatment for confirmed local recurrence following a conservative approach [17].

In one study, local excision after regrowth is compared with patients with ncCR after neoadjuvant treatment who underwent LE [10]. One study analyzes the two different surgical strategies in case of regrowth, namely local excision versus TME [9]. Two studies evaluate WW surveillance and management of local regrowth in general [11, 18]. Only one study specifically targets patients who developed local regrowth and underwent local excision, with a 6-month cutoff after the end of radiation to evaluate early and late LE [12].

Patient characteristics

A total of 159 patients who underwent local excision of suspected regrowth are included. The included population across these studies consists of patients with locally advanced rectal cancer who were managed with a watch-and-wait strategy after achieving a complete or near-complete response to neoadjuvant therapy. Median reported age ranges between 60 and 66 years. Population gender was reported for 103 patients (62.8%): 22 female (31.1%) and 71 male (68.9%).

Tumors are mainly located in the lower rectum, with a 3–7 cm distance from the anal verge. In two studies, tumors undergoing local excision are found to be smaller (35.9 ± 11.0 mm versus 43.4 ± 15.1 mm; p = 0.04) and earlier in stage (cT2 40.9% versus 18.6%; p = 0.05) than those undergoing TME [9], with a predominance of cN0 (71%) over cN+ (29%) [10]. In a larger study involving 77 local excisions, on the other hand, a broader range of tumor stages is described, with cT1, cT2, cT3, and cT4 accounting for 8%, 25%, 61%, and 7% of cases, respectively. Tumor nodal status is also variable with 43% being cN0, 33% cN1, and 25% cN2 [12].

Neoadjuvant treatment

The period analyzed by the various studies, spanning from 1993 to 2022, witnessed significant changes and innovations in the field of neoadjuvant treatment for rectal cancer. As a result, the treatment protocols reported across these studies are highly variable and heterogeneous.

Indications for neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) are reported to include high-risk features at staging, such as cT3/T4, cN+, extramural venous invasion (EMVI)+, and/or threatened circumferential resection margin [9].

Of the 159 total patients, 118 patients (74.2%) were treated with nCRT consisting of radiotherapy (45–50.8Gy) and capecitabine or fluorouracil; 30 patients (18.9%) underwent chemoradiotherapy and consolidation or induction chemotherapy in a total neoadjuvant setting; 11 patients received short-course RT (6.9%).

Treatment of suspected regrowth

Across the six studies, 500 local regrowth are reported. Time to regrowth varies between 9 and 18 months. The median (interquartile range, IQR) time between the end of nCRT and LE is reported to be 7 (4–12) months.

Treatment of regrowth included 159 local excisions (31.8%), 308 total mesorectal excisions (61.6%) encompassing 205 low anterior resections (LAR) and 103 abdominoperineal resections (APR); 4 cases of palliative care (0.8%), brachytherapy in 2 cases (0.4%), and radiotherapy in 1 case (0.2%). For 26 patients, treatment of regrowth was not reported.

The surgical decision was reported to be based on many factors, the main being the extent of the disease, with tumor candidates for LE being smaller and earlier stage compared with those treated with TME. Secondly, patient preference and comorbidities, and above all the persistent will to preserve the rectum, directed the multidisciplinary tumor board (MDT) and surgeons toward this less invasive approach. Finally, local excision is used as a diagnostic tool when a small regrowth without signs of involved lymph nodes on MRI was not histologically proven.

Pathological findings

Among the 159 patients who underwent local excision for regrowth, histopathological data were available for 148 cases (93.1%). Of these, 24 were classified as ypT0, 21 as ypT0–1, and 18 as ypT1. An additional 18 cases were reported into a “ypT1–2” category. The majority—57 cases (38.5%)—were ypT2, while 10 were reported as ypT3–4 (Table 2). In the largest study, involving 77 local excisions [12], an R0 resection rate of 92% was documented.

Oncological outcomes after local excision of regrowth

One study reports an overall survival (OS) after surgery of regrowth in general [11] (2-year OS 98.4%), while only one study refers specifically to OS after local excision of regrowth. Geubels et al. [12], in their cohort of 77 local excisions, report a 2-year OS of 99% and a 5-year OS of 96%.

Specifically targeting local excision, the 3-year and 5-year locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS) are respectively reported to be 87% and 76% by Fernandez et al. [9], while Geubels et al. [12] reported a 5-years LRFS of 71%. Systemic recurrence occurs in 9.1% of cases [9], while 5-year distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) reaches 87% according to Jehaes et al. [10].

Finally, organ preservation rates following LE are reported at 79% at 2 years and 63% at 5 years [12].

Postoperative outcomes

Postoperative outcomes were evaluated in only two studies, likely reporting data from partially overlapping cohorts. Van der Sande et al. [11] compared operative variables and postoperative complications between LE and TME, highlighting significantly shorter operative time (80 min versus 232 min), reduced intraoperative blood loss (0 mL versus 100 mL), and shorter hospital stay (2 [IQR 1–3] days versus 8 [IQR 5–13] days) in the LE group. Notably, no severe postoperative complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 3) were reported after LE.

The Dutch Watch and Wait Consortium [12] reported that 6 out of 77 patients (8%) who underwent LE experienced Clavien–Dindo grade 2 or 3a, including one medical and five surgical events, specifically two postoperative bleedings, two rectal wound dehiscences, and one abscess—all managed conservatively.

Among those who underwent completion TME, 3 out of 13 patients (23%) experienced Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2, including one case requiring surgical intervention for anastomotic leakage. Similarly, in the salvage TME group, 21% experienced Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2 complications, with one patient also requiring surgery for an anastomotic leak.

Salvage surgery

Two studies (reporting on 103 patients in total) provide data on salvage surgery. Forty-two patients had ypT2–T3 disease after LE, and one had a positive resection margin (R1), leading to an indication for salvage TME in 43 cases. However, only 18 patients (42.8%) underwent TME, while the remaining 25 either declined or were deemed unfit for major surgery. Final pathology of the 18 salvage TME specimens showed no residual disease in four cases (22.2%). Colostomy was required in 11 patients.

An additional 15 patients underwent salvage TME for confirmed recurrence after LE. One more patient underwent transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM) for suspected regrowth, which revealed ypT3 on histology. Since TEM is still considered a form of LE, this patient subsequently underwent an abdominoperineal resection (APR), which showed ypT0 at final pathology. The overall colostomy rate by salvage surgery remained relatively low at 12.8%.

Discussion

Treatment of local regrowth is quite heterogeneous, ranging from local excision to TME [19, 20]. Most studies addressing this issue are observational and retrospective in design, often lacking a clearly defined protocol or treatment algorithm to guide therapeutic decision-making. Reported indications for LE after regrowth are largely individualized, depending on patient preference, multidisciplinary team evaluation, and institutional expertise, rather than uniformly established guidelines. Typically, LE is reserved for small, favorable tumors (e.g., cT2, cN0) without radiologic evidence of nodal involvement [15], whereas TME is recommended for more advanced or aggressive disease profiles [21].

However, as demonstrated by Geubels et al. [12], clinical and radiological restaging of regrowth is often inaccurate. In their series of patients with suspected locally advanced regrowth (68% cT3–4; 58% cN+), final histopathology following LE revealed actual regrowth (pT1–3 and/or pN+ at completion surgery) in only 36% of cases—meaning that 64% of patients were cancer-free despite concerning imaging features. As emphasized by Cerdán-Santacruz et al. [19], identifying true regrowth is considerably more challenging than detecting distant metastasis. Misdiagnosis of incomplete responders who were initially misclassified as having achieved a clinical complete response (cCR) can lead to both locoregional recurrence and systemic failure.

Despite these diagnostic limitations and the variability of follow-up and neoadjuvant protocols described in the literature, LE after regrowth has demonstrated favorable oncological outcomes. The 2-year distant metastasis-free survival rate following LE is reported at 91.8%, suggesting that, in appropriately selected patients, LE may offer excellent disease control. These results likely reflect both a favorable initial disease stage and the relatively earlier timing of regrowth, factors that support the choice of LE over radical interventions. According to the GRECCAR group, patients undergoing TME or APR for regrowth tend to present with more advanced disease at diagnosis and have a significantly lower 5-year DMFS, likely due to intrinsic tumor biology rather than the type of surgical approach itself [10]. Similarly, Dattani et al. [17] observed that 60% of metastases in WW patients occurred in the absence of local regrowth, suggesting that many distant failures represent the natural course of preexisting micrometastases—unlikely to be altered by upfront TME.

Importantly, all reported local recurrences after LE were successfully managed with salvage TME, with no further recurrence observed [12]. These findings echo the long-term results of the OPRA trial [22], which supports rectal-sparing approaches within structured follow-up frameworks.

From a critical perspective, local excision following regrowth should not be seen as merely a technical alternative to radical surgery, but as a strategic component of a broader, risk-adapted treatment paradigm. Its role becomes most appropriate in highly selected patients—those with limited regrowth, cN0 status on imaging, favorable tumor biology, and strong preference for organ preservation or significant comorbidities precluding major surgery. Nevertheless, a consensus on optimal oncologic selection criteria remains elusive. Ideally, candidates for LE after regrowth should fulfill both clinical (small, well-located lesions with clear margins achievable, absence of nodal involvement) and biological (slow-growing, low-grade histology, initial major response to nCRT) prerequisites.

In this context, LE can serve both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. It may clarify uncertain restaging findings or prevent overtreatment in cases where definitive TME might not be necessary. Therefore, LE should be integrated into a flexible and individualized algorithm rather than a binary choice between WW and radical surgery. Accurate and standardized restaging protocols—including advanced imaging, biopsies, and expert interpretation—will be essential to improve selection and reduce both under- and overtreatment.

Shared decision-making is also pivotal, as rectal cancer care often involves trade-offs between oncologic safety and functional outcomes. Yet, patient preferences are still underexplored and rarely incorporated systematically into treatment decisions, especially when discussing long-term implications such as permanent stomas or bowel dysfunction [23–25].

When comparing surgical approaches, LE offers clear procedural advantages over TME. Van der Sande et al. [11] reported shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, shorter hospital stay, and fewer severe complications with LE. Similarly, the Dutch WW Consortium [12] observed low Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ 2 complication rates (8%) after LE, all managed conservatively. In contrast, salvage or completion TME showed higher complication rates (21–23%), including surgical reintervention for anastomotic leakage.

With rectal preservation rates of 79% at 2 years and 63% at 5 years, the data support its potential to maintain rectal function even after regrowth. This is particularly meaningful in terms of quality of life and body image. Although the colostomy rate after regrowth is higher in patients undergoing salvage TME following LE compared with primary TME [12], it remained relatively low across the full cohort of patients included in this review.

However, data on functional outcomes and health-related quality of life remain scarce. Previous studies have shown that up to one-third of WW patients experience severe low anterior resection syndrome, likely due to the long-term effects of radiotherapy on bowel function [19]. Future studies should aim to include validated patient-reported outcomes to better guide clinical decisions and refine treatment algorithms.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Only five retrospective studies specifically addressed local excision after regrowth, with substantial heterogeneity in treatments, follow-up protocols, and patient populations. This limits generalizability and precludes strong causal inferences. Most patients were managed in high-volume centers with access to multidisciplinary teams and advanced technologies, which may not reflect everyday clinical practice. Additionally, some patients who underwent local excision for suspected regrowth were ultimately ypT0, meaning no true regrowth was present. While this may overestimate LE use, we included these cases given the diagnostic and therapeutic role of LE in unclear situations, reflecting real-world practice.

Lastly, some studies likely included overlapping patient cohorts. A sensitivity analysis excluding those studies showed consistent results, indicating minimal effect on overall conclusions.

Conclusions

Despite inherent limitations—such as study heterogeneity, retrospective design, and possible patient overlap—this review offers a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence on local excision after suspected tumor regrowth in the watch-and-wait setting, reflecting the novelty and complexity of this emerging therapeutic strategy. While caution is warranted in interpreting oncological outcomes, available evidence supports the feasibility and safety of local excision in selected patients with local regrowth after initial major clinical response, especially when used as a diagnostic–therapeutic tool in uncertain cases.

However, the applicability of these findings outside specialized centers remains uncertain. Future prospective, multicenter studies with standardized protocols and longer follow-up will be crucial to validate these results and guide the integration of local excision into broader clinical practice.
