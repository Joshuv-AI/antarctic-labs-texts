Clinical, Functional, and Quality‐of‐Life Outcomes After Contemporary Transcatheter Edge‐to‐Edge Repair for Primary Mitral Regurgitation in the Very Elderly
Authors: Matthew J. Price, Gagan D. Singh, Jason H. Rogers, Michael J. Rinaldi, Paul Mahoney, Paolo Denti, Wolfgang Rottbauer, Federico M. Asch, Jose L. Zamorano, Janani Aiyer, Kelli Peterman, Francesco Maisano, Ralph Stephan von Bardeleben, Evelio Rodriguez, Saibal Kar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvanced age may influence the benefits derived from transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair for primary mitral regurgitation (MR). We explored the impact of age on clinical, echocardiographic, functional, and patient‐reported outcomes after contemporary mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair.MethodsPatients with primary MR were identified from a pooled, patient‐level analysis of the EXPAND and EXPAND G4 studies. Key outcomes included 30‐day and 1‐year major adverse events, MR severity, New York Heart Association class, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire – Overall Summary score, and mortality.ResultsA total of 854 patients were included. The oldest quartile ranged in age from 86 to 99 years, while the younger quartiles ranged from 21 to 85 years. The 30‐day major adverse events rate was not different in the oldest compared with the younger quartiles (5.7% versus 4.3%, P=0.46). At 1 year, MR ≤1+ was present in 81% of the oldest versus 88% of the younger patients (P=0.08). LV end‐diastolic volumes improved to similar extent (−12.0±28.8 mL, P=0.003 and −14.1±31.5 mL, P<0.0001, P=0.34 across groups). Functional status and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire – Overall Score improved significantly across age groups, without differences according to age. Adjusted 1‐year mortality was higher in the oldest compared with the younger quartiles (adjusted hazard ratio 1.74 [95% CI, 1.02–2.98], P=0.04).ConclusionsAlthough 1‐year mortality was higher among the very elderly, mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair using next‐generation devices was associated with low complication rates and substantial improvements in ventricular remodeling, functional status, and quality of life across age groups. These findings support the use of mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair in appropriately selected elderly patients and suggest that age alone should not be a barrier to treatment.

Transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair (TEER) is an established therapy for patients with severe, symptomatic primary mitral regurgitation (MR) who are at high or prohibitive surgical risk. 1 , 2 , 3 Post‐procedural prognosis is a key component in patient selection, and prior studies of pooled cohorts of primary and secondary MR have identified age as a predictor of mortality and heart failure hospitalization (HFH) after mitral TEER using early‐generation systems. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, improvements in functional status and quality of life as well as avoidance of complications that may limit individual autonomy are often prioritized over hard clinical outcomes in elderly patients.

Advancing age may influence safety, echocardiographic, functional status, and clinical outcomes after mitral TEER for primary MR through several mechanisms. These include greater anatomical complexity because of valve thickening, calcification, or other age‐related degenerative changes that might reduce procedural success; higher rates and consequences of procedure‐related complications; and clinical futility attributable to multiple comorbidities. Newer‐generation mitral TEER devices have demonstrated improved technical outcomes, 8 , 9 even in valvular anatomies previously considered unsuitable. 10 However, it remains unclear whether these high rates of success extend to the very elderly.

Prior studies that combine these patients with primary and secondary MR may obscure the specific impact of age on post‐TEER outcomes, as the underlying pathophysiology and prognoses these 2 entities differ. 4 Furthermore, the effect of mitral TEER on New York Heart Association (NYHA) class and Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire Overall Summary (KCCQ‐OS) score across different age groups has not been well studied for primary MR. Functional class and patient‐reported outcomes such as quality‐of‐life may be particularly meaningful in older populations.

Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the comparative safety and clinical effectiveness of contemporary mitral TEER for primary MR in the very elderly, as well as to explore its impact on echocardiographic outcomes, functional class, and health status, using a pooled, patient‐level analysis of 2 large, prospective registries of latest‐generation MitraClip devices.

Methods

Study Design and Population

The EXPANDed data set and associated analyses and study materials will not be shared without permission from the sponsor. The EXPANDed cohort is a pooled, patient‐level analysis of the EXPAND and EXPAND G4 studies. These studies are global, prospective, multicenter, real‐world, single‐arm post‐market studies initiated to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the 3rd and 4th‐generation MitraClip Systems for the treatment of symptomatic, moderate‐to‐severe and severe primary or secondary MR. 8 , 9 Inclusion and exclusion criteria were minimal to reflect real‐world use of the study devices; all patients met regional indications‐for‐use guidelines and patient selection standards. Clinical and echocardiographic follow‐up occurred at discharge, 30 days, and 1‐year post‐procedure. Patients were enrolled between 2018 to 2022 across 91 centers in the North America, Europe, Japan and the Middle East. All participants provided written informed consent and met eligibility criteria based on the study protocols and local recommendations. The studies were approved by institutional review boards or ethics committees and national competent authorities, as applicable. These studies adhere to good clinical practice standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The studies are registered at ClinicalTrials.gov ([EXPAND]; NCT03502811 and [EXPAND G4]; NCT04177394).

Echocardiographic Assessments

Echocardiograms were assessed by 2 independent echocardiographic core laboratories. MR severity and etiology for all subjects were assessed by the MedStar Health Research Institute (Washington DC, USA) per American Society of Echocardiography guidelines. Mitral valve anatomical characteristics and left ventricular parameters were assessed by Medical Research Development S.L. (Madrid, Spain) and MedStar Health Research Institute for the EXPAND and EXPAND G4 cohorts, respectively. Complex anatomy was defined as the presence of one or more of the following characteristics: primary jet outside the A2‐P2 coaptation zone, more than one significant jet, severely degenerative leaflets or wide flail gap (>10 mm)/width (>15 mm), wide jet, significant cleft or scallop, bileaflet flail or prolapse, mitral valve area <4.0 cm2, calcified landing zone, or minimal leaflet tissue.

Analysis Population

Patients in EXPANDed with primary MR pathology as evaluated by the echocardiographic core laboratory were included in the analysis, while subjects with secondary MR were excluded.

Outcomes

Major adverse events were defined as the composite of death, myocardial infarction, stroke, the need for surgical mitral valve replacement, single leaflet device attachment, and device embolization. These events were adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee. Other key outcome measures included MR severity, NYHA class, KCCQ‐OS score, HFH, and mortality through 1 year.

Statistical Analysis

Patients with primary MR were stratified into 4 quartiles based on age at the time of enrollment. Patients in quartile 1 (Q1), quartile 2 (Q2) and quartile 3 (Q3) were pooled and compared with quartile 4 (Q4); comparisons were also made across all 4 individual quartiles. Categorical variables are reported as counts (percentages), and continuous variables are reported as mean ± SD or median and interquartile range, where appropriate. Categorical variables were compared across pooled quartiles with Chi‐square test or Fisher exact test and across individual quartiles with Cochran‐Armitage Trend test. The Bowker test was used for paired nominal data. Continuous variables were compared across pooled quartiles with t‐tests unless the data weren't normally distributed, in which case the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used. Continuous variables were compared across individual quartiles using linear regression for normally distributed data and Jonckheere‐Terpstra test for data that were not normally distributed. Changes in KCCQ and left ventricular (LV) volumes from baseline to later intervals were performed by paired t‐tests for normally distributed data or signed‐rank tests for non‐parametric data. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to assess mortality and the composite of mortality and HFH rates at 1 year. Comparisons between quartiles and pooled quartiles were made using the log rank method and using Cox proportional hazards models adjusting for sex, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, prior cardiac surgery, and LV ejection fraction (LVEF). A cubic spline was performed to explore non‐linearity between age and mortality. Five knots were placed at percentiles 5, 27.5, 50, 72.5, and 95. All analyses were intention‐to‐treat. A 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A primary mechanism of MR was identified in 854 of the 2205 patients (38.8%) enrolled in EXPANDed. Patients were stratified into 4 age quartiles: Q1 (n=207) included patients younger than 77 years (range: 21–76 years), Q2 (n=208) included those aged 77–81 years; Q3 (n=212) included those aged 82–84 years; and Q4 (n=227) included patients aged 85 years or older (range: 85–99 years). Baseline clinical, echocardiographic, and anatomical characteristics are shown in Table 1. Compared with the pooled younger quartiles (Q1 through Q3), patients in Q4 had a significantly higher Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) predicted risk of mortality, were less likely to have undergone prior cardiac surgery, more frequently had hypertension, had a higher mean LVEF and smaller mean LV volumes. The prevalence of complex mitral valve anatomy was similar between groups (28.8% versus 27.4%, P=0.70), with no significant differences in individual anatomical characteristics, except for bileaflet prolapse or flail, which was less common in Q4 (6.3% versus 10.8%, P=0.05). Procedural Characteristics, Lengths of Stay, and Post‐Procedural Disposition

The number of clips implanted per patient was not significantly different between those in Q4 compared with the pooled younger quartiles (P=0.53); most patients received 1 clip (53.9% Q1 through Q3, 59.5% Q4). The median hospital length‐of‐stay in Q4 was 2 days (interquartile range, 1–5 days) compared with 3 days (interquartile range, 1–6 days) in the pooled younger quartiles (P=0.17). There was no difference in discharge disposition (P=0.48), and 91.9% of patients in Q4 were discharged to home compared with 92.5% of patients in Q1 through Q3. Across individual age quartiles, there were no significant differences in the hospital length‐of‐stay (P=0.09) or discharge disposition (P=0.98). (Table S2).

Safety Outcomes

At 30 days, the rate of major adverse events was 5.7% in Q4 compared with 4.3% in Q1 through Q3 (P=0.46, Table 2). In Q4, there were no myocardial infarctions, 2 patients (0.9%) suffered a stroke, 1 patient (0.4%) required surgical mitral valve replacement, and there was no device embolization. These rates were not significantly different than those observed in the pooled younger quartiles. Single leaflet device attachment occurred in 4 patients (1.8%) in Q4 and in 9 patients (1.4%) in the pooled younger quartiles (P=1.0). No significant differences were observed in the 30‐day major adverse event rate or its components across individual quartiles (P=0.63, Table S3).

Echocardiographic Outcomes

At 1 year, MR ≤1+ was present in 81% of the patients in Q4 compared with 88% of the patients in the pooled younger quartiles (P=0.08, Figure 1). MR ≤2+ was present in 96% in Q4 compared with 97% in the pooled younger quartiles (P=0.91). Paired and unpaired MR severities over follow‐up stratified by individual quartile are shown in Figures S1–S5 and S2, respectively. There were no differences in mitral gradients at 1 year (Q4, 3.4 ± 1.6 mm Hg versus Q1 through Q3, 3.7 ± 1.9 mm Hg, P=0.20). At 1‐year, LV end diastolic volume significantly decreased compared with baseline in Q4 and in the younger 3 quartiles (∆−12.0 ± 28.8 mL, P=0.003 and ∆−14.1 ± 31.5 mL, P<0.0001) to a similar extent (P=0.34). Similarly, LV end diastolic dimension decreased significantly in both groups (Δ‐2.0 ± 0.6 mm, P=0.001 and Δ‐2.0 ± 0.5 mm, P<0.001) with no significant difference between them (P=0.53). There was no significant change in LV end‐systolic volume or LV end‐systolic dimension from baseline to 1 year according to age (Table S4). These results were broadly similar when analyzed across individual quartiles.

Functional and Quality‐of‐Life Outcomes

NYHA class improved significantly from baseline to 1 year in Q4 (NYHA class I/II, 36.0% versus 81.3%, P<0.0001) and in the pooled 3 younger quartiles (NYHA class I/II, 38.1% versus 85.6%, P<0.0001, Figure 2). KCCQ‐OS score increased significantly at 1 year in Q4 and in the pooled younger quartiles (∆+17.7 ± 24.4, P<0.0001; ∆+19.1 ± 23.4, P<0.0001); there was no difference in the magnitude of KCCQ‐OS score improvement between these 2 groups (P=0.76). After 1‐year post‐procedure, 52.9% of patients in Q4 were alive with moderate improvement in quality‐of‐life (Δ KCCQ‐OS ≥10 points), 36.6% were alive with a large improvement in quality‐of‐life (Δ KCCQ‐OS ≥20 points), and 62.2% were alive and well (KCCQ‐OS ≥60 and no decline from baseline >10 points); these were not different compared with the younger patients (Figure 3). Categorical health status outcomes stratified by individual age quartile are shown in Table S5. 6MWD improved significantly from baseline to 1 year in both groups (Q4, Δ 275.4 ± 383.2, P=0.001; Q1‐3, Δ166.9 ± 352.3, P=0.0003) with no differences in the magnitude of 6MWD improvement between the groups (P=0.15).

Clinical Outcomes

At 1 year, the Kaplan–Meier estimated rate of mortality in Q4 was 12.2% compared with 9.9% in the younger quartiles (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.74 [95% CI, 1.02–2.98], P=0.04) and the Kaplan–Meier estimated rate of the composite of mortality or HFH was 21.1% versus 17.5% (adjusted HR, 1.61 [95% CI, 1.08–2.41], P=0.02; Figure 4). Cubic spline analysis evaluating age as a continuous variable demonstrated a significant association between increasing age and all‐cause mortality, with an inflection point observed at approximately 80 years (Figure S3).

Discussion

The eligible population for mitral TEER in the setting of primary MR is generally older given that the procedure is indicated in patients at high or prohibitive risk for cardiac surgery. The median age of patients undergoing mitral TEER for primary MR within the United States is 82 years, 11 and therefore a substantial proportion of procedures are performed on the elderly. Elderly patients with primary MR may not derive the same benefits with mitral TEER as younger patients for several potential reasons, such as an increased risk of procedure‐related adverse events, complex mitral anatomy (eg, annular and/or leaflet calcification) that may reduce procedural success, or a general inability to manifest improvements in functional class or quality of life even after a successful procedure. Newer generation mitral TEER devices, which have been associated with improved MR reduction 8 , 9 as well as procedure success in anatomies originally thought to be unsuitable, 10 have not been well represented in prior, large cohort studies of mitral TEER. 4 , 7 , 11 , 12 Moreover, analyses of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons/American College of Cardiology (STS/ACC) TVT (Transcatheter Valve Therapies) Registry are limited as they did not use echocardiographic core laboratories, and therefore the relationships among age, leaflet anatomy, MR reduction, and ventricular remodeling could not be evaluated. 4 , 7 , 11 , 12 In the current study, we explored the influence of age on clinical, echocardiographic, functional, and patient‐reported outcomes after contemporary mitral TEER for primary MR using a pooled analysis of 2 large, prospective, international multi‐center registries that incorporated independent echocardiographic core laboratories and clinical events committees. We found that although the adjusted risk of mortality at 1‐year was higher among the oldest patients, they derived similar echocardiographic, functional and quality‐of‐life benefits as younger patients without an increased procedural hazard. In specific, mitral TEER in the oldest age quartile (1) was associated with a low and similar rate of 30‐day major adverse events without an increase in leaflet‐related injury or complications; (2) was followed by high rates of post‐procedure discharge to home, consistent with sustained patient autonomy; (3) provided numerically less MR reduction at 1‐year follow‐up but without differences in inflow gradients or ventricular remodeling; and (4) delivered substantial and sustained improvements in functional outcomes and health status, without significant differences across age groups.

The safety of mitral TEER for the treatment of primary MR is well established 11 , 13 and is a key factor contributing to its therapeutic role in patients at high or prohibitive risk for mitral valve surgery. However, whether the safety of mitral TEER is maintained in the very elderly has not been examined in a pure population of patients with primary MR using the latest‐generation MitraClip G3 and G4 systems, which provide multiple clip lengths and widths. This might influence the rates of leaflet injury, single leaflet device attachment or clinically significant inflow gradients in elderly patients in whom leaflet tissue may be more friable, inelastic, or prone to device‐related complications. In this prospective study with an independent clinical events committee, the eldest patients (age range, 86 to 99 years) had low and similar 30‐day rates of major adverse events, death, and leaflet injury compared to relatively younger patients. These findings provide reassurance regarding the safety of the next‐generation MitraClip systems even at the extremes of age.

It is conceivable that age‐related degenerative changes might impact MR reduction. In an analysis of the STS/ACC TVT Registry that included patients with primary MR treated predominantly with early‐generation MitraClip systems, age <80 years was an independent predictor of procedural success (defined as ≤2+ MR without severe stenosis). 11 In the current study, MR reduction to ≤1+ in the oldest age quartile occurred less frequently than in the younger quartiles (88% versus 82%) but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.08). However, our ability to detect a significant difference in MR reduction is limited by our small sample size. The mechanism underlying the potentially inferior MR reduction among the very elderly (ie, the oldest age quartile) is unclear, as the prevalence of complex leaflet anatomy was similar across age quartiles. Post‐procedural gradients in the eldest quartile were similar to those observed in younger patients despite greater residual MR, and this may have limited attempts to further reduce MR with additional devices. Other possible explanations include the presence of complex leaflet characteristics that were not collected by the echocardiographic core laboratory or operator preference/bias (ie, accepting more residual MR in older patients).

Elderly patients may forgo more invasive therapies because of a perceived negative impact on post‐procedural autonomy, functional status and/or quality‐of‐life, which are often prioritized by patients and physicians over hard clinical outcomes. Current guidelines for mitral TEER recommend that patients should only be treated if they have an expected life expectancy of at least 1 year. 3 However, our results underscore the importance of patient‐centered decision‐making for the treatment of primary MR in the very elderly. Patient autonomy appeared to be generally preserved after mitral TEER irrespective of age, as discharge disposition was not significantly different across age quartile, with 92% of the very elderly patients discharged to home. Furthermore, even the oldest patients were able to reap the functional and quality‐of‐life benefits of mitral TEER. Categorical outcomes that incorporate both survival and patient‐reported health‐status may better reflect an acceptable result for a particular patient as they combine both quality and quantity of life benefits (or harms). 14 We observed that 62% of the patients in the eldest quartile were alive and well at 1‐year post‐procedure, which was not different from that observed in the other age groups. Our findings confirm and extend upon an analysis of the TVT registry that included patients with predominantly primary MR (89%) undergoing mitral TEER with early‐generation systems. 7 In that study, there were no statistically significant differences in 1‐year KCCQ‐OS scores or rates of alive and well status according to age. Our finding that LV diastolic dimensions significantly improved after mitral TEER irrespective of age provides a possible mechanistic explanation for this observation. Therefore, when appropriately selected, very elderly patients undergoing mitral TEER for primary MR with next‐generation devices can expect similar discharge disposition, functional improvements, and health status recovery as their younger peers.

Very elderly patients may be denied mitral TEER because of concerns about clinical futility. In the current study, most elderly patients had an increased adjusted risk of 1‐year mortality compared to younger patients. This finding aligns with prior analyses. Increasing age was a significant predictor of post‐TEER mortality among patients with primary and secondary MR in the STS/ACC TVT registry. 4 While a smaller, retrospective, single‐center study of patients undergoing mitral TEER for both primary and secondary MR reported that nonagenarians had similar rates of mortality and HFH than younger patients, 15 age was an independent predictor of mortality over follow‐up in an international registry of mitral TEER, and age ≥75 years is a component of the MitraScore, an algorithm to predict post‐mitral TEER mortality risk. 5 Our finding that post‐procedural major adverse events, functional class, quality‐of‐life, and ventricular remodeling after mitral TEER were similar across age groups suggest that the difference in observed mortality is unlikely to be directly related to the mitral TEER procedure itself. Therefore, the modest absolute increase in mortality should not be a determining factor whether to offer this therapy to elderly patients. Our findings also demonstrate that current society guidelines, which provide a class IIa recommendation for mitral TEER in patients with primary MR at high or prohibitive surgical risk, 3 remain relevant, and appropriate for guiding treatment decisions in older adults.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Selection bias may have influenced outcomes, as physicians may have preferentially selected very elderly patients who were more likely to benefit from mitral TEER. Additionally, follow‐up was limited to 1 year, which may not fully capture the long‐term trajectory of outcomes in this population. Further studies with extended follow‐up are warranted. We did not collect detailed information on frailty, which may strongly influence post‐procedural recovery, health status gains, and mortality.

Conclusions

Although 1‐year mortality was higher among the very elderly, mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair using next‐generation devices was associated with low complication rates and substantial improvements in ventricular remodeling, functional status, and quality of life across age groups. These findings support the use of mitral transcatheter edge‐to‐edge repair in appropriately selected elderly patients and suggest that age alone should not be a barrier to treatment.
