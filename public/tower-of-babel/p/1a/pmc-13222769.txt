Treatment Satisfaction with Tenapanor: Real-World Survey of Patients with End-Stage Renal Disease and Hyperphosphatemia
Authors: Jessica B Kendrick, Luisa L Scott, Belinda Gist, Laura A Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTenapanor (XPHOZAH), approved by the US FDA in October 2023, is indicated to reduce serum phosphorus in adults with chronic kidney disease on dialysis as add-on therapy in patients who have an inadequate response to or are intolerant of any dose of phosphate binders. Unlike phosphate binders, tenapanor inhibits phosphate absorption by reducing permeability through the paracellular pathway. We report results of a real-world survey on patient perspectives of serum phosphate management while receiving tenapanor for hyperphosphatemia.Patients and MethodsSurvey invites were distributed via the ArdelyxAssist patient assistance program and survey responses were coupled to pharmacy dispensing data. Eligible participants were patients with hyperphosphatemia on maintenance dialysis receiving tenapanor for at least 7 weeks, last dispensed no more than 12 weeks before the survey.ResultsAmong 163 respondents (60% male, median age 60), the median duration of tenapanor use was 133 days. Many patients were unaware of their current serum phosphate levels (39%) or changes since starting tenapanor (57%). Excluding these patients, 74% reported good/very good serum phosphate control and 63% noted improved control. Longer duration of tenapanor use correlated with increased awareness of (P under 0.001) and perceived improvement in phosphate control (P under 0.05). Most patients (71%) reported tenapanor was easy to take. Median prescription fill adherence was 94% and stable over time. Fifty-seven percent of patients reported improved outlook on serum phosphate management, citing better bowel movements (49%), improved serum phosphate control (33%), and reduced pill burden (14%). Patient education questions revealed that 58% of patients received information from non-clinical sources and 20% from nephrologists.ConclusionThe survey results suggest that patients experience positive outcomes with real-world use of tenapanor, including improved bowel movements, serum phosphate control, and reduced pill burden. These findings highlight the ability of tenapanor to address unmet needs in hyperphosphatemia management.Keywords: serum phosphate control, patient perspective, pill burden, bowel movements

Introduction

In stage 4 and 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD), reduced kidney function upends the normally tight regulation of phosphate balance and results in hyperphosphatemia (serum phosphate >4.5 mg/dL).1–3 In the United States, approximately 70–75% of patients with CKD on maintenance dialysis develop hyperphosphatemia, equaling over 600,000 patients.4 Hyperphosphatemia leads to comorbidities such as CKD-mineral bone disorder and is associated with cardiovascular morbidity, decreased vascular health, and increased mortality.3,5–7 Hyperphosphatemia may also, in some cases, delay or prevent transplant wait listing.8 Available dialysis techniques and dietary restriction are not sufficient strategies to manage hyperphosphatemia in CKD.9 Phosphate binders are the mainstay for controlling serum phosphate, but are associated with a high pill burden, adverse gastrointestinal (GI) effects, and poor adherence. Many patients receiving phosphate binders still have elevated serum phosphate.9–11 Data from the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study indicate that, in the most recent month, 41% of patients receiving phosphate binders had serum levels exceeding 5.5 mg/dL, while 72% had levels above 4.5 mg/dL.12 Moreover, 93% were unable to maintain serum phosphate levels below 4.5 mg/dL over a 6-month period.12

Tenapanor (XPHOZAH) was approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration in 2023 to reduce serum phosphorus in adults with CKD on dialysis as add-on therapy in patients who have an inadequate response to phosphate binders or who are intolerant of any dose of phosphate binder therapy.13 Tenapanor reduces intestinal phosphate absorption by reducing permeability through the paracellular pathway, a mechanism separate from that of phosphate binders.1,14,15 In clinical trials, tenapanor has been shown to be safe and efficacious in lowering serum phosphate.16–21 However, there is a lack of real-world data on the experience of patients taking tenapanor for management of hyperphosphatemia.

This real-world study was based on a survey of patients in the ArdelyxAssist program, which was created to improve access for patients. The goal of this study was to better understand patient-reported outcomes and experiences with tenapanor for the treatment of hyperphosphatemia in CKD, as well as sources of education about serum phosphate management.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Data Collection

This real-world study was based on a patient survey that was conducted between October 22, 2024, and November 16, 2024. The survey was designed by a committee with expertise in nephrology and patient-reported outcomes (Supplemental Method 1). Survey invites were sent via text message to eligible individuals in the ArdelyxAssist program on October 22, October 30, and November 7, and data collection closed on November 16 (Supplemental Method 2). Invites and surveys were sent in English or Spanish, according to the individual’s preferred language. Participants had 1 week to complete the survey once they clicked on the link provided in the invite (Supplemental Method 3). Only 1 response could be selected per question. Participants could skip questions but were unable to go back to previous questions. Partial responses were included in the analysis.

Pharmacy data, sourced from the specialty pharmacy network, were collected from November 6, 2023, to October 22, 2024, and analyzed retrospectively. Some patients were receiving tenapanor through the Ardelyx patient assistance program (PAP) while others were receiving tenapanor through their insurance (non-PAP). Tenapanor dispense data, prescriber state, and PAP status were available for all patients. Age and sex were available only for PAP patients. Duration of use was defined as the days from first dispense to survey start. Days dispensed was the summation of quantity dispensed across all dispenses divided by 2 (ie, 2 pills per day) Prescription fill adherence, defined as the percentage of days for which the patient had medication available, was calculated as the prorated days dispensed divided by the duration of use, multiplied by 100.

Survey data were merged with pharmacy data using an external reference number that was added to the survey response data at collection. Each participant’s external reference number matched a randomly assigned ArdelyxAssist case ID number in the Ardelyx data warehouse. A single table of de-identified data (flat file) was generated to serve as a list for all outcomes data.

Patients

Eligible patients were required to have consented to join the ArdelyxAssist program and receive text messages. Patients were adults (aged ≥18 years) with end-stage renal disease who were prescribed and dispensed tenapanor for the treatment of hyperphosphatemia. Patients must have initiated tenapanor ≥7 weeks before the survey (first dispense on or before September 1, 2024) and received their most recent dispense in the last 12 weeks (on or after July 30, 2024).

Statistical Analyses

Missing data were tallied. No imputation or other methods were used to handle missing data. Data were analyzed descriptively, and the following inferential analyses were performed, with alpha set at 0.05:

To determine if patients reported improved serum phosphate control with longer durations of use, ordered logistic regression analysis was used after first removing “I don’t know” responses.

Results

Patients

Survey invites were sent to 2904 individuals who met the inclusion criteria (1841 PAP patients and 1063 non-PAP patients). The full analysis set comprised of 163 responders who consented to study participation and answered ≥1 survey question. The overall response rate was 5.6%, with response rates of 6.3% among PAP patients and 4.4% among non-PAP patients (Figure S1). Out of 2787 individuals who received English language invites, 161 responded, and out of 117 who received Spanish invites, 2 responded. There was broad geographic representation across 43 states. In the PAP patient set (N = 116; 71%), the mean (SD) age was 60 (13) years and 60% were male. In the full analysis set, the median (min, max) duration of tenapanor use was 133 (32, 164) days, and the median (min, max) fill adherence was 94% (54, 329) (Table 1).

Phosphate Control

Many patients reported a lack of understanding of their phosphate control, as indicated by the most common response, “I don’t know,” when asked to describe their current phosphate control with tenapanor (n = 63/161, 39%; Figure 1A) or how their phosphate levels changed since starting tenapanor (n = 79/138, 57%; Figure 1B). However, when respondents who answered “I don’t know” were excluded, remaining patients expressed positive feedback regarding their phosphate control with tenapanor: 73% (n = 72/98) rated their current phosphate control as good or very good (Figure 1A) and 63% (n = 37/59) reported that their phosphate levels were a little or much better since starting tenapanor (Figure 1B).

Patient-reported serum phosphate control. (A) Current phosphate control with tenapanor (n = 161) and (B) changes in phosphate levels since initiating tenapanor (n = 138). (C) Reported change in serum phosphate level since initiating tenapanor by duration of tenapanor use.

Abbreviation: OR, odds ratio.

To understand how patient perceptions change over time, the reported change in phosphate control was plotted against the duration of tenapanor use, derived from pharmacy data (Figure 1C). With longer durations of use, patients were more likely to report positive changes in serum phosphate control and less likely to report that they did not know. Based on logistic regression analysis, for each additional month of use, the odds of moving to a higher serum phosphate response category (ie, moving from “a little” to “much” better) increased by ~21.1% (OR, 1.211; 95% CI: 1.023 to 1.434; P = 0.026) and the odds of responding “I don’t know” decreased by ~23.8% (OR, 0.762; 95% CI: 0.657 to 0.883; P < 0.001).

Treatment Outlook

Most patients (n = 58/101, 57%) reported that their outlook on phosphate control was a little or much better since starting tenapanor (Figure 2A). These patients were asked to select the best reason for their improved outlook and 49% (n = 25/51) selected improved bowel movements, 33% (n = 17/51) better phosphate control, 14% (n = 7/51) lower pill burden, and 4% (n = 2/51) “other” (Figure 2A). Of the patients who reported a change in serum phosphate levels, 69% (37/54) indicated that their outlook on serum phosphate control was a little or much better (Figure 2B); 44% of those patients attributed the improved outlook to better phosphorus control, 39% to improved bowel movements, and 14% to reduced pill burden (Figure 2B). Of the patients that responded “I don’t know” to their change in phosphate control with tenapanor (Figure 1B), 45% (n = 21/47) reported an improved outlook (Figure 2B); 73% of those patients attributed the improved outlook to improved bowel movements and 13% to reduced pill burden (Figure 2B).

Tenapanor Use

Of the survey respondents, 71% (n = 69/97) said that tenapanor was easy or very easy to remember to take as prescribed (Figure 3A). Consistent with these responses, pharmacy data showed high fill adherence (median, 94%) for the full analysis set (Table 1). Prescription fill adherence was similar between PAP and non-PAP patients (mean, 90% and 86%, respectively; Figure 3B). Prescription fill adherence greater than 100% may occur when patients refill their prescriptions earlier than scheduled. Fill adherence remained high even with increasing duration of use (Figure 3C).

Patient Education

Patients were also asked which type of healthcare staff provides the most education about serum phosphate control. Twenty percent of patients selected kidney doctor, 10% selected nurse practitioner, 7% selected dietitian at dialysis facility, and 5% selected nurse at dialysis facility; however, most (58%) selected “other” (Figure 4A). When subsequently asked about the utility of the information they receive about phosphate levels from their kidney care team, the results were split, with 54% of patients reporting that it was fairly or very useful and 26% reporting that it was not useful at all (Figure 4B). Among patients who selected nephrologists as the primary source of information, 73% (n = 11/15) found the information fairly or very useful and 20% (n = 3/15) found it not useful (Figure 4C). For patients who selected “other” as the primary source of information, 42% (n = 16/38) found the information fairly or very useful and 34% (n = 13/38) found it not useful (Figure 4C).

Responses from PAP Patients versus Non-PAP Patients

When comparing responses between PAP and non-PAP patients, there was no consistent pattern indicating that PAP participation was associated with systemically more positive or negative responses. Among the patients who knew their serum phosphate levels, a numerically higher proportion of non-PAP patients reported very good or good control (79% vs 71%; Figure S2A) and a little or much better outlook on their serum phosphate control (63% vs 55%; Figure S3). Conversely, a numerically higher proportion of PAP patients, who knew their serum phosphate levels, reported a little or much lower serum phosphate levels since starting XPHOZAH (67% vs 53%; Figure S2B) and that tenapanor was easy or very easy to remember to take (73% vs 68% non-PAP patients, Figure S4). These differences were descriptive and did not suggest a directional effect.

Discussion

In this real-world survey, patients with end-stage renal disease taking tenapanor for management of hyperphosphatemia reported satisfaction with the drug. Although limited, the available patient demographics are consistent with the patient population in tenapanor randomized controlled trials among patients with CKD.16–21 Among patients who knew their phosphate levels, nearly three-quarters rated their serum phosphate control as good and two-thirds said their serum phosphate levels were better after tenapanor initiation. Over 70% of patients reported that tenapanor was easy to use and pharmacy data demonstrated high prescription fill adherence, even over long durations of use. Additionally, over half of participants expressed an improved outlook on serum phosphate management since initiating tenapanor even though many of these patients did not know their phosphate levels.

Diarrhea was the most common adverse event, reported by 43–53% of patients during the clinical trials of tenapanor.13 Notably, in this study, improved bowel movements were the main driver of improved outlook with tenapanor for over half of all respondents and for nearly three-fourths of respondents who did not know their phosphate levels. Constipation is reported among 14–72% of patients undergoing dialysis.22 This condition has been shown to impact health-related quality of life across all dialysis modalities negatively.23 In patients receiving peritoneal dialysis, constipation is further associated with an increased risk of peritonitis and reduced dialysis efficacy, often due to catheter malposition or dysfunction.24 To manage the constipation, many patients rely on stool softeners and laxatives. Reported improvement in bowel movements, likely related to reduced constipation, is in line with the mechanism of action of tenapanor, which leads to retention of water in the intestinal lumen causing accelerated intestinal transit and softer stools.15,25–28 The survey responses highlight the importance of this aspect of tenapanor to the patient experience.

In addition to improved bowel movements, about 14% of patients reported lower pill burden as the top reason for their improved outlook. The use of phosphate binders requires patients to ingest a substantial number of large pills with every meal, which is both unpleasant and logistically difficult, particularly when coupled with daily fluid intake restrictions.29 Phosphate binders can increase the total daily pill burden by approximately 50% for patients receiving dialysis, thereby negatively impacting the quality of life in these patients.11,29 For the binders to work efficiently, the timing of pill ingestion is critical, and patients may forget to take the pills if they are away from home and at work or a restaurant.29 Compared with phosphate binders, tenapanor requires fewer pills taken fewer times a day and does not need to be taken with every meal.1 Differences in pill burden arise from their distinct mechanisms of action. Phosphate binders reduce phosphate absorption by binding dietary phosphate within the GI tract, which requires multiple pills with meals, whereas tenapanor inhibits phosphate absorption via the paracellular pathway, which may reduce pill burden (Figure 5). A reduction in pill burden in patients with CKD treated with tenapanor and phosphate binders has been demonstrated in two clinical studies. In NORMALIZE, an 18-month open-label extension study of the Phase 3 PHREEDOM study, the median number of phosphate binder pills decreased from 8.5 to 6 pills per day in patients receiving tenapanor with the addition of sevelamer carbonate when serum phosphate was > 4.5 mg/dL.20 Similarly, in OPTIMIZE, a 26-week open label post-registrational study, the median daily pill number decreased from 9 to 4 pills per day in patients who discontinued phosphate binders and initiated tenapanor, and 9 to 6 pills per day in patients who reduced their phosphate binder dose by ≥50% and added tenapanor.21 These reductions correspond to decreases of approximately 16–44% in daily pill burden,20,21 which supports the reported lower pill burden as the top reason for improved treatment outlook. Information about medication history with phosphate binders before and after initiating tenapanor was not collected in this survey. However, based on the label, most patients would likely have received phosphate binders prior to tenapanor treatment and would have continued or reduced phosphate binder use after initiating tenapanor.

Comparative mechanisms of action of (A) phosphate binders and (B) tenapanor. Phosphate binders act within the gastrointestinal lumen by binding dietary phosphate, whereas tenapanor inhibits phosphate absorption by blocking the paracellular pathway of phosphate absorption.

The results of this patient survey highlight an important and overlooked gap in patient awareness and patient education regarding serum phosphate control in CKD. Notably, most patients were unaware of their current phosphate levels or how their phosphate levels changed since starting tenapanor. Furthermore, 20% of patients reported that the information they received regarding their phosphate levels was not useful. Together, these findings suggest that patient understanding of phosphate management remains limited, underscoring a disconnect between treatment and patient engagement. Although patient understanding appeared to improve with longer duration of tenapanor use, patients treated with tenapanor for more than 6 months were less likely to report they did not know their change in phosphate levels and more likely to report improvements in phosphate levels, overall awareness was poor.

Importantly, these findings also point to gaps in education across the multidisciplinary care team. Fifty-eight percent of patients reported receiving educational materials from sources other than physicians, dietitians, nurses, or nurse practitioners. Other sources of information, such as technicians, were not included in the response choices. As the survey did not allow for open text responses, it was not possible to gain further insight into any other sources of information. Future studies can be designed more specifically to address this question.

Furthermore, the perceived usefulness of information from healthcare staff about phosphate levels was variable, while approximately half of patients found the information fairly or very useful, about one-fourth of patients found it not useful. When the source of the information was a nephrologist, the proportion of patients who found the information fairly or very useful increased to about two-thirds and the proportion of patients who found the information not useful decreased to about one-fifth. Information provided by nephrologists was rated as more useful when compared with other sources, but only approximately 20% of patients identified their nephrologists as the primary source of information. Overall, these results highlight a need for more consistent patient-centered educational practices across nephrologists, dialysis staff, and dieticians to improve patient understanding of phosphate management. It may be timely to explore innovative ways of providing educational materials to medically complex patients with the use of technology-driven tools, practical and tailored education, visual aids, and possibly interactive games to improve patient engagement, support adherence, and ultimately contribute to better clinical outcomes.

A limitation of this study is that patients lacked knowledge about their phosphate control, reducing the number of patients that could report on their phosphate management. Additionally, bias may have been introduced in responses from PAP patients, who receive assistance from the Ardelyx Patient Assistance Program that was not available to non-PAP patients. However, among patients invited to participate in the survey, only 6.3% of PAP patients and 4.4% of non-PAP patients responded. Thus, willingness to participate may represent a greater source of bias than receipt of assistance from the program. Furthermore, this study did not capture sufficiently granular, patient-level data to assess the relationship between GI effects, such as diarrhea, which is associated with the mechanism of action of tenapanor, and patient-reported improved treatment outlook. Hence, it was not possible to determine whether patients reporting improved treatment outlook were those who successfully managed the GI effects, or whether those who did not report an improved outlook were experiencing these GI effects. Future studies incorporating GI tolerability and patient experience measures are necessary to better clarify the relationship between GI side effects and treatment satisfaction. Additional limitations of this study include the low response rate and the collection of minimal patient characteristics. Specifically, data on dialysis modality and concomitant use of phosphate binders were not captured, which limited the ability to evaluate dialysis modality-specific and phosphate binder-specific differences in patient experience. Other limitations include the small number of questions, potential for recall bias, and the inability of patients to write in answers.

No consistent differences were observed between PAP and non-PAP patients, suggesting there was no potential influence of financial support in the patient assistance program on patient-reported satisfaction and adherence. However, reducing out-of-pocket costs may make treatment feel less burdensome and improve how patients feel about it, regardless of how well it works clinically. As this analysis was descriptive and not adjusted for potential confounders, further research would be needed to better understand the relationship between financial assistance, treatment satisfaction, and adherence.

Overall, the results of the survey suggest that many patients on maintenance dialysis are experiencing positive outcomes with the use of tenapanor in the real world, including improved bowel movements, improved serum phosphate control, and lower pill burden. The data highlight the ability of tenapanor to address the continued unmet need surrounding hyperphosphatemia management in patients on maintenance dialysis that are not controlled on phosphate binders alone.
