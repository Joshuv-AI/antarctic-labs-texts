The prognostic value analysis of PIV, PLR, LMR, NPR, and NLR in hilar cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Hongyang Guo, Xueyan Bai, Jin Shi, Zhen Liu, Zhaoyang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHilar cholangiocarcinoma (HCCA) has a high propensity for early recurrence. We evaluated the prognostic value of preoperative inflammatory biomarkers, Pan‑Immunological Value (PIV), Platelet‑Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), Lymphocyte‑Monocyte Ratio (LMR), Neutrophil‑Platelet Ratio (NPR), and Neutrophil‑Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR), in resected HCCA.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included 373 HCCA patients undergoing radical resection from January 2017 to May 2022. Patients treated between January 2017 and December 2020 were assigned to the training set (n = 236) for model development. Those treated between January 2021 and May 2022 were assigned to the independent temporal validation set (n = 137), this split approximates a 6:4 ratio. Baseline characteristics were comparable between sets (all P > 0.05). Patients were categorized based on one-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) into early and non-early recurrence groups. Clinical, pathological, and preoperative inflammatory marker data were collected. Statistical analyses included univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression, Kaplan-Meier curves, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to assess the prognostic value of the inflammatory markers.ResultsIn univariate analysis, higher PIV, PLR, NLR, NPR and lower LMR were associated with early recurrence (all P < 0.05). To avoid statistical collinearity, separate multivariable Cox models were constructed, each adjusting for clinicopathological factors but including only one inflammatory marker. In these models, PLR (HR = 1.026, P = 0.026), NLR (HR = 1.523, P = 0.011), NPR (HR = 1.831, P = 0.010), and LMR (HR = 0.483, P = 0.001) remained independent predictors of early recurrence; PIV did not (HR = 1.001, P = 0.425). Kaplan‑Meier curves confirmed shorter RFS for patients with high PIV, PLR, NLR, NPR or low LMR (all log‑rank P < 0.001). The combined model integrating all five markers achieved AUCs of 0.857 (training) and 0.908 (validation), outperforming individual markers (AUCs 0.616–0.637).ConclusionThe combined predictive model integrating inflammatory markers PIV, PLR, NLR, NPR, and LMR shows improved prognostic accuracy in HCCA, potentially providing clinically actionable stratification alongside traditional clinical parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-15812-x.Keywords: Cholangiocarcinoma, Inflammatory biomarkers, Prognosis, Radical resection, Pan-Immunological Value, Predictive model

Introduction

Cholangiocarcinoma, a malignant tumor originating from the bile duct epithelium, accounts for approximately 3% of all gastrointestinal cancers and presents formidable challenges in terms of diagnosis and treatment [1, 2]. Its insidious onset and aggressive nature often result in late-stage diagnosis, contributing to poor survival rates [3, 4]. Among the subtypes of cholangiocarcinoma, hilar cholangiocarcinoma (HCCA) is the predominant form, characterized by its location at the confluence of the right and left hepatic bile ducts [5, 6]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and perioperative care, radical surgical resection remains the only potential curative approach [7], yet many patients experience recurrence and a early recurrence postoperatively.

Understanding prognostic factors in HCCA is imperative for improving patient stratification and tailoring individualized treatment plans. Traditionally, the assessment of prognosis has relied heavily on clinical parameters and pathological staging systems. However, these conventional indicators often fail to fully capture the complexity of tumor biology and the tumor-host interactions, prompting the exploration of novel prognostic biomarkers. In recent years, the role of inflammation in cancer progression has garnered significant interest, with numerous studies highlighting the prognostic value of inflammatory markers across various malignancies [8, 9]. Inflammation has been implicated in various processes integral to cancer development, including tumor initiation, promotion, and metastasis [10].

Inflammatory markers, easily obtainable through routine blood tests, present a minimally invasive method for prognostic evaluation. Among these markers, this study focuses on the Pan-Immunological Value (PIV), Platelet-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR), Lymphocyte-Monocyte Ratio (LMR), Neutrophil-Platelet Ratio (NPR), and Neutrophil-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) have emerged as potential indicators of systemic inflammation and immune status in cancer patients [11, 12]. Our focus is on PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR because: PIV, calculated using neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts, reflects the cumulative pro-inflammatory and thrombogenic profile of a patient [13]. Elevated levels of PLR and NPR suggest increased platelet activity, which is known to facilitate tumor cell dissemination and immune evasion, while a high NLR indicates a shift towards a more suppressive immune environment that favors tumor growth [14]. Conversely, a high LMR may signal a robust anti-tumor immune response, given the critical role of lymphocytes in orchestrating anti-cancer immunity [15].

Despite these advances, the prognostic utility of combinatorial inflammatory markers (PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, NPR) in HCCA—a malignancy characterized by unique anatomical constraints, complex tumor-stromal interactions, and limited therapeutic options—remains largely unexplored. While systemic inflammation is implicated in gastrointestinal malignancies, HCCA’s propensity for perineural invasion and early lymphovascular spread suggest distinct inflammatory dynamics. No prior study has comprehensively evaluated this five-marker panel or developed an integrated prognostic model for resected HCCA, representing a critical knowledge gap.

This study seeks to fill this gap by evaluating the prognostic value of a panel of accessible inflammatory markers (PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, NPR) in resected HCCA. Our aim is to assess whether these markers, individually or in combination, hold prognostic value and whether they can provide supplementary information to traditional clinicopathological staging systems, potentially aiding in more nuanced post-operative risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study, reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [16], included 373 consecutive HCCA patients undergoing radical resection at our hospital from January 2017 to May 2022. To ensure a robust temporal validation, the cohort was divided chronologically into a training set and a validation set. Patients treated between January 2017 and December 2020 were assigned to the training set (n = 236) for model development. Those treated between January 2021 and May 2022 were assigned to the independent temporal validation set (n = 137), this split approximates a 6:4 ratio and is designed to test the model’s performance on future patients. Demographic information for the patients, including general data, inflammatory markers, imaging results, and perioperative parameters, was systematically collected. This study received approval from the hospital’s Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee, adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria, and grouping standards

Inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Patients aged between 30 and 80 years; (2) Completion of requisite preoperative imaging and diagnostic tests, including abdominal enhanced CT, MRI, MRCP, PET-CT, and tumor markers, confirming a preliminary diagnosis of HCCA and ruling out distant metastasis (M1); (3) Successful radical resection (R0 resection) with postoperative pathology confirming HCCA, clear margins, and the absence of residual cancer; (4) No history of tumor treatment other than surgical resection either before or during the surgery; (5) Patients were in generally good health, with no serious systemic diseases, major organ dysfunctions, or concurrent malignant tumors; (6) A blood routine test conducted within one week prior to surgery at our hospital; (7) Availability of complete clinical and postoperative follow-up data; (8) No postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy was administered.

Exclusion criteria for this study included the following: (1) Patients who received neoadjuvant or bridging therapy prior to surgery; (2) Patients with preoperative acute or chronic inflammatory diseases (including active cholangitis), clinical signs of infection, or fever; additionally, patients who underwent preoperative biliary drainage within 4 weeks prior to blood sampling were excluded to minimize the impact of drainage-related inflammation on biomarker levels; (3) Patients who were taking medications such as aspirin or clopidogrel, which affect peripheral blood cell indices, before surgery; (4) Patients diagnosed with hematologic disorders or immunological diseases; (5) Patients who underwent only palliative surgery or had confirmed distant metastases; (6) Patients with incomplete clinical and pathological data, or those who were lost to follow-up.

Patients were randomized into training and validation sets at an approximately 6:4 ratio, ensuring comparable distributions of age, gender, and tumor stage (all P > 0.05, Table S1). The primary endpoint of this study was recurrence-free survival (RFS). Patients were stratified into two prognostic groups based on RFS at one year post-surgery. This cut-off was selected because early recurrence (≤ 1 year) is a strong predictor of aggressive tumor biology and poor long-term survival in cholangiocarcinoma [17]. The same grouping criteria were applied to both the training set (n = 236) and the validation set (n = 137). In the training set, patients without recurrence or death within one year were classified as having a non-early recurrence (n = 62), while those with recurrence or death from any cause within one year were classified as having a early recurrence (n = 174). In the validation set (n = 137), which adhered to identical inclusion and exclusion criteria, 51 patients were classified as having a non-early recurrence and 86 patients were classified as having a early recurrence.

Data collection

General Patient Data: The study collected data on age, body mass index (BMI), gender, educational level, employment status, marital status, residence, smoking history, drinking history, presence of hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, as well as T and N cancer stages, and Bismuth-Corlette classification. Preoperative Inflammatory Markers: The markers included PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR. For blood testing, a 5 ml sample of venous blood was drawn from fasting patients before 8 a.m. Neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and monocytes were measured with the DxH800 blood analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Preoperative Imaging Data: Tumor characteristics, such as number, maximum diameter, and degree of differentiation, along with invasions into lymph nodes, liver parenchyma, vessels, and nerves, were assessed. Imaging data were obtained in priority order: abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and ultrasound.

Perioperative Parameters: Parameters included the surgical approach, intraoperative conditions such as lymph node dissection, intraoperative blood transfusion, and blood loss, as well as the duration of the postoperative hospital stay.

Follow-up methods

This study primarily conducted follow-ups through outpatient clinic visits, scheduled every three months. For patients who did not attend these planned check-ups, follow-up was conducted via phone calls to their families at designated times. The follow-up period extends until May 2024. RFS was defined as the time from surgery to the first radiological or pathological confirmation of disease recurrence, or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Follow-up data included the patient’s recurrence and survival status. For deceased patients, the cause and date of death were meticulously recorded.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 29.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data were expressed as [n (%)]. The chi-square test was applied when the sample size was ≥ 40 and the theoretical frequency (T) was ≥ 5, with the chi-square statistic represented as χ². If the sample size was ≥ 40 but the theoretical frequency was within 1 ≤ T < 5, a correction was applied to the chi-square test. For sample sizes < 40 or theoretical frequencies T < 1, Fisher’s exact test was used for statistical analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was utilized to assess the normality of continuous variables. Univariate Cox regression was performed for all variables. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis, including inflammatory markers and clinicopathological factors, were considered for multivariable analysis. To avoid statistical collinearity arising from the mathematical interdependence of the inflammatory indices (all derived from neutrophil, lymphocyte, monocyte, and platelet counts), five separate multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed. Each model included the same set of significant clinicopathological covariates but only one inflammatory marker (PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, or NPR). This approach was designed to evaluate whether each inflammatory marker retained independent prognostic significance after adjustment for clinicopathological factors. Multicollinearity within each model was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF); all variables had VIF < 10. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to visualize RFS, with patients censored at the date of last follow-up if no event (recurrence or death) had occurred. Log-rank tests compared groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluated predictive performance. The optimal cut-off values for inflammatory markers were determined using Youden’s index to maximize sensitivity and specificity. For continuous data that were normally distributed, results were presented as (), and a t-test was used for comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Differences analysis of general data

In this study investigating the prognostic significance of inflammatory markers in cholangiocarcinoma, a detailed analysis of the general data between patients with non-early recurrence (n = 62) and early recurrence (n = 174) revealed several significant findings (Table 1). Age, BMI, gender, educational level, employment status, marital status, residence, smoking history, drinking history, hypertension, diabetes, and coronary heart disease showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups (P > 0.05). However, significant disparities were observed in the T stage distribution (χ²=18.738, P < 0.001), where patients with a non-early recurrence were more likely to have T1 and T2 stages, whereas those with a early recurrence were disproportionately represented in T3 and T4 stages. The Bismuth-Corlette classification also showed significant variation (χ²=14.559, P = 0.002), with a higher occurrence of Type I and II classifications in the non-early recurrence group and Types III and IV being more prevalent in the early recurrence group.

Baseline inflammatory markers

Patients with a non-early recurrence exhibited lower PIV (283.11 ± 137.62) compared to those with early recurrence (357.13 ± 146.77), with a significant difference (t = 3.465, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). Similarly, the PLR was significantly lower in the non-early recurrence group (109.76 ± 20.34) than in the early recurrence group (118.88 ± 21.47) (t = 2.909, P = 0.004) (Fig. 1B). The LMR was higher in patients with non-early recurrence (2.27 ± 0.86) compared to those with early recurrence (1.85 ± 0.79), revealing a significant association (t = 3.504, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Additionally, the NLR differed significantly between the two groups, with values of 2.26 ± 1.12 for non-early recurrence and 2.82 ± 1.21 for early recurrence (t = 3.188, P = 0.002) (Fig. 1D). Lastly, the NPR was lower in the non-early recurrence group (1.89 ± 0.63) compared to the early recurrence group (2.19 ± 0.78), also showing statistical significance (t = 2.672, P = 0.008) (Fig. 1E). These findings suggest that these inflammatory markers may serve as important prognostic indicators in cholangiocarcinoma.

Multivariable cox regression analysis

To circumvent the inherent mathematical collinearity among the five inflammatory indices derived from the same blood cell components, five separate multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were constructed. Each model adjusted for significant clinicopathological factors (T stage, N stage, Bismuth-Corlette classification, tumor number, tumor size, differentiation, and lymphovascular invasion) but included only one inflammatory marker (Table 4). In the model containing PIV, this marker was not an independent predictor of RFS (HR = 1.001, 95% CI 0.998–1.004, P = 0.425). Conversely, in their respective models, PLR (HR = 1.026, 95% CI 1.003–1.049, P = 0.026), NLR (HR = 1.523, 95% CI 1.103–2.104, P = 0.011), and NPR (HR = 1.831, 95% CI 1.152–2.912, P = 0.010) remained significantly associated with shorter RFS. A higher LMR was independently associated with longer RFS (HR = 0.483, 95% CI 0.313–0.745, P = 0.001). Across all five models, advanced T stage (T3/T4 vs. T1/T2), lymph node metastasis (N + vs. N0), and poor tumor differentiation consistently emerged as strong, independent predictors of early recurrence (all P < 0.05).

Prognostic value and predictive performance of PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR in cholangiocarcinoma

This study analyzed the predictive values of PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR for the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma. The AUC values were 0.637, 0.616, 0.637, 0.626, and 0.625, respectively (Fig. 2). Cutoff values determined by ROC curve for patients with high PIV 313.88, PLR 114.83, NLR 3.30, NPR 2.13, and low LMR 2.73 were significantly associated with early recurrence disease recurrence or death from any cause. The Kaplan-Meier curves validated the prognostic significance of inflammatory markers, showing that higher levels of PIV, PLR, NLR, and NPR, as well as lower LMR, were strongly associated with a shorter RFS (all Log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 3). These results indicate that the predictive values of PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR for the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma are suboptimal, and further exploration is needed to identify better tools for predicting the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma.

Combined predictive model

In the training set, a combined predictive model was developed by integrating the aforementioned indicators: PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR, to forecast the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma. This model demonstrated an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.857 (Fig. 4), indicating its efficacy in predicting the prognosis of cholangiocarcinoma.

Differences analysis of clinical and pathological data between the two groups of patients in the validation set

Patients with a non-early recurrence were more frequently in early T stages (T1 43.14% and T2 29.41%) compared to the early recurrence group, which had higher proportions in advanced T stages (T3 39.53% and T4 18.60%) (χ²=13.919, P = 0.003) (Table 5). The Bismuth-Corlette classification also revealed significant differences, with Type I and II more common in the non-early recurrence group, whereas Types III and IV were prevalent among those with early recurrence (χ²=8.407, P = 0.038). Inflammatory markers, including PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR, were significantly different between the groups, with poorer prognoses linked to higher PIV (P = 0.002), PLR (P = 0.005), NLR (P = 0.002), NPR (P = 0.003) and lower LMR values (P = 0.006). Furthermore, compared to those with a non-early recurrence, patients in the early recurrence group had a higher incidence of multiple tumors (χ²=9.384, P = 0.002), larger tumor diameters (χ²=7.023, P = 0.008), and poorly differentiated tumors (χ²=6.979, P = 0.031). Lymph node metastasis, liver invasion, vascular invasion, and neural invasion were more frequent in the early recurrence group (P = 0.011, P = 0.035, P = 0.002, and P = 0.009, respectively). In contrast, age, BMI, gender, educational level, employment status, marital status, residence, smoking history, drinking history, hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, surgical approach, lymph node dissection, intraoperative blood transfusion, intraoperative blood loss, and length of hospital stay did not show significant differences between the prognosis groups (all P > 0.05). These findings underscore the importance of specific clinical and pathological factors in influencing prognosis in cholangiocarcinoma.

Discussion

In the present study, we retrospectively evaluated the prognostic value of various inflammatory markers—PIV, PLR, LMR, NLR, and NPR—in patients with HCCA undergoing radical resection. Univariate analyses confirmed significant associations between higher levels of PIV, PLR, NLR, NPR, lower LMR, and reduced RFS. More importantly, when evaluated in separate multivariable models—each constructed to include key clinicopathological covariates but only one inflammatory marker to avoid collinearity—PLR, NLR, NPR, and LMR remained independent predictors of early recurrence, whereas PIV did not. The combined model integrating these informative markers demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to individual markers, suggesting its potential for clinically actionable risk stratification.

These findings align with established evidence that systemic inflammation promotes tumor progression through mechanisms such as neutrophil-mediated matrix degradation, platelet-driven immune evasion, and lymphocyte suppression [18–20]. Moreover, recent evidence highlights the interplay between systemic inflammation and nutritional status in gastrointestinal cancers. For instance, integrated inflammation-nutrition indices have shown prognostic value, underscoring how inflammatory markers may reflect not only immune dysregulation but also a catabolic state that favors tumor progression and recurrence [21]. This supports the biological plausibility of our model, where markers like NLR and PLR may capture both inflammatory and nutritional deterioration, thereby offering a more holistic view of the host–tumor interface. Elevated levels of neutrophils and platelets can foster tumor growth and metastasis; neutrophils contribute via secretion of matrix-degrading enzymes and pro-angiogenic factors [22–24], while platelets may facilitate immune evasion by shielding circulating tumor cells and aiding their metastatic dissemination [25–27], potentially explaining the poor outcomes associated with high PLR and NPR in our cohort.

Conversely, the negative correlation between LMR and early recurrence suggests that a relative increase in lymphocytes and a corresponding decrease in monocytes may play a protective role against tumor progression. Lymphocytes were central to anti-tumor immunity, contributing to the immune system’s ability to identify and destroy malignant cells [28, 29]. In contrast, monocytes and their derivative macrophages can support tumor development and immune evasion, particularly within the tumor microenvironment [30]. A higher LMR may reflect a more robust immune response, thereby hindering tumor progression and leading to better clinical outcomes.

These relationships highlight the dual roles of the immune system in cancer, offering both tumor-promoting and tumor-controlling effects that can shape clinical trajectories. The core finding of our study is that specific inflammatory markers (PLR, NLR, NPR, LMR) provide prognostic information incremental to established clinicopathological factors such as T stage, N stage, and tumor differentiation, as evidenced by their retained independence in the respective multivariable models adjusted for these factors. This suggests that the systemic inflammatory landscape captured by these indices reflects biological aggressiveness not fully captured by anatomy-based staging alone. Therefore, the combined inflammatory model does not replace but rather complements traditional systems like the TNM stage and Bismuth-Corlette classification. Its incremental clinical value lies in refining risk stratification within conventional stage categories. In clinical practice, patients identified as high-risk by the combined model—particularly those with early anatomical stage but elevated PLR, NLR, NPR, and low LMR—could be prioritized for intensified postoperative surveillance, such as more frequent cross-sectional imaging (e.g., every 3–4 months instead of 6 months) and closer monitoring of tumor markers. This model may also aid in selecting patients for adjuvant therapy trials or future interventions targeting the inflammatory tumor microenvironment. Conversely, patients with advanced-stage disease but a favorable inflammatory profile might be considered for standard surveillance intervals, potentially reducing unnecessary imaging and healthcare burden. Such risk-adapted management could enable more personalized and efficient use of postoperative resources.

The moderate predictive power of individual markers (AUCs 0.616–0.637) suggests they may not fully capture the complexity inherent in cancer biology. However, the notable improvement in prognostic prediction by integrating these markers into a combined predictive model suggests an additive or synergistic interaction among them, which may better approximate the multifaceted immune response to cancer. The combined model’s high AUC further emphasizes the potential role of multifactorial panels incorporating multiple inflammatory markers in enhancing the precision of prognosis in clinical settings.

Several potential mechanisms could underlie the observed differences in inflammatory marker expressions between patient groups. Activation of systemic inflammatory pathways may reflect the host’s response to tumor presence and aggressiveness, potentially mediated through cytokine secretion and immune cell mobilization. These processes can modulate not only tumor biology directly but also influence the systemic milieu that supports cancer-related inflammation and immune suppression. For example, systemic inflammation might promote myelopoiesis, leading to increased circulating neutrophils and monocytes, which could potentiate a pro-tumoral environment [31, 32]. Understanding these mechanisms further may guide novel therapeutic strategies designed to modulate the immune microenvironment.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design carries inherent risks of selection bias and unmeasured confounding, despite statistical adjustments. Second, we excluded patients receiving neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy, which limits the applicability of our findings to the growing population of HCCA patients treated with multimodal strategies. Our cohort comprised only R0-resected patients in generally good health, which may not represent higher-risk or unresectable cases, potentially underestimating the prognostic range of these markers. Furthermore, while we excluded patients with recent biliary drainage or active cholangitis, subclinical inflammation or prior interventions might still affect baseline biomarker levels. Future prospective studies should standardize blood sampling timing and integrate these inflammatory markers with other prognostic factors (e.g., genomic profiles, liquid biopsies) into unified risk prediction models. Such efforts would advance precision oncology by enabling dynamic, biologically informed stratification for adjuvant therapy trials and personalized surveillance protocols. While the internal temporal validation supports the robustness of our model, external validation in multi-center and ethnically diverse cohorts remains essential. Geographic variations in HCCA incidence, genetic backgrounds, and regional treatment protocols (e.g., surgical techniques, perioperative care) may influence inflammatory marker distributions and their prognostic cut-offs. Future studies should validate our model across different healthcare settings to confirm its generalizability and ensure its applicability in broader clinical practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study illuminates the prognostic significance of inflammatory markers in HCCA, offering novel insights into the cancer-immunity interplay. These findings advocate for the integration of inflammatory markers into routine prognostic assessments to more comprehensively capture tumor biology and patient outcomes. Moreover, the potential utility of a combined model showcases the promise of multifaceted prognostic tools in achieving personalized medicine’s goals, allowing for more accurate risk stratification and potentially informing tailored therapeutic approaches. Future research should focus on validating these findings in prospective studies and exploring the potential for therapeutic interventions aimed at modifying the inflammatory landscape to improve patient outcomes.
