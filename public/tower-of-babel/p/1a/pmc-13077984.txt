Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid lymphocytosis in immune checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis in patients with non-small cell lung cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yaozhong Zhang, Yi An, Yajie Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) enhance survival rate in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), but lead to immune-related adverse events, among which checkpoint-inhibitor pneumonitis (CIP) is a leading cause of death. The utility of bronchoalveolar-lavage (BAL) lymphocytosis for diagnosing CIP and predicting its severity remains uncertain.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines.ResultsA total of ten studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising a total of 168 CIP samples. The pooled estimate for BAL lymphocyte percentage was 56.43% (95% CI, 43.89–68.98, I2 = 99.1%) in CIP, 14.85% (95% CI, 12.04–17.66, I2 = 9.0%) in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and 43.76% (95% CI, 24.43–63.09, I2 = 99.3%) in NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF. Significant differences were observed between CIP and IPF (p < 0.0001), NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF (p = 0.0024). Additionally, the BAL lymphocyte percentage varied between low-grade and high-grade CIP (SMD = -1.15, 95% CI, -2.26–-0.04, I2 = 82.4%). Similar results were also calculated on the BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio. The included studies demonstrated significant heterogeneity.ConclusionBAL lymphocyte percentage is elevated and CD4:CD8 ratio is reduced in NSCLC patients with CIP, with higher lymphocyte percentages associated with severe disease. A deeper understanding of the relationships between immunophenotyping, clinical factors and lymphocytosis will guide the use of BAL in the diagnose and evaluation of CIP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04224-z.Keywords: Checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis; Lymphocyte, Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid; Non-small cell lung cancer

Introduction

According to the Global Cancer Statistics 2024 report, lung cancer is the second most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer related deaths globally, responsible for about 18% of all fatalities and having the highest incidence rates. Non-small cell lung cancers (NSCLC) account for about 85% of lung cancer cases based on histological analysis. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) offer cancer patients improved tumor control and survival benefits, yet they can also cause immune-related adverse events (irAE). Checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis (CIP) is a common irAE and leading cause of irAE related deaths. A systematic review, including 48 trials, found that patients with NSCLC had a 2.5-fold increased risk of developing CIP compared to those with other cancers [1]. Furthermore, 10–20% of patients with ICP are unresponsive to standard corticosteroid therapy, presenting a significant challenge for clinicians [2, 3]. Currently, effective treatments targeting the pathophysiological mechanisms of CIP are lacking.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis is crucial for diagnosing, assessing treatment efficacy, evaluating prognosis, and studying the pathogenesis of respiratory diseases, especially those affecting the lower airways. However, the additional benefits of bronchoscopy in CIP management remain underexplored. Recent research indicates that BALF can be utilized for additional analysis, with BALF lymphocytosis linked to CIP development [4, 5]. Lymphocytosis and increased cellularity are linked to CIP, with lymphocyte counts potentially indicating the severity of alveolitis. However, there is few studies concentrating on the role of BAL lymphocytosis in the diagnosis and evaluation of CIP. In a study of CIP patients, a major characteristic of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) analysis in CIP patients with NSCLC was an increased proportion of lymphocytes [6]. Zhancheng Gao et al. found twelve metabolites, enriched in the CIP group, were positively correlated with the proportion of CD8 + T cells [7]. Meanwhile, the BAL lymphocytosis CD4:CD8 ratio could potentially serve as a treatment strategy for CIP [8]. However, no consensus was reached on the importance of BAL lymphocytosis. These findings underscore the need for research on BAL lymphocytosis in the diagnose and evaluation of CIP.

BAL lymphocytosis may be influenced by several variables, including the presence and extent of fibrosis, the timing relative to antigen exposure, smoking history and the procedural technique for BAL collection. The role of BAL lymphocytosis in establishing a diagnosis of CIP remains unclear. The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to explore BAL lymphocyte in CIP and compare these findings to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF. We further sought to test the difference in the BALF features between patients with low-grade CIP and those with high-grade CIP.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

Following Preferred Reprting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis [9]. Meanwhile, we also registered the protocol of this study in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under registration number CRD420251161746. A comprehensive search was performed across Pubmed, Scopus, Medline, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Clinical Trials, and Wanfang databases from their inception until October 2025. The detailed search strategy is outlined in supplementary table S1. All authors contributed to the search strategy, which incorporated both text words and controlled vocabulary. All titles and abstracts underwent thorough screening and review. If no sufficient data in publications were extracted, the authors contacted the corresponding authors to get relevant data to analysis. To avoid double counting patient cohorts, we included only the study with the largest participant pool when multiple publications had overlapping study periods. All the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed when patients developed with suspected ICP.

Two reviewers (Y.Z. and Y.A.) independently screened and reviewed the articles, with discrepancies resolved by consensus and/or review by an additional adjudicator (Y.H.).Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) they must be original research; (2) they comprised patients made a definite diagnosis of NSCLC and CIP; (3) they reported detailed BAL lymphocyte data; (4) they had full text available in English or Chinese. On the contrary, studies beyond the inclusion criteria were excluded. Exclusion criteria were as the following criteria: (1) they were without sufficient data and unable to calculate data of interest; (2) they included multiple primary cancer; (3) they included pediatric patients (less than 18 years old); (4) they belonged to case report, comment, letter, review, or meta-analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers (Y.Z. and Y.A.) independently performed data extraction and quality assessment of all eligible studies. Data collected included details of study design, population characteristics, BAL lymphocyte percentage, BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio, pneumonitis grade, smoking history, published year, the stage and pathological type of NSCLC. The defining and grades CIP were detailed in supplementary table S2. The risk of bias in individual studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale tool [10]. Two reviewers (Y.Z. and Y.A.) independently performed data extraction and quality assessment of all eligible studies. An additional adjudicator (Y.H.) would be invited into the discussion to made final decision if no agreement was reached after two reviewers discussed. The Newcastle–Ottawa scores are presented in the supplementary table S3. The low quality study was defined as 0–3 scores, and 7–9 scores was defined as high quality. As all data were sourced from prior ethically approved studies, obtaining patient consent and additional ethical approval was unnecessary.

Statistical analysis

We performed statistical using the meta package in R (version 4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) within RStudio (version 4.0.4, RStudio). An unpaired t-test was used to compare pooled lymphocyte percentage estimates for CIP with those for specific diagnostic categories. Studies with I2 statistics of 0%, 25%, 50%, and 75% indicate no, low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [11]. Subgroup analysis and meta-regression were conducted to examine heterogeneity if needed. We also conducted an influence analysis for the unadjusted pooled estimates using the leave-one-out method. Similar methods were used to evaluate the CD4:CD8 ratio in CIP, contrasting it with IPF and other NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF. Egger's and Begg's tests were conducted to further validate potential publication bias [12]. All statistical analyses were two sides; and p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ten eligible studies [4–8, 13–17] with 168 patients with NSCLC and CIP were included in the meta-analysis. The Fig. 1 showed a full flow chart of this meta-analysis. Among the eligible studies, six used NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF as controls [5, 7, 14–17], while only three included IPF [5, 7, 14]. In addition, seven studies evaluated the grades of CIP [4, 6, 8, 13, 15–17]. The characteristics of each study included in the current meta-analysis are reported in Table 1. All investigators agreed on review of the extracted data. The studies were all of moderate or high quality.

BAL lymphocytosis percentage

Data on BAL lymphocytes was collected from ten eligible studies [4–8, 13–17] with 273 patients, including 168 with CIP and NSCLC, 73 with NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF, and 32 with IPF. The percentage of BAL lymphocytes in CIP was calculated as 56.43% (95% CI, 43.89–68.98, I2 = 99.1%) (Fig. 2A). The pooled estimate BAL lymphocytosis percentage for IPF was only 14.85% (95% CI, 12.04–17.66, I2 = 9.0%), whereas the pooled estimate BAL lymphocytosis percentage for NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF was 43.76% (95% CI, 24.43–63.09, I2 = 99.3%) (Fig. 2B). The heterogeneity tests indicated high heterogeneity for the CIP, IPF and NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF. Therefore, the data was pooled in a random effects model. For BAL lymphocyte percentage, there were significant differences between CIP and IPF (p < 0.0001), and between it and NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF (p < 0.0001). Furthermore, we conducted meta-regression to evaluate the heterogeneity of results, only the smoking history was significantly correlated with intra-study variability (p = 0.0024). The results of meta-regression were listed in Table 2.

BAL lymphocytosis CD4: CD8 ratio

Based on the nine studies [4–8, 13, 15–17], we calculated BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio as 0.62 (95% CI, 0.50–0.74, I2 = 76.9%) (Fig. 3A) in patients with NSCLC and CIP. The pooled BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio for IPF was estimated as 4.98 (95% CI, 3.92–6.04, I2 = 0%), and it is 1.69 (95% CI, 1.07–2.31, I2 = 88.9%) (Fig. 3B) for NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF. Again, the heterogeneity tests suggested high heterogeneity for the CIP, IPF and NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF. Accordingly, the data from including studies was pooled using a random effects model. The BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio did differ CIP with IPF (p = 0.0007) and NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF (p = 0.0038), respectively. As showed in Table 2, the meta-regression analysis showed no significant role of a variable to account for the heterogeneity of BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio (Fig. 4).

Correlation between BAL lymphocytosis and grades of CIP patients

Patients with high-grade CIP have a high mortality rate and poor prognosis. There are seven studies concentrated on the severity of CIP [4, 6, 8, 13, 15–17]. There was significant heterogeneity in both the low-grade and high-grade CIP groups. Using a random effects model, the BAL lymphocyte percentage significantly differed between low and high-grade CIP (SMD = −1.15, 95% CI, −2.26–−0.04, I2 = 82.4%), whereas the BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio showed no significant difference between these groups (SMD = −0.48, 95% CI, −1.47–0.51, I2 = 89.0%).

Publication bias

The Egger's and Begg's tests indicated no significant publication bias in this meta-analysis. The analysis revealed no significant publication bias for both BAL lymphocyte percentage (Egger’s test p = 0.511 and Begg’s test p = 0.764) and BAL lymphocyte CD4:CD8 ratio (Egger’s test p = 0.220 and Begg’s test p = 0.448) showed no significant publication bias. The conclusions were not changed after adjustment for publication bias by using the trim and fill method [18]. We employed the leave-one-out method for analysis and created funnel plots to evaluate potential publication bias.

Discussion

While BAL is considered a diagnostic tool for CIP, its definitive role remains controversial [19, 20]. Unfortunately, there wasn't enough evidence to support the application of BAL lymphocyte percentage and CD4:CD8 ratio in a patient with suspected CIP. In this study, we examined the findings of BAL lymphocyte percentage is higher in patients with CIP compared to IPF and other NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF, and the CD4:CD8 ratio was lower. Our study also suggested that higher lymphocyte percentage in BAL was associated with high-grade CIP in patients with NSCLC.

ICIs are crucial for treating advanced or recurrent NSCLC, especially in cases lacking specific targeted genetic mutations [21]. For patients with NSCLC, Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and programmed death 1 (PD-1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) are the most relevant targets for immunotherapy [22]. However, as the opportunities for using ICIs increase, the frequency of CIP is also increasing, especially for NSCLC patients. The incidence of CIP after the application of PD-1 monoclonal antibody is higher than that after the application of PD-L1 monoclonal antibody, and compared with PD-L1 or CTLA-4 monotherapy, the combination of PD-L1 monoclonal antibody and CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody treatment has a higher risk of CIP [23]. The overall incidence of grade 3 or higher CIP is estimated to be 1.1 to 3.2% [2].

Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) rely on clinical manifestation, while National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines are mainly based on radiographic findings in NSCLC patients with CIP. Up to now, there are no objective indicators for CIP grading that can be referenced. BAL is a versatile procedure that can be used to identify and remove airway obstructions, deliver targeted medications directly into the lungs, and accurately stage lung cancer to guide treatment decisions. Elisabeth Bendstrup et al. focus on BAL for diagnosis and treatment modalities including whole lung lavage (WLL) and inhaled GM-CSF therapy [24]. This study is the first meta-analysis to systematically examine BALF lymphocyte levels and their correlation with NSCLC patient CIP grades. BALF lymphocytosis and eosinophilia are associated with CIP, but their ability to discriminate CIP from other conditions is modest. BAL immune cell percentages may have prognostic value for 1-year survival [25]. The significant variability among studies and the prevalent use of BAL cellular analysis in CIP diagnostics suggest a high risk of incorporation bias, likely inflating test characteristics.

The risk factors for severe CIP, grade 3 or higher, remain poorly understood. The potential severity of CIP highlights the importance of identifying baseline predictive factors to evaluate the individual risk–benefit ratio of a treatment. The recurrence rate of CIP was 13.2% to 25.6%, encompassing patients without or with ICIs rechallenge, highlighting the importance of risk assessment for CIP recurrence in lung cancer patients [3]. Research on risk factors for CIP recurrence is also scarce. A higher BALF lymphocyte percentage and a CD4/CD8 ratio greater than 1 in BALF lymphocyte subsets were reported to be associated with increased susceptibility to relapse during CIP treatment [13]. This is primarily due to the predominance of CD4 + central memory subsets (Tcms, CD4 + CD45RA–CD62L +) in the BAL of CIP patients, which exhibited greater resistance to steroid-induced apoptosis compared to other conventional T cells [26]. Th17 cells in circulation and the lungs play an important role in pulmonary fibrosis and inflammation. An imbalance of circulating Th17 cells and regulatory T (Treg) cells was associated with the deterioration of pulmonary injury [27]. Corticosteroids represent the first line treatment for CIP. However, the starting doses, duration and timing of combination with other immunosuppressive agents have not been fully studied and are unclear. High-dose corticosteroids have significant immunosuppressive effects and have been associated with a higher risk of infection [28].

The pooled estimates for the CD4:CD8 ratio of BALF lymphocytes exhibit significant heterogeneity, and due to study characteristics, robust subset analyses to elucidate these differences were not feasible. The BAL lymphocytosis CD4: CD8 ratio was observed to be lower in CIP patients compared to those with IPF and NSCLC patients with no ICP or IPF, with significant variability across studies. The CD4:CD8 ratio is known to be influenced by exposure, smoking and disease severity, and this test has largely fallen out of favor in clinical practice [14]. In addition, ICIs treatment itself may promote lymphocyte migration to lung tissue, potentially leading to underestimation of differences between CIP and control groups. Future studies should prioritize ICI-treated patients without CIP as controls to more accurately assess CIP-specific effects on BALF immune cells.

This meta-analysis still has several limitations. First, International guidelines exist to guide the BALF collection procedure, yet there remains heterogeneity in collection, processing and analysis and there remains the potential for bias. Although our literature search was extensive, some relevant references might have been overlooked. The absence of extensive head-to-head randomized controlled trials hinders definitive conclusions. Finally, the small sample size precludes analysis of CIP grade and lung cancer histological type, making it challenging to establish a link between pathology type and treatment or prognosis. This notwithstanding, our study possesses several significant strengths. As China contributed the highest publication volume [29] and bronchoscopy rates remained lower in Western countries [30], seven of the ten included studies were conducted in China.

This study offers the most thorough evaluation of BAL cellular characteristics in this patient population to date. While our findings suggest that BAL lymphocytosis may serve as a potential biomarker for CIP diagnosis and severity assessment, the clinical utility of routine BAL implementation remains to be established through prospective studies evaluating diagnostic performance thresholds, clinical decision-making impact, and patient outcomes. Further research is warranted to determine whether BAL guided management strategies can improve clinical outcomes in patients with suspected CIP.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this meta-analysis suggested BAL lymphocyte percentage is higher and CD4/CD8 ratio is lower in NSCLC patients with CIP compared to those without CIP or with IPF. For NSCLC patients, BAL lymphocytosis was significantly associated with the severity of CIP. However, there was no significant difference in CD4:CD8 ratio of BAL lymphocyte subsets between NSCLC patients with low-grade CIP and those with high-grade CIP. A deeper understanding of the relationships between immunophenotyping, host factors and alveolar lymphocytosis will guide the use of BAL in the diagnostic evaluation of CIP.
