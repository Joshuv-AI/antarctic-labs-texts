ABSTRACT
Living shorelines that comprise oyster reefs within a mosaic of multiple coastal habitats can be a resilient and adaptive coastal protection alternative to conventional engineered structures. The success of an oyster reef living shoreline depends on the evolution of a stable base substrate that provides initial short-term coastal protection, to the growth of a living oyster reef and associated habitats in the longer-term, which can enhance protection and provide other ecosystem services. Interdisciplinary global teams that include ecologists, biologists, engineers, techno-economists, and industry are leading the development and implementation of innovative reef-based coastal protection (“Reefense”) solutions that can be scaled to face the challenges of climate change.

Reef-forming oysters are foundation species that once formed extensive habitats in shallow estuarine and coastal waters globally. Cumulative pressures from overharvesting, habitat degradation, pollution, disease, invasive species, and climate change have destroyed an estimated 85% of oyster reefs worldwide (1). In many locations, losses exceeding 95% have led to oyster reefs being declared functionally extinct and unable to provide essential ecosystem services, such as biodiversity provision, water filtration, and coastal protection (2). When oysters form complex three-dimensional biogenic structures from dense aggregations of individuals, they become ecosystem engineers (3). The reef structure and roughness of oyster reefs can effectively attenuate wave energy through wave reflection, breaking, and drag forces, thereby mitigating wave-driven coastal hazards (4). Due to oyster reef removal, nearshore wave energy may be 30 to 200% higher compared to historical baselines (5), with potential implications for contemporary shoreline erosion and stabilization (6). Sea level rise and increased storminess due to climate change is increasing the risk of erosion and flooding to communities and infrastructure, and elevating demand for shoreline management (7). While conventional engineered coastal protection structures (e.g., seawalls and breakwaters) have long been used to manage hazard risk, there is growing interest in nature-based solutions, such as oyster reefs, for coastal protection (8).

Global efforts to restore oyster reefs and their services are accelerating (9). While many oyster reef restoration projects are primarily intended for habitat recovery or enhancing fisheries or water quality, an increasing number aim to provide coastal protection known as “living shorelines” (10). Living shorelines are one example of a nature-based solution, defined as actions that use natural processes and ecosystems to address societal challenges while delivering cobenefits for people and nature (11). Oyster reef living shorelines often involve the placement of a fringing or breakwater reef substrate that is either naturally colonized by oysters or seeded with hatchery-reared spat in recruitment-limited systems (12). The capacity of oysters to adapt to climate-driven changes, such as sea level rise, is one benefit of living shorelines compared with conventional engineered coastal protection structures (13). Conversely, the potential vulnerability of oysters to factors such as disease and predation poses a risk to their use for coastal protection (14, 15). For an oyster reef living shoreline to be successful, it must establish a self-sustaining population of oysters that results in reef growth over time, as well as achieve the targeted coastal protection goals (e.g., wave attenuation, erosion mitigation; 8). The design of an oyster reef living shoreline, therefore, needs to integrate the biology and ecology of oysters with the engineering design of coastal structures that account for the influence on physical processes (i.e., hydrodynamics and sediment transport), requiring interdisciplinary efforts to succeed.

The integration of ecology and engineering in methods for coastal hazard management represents a paradigm shift in a field that has traditionally been delivered by engineers designing fixed structures, such as seawalls and breakwaters or, in more recent decades, by ecologists seeking to restore or rehabilitate biogenic habitats. Indeed, there is evidence that living shoreline design and implementation are still occurring in a mostly siloed approach among disciplines, which has resulted in oyster reef living shorelines achieving either engineering or ecological goals, but not both (12, 16). Effective interdisciplinary collaboration is required to provide the technical guidance currently lacking for oyster reef living shorelines. Without this technical guidance, oyster reef living shorelines are generally not implemented at scale due to perceived risk or delivered by end users through a trial-and-error approach with higher likelihood of failure (17).

Advancing “Reefense” Solutions

“Reefense” is an innovative program established by the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) to design and construct biogenic reef-based living shorelines from first principles, integrating engineering, ecology, adaptive biology, and techno-economics. Its objective is to develop a foundational framework for low-cost reef structures that provide initial wave attenuation while supporting the colonization of self-sustaining populations of reef-building organisms, offering long-term and adaptable coastal protection. This foundation is then combined with site-specific information to deliver field demonstrations of reef-based living shorelines. Here, we use Reefense as an example to illustrate an interdisciplinary approach to the design of living shorelines, with the aim of informing future development of global teams capable of producing the research required to underpin large-scale implementation. Although examined here through the lens of living shorelines, these findings reflect challenges facing nature-based solutions more broadly, where limited interdisciplinary integration continues to constrain effective design, performance assessment, and scalability (18).

This program brought together an interdisciplinary team across the United States and Australia to advance a Reefense solution for reef-building oysters (Fig. 1). Advancing the use of living shorelines requires that they be effective (i.e., achieve project outcomes), sustainable (i.e., adaptable over time), and widespread (i.e., adopted by government and industry) (19). Reefense developed functional design criteria using a similar framework that prioritized shoreline protection, durability, and cost (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S1). These metrics reflect the anticipated evolution of the reef structure where in the short-term, the durability of the base structure is important to provide instantaneous coastal protection and a stable substrate for oyster colonization. In the longer-term, it is the development of a self-sustaining and adaptive living oyster reef that provides ongoing durability and coastal protection with reduced maintenance costs. Shoreline Protection

Structural Component.

Oysters spawn free-swimming larvae that navigate the water column until finding a suitable hard substrate to settle on permanently (20). For natural reefs, this settlement substrate is often the shells of dead or live oysters, and unconsolidated or consolidated (e.g., bagged) shell has been a popular substratum for oyster reef restoration (21). For oyster reef living shorelines, the reef structure serves as a settlement substrate and often provides immediate coastal protection while oysters colonize and grow, thereby providing additional protection (8). The use of the reef substratum for wave attenuation and oyster colonization in living shorelines has led to innovations in the design of modular reefs that provide both outcomes, often constructed from precast concrete units (12).

Submerged or “low-crested” breakwaters are conventional engineered structures that are usually constructed from rock with a crest elevation at or below mean sea level. For these breakwaters, the crest submergence depth (i.e., “freeboard”), the incident wave height, and the crest width are essential parameters that determine wave transmission (22, 23). When the freeboard increases, wave transmission increases, and the crest width tends to have a greater influence on wave attenuation as waves interact with reef roughness over longer distances (4). However, when the freeboard decreases (specifically relative to the incident wave height), depth-induced wave breaking controls wave attenuation (22, 23). Consequently, the dimensions of these structures are usually adjusted to the incident wave climate under consideration and often undergo physical and/or numerical modeling to identify the critical design parameters and their performance for sites with different characteristics and hydrodynamic conditions (e.g., wave heights, water depths).

Existing knowledge of submerged breakwaters can inform the initial design of an oyster reef substratum; however, additional design criteria are needed to ensure that the constructed reef provides suitable oyster habitat. For intertidal oysters, inundation time dictates the tidal elevation at which reefs can form (24). The optimal inundation time is a trade-off among limiting factors such as increased biofouling, predation, competition, or sedimentation in the subtidal, and the availability of filter-feeding time and exposure to extreme temperature and desiccation stress at maximum intertidal elevations (25, 26). The eastern oyster (Crassostrea virginica), native to eastern North America, is one of the most well-studied oyster species globally and has been shown to have an inundation threshold of at least 50% (27, 28). For other oyster species, such as the native Australian flat oyster (Ostrea angasi), this threshold is unknown due to the scarcity of natural reefs (29). Addressing these and similar ecological data gaps across oyster species is necessary to guide living shoreline design.

In Reefense, physical modeling of the reef design to achieve the performance metric (90% wave energy attenuation for the 20-y return period storm wave condition within 5 y; SI Appendix, Table S1) was conducted with a crest height at mean sea level to account for the inundation tolerances of the eastern oyster. With an increased freeboard, the design of the Reefense Module™ and spatial arrangement (Fig. 3) sought to maximize the loss of wave energy due to the work done by drag forces (hereafter drag-induced dissipation) as well as oyster reef building. The modules were porous (i.e., had holes throughout) and included horizontal and vertical surfaces to increase the area for oyster attachment and potentially provide refuges from stressors (e.g., predation, sedimentation, temperature; discussed further in Durability below). The module porosity was optimized for drag-induced dissipation by offsetting the hole alignment through the module, which increased the flow path of water (Fig. 3). The modular structure allows adaptable configurations for different performance metrics and environments. Further, the planar shape was designed to be self-interlocking to increase stability under hydrodynamic loads, and to improve scalability and deployment efficiency (Fig. 3). Porous and modular structures are not typical of conventional engineered structures; therefore, the Reefense Modules™ underwent analogous physical model testing as conventional engineered structures to determine the configuration that would achieve the wave energy attenuation target and performance over a range of wave conditions and water depths for broad applicability.

It was found that 70% wave energy attenuation could be achieved with a uniform crest height of three units wide (Fig. 4A), while 90% wave energy attenuation could be achieved with a uniform crest height of seven units wide (30). However, as field surveys of natural oyster reefs revealed a trapezoidal cross-section (Fig. 5A), sloping sides constructed with three-quarter and half-height units were added to mimic the observed gradual transition from the seabed to reef crest (Fig. 5C). Further, the units with crest height at a lower tidal elevation have a greater vertical space for oysters that can eventually grow to reach the tidal elevation of the full-height units (i.e., the oyster growth ceiling of 50% inundation). As the optimum submergence time of oysters can vary depending on complex ecological factors (31, 32), substrate offered across a range of submergence levels can also increase the probability of long-term oyster recruitment and growth.

Ecological Component.

A temporal change in wave transmission of the reef structure is expected with oyster recruitment as shell growth reduces module porosity and increases canopy roughness (Figs. 3 and 5C). Spatial and temporal variation in the ecological component of living shorelines is complex and rarely addressed in living shoreline parameterization (36). However, it is important to account for ecologically driven changes in living shoreline structure to ensure long-term performance. In the case of Reefense, the physical model testing was done on modules with and without oyster colonization, the former achieved through 3D printing the oyster canopy from photogrammetry of a colonized reef (Fig. 5C). At deeper relative freeboards (defined as freeboard/incident wave height) the uncolonized reef with greater porosity had higher wave energy attenuation due to drag-induced dissipation (Fig. 4B). However, as the relative freeboard approached zero (i.e., water level is at the reef crest), the reef colonized with oysters attenuated 15% greater wave energy than the reef without oysters (Fig. 4B) due to the oysters increasing wave breaking through a reduction in porosity. While oyster reefs can effectively reduce wave energy under specific design conditions, their performance diminishes in deeper water (e.g., during storm surges). Integrating multihabitat living shoreline elements may help mitigate these limitations.

Natural oyster reefs exist in a mosaic of interacting habitats, such as submerged and emergent vegetation, that are a defining feature of healthy seascapes (37–39). Across the land–sea continuum, habitats are connected through biological (e.g., refugia from predation, reduced competition), physical (e.g., modification of light and temperature, substrate provision, wave attenuation, and sediment trapping), and biogeochemical (e.g., water filtration, nutrient modification, oxygenation of sediment) interactions (39). Oyster reefs can facilitate seagrasses, mangroves, and saltmarshes through wave attenuation and sediment stabilization that lowers hydrodynamic conditions required for vegetation establishment (40–43). Additional bivalve species, such as ribbed mussels (Geukensia demissa), can proliferate in saltmarsh creeks and shorelines, further stabilizing sediments and facilitating marsh accretion via their biodeposits (44). Through water filtration, oyster reefs and mussel beds can improve water quality for photosynthetic organisms and regulate nutrients that impact the growth of coastal vegetation (45, 46). In turn, coastal vegetation can provide firm, stable substrate for oyster and mussel establishment (e.g., mangrove roots; 47), decrease hydrodynamic (48) and thermal (49) stress, and provide protection from predation and competition (50, 51). While positive species interactions among habitat mosaics are known to support coastal ecosystem resilience and expansion (45, 52), and there have been calls for seascape approaches in restoration (53), there are few examples of restoration projects using multihabitats to increase success (39). Yet, the landscape/seascape context does affect the habitat provisioning outcomes of restored oyster reefs (37, 54) and supports the concept of increased benefits from multihabitat approaches.

In living shorelines, the use of multiple habitats may not only increase the resilience of the mosaic components that underpin sustained coastal protection but also result in a greater wave attenuation performance and sediment stabilization. For example, a wave flume experiment showed that the wave energy attenuation of cordgrass and oyster reef combined (67%) was greater than the attenuation of the habitats individually (31% and 45%, respectively) (55). This finding is due to different habitats being found across a gradient of water depths (cross-shore locations), which creates a greater width of habitats combined for wave attenuation. Thus, it may be possible to achieve wave energy attenuation targets with multiple habitats that cannot be achieved by individual habitats. In Reefense, a Living Shoreline Mosaic™ (LSM) in the lee of the larger oyster reef was designed that integrated oyster reef patches to support submerged aquatic vegetation and plantings of emergent marsh grasses that encourage colonization of mutualistic ribbed mussels (Geukensia demissa) (56). These habitat mosaics augmented what is found naturally in this US Gulf Coast region (Fig. 3), with the aim of enhancing the coastal protection of the oyster reef and increasing the resilience of the solution.

Durability

Reef Material.

Vertical height of reef-building oysters occurs when spat, or juvenile oysters, settle on the shells of dead or live adults, such that natural reefs have a core of dead shells with a layer of live oysters that maintain reef integrity (34). For oyster reef living shorelines where the reef substrate is used for the dual purposes of initial wave attenuation and settlement substrate, the reef material needs to be durable enough to withstand the hydrodynamic forces at a site upon installation but could weaken over time as it will be reinforced by calcareous oyster colonization. In oyster reef restoration, there is an increasing use of novel biodegradable materials (e.g., starch-based BESE-elements®; (57) as reef substrate that create the conditions needed for oyster reefs to establish but also recognize that this substrate is not required longer-term when the reefs become self-sustaining. These biodegradable materials have seen less use in living shorelines as their often-low-profile structure does not provide initial coastal protection, and the material can have lower integrity in higher energy conditions (57). This has led to a prevalence of concrete and rock rubble as oyster reef substrate in living shorelines.

Concrete is a common building material for engineered marine structures as it is valued for its strength, durability, and low-cost. It is also a versatile material that can be molded to create units for modular structures of various sizes and shapes that can integrate well in marine ecosystems (58) and is a suitable settlement substrate for oysters (59). However, the cement industry accounts for approximately 8% of the global CO2 emissions (60). Further, the use of durable, long-lasting materials such as concrete can be misaligned with the design life of a project and can promote the overengineering of oyster reefs due to the focus being on the structure achieving the coastal protection outcomes rather than the development of a self-sustaining oyster population (8).

In Reefense, two materials were developed for reef construction: a biodegradable (or service-life controlled) concrete and a low-carbon self-consolidating concrete. The biodegradable concrete used a mixture of 75% ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) cement to reduce the carbon impact and biopolymer that enables slow degradation. The biodegradable concrete had an initial strength of 50 MPa at 7 d ensuring adequate structural capacity while facilitating oyster attachment and growth. The concrete was designed to degrade over ~15 years in the marine environment leaving behind a wholly oyster-based structure, although the potential for bioprotection to slow material loss still requires consideration. A low-carbon self-consolidating concrete was developed by blending supplementary cementitious materials and an inert calcium carbonate filler that resulted in a 60% reduction in carbon footprint while still maintaining strength and durability requirements for widespread usage in reef applications. There is a growth of new materials and manufacturing technologies for oyster reef substrate in living shorelines (61). The choice of materials at a site needs to consider those that balance mechanical and durability performance, biogenic reef development, and sustainability with regard to climate change mitigation and resource extraction of constituent material.

Settlement and Postsettlement Processes.

For intact oyster habitats, the dense aggregation of individual oysters results in emergent traits, which are self-facilitating feedback processes that promote the establishment of oyster reefs under conditions that individuals would not be able to survive (62). Oyster reefs provide a complex habitat for settling larvae that alleviates stress from physical (e.g., hydrodynamics, sedimentation, temperature) and biotic (e.g., predation) factors. Self-facilitation becomes increasingly important as external physical conditions become harsher (63). There exist two thresholds: a lower threshold where below self-facilitation is unimportant as conditions are benign, and a higher threshold where above self-facilitation is inefficient, and a habitat can either not exist, or permanent engineering solutions are required (e.g., maintaining offshore breakwaters to support vegetation persistence in the lee; 61). An understanding of the thresholds for oyster reef establishment within the context of the living shoreline site is therefore fundamental in optimizing a living shoreline design. This, however, is a large knowledge gap for many habitat-forming species (64). Under conditions where self-facilitation is important, recreation of these emergent traits to enable successful habitat establishment can be challenging.

Mimicry of emergent traits is a novel advancement in restoration and living shorelines, aimed at increasing the survival of transplants or naturally recruited individuals (63). For example, clumped cordgrass transplants that enhanced self-facilitation survived better than individuals spaced out to avoid competition (65). Self-facilitation in cordgrass can be mimicked by surrounding a transplant with biodegradable mats that simulate dense patches of stiff-stem canopies, thereby improving survival (62). Similarly, for shellfish reefs that rely on larval recruitment cued to turbulence (66, 67), the three-dimensional complexity of a natural canopy with high rugosity and fractal dimension (Fig. 5B) is strongly related to creation of turbulence above and within the canopy (68), and therefore potentially recruitment success. For artificial reef substrates, the surface roughness and structural complexity can be manipulated to improve settlement and postsettlement survival of larvae like a natural oyster reef (35). In Reefense, the settlement of oyster larvae in the laboratory was 3.7 times greater on sandblasted concrete that increased roughness compared to flat (or nonsand blasted) concrete, and settlement was increased by 4 to 10 times through the addition of surface complexity (cm scale) (35). The greater larval settlement on rougher and more complex substrates corresponded with substrate-mediated changes to the boundary layer hydrodynamics, which increased larval retention (35). Similarly, at larger scales, changes in the water velocity resulting from flow patterns within and around the modular reef may also affect initial oyster recruitment and subsequent growth (69).

Complex surfaces can create a predation refuge for juvenile oysters (58), although the scale of these features for predator-exclusion will depend on the predator(s) at a site and trophic interactions (70). Oyster predation can occur on larval stages by other filter feeders such as barnacles and anemones, while spat may be fed on by flatworms and small crabs, and older spat, first-year oysters and adults by larger predators such as whelks, crabs, fish, and birds. Predation on oysters can be intense (15) and can be a limiting factor for restoration success (71). In Reefense, the effectiveness of multiple reef design factors in reducing predation pressure on oysters from gastropods (e.g., oyster drills and conchs) and shellfish (e.g., crabs) is being evaluated. The design factors tested include reef substrate slope, vertical layering and spacing, elevation of substrate above the sediment, and add-on predator exclusion components such as spikes and inverted cone barriers. Preliminary testing indicates that elevation, layering, and large spacing between layers show potential for reducing predation pressure. More broadly, continued exploration of structural complexity, spatial configuration, and physical deterrent elements is likely to provide further insights into how engineered reef designs can minimize predation and enhance oyster survival in restoration and living shoreline applications. This work can be grounded in, and expanded from, the extensive habitat-complexity literature linking three-dimensional structure to recruitment, refuge provisioning, and enhanced survival, allowing new design innovations to build explicitly on ecological mechanisms already well understood from natural reefs (70, 72). These considerations are especially relevant in environments where recruitment is limited or heavily constrained by predation pressure, or where the use of hatchery-reared seed is necessary to establish or maintain oyster populations.

Adaptive Biology.

Oyster-based living shorelines often rely on natural recruitment by wild oysters. In systems that are recruitment-limited, however, hatchery-reared oysters that are often attached to a settlement substrate (e.g., shell) are distributed onto a reef (73). Oyster restoration projects often combine both the addition of substrate to attract wild oyster larvae and the supplementation of the reef with wild or hatchery seed and adult oysters as a broodstock to increase larval supply (74, 75). Selecting the oyster stock to use is important for success and the use of local populations is generally preferred for their adaptation to the local conditions where the reef will be created (75, 76). Local adaptation has been documented in oyster populations, in reciprocal transplant or common-garden experiments, observing a better fitness (e.g. growth, reproduction, or survival) of resident populations in their own environment than populations from other habitats (76, 77).

In natural systems, oysters have adapted to a wide range of salinity, temperature, and hydrodynamic environments, and are exposed to varying disease loads. Restoration strategies must emphasize resistance to epizootic diseases that can cause catastrophic mortality among oyster species (e.g., dermo and MSX in C. virginica, bonamiasis in O. angasi, Pacific Oyster Mortality Syndrome in Crassostrea gigas, etc.). Dermo, the disease caused by the protist Perkinsus marinus can kill 50 to 90% of the affected oysters (78, 79). Breeding for dermo resistance has relied on phenotypic selection, where oysters are deployed in the field and the survivors of the disease are selected to produce the next generation. This method can be effective but is slow for dermo resistance (80, 81). Many factors make selective breeding based on phenotype less effective, among them the intermittent pressure of the disease in wild and selected populations due to the cyclic nature of the environmental conditions that favor disease progression, and the likely polygenic inheritance nature of dermo resistance with low heritability (82, 83). Overall, selective breeding has led to improvements in dermo resistance (84–86) but selected lines still show considerable susceptibility to dermo.

Genomic selection is based on genome-wide variation and is expected to be more effective for polygenic traits that are controlled by many genes of small effects. It can increase selection accuracy and efficiency when heritability is low, and phenotyping is difficult or time-consuming. Genomic selection has not been evaluated as a tool to improve dermo resistance, but it has been widely used in the genetic improvement of livestock and more recently of aquatic species for various traits including disease resistance (87–90). The Eastern Oyster Breeding Genomic Consortium developed high-density single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays that facilitate the use of genomic selection for dermo resistance and fast growth (91).

In Reefense, oysters from the local population where the reef was constructed were collected and divided into a training and a breeding population. Oysters from the training population were challenged with P. marinus in the laboratory, separated into susceptible (dead) and resistant (survivor) oysters and genotyped with the high-density SNP array (Fig. 6A). Their phenotype and genotype were used to develop a model to predict the potential of individuals in the breeding population to transmit disease resistance (i.e., the genomic estimated breeding values; GEBVs) based on their genotypes (Fig. 6B; 83). Oysters were selected for breeding based on their GEBVs (high versus low/control GEBV values; (83). Survivors of the training population were also used as a broodstock for a phenotypically selected control. The progenies (F1) of these three groups were tested in the lab for dermo resistance and results confirmed that genomic selection can significantly improve dermo resistance with only 28% mortality in the genomic up-selected line versus 40% in the down-selected control line, corresponding to a 20% improvement in survival (Fig. 6B; (83).

Adaptive biological approaches, such as selective breeding and genomic selection, can be applied across multiple generations to develop oyster lines with enhanced resilience to stressors including disease, heat, and salinity extremes that have utility in nature-based solutions applications. Integrating these improved lines into living shoreline and restoration projects allows for more robust evaluation of their performance in both laboratory and field environments, supporting the development of populations that are better able to withstand emerging environmental pressures and contribute to long-term ecosystem stability. However, the realized value of improved oyster lines will ultimately depend on local recruitment dynamics and the intensity of site-specific stressors.

Costs and Scaling Adoption

While a solution may be technically effective and sustainable, its widespread adoption depends on a combination of political, sociocultural, and economic factors (19). Political support, including regulatory frameworks, funding commitments, and institutional capacity shapes whether projects can be planned, permitted, and maintained at scale. Permitting is a frequently cited barrier to the implementation of living shorelines (17). In Reefense, the experimental reef deployed at Baker Point near Panama City, Florida (Fig. 1) was authorized under a state-level streamlined living shoreline exemption. In the United States, several jurisdictions have implemented simplified permitting pathways for living shorelines that meet specific criteria, which has contributed in part to their increased adoption. However, it is important to emphasize that project design should be guided by site-specific suitability and ecological considerations, rather than by permit restrictions alone. In other countries, such as Australia, there are currently no dedicated permitting pathways for restoration or living shorelines (92), which can create additional challenges for scaling such projects. Collaboration across research and industry, particularly with consultants experienced in permitting coastal projects, can help ensure that designs are both technically robust and compliant with regulatory requirements.

Sociocultural acceptance, reflecting community values, cultural heritage, and perceptions of legitimacy, influences engagement, stewardship, and long-term support. Active participation throughout all project stages, combined with coproduction and targeted information sharing with local communities, is essential for project success (93). Industrial-scale adoption of living shoreline mosaics must balance the costs and economic benefits of protecting shorelines. Economic viability, including upfront costs, lifecycle benefits, supply chains, and financing mechanisms, affects the feasibility of large-scale implementation. Techno-economic assessments are increasingly applied to living shorelines to evaluate both technical performance (e.g., erosion control, wave attenuation) and economic outcomes, including installation costs, maintenance, and monetized cobenefits such as fisheries, carbon sequestration, and recreation, although they are less standardized than for conventional coastal protection infrastructure (61, 94). Techno-economic analysis for Reefense has shown that to reduce the cost of novel living shoreline technologies, a network of competent contractors across a region or country that can deliver multiple large projects is required to enable local production and deployment, bidding competition, economies of scale, and accelerated learning. Further, a design that streamlines reef deployment and reduces labor costs, such as the self-interlocking feature of Reefense Modules™ will increase competitiveness with conventional engineered structures. Standardized frameworks for techno-economic assessments of living shorelines that integrate site-specific performance, lifecycle costs, and monetized cobenefits are important to enable consistent, comparable, and decision-relevant evaluations.

Interdisciplinary evidence demonstrating the shoreline protection, durability, and cost outcomes of living shoreline technologies can reduce the perceived risk associated with these technologies (95). While there are calls for engineers and ecologists to work more collaboratively on living shoreline implementation (96), there are few exemplars for this process. Functional design criteria for research programs or projects should be grounded in a nonsiloed framework that integrates disciplines from the outset and requires them to collaborate for success. Here, we present a program codesigned and delivered by ecologists, biologists, engineers, techno-economists, and industry to demonstrate the interdisciplinary teams required to move beyond a conventional engineered approach to a nature-based coastal protection approach. Similar cross-disciplinary collaboration, and engagement of experts beyond the primary research focus, is needed across other nature-based solutions to develop integrated, multifaceted approaches to complex global issues (97). Future teams involved in living shorelines may extend expertise further, for example, by including landscape architects and social scientists. The result is an innovative, sustainable solution with the potential to be scaled globally and to have broad applicability for the protection of built, natural, and cultural assets vulnerable to the increasing impacts of coastal hazards.
