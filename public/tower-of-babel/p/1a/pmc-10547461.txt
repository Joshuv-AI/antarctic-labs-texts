Bronchiolitis, Regardless of Its Etiology and Severity, Is Associated With Increased Risk of Asthma: A Population-Based Study
Authors: Cintia Muñoz-Quiles, Mónica López-Lacort, Javier Díez-Domingo, Alejandro Orrico-Sánchez
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn association exists between severe respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)–bronchiolitis and a subsequent increased risk of recurrent wheezing (RW) and asthma. However, a causal relationship remains unproven. Using a retrospective population-based cohort study (339 814 children), bronchiolitis during the first 2 years of life (regardless of etiology and severity) was associated with at least a 3-fold increased risk of RW/asthma at 2–4 years and an increased prevalence of asthma at ≥5 years of age. The risk was similar in children with mild bronchiolitis as in those with hospitalized RSV-bronchiolitis and was higher in children with hospitalized non-RSV-bronchiolitis. The rate of RW/asthma was higher when bronchiolitis occurred after the first 6 months of life. Our results seem to support the hypothesis of a shared predisposition to bronchiolitis (irrespective of etiology) and RW/asthma. However, 60% of hospitalized bronchiolitis cases in our setting are due to RSV, which should be paramount in decision-making on imminent RSV prevention strategies.Keywords: RSV, respiratory syncytial virus, asthma / recurrent wheezing, bronchiolitis, laboratory confirmation, retrospective cohort study

Bronchiolitis is the most common cause of respiratory-related hospital admissions in children <2 years of age, which represents a major public health problem worldwide [1, 2]. Multiple viral agents have been identified as causative of bronchiolitis, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is responsible for 60%–80% of cases of bronchiolitis requiring hospitalization [3–5].

The relationship between viral bronchiolitis and asthma has been widely studied [6]. Asthma constitutes the most common chronic reactive airway disease, related with significant healthcare burden [7, 8]. Although wheezing involving allergens seems more likely to persist than wheezing involving only viruses [9], respiratory viral infections that occur during childhood, when immune maturation is not yet fully developed, seem to predispose to chronic asthma [10, 11].

Several studies have shown an association between severe RSV-bronchiolitis and subsequent recurrent wheezing (RW) and asthma in later childhood [12–15]. Controversy exists as to whether RSV-bronchiolitis is a causal factor or a marker of susceptibility to respiratory illness (RW/asthma) [12, 16, 17]. Some studies have shown that bronchiolitis caused by viruses other than RSV, especially rhinovirus, would result in an even greater risk of asthma than RSV itself [11, 18–20]. If causality between early viral infection and development of asthma were proven, potential asthma preventive targets would be available. In the case of RSV, its potential role in RW and asthma would further highlight the need for interventions to reduce the burden of disease and would have a direct impact on the cost-effectiveness estimates of these interventions.

Therefore, understanding the regional trends in bronchiolitis, its etiology and severity, and its potential association with subsequent RW/asthma is crucial to assess the impact of the coming prevention strategies [21–23].

Most of the epidemiological studies on bronchiolitis are focused on hospitalizations [24–26]. However, as previously demonstrated using the Valencia Health System Integrated Database (VID) [27], >85% of the bronchiolitis cases in children <2 years of age are managed in the primary care (PC) setting [3]. This implies an underestimation when only hospitalizations are considered in epidemiological burden of disease studies [28–31]. Despite this high percentage, all studies assessing the association of bronchiolitis or RSV infections with RW and asthma focus only on severe hospitalized cases [6, 20]. Moreover, many of them are based on clinical diagnoses, without laboratory tests to confirm the etiology of bronchiolitis [12, 20], which can lead to misclassification.

The VID, in addition to the records of all PC and hospital contacts, includes the microbiology database, which allows near-real-time sharing of individual-level microbiological test results (including laboratory-confirmed RSV information) across the region.

This study analyzes the association between previous bronchiolitis (PB) (during the first 2 years of life), according to etiology (nonspecific, laboratory-confirmed RSV positive and RSV negative), severity (attended in PC or hospital admission), and age at the time of PB, and subsequent risk of RW/asthma in children aged >2 years, using the VID.

METHODS

Study Design, Population, and Setting

This was a population-based, retrospective cohort study using electronic healthcare registries. All children born between 2009 and 2015 residing in the Valencia region were included and followed up until 2019 or the cessation from the Public Health System (PHS) [27].

Children were excluded from the study if they (1) were born abroad or were not registered in the region due to lack of complete information; (2) had <6 months’ follow-up period; (3) were registered with ≥42 days of age, ensuring adherence to the PHS; (4) had codification errors; (5) were vaccinated with at least 1 dose of pneumococcal vaccine in private centers, because they might be private users with no visits registered in VID; or (6) had <1 year of follow-up after their second birthday.

Data Sources

Registries in VID have been described elsewhere [27]. The following registries were used: (1) The regional population-based administrative database, which collects demographic data, health services assignment, and usage of the health system; (2) the Ambulatory Care Information System (SIA), which contains medical information for each patient attended in the PC setting (general practitioners and specialists); (3) Care Provision Management, which gathers drug prescriptions and dispensations data using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification System (ATC); (4) the minimum basic data set (MBDS), which collects all diagnoses and procedures from hospitalizations; (5) the Microbiological Surveillance Network, which contains the results (positive and negative) of the microbiological analyses performed in the Valencian Health System; and (6) the Vaccine Information System, which stores all the information on vaccination. Both SIA and MBDS used the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification coding system (ICD-CM) for codification. All of these registries can be linked at the individual level through a unique personal identification number.

Previous Bronchiolitis Cohorts

Previous bronchiolitis was searched in children <2 years old and organized by etiology and severity into 5 cohorts as follows:

Severe RSV-PB: The first RSV-bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.11 or J21.0) or bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, or J21.9) in any diagnostic position with a laboratory-confirmed RSV result.

Severe RSV-PB: The first RSV-bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.11 or J21.0) or bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, or J21.9) in any diagnostic position with a laboratory-confirmed RSV result.

Severe non-RSV-PB: The first bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, or J21.9) in any diagnostic position with laboratory-negative RSV result.

Severe non-RSV-PB: The first bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, or J21.9) in any diagnostic position with laboratory-negative RSV result.

Severe miscellaneous-PB: The first bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, or J21.9) in any diagnostic position without laboratory-associated result.

Severe miscellaneous-PB: The first bronchiolitis hospitalization (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, or J21.9) in any diagnostic position without laboratory-associated result.

PC-PB: The first bronchiolitis-associated consultation at PC (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, J21.9, 466.11, or J21.0).

PC-PB: The first bronchiolitis-associated consultation at PC (ICD codes 466.1, 466.19, J21.8, J21.9, 466.11, or J21.0).

No-PB: Children with no registers of bronchiolitis either at PC or hospital settings.

No-PB: Children with no registers of bronchiolitis either at PC or hospital settings.

Outcome Case Definitions

The diagnosis of asthma in children <5 years of age is controversial, partly due to the difficulty of using the standardized tests (eg, spirometry) needed to confirm it. Moreover, the recurrent symptom of wheezing is common in children <5 years of age and is not necessarily an indicator of later asthma [32, 33]. Therefore, it is recommended to use a broader term “RW/asthma” rather than singular terms of “asthma” or “wheeze” to describe trajectories in children 0–5 years of age. Following these considerations, 3 outcomes of interest were defined:

RW/asthma: Children 2–4 years old with ≥3 RW-related ICD codes at least 1 month apart (466.XX, J21.X, J20, R0.62, J98.01, 519.11, 786.07) within a year or at least 1 asthma code (493.XX, J45, J46, J45.9, J45.8, J45.1, J45.0) in the follow-up period at hospital or PC.

RW/asthma: Children 2–4 years old with ≥3 RW-related ICD codes at least 1 month apart (466.XX, J21.X, J20, R0.62, J98.01, 519.11, 786.07) within a year or at least 1 asthma code (493.XX, J45, J46, J45.9, J45.8, J45.1, J45.0) in the follow-up period at hospital or PC.

Asthma: Children aged ≥5 years with at least 1 diagnostic code attributed to asthma from 5 years of age to the end of the follow-up (maximum age, 11 years).

Asthma: Children aged ≥5 years with at least 1 diagnostic code attributed to asthma from 5 years of age to the end of the follow-up (maximum age, 11 years).

Persistent asthma: Children aged ≥5 years with asthma (as defined above) and with a previous history of RW/asthma at the age of 2–4 years.

Persistent asthma: Children aged ≥5 years with asthma (as defined above) and with a previous history of RW/asthma at the age of 2–4 years.

A sensitivity analysis using more restrictive case definitions was assessed by adding the receipt of at least 1 anti-asthma drug prescription for obstructive airway diseases to both outcomes (RW/asthma and active asthma at 5 years).

Covariates

The following covariates were considered: year and month of birth, age at PB, sex, health department, anti-pneumococcal vaccination, antibiotic consumption in the first year of life (ATC code: J01), and high-risk preterm condition (defined as infants who required hospitalization for prematurity identified by shortened gestation and low-birthweight-related codes 765.XX or P07.XX in MBDS).

Data Analysis

General demographic characteristics are summarized overall and by PB cohort. Sex, year and month of birth, age at PB (0–5, 6–11, 12–17, 18–23), preterm condition, antibiotic consumption in the first year of life, and anti-pneumococcal vaccination are presented in proportions including frequencies. Follow-up duration in years and the age at PB in months are summarized by mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, and minimum–maximum range.

The association between PB and RW/asthma in children 2–4 years old was estimated by 2 logistic regression models. One assessed the likelihood of RW/asthma among children in the 5 PB cohorts. The second studied the same but stratified by the age at PB. Both models were also adjusted by prematurity, sex, antibiotic consumption in the first year of life, vaccination against pneumococcus, health department, and year and month of birth.

To assess the prevalence of asthma, we estimated the proportion of children ≥5 years old with asthma (including 95% confidence interval [CI]) overall, regarding the PB cohort and the previous history of RW/asthma.

Ethical Statement

The study protocol, observational in design and using retrospective anonymized nonidentifiable data transferred from the Valencia Ministry of Health to the research team according to Spanish laws and institutional requirements, was approved by the Ethics Committee of Arnau de Vilanova Hospital of Valencia. According to Spanish law, ethics approval informed consent is exempt for this kind of register-based research, and the Ethics Committee accepted the exemption.

Characteristics by Cohort

A total of 79 007 (27.5%) children had an episode of PB during the first 2 years of life and were included in 1 of the 4 cohorts with PB. Of them, 69 325 (87.8%) were attended at PC (PC-PB cohort). A total of 9682 (12.3%) were hospitalized, of which 5813 (60%) had RSV-bronchiolitis (severe RSV-PB cohort), 2475 (25.6%) belonged to the non-RSV-PB cohort, and 1394 (14.4%) belonged to the severe miscellaneous-PB cohort; 85.5% of the hospitalized cases and 43% of the PC-PB cases occurred during the first 6 months of life. The mean age of PB was 7.5 months for PC-PB, 2.5 months for severe RSV-PB, 3.3 months for severe non-RSV-PB, and 3.9 months for severe miscellaneous-PB cohorts. Hospitalized PB overall was more frequent among boys and among children born in September through January, especially for the severe RSV-PB cohort (Table 1). Antibiotic consumption during the first year of life and vaccination against pneumococcus were more frequent in children with PB compared to the no-PB cohort. These differences led us to adjust for all of these variables in the logistic regression models.

Risk of RW/Asthma (2–4 Years Old)

Sixteen percent of the overall cohort (44 655) had RW/asthma between 2 and 4 years of age (Table 2). The percentage was higher in the cohorts with PB (28%–36%) respective to the no-PB cohort (11%).

Asthma (≥5 Years Old)

Of the 249 081 children aged ≥5 years in our overall cohort, 15 831 (6.4%) registered an episode of asthma after their fifth birthday (Table 3). A total of 8821 (55.7%) asthma cases corresponded to children from the no-PB cohort, and 7010 (44.3%) corresponded to the cohorts with PB. Among children with PB, the percentage of children with asthma was higher in the severe non-RSV-PB and miscellaneous-PB cohorts (13% and 15%, respectively) than in the severe RSV-PB and PC-PB cohorts (9% and 10%, respectively). In the no-PB cohort the percentage of children with asthma was 5%.

Preterm Children

The preterm subcohort included 14 465 children. Demographic characteristics are described in Supplementary Table 5. Twenty-three percent of the overall cohort (3264) had RW/asthma between 2 and 4 years of age (Supplementary Table 6). The association between PB and risk of RW/asthma and asthma after age 5 years in the preterm cohort followed the same patterns as in the general population (Supplementary Tables 7–9). Of the 12 292 preterm children aged ≥5 years, 998 (8.1%) registered an episode of asthma after their fifth birthday (Supplementary Table 9).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first European study that evaluates the epidemiologic features of RSV-positive and -negative bronchiolitis and its association with subsequent RW/asthma using a large population-based cohort (339 814 children), including both hospitalizations and PC visits with a long-term follow-up period (7 years).

The main findings of this study are as follows: (1) Having bronchiolitis (regardless of etiology and severity) was associated with at least a 3-fold increased risk of RW/asthma at 2–4 years of age; (2) the risk was similar for children suffering mild PC-bronchiolitis than those with hospitalized RSV-bronchiolitis; (3) children with severe RSV-negative bronchiolitis were more likely to develop RW/asthma than children with severe RSV-bronchiolitis; (4) the risk of RW/asthma was higher when PB occurred at older ages; (5) prevalence of asthma in children ≥5 years old was similar in the severe RSV-PB and PC-PB cohorts and higher in the severe non-RSV-PB and miscellaneous-PB cohorts; and (6) risk of RW/asthma and prevalence of asthma were higher in preterm children. However, the association between PB (in terms of etiology and severity) and the risk of RW/asthma was similar to that of full-term infants.

The association between severe RSV-bronchiolitis and an increased risk of respiratory diseases later in life has been demonstrated [6, 18]. However, a causal relationship remains unproven [12, 16] and, alternatively, there may be a shared predisposition to severe bronchiolitis (caused by RSV or other viruses) and RW/asthma, and having a severe (or even mild) respiratory infection may be a marker for this predisposition.

The magnitude and details of the association between PB and RW/asthma vary between populations and has not been described in Spain using both hospitalization and PC registries [18, 34]. Our group described that >87% of the bronchiolitis in children younger than 2 years was attended in PC settings [3]. Here, we showed that the risk of suffering RW/asthma at 2–4 years and the prevalence of asthma after 5 years was similar in the cohorts of PC-PB and severe RSV-PB. These results are important as even mild bronchiolitis attended at PC may be a predictor of later respiratory morbidity (including asthma). Furthermore, in line with the results from the recent BARI (Burden of Acute Respiratory Infections) study [26], they also highlight the underestimation of disease burden when only hospitalizations are considered.

Our results suggest that around 28% of children had at least 1 episode of bronchiolitis during the first 2 years of life and 12% of them were hospitalized; the rest (88%) were treated in PC. Consistent with our previous studies, at least 60% of hospitalizations for bronchiolitis were due to RSV [3, 4].

Although RSV is the main cause of bronchiolitis in infants [5, 34, 35], other viruses such as RV, bocavirus, or even coinfections also play an important role [11, 34, 36–38]. Previous studies already showed that children hospitalized with bronchiolitis caused by other viruses than RSV developed RW/asthma at higher rates than children with RSV-bronchiolitis [20, 36, 39–41]. In a recent meta-analysis, rhinovirus-bronchiolitis resulted more strongly associated with RW/asthma than RSV-bronchiolitis [19]. Attempts have been made to identify distinct bronchiolitis subgroups in order to predict the profile of increased risk of developing asthma at different ages [22, 42, 43]. In a Spanish work considering only severe bronchiolitis [34], respiratory morbidity at 7–9 years of age was significantly higher in patients with single-rhinovirus or viral coinfection than in single-RSV infections. In the same line, our results showed higher prevalence of asthma after 5 years of age in the cohorts of severe miscellaneous and severe non-RSV-PB. Rhinovirus, among other viruses, could be the cause of these cases of bronchiolitis.

Surprisingly, our results indicated that the risk of RW/asthma was higher when PB occurred at older ages. One possible explanation could be that children who require hospitalizations for bronchiolitis after 6 months of age might have some underlying conditions that increase their susceptibility to develop asthma later in life. These results are in agreement with other studies where the burden of subsequent asthma was higher in children who developed severe RSV disease at age ≥6 months [7]. Our results evidenced that this occurred independent of the etiology of the bronchiolitis (RSV and non-RSV), which may support the susceptibility hypothesis in these children.

A higher proportion of preterm children experienced RW/asthma (23%) and asthma at ≥5 years of age (8%) compared to the overall cohort (16% and 6%, respectively). However, the association between PB and the risk of RW/asthma was similar to that of term infants, with higher risk in severe non-RSV and miscellaneous-PB cohorts than in PC-PB and severe RSV-PB. The impact of RSV hospitalization on future respiratory pathologies in preterm infants has been previously described [44–47]. However, controversy remains as to whether palivizumab prophylaxis improves the outcome of the premature infant in terms of RW/asthma risk [44], which does not allow a causal association between RSV infection and RW/asthma to be established.

The strengths of this study include the population-based design, the large sample, the individual-level data, and the availability of both PC and hospitalizations and positive and negative laboratory results for RSV-bronchiolitis. The VID has proven to be very reliable in previous epidemiological studies [3, 27, 48–50]. We were able to identify bronchiolitis and RW/asthma episodes treated in hospitals and PC and outpatient clinics all over the Valencia region, thus allowing a more complete outcome determination than studies relying on data only from hospitals [20, 41]. Among the limitations of the study, although the VID collects all RSV test results (positive and negative) from across the region, the tests are mostly requested during hospitalizations, preventing us from specifying the etiology of bronchiolitis in PC. Improvement of RSV testing and codification is crucial for the definition of evidence-based preventive policies. There was some variability in the tests used in the detection of RSV. About 30% of the results were obtained by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), 30% by immunochromatography, and about 10% by immunofluorescence; for about 30% of the results, the type of test used was not recorded. However, given that 60% of hospitalizations coded as bronchiolitis were associated with a positive result for RSV and that this coincides with the information recorded in our hospital network of active surveillance for respiratory viruses [4], in which multiplex PCRs are performed for all respiratory infections, we do not anticipate any misclassification in this regard. We cannot assess whether viral etiology during bronchiolitis is associated with different asthma phenotypes (atopic, nonatopic) as our definition of asthma encompasses all types of asthma. The preterm subcohort included infants at higher risk at birth who required hospitalization. Nonhospitalized infants and those without complications other than shortened gestation and low birth weight may not have been recorded as preterm. This may be the reason why the percentage of preterm infants in our study is lower than that published elsewhere. Although comorbidities were not included, pneumococcal vaccination and antibiotic treatment during the first year are indirect measures of healthcare-seeking behavior, indicating that children who visit the doctor more frequently are more likely to be vaccinated, are prescribed more antibiotics, and are likely to have more recorded episodes of bronchiolitis, RW, or asthma. These covariates have been used for the adjustment in or study. However, although we accounted for a considerable number of important confounders for the adjustment, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrated a strong association between RW/asthma and early bronchiolitis, irrespective of its severity and etiology. Having a bronchiolitis (regardless of etiology and severity) was associated with at least a 3-fold increased risk of RW/asthma up to age 5 years and an increased prevalence of asthma from 5 years compared to children without bronchiolitis. The risk of RW/asthma was similar in children with mild bronchiolitis as in those with hospitalized-RSV-bronchiolitis. But it was higher in children hospitalized with RSV-negative bronchiolitis. The rate of RW/asthma was higher when bronchiolitis occurred after the first 6 months of life. Our results seem to support the hypothesis of a shared predisposition to bronchiolitis (irrespective of etiology) and RW/asthma. However, it is the severity and high disease burden of RSV-bronchiolitis (86% of hospitalizations for bronchiolitis occur in children younger than 6 months, and 60% of hospitalized bronchiolitis in our setting are due to RSV) that should be paramount in decision making about imminent RSV prevention strategies.
