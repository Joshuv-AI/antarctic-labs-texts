Switchable Amination Pathways of Chromones via Iodonium Salt/Amine Modulation
Authors: Shuoshuo Zhang, Zhifang Yang, Haofeng Shi, Xuemin Li, Hui Zhao, Yunfei Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Iodonium salts have shown great potential as metal-free reagents in various transformations, offering a promising alternative to transition-metal-catalyzed arene amination. Herein, we report the design and synthesis of chromone-based iodonium salts (CMISs) and demonstrate their efficiency in selective chromone amination. Unlike the conventional ligand exchange/coupling pathway, mechanistic studies reveal a unique amination pathway specific to the chromone scaffold, in which the aryliodonium unit simultaneously facilitates N-Michael addition and the formation of a newfound chromone-derived strained alkyne intermediate, leading to mechanistic competition while directing product configuration. By further modulating the basicity and steric hindrance of amines, three distinct classes of aminated products ((Z)-2-(aminomethylene)­benzofuranones, β-aminochromones and α-aminochromones) were accessed.

Amino scaffolds are widely present in bioactive molecules and advanced materials, − continually motivating the development of new methods for amine incorporation into diverse substrates. Transition-metal-catalyzed reactions have greatly advanced this field and remain the dominant approach, − yet the high cost of catalysts and the requirement for complex ligands also make the search for simple, low-cost, and particularly metal-free alternative strategies meaningful. − Among these, λ3-iodanes has emerged as a promising option, where the weak and highly polarized C–I–C bond, electron-deficient iodonium moiety and etc., − ,− enables a pioneering transition-metal-mimetic pathway, leading to significant advances in the metal-free synthesis of complex small molecules − and the efficient amination of specific compounds. − Within this context, iodonium salts, an important branch of λ3-iodanes, have offered a powerful approach for in situ amination. − , Representative studies by the Sandtorv and Stuart (2016) and the Olofsson team (2018) achieved the selective amination of benzene ring using diaryliodonium salts and innovatively proposed a ligand exchange/coupling mechanism (Figure A). More recently, the Novák group (2022) reported the amination of alkanes using designed hydrofluoroolefin-based iodonium salts. Collectively, these approaches offer a simple, mild and metal-free alternative to transition-metal catalysis; therefore, the synthesis of iodonium salts bearing potential pharmacophores and exploration of their use in mild, straightforward amination applications hold considerable promise.

Chromones, as privileged pharmacophores in natural products, exhibit diverse bioactivities, including antitumor − and antimicrobial, − etc. − As fundamental scaffolds in drug discovery, , chromones have attracted extensive research interest, and significant progress has been made in their functionalization. − Nonetheless, systematic studies on the amination of chromones remain scarce to date. − Previous halogen-based methods have demonstrated preliminary success in synthesizing azolylated chromone derivative. , However, efficient strategies for the assembly of other amine-derived chromone derivatives remain underdeveloped. Based on the advantages of hypervalent iodine reagents in past amine incorporation, we questioned whether they could enable efficient coupling between chromones and amines.

Herein (Figure B), we designed and synthesized a series of chromone-based iodonium salts (CMISs) by introducing an aryliodonium moiety at the α-position of chromone scaffold. Leveraging the high reactivity of iodonium salts, we successfully achieved the amination of chromones. Notably, in contrast to the conventional ligand exchange/coupling mechanism, our mechanistic investigations revealed a unique amination pathway specific to the chromone scaffold, in which, the aryliodonium moiety simultaneously facilitates N-Michael addition and the formation of a newfound chromone-derived strained alkyne intermediate, leading to mechanistic competition. Additionally, its considerable steric hindrance was found to influence both mechanistic pathways and product configuration. By modulating the amine’s basicity and steric hindrance, three distinct reaction pathways were enabled, yielding three classes of aminated chromone derivatives: (i) N-Michael/rearrangement products, (Z)-2-(aminomethylene)-benzofuranones; (ii) N-Michael addition/elimination products, β-aminochromones; and (iii) α-aminochromones formed via newfound chromone-based alkyne intermediates.

This study commenced with the synthesis of CMISs (Figure ). A systematic ligand screening was performed using a range of arenes, including mesitylene, toluene, benzene, chlorobenzene, bromobenzene and fluorobenzene. Among them, chlorobenzene exhibited the highest reactivity, affording the target CMIS c1e in 89% yield. Notably, the reaction proceeded efficiently on a gram scale, providing c1e in 83% yield. Subsequently, the substrate scope of chromones bearing various substituents was explored under the optimized conditions. Chromones containing halogens (F, Cl, Br), methyl, cyano or methoxy groups were well tolerated, delivering the corresponding iodonium salts c2-13 in yields ranging from 67% to 90%. Furthermore, the structure of iodonium salt c9 was confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis.

To assess the amination protocol, aniline and 1-phenylpiperazine were chosen as representative substrates. Both amines underwent initial nucleophilic substitution with iodonium salt c1e in 1,4-dioxane using triethylamine (TEA) as base under Ar (Table ). The molecular structures of the resulting products were confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis. Notably, 1-phenylpiperazine afforded α-aminochromone f1 in 83% yield (entry 1a), while the reaction with amine unexpectedly generated the benzofuranone derivative e1 in 76% yield (entry 1). Subsequently, the reaction conditions for the two pathways were optimized separately, focusing primarily on the solvent and base. For aniline, preliminary solvent screening (1,4-dioxane, THF, toluene, DMSO, DMF, MeCN, DCE; entries 1–7) identified toluene as the optimal medium, affording e1 in 90% yield (entry 3). Subsequent base evaluation (BDMEP, DBU, t-BuOK, Cs2CO3; entries 8–11) confirmed TEA to be the most effective additive. Furthermore, replacing the iodonium salt c1e with its p-fluorophenyl analogue c1f led to a reduced yield of e1 (84%, entry 12). For 1-phenylpiperazine, the 1,4-dioxane/TEA system was demonstrated to be the optimal choice (entry 1a). Conversely, the above system for aniline afforded product f1 in only 69% yield (entry 1b).

Upon the optimizing reaction conditions, a comprehensive evaluation of the substrate scope was performed using a range of arylamine derivatives (Figure ). The methodology exhibited excellent functional group tolerance toward p-substituted anilines, accommodating a broad range of substituents, including electron-donating groups (t-butyl, methoxy, methyl), phenyl, halogens (Cl, Br), and electron-withdrawing moieties (methoxycarbonyl, cyano). All substrates afforded the corresponding benzofuranone derivatives e2-9 in yields of 72–93%. Notably, the structures of compounds e1 and e3 were confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis, revealing adoption of the Z-configuration. To evaluate the effect of positional isomerism, anilines bearing m-nitro/chloro and o-methyl/iodo substituents were examined. All substrates successfully afforded the corresponding products e10-13 in 71–84% yields, indicating that the position of the substituents had little influence on the reaction. Notably, NMR characterization of the ortho-substituted derivatives e12 and e13 revealed two distinct sets of proton resonances, suggesting the presence of atropisomerism likely due to restricted rotation between the C6 and C14 substituents. The scope of the methodology was further expanded to poly substituted anilines and heteroaromatic amines under standard conditions. Substrates including 5-amino-1,3-benzodioxole, 3-chloro-4-fluoroaniline, 3,4,5-trifluoroaniline, 2-aminopyridine, 1-naphthylamine, and 2-naphthylamine afforded products e14-19 in 75–88% yields. Moreover, the product e17 generated from 1-naphthylamine also exhibits pronounced atropisomerism. N-Methylaniline was also compatible, affording product e20 in 56% yield. To our disappoint-ment, diarylamines such as diphenylamine, bis­(4-methoxy-phenyl)­amine and bis­(4-chlorophenyl)­amine failed to deliver the desired products under the standard conditions in each case. Furthermore, substituted CMISs showed good reactivity. Halogen-substituted iodonium salts c5-6 and c10, 6-methyl-substituted salt c3 and polysubstituted salts c11-13 reacted efficiently with aniline, affording products e21-27 in 72–87% yields.

We next explored the reaction scope with aliphatic amines under standard conditions B (Figure ). A variety of primary amines, including cyclohexylamine, cyclopropylamine, benzylamine, cyclo-hexylmethylamine and 2-thiophenemethylamine, produced the corresponding (Z)-2-(aminomethylene)­benzofuranones e28-32 in 68–84% yield. Notably, the structure of compound e28 was unambiguously confirmed by X-ray diffraction analysis, which also confirmed its Z-configuration. Differently, diethylamine, a secondary amine, produced β-aminochromone g1 with no isolable benzofuranone byproduct. We then examined other secondary amines, such as pyrrolidine and N-methylcyclohexylamine, which also afforded only the corresponding β-aminochromones g2-3. We speculate that this reaction selectivity may be related to the basicity of the amine. Subsequently, we optimized the reaction conditions for this class of transformations, and the results showed that replacing TEA with Cs2CO3 significantly improved the yields, affording β-aminochromones g1-3 in 74–81% yields. Next, various CMISs were reacted with aliphatic amines. The reaction of 6-methyl-substituted iodonium salt c3 with cyclohexanamine produced e33 in 81% yield. Intriguingly, 6-bromo-substituted iodonium salt c5 exhibited divergent reactivity, producing both benzofuranone derivative e34 in 70% yield and β-aminochromone g4 in 21% yield. This result may result from electronic effects: the electron-withdrawing bromo substituent reduces the nucleophilicity of the p-oxygen, suppressing oxygen migration and favoring formation of g4. This hypothesis was further validated by employing iodonium salts bearing stronger electron-withdrawing groups (c6 with 6-chloro and c7 with 6-cyano). An inverse correlation was observed between the electron-withdrawing strength and the yields of rearranged products (e35-36, 58–30%). Concurrently, yields of competing β-aminochromones increased (g5-6, 35–57%). Moreover, 5-methyl substitution in c11 partially counteracted the electron-withdrawing effect of the 6-chloro substituent, resulting in benzofuranone derivative e37 in 81% yield. The substrate scope of CMISs was further extended to system involving secondary amines. Under optimized conditions C, 6-methyl/chloro-substituted iodonium salts c3 and c6 reacted with diethylamine or N-methylcyclohexanamine, respectively, to produce the corresponding β-amino products g7-8 in 74–77% yields.

Azole derivatives, particularly triazole, have recently emerged as valuable building blocks for the structural diversification of chromone scaffolds. , This synthetic advantage originates from its excellent leaving group ability, which enables efficient β-functionalization of the chromone core. , In previous studies on the β-azolation of chromones, Antonchick’s group employed an I2-catalyzed thermal process using chromones as substrates, whereas Sugita’s group achieved β-azolation by reacting α-iodochromones with azoles under heating. Furthermore, Liu’s group developed a copper-catalyzed system to synthesize the corresponding products from enaminones. In comparison, our methodology enables efficient chromone azolation under metal-free, room-temperature and mild conditions, affording the desired products in higher yields and with a broader substrate scope (Figure ). We first evaluated the reactivity of azoles, including 1,2-diazole, 1,3-diazole and 1,2,4-triazole. In addition, a range of benzazole derivatives, such as indazole, 3-bromoindazole, benzotriazole, 1H-pyrazolo­[4,3-c]­pyridine, 4-azaindazole and 6-chloro-1H-indazole, were also examined. These reactions afforded β-azolylated chromones g9-17 in 62–92% yields. Subsequently, the scope of iodonium salts was investigated. Compatibility studies with azoles demonstrated good functional-group tolerance; both the 6-chloro- and methyl-substituted iodonium salts (c3 and c6) reacted efficiently with indazole and pyrazole to furnish compounds g18-20 in 84–93% yield.

To further evaluate the chemoselectivity of these transformations, a one-pot reaction was conducted using iodonium salt c1e in the presence of aniline, pyrazole and diethylamine (Figure ). The results indicated that the diethylamine-derived product g1 was obtained in the highest yield (53%), followed by the pyrazole-derived compound g9 (23%), whereas the aniline-derived product e1 was formed in only 12% yield. Furthermore, a comparable selectivity pattern was observed in the reaction involving only aniline and pyrazole, affording g9 in 49% yield and e1 in 33% yield. These results suggest that the reaction preferentially proceeds through the pathway mediated by the more nucleophilic amine. Furthermore, sterically hindered N-heterocycle 1-phenyl-piperazine, which failed to afford the corresponding β-amino product in the initial optimization stage, could be efficiently transformed by leveraging the good leaving ability of the triazole moiety. The synthesized compound g11 thus enabled access to the desired β-amino products (Figure ). In the presence of K2CO3, a variety of N-heterocycles, including 1-phenylpiperazine, piperidine, 2-methyl-piperidine, ethyl 4-piperidinecarboxylate, 1,2,3,4-tetrahydroisoquinoline and 1,4-oxazepane, reacted smoothly with g11 to furnish the corresponding β-amino products g21-26 in 68–94% yield.

Initial validation using 1-phenylpiperazine under TEA/1,4-dioxane systems afforded α-aminochromone f1 in 83% yield. We then explored the substrate scope of N-heterocycles under the standard conditions D (Figure ). Six-membered N-heterocycles bearing various substituents, such as 1-acylpiperazine, morpholine, thiomorpholine and 2-phenylmorpholine, were well tolerated, affording products f2-5 in 67–82% yields. However, reactions with piperidine derivatives revealed notable differences in regioselectivity. The unsubstituted piperidine delivered α-aminated product f6 in 43% yield alongside β-aminochromone g21 in 37% yield. Further investigation revealed that steric hindrance from a 4-substituent on the piperidine ring significantly influenced the α/β-product ratio. Piperidine derivatives bearing methyl or carbethoxy groups favor α-amination (f7-8, 57–64%) while strongly suppressing β-aminochromone formation (g22-23, 18% to trace). In contrast, 2-substitution exerted minimal influence on regioselectivity. For example, 2-methylpiperidine provided both products in comparable yields (f9, 34%; g24, 39%). The observed regioselectivity likely originates from steric interactions between the chromone scaffold and the aryliodonium moiety (Figure A). Steric hindrance prevents bulky amines from approaching the β-position, thereby favoring α-selective amination. Furthermore, benzannulated systems including tetrahydroisoquinoline and 1-phenyltetrahydroisoquinoline, reacted smoothly with CMIS c1e, giving products f10-11 in 62–67% yields. The protocol was also applicable to seven-membered 1,4-oxazepane, affording f12 in 62% yield. To examine electronic effects, we employed iodonium salts bearing various substituents, including electron-withdrawing (c2 with 5-fluoro), electron-donating (c3 with 6-methyl and c8 with 7-methoxy), disubstituted (c11 with 6-chloro and 7-methyl) and extended aromatic (c13, naphthyl) motifs. All these CMISs exhibited good reactivity with thiomorpholine and 1-phenylpiperazine, affording the corresponding α-aminochromones f13-18 in 68–94% yields without apparent electronic effect.

Finally, systematic mechanistic investigations were conducted to elucidate the formation pathways of the three distinct products (Figures and ). Diethylamine and aniline were first employed as model substrates to explore the divergent formation pathways of (Z)-2-(aminomethylene)-benzofuranone e1 and β-aminochromone g1. Deuterium-labeling experiments (Figure i) confirmed that the β-position of the chromone scaffold serves as the nucleophilic amination site for e1 in the rearrangement pathway. Therefore, we infer that both aniline and diethylamine initially undergo N-Michael addition to CMIS c1e. Regarding the divergence in product formation, we hypothesize that it may be related to the basicity of the amines. After the N-Michael addition, the higher basicity of diethylamine promotes β-elimination, leading to β-aminochromone g. In contrast, aniline, with its insufficient basicity, fails to undergo the elimination reaction, while the highly reactive aryliodonium moiety induces a nitrogen-assisted [1,2]-σ shift, resulting in the formation of the rearranged product e. To test this hypothesis, the strongly basic primary amine t-butylamine was selected. As expected (Figure ii), its reaction with CMIS c1e afforded trace amounts of the rearranged product e38 (detected by TLC and HRMS) and instead produced β-aminochromone g28 in 72% yield. To further confirm the mechanistic rationale, we next examined whether the rearranged product could form when the elimination pathway was suppressed. For this purpose, a β-methyl-substituted CMIS c14 was designed to prevent β-elimination (Figure iii). As expected, β-methyl-CMIS c14 reacted with N-methylcyclohexylamine to afford exclusively the rearranged product e39 in 70% yield. In addition, the potential interconversion between compounds e and g was examined, but no conversion was observed under either acidic or basic conditions. Consequently, plausible mechanistic pathways were proposed (Figure A): both amines first undergo N-Michael addition to the α,β-unsaturated ketone motif of CMIS c1e, affording intermediates g1a and e1a. In the strongly basic dimethylamine system (path A), intermediate g1a undergoes β-elimination to form intermediate g1b, which subsequently furnishes β-aminochromone g1 via proton dissociation. In contrast, this elimination is not feasible with weakly basic aniline. Here, the aryliodonium unit serves as a leaving group, enabling a nitrogen-assisted [1,2]-σ shift to give intermediate e1b, followed by olefin isomerization to afford the stable rearranged product e1. Meanwhile, the formation of Z-configuration in compound e can be likely attributed to the steric effects within the intermediate. In intermediate e1a, the bulky aryliodonium moiety imposes significant steric constraints that restrict rotation around the C–N bond, thereby orienting the phenyl ring away from the iodonium center. Subsequently, the excellent-leaving aryliodonium group facilitates the [1,2]-σ migration, which preferentially furnishes the Z-configured product. Together, these mechanisms provide a plausible rationale for the previously observed suppression of rearranged products under diminished oxygen nucleophilicity.

Mechanistic studies for (Z)-2-(aminomethylene)-benzofuranone e and β-aminochromone g.

To elucidate the formation mechanism of the α-amino products, two probable pathways were proposed (Figure i): Path C, involving an N-Michael addition/annulation/elimination cascade, and Path D, featuring an aryliodonium-mediated ligand exchange/coupling cascade. Theoretically, neither was expected to display significant β-substituent effects; however, control experiments revealed clear inconsistencies (Figure ii). β-Methyl-CMIS c14 failed to furnish α-amino product f with either N-phenylpiperazine or thiomorpholine, affording instead the Z-configured rearrangement products e40-41 in 41–45% yields, whose structure was unambiguously confirmed by X-ray diffraction. In addition, β-tetrahydropyran-CMIS c15, possessing greater steric bulk in β-position, was completely unreactive toward either amine, underscoring the critical dependence of α-amino product formation on β-hydrogen accessibility and steric effects. The addition of TEMPO or BHT exerted minimal influence, rendering a radical pathway unlikely. Notably, the iodonium salts exhibited pronounced instability under basic conditions, accompanied by substantial formation of p-chloroiodobenzene. Furthermore, we noticed that the linear α,β-unsaturated carbonyl-containing iodonium salt h could afford the target alkyne i in 93% yield using TEA as the sole additive (Figure iii), consistent with a β-elimination facilitated by excellent leaving-group nature of the aryliodonium moiety. ,,,, These observations collectively point to the involvement of a chromone-derived strained alkyne intermediate. To probe its existence, furan was employed as an alkyne trap and gratifyingly, the characteristic molecular-ion peak of the Diels–Alder adduct j was detected by HRMS under condition D, with signal enhancement in the LiHMDS/toluene system (Figure iii, isolation of adduct j remains challenging; the complexity of the reaction mixture may arise from the pronounced ring strain of the aryne intermediate, − which facilitates C–O bond cleavage, and further investigations are underway). Based on these considerations, we herein propose a plausible mechanistic pathway involving a strained alkyne intermediate (Figure B), wherein steric congestion between the chromone framework and the aryliodonium moiety, particularly in the presence of a bulky six-membered N-heterocycle, suppresses the N-Michael addition, while base-induced β-elimination generates a chromone-based strained alkyne intermediate fa that undergoes regioselective α-addition of the amine. Moreover, we attribute the observed nucleophilic addition selectivity between the amine and the strained ring to the electron-withdrawing inductive effect − exerted by the ring oxygen atom. Further investigations into this chromone-based oxygen-containing strained alkyne are currently underway in our laboratory.

In summary, we have developed a distinct, metal-free strategy that enables switchable amination of chromones through rational CMIS/amine modulation. This work represents the first demonstration of controlling reaction pathways through the electronic effects, steric hindrance, and leaving-group ability of the aryliodonium moiety, enabling access to three divergent chromone-derived scaffolds. Moreover, we have revealed a general trend in reaction selectivity: arylamines and weakly basic primary amines preferentially deliver the rearranged products, more strongly basic secondary amines favor the formation of β-aminochromones, whereas sterically hindered N-heterocycles predominantly furnish α-aminochromones. Notably, the discovery of a novel chromone-based strained alkyne intermediate expands the fundamental reactivity profile of iodonium salts and introduces a powerful platform for chromone-core modification. Given its operational simplicity and broad functional-group tolerance, this strategy shows considerable promise for future applications in late-stage functionalization.
