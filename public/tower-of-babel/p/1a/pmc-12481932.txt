Prevalence of antibiotic-resistant Salmonella in kratom: Three-year monitoring study
Authors: Vaclav Peroutka, Katerina Demnerova, Jana Kadava, Simona Lencova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesKratom, powder from Mitragyna speciosa leaves, is a popular psychoactive drug. It poses a health risk to consumers not only because of its narcotic and stimulating effects, but also because its quality is not controlled by the same laws as other food and pharmaceutical products. Due to the absence of mandatory quality control, kratom carries an increased risk of pathogenic bacteria contamination. Based on previous studies, the most relevant of these from a public health perspective are bacteria of the genus Salmonella, particularly serotypes that may cause severe gastrointestinal illness and exhibit resistance to antimicrobial agents. To assess the scale of the problem, we monitored the occurrence of Salmonella in commercially available kratom in the Czech Republic and determined its antimicrobial resistance.Study designOriginal microbiological research (long-term monitoring).MethodsIn 2022–2024, we tested 96 different kratom samples, from which Salmonella was isolated by the cultivation method according to EN ISO 6579. Series of biochemical and agglutination tests were used to determine the species, subspecies and serotypes. The disk diffusion method was used for testing susceptibility to 16 antimicrobial agents, and the results were interpreted using EUCAST and CLSI criteria.ResultsSalmonella was isolated from 16 out of all 96 tested samples. Biochemical and agglutination tests revealed that Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica was the most prevalent species and subspecies (14 samples), with some of the identified serotypes being relatively rare. Regarding the susceptibility to antimicrobial agents, out of all isolates 15 were resistant to at least one tested antimicrobial agent, and three isolates were classified as multidrug resistant.ConclusionsBased on our analysis, the rate of Salmonella contamination did not differ statistically over the three years of study. Our results highlight the need for the continuous monitoring of Salmonella in kratom and provide a basis for the implementation of legal restrictions of its sale.Keywords: Kratom, Food legislation, Salmonella, Antibiotic resistance

Introduction

Kratom is a psychoactive substance that is traditionally prepared by grinding the leaves of the Mitragyna speciosa plant. The psychostimulant and narcotic effects of kratom are mainly attributed to its primary alkaloid mitragynine, along with 7-hydroxymitragynine, a more potent active metabolite present in smaller quantities [1,2]. Kratom is widely available for sale on the internet and its consumption is particularly popular among young people. Apart from availability, this is due to its simple administration and low cost, together with the lack of legislation prohibiting its use in many countries or states. According to Schimmel et al. [3], kratom consumption among the US adult population reached 0.8 %, over two million people, in 2020 [3].

Because kratom powder is usually consumed orally, without heat treatment, after mixing with both cold and warm water, tea, sweetened drinks or food [4], it attracted the attention of the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The FDA identified kratom as a new dietary ingredient and, with insufficient data to determine the health risks it poses, declared it an unsafe food additive. Its use in food and food supplements is considered food adulteration.

At the time of writing this article, the sale of kratom for direct consumption is banned in many European countries [[5], [6], [7], [8]]; however there are exceptions. In Albania, Greece, Hungary, Spain and the Netherlands, the use of kratom is unregulated, thus legal [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. In other countries, such as the Czech Republic and Germany, the sale and possession of kratom are permitted provided that the product is labelled as not intended for human consumption [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. This legal workaround often results in kratom being marketed for "collector's purposes" or similar non-consumable uses. The information regarding kratom's legal status is based on data from multiple different servers and should be treated with caution, as national regulations are evolving rapidly. Currently there is ongoing debate at both the European Parliament and member state levels on whether to impose a ban on kratom or regulate it within the framework of EU General Food Law. Under the latter approach, kratom would be subject to strict hygiene standards and monitored through the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (RASFF), thereby enhancing consumer safety.

Kratom toxicity is primarily assessed in terms of microbial contamination. The microbial contaminants of kratom typically include Salmonella, a gram-negative bacterium from the family Enterobacteriaceae and one of the most dangerous foodborne pathogens in developed countries [9,10]. In 2018, the FDA reported an outbreak of gastrointestinal illnesses caused by Salmonella and linked it with kratom consumption: of 199 cases, 54 patients were hospitalized, though with no related deaths.

To date, few kratom toxicity risk studies have been published, and those that have are all based on US data. They present something of a mixed picture. In 2019, Dixon et al. [11] confirmed the presence of Salmonella enterica in three out of 16 kratom samples purchased on the US market. Conversely, Prozialeck et al. [12], testing eight US samples also purchased in 2019, reported that no Salmonella was present, but that seven contained significant heavy metal levels. More recently, in a study of 76 FDA-collected samples, 55 % tested positive for Salmonella [13]. Overall, the impression is that the greater risk is of ingesting Salmonella-contaminated kratom, but, as shown above, the published studies are very limited. The situation is that kratom is not sufficiently routinely monitored, with this even being more the case in Europe.

To deepen knowledge in this area and contribute to an objective assessment of the microbial risks posed by kratom, we conducted what we believe is the first study of the prevalence of antibiotic-resistant Salmonella in kratom in Europe. For three years (2022–2024), we monitored the availability of commercial kratom samples in the Czech Republic, testing a set of 96 different samples for the presence of Salmonella. The isolates were identified and then subjected to antibiotic susceptibility analysis to 16 commonly used antimicrobial agents.

Methods

Kratom samples

Between 2022 and 2024, 96 different commercial kratom samples were taken from the market network. The samples were stored in dark at room temperature (21 °C), sterilely divided into aliquots, and analysed immediately after obtaining.

Detection of Salmonella in kratom samples

Qualitative analysis of kratom samples was provided according to the European standard EN ISO 6579–1:2017 (560088): Microbiology of the food chain - Horizontal method for detection, counting and serotyping of bacteria of the genus Salmonella - Part 1: Detection of bacteria of the genus Salmonella. Briefly, the samples (25 g) were non-selectively multiplicated in 225 ml of buffered peptone water and cultivated (37 °C, 24 h). The resuscitated samples were selectively enriched in (i) Muller Kaufmann broth (Merck, Germany) at 37 °C for 24 h, and (ii) Rappaport Vassiliadis broth (Merck, Germany) at 41.5 °C for 24 h. The use of both enrichment broths follows ISO 6579–1:2017 recommendations and increases the likelihood of recovering diverse Salmonella serotypes by combining different selective pressures. Then, the suspensions were inoculated onto solidified selective diagnostic media Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate agar (XLD; Merck, Germany) and Chromocult RAMBACH™ Agar (Merck, Germany) and cultivated (37 °C, 24 h).

Characterisation of bacterial isolates

First tentative identification of the isolated bacteria was provided using the ENTEROtest 24 N conventional kit (Erba Lachema, Czech Republic) – a non-reagent kit containing 24 biochemical tests designed for identification of Enterobacteriaceae strains within 24 h. E. Coli ATCC25922 was used as an internal quality control. Although molecular methods such as PCR or MALDI-TOF offer higher specificity and speed, ENTEROtest 24 N remains a validated and cost-effective option for initial identification in routine screening workflows, especially when followed by confirmatory serotyping. A suspension was prepared from a 24 h pure culture, homogenized, and adjusted to a turbidity of 1 McFarland standard. According to standard protocol the suspension (0.1 ml) was inoculated into wells of the ENTEROtest 24 N plate and incubated (37 °C, 24 h).

Serotyping of Salmonella isolates was performed according to ISO 6579–1:2017 and ISO 6579–3:2014 by the National Reference Laboratory for Salmonella, Czech National Institute of Public Health (NIPH) accredited under ČSN EN ISO 15189. To maximize resolution and minimize the risk of cross-reactivity, a comprehensive panel of commercial O-, H-, and Vi-antisera was applied. Slide agglutination assays for somatic and flagellar antigens and tube agglutination assays for phase variants were used in combination. Although molecular serotyping was not conducted, this multi-phase agglutination protocol is the standard method for reliable Salmonella serotype identification and is routinely used in national surveillance systems. In accordance with the ISO method, Salmonella Typhimurium ATCC 14028, Salmonella Enteritidis ATCC 13076 and Salmonella Paratyphi A ATCC 9150 were used as primary quality control for testing, and E. coli ATCC25922 was used as a negative control.

Testing the sensitivity of isolates to antimicrobial agents

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed for all 16 Salmonella isolates using the disc diffusion method in accordance with the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, Version 14.0, valid from 2024 to 01-01). Where EUCAST breakpoints were unavailable, interpretive criteria from the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI M100, 27th edition) were applied. The panel of 16 antimicrobial agents was selected to represent all major antimicrobial classes relevant to Salmonella spp., including those commonly used in clinical treatment and surveillance. Among the tested antimicrobial agents were nalidixic acid, gentamicin, streptomycin, ciprofloxacin sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, cefotaxime, neomycin, meropenem, compound sulfonamide, trimethoprim, kanamycin, ampicillin, tetracycline, chloramphenicol, amikacin and colistin. Bacterial suspensions were prepared from 24-h pure cultures, adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard, and spread onto the Mueller-Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK). Antibiotic discs were applied immediately. After incubation at 35 °C for 18 h, inhibition zone diameters were measured and interpreted according to EUCAST or CLSI standards. Susceptible Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as a quality control strain for test validation.

Results

Out of 96 analysed kratom samples, 16 (16.7 %) were found to be contaminated with bacteria identified through subsequent analyses as Salmonella species. Information regarding the production and import of these samples is either unavailable or similarly to the data on their origin lacks sufficient transparency and reliability. However, the proportion of Salmonella-positive kratom samples has not changed significantly from year to year. Specifically, Salmonella was detected in 3 of 19 samples in 2022 (15.8 %), 9 of 53 samples in 2023 (17.0 %), and 4 of 24 samples in 2024 (16.7 %). A Cochran-Armitage test for trend was performed to assess whether the proportion of positive samples changed significantly over time. The result was not statistically significant (p > 0.95), indicating no clear upward or downward trend during the study period, suggesting that the contamination rate is consistent among kratom products available on the Czech market.

The summary of isolated Salmonella species, including their serotypes and antigenic patterns, is presented in Table 1. Among the 16 isolates, all were identified as Salmonella enterica. The majority (14/16) belonged to subspecies enterica (subspecies I), while 2 isolates were identified as subspecies houtenae (subspecies IV). Serotyping revealed diverse antigenic profiles including variants that are relatively uncommon in human medicine. The most frequent serotype was S. Weltevreden (5 isolates), followed by S. Okatie (4 isolates). One isolate (4,5,12:b:) is likely monophasic S. Paratyphi B or S. Abony, based on its antigenic formula. Other detected serotypes included Oslo, Newport, Thompson, and Orientalis.

Discussion

Monitoring the prevalence of Salmonella in kratom over a three-year period (2022–2024) has yielded crucial insights into the microbial risks associated with this increasingly popular product. Among the 96 kratom samples tested in the Czech Republic, 16 (16.7 %) were positive for Salmonella enterica. Although this level of contamination represents a significant public health risk compared to an earlier study (Nsubuga et al.) [13] where 42 of 76 samples (55.2 %) were positive, this is a relatively small interception. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the geographical origin, quality of the tested kratom samples, differences in sample handling, testing protocols. However, it is likely primarily due to the context of the aforementioned study which was conducted in response to a specific outbreak of salmonellosis in 2018 that led to 54 hospitalizations in the United States linked to contaminated kratom. At the time of this epidemic a larger quantity of heavily contaminated kratom probably entered the market at one point in time. In contrast, our current study represents a longitudinal investigation carried out during a period without a known epidemic of Salmonella-induced gastroenteritis. As such, a lower prevalence of contamination is expected. Furthermore, our findings align with those of Dixon et al. [11], who reported a contamination rate of 18.75 % (3 out of 16 samples) in their study, despite the smaller sample size. The consistency of these results supports the validity of the present study.

All Salmonella isolates captured during the course of our study belonged to the species Salmonella enterica, and all but two subsp. houtenae were also of subsp. enterica. Serotyping revealed a diverse array of serotypes, including Weltevreden (5 isolates), Okatie (4 isolates), Orientalis (1), Oslo (1), Thompson (1), Newport (1). Based on antigenic formula (4,5,12:b:), one isolate was classified as likely monophasic S. Paratyphi B or S. Abony. Typhimurium, Newport, and Thompson are well-documented causes of human salmonellosis [14,15], while Weltevreden and Okatie, though less common in Europe, have been increasingly reported in Southeast Asia and are recognized as emerging foodborne pathogens with zoonotic potential [11,16,17]. Notably, a study by Schwensohn et al. [15] examining the 2018 kratom-linked United States outbreak identified several serotypes consistent with our findings, such as the monophasic 4,[5],12:b: , Okatie, Weltevreden, and Thompson. The repeated presence of the very specific monophasic serotype 4,5,12:b: may also reflect the geographical origin of kratom and sources of environmental contamination during processing. Weltevreden serotype was also previously isolated from kratom by Dixon et al. [11] and has been identified as a quite common foodborne pathogen in the past, particularly in Southeast Asian countries where it is increasingly isolated from vegetables, meat and seafood [16,17]. Many of isolated serotypes are generally associated with zoonotic infections in domestic and wild animals, specifically birds and monkeys [18]. This suggests that kratom contamination may originate from contact with animal faeces during production. Such contamination could plausibly occur during the drying of Mitragyna speciosa leaves. However, contamination may also be due to inadequate or completely absent hygiene measures during production, including the use of contaminated water for rinsing [19].

The antimicrobial susceptibility analysis of the Salmonella isolates revealed notable resistance patterns. Among the 16 isolates, 15 showed resistance to at least one antimicrobial agent and three isolates (D, P and N) were classified as multidrug resistant (resistance to ≥3 classes). A particularly high resistance to sulfonamides was observed. This finding aligns with the results of Pavelquesi et al. [20], who reported similar resistance levels in related studies. Interestingly, none of the isolates in this study exhibited resistance to nalidixic acid. This contrasts with the findings of Castro-Vargas et al. [21], who reported nalidixic acid resistance in foodborne pathogens belonging to the genus Salmonella enterica. Notable resistance to aminoglycosides was also observed, specifically 68.8 % for gentamycin, 43.8 % for streptomycin, 68.8 % for neomycin, 56.3 % for amikacin and 81.3 % for kanamycin. These values exceed the average resistance rates reported for foodborne pathogens, which, according to Sabeq et al. [14], typically show aminoglycoside resistance around 48.6 %. In contrast, all isolates in this study were susceptible to tetracycline. This differs from the 17 % resistance rate commonly observed in foodborne Salmonella isolates [14]. These differences may be attributed to the relatively smaller sample size or to the specific geographic origin of the isolates. Regional variations in antibiotic use and environmental pressures could influence resistance profiles. When analysed by serotype, resistance was most frequently observed among isolates of Weltevreden (except for isolate B) and Okatie serotypes. The isolate displaying the highest resistance profile (resistant to 9 of 16 antibiotics) belonged to S. enterica subsp. houtenae. Conversely, serotype Thompson and the monophasic 4,5,12:b: exhibited narrower resistance profiles. While this suggests some serotype-specific variation, further study with a larger dataset would be needed to draw statistically robust conclusions. Although many of the isolates analysed in this study are not directly linked to severe human health issues, the presence of resistant phenotypes remains a concern. These isolates have the potential to disseminate resistance genes through horizontal gene transfer in the environment, posing a broader risk to both public and environmental health.

Despite the fact that kratom is sold in the Czech Republic and some other European states as a "collector's item", which is definitely not intended for consumption, some producers and/or distributors (including multiple distributors of Salmonella-positive kratom tested in our analysis) claims to sterilize their products using gamma or UV radiation. Since the sale of kratom is not covered by the same legislation as the sale of food or pharmaceuticals, the seller is not obliged to perform these sterilisation techniques on a product such as kratom, and it is not possible to trace or confirm that such sterilisation has really taken place or apply any other otherwise obligatory means of quality control. Whatever the case, it is clear from our results that the sterilisation process is not remarkably effective given the number of positive detections.

Limitations

According to the seller's information, the samples originated exclusively from Southeast Asia, specifically Vietnam, Thailand, Burma, Laos, and predominantly from the Indonesian islands of Java and Borneo. However, as kratom is not marketed as a food or food supplement in the Czech Republic, its import and distribution lack sufficient transparency and reliability. Consequently, the seller's information cannot be regarded as fully credible.

While susceptibility testing followed EUCAST and CLSI guidelines and provides robust phenotypic data, no molecular analyses (e.g., PCR-based detection of resistance genes) were performed to characterize the underlying mechanisms of resistance. This represents a limitation of the study, which was designed as a surveillance-oriented screening effort. Future research should benefit from incorporating molecular methods to identify specific resistance genes and better understand the genetic basis of observed phenotypes.

Conclusion

The results of our study emphasize the microbial hazards posed by kratom, particularly due to the relatively frequent contamination with Salmonella and their resistance to antimicrobial agents and highlight the urgent need for legislative intervention. Regulatory measures could follow existing models (Regulation (EC) No 852/2004; Regulation (EC) No 2073/2005) used for dried herbal products and dietary supplements, such as mandatory microbial testing, batch traceability, and adherence to good manufacturing practices (GMP), which have proven effective in reducing microbial risks in similar product categories. The implementation of HACCP strategies could also contribute to reducing microbial contamination of kratom improving its safety. This could uniform European legislation, or at least introduce mandatory sterilisation protocols, and mandating compliance with hygiene standards in the production, packaging, storage, dispatch, and routine assessment of the microbial quality of kratom placed on the EU market. The introduction of such measures would not only protect consumers but also help mitigate the wider risks associated with the spread of antimicrobial resistance in the environment.
