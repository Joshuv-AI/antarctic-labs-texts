Active friction-regulated inertia impact piezoelectric actuator
Authors: Zhipeng Jin, Xin Song, BaoShan Guo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn inertia impact piezoelectric actuators, the phenomenon of high-frequency drive-induced back-stepping poses a significant limitation to their overall performance. The ultra-fast response time of the piezoelectric stack enables the resolution of this issue. This paper introduces an inertia impact piezoelectric actuator operating under a novel Dual-Stack Motion Mode (DCMM), diverging from the traditional operation in the Single-Stack Motion Mode (STMM) that involves a solitary piezoelectric stack (PES) for active friction control. A comprehensive description of the actuator's structure and its operational principles under DCMM is provided. By constructing and experimentally evaluating the actuator using a controlled variable approach, a comparative analysis of performance between DCMM and STMM across various scenarios including different inertial mass blocks, driving voltages, frequencies, and load conditions was conducted. The experimental results indicate that DCMM significantly enhances the actuator's output performance, achieving a maximum speed of 1142.79 μm/s and a stable single-step displacement of 0.5 μm. The actuator features a simple yet effective structure and driving mechanism, allowing for multiple driving modes through the assembly of different inertial masses, thereby providing a substantial competitive advantage in output performance. The feasibility of using DCMM to improve actuator performance is corroborated by both theoretical and experimental studies. The ultra-fast response of the piezoelectric stacks expands the operational bandwidth of the actuator, achieving a seamless integration of speed and precision.Keywords: Inertia impact piezoelectric actuator, Dual-stack motion mode (DCMM), High-frequency drive-induced back-stepping, Controlled variables approach, Piezoelectric stack(PES)

Introduction

In recent years, piezoelectric actuators have garnered significant research attention due to their vital applications in fields demanding stringent output precision, distance, speed, and electromagnetic interference resistance [1], such as aerospace [[2], [3], [4]], precision and ultra-precision machining [[5], [6], [7], [8]], and chip engineering [9]. As a novel driving technology, piezoelectric precision actuation boasts significant advantages such as high displacement resolution, rapid response, and compact structure, presenting promising application prospects [[10], [11], [12]].

Existing piezoelectric actuators primarily include direct-driving piezoelectric actuators, inchworm piezoelectric actuators, ultrasonic piezoelectric actuators, stick-slip piezoelectric actuators, and inertia impact piezoelectric actuators [[12], [13], [14]]. Direct-driving piezoelectric actuators are known for their high precision but suffer from limited stroke length, which remains in the range of a few hundred micrometers even after stroke amplification [15,16]. Inchworm piezoelectric actuators, while capable of handling large loads with high precision, are characterized by complex structures and control systems that demand high manufacturing and assembly accuracy [17,18]. Ultrasonic piezoelectric actuators operate under resonant conditions, offering fast speeds and high loads but at the cost of lower precision and increased wear [19]. Stick-slip piezoelectric actuators, their simple structure, are significantly influenced by frictional and viscous forces. In the above types [20,21], inertia impact piezoelectric actuators, with their simple structure, convenient control, high output force, and high response speed, have garnered widespread attention [22,23]. For example, Xiaotao Li conducted theoretical analysis and prototype development of a novel inertia impact single oscillator actuator [24]. Similarly, Hongzhuang Zhang proposed a cantilevered piezoelectric bimorph as the driving element for a multi-degree-of-freedom inertia impact precision actuator based on inertia impact principles, undergoing both theoretical and experimental studies [25]. Both sets of research underscored the broad application prospects of inertia impact piezoelectric actuators.

Inertial impact piezoelectric actuators primarily utilize two types of components: piezoelectric twin bimorphs and piezoelectric stacks. These actuators with twin bimorphs are commonly designed with two layout methods: a cantilever arrangement and a fixed arrangement at both ends. One example of a cantilever arrangement is the piezoelectric twin-chip two-degree-of-freedom inertial impact precision actuator developed by Zhang Hongzhuang [25]. Additionally, Kang Chen et al. introduced a piezoelectric inertial rotary actuator with bias element that utilizes an asymmetric clamping structure, enabling higher angular velocity and load capacity [26]. Conversely, the fixed arrangement enhances structural simplicity and stability. Exemplified by En Zhang's linear actuator and Qitao Lu's vibratory actuator, both employing twin bimorphs in a fixed beam structure [27]. Despite their designs, these actuators often suffer from low resonant frequencies, reducing their effective bandwidth and limiting high-speed precision integration [28]. For instance, Dezhu Shen's asymmetric actuator [29], and Ying Hu's Piezo-Lastomer Linear Motor (PLLM) [30] despite having high resolution and good stability, the exhibit low efficiency and slow response speed.

Piezoelectric stack-based actuators are particularly notable for their high-power output. In 2018, Hiroki Yokozawa introduced an environmentally friendly resonant impact driver that utilizedKNaLiNbO3 instead of PZT, achieving speeds up to 53 mm/s and thrusts of 69 mN [31]. In 2020, Hiroyuki Suzuki and Robert J. Wood created a miniature piezoelectric-driven robotic hand for microsurgery, utilizing the high speed and precise positioning capabilities of piezoelectric stack-based actuators [32].

Despite their advantages, piezoelectric stack-based actuators face several challenges. While piezoelectric stack actuators often use a cantilever configuration prone to generating destabilizing overturning moments as shown in Fig. 2.1, improvements are possible. In 2018, Zhong et al. developed a design reducing overturning moments, thus enhancing motion performance, where a 1 mm reduction in the moment increased output speeds by up to 0.17 mm/s [33].

The typical structure of traditional inertial actuators driven by PES.

Although the bandwidth of piezoelectric actuators based on piezoelectric stacks is relatively high, during high-frequency operation, they may exhibit a phenomenon of motion regression. This study tackles the issue through active friction regulation, regulating the friction differential between the actuator's forward and backward movements within a single motion cycle. Ikuta et al. designed a linear cybernetic actuator combining piezoelectric impact drive with electromagnetic clamping [34]. The driving force for movement was generated by a piezoelectric stack, while the clamping force to control friction between the actuator and guide rail was produced by an electromagnetic coil. However, the friction differential in this study cannot be controlled continuously, and the response time is relatively long. Subsequently, Zhu [35] Wang [36] et al. investigated the drive waveforms of the actuator, employing waveform superposition, optimization, and composite waves to achieve active friction regulation. However, composite waves can compromise the stability of the actuator's motion. Xia Huang et al. [37]. utilized magnetic force to actively adjust the friction force; however, the slow response of the magnetic force restricts the actuator's maximum driving frequency to just 40 Hz.

To address the aforementioned challenges and achieve a balance between speed and accuracy, this paper presents the design of an active friction-regulated inertia impact piezoelectric actuator. By leveraging the ultra-fast response characteristics of the piezoelectric stack for active friction control, the actuator ensures non-regressive motion across a broader operational range. Furthermore, the symmetric structure of the actuator effectively prevents the generation of destabilizing overturning moments.

Design of the novel piezoelectric actuator

Structural design

Fig. 2.2 illustrates the structural design of the active friction-regulated inertia impact piezoelectric actuator, with dimensions of 104 mm by 43 mm. The actuator is comprised of Driving Unit, the Friction Control Unit, and a U-shaped guide base. The Friction Control Unit, positioned at the center of the actuator, is connected to the inertial mass block via a restoring hinge. Driving Units I and II, along with the Friction Control Unit, are sequentially arranged to form a cohesive assembly, where Driving Units I and II are structurally identical and symmetrically positioned relative to the Friction Control Unit. Symmetrical drive units enable the actuator to move in both directions along the U-shaped guide base. The actuator is embedded in the U-shaped guide base in a closely-fitted manner.

Structure of active friction-regulated inertia impact piezoelectric actuator.

Operating principle

The operating principle of the STMM mode inertial impact piezoelectric actuator is straightforward, relying on the rapid extension and slow retraction of the piezoelectric stack in one driving unit to facilitate actuator movement. When the piezoelectric stack is stimulated by a triangular waveform, as depicted in Fig. 2.3, the piezoelectric stack rapidly extends during the phase of rapid voltage increase, pushing the inertial mass block within the driving unit and generating an impact force that propels the actuator forward. As the voltage gradually declines, the piezoelectric stack slowly contracts to its original state, setting the stage for the next step.

Motion diagram and voltage waveform diagram of each stage of actuator (STMM).

The DCMM mode is more complex than the STMM mode. The operation of the active friction-regulated inertia impact piezoelectric actuator is predicated upon the impact inertia generated by the piezoelectric stack within the drive unit propelling the inertial mass block. The friction control unit generates adjustable friction differentials. The driving signal is segmented into five phases within a cycle as depicted in Fig. 2.4, denoted as “a” for the initial state and “e” for the final state. Hence, the entire motion process can be described in the following five stages.

(a): Before the actuator initiates movement, the central piezoelectric stack II within the friction control unit is subjected to a low voltage, keeping it in a contracted state. This configuration results in minimal friction between the upper and lower feet and the U-shaped guide base. Concurrently, piezoelectric stack III is also under low voltage and remains compressed under the action of restoring hinge.

: Before the actuator initiates movement, the central piezoelectric stack II within the friction control unit is subjected to a low voltage, keeping it in a contracted state. This configuration results in minimal friction between the upper and lower feet and the U-shaped guide base. Concurrently, piezoelectric stack III is also under low voltage and remains compressed under the action of restoring hinge.

(b): A sudden voltage increase to piezoelectric stack III causes it to rapidly extend, generating an instantaneous force greater than the maximum static friction force faced by the actuator on the guide rail, hence moving the actuator body to the left and resulting in displacement.

(c): As piezoelectric stack III extends to a predetermined position, a high voltage is instantaneously applied to central piezoelectric stack II within the friction control unit, causing the flexible hinge to instantly tighten. This action induces significant friction between the upper and lower feet and the U-shaped guide base, achieving an instantaneous braking effect on the actuator.

: As piezoelectric stack III extends to a predetermined position, a high voltage is instantaneously applied to central piezoelectric stack II within the friction control unit, causing the flexible hinge to instantly tighten. This action induces significant friction between the upper and lower feet and the U-shaped guide base, achieving an instantaneous braking effect on the actuator.

(d): After braking, the voltage applied to piezoelectric stack III is gradually reduced, allowing it to slowly retract to its starting position under the influence of restoring hinge, preparing it for the next step.

: After braking, the voltage applied to piezoelectric stack III is gradually reduced, allowing it to slowly retract to its starting position under the influence of restoring hinge, preparing it for the next step.

(e): The voltage applied to the central piezoelectric stack II within the friction control unit decreases, causing the piezoelectric stack to contract to its initial position under the action of the flexible hinge. The friction between the upper and lower feet and the U-shaped guide base is thereby diminished, priming the actuator for its next stepping motion.

: The voltage applied to the central piezoelectric stack II within the friction control unit decreases, causing the piezoelectric stack to contract to its initial position under the action of the flexible hinge. The friction between the upper and lower feet and the U-shaped guide base is thereby diminished, priming the actuator for its next stepping motion.

Repeating the aforementioned five steps enables the actuator to achieve high-frequency step movements without back-stepping.

Due to the negligible friction between the friction control unit and the guide rail during the leap phase, normal stepping of the actuator is facilitated. During the contraction phase of the piezoelectric stack, significant friction generated between the friction control unit and the guide rail prevents the actuator from back-stepping as the piezoelectric stack contracts. This mechanism enhances the operational bandwidth of the actuator, enabling the integration of high speed and high precision in its performance.

Experiment and measurement

Experimental system

To evaluate the output performance of the designed actuator, an experimental system was established as shown in Fig. 4.1. The system operates by generating a sawtooth driving voltage with a phase difference of π using a signal generator (DG4062, RIGOL Technologies, China). This voltage is then amplified by a power amplifier (E01. A3, Harbin Xinmingri Technology Co., Ltd., China) before being applied to two PES mounted on the actuator. The linear displacement generated by the actuator is collected by a laser displacement sensor (ILD2300-2, Micro-Epsilon, Germany) and stored on a personal computer. It is important to ensure the parallelism of the U-shaped guide base during the experiment. Each half of the U-shaped guide base is adjusted to its maximum lateral limits, fixing one half while rotating the knob on the adjusting fixture. The distance between the rails is measured with calipers to match the actuator's width. After installing the actuator within the guide rails, fine adjustments are made. At this point, the actuator moves smoothly between the rails, and when only the piezoelectric stack in the Friction Control Unit is driven, there is no displacement of the actuator.

Experimental measurement system.

To investigate the performance advantages of the Dual-Stack Synergistic Working Mode (DCMM), we conducted studies under the same research conditions on the traditional Single-Stack Motion Mode (STMM). By comparison, the advantages and the necessity of research on the DCMM mode were determined. The experimental results presented below are obtained by averaging multiple sets of reliable experimental data (At least ten sets of data), confirming that the actuator exhibits excellent repeatability.

Displacement of the actuator in STMM and DCMM modes

The output displacements in the Single-Stack Motion Mode (STMM) and Dual-Stack Motion Mode (DCMM) were compared. The relationship between displacement and time obtained at a given driving voltage of 100 V and a driving frequency of 30 Hz and 700 Hz is shown in Fig. 4.2. The mass of the inertia block is 100 g. The back-stepping displacement observed in each step under the STMM mode is clearly visible, whereas the back-stepping displacement under the DCMM mode is significantly reduced. As a result, the effective displacement in DCMM mode is significantly greater than in STMM mode. This experimental result conclusively demonstrates that the actuator operating under the DCMM mode achieves suppression of back-stepping motion, enhancing the actuator's performance significantly. At lower excitation frequencies, the actuator maintains excellent single-step stability, for example, at an excitation frequency of 30 Hz, the actuator's single-step stability is maintained at 0.5 μm, which can be adjusted by adjusting the mass of the inertia block. At higher excitation frequencies, the actuator achieves stable high-speed uniform linear motion, such as at 700 Hz excitation, the actuator's motion speed can reach 1142.79 μm/s.

Output speed of the actuator under conditions of varying inertial mass blocks

As elucidated by the operating principle of the actuator described previously, the driving force originates from the rapid expansion of the Piezoelectric Stack (PES) within the driving unit, propelling the inertial mass block. This action, in turn, generates a reactive force that advances the actuator forward. Hence, the mass of the inertial mass block significantly impacts the efficiency of the actuator. To investigate the effect of different inertial mass blocks on the output efficiency of the actuator, experiments were conducted under a driving voltage of 100 V and a frequency of 30 Hz with varying inertial mass blocks. The relationship between the mass of the inertial mass blocks and the actuator speed was mapped out, as shown in Fig. 4.3(a).

The relationship between the speed and mass of the inertia block under these two modes. The driving voltage is 100 V. The driving frequency is kept to be 30 Hz(a); Single step length of actuator under different inertial mass (b).

Experimental data indicate that with lighter inertial mass blocks, an increase in mass leads to a stable growth in driving speed for both DCMM and STMM modes. However, when the mass reaches a certain threshold, an increase in the actuator's own mass leads to a decrease in driving speed, aligning well with established theories. Beyond a mass of 100 g, a rapid decline in the driving speed of the DCMM mode is observed, likely due to excessive local friction between the actuator and the guide rails caused by severe vibrations. This results in energy loss in the direction of motion.

Fig. 4.3(b) illustrates the concept of multi-mode driving step lengths of the actuator in DCMM mode, utilizing different inertial mass blocks. Single-step length tests were conducted with six available inertial mass blocks under the conditions of 100V driving voltage and 30Hz frequency. Insufficient mass of the blocks resulted in inadequate driving force, leading to shorter step lengths and lower speeds. Conversely, overly large mass blocks caused slight collisions between the actuator and the guide rails, resulting in oscillations and unstable step lengths. The optimal performance was achieved with a 100g inertial mass block, providing maximum step length and stable operation, leading to the selection of 100g for the experimental inertial mass. Additionally, we can adjust the mass of the inertial blocks according to specific requirements to achieve desired driving modes, which is a significant highlight of this research.

Output performance of the actuator at different driving voltages and frequencies

The actuator's output speed can be modulated by adjusting the driving voltage and frequency. The speed of the actuator was measured under various driving voltages at a fixed driving frequency of 700 Hz. Fig. 4.4 presents the results, showing a steady and gradual increase in driving speed under both STMM and DCMM modes. This increment is attributed to the increased elongation of the PES with rising driving voltages. The DCMM mode outperforms the STMM mode, as the rapid contraction of the PES in STMM mode can generate a reverse force, leading to back-stepping.

The relationship between the speed and driving voltage obtained under these two modes. The driving frequency is kept to be 700 Hz. The mass of the inertia block is 100 g.

The driving voltage of the actuator exhibits a roughly linear relationship with speed, which aligns with our expectations for their correlation. By applying the least squares method for linear fitting, we obtained the correlation coefficients R for both DCMM and STMM: R (DCMM) = 0.9915 and R (STMM) = 0.9932. In future research, we aim to develop improved design proposals for the actuator that enhance leading performance.

The actuator's speed was also measured under different driving frequencies at a fixed driving voltage of 100 V. The results displayed in Fig. 4.5 indicate that driving speeds under both STMM and DCMM modes gradually increase with the driving frequency. Initially, due to insufficiently high driving frequencies, the speed difference is not markedly pronounced. However, as driving frequency increases, the speed increment in STMM becomes limited due to the rapid contraction of the PES generating a reverse pull force, exacerbating back-stepping phenomena. This effect leads to a scenario where, at excessively high frequencies, the actuator speed may decline as the back-stepping becomes more severe with increasing excitation frequency. Moreover, it is observed that the driving speed of STMM mode is abnormally reduced under 600Hz excitation. Fig. 4.6(a) presents the simulation results of the first six natural frequencies of the actuator obtained using software (ANSYS Workbench 2022 R1). The first and second natural frequencies of the actuator are approximately 600 Hz, at which the actuator experiences a sudden decrease in speed under 600 Hz excitation in STMM mode. In contrast, during DCMM mode, under the action of friction control unit (PES), the actuator attains the highest driving speed under excitation in the 600–800 Hz frequency range. The third and fourth natural frequencies are around 930 Hz, where the actuator exhibits low movement speeds in both modes. This is believed to be influenced by the actuator's natural frequencies as well as the precision of the driving signal generator and associated equipment. The fifth and sixth natural frequencies are approximately 3320 Hz, which pose challenges for our experimental setup to operate effectively at such high frequencies. Fig. 4.5(b) presents eleven sets of data for the DCMM under 700 Hz excitation and the STMM under 670 Hz excitation, demonstrating the actuator's high repeatability.

Output performance of the actuator under external loads

An actuator is designed to carry or pull external loads, making load testing crucial. There are two types of external loads applied to the actuator: compressive loads, also known as vertical loads, and tensile loads, also referred to as horizontal loads, as illustrated in Fig. 4.6. To study the impact of these two different external loads on actuator performance, load mass was gradually increased under a driving voltage of 100 V and a driving frequency of 30 Hz to test the actuator's performance.

Output performance of the actuator under compressive load

Under a driving voltage of 100 V and a driving frequency of 100 Hz, the relationship between driving speed and vertical load is shown in Fig. 4.7. The driving speeds of both STMM and DCMM decrease with the increase of vertical load. This reduction in speed is more pronounced at the initial stage of load increase and tends to stabilize after the compressive load exceeds 60 g. Experimental data indicate that DCMM outperforms STMM in bearing compressive loads, maintaining better stability even when the load exceeds 60 g.

The relationship between the speed and the mass of the vertical loads under these two modes. The driving voltage is 100 V. The mass of the inertia block is 100 g. The driving frequency is kept to be 100 Hz.

Output performance of the actuator under tensile load

Under a driving voltage of 100 V and a driving frequency of 100 Hz, the relationship between driving speed and horizontal load is presented in Fig. 4.8. The driving speeds of both STMM and DCMM decrease with the increase of horizontal load, and this reduction is significant throughout the entire range. Unlike compressive loads, when the tensile load reaches a certain mass, the speed of the actuator drops more swiftly to zero. Combining experimental data, it is deduced that DCMM slightly surpasses STMM in bearing tensile loads. However, the performance under tensile loads is evidently inferior to that under compressive loads, suggesting that compressive loads are a more rational choice for practical applications.

Conclusion

In conclusion, to address the challenges of high-frequency back-stepping, broaden the operational bandwidth, and enhance the overall performance of piezoelectric actuators, this study introduced an innovative inertia impact piezoelectric actuator utilizing a Dual-Stack Motion Mode (DCMM). This actuator leverages the ultra-fast response of piezoelectric stacks, a key innovation that significantly expands the actuator's operational bandwidth. Detailed investigations into the structure and working principles of the DCMM mode were conducted, with comparative analysis against the traditional Single-Stack Motion Mode (STMM). The following conclusions have been drawn from the experimental results and analysis.

(1)DCMM effectively suppresses back-stepping motion, The drive frequency is greatly improved, thereby increasing the output speed of the actuator. The designed actuator achieved a maximum speed of 1142.79 μm/s.

(4)DCMM strengthens the impact of vertical loads on output performance while maintaining motion stability. The designed actuator exhibits stable output power under loads up to 200g. Additionally, DCMM can appropriately handle increased horizontal loads.

In summary, the implementation of DCMM significantly enhances the actuator's performance across multiple parameters. The utilization of piezoelectric stacks with ultra-fast response times is a critical innovation that broadens the actuator's operational bandwidth, integrating high speed with precision. These advancements have been validated through experimental verification, highlighting the DCMM mode as a superior solution for achieving high-performance piezoelectric actuation.
