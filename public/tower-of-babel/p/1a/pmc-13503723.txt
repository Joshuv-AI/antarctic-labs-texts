Distinct Filament Conformation for Receptor-Bound Amyloid-β from Alzheimer’s Disease Brain
Authors: Mikhail A Kostylev, Carmen Butan, Graham P Roseman, Yangyi Liu, Tanina Arab, Ruibing Wu, Pallavi Gopal, Stephen M Strittmatter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease is triggered by amyloid-β, with symptoms linked to synapse loss. Oligomeric amyloid-β, rather than monomeric or fibrillar amyloid-β, has been proposed to be the proximate mechanistic cause, but the relevant molecular characteristics have remained unclear. To define the biologically relevant species, we isolated a receptor-bound amyloid-β pool from Alzheimer’s disease brain by release with a receptor antagonist and purification to homogeneity. This discrete receptor-bound amyloid-β pool is ten times more abundant than free unbound amyloid-β and is comprised of 65 nm long filaments. The tips of these short filaments bind to prion protein and drive synapse loss from human neurons. Cryo-electron microscopy shows that the receptor-bound amyloid-β consists of two symmetric S-shaped amyloid-β monomers per filament rung. The overall structure is similar to much longer plaque-associated amyloid-β filaments from the same brain and fails to reveal an unrelated “oligomeric” conformation. Nonetheless, the receptor-bound amyloid-β can be distinguished from plaque filaments by the tilt angle of repeating amyloid-β subunits within each filament rung, by amino terminal conformation, by length and by amyloid seeding properties. Characterizing receptor-bound amyloid-β provides insight into neuronal dysfunction separate from plaque aggregation.Subject terms: Alzheimer's disease, Alzheimer's disease, Cryoelectron microscopy

Introduction

The defining pathology of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques in the brain, with associated neurofibrillary tangles, gliosis, and loss of neurons1,2. Recent anti-Aβ antibody studies3,4, coupled with biomarker progression data5 and rare dominantly inherited causative mutations6, support a causative role for Aβ accumulation in triggering the complex disease process7. During the early symptomatic stage, synaptic dysfunction and loss correlate closely with cognitive decline. Soluble, oligomeric forms of Aβ (Aβo), as opposed to fibrillary plaque Aβ or monomeric Aβ, have been postulated to be causative for synaptic phenotypes6,8. Confusingly, the term Aβo has been applied to many different assemblies of the peptide, and the relevant species in the AD brain has remained unclear9. Further, the structure of various Aβo preparations has never been defined, generating uncertainty in the field. In contrast, the structure of fibrillary AD plaque-associated Aβ has now been clarified10,11, and similarities between sporadic and inherited cases have been assessed12.

Reported phenotypes attributed to soluble Aβo include impairment of synaptic plasticity, the triggering of microglial engulfment of synaptic structures, the loss of dendritic spines, the stimulation of phosphorylated tau accumulation, and endolysosomal dysfunction6,8. A widely studied Aβo preparation of synthetic Aβ42 in F12 media, termed amyloid-β derived diffusible ligands (ADDLs), appears as metastable 500 kDa spherical peptide assemblies13. Globulomers of Aβ42 are prepared in SDS and then isolated as stable 12-mers14. From AD brain tissue, freely soluble Aβo species of about 1000 kDa have been collected after tissue homogenization or soaking of diced brain in buffered saline15,16. A recent study suggests that, at least under certain conditions, these preparations contain Aβ filaments that can be sedimented with high centrifugal force16 and that are indistinguishable from insoluble plaque filaments. Thus, the existence of a single disease-relevant oligomeric state of Aβ is uncertain16.

The response of the AD brain to the presence of misfolded Aβ depends on interaction with cellular components, including specific receptors. A range of cell surface Aβ binding sites has been described, with cellular prion protein (PrPC) being unique in its specificity for diffusible assemblies of Aβ, but not monomers or fibrils, and its discovery by unbiased screening17–20. The high-affinity interaction of Aβ with PrPC has a slow off-rate with a half-life of days21. In multiple studies, deletion or blockade of PrPC protects the mouse brain from the deleterious effects of Aβ accumulation, such as memory impairment22 and behavioral deficits23. Additionally, blocking the interaction between PrPC and Aβ using anti-PrP antibodies24, polymers21, or indirectly using allosteric modulators targeting mGluR525,26 has been shown to reduce the toxic effects of Aβ in cellular and animal models of AD. Specifically, Aβ/PrPC complexes acting with mGluR5 as a co-receptor impair long-term potentiation in hippocampal slices, trigger Fyn, Pyk2, and eEF2 kinase activity, increase phosphorylated tau accumulation, disrupt the neuronal transcriptome, recruit C1q to damaged synapses, mediate synapse loss, and lead to impaired spatial and object recognition memory in multiple mouse models of brain amyloidosis21–24,26–31. The various soluble Aβo assemblies described above each interact with PrPC15 32,. Importantly, binding in the appropriate ratio leads to a hydrogel phase state that sequesters Aβ in a bound state with an altered PrPC conformation32. This hydrogel is present in the AD brain and can be disrupted by a polymeric receptor antagonist, termed poly [4-styrenesulfonic acid-co-maleic acid] (PSCMA)21,32.

Here, molecular understanding of Aβ interaction with cellular components is advanced by assessing receptor-bound Aβ species, a species that is distinct from the soluble-free-unbound pool and the insoluble-plaque pool of Aβ. Extraction and purification of receptor-bound Aβ allowed biochemical characterization and structure determination. The receptor-bound pool consists of a filamentous conformation distinguishable from plaque Aβ without evidence for a distinct oligomeric species.

Results

Receptor-bound pool of amyloid-β

Our goal was to separate Aβ species bound to receptor sites from free, unbound Aβ and from plaque-associated fibrillary Aβ. First, we processed fresh frozen autopsy brain tissue from the cerebral cortex of Braak stage V/VI cases or neurologically intact control cases by Dounce homogenization in aqueous buffer, followed by ultracentrifugation. A fraction containing monomers and free oligomers was released into the supernatant from AD autopsy brain, and repeated extractions revealed near-complete depletion of this pool (Fig. 1a). Previously measured in vitro Aβo dissociation rates from receptors are a day or more at room temperature21, so most receptor-bound Aβ remained in the ultracentrifuge pellet together with Aβ fibrils during these steps at 4 °C.

To release receptor-bound Aβ species from AD brain, we utilized the PrPC antagonist, PSCMA, which was identified as a competitive blocker of Aβ binding21, and is capable of dissociating human brain hydrogels composed of Aβ and PrPC receptors32. Resuspension in the presence of PSCMA released an Aβ fraction (Fig. 1a) containing ten-fold more total Aβ immunoreactivity than what was present in the sum of repeated TBS extracts from the same brain (Fig. 1b). This PSCMA-extracted material was a distinct pool, as demonstrated by sequential extractions yielding progressively less Aβ release, and by the majority of the Aβ species remaining in the ultracentrifuged pellet, as expected for Aβ fibrils (Fig. 1a, b). Receptor-bound Aβ constituted 4.8% of all Aβ in the AD brain (Fig. 1b inset). Only a small amount of other proteins was extracted by PSCMA in the receptor-bound Aβ pool, providing a near 200-fold enrichment of Aβ immunoreactivity relative to total protein compared to the TBS-extractable pool (Fig. 1a, b).

Based on our previous work with recombinant Aβ and purified PrPC 32, we hypothesized that the elution of receptor-bound Aβ by PSCMA was largely the result of release from PrPC-dependent hydrogels through a multivalent disruption of the Aβ binding to PrPC. We tested this hypothesis for the origin of receptor-bound Aβ using a transgenic mouse model, APPswe/PS1∆E9 (APP/PS1), on a wild-type versus Prnp−/− background. The amount of Aβ released from APP/PS1 brain was reduced by more than 50% when PrPC was absent (Fig. 1c). The PrPC effect was specific to the receptor-bound Aβ pool, since total Aβ load and the free soluble Aβ fractions were unchanged by Prnp−/− status. The minor fraction of PSCMA-extractable Aβ species in APP/PS1 brain lacking PrPC may reflect the PSCMA sensitivity of alternate Aβo receptors21.

The receptor-bound Aβ species extracted by PSCMA was highly enriched, but not homogeneous. To further enrich the material, we developed a single chain anti-Aβ antibody resin for moderate affinity-based purification, allowing removal of PSCMA and achieving rapid elution under mild conditions (Fig. 2a). Analysis of the final preparation by SDS-PAGE yielded a single prominent protein band by silver stain, which migrated at the same mobility as recombinant Aβ42 peptide (Fig. 2b). Compared to TBS-soluble Aβ immunoreactivity in AD brain, the preparation was purified one million-fold relative to total protein (Fig. 2c). Similar purified receptor-bound Aβ preparations were generated from cerebral cortex of five different Braak stage V/VI AD brain autopsy samples (Fig. 2d).

Characterization of the receptor-bound pool of amyloid-β

We sought to assess immunologic and physical properties of the receptor-bound Aβ pool from the AD brain. A panel of anti-Aβ antibodies was utilized to compare the receptor-bound Aβ from the four different AD brains to fibrillary Aβ sedimenting in the presence of PSCMA from the same brains (Fig. 3a, b). For this comparison, the PSCMA-released Aβ pool prior to antibody affinity retention was compared to PSCMA-resistant material containing plaque-associated Aβ. The PSCMA-resistant material was further extracted with sarkosyl, and the sarkosyl-insoluble pellet was resuspended for immunoblot. The Aβ immunoreactivity with different amino-specific and carboxyl-specific antibodies was assessed after normalization to a mid-region anti-Aβ antibody. The receptor-bound Aβ pool was five-fold enriched for the most amino-terminal residues of Aβ1–42 (Fig. 3a, b). The level of reactivity for pyroglutamylated Aβ was similar in the two preparations. At the carboxyl terminus, reactivity was similar for the two pools with anti-Aβ42 and anti-Aβ43 specific antibodies, but greater for Aβ40 in the receptor-bound pool. These differences confirm the distinct nature of the receptor-bound Aβ species relative to fibrillary Aβ and show more extensive proteolysis of the amino-terminus for the insoluble fibrillary species.

The FDA-approved anti-Aβ antibody, lecanemab, is reported to have preferential affinity for oligomeric and protofibrillary Aβ species, with less avidity for Aβ filaments and minimal interaction with monomeric Aβ33. We confirmed its high affinity for the receptor-bound Aβ species and reduced avidity for sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Next, we characterized the physical size of the receptor-bound Aβ pool by electron microscopy (EM) and size exclusion chromatography (SEC). Receptor-bound Aβ exhibited a rod-like shape in EM images with a length of 60.5 ± 23.4 nm, 69.2 ± 24.4 nm, and 71.1 ± 29.3 nm (mean ± SD) from three different AD brains (Fig. 3c), demonstrating a filamentous nature for the receptor-bound Aβ assemblies in multiple brain samples. To characterize size using SEC, the purified receptor-bound Aβ needed to be first mixed with a protein-rich preparation to avoid non-specific adsorption during chromatography. SEC fractions of TBS extracts from control brain are protein-rich but show no background of detectable PrPC-interacting Aβ immunoreactivity (Fig. 2e). When these TBS extracts are mixed with purified receptor-bound Aβ from AD brain, there is a single PrPC-interacting Aβ immunoreactivity peak with a size matching migration of globular proteins in the 650 kDa range, consistent with the EM appearance and an assembly of about 250 Aβ monomers (Fig. 2e). This is similar to the SEC elution profile for free TBS-soluble Aβo species from multiple AD mouse models and AD brain15.

Signaling by the receptor-bound pool of amyloid-β

As an Aβ receptor, PrPC is of high affinity and essential for a range of phenotypes in models of brain amyloidosis. We compared the PrPC binding properties of the human brain receptor-bound Aβ pool with those of other Aβ preparations (Fig. 3d). Expressed in monomer equivalents, the apparent avidity in a plate-based PrPC-interacting Aβ immunoreactivity (PrP-linked immunosorbent assay (PLISA)) assay15 was similar to that for recombinant Aβ42 globulomer (12mer) and a synthetic Aβ42 ADDL preparation in F12 medium (apparent MR of 500,000). In contrast, the affinity of synthetic Aβ42 fibrils was nil or at least two orders of magnitude lower.

The interaction of the rod-like shape of human brain receptor-bound Aβ with PrPC raises the question of whether receptor interactions occur along the side, or at the tips, two clearly different environments. Since the principal EM appearance difference between receptor-bound Aβ and insoluble fibrillary Aβ is a higher ratio of tip to shaft length, tips may be key for PrPC interaction. We compared PrPC binding of intact Aβ42 synthetic fibrils to sonicated preparations that had been sheared to a length of 50–100 nm as confirmed by negative stain transmission EM. The breakage of fibrils and the creation of multiple tips supported PrPC binding to the otherwise non-interacting Aβ42 fibril preparation, with an increase in avidity of greater than 20-fold (Fig. 3d). This strongly suggests that tip concentration is crucial for PrPC interaction. In contrast to PrPC binding, detection of oligomeric Aβ species using two competing antibodies was not altered by sonication of fibrils to a smaller size (Fig. 3e). The ratio of Aβ capture by PrPC versus antibody from the fibril preparation increased after fragmentation to levels matching that of receptor-bound Aβ (Fig. 3f).

To observe the interaction of Aβ filament tip with PrPC directly, we incubated human brain receptor-bound Aβ with recombinant PrPC, applied it to an EM grid, and stained with anti-PrPC antibody (3F4-mouse primary antibody) and then immunogold anti-mouse antibody. Gold particles associated with Aβ structures were localized relative to the tip position, and nearly all particles were within 5 nm of the tips (Fig. 3g). A similar conclusion has been suggested from light microscopy studies of fluorescently tagged synthetic Aβ42 peptide34. This confirms the discrete localization of binding sites for this receptor and explains the strong binding preference of PrPC for oligomeric Aβ or sonicated fibrils over intact Aβ fibrils.

Since PrPC binds to the tips of receptor-bound Aβ, its presence is predicted to inhibit further polymerization in the presence of Aβ42 monomers. Indeed, the presence of recombinant PrPC reduced the rate of Aβ42 polymerization or Aβ42 polymerization seeded by fibrils (Supplementary Fig. 2). This finding is consistent with several in vitro kinetic reports35,36, although PrPC deletion does not modify the extent of chronic in vivo Aβ plaque accumulation in mouse models22,23,27 where multiple metabolic factors are at play.

Various preparations of Aβo from synthetic, recombinant, or brain sources have been shown to alter synaptic signaling acutely and induce anatomical synapse loss, both of which can be blocked by PSCMA21. We assessed biological activity by exposure of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC)-derived cortical excitatory neurons (60 days post differentiation by Neurogenin2 induction) to the purified receptor-bound Aβ species (16–160 pM assembly, 4–40 nM monomer equivalent) for 7 days. The density of synaptic profiles identified by overlap of Synapsin1/2 and Homer1b/c staining at MAP2-immunoreactive dendrites was significantly reduced by receptor-bound Aβ from three different AD brains as compared to control brain samples (Fig. 4a–c). Co-administration of 200 nM anti-PrP antibody or 200 nM lecanemab antibody together with receptor-bound Aβ prevented the loss of synaptic density (Fig. 4d). These results confirm a potent biological effect of receptor-bound Aβ with potential clinical relevance.

As noted above, sonication of synthetic Aβ 1–42 fibrils yields potent PrPC-interacting species, presumably by creating many more fibril tip PrPC binding sites. We tested whether sonication also generated synapse-reducing activity from otherwise inert synthetic Aβ fibrils. Indeed, sonicated fibril preparations, but not intact fibrils, suppressed human cortical neuron synapse density in this assay (Fig. 4e).

Cryo-EM of receptor-bound amyloid-β

We utilized cryo-EM to determine the structure of receptor-bound Aβ purified from three AD brains. In each case, the majority of particle images yielded an electron density map that we term Conformation 1 of the receptor-bound Aβ with a resolution of 2.8 Å from Brain A (75% prevalence), 3.1 Å from Brain B (55% prevalence), and 3.4 Å from Brain C (100% prevalence) (Fig. 5a, d, f, Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Fig. 10). A less prevalent Conformation 2 with resolution of 3.1 Å from both Brain A (25% prevalence) and Brain B (45% prevalence), but not Brain C, was also observed (Fig. 5b, e). Atomic models for both conformations were built from these maps and included Aβ42 residues 9–42 (Fig. 6a–d, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). These models consist of two symmetrically folded S-shaped Aβ peptides in each rung that form a twisting amyloid filament. The rungs are spaced at 4.75 Å, so a 65 nm length implies a total of about 300 monomers per assembly (Fig. 6a, c). Residues 1–8 are not resolved but may account for fuzzy electron density extending from residue 9 and lying alongside the filament.

The two conformations differ from one another in the amino terminal region with a different rotameric state for the sidechain of Q15 (Fig. 6b, e in comparison to 6d, f), and a shift in the peptide backbone (Fig. 7a–c). This results in a different pattern of intra-molecular hydrogen bonding for V12-Q15 (Fig. 7a, d, e versus 7g, h). In Conformation 1, there is 1:1 bonding to the next rung in the filament at each of these amino acid residues. For example, the amide amino group Q15 sidechain to the amide carbonyl of Q15 sidechain in the next rung (Fig. 7d, e). In contrast, Conformation 2 bonding to the next rung is more frequently shared by two residues in the next rung, for example, the amide amino group Q15 sidechain to both carbonyl of V12 backbone and amide carbonyl of Q15 sidechain in the adjacent rung (Fig. 7g, h).

The different rotameric state of the sidechain of Q15 also affects the intermolecular interactions between Aβ peptides in the two opposing protofilaments (Fig. 7f versus 7i). In Conformation 1, the amide group of Q15 sidechain within one Aβ peptide forms a hydrogen bond with both the G38 backbone carboxyl and amino terminus from the opposing Aβ peptide in an adjacent rung (Fig. 7f). These interactions may stabilize the greater twist observed for Conformation 1. In comparison, the orientation of Q15 in Conformation 2 does not allow for such interactions (Fig. 7i).

Accompanying this monomer change, the stacking and twist of monomers differs between Conformation 1 and Conformation 2 (Figs. 6a and 7b for Conformation 1 versus Figs. 6c and 7c for Conformation 2). The angle between the plane of one peptide and its partner in the same rung is 178.16 degrees for Conformation 1 and 178.52° for Conformation 2 (Table 1). Over the 65-nm length of the receptor-bound Aβ42 rod, the position of the Aβ42 monomer rotates 507 degrees around the long axis in Conformation 1 versus 407 degrees for Conformation 2. Thus, the two ends of a 65 nm rod differ by 100 degrees of rotation. More strikingly, amino-terminal residues 9–15 of one Aβ42 rung are adjacent to carboxyl residues of a monomer two rungs below in Conformation 1, but one rung below in Conformation 2 (compare Fig. 7b and c; Supplementary Fig. 4c, d).

Cryo-EM data collection and processing

We considered whether each receptor-bound filament contained only one Conformation of Aβ or the two conformations were intermixed in a single filament. The positions of filament segments contributing to the resolved conformations were traced on individual filaments in the cryo-EM micrographs. This shows that certain filaments contained distinct segments composed of Conformation 1 and Conformation 2, while other filaments contained only Conformation 1 and, less frequently, only Conformation 2 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Thus, Conformation 1 and Conformation 2 can exist within the same filament, and the shift in residues V12-Q15 does not require filament breakage.

A small minority of receptor-bound Aβ filaments exhibited a greater diameter and electron density maps revealed a doublet of the two symmetric folded S-shaped Aβ peptides observed in the more prevalent conformations (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Although the limited number of these doublet filaments prevented 3D reconstructions with high resolution, it was clear that the doublets from Brain A were composed of Aβ in Conformation 2, as opposed to Conformation 1, from the observation of density corresponding to Q15. The twist of Conformation 1 may restrict the formation of doublets.

The structure of the two receptor-bound Aβ species was compared to reported Aβ structures isolated from AD brain. The less prevalent Conformation 2 found in the receptor-bound Aβ pool is closely similar to the filament structure reported for sarkosyl-insoluble plaque material throughout Aβ9–42 (Supplementary Fig. 7c)10. In contrast, Conformation 1 shows the same degree of deviation from sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ filaments as it does from Conformation 2 (Supplementary Fig. 7a, c). We considered whether this might be due to subject-specific conformations or to different conformations within the same brain for receptor-bound Aβ as compared to filamentous PSCMA-resistant plaque-associated Aβ. We therefore examined material from Brain A, which remained in the ultracentrifuge pellet after sequential extraction with TBS and PSCMA by further extracting with sarkosyl as previously published10 to generate sarkosyl-insoluble material. The sarkosyl-insoluble material had at least three filamentous forms visible by cryo-EM in different proportions (Supplementary Fig. 8). One type showed electron density maps consistent with Aβ filaments, one with tau filaments, and one with TMEM106B filaments. The Aβ filaments were found primarily as long single filaments, but there was a small proportion found as doublet fibrils (Supplementary Fig. 6b). The electron density map for Aβ filaments resolved at 3.0 Å (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 11), and an atomic model matched the published sporadic AD brain Aβ42 filament and Conformation 2 very closely (0.265 Å rmsd and 0.160 Å rmsd, respectively), while differing from receptor-bound Aβ Conformation 1 from the same brain (1.505 Å rmsd) (Fig. 8a, b, Supplementary Fig. 7b). Conformation 1 is most similar to a structure reported for material isolated from Down’s syndrome brain37. These data support the enrichment of distinct Aβ42 conformations in these different pools of Aβ.

Impact of receptor-bound Aβ conformation

The biochemical separation and structural analysis of Conformation 1 found in receptor-bound Aβ reveals a different filament conformation from that of the sarkosyl-insoluble filamentous plaque Aβ. We considered whether the two conformations might exhibit different fibril-seeding properties when compared directly under similar conditions. We seeded the two AD brain species at 20 nM monomer equivalent concentration into solutions of 5 µM monomeric Aβ42 (1:250 molar ratio) and monitored polymerization rates using thioflavin T fluorescence (Fig. 8c–h). We also assessed Aβ fibrils prepared from a synthetic peptide in vitro and similarly fragmented by sonication. As expected, the seeding rate of Aβ42 was accelerated about two-fold in the presence of seeds from either sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ or synthetic fibrils as compared to Aβ42 monomers without seeds (Fig. 8e). The maximal fluorescence was similar with and without fibril seeds (Fig. 8d). In contrast, the receptor-bound Aβ species significantly slowed polymerization and reduced the maximal fluorescence achieved (Fig. 8c–e). We examined the material present at the completion of the seeding reaction by negative stain EM (Fig. 8f–k). The amyloid filaments generated with no seeds (Fig. 8f), sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ seeds, or synthetic fibril seeds (Fig. 8g) were long, averaging more than 2000 nm in length. The filament lengths generated by seeding with receptor-bound Aβ purified from four different brains were significantly shorter, with most in the 200–500 nm length range (Fig. 8h–k). These data indicate that receptor-bound Aβ seeds a unique conformation as compared to filamentous plaque Aβ or synthetic fibrils.

Discussion

We have isolated, purified to homogeneity, characterized, and determined the structure of the receptor-bound Aβ pool from late-onset sporadic AD brain. The receptor-bound pool is tenfold more prevalent than the free soluble Aβ fraction (4.8 ± 1.1% versus 0.4 ± 0.1% of total Aβ). Receptor-bound Aβ is uniform in shape, being composed of about 65 nm long rods containing on the order of 300 monomers in an amyloid filament. The distinctness of this pool is demonstrated by sequential extractions, by PrPC dependence, by cryo-EM-determined structure, and by seeding properties. By virtue of its elution from brain receptor sites, the receptor-bound Aβ species has the most direct relevance for disease pathophysiology.

Across the spectrum of AD-related Aβ peptide aggregation, the definition of plaque-associated fibrils and soluble monomers is clear. In contrast, oligomers and protofilaments are less well defined. The term oligomer is typically employed to delineate diffusible species ranging from 2 to 100s of Aβ monomers, without a fibrillar nature. The term protofilament has generally been applied to soluble, high molecular weight thread-like assemblies that have the capacity to add monomers and elongate into an insoluble fibril. Previous work had postulated an oligomeric state based on dimer or trimer assemblies, but a defined oligomer has never been isolated and characterized at the atomic level. Moreover, one original report supporting an oligomeric form has been retracted38. Our characterization of receptor-bound Aβ documents an assembly that would most commonly be termed a protofilament, and not an oligomer.

While the structure of receptor-bound Aβ substantially overlaps with long Aβ filaments from insoluble fractions that are largely plaque-derived, the cryo-EM-derived atomic structure of the two forms is distinct, even when comparing fractions from a single brain. Conformation 1 is only found in the receptor-bound fraction and has unique fibril properties. The altered conformation of amino-terminal Aβ residues 9–15 is coupled with different tilts for Aβ monomers in each rung, and a different twist for the receptor-bound Aβ filament. Receptor-bound Aβ abundance may be crucial for disease activity and progression. In particular, we hypothesize that the concentration of Aβ assembly tip ends and the presence of Conformation 1 are key factors for neuronal damage in AD.

Nonetheless, a single receptor-bound filament can show structural heterogeneity with intermixing of segments containing Conformation 1 with Conformation 2. This correlates with in vitro studies of synthetic Aβ fibrils showing heterogeneity within an individual fibril39,40. The temporal relationship between receptor-bound Aβ and fibrillary Aβ is not yet clear. They may interconvert freely or sequentially, or be relatively separate, non-interchangeable pathways of polymerization. However, the different states of irreversible proteolytic processing imply that the pools are not fully interconvertible within the time course of Aβ proteolysis.

PrPC binds several different ß-sheet-rich Aβ assemblies, including globulomers, ADDLs, TBS-soluble human brain Aβo, and receptor-bound Aβ. While fibrillary Aβ from synthetic sources shows little affinity for PrPC, fragmentation to create filament tips supports robust PrPC avidity. These findings imply a specific interaction of PrPC with filament tips and a role in titrating Aβ filament elongation but not side-to-side templating. While PrPC binds to the tip ends of different conformations, it may favor or induce the more twisted Conformation 1 by virtue of its presence. Regardless, this observation highlights the potential importance of assessing tip end concentration in biological samples.

The molecular size and PrPC interactions of Aβ extracted from AD brain have been examined15,41–43. In previous studies without PSCMA, the vast majority of receptor-bound Aβ remains in the insoluble fraction, mixed with abundant insoluble fibrillar Aβ species. Previous work has indicated that a portion of such insoluble Aβ from AD brain is physically associated with PrPC, and that insoluble Aβ species can interact with PrPC ex vivo42,43. TBS-soluble fractions of AD brain contain only relatively minor amounts of Aβ, and density gradient ultracentrifugation indicates assemblies of 8–500 kDa44. The TBS-soluble Aβ assemblies capable of interacting with PrPC are of 100–500 kDa15, and they trigger Fyn kinase activity in WT neurons but not in PrPC null neurons or in the presence of anti-PrPC antibody41. The high molecular weight PrPC-interacting “oligomeric” species found in the TBS-soluble fraction may or may not be on a folding pathway that leads to the receptor-bound Aβ conformation. The structure of free, unbound, soluble Aβo is not explored here. A recent study in which chunks of minced brain tissue were soaked to release these species has suggested that they may be most similar to insoluble Aβ filaments of Conformation 216.

Why are the receptor-bound Aβ species limited to the 65 nm size range? This may reflect an equilibrium between the rate of Aβ polymer extension at brain Aβ monomer concentrations and the capping of Aβ filament ends determined by brain concentrations of receptor binding sites such as PrPC. Alternatively, the twist of rungs and the tilting of monomers within rungs for Conformation 1 may make further polymerization unfavored. Our comparative seeding assays support the hypothesis that the receptor-bound Aβ conformation is a distinct “strain” of Aβ amyloid.

It is known that Aβ undergoes some post-translational modifications, such as proteolysis, pyroglutamylation, phosphorylation, and isomerization45. Here, epitope profiling demonstrated similar amounts of pyroglutamylation for receptor-bound Aβ and sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ. In contrast, sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ exhibits more extensive N-terminal proteolysis as compared to receptor-bound Aβ. Due to the electrostatic nature of the PrPC-Aβ interaction32, it is conceivable that increased proteolysis of the N-terminus of Aβ, which contains multiple negatively charged amino acids, would reduce PrPC binding affinity for the sarkosyl-insoluble fraction. Future studies will provide a more comprehensive assessment of variable posttranslational modification between Aβ pools and will determine whether they alter PrPC binding.

Previous studies have indicated different conformations for Aβ filaments from familial AD cases and from mouse transgenic preparations10,12. We have not assessed receptor-bound Aβ species from these sources. Studies of these alternate sources may further illuminate the relationship between insoluble fibrillary Aβ and receptor-bound Aβ. If receptor-bound conformations are shared between sources while fibrillary conformations are not, this would support a hypothesis that Conformation 1 and Conformation 2 are not exchangeable and are distinct polymerization paths.

Therapeutic anti-Aβ antibodies have recently received regulatory approval. Of note, mAb158, the murine version of lecanemab, was initially raised against protofibrils generated by recombinant Aβ peptide containing the Arctic mutation, which favors protofilament and oligomer reactivity3,33,46, and we show that receptor-bound Aβ is recognized by this antibody. Donanemab development was based on pyroglutamylated Aβ4,47, which we show is present equally in receptor-bound Aβ and insoluble fibrillary Aβ. The efficacy of these antibodies may be derived from the clearance of the receptor-bound Aβ conformation described here, even though they also recognize Conformation 2 of insoluble fibrillary Aβ and clear plaque. The development of antibodies that are specific for Conformation 1 versus Conformation 2 is an intriguing possibility. Such a reagent would allow an assessment of whether the receptor-bound species is selectively responsible for neuronal dysfunction in vivo. If so, selective Confirmation 1 blockade might achieve clinical benefit without the amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA)-associated complications48 of more complete Aβ clearance from plaque and vasculature with the existing non-selective antibodies.

Methods

Ethical statement

The use of pre-existing de-identified human brain samples here was deemed exempt from ethics regulation by the Yale University Human Investigation Committee. Autopsies were performed prior to this study by Yale University with written informed consent from next of kin. In the current study, only previously collected brain tissue without personal identifying information was used. All of the procedures here were determined to be “Not Human Research” and not subject to Common Rule—Research 45 CFR 46.102(l) by review of the Yale Human Investigation Committee. All mouse experiments, including animal husbandry, genotyping, and euthanasia, were approved by Yale’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, protocol #07281).

Human brain tissue samples

The autopsy brain specimens were collected and fresh-frozen at −80 °C at Yale University in New Haven, CT. Exclusion criteria for tissue specimens included prolonged agonal state and other confounding medical conditions. Each sample includes a pseudonymized full autopsy report of neuropathology evaluation and neuro/AD diagnosis based on recommendations of the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA)49, including stains with antibodies to a-synuclein, Aβ, and tau as well as CERAD and Braak staging. A detailed description of the age and sex, race/ethnicity of the donors, histopathology, and clinical diagnosis is provided in Supplementary Table 1. There is no remaining tissue from these samples.

IC16 antibody design, expression, purification, and conjugation to HaloLink resin

The protein sequence of IC16 ScFv was previously published50. A gene fragment based on reverse-translated IC16 ScFv amino acid sequence was synthesized by IDT technology, amplified by PCR, and cloned into pcDNA3 mammalian expression vector as an N-terminal fusion to the HALO tag with IgG kappa signal peptide to achieve secreted expression. IC16 antibody was expressed using the Expi293 Expression system and purified by nickel affinity chromatography. In brief, the media from transfected cells was bound in batch on nickel NTA resin (Qiagen). Resin was collected and washed with 20 bed volumes of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) then 20 bed volumes of PBS plus 10 mM imidazole. IC16 was eluted using PBS plus 250 mM imidazole. The eluted IC16 antibody was pooled, concentrated to 1 mg/mL, and dialyzed three times against PBS. The resulting purified antibody was stored at 4 °C, since we noted that freeze–thaw cycles have greatly reduced affinity.

To conjugate purified IC16 to a resin, the antibody was diluted to 0.25 mg/mL with PBS and mixed with HaloLink Resin (Promega) (1 mg of IC16 to 1 mL of resin) for at least 4 h at room temperature. Conjugation was monitored by reading the unbound absorbance at 280 nm until >95% of the IC16 was conjugated to the resin. The resin was then collected and washed with 5 bed volumes of PBS + 0.001% DDM, 5 bed volumes of water, 1 bed volume of 0.2% ammonium hydroxide, and then immediately with 10 bed volumes of PBS. IC16-conjugated HaloLink resin was stored in PBS with 0.01% sodium azide.

To prepare the denatured IC-16 conjugated HaloLink resin used for preclearing, the resin was washed with 3 bed volumes of 6 M guanidine hydrochloride and then incubated with 3 bed volumes of 6 M guanidine hydrochloride for 60 min at room temperature. After denaturation, the resin was washed thoroughly with at least 20 column volumes of PBS. This completely inactivates the IC16-ScFv, but leaves it covalently attached to the resin beads, allowing the use of this resin for nonspecific binder absorption.

Recombinant PrP expression and purification

Recombinant PrPC was produced as described32,51. In short, the human PrP plasmid in the pRSET-A vector containing a 6His-tag and a thrombin cleavage site was transformed into BL21(DE3) competent cells (NEB). Overnight growth of a starter culture in non-inducing medium MDAG135 was followed by a large-volume growth at room temperature in LB5052 autoinduction medium containing 1:1000 trace metals (Teknova, T1001), 2 mM MgSO4, 100 µg/mL ampicillin, 1× salt M, and 1 × 5052 autoinduction sugar mix52. Cells were harvested by a 30-min centrifugation at 4000×g and lysed in Buffer G (6 M Guanidine HCl, 10 mM reduced glutathione, 100 mM Na2HPO4, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8). The lysate was centrifuged at 17,700 × g for 90 min, and the supernatant fraction was loaded onto HisPur Ni–NTA resin (Qiagen) in a gravity flow column. Protein was allowed to refold with a stepwise gradient of Buffer B (100 mM Na2HPO4, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8) in Buffer G and eluted with 600 mM imidazole in 10 mM Na2HPO4, pH 5.8. Eluted protein was dialyzed in 3.5 kDa Slide-A-Lyzer cassettes (ThermoFisher), in 5 mM Na2HPO4, pH 5.8, for 2 h, and then overnight in water. His-tag was removed by overnight incubation with thrombin at 1 unit/mg. Subsequent cation exchange chromatography was performed using a Source 15S column in AKTApure FPLC, equilibrated in 10 mM Na2HPO4, pH 6.5, and eluted in a 0–100% 20 CV gradient of 1 M NaCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, pH 6.5. Fractions were collected based on A280 in a single peak on the chromatogram and followed the same dialysis procedures.

Preparation of synthetic Aβ oligomers

For comparison with brain-derived receptor-bound Aβ, samples of synthetic Aβ42 oligomer as ADDLs (F12) and globulomer were prepared as described previously15,32.

Immunoassays

To prepare the material for the epitope profiling studies by immunoblot, both the purified receptor-bound Aβ and sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ fractions were lyophilized in screw-cap microcentrifuge tubes, then resuspended in HFIP and incubated for 2 h at 70 °C to monomerize the Aβ assemblies. The tubes were then opened and kept in the fume hood overnight at room temperature to evaporate the HFIP. The remaining protein films were resuspended in 1× Laemmli buffer supplemented with 8 M urea. The resulting samples were separated on 4–20% gradient Criterion Tris-Glycine gels (BioRad), transferred onto the nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with Rockland fluorescent Western Blot blocking buffer, and immunoprobed with the appropriate detection antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed in PBST, incubated with the IRdye conjugated secondary antibodies (LiCor) for 1 h at RT, and imaged on the near-infrared scanner (LiCor Odyssey). Both primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in Rockland fluorescent Western Blot blocking buffer. The immunoblot data quantification was performed using the LiCor ImageStudio software.

To measure the levels of Aβ assemblies in complex lysates as well as various steps of purification, several time-resolved fluorescence immunoassays were used. The general protocol has been previously published15. White 384-well MaxiSorp polystyrene microplates (Thermofisher, 460372) were coated overnight at 4 °C with 20 µL/well of coating solution (30 mM Na2CO3, 80 mM NaHCO3, pH 9.6) containing 250 nM PrPC (for PLISA assay), 100 nM IC16-Halo ScFv (for IC16–8243 assay), 5 µg/mL lecanemab (Sellekchem #A3112) (for lecanemab–8243 assay), or 5 µg/mL Human IgG1 isotype control antibody (Syd #PA007125). Following the coating, the plates were washed with 3 dispensing/aspiration cycles of 100 µL/well of PBST (PBS, 0.05% Tween 20) using ELx405 microplate washer (BioTek) and blocked with 100 µL/well of Protein-Free T20 PBS blocking buffer (ThermoFisher, 37573) for 4 h at room temperature and then washed with PBST as above. Alongside the tested samples, three-fold serial dilutions of ADDL Aβo13,15,17 in F12 in PBSTB (PBST + 0.5% BSA) were applied to the coated plates in triplicate at 20 µL/well. The samples were diluted twofold with PBSTB and applied in triplicate. Additionally, three 4× dilutions were made for each sample to ensure linear quantitation in the samples that have widely different Aβ levels. The plates were incubated with samples overnight at 4 °C, washed 3× with PBST, and then incubated with D54D2 anti-Aβ rabbit monoclonal antibody (Epitope in N terminus of Aβ peptide, Cell Signaling Technology, #8243) diluted 1:2000 in PBSTB overnight at 4 °C. After 3× PBST wash to remove the unbound primary antibody, 20 µL/well of 1:8000 dilution of Eu-N1 Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Aβ and human lysate plates, Perkin-Elmer AD0105) in DELFIA assay buffer (Perkin Elmer 4002-0010) was applied for 2 h. Finally, for all the plates, a final PBST wash was performed, 20 µL of DELFIA Enhancement Solution (Perkin Elmer 4001-0010) was added to each well, and time-resolved Europium fluorescence was measured using Victor 3 V plate reader (Perkin Elmer).

Receptor-bound Aβ purification from AD brain tissue

Post-mortem human brain slices were collected, fresh frozen on dry ice, and stored at −80 °C as described10. For large-scale purifications, approximately 80 g (wet weight) of brain was used. To prepare purified Aβ from human brain, frozen frontal lobe cerebral cortex was minced into 0.25–0.5 mm3 pieces and Dounce-homogenized on ice in four volumes of homogenization buffer 1× Tris-buffered saline (TBS) supplemented with 1× complete EDTA-free protease inhibitors (Roche). After homogenization, the homogenate volume was adjusted with homogenization buffer to result in 10 volumes of homogenization buffer per gram of brain. The homogenate was centrifuged for 30 min at 100,000 × g at 4 °C. The resulting pellet, P1, was then homogenized with 10 volumes of homogenization buffer and centrifuged again for 30 min at 100,000 × g at 4 °C. The resulting pellet, P2, was homogenized again in 10 volumes of 0.25 × phosphate-buffered saline (0.25× PBS) and centrifuged again for 30 min at 100,000 × g at 4 °C to generate a P3 pellet. For exhaustive extraction, the P3 pellet was homogenized again in 0.25× PBS as above and centrifuged an additional 3–5 times. To generate purified Aβ used for cryo-EM and biological assays, the P3 pellet was first homogenized in five volumes of 0.25× PBS, and then five volumes of 1/4× PBS with 200 µM 20 kDa Poly(4-Styrenesulfonic acid-co-maleic acid) sodium salt (PSCMA, Sigma 434566-250 G) was added (100 µM PSCMA final concentration) and incubated on a nutator for 1 h at 4 °C. This extract was then centrifuged for 60 min at 100,000×g at 4 °C to generate supernatant S4, which contains PSCMA-extracted Aβ species and P4 pellet. At that point, the S4 supernatant and pellets were frozen and stored at −80 °C until further steps.

To purify and enrich receptor-bound Aβ further, S4 was subjected to immunopurification. First, S4 was precleared with denatured IC16 conjugated HALO resin on a nutator for 4 h at 4 °C (1 mL resin to 80 mL of extract) to adsorb the proteins nonspecifically binding to the resin. After collecting the preclearing resin by centrifugation, the precleared supernatants were collected, and IC-16 conjugated HALO resin was then added (1 mL resin to 40 mL of precleared S4) and mixed overnight at 4 °C to bind Aβ to the resin. After binding, the resin was collected in a large column and washed with five column volumes (CV) of PBS. The resin was then transferred to 10 mL spin columns and washed further with another 5 CV PBS. Resin was washed 2 × 2 CV milliQ water via centrifugation. The resin was slightly dry after the second centrifugation step. Aβ was eluted via centrifugation three times with one CV elution buffer (0.2% ammonium hydroxide). After each elution, the pH was neutralized using 0.3 volumes of 1% formic acid to pH 7.4. The resin was then washed thoroughly with PBS, and the unbound was then bound and eluted for a second passage. After elution, n-Dodecyl-beta-Maltoside (DDM) was added to 0.001% to prevent sample sticking to spin concentrators. The sample was finally concentrated 50-fold using 0.5 mL Amicon 50 kDa spin concentrators (Millipore). The resulting concentrate was aliquoted and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

To generate sarkosyl-insoluble fibrils, the pellet P4 was extracted by a previously published protocol10. In brief, the P4 pellet was homogenized in 20 volumes of extraction buffer (10 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 800 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 10% sucrose, and 1× complete EDTA-free protease inhibitors). Sarkosyl was then added to 2% to the homogenate and incubated for 60 min shaking at 37 °C. This was then spun for 10 min at 10,000 × g at 25 °C, and the supernatant was subsequently spun at 100,000 × g. The pellet was then resuspended in the extraction buffer at 1 mL/gram of the initial P4 pellet. This was diluted threefold with 50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 10% sucrose, and 0.2% sarkosyl. This was first spun for 3 min at 3000 × g, and then the supernatant was spun at 100,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting pellets were washed by resuspending the pellet with resuspension buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl) and collected by centrifugation at 18,000 × g for 30 min. This washing procedure was done three times. After the third wash, the pellets were resuspended in 0.1 mL resuspension buffer per 1 gram of initial P4 pellet, aliquoted, and flash frozen and stored at −80 °C.

Sequential extractions from mouse brain tissue

APPswe/PSen1ΔE9 mice on WT or Prnp−/− background were previously described22. The mice were housed and handled in compliance with IACUC regulations. Animals were sacrificed at 9–10 months of age, and the extracted brains were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use. The previous study22 showed no difference in amyloid-ß levels between males and females for these strains, so sex was not considered as a variable. To perform the biochemical brain fractionation, the tissue was first Dounce-homogenized in 10 volumes of TBS. The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 100,000×g for 30 min, the TBS supernatants were saved and frozen at −80 °C, and the pellets were resuspended in 10 volumes of TBS containing 1% Triton X100 (TritonX), incubated 30 min at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 30 min. The TritonX supernatant was collected, and the pellets were resuspended in 1/4× PBS and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 30 min. The pellets resulting from this 1/4× PBS wash were resuspended in 30 µM PSCMA dissolved in 1/4× PBS, incubated 1 h at 4 °C, and then centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h. The resulting supernatant with PSCMA-extracted material was collected and frozen at −80 °C, and the pellets were then used for the extraction of insoluble proteins using 8 M urea, 4% SDS dissolved in TBS. The supernatants from TBS, TritonX, and PSCMA extractions were then analyzed using plate-based Aβ assays.

Synapse loss from human iPSC-derived cortical neurons

Human neurons derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) with Neurogenin2 (Ngn2) stably integrated under doxycycline control were prepared and cultured as described53. No new cells were generated in this work. The iPSC cells were obtained from Martin Kampmann, as cited in ref. 53 and used without modification. Poly-D-Lysine-coated 384 well plates (Corning # 354) were seeded with 2000 cells in 50 µL of Classic Neuronal Media (CNR: 50% DMEM/F12 (Gibco), 50% Neurobasal-A (Gibco), 0.5× Glutamax (Gibco), 1× MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids Solution (Gibco), 0.5× B27 supplement (Gibco), 0.5× N-2 supplement (Gibco), 10 ng/mL BDNF (PeproTech), 10 ng/mL NT-3 (PeproTech), 1 µg/mL mouse Laminin (Gibco)) containing 2 µg/mL doxycycline hydrochloride (Sigma). On DIV 7 and 14, 25 µL of CNR without doxycycline hydrochloride was added to each well. Every week after that, 30 µL of media was removed from each well and replaced with 30 µL of fresh media. Cells were grown for at least 60 days before treatment with receptor-bound Aβo.

Receptor-bound Aβ and the equivalent control brain fraction were prepared by dialyzing three times against 1000× volume of Neurobasal-A using 100 µL dialysis cartridges (Thermo 69555) at 4 °C. On the day of the experiment, 75% of the media was removed and mixed 1:1 with fresh CNR media, and then Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Gibco) was added to 1× and filtered. The rest of the media was carefully flicked out of the plate, and then 90 µL of the filtered media was added back to each well, and the plates were returned to the 37 °C incubator for 2 h. After 2 h, 10 µL of dialyzed brain-derived receptor-bound Aβ (with or without 200 nM anti-PrP antibody or 200 nM lecanemab), control brain, dialysis media, or synthetic Aβ 1–42 fibrils (with and without sonication) were added to the 384-well plate, and the plate was returned to the incubator for 7 days. A minimum of four wells from each culture were dosed with each preparation and were replicated on three separate plates.

After 7 days, plates were removed from the incubator, media was flicked out and replaced with 17 µL of fixation buffer (4% paraformaldehyde, 6% sucrose, and 1× PBS) and then returned to a 37 °C incubator for 30 min. The plates were washed two times with 1× PBS using a plate washer (Agilent) and 17 µL of blocking buffer (10 % horse serum (Gibco) in 1× PBST (PBS with 0.1% TritonX-100 (Sigma)) and incubated at room temperature for 30–60 min. Blocking buffer was removed and replaced with 17 µL of primary antibody solution (1% horse serum in PBST) containing 1:500 guinea pig anti-Synapsin 1/2 (Synaptic Systems # 106004), 1:500 rabbit anti-Homer1b/c (Synaptic Systems #160022), and 1:3000 chicken anti-MAP2 (Novus Biologics # NB300-213) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Plates were then washed two times with 1× PBS and replaced with secondary antibody solution (1% horse serum in PBST) containing 1:500 goat anti-guinea pig Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo), 1:500 goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo), 1:500 goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 647 (Thermo), and 1:500 DAPI (Thermo) for 60 min at room temperature. Plates were then washed five times with 1× PBS and imaged. Cultures were imaged using ImageXpress Micro Confocal High-Content Imaging System (Molecular Devices) equipped with a 40× water objective. Four images from each well were collected as maximum intensity projections of a 3.6 µm Z-stack with a 0.4 µm spacing.

Image analysis was performed using ImageXpress built in analysis software, MetaXpress. First, the MAP2 image was thresholded using an adaptive thresholding set to 1–10 µm, and then the mask was expanded by 5 pixels. Then, both the Synapsin1/2 and Homer1b/c channels were run through a TopHat filter to even out the background signal and then through a find-round-objects filter to pick out the pre- and post- synaptic features for Synapsin1/2 and Homer1b/c, respectively. Synapse density was calculated by dividing the number of colocalized Synapsin1/2 and Homer1b/c puncta within the thresholded MAP2 area by the total MAP2 area. To exclude images with sparse or over-confluent cell densities, we removed images with total MAP2 intensities falling outside two standard deviations of the mean. Then the values for the MAP2 area or synapse density for each image collected from the same well were averaged. Then, the fold change relative to the media-only control for each measurement was calculated as (Synaptic measurement (experimental condition))/(Synaptic measurement (control condition)).

Immunogold negative staining

Six microliter of brain-extracted receptor-bound Aβ were mixed with 2 µL of recombinant PrPC (50 nM) directly on a glow-discharged 300 mesh carbon-coated copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and allowed to incubate for 5 min at room temperature. After that, excess liquid from the grid surface was blotted off, and the grid was floated onto a 40 µL paraformaldehyde (PFA) 2% for 2 min. Next, the carbon-coated side part of the grid was immediately floated onto a 40 µL droplet of blocking buffer deposited onto a Parafilm in a humidified chamber for 10 min. The blocking buffer consisted of a mixture of 0.2 g of BSA dissolved in 25 mL PBS with 25 µL of cold-water skin fish gelatin and 0.5 mL of 1% Tween20 (blocking buffer for TEM, Cold Spring Harbor Protocol). Subsequently, the excess solution was gently removed from the grid surface, and the carbon-coated side of the grid was floated onto a 40 µL droplet of primary antibody, mouse anti-PrP (3F4—mouse primary antibody), diluted 1:50 in blocking buffer) for 1 h at room temperature in the same humidified chamber. Following that, the grid was washed with 5 drops of the blocking buffer (each washing was 2 min in 40 µL of blocking buffer), with the excess liquid blotted off between each wash. Subsequently, the grid was incubated with 6 nm gold-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (diluted 1:20 in the blocking buffer) for 30 min at room temperature inside the humidified chamber. After five washes with Milli-Q water (each washing was 2 min), the grid was stained with 2 drops of 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate. The grid was briefly stained in the first drop, and in the second drop, the grid was stained for 2 min. The sample was air-dried, and electron microscopy images were acquired on an FEI Tecnai T12 transmission electron microscope. The negative staining images revealed the presence of PrPC protein in close proximity to the tips of receptor-bound Aβ. Filament length measurements for the location of gold-labeled PrPC were done in Bsoft54.

Thioflavin-T polymerization assays

Fibril polymerization was monitored using Thioflavin-T (ThT) fluorescence, similar to previous studies55–60. The polymerization reactions were prepared in a 96-well half-area clear-bottom plate (Corning) containing 100 µL/well. The plates were sealed with a plate sealer to prevent evaporation and incubated at 37 °C in the Victor Nivo multimode plate reader with continuous shaking (double orbital, 600 rpm). ThT fluorescence readings were recorded every 2 min for an interval of 36-48 h with excitation and emission wavelengths of 435 nm and 480 nm, respectively.

Prior to the polymerization reaction, to remove preformed aggregates and monomerize synthetic Aβ42 (The ERI Amyloid Laboratory, LLC), the peptide was first dissolved in 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol58,61 (HFIP) to 10 mg/mL, heated for 2 h at 70 °C in sealed tubes, aliquoted into microfuge tubes (0.5 mg/tube), dried overnight, and then put under vacuum for one hour to remove residual HFIP. The HFIP-treated Aβ42 film was first dissolved in 20 µL of anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and vortexed for 2 min to achieve a 5 mM Aβ42 solution. This was diluted to 20 µM with 20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, and immediately centrifuged at 14,000×g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble aggregates. The resulting supernatant containing Aβ42 monomer was used for polymerization reactions, and monomerization was confirmed using negative stain EM (data not shown).

For the self-polymerization of synthetic Aβ42 monomer, each 100 µL reaction mixture consisted of 5 µM synthetic Aβ42 monomer and 10 µM ThT in polymerization buffer (20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4).

For seeding reactions with synthetic Aβ42 fibril, the seeds were added in each well to generate a final concentration of 20 nM monomer equivalent concentration of Aβ42 seeds, equaling 0.4% of the unpolymerized Aβ42 in the reaction. These seeds were derived from Aβ filaments quiescently grown over 3 weeks at 23 °C from 100 µM Aβ42 in 20 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, pH 7.4. The resulting Aβ42 filaments were diluted to 2 μM monomer equivalent concentration in a final volume of 2 mL, and seeds were prepared by sonication using a probe sonicator (Branson) for 2 min on and 2 min off for a minimum of six times at 50% duty cycle and 25% output power. In control reactions, this sonication yielded filaments of 50–100 nm average length by negative stain EM.

For the seeding with sarkosyl-insoluble human Aβ fibril seeds, the reaction mixture consisted of 5 µM final concentration of synthetic Aβ42 monomer, 10 μM final concentration of ThT, and appropriate dilutions of sarkosyl-insoluble human Aβ fibril seeds in each well. Prior to adding to the seeding reaction, the sarkosyl-insoluble human Aβ fibrils from Brain A and B were dialyzed against the polymerization buffer for at least 24 h. After dialysis, the sarkosyl-insoluble human Aβ fibrils were diluted to approximately 4 µM monomer equivalent concentration with polymerization buffer, and seeds were generated using a probe sonicator (Branson) for 2 min on and 2 min off for a minimum of 6 times at 50% duty cycle and 25% output power. In control reactions, this sonication yielded filaments of 50–100 nm average length by negative stain EM.

For the seeding with AD purified receptor-bound Aβ, the reaction mixture consisted of 5 µM final concentration of synthetic Aβ42 monomer, 10 µM final concentration of ThT, and 10–40 nM (monomer equivalent) final concentrations of receptor-bound Aβ (from Brain A, B, C, and D) and polymerization buffer in each 100 µL volume well. Prior to adding to the seeding reaction, the human receptor-bound Aβ was dialyzed against the polymerization buffer for at least 24 h. The following control conditions for receptor-bound Aβ effects were similarly dialyzed and found to have no effect on Aβ42 monomer polymerization: (a) parallel preparations from control brain, (b) the vehicle buffer used for receptor-bound Aβ, (c) 1.7 nM IC16 antibody, and (d) 5 nM PSCMA.

For the self-polymerization of synthetic Aβ42 monomer in the presence of PrPC, the seeding reactions contained 5 µM synthetic Aβ42 monomer, 400 nM recombinant PrPC, and 10 µM ThT in polymerization buffer. For seeding reactions with synthetic Aβ42 fibril seeds and PrPC, the final concentrations were 5 µM Aβ42 monomer, 30 nM monomer equivalent concentration of Aβ42 seeds, 400 nM PrPC, and 10 μM ThT in polymerization buffer.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data acquisition

Four µL of the purified receptor-bound Aβ or sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ fibrils were applied on the carbon side of glow-discharged (Edwards S150B) holey-carbon-coated grids (Quantifoil 300 mesh gold grids R 1.2/1.3, from Electron Microscopy Sciences). Subsequently, the sample was blotted using filter paper (Ø55/20 mm, Grade 595, Ted Pella) for 3–4 s at a ‘blot force’ of 0 or ‘−1’ and plunged frozen in liquid ethane with a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo). The sample chamber of the Vitrobot was maintained at 4 °C and a humidity level of 100%. The vitrified grids were screened on a Glacios transmission electron microscope (Thermo) operated at 200 keV and equipped with a K2 Summit direct electron detector. Electron micrograph movies for three-dimensional image reconstruction were acquired in super-resolution counting mode using a 300 keV Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo) equipped with a high brightness field emission gun (XFEG), a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan), and a GIF Quantum LS imaging energy filter (Gatan) (slit width of 20 eV) at the Yale Cryo-EM resources. The Serial-EM software62 was used for the automated collection of the micrograph movies at a nominal magnification of 81,000× (corresponding to a pixel size of 1.068 Å at the specimen level) with a defocus range of −1.25 to −2.5 µm. All parameters corresponding to the cryo-EM data acquisition are presented in Table 1.

Cryo-EM data processing

Data sets were processed using standard amyloid helical reconstruction procedures implemented in RELION 4.063. Dose-fractionated micrograph movies were corrected for beam-induced motion using the RELION’s own implementation of motion correction64. Contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated from the motion-corrected micrographs using CTFFIND-4.165. The start-end coordinates of the Aβ filaments were manually marked in the aligned micrographs using RELION 4.0. Segments were extracted with an inter-box distance of 12-Å, a helical rise of 4.75 Å and using a box size of 200 pixels with 1.068 Å per pixel. Extracted segments were subjected to several rounds of reference-free two-dimensional (2D) classification to remove suboptimal segments and to select for segments showing detailed features along the filament axis. A featureless cylinder generated using the “relion_helix_toolbox” utility in RELION or a low-pass filtered (50 Å) previous reconstruction was used as a reference for an initial 3D classification of the helical segments retained after the 2D classification steps. The initial 3D classification performed on the set of optimal 2D-segments assumed a helical rise of 4.75 Å and a value of the helical twist as estimated from the crossover distances in the 2D class averages. Several rounds of 3D classification with optimization of helical twist and rise were performed to further select the best 3D classes for high-resolution 3D auto-refinement. Through 3D classification, two conformations were separated for the Aβ42 peptide monomer in the receptor-bound Aβ and one conformation for the Aβ42 peptide monomer in the sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ fibrils. A final round of 3D auto-refinement was performed after Bayesian polishing and per-particle CTF refinement64,66. The final reconstructions were post-processed with a soft-edge mask and B-factor sharpened. The resolution of each 3D reconstruction was estimated from the value of the Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) curve at 0.143 cutoff between two independently refined half-maps using phase randomization to correct for convolution effects of a generous, soft-edged solvent mask67. Details about cryo-EM data processing are presented in Table 1.

Model building and refinement, structural analysis, and visualization

Initial models were generated by rigid body fitting of PDB entry 7Q4B10 into the postprocessed electron microscopy maps of receptor-bound Aβ (Conformation 1, Brain A), receptor-bound Aβ (Conformation 2, Brain B), and sarkosyl-insoluble Aβ fibrils (Brain A). Rigid body fitting was performed using the fit-in-map tool in Chimera68. Subsequently, the atomic models were manually built and adjusted in Coot iteratively69 and refined using real-space refinement implemented in PHENIX70 for five macro-cycles with applied secondary structure, rotamer, and Ramachandran restraints. The models were validated using MolProbity71 and cryo-EM validation tools within PHENIX. Figures were prepared using UCSF Chimera and ChimeraX72.
