Iatrogenic cerebral amyloid angiopathy with inflammatory pathogenesis: A case report and literature review
Authors: Ryo Miyaoka, Shigeru Nishizawa, Yuko Tanaka, Junkoh Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Iatrogenic cerebral amyloid angiopathy (iCAA) is a rare form of amyloid β (Aβ)-mediated small vessel disease associated with prior neurosurgical exposure, such as cadaveric dura mater grafting or other Aβ-contaminated materials. The condition generally manifests decades later, presenting as intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), seizures, or cognitive decline. While the presence of inflammatory forms of sporadic cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is well-documented, an inflammatory variant of iCAA has not been previously documented. The aims of this report are to present the first case of inflammatory iCAA, integrating clinical, radiological, and pathological findings, and to highlight its therapeutic implications.Case Description: A 38-year-old man with a medical history of cadaveric dura mater grafting in infancy presented with recurrent ICHs and progressive cognitive decline. A brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed extensive cortical superficial siderosis, leptomeningeal enhancement, and arterial wall enhancement. These features are suggestive of inflammatory CAA. Brain Biopsy revealed cortical vessel Aβ deposition and mild parenchymal lymphocytic infiltration. We diagnosed inflammatory form of iCAA with the clinical findings. Steroid pulse therapy followed by a taper of oral prednisolone led to clinical and radiological improvement, with no recurrence of symptoms or hemorrhage during 24-month period of follow-up.Conclusion: This case offers the first neuroradiological evidence of an inflammatory phenotype in iCAA. The favorable response to immunosuppressive therapy indicates that inflammation may play a pathogenic role in this case. Early immunosuppressive therapy should be considered when inflammatory features are present in iCAA.

INTRODUCTION

Sporadic cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is a common cerebral small vessel disease primarily affecting older individuals. It is characterized by amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition in the media and adventitia of cortical and leptomeningeal vessels, caused by impaired clearance of Aβ through the intramural periarterial drainage and glymphatic systems.[4] This deposition leads to vascular endothelial damage, impaired vascular reactivity, intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and cerebral infarction, resulting in cognitive decline due to pathological changes in leptomeningeal and small cortical vessels.[18]

Recently, transmission of CAA through surgery has been reported and is recognized as iatrogenic CAA (iCAA).[2,8] Diagnosis usually occurs when patients develop ICH, cognitive decline, seizures, or transient focal neurological episodes (TFNE) after a latency exceeding three decades, following exposure to Aβ through cadaveric dural grafts, embolization material, human growth hormone, or surgical instruments.[7,9] Newly published diagnostic criteria have helped standardize the evaluation of this CAA subtype.[3,10]

The pathogenesis of iCAA is currently viewed as an irreversible process caused by Aβ deposition on vessel walls, similar to sporadic CAA. To date, no effective treatments have been identified.[3,8] We recently treated a patient with iCAA using immunosuppressants, achieving a successful response both immediately and during follow-up. Here, we report this case, discuss the speculated pathogenesis and treatment options, and review previous literature.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 38-year-old man experienced recurrent headaches and memory disturbances for 3 months. He was transferred to our university hospital following a headache and a TFNE involving right-sided hemiparesis, paresthesia, and dysarthria.

Computed tomography (CT) revealed an acute subcortical cerebral hemorrhage and convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage in the left frontal lobe. Post-craniotomy changes were noted in the right frontotemporal skull [Figures 1A]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed chronic intraparenchymal iron deposits in the left thalamus and disseminated cortical superficial siderosis (cSS) with leptomeningeal enhancement in both hemispheres [Figures 1B-D]. Vessel wall imaging revealed enhancement of both middle cerebral arteries [Figure 1E], and digital subtraction angiography showed multiple stenoses with a strings of beads appearance [Figure 1F]. Laboratory tests revealed no abnormalities in immune, coagulation, or hormonal function. A mild inflammatory response was observed (elevated C-reactive protein), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed elevated protein (77.1 mg/dL) without pleocytosis. The patient had no family history of stroke, dementia, or CAA but had undergone surgical evacuation of a traumatic right subdural hematoma at the age of 8 months, involving a cadaveric dura mater graft.

Following admission, TFNEs persisted despite antiseizure medication. Three months later, perampanel reduced TFNE frequency, with full remission at 4 mg/day. Repetitive electroencephalography showed no epileptic discharges, except intermittent slow waves in the right occipital lobe. Cognitive function declined rapidly, with the Mini-mental state examination (MMSE) score dropping from 25/30 to 19/30 within 3 months. Five recurrent ICHs occurred within 7 months [Figures 2A-E]. Multiple punctate foci with restricted diffusion were also observed throughout the disease course [Figures 2F-J].

To confirm the diagnosis, a pathological examination was performed. A biopsy from a non-lesional area of the left frontal cortex revealed CAA involving cortical vessels with diffuse parenchymal Aβ deposits. Mild lymphocytic infiltration was observed in the parenchymal lesion with obscure findings of perivascular inflammation, vasculitis, or fibrinoid degeneration [Figure 3]. No genetic mutations were found in APP, PSEN1, or PSEN2, and the apolipoprotein E genotype was ε3/ε3. These pathological, clinical, and imaging findings supported the diagnosis of iCAA with a vasculitis-like component, in accordance with diagnostic criteria.[3,4]

Steroid pulse therapy was initiated with 1 g/day methylprednisolone on day 110, followed by oral prednisolone at 1 mg/kg, tapered over 8 months. This led to marked improvement in cognitive function (MMSE score: 29/30) and resolution of headaches within 10 months. Leptomeningeal and arterial inflammation gradually subsided on MRI imaging, through cSS and cerebral microbleeds worsened [Figure 4]. The patient recovered well, with only partial visual field defects. At 24-month follow-up, no symptom recurrence was observed.

DISCUSSION

General concepts of inflammatory CAA according to the literature

Within the spectrum of CAA, there is a subgroup of amyloid-affected vessels referred to as inflammatory CAA.[1] Patients with inflammatory CAA exhibit specific clinical features, including subacute cognitive decline, seizures, TFNE, and headache. Inflammatory CAA also includes two types, Aβ-related angiitis (ABRA) and CAA-related inflammation. ABRA involves an angio-destructive process characterized by vasculitic, often granulomatous, transmural inflammatory infiltration. In contrast, CAA-related inflammation is marked by an inflammatory reaction around amyloid-laden vessels without angio-destructive features.[17] However, whether these two conditions represent a single pathological spectrum remains unclear. Furthermore, the mechanisms inducing inflammation in CAA, or whether both forms can coexist, have not been clarified.[16]

Aggressive form of iCAA

According to the literature, the annual recurrence rate of ICH in iCAA is 54–77%,[3,10] while that of sporadic CAA is 8.4%. Some iCAAs are more aggressive than sporadic CAA, suggesting distinct mechanisms of ICH recurrence in these cases.

In our review of reported cases, we identified a characteristic course called the “chain of recurring ICHs” [Table 1],[5,6,11,12,13,20] defined as at least two recurrences within 6 months and three recurrences within 2 years. Patients without an MRI were excluded from analysis.

Several clinical and imaging features are associated with aggressive iCAA. First, MRI frequently reveals progressive cSS, which is known to predict ICH in CAA due to local reactive astrogliosis.[15] Second, most cases show generalized symptoms, such as seizures, TFNEs, consciousness disturbance, or rapidly progressive cognitive impairment. Localized cortical spreading depolarization is postulated as a mechanism for TFNEs, as it induces metabolic and blood flow imbalance, vasoconstriction, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier, leading to ICH recurrence. TFNEs are known to increase the risk of future symptomatic ICH.[19] Third, imaging often shows signs suggestive of inflammation, including parenchymal swelling, T2 hyperintensities in white matter, and leptomeningeal enhancement, indicating that inflammation may trigger ICH recurrence.

Considerations of the present case

Based on the above, the pathogenesis in the present case is considered as follows: the iCAA is suggested as ABRA within inflammatory CAA, based on clinical and neuroradiological features. The findings fulfilled diagnostic criteria for inflammatory CAA: (1) clinical manifestations of progressive headache, cognitive dysfunction, recurrent cerebral hemorrhage, and infarction; (2) neuroradiological evidence of leptomeningeal enhancement; and (3) elevated CSF protein levels.

From a neuroradiological standpoint, vessel wall enhancement and multiple strings of beads, like stenotic lesions in the cerebral arteries, were observed. These findings support the presence of angio-destructive vasculitis and have not been previously reported.[16,17] To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report providing direct neurobiological evidence of ARBA arterial involvement.

Histological examination was also performed to confirm inflammation, as no previous reports have demonstrated inflammatory pathology in iCAA. In this case, the specimen revealed diffuse lymphocytic infiltration in the parenchyma, though findings of perivascular infiltration, vasculitis, or fibrinoid generation were obscure [Figure 3]. These ambiguous results may have resulted from sampling errors due to the patchy nature of inflammation, emphasizing the need for careful specimen collection during biopsy.

Regarding treatment, immunosuppressive therapy using a steroid pulse followed by oral prednisolone was implemented. Since an inflammatory mechanism is suspected in this iCAA, steroids, cyclophosphamide, or mycophenolate have been reported as effective treatments.[14] It is also suggested that inflammatory CAA may have better outcomes than sporadic CAA when treated optimally. In this case, the patient recovered without recurrence of neurological or cognitive symptoms, reinforcing inflammation as a key factor in symptom development. No established treatment is available for iCAA; therefore, immunosuppressive therapy should be considered when inflammatory CAA is suspected based on neurological and radiological findings.[17] While pathological confirmation is helpful in guiding treatment, collecting representative surgical samples remains challenging. Careful and targeted biopsy procedures are therefore essential.

CONCLUSION

We report a case of iCAA with a rapidly progressive clinical course. Neurological and imaging findings suggested an underlying inflammatory pathology. The patient responded well to immunosuppressive therapy with a pulse steroid regimen. In cases of iCAA presenting with rapid clinical deterioration, inflammation may be a contributing factor. Early diagnosis based on clinical and neuroradiological findings is essential, and prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy – such as steroid pulse treatment – should be considered.
