The DELAYED ABAXIAL TRICHOMES Helitron has dual functions in vegetative and pollen development in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Erin Doody, Jianfei Zhao, Bishwas Sharma, R. Scott Poethig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceTransposable elements are important sources of genomic novelty across plants and animals. Via a genome-wide association study in Arabidopsis thaliana, we have identified a polymorphic Helitron/RC transposable element that is involved in multiple aspects of plant development. A fragment of this Helitron is required for gametogenesis and impacts the expression of a wide range of genes in trans, potentially through RNA-directed DNA methylation. Despite transposable elements being widespread in eukaryotic genomes, this study provides evidence that Helitrons can acquire anessential functions in plants. Thus, our findings provide a key role for transposons during genome evolution.

Transposable elements (TEs) make up a large portion of the genomes of higher plants and animals and play an important role in shaping genome evolution (1). They do this by rearranging the genome to create novel chromosome structures, by inserting within genes, introducing novel promoters, or influencing the chromatin structure of the region near their insertion site (2, 3). TEs can also impact gene expression in trans. In plants, this primarily involves the silencing of other TEs of the same type, which can also impact the expression of nearby protein-coding genes. This process is mediated by the production of small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that recruit the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) machinery to TEs with which they share complementarity, thus preventing their transposition and impacting the expression of adjacent protein genes (4–7).

Some TEs can directly regulate the expression of endogenous genes in trans. In Arabidopsis, Athila LTR retrotransposons and AtENSPLM5 transposons produce 21-nucleotide small interfering RNAs (21-nt siRNAs) when they become epigenetically activated. These siRNAs have homology to 24 endogenous genes, and silence at least seven of them via the RNA interference (RNAi) and RdDM pathways (2, 4). Transposon impact on gene expression in trans has been reported in numerous species, including Drosophila and humans, and it is well known that transposon-derived 24-nt sRNAs are important for establishing RdDM in trans to establish epigenomic landscape in gametophytes (8–11). However, the exact mechanism by which this happens is still not fully understood (12–14).

Helitrons, a class of repetitive transposable element, also have the potential to silence endogenous genes (15). Helitrons capture gene fragments during their transposition and typically carry sequences from two or more genes. In Arabidopsis, AtRep2 Helitrons contain fragments of several genes involved in response to herbivory. When activated, they produce 21-nt siRNAs homologous to these genes and interact with AGO1 and RdDM machinery to initiate DNA-methylation (16). However, it is unclear if these siRNAs are functionally important because factors that activate AtRep2 do not produce an increase in the abundance of these siRNAs, nor do they produce the expected decrease in transcript level of their predicted targets (16). In maize, members of the three classes of TEs that capture gene fragments (Helitrons, Pack-MULEs, sireviruses), have homology to and produce 24-nucleotide (24-nt) siRNAs that target these genes (17). In some of these targets, the region of homology to 24-nt siRNAs was methylated, suggesting that the siRNAs were responsible for this methylation. However, the expression of these methylated genes was unaffected. The functional consequences of trans regulation of methylation within endogenous genes by TEs is therefore still an open question.

In a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for the timing of vegetative phase change in Arabidopsis thaliana, we identified a polymorphic Helitron (AT3TE86735) that is associated with variation in the onset of abaxial trichome production (an adult leaf trait) during shoot development. We named this locus DELAYED ABAXIAL TRICHOMES (DAB). DAB has two major haplotypes, one of which delays abaxial trichome production and has a major effect on gene expression. CRISPR/Cas9 deletions of DAB in both haplotypes demonstrate that it also plays an essential role in postmeiotic pollen development; this function maps to 329 bp region within DAB that produces a unique set of 24-nt siRNAs. Thus, our results demonstrate how transposable elements can be co-opted to regulate essential processes in plant development.

Results

A QTL on Chromosome Three Is Linked to Delayed Abaxial Trichome Production.

Vegetative phase change refers to the transition from a juvenile to an adult pattern of development during the vegetative phase of shoot growth. It is dependent on a decline in the level of the microRNA, miR156, which produces an increase in abundance of its targets, SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE (SPL) transcription factors (18, 19). Recently, a number of studies have found that unknown mechanisms regulate phase-specific vegetative traits independently of miR156 (20–23); furthermore, natural genetic variation in the timing of vegetative phase change cannot be fully explained by the miR156/SPL pathway (24–27).

To identify additional loci responsible for natural variation in the timing of vegetative phase change we conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for the number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes, an adult leaf trait. We examined this trait in 77 natural accessions from diverse locations (Fig. 1A). Accessions were vernalized at 4 °C for 4 wk and then grown under noninductive short-day (SD) conditions to reduce the effects of floral induction on leaf development (28, 29). The number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes ranged from 2 to 18 leaves in these accessions (Fig. 1 B and C).

These abaxial trichome data were analyzed with the EasyGWAS portal (30) using an accelerated mixed-model association model (AMM) and the Tair 9 assembly as a reference. This analysis detected a quantitative trait locus (QTL) on the bottom of chromosome three associated with a significant difference in the onset of abaxial trichome production (Fig. 1D). The most statistically significant single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) within this QTL was a T to C polymorphism (−log(P-value = 9.18)) and the majority of the accessions that contain this SNP had delayed abaxial trichome production compared to the rest of the accessions in the GWAS (Fig. 1E). This and other closely linked SNPs are within a Helitron (AT3TE86735; Ath-b10r1-10302) in the AtRep10d family, which is located between the protein-coding genes REMORIN 4.1 (REM4.1; AT3G57540) and ARABIDOPSIS GUANALYTE KINASE 2 (AGK2; AT3G57550) (Fig. 1F).

Closer examination of this locus revealed a haplotype of SNPs that was present in all fifteen of the accessions that contained the T to C SNP, and resequencing revealed that this region contained deletions and insertions that were not annotated in the 1001 Genomes database (Fig. 2A). This haplotype was present in most, but not all, of the accessions with delayed abaxial trichome production compared to Col-0 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). For this reason, we named this locus DELAYED ABAXIAL TRICHOMES (DAB).

To investigate the role of DAB in vegetative phase change, we tested its impact on the timing of abaxial trichome development using a “Traffic Line” (TL) that spans the region. TLs contain two closely linked seed-specific green and red fluorescent transgenes, which visually mark the chromosome segment located between these transgenes (31, 32). We crossed four accessions to TL3.46, a TL in the Col-0 background that has markers on either side of the DAB locus (32) and self-pollinated the resulting F1 progeny to generate an F2 population (Fig. 2B). Seedlings from bright yellow (BF) and nonfluorescent (NF) F2 seeds were then scored for the number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes, under the assumption that they contained the Col-0 (BF) or the alternative (NF) allele of DAB.

The NF progeny derived from crosses of TL3.46 to Co-1 and IP-Orb-10, two accessions that contain the delayed trichome DAB haplotype, had significantly later abaxial trichomes than their BF siblings (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). In contrast, the BF and NF F2 progeny of plants derived from crosses between TL 3.46 and Xan-1 and Dog-4 (two accessions that have delayed abaxial trichome production but do not contain the most common “delayed trichome” haplotype of DAB) did not show a difference in number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). These results suggest that the haplotype of DAB uncovered by GWAS, or a region linked to this locus, is associated with delayed abaxial trichome production in Co-1 and IP-Orb-1. In addition, it shows that DAB is not the only locus that contributes to the delayed timing of abaxial trichomes production across all natural accessions of A. thaliana.

Protein-Coding Genes Linked to the Chromosome Three QTL Are Not Involved in Abaxial Trichome Production.

Once we established that DAB is linked to a gene or genes that have a role in timing of abaxial trichome production, we investigated whether the protein-coding genes adjacent to this locus are responsible for this phenotype. The gene directly downstream of DAB, AGK2, is a guanylate kinase involved in nucleotide metabolism (33), and was the most likely candidate because DAB lies in or near its promoter. We found that most accessions with the late DAB haplotype had significantly lower AGK2 expression than Col-0, which has the early haplotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). However, T-DNA mutants of agk2 (Col-0) that nearly completely eliminate its expression did not influence abaxial trichome production, despite having a reduced leaf base angle (Fig. 2 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E, G, and H). Additionally, accessions that contain the haplotype with reduced AGK2 expression have delayed abaxial trichomes, which is opposite to the “adult” leaf shape phenotype of mutants lacking AGK2 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and D).

The gene directly upstream of DAB is REM4.1. REM4.1 is a member of the Remorin family of genes, which mediate immune responses to viruses and bacteria (34). Two T-DNA insertions within the coding region of REM 4.1 (rem4.1-1 and rem4.1-2) had no observable effect on either the angle of the leaf base or on number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes (Fig. 2 E and G and SI Appendix, Fig S1 F, I, and J), demonstrating that this gene is not responsible for the phenotype of DAB (35). We also examined the phenotype of T-DNA insertions in five genes up- and downstream of the DAB QTL, and two insertions within the intergenic region between AGK2 and REM4.1. None of these insertions showed changes in timing of abaxial trichome production, or any phenotype associated with variation in the timing of vegetative phase change (SI Appendix, Fig. S1K). These data suggest that DAB—not adjacent protein-coding genes—contributes to natural variation in the timing of vegetative phase change.

DAB Is a Noncoding 24-nt siRNA-Producing Locus that Regulates the Timing Adaxial Trichome Production.

To test the possibility that DAB produces one or more noncoding RNAs, we examined small RNA (sRNA) sequencing data from published small RNA databases (36, 37). We identified a number of 24-nt siRNAs and a few siRNAs of other lengths that map exclusively to this locus (Fig. 3 A and B). sRNA sequencing data from various tissues and organs showed that these 24-nt siRNAs are more highly expressed in embryonic and juvenile tissues than in developmentally older tissues (Fig. 3C). These siRNAs are produced from two main peaks within the DAB locus, with the upstream peak being more polymorphic between Col-0 (early haplotype) and Co-1 (late haplotype) than the second (peak 1: p-distance = 0.633, peak 2: p-distance = 0.430) (Fig. 3B). Additionally, both regions have sequence homology to close relatives of A. thaliana, including Arabidopsis lyrata and Arabidopsis halleri (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). RT-qPCR using primers that span the intergenic regions containing DAB (Fig. 3A) and small-RNA sequencing (sRNA-seq) in immature flower buds of Col-0 and Co-1 confirmed that both ecotypes produce very low, but similar amounts of RNA from several sites within this region, with Co-1 producing slightly, but not significantly, more sRNAs (Fig. 3 D and E).

We ran target prediction software on the 24-nt reads produced by Col-0 and Co-1 (39) and found over 2,000 potential targets, with a surprising lack of overlap between Col-0 and Co-1 targets (results shown in Datasets S1–S4). The predicted targets had a wide range of biological functions, and no single Gene Ontology (GO) category was overrepresented on these gene lists. However, the majority of the top 100 predicted targets for both accessions (maximum expectation rating < 2) are highly expressed in embryos, pollen, and seeds (Fig. 3F, Datasets S1–S4, and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B) (38, 40). This is consistent with the spatiotemporal expression of DAB siRNAs and suggests that DAB regulates the expression of a variety of genes early in embryo and seedling development.

DAB Is Involved in Both Vegetative and Pollen Development.

To identify the function of DAB, we cloned the intergenic region between REM4.1 and AGK2 from the Col-0 and Co-1 accessions and transformed these sequences into both genetic backgrounds (Fig. 4A). Co-1 is an accession that has delayed abaxial trichome production and elevated miR156 levels, but no change in heteroblastic leaf development—measured by leaf four angle—compared to Col-0 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–E). Transformation of the Col-0 version of this intergenic region (DABCol-0) into either Col-0 or Co-1 had no effect on the timing of abaxial trichome production (Fig. 4 B and C). However, transformation of the Co-1 version (DABCo-1) into both Col-0 and Co-1 caused a slight but significant delay in abaxial trichome production under both 8-h SD (Fig. 4 B and C) and longer 10-h SD conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). Addition of DABCo-1 to Col-0 also produced a small, but significant increase in the level of miR156 (Fig. 4D). Neither transgenic line mediated a change in leaf four angle in the Col-0 background (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H), which is consistent with the phenotypic difference between Co-1 and Col-0 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A-E). Given that there are no protein-coding genes in this intergenic sequence, these results support the conclusion that DAB controls the timing of abaxial trichome production and the level of miR156 expression in trans via siRNAs.

To determine if the 24-nt siRNAs that map to DAB are responsible for the phenotype of this intergenic sequence, we generated deletions of this sequence using CRISPR/Cas9-directed mutagenesis (Fig. 4E). 1,093 bp deletions of the region that produces siRNAs were identified in both the Col-0 (dab-1Col-0) and Co-1 (dab-1Col-0) accessions. Surprisingly, we were unable to isolate plants homozygous for these deletions in the self-progeny of dab-1Col-0/+ or dab-1Co-1/+ plants. PCR analysis of 44 self-progeny of dab-1/+ plants revealed that ~50% of these progeny were heterozygous for dab-1, while none were homozygous for this mutation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). The siliques of dab-1/+ plants did not show decreased fertility or dead embryos, and seeds produced by these plants germinated normally (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), suggesting the dab-1 deletion is lethal to either microspores or megaspores following meiosis.

We examined the pollen of Col-0, Co-1, and plants heterozygous for the dab-1 deletion and found that roughly 50% of the pollen in undehisced anthers of the heterozygous plants was defective (Fig. 4F). Reciprocal crosses between Col-0 and dab-1Col-0/+ plants revealed that dab-1Col-0 was present the progeny of this cross when dab-1Col-0/+ was the female parent, but not when dab-1Col-0/+ was the pollen donor (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). These results demonstrate DAB is required postmeiotically for pollen development but is not essential for embryo sac development.

In addition to dab-1Col-0, we isolated a second, smaller deletion of DAB (dab-2Col-0) in Col-0. dab-2Col-0 has the same right breakpoint as dab-1Col-0, but is 329 bp shorter than dab-1Col-0. dab-2Col-0 removes the region of DAB that produces peak 2 siRNAs but does not affect the region that produces peak 1 siRNAs (Fig. 4E). Unlike dab-1Col-0, dab-2Col-0 can be made homozygous. dab-2Col-0/dab-2Col-0 plants are completely fertile, have the same number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes as Col-0, and appear identical to Col-0 (Fig. 4 G and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). Thus, the locus responsible for the effect of DAB on abaxial trichome production and pollen development is within the 329 bp region that generates peak 1 siRNAs.

DABCo-1 Mediates Transcriptional Reprogramming In Trans.

To better understand the function of DAB, we sequenced mRNAs in 5-d-old seedlings of Col-0, Co-1, and Col-0 DABCo-1 As expected, the overall transcript abundance of most genes was similar in Col-0 and Col-0 DABCo-1, with Co-1 being most different from the other two libraries (Fig. 5 A–C and Datasets S5–S7). However, there were 1,618 genes that were differentially expressed from Col-0 in both Co-1 and Col-0 DABCo-1. When isolating just these genes, their expression pattern was more similar between Col-0 DABCo-1 and Co-1 than to Col-0 (i.e., either up or down in both genotypes compared to Col-0) (adjusted P-value < 0.05) (Fig. 5 B and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–H). This finding suggests that the Co-1 allele of DAB has a reproducible impact on gene expression.

GO analysis of these differentially expressed genes revealed a significant enrichment for genes involved in ribosome biogenesis, and polysaccharide and glucan metabolism (Fig. 5E). All of these processes are directly involved in reproductive development and embryogenesis (41). For example, null mutations in the ribosomal components that are differentially expressed Co-1 and Col-0 DABCo-1 seedlings relative to Col-0 (such as EMB2719, EMB3144, EMB2788, and numerous others), cause defects in gametogenesis and embryo lethality (Fig. 5F) (42). Deficiencies in the glucan and polysaccharide synthesis pathways (e.g., resulting from null mutations in GLUCAN SYNTHASE-LIKE 1, (GSL1) and GLUCAN SYNTHASE-LIKE 8 (GSL8)), impact male gametophyte development (Fig. 5F) (41).

Our GO results are also consistent with a role for DAB in vegetative phase change. Genes involved in sugar metabolism, such as HEXOKINASE1 (HXK1) and TREHALOSE-6-PHOSPHASE SYNTHASE 10 (TPS10), and genes that regulate light responses, such as PHYTOCHROME A (PHYA), CRYPTOCHROME 1 (CRY1), and PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTOR 1 (PIF1), are differentially expressed in both Co-1 and DABCo-1 seedlings relative to Col-0 (Fig. 5F). These genes are known to directly impact the abundance miR156 and timing of vegetative phase change (43–45). Several SPL genes important for vegetative phase change (SPL9, SPL11, SPL13) are down-regulated in Co-1 and in Col-0 DABCo-1, which is in agreement with the elevated level of miR156 and delayed vegetative phase change phenotype of these genotypes (Figs. 4D and 5F). Consistent with the abaxial trichome phenotype of these genotypes, GLABRA 1 (GL1)—a gene required for abaxial trichome production (46) —is downregulated in Co-1 and Col-0 DABCo-1 seedlings compared to Col-0 (Fig. 5F).

A Role for DAB in Epigenetic Regulation of Gene Expression.

GO analysis revealed that DAB also regulates genes involved in noncoding RNA processing (Fig. 5E), including several members of the Argonaute family (AGO1, AGO3, AGO7, AGO10) as well as RNA-DIRECTED DNA METHYLATION 1 and 4 (RDM1, RDM4) (Fig. 5F). These genes are essential for the processing and functioning of miRNAs and siRNAs involved posttranscriptional gene silencing (AGO1, AGO7, AGO10) and RNA-dependent DNA methylation (AGO3, RDM1, RDM4). These processes are required for almost all aspects of plant development, including gametogenesis, embryogenesis, seed development, and vegetative and reproductive phase changes, making it difficult to determine exactly how DAB impacts the expression of these genes either locally or globally (47–52, 53).

AGO3 and AGO7/ZIPPY share sequence complementarity to the 24-nt siRNAs produced by DAB and, consistent with this observation, both mRNAs were down-regulated in Col-0 DABCo-1 plants (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A–C). In contrast, AGO1 and AGO10 are both elevated in Col-0 DABCo-1 compared to Col-0, suggesting that they are indirectly impacted by DABCo-1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 D–G). Neither RDM1 nor RDM4 have sequence complementarity to DAB siRNAs, but both genes are expressed at a significantly lower level in Co-1 and Col-0 DABCo-1 than in Col-0 (Fig. 6 B and C). AGO7 and AGO1 have vegetative phase change phenotypes (47, 54), and AGO1 and AGO10 both have a role in embryogenesis and gametogenesis (55, 56); however, more work is needed to determine if these changes in gene expression impact the epigenomic landscape.

To further explore the role of DAB on the RdDM pathway, we examined the vegetative phenotypes of two mutant lines that significantly reduce DNA methylation: the domains rearranged methyltransferase1, domains rearranged methyltransferase2, chromomethylase3 triple mutant (ddc), and methyltransferase1-3 (met1-3) (59–61). Both of these mutants have well characterized defects in fertility: ddc mutants are partially sterile (producing shortened siliques and fewer seeds) and met1-3 has defects in embryogenesis and a reduction in pollen viability (62, 63). We found that both mutants also have delayed abaxial production compared to Col-0 (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 H and I), suggesting RdDM promotes abaxial trichome production, presumably by affecting the transcription of one or more genes involved in this process. These observations, and the effect of DABCo-1 on the expression of RDM1, RDM4, and AGO3 support the conclusion that DABCo-1 delays abaxial trichome production by interfering with RdDM.

The Basis for Differences in the Activity of DABCo-0 and DABCo-1.

The difference in the activity of DABCo-1 and DABCo-1 could be due to differences in the types of siRNAs produced by these loci, the expression level of these siRNAs, or both. We sequenced sRNAs from young seedlings of Col-0, Co-1, and DAB transgenics, but transcripts from DAB were so rare that we were unable to determine conclusively if DABCol-0 and DABCo-1 make qualitatively different sRNAs (SI Appendix, Fig. S7J). We then considered the possibility that these loci might be differentially expressed, perhaps because of differential levels of DNA methylation. As noted above, RDM1 and RDM4 are expressed at a lower level in Co-1 than in Col-0. RDM1 and RDM4 recruit RNA polymerase V and other proteins to deposit and maintain DNA methylation marks during gametogenesis and other stages of development (49, 64).

To investigate if this might explain the difference in the activity of DABCol-0, and DABCo-1 we examined DNA methylation data from the 1001 epigenomes project (57). We found that the DAB locus is methylated in adult leaf tissue of Col-0 but is unmethylated Co-1 (Fig. 6E). Furthermore, accessions with the Col-0-like haplotype have Col-0-like DNA-methylation at the DAB locus, but accessions with the Co-1-like haplotype have reduced DNA-methylation, show no methylation marks at the “peak one” siRNA producing locus (Fig. 6E). Consistent with this observation, sRNA-seq from 5-d-old seedlings indicate DAB is present at slightly higher—albeit statistically insignificant—levels in Co-1 than in Col-0 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7J).

To further explore the effect of DAB on gene expression in trans, we measured the levels of FATTY ACID REDUCTASE 7 (FAR7) in bud tissue of Col-0, Co-1, Col-0 DABCol-0, and Col-0 DABCo-1. FAR7 is a homologue of MALE STERILITY 2 (MS2, FAR2), a gene required for the proper production of pollen cell walls (65). FAR7 shares sequence complementarity to peak 1 of both Col-0 and Co-1 alleles of DAB over a 150 bp region in two locations, with higher sequence similarity to Co-1 (p-distance 0.4125 compared to Col-0 vs 0.3438 compared to Co-1), suggesting it may be a target of DAB (Fig. 6F). We found that FAR7 is more abundant in flower buds of Col-0 DABCo-1 and Co-1 compared to Col-0 DABCol-0 and Col-0, further suggesting these two alleles of DAB have different impacts on their targets (Fig. 6G). Consistent with this result, the 150 bp region of complementarity in FAR7 is less methylated in Co-1 compared to Col-0 (57) (Fig. 6H). Mutations that interfere with RdDM—specifically the quintuple mddcc mutant—have significantly reduced levels of methylation in this region of FAR7, suggesting that this methylation is mediated via the RdDM pathway (Fig. 6I) (58). However, ddc had no effect on the abundance of FAR7 transcripts in unopened flower buds (SI Appendix, Fig. S7K). Although it seems likely that DAB promotes DNA methylation of FAR7, this does not explain the effect of DABCo-1 on the expression of this gene, which is not uncommon for gene-body methylation (66, 67).

Discussion

TEs regulate the expression of other sequence-similar TEs by producing siRNAs that mediate RNA-dependent DNA methylation (RdDM) or RNA interference (RNAi) (64, 68). When TEs are located in the promoter of a protein-coding gene, RdDM of the TE can lead to changes in chromatin structure that affect the expression of the adjacent gene (69–71). Whether TEs can directly regulate the expression of genes through homology-dependent mechanisms is less clear (72). This question is of considerable interest because TEs that have captured fragments of genes make up a significant portion of plant and animal genomes (2).

In a GWAS designed to identify genes involved in vegetative phase change in A. thaliana, we identified DAB, a Helitron/RC transposable element that regulates the expression of numerous genes in trans and is essential for pollen development. The causal locus underpinning these functions maps to a 329-bp region within DAB that generates 24-nt siRNAs, some of which are similar to protein-coding genes, but not other Helitrons in the genome. Previous studies have suggested that Helitrons are capable of targeting protein-coding genes, although there was no evidence that this is functionally significant (12, 13). Together, this study provides evidence that Helitrons can acquire an essential function in plants.

Because DAB is expressed at very low levels, we were unable to determine the precise mechanism by which DAB impacts gene expression, abaxial trichome production, and postmeiotic pollen development. In both young seedlings and flower buds, next generation sequencing only identified a few siRNAs that map exclusively to this locus. This makes it difficult to compare the types of siRNAs produced by different DAB haplotypes, which is essential to determining how these haplotypes regulate vegetative phase change. It may be that DAB is only highly expressed in a few cells, or at a very specific time in development. Future research should focus on DAB expression in microspores immediately following meiosis, since DAB is essential to their development.

However, sRNA-sequencing performed by us and others indicates that DAB primarily produces 24-nt siRNAs. This class of siRNAs typically promotes RdDM, and it is reasonable to assume that this is how DAB functions. The best evidence for this is provided by FAR7. DAB has extended sequence similarity to FAR7, suggesting that this region of FAR7 was captured by DAB during its transposition. DAB produces 24-nt siRNAs from this region, so it is significant that the corresponding region of FAR7 is methylated in the Col-0 ecotype. This region of FAR7 is less methylated in Co-1, perhaps because of polymorphisms that reduce the similarity of Co-1 siRNAs to FAR7, or because of the effect DAB has on the expression of genes involved in RdDM. The second scenario seems more likely because FAR7 expression is elevated in Col-0 DABCo-1, and it is difficult to imagine how DABCo-1 could directly elevate FAR7 expression if the siRNAs produced by DAB promote RdDM at this locus. It should be noted that DNA methylation within genes—as observed for FAR7—is not necessarily associated with a decrease in transcription. In fact, the functional significance of this modification is still a mystery (66, 67, 73). Thus, even if DABCo-1 promotes DNA methylation of FAR7, the effect of DAB on FAR7 expression is almost certainly an indirect result of its effect on the expression of a transcriptional regulator of FAR7.

Despite its very low expression level, mRNA-seq in seedlings revealed that the DAB haplotype associated with late abaxial trichomes (i.e., DABCo-1) has a major effect on gene expression in young seedlings and leaf primordia in a Col-0 background. Most of these differentially expressed genes are indirectly regulated by DABCo-1 because they lack sequence similarity to the siRNAs produced by DAB. This suggests the overall effect of DABCo-1 on gene expression is likely mediated by its effect on the expression of genes involved in RdDM or RNAi, because several genes involved in these processes are down-regulated in Col-0 DABCo-1. Although RdDM is generally thought to function via AGO4/6/9-associated 24-nt siRNAs, recent studies have found that other AGO proteins can also associate with 24-nt siRNAs (64, 73, 74). For example AGO1 and AGO3, two of the predicted targets of DAB, are more often associated with 24-nt siRNAs than 21-nt siRNAs in a variety of tissues (53, 75). Thus, an alternative possibility is that DAB functions either by regulating the transcription of AGO genes, or by producing siRNAs that compete with the normal substrates of these proteins. Variation in AGO gene expression could explain, at least in part, both the vegetative and male-sterile phenotypes of DAB. Null alleles of ago7 have an obvious vegetative phenotype (76) that is not shared by Col-0 DABCo-1, although this could be because the expression of AGO7 is only slightly reduced in Col-0 DABCo-1. Severe ago1 mutants—which is a predicted target of DAB but is slightly upregulated in Col-0 DABCo-1 seedlings—are male-sterile in A. thaliana (55). For these reasons, it is difficult to know whether the effect of DABCo-1 on these genes is functionally significant.

Another possibility is that the effect of DABCo-1 on global gene expression is mediated by its effect on RDM1 and/or RDM4, both of which are required for RdDM and are down-regulated in Col-0 DABCo-1. However, because DAB lacks sequence similarity to both of these genes, it is unclear why their expression is decreased in Col-0 DABCo-1. Deletion of a 329-bp region within DAB leads to postmeiotic pollen abortion in both Co-1 and Col-0. While this result indicates that DAB has a vital role during pollen development, it precludes determining the processes affected by DAB because this deletion cannot be made homozygous. However, it is reasonable to predict that the effect of DAB on pollen development is mediated by its effect on RdDM or RNAi, as RdDM plays an important role in both male and female gametogenesis; indeed, rdm1 mutants in maize are male-sterile (77, 78).

Although further work will be necessary to determine the molecular function of DAB, this study reveals an essential role for Helitrons in plant development, and further demonstrates that this class of transposons can have a global effect on gene expression. It also highlights the value of using natural variation to identify new, functionally important loci, as it is unlikely that DAB would have been identified by conventional forward or reverse genetic screens.

Materials and Methods

Genome-Wide Association Study.

77 natural accessions (n > 6 plants for each genotype) were vernalized for 4 wk and grown in SD conditions. The average number of leaves lacking abaxial trichomes within each genotype was converted into PLINK format (79), using the 1001 Genomes accession ID numbers (https://1001genomes.org) (SI Appendix, Table S1). Data were uploaded to the easyGWAS server (26) and analyzed using 1001 Genomes SNP database, TAIR10 reference genome, and an accelerated mixed model method (AMM) (80).

Using Traffic Lines to Confirm the GWAS Locus.

Crosses to traffic lines are described in ref. 32 and in SI Appendix.

Preparation of Genetic Constructs for CRISPR-Cas9 Mutagenesis.

sgRNA assembly into the Cas9 vector is described in ref. 81 and in SI Appendix.

All other materials and methods and described in detail in SI Appendix.
