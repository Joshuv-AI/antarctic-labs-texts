Biosynthesis of zinc oxide and silver/zinc oxide nanocompositesusing Kalanchoe pinnata leaf extracts for antibacterial and anticancer applications
Authors: Krishnan Shreema, Shanmugam Mathammal, Gopi Somasundaram, Viswanathan Kalaiselvi, Nadir Ayrilmis, B. Blessymol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Green synthesis of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles has attracted considerable attention due to its eco-friendly nature and enhanced functional performance. Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles have been extensively explored; however, their efficiency is often limited by rapid charge recombination and moderate biological activity. The incorporation of silver (Ag) and the use of plant-mediated synthesis offer a promising strategy to overcome these limitations.In the present study, Ag–ZnO nanoparticles were synthesized via a green route using Kalanchoe pinnata leaf extract as a natural reducing and stabilizing agent. The synthesized nanoparticles were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), UV–Visible spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDAX). The biological activities were evaluated using standard antioxidant and antibacterial assays.XRD analysis confirmed the crystalline nature of ZnO with the successful incorporation of Ag, without the formation of secondary impurity phases. The FTIR spectra revealed the presence of phytochemical functional groups responsible for nanoparticle stabilization. SEM images demonstrated nearly spherical and well-dispersed nanoparticles, while EDAX analysis confirmed the elemental composition of Ag and Zn. UV–Visible spectroscopy indicated enhanced optical absorption due to Ag incorporation.The Ag–ZnO nanoparticles exhibited significantly improved antioxidant and antibacterial activities compared to pure ZnO, which can be attributed to the synergistic effect of Ag and bioactive phytochemical capping. This study demonstrates that Kalanchoe pinnata-mediated green synthesis effectively enhances the structural, optical, and biological performance of Ag–ZnO nanoparticles, highlighting their potential for eco-friendly biomedical applications.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Nanoparticles have attracted significant research interest owing to their unique physical and chemical properties, which differ markedly from those of bulk materials and individual atoms [1]. Nanotechnology is one of the most rapidly advancing interdisciplinary fields, with substantial contributions to biological and biomedical sciences [2]. It focuses on engineering nanoparticles with well-defined dimensions and structures to enhance their applicability in improving human welfare. Conventionally, nanoparticle synthesis involves physical and chemical methods; however, these approaches often suffer from drawbacks such as high cost, energy consumption, and adverse environmental impact [3]. Comparative studies between chemical, physical, and biological methods have identified green synthesis as a preferred alternative due to its cost-effectiveness, eco-friendly nature, and elimination of toxic surfactants [4]. Nanotechnology is one of the most rapidly advancing fields in biological and biomedical sciences, aiming to engineer nanoparticles with precise dimensions and structures to enhance their utility for improving human welfare [5].

In green synthesis, plant extracts play a crucial role as both reducing and capping agents. Plant-derived nanoparticles are generally more uniform, stable, and diverse in size and morphology compared to those synthesized using microorganisms or chemical routes [6]. In recent years, metal and metal oxide nanoparticles have gained considerable attention because of their remarkable optical, chemical, electrical, and magnetic properties [7].

Among these, zinc oxide (ZnO) is regarded as an important industrial material due to its inherent physicochemical properties. Parameters such as crystallite size, surface area, crystallinity, and band gap of ZnO nanoparticles are closely associated with their functional performance, including high electrical conductivity [8], excellent thermal stability, strong antimicrobial activity, and enhanced photocatalytic efficiency [9]. ZnO nanoparticles find extensive applications in medicine, electronics, cosmetics, paints, and coatings. Inorganic metal oxides are particularly advantageous as antibacterial agents because they contain mineral components essential to human health and exhibit strong antimicrobial activity even at low concentrations [10]. Silver was incorporated with ZnO to form a metal–semiconductor heterostructure that enhances charge carrier separation at the Ag–ZnO interface. Metallic Ag acts as an electron sink, suppressing electron–hole recombination and promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which significantly improves antibacterial and cytotoxic performance compared to pristine ZnO [11–13].

The incorporation of silver into ZnO nanoparticles significantly influences crystallite size, morphology, and electronic properties, particularly by reducing electron–hole recombination rates. This reduction promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals, which play a vital role in disrupting microbial cell membranes, thereby enhancing antimicrobial efficacy [14]. Silver (Ag) nanoparticles are well known for their broad-spectrum biological activity and are widely used in medicine, food packaging, optics, electronics, and biomedical applications [15]. The synergistic combination of Ag with ZnO further amplifies the bioactivity of the resulting nanocomposites.

Kalanchoe pinnata, commonly known as the Cathedral bells plant, has a long history of use in Ayurvedic medicine for its astringent, analgesic, and carminative properties, and for the treatment of nausea and vomiting [16]. Pharmacological investigations have demonstrated that K. pinnata possesses diverse biological activities, including anthelmintic, immunosuppressive, wound-healing, hepatoprotective, antinociceptive, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, nephroprotective, antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticonvulsant, and neuropharmacological effects. Subrata Kumar Biswas et al. [17] reported the presence of novel flavonoids, such as 5′-methyl-4′,5,7-trihydroxyflavone and 4′,3,5,7-tetrahydroxy-5-methyl-5′-propenamine anthocyanidines, which are believed to contribute significantly to the potent antimicrobial activity of K. pinnata. Additionally, bufadienolides isolated from its leaves have shown promising potential in cancer chemoprevention [18]. Kalanchoe pinnata was selected due to its rich phytochemical profile, including flavonoids, phenolics, and bufadienolides, which function as efficient reducing and capping agents. These biomolecules facilitate controlled nucleation, surface stabilization, and biocompatibility of the synthesized nanostructures while imparting additional bioactivity.

The primary aim of this study is to develop a sustainable, plant-mediated synthesis route for Ag–ZnO metal–semiconductor heterostructures and to systematically investigate the influence of silver concentration on their structural, optical, and biological properties, with particular emphasis on antibacterial activity and preliminary anticancer performance.

Materials and methods

Kalanchoe pinnata plant species was taxonomically identified and authenticated by a qualified botanist at the Department of Botany, Tamil Nadu Agricultural University, Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu, India. Fresh Kalanchoe pinnata leaves were thoroughly washed with running tap water followed by double-distilled water, shade-dried for 7 days, and finely chopped. 10 g of dried leaf material was extracted in 100 mL of double-distilled water at 60–70 °C for 30 min under continuous stirring. The extract was cooled, filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use. Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn (CH3COO)2.2H2O) and silver nitrate (AgNO3) were used to synthesize nanoparticles. The sodium hydroxide pellets were used to maintain the pH level of the solution. The aqueous solutions were prepared using double distilled water. All glassware was cleaned and washed with distilled water and dried before use. The reaction mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min to collect the precipitate, which was washed three times with double-distilled water and ethanol to remove unreacted phytochemicals and residual ions. The co-precipitation method was used to synthesize silver/zinc NCs. This method represents a simple, low cost and very easy-to synthesize-nanoparticles.

Synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles

The synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles was initiated with the amalgamation of 0.35 M zinc acetate dihydrate and 10 ml of the extract of the leaves, which was followed by the incorporation of 50 ml of double-distilled water. The mixture was stirred for 30 min using a magnetic stirrer. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 13 by adding sodium hydroxide pellets. After stirring for 3 h, a pale white precipitate forms. In order to remove impurities, the precipitate was washed three times with distilled water. The specimen was then dried in a hot air oven at 80 °C for 3 h and then calcined in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 4 h. Finally, the dried product was ground by a mortar and pestle to obtain fine nanoparticles.

Preparation of biosynthesized silver/zinc oxide NCs

Silver nitrate at varying concentrations (0.05 M, 0.1 M, 0.15 M, 0.2 M, and 0.25 M) is mixedwith 0.35 M zinc acetate dihydrate. For each concentration, 10 ml of leaf extract was added to 50 ml of distilled water, followed by stirring with a magnetic stirrerfor 30 min. Sodium hydroxide pellets were added to adjust the pH of the solution to 13. After complete stirring, a dark brown precipitate formed. Calcination at 400 °C for 4 h was selected to ensure complete decomposition of residual organic phytochemicals, promote crystallization of the ZnO wurtzite phase, and enhance structural stability of the nanocomposites, as reported in similar green synthesis studies [19, 20] (Fig. 1).

Characterization techniques

The pure zinc oxide and green synthesized silver/zinc NCs were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectral analysis (FTIR), energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX), X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD), UV-visible spectroscopy, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (XRD) was determined by Shimadzu XRD 6000. The optical analysis was carried out using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 35) and FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer spectrum two). The surface morphology and composition analysis are performed by EV018, Carl Zeiss instrument.

Antibacterial activity

The standardized inoculum was inoculated onto plates that have been prepared aseptically. A sterile swab was dipped into the inoculum. Excess inoculum was removed by pressing the swab firmly against the edge of the culture tube above the liquid level and rotating the swab. The swab was then streaked across the surface of the medium by rotating the plate through an angle of 60º three times after each application. Additionally, the swab was run around the edge of the agar surface. The plates were then covered and allowed to dry at room temperature. The specimens, such as pure zinc oxide and green synthesized silver/zinc nanocomposites (NCs) at a concentration of 100 µg, were prepared. Using sterile forceps, these specimens, soaked overnight in the specimen solution, were placed on the inoculated plates together with a standard ciprofloxacin disc (10 µg). The Petri dishes were then refrigerated at 4 °C for one hour to allow diffusion. After the diffusion period, the plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the zones of inhibition formed by the different specimens were examined. The diameter of each zone of the inhibition was measured twice, and the average diameter was calculated using a scale [21].

Anticancer activity

The pure zinc oxide and silver/zinc nanocomposites (NCs) green synthesized were evaluated HT-29 cells via the MTT assay for in vitro cytotoxicity. HT-29 cells were harvested bytrypsinization and collected in a 15 mL centrifuge tube. The cells were then seeded in a 96-well tissue culture plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells/mL, with 200 µL per well, in the DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antibiotic solution. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h. After incubation, wells were washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and treated with different nanocomposite concentrations in serum-free DMEM. Each specimen was repeated three times and cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. MTT (20µL at 5 mg/ml) was then added to each well and cells were allowed to incubate for a further 2–4 h until purple precipitation was visible under the inverted microscope. Finally, the medium together with MTT (220 µL) was aspirated from the wells and rinsed with 1X PBS (200 µL), DMSO (100 µL) was added to dissolve formazan crystals and the plate was agitated for 5 minutes. The absorbance for each well was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Structural analysis

The crystallinity and purity of the synthesized zinc oxide and silver/zinc nanocomposites using Kalanchoe pinnata leaf extract were investigated by X-ray diffraction analysis. The XRD patterns of the zinc oxide and silver/zinc NCs synthesized by Kalanchoe pinnata leaf extract are shown in Fig. 2. They had a hexagonal wurtzite structure. The broad and sharp diffraction peaks appeared at 31.59˚, 34.38˚, 36.24˚, 47.64˚, 56.65˚and 62.96˚ corresponding to (100), (002), (101), (102), (110) and (103) phases of zinc oxide nanoparticles respectively. These 2θ values were in good agreement with JCPDS card no. 36-1451. The diffraction peaks (111) and (200) planes that appeared at 2θ were 38.40˚and 44.35˚, respectively showed the presence of the silver in the zinc oxide lattice with the JCPDS card no.04-0783. The XRD peaks for the silver/zinc indicate the formation of well-defined crystalline phases for both zinc oxide and silver. The distinct phases observed for the zinc oxide and silver in the silver/zinc specimen confirm the successful synthesis of a crystalline nanocomposite. The sharp and well-defined peaks further suggest the high purity of the resulting nanocomposite. The stiff and narrow diffraction peaks indicated that the nanoparticles were crystalline in nature [22].

Fourier transform infra-red spectral analysis (FTIR)

The FTIR spectrum of the K. pinnata leaf extract exhibited a broad absorption band around 3431 cm⁻¹ corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl and phenolic groups [30]. The peak observed at 1595 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C = O stretching vibrations of carboxylic acids and amide groups, while bands at 1390 cm⁻¹ and 875 cm⁻¹ correspond to C–O stretching and C–H bending vibrations, respectively. These functional groups originate from phytochemicals such as flavonoids, phenols, proteins, and organic acids present in the plant extract [8] .In the FTIR spectra of pure ZnO and Ag/ZnO nanocomposites, noticeable shifts and reductions in intensity of the above-mentioned bands were observed when compared with the extract spectrum, indicating the involvement of these phytochemical groups in the bioreduction and capping of the nanoparticles. The reduced intensity of absorption peaks in Ag/ZnO nanocomposites suggests lower infrared absorption due to silver incorporation. A sharp absorption peak observed at 509 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the characteristic stretching vibration of the Zn–O bond, confirming the formation of ZnO-based nanostructures [31].The comparative FTIR analysis of the plant extract and the synthesized nanoparticles confirms that functional groups such as amines, alcohols, ketones, and carboxylic acids play a crucial role in the reduction, stabilization, and surface modification of the green-synthesized Ag/ZnO nanocomposites [31]. Antibacterial activity

The present investigation aimed to evaluate the antibacterial performance of zinc oxide and biosynthesized silver/zinc nanocomposites (NCs) against four bacterial strain, representing both gram-positive and gram-negative types (Table 3). The agar well diffusion method was used to measure the extent of inhibition zones formed around the wells containing the NCs against the respective bacteria. Ciprofloxacin was used as the standard antibacterial agent for comparison across all bacterial strains. Table 3 shows the inhibition zone diameters for zinc oxide and silver/zinc NCs synthesized using fresh leaf extract of Kalanchoe pinnata. The results show substantial antibacterial activity of both zinc oxide and biosynthesized silver/zinc NCs against all bacterial strains tested. The inhibition zones are quantified in millimetres using a vernier caliper. A concentration-dependent increase in the inhibition zone was observed for both gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria with increasing silver concentration. Comparing two gram-positive bacteria, Staphylococcus aureus (27 mm) shows a larger inhibition zone for biosynthesized silver/zinc nanocomposites than Bacillus subtilis (19 mm). For gram-negative bacteria, Escherichia coli (28 mm) has the highest zone of inhibition for biosynthesized Silver/zinc nanocomposites compared to Pseudomonas aeruginosa (26 mm). The zone of inhibition varies according to different types of microorganisms and concentrations used [5, 33–35].

The capacity of microorganisms to demonstrate resistance to the antibacterial properties of biosynthesized silver/zinc nanocomposites is contingent upon their membrane structure or cell wall composition. These nanocomposites have the capacity to engage directly with bacterial membranes through a number of mechanisms: (i) the release of silver and zinc ions upon surface oxidation, (ii) intricate porosity, or (iii) electrostatic attraction between the released ions and the negatively charged cell wall [14]. The deposition of silver nanoparticles on zinc oxide nanoparticles facilitates electron transfer induced by the photocatalytic reactions of the zinc oxide nanoparticles. Additionally, the synergy between the semiconductor zinc oxide and the metallic silver results in cell membrane rupture, thereby enhancing antibacterial activity.A substantial body of research suggests that silver/zinc nanocomposites demonstrate superior antibacterial potency in comparison to zinc oxide nanoparticles alone. It has been demonstrated that the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by silver/zinc nanocomposites, including superoxide and hydroxyl, can result in the disruption of bacterial cell membranes, leading to leakage of the cytoplasm and eventual cell death, due to the alteration of membrane protein or enzyme activity induced by ROS radicals. At the molecular level, silver/zinc nanocomposites are known to inhibit DNA replication, thereby reducing the expression of essential proteins and enzymes vital to bacterial viability. This effect is purportedly achieved through the direct action of silver and zinc ions, or the generation of ROS. In addition, silver ions have been observed to interact with ribosomal protein S30, thereby inhibiting the synthesis of vital bacterial proteins. At lower Ag concentrations, ZnO exhibited comparable or higher inhibition zones than Ag–ZnO nanocomposites, suggesting that a threshold Ag content is required to establish effective Ag–ZnO interfacial synergy. Below this threshold, the antibacterial response is dominated by ZnO-induced ROS generation and surface charge effects rather than silver ion release [36–38].

Anticancer activity

The synthesized zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles and biosynthesized silver/zinc nanocomposites (Ag/Zn NCs) were evaluated for their anticancer activity against human colon cancer (HT-29) cells. To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports are available on the anticancer evaluation of ZnO and Ag/Zn nanocomposites synthesized using Kalanchoe pinnata leaf extracts, highlighting the novelty of the present study.Cell viability was assessed using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) assay, which is based on the ability of mitochondrial dehydrogenase enzymes in viable cells to reduce the pale-yellow MTT into insoluble dark blue formazan crystals. These crystals accumulate in metabolically active cells and serve as an indicator of cell viability, providing reliable information on cellular survival and cytotoxicity [39]. The cytotoxic effects of ZnO and Ag/Zn NCs synthesized using K. pinnata leaf extract were evaluated against HT-29 cells at various concentrations. As shown in Tables 4 and 5, cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner for both samples. Microscopic images of HT-29 cells treated with ZnO nanoparticles and Ag/Zn NCs at different concentrations are presented in Figs. 8 and 9, respectively. Compared to the control group, both ZnO and Ag/Zn NCs significantly inhibited HT-29 cell viability with increasing nanoparticle concentration.

The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values for ZnO nanoparticles and Ag/Zn NCs were found to be 54% and 27%, respectively, indicating that Ag/Zn NCs exhibit stronger anticancer activity than pure ZnO nanoparticles. The lower IC₅₀ value of Ag/Zn NCs suggests enhanced cytotoxicity, which can be attributed to the synergistic effect of silver incorporation and phytochemical surface functionalization [40, 41]. These results demonstrate that biosynthesized Ag/Zn NCs effectively sensitize cancer cells and exhibit promising anticancer potential. The lack of cytotoxicity testing on non-cancerous cells limits conclusions regarding therapeutic selectivity and biosafety. Future studies will include comparative cytotoxic evaluation using normal cell lines such as fibroblasts or epithelial cells. The anticancer activity was evaluated only against the HT-29 cell line without comparison to normal cell lines or a standard chemotherapeutic agent, and therefore the results should be interpreted as preliminary. Where available, we have now recalculated IC₅₀ values in terms of concentration (µg/mL) and included them alongside percentage viability values. The language has been revised to describe the results as “preliminary anticancer potential” rather than definitive therapeutic efficacy. Ciprofloxacin (10 µg) was used as the positive control, while sterile distilled water-soaked blank discs served as the negative control to validate the antibacterial activity of the synthesized samples [11–13, 42].

While the present study primarily focused on evaluating anticancer activity against HT-29 colon cancer cells, cytotoxicity assessment on non-cancerous normal cell lines is recognized as an important parameter for determining biocompatibility and selective toxicity. Such studies will be undertaken in future work to further validate the safety and therapeutic applicability of the synthesized nanomaterials.

Conclusion

Pure ZnO nanoparticles and Ag/ZnO heterostructured nanocomposites were successfully synthesized using Kalanchoe pinnata leaf extract via a green co-precipitation method at varying silver concentrations. X-ray diffraction analysis confirmed the formation of wurtzite ZnO along with distinct metallic Ag phases, indicating the formation of a metal–semiconductor heterostructure rather than lattice-doped ZnO. The average crystallite size of the Ag/ZnO nanocomposites varied from 13.15 to 17.56 nm with increasing silver content, suggesting a silver-dependent influence on particle growth behavior. UV–Visible spectroscopy revealed an apparent reduction in optical band-gap energy with increasing absorption wavelength, which may arise from metal–semiconductor interfacial effects rather than intrinsic modification of the ZnO band structure.FTIR analysis identified phytochemical functional groups associated with the plant extract, indicating their role in nanoparticle reduction and surface stabilization. SEM analysis revealed predominantly spherical nanoparticles with partial agglomeration, while EDX confirmed the elemental presence of Zn, O, and Ag in the synthesized nanocomposites. The antibacterial activity of the Ag/ZnO heterostructures increased with silver concentration, as evidenced by enlarged zones of inhibition against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, highlighting the contribution of metallic Ag to enhanced antibacterial performance.In vitro cytotoxicity studies against HT-29 human colon cancer cells demonstrated concentration-dependent inhibition of cell viability, with Ag/ZnO nanocomposites exhibiting greater cytotoxic effects than pristine ZnO nanoparticles. However, the absence of cytotoxicity evaluation on non-cancerous cell lines limits conclusions regarding anticancer selectivity. Additionally, the lack of a plant-extract-only control restricts differentiation between the intrinsic biological activity of Kalanchoe pinnata and that of the synthesized nanoparticles.Future studies should include appropriate biological controls, cytotoxicity assessment on normal cell lines, and direct mechanistic investigations such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) quantification to establish structure–property–bioactivity relationships more conclusively. Furthermore, advanced structural analyses and in vivo validation are required to substantiate the biomedical applicability of these green-synthesized Ag/ZnO heterostructured nanomaterials.
