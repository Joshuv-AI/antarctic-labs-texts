Porphyromonas gingivalis links Alzheimer’s disease and periodontal disease: Neuroinflammatory mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Siyu Jin, Xing Wu, Yulong Zhang, Fei Xu, Wenyu Zhen, Yuqiang Sun, Yufei Pan, Yutong Lu, Ying Yang, Wansu Sun, Haitao Wang, Hengguo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global increase in the aging population has significantly increased the prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), placing substantial burdens on healthcare systems. Emerging evidence highlights a potential link between AD and periodontal disease (PD), implicating Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) as a key microbial driver. In this review, we explore the bidirectional relationship between AD and PD through epidemiological evidence, Pg dissemination along the oral–brain axis, virulence factor-driven neuroinflammation and blood–brain barrier dysfunction, as well as aging-mediated susceptibility and therapeutic implications. Available data suggest that Pg and its virulence factors reach the central nervous system through hematogenous dissemination and neural pathways. Once in the brain, these agents trigger microglial and astrocytic activation, fostering chronic neuroinflammation and accelerating amyloid-β deposition and tau hyperphosphorylation, two hallmark pathologies of AD. Aging may further amplify host susceptibility and inflammatory responses. Although gingipain inhibitors have demonstrated neuroprotective potential in preclinical models, their clinical applications remain limited. Importantly, most existing evidence is based on preclinical and epidemiological studies and remains to be validated in well-designed human studies. Future studies integrating multi-omics approaches and precision medicine strategies are warranted to identify individuals at high risk and develop targeted interventions aimed at mitigating Pg-associated neurodegenerative processes and reducing the AD burden.Keywords: Porphyromonas gingivalis, Alzheimer’s disease, periodontal disease, neuroinflammation, oral–brain axis

Introduction

The global demographic landscape is undergoing a significant shift toward an aging population, with profound implications for public health and socioeconomic systems. As life expectancy increases, the prevalence of age-related neurodegenerative diseases has increased substantially [1,2]. In 2019, nearly 57.4 million people worldwide suffered from dementia, a figure projected to triple to 152.8 million by 2050 [3]. As the most common form of dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), accounts for 60%–80% of all cases [4] and imposes a significant economic burden [5]. Moreover, according to the World Health Organization, AD and other dementias are currently the seventh leading cause of death globally [6]. Managing AD is a formidable global financial and medical challenge. However, currently, there is no effective solution available, underscoring the urgent need for effective prevention and intervention strategies.

AD is a chronic, progressive brain disorder that is characterized by cognitive decline and memory impairment. Initially, subtle brain changes progress to noticeable symptoms such as memory loss, cognitive dysfunction, and language difficulties. As the disease advances, patients experience impaired communication, disorientation, and behavioral disorders, eventually losing the ability to speak, swallow, or walk [6,7]. The most widely recognized pathogenic mechanisms of AD focus on two key protein abnormalities, amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau [8,9]. Aβ plaques are insoluble aggregates resulting from the abnormal cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) [10]. An imbalance between Aβ production and clearance leads to its accumulation, which subsequently triggers synaptic dysfunction, neuronal damage, and cognitive decline [11]. Tau is a microtubule-associated protein; when abnormally phosphorylated, it disrupts microtubule stability and contributes to neuronal degeneration [12]. In addition to these protein-centric mechanisms, oxidative stress and neuroinflammation have emerged as critical contributors to neurodegeneration in AD [13,14].

What leads to the development of Alzheimer’s disease pathological changes in the brain?

Some findings provide valuable clues. In a large-scale cohort study of 262,349 participants from the Republic of Korea, individuals with periodontal disease (PD) had a 1.06-fold higher risk of developing dementia than controls [15]. A 5-year prospective study further reported that severe PD and a greater periodontal inflamed surface area were independently associated with a higher incidence of mild cognitive impairment [16]. Furthermore, a retrospective analysis involving 27,963 participants found that patients with a ≥10-year history of PD had a markedly elevated AD risk [17]. Another nationwide population-based cohort study also linked periodontitis with an increased risk of subsequent dementia [18]. Importantly, clinical evidence further indicates that oral interventions may confer functional benefits. In a pre–post clinical study involving patients with mild AD, comprehensive dental treatment was associated with significant improvements not only in oral health and orofacial pain but also in cognitive- and function-related measures, as well as quality of life [19]. A 12-year prospective cohort study also showed that receipt of gum treatment was associated with slower cognitive decline [20]. Collectively, these population-based and clinical findings suggest that PD may be associated with accelerated cognitive decline. Mechanistically, Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) and its virulence factors have been directly detected in postmortem brain tissue of AD patients [[21], [22], [23]]. Pg is a pivotal pathogen for PD. Its virulence arsenal includes lipopolysaccharide (LPS), gingipains, fimbriae, and capsule polysaccharides. These components collectively facilitate host tissue invasion and immune evasion, promoting chronic periodontal inflammation and systemic dissemination of inflammatory mediators [24].

Evidence of Pg translocation to the central nervous system (CNS) has been supported by both animal models and human studies [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Oral administration of Pg in mice resulted in its detection in hippocampal and frontotemporal lobe tissues [26,30]. Transmission electron microscopy revealed the intracellular colonization and sustained survival of Pg within neurons differentiated from neural progenitor cells [31]. The gingipains are secreted by Pg, including lysine-gingipain (Kgp) and arginine-gingipains (RgpA/B) [23]. These gingipains, along with Pg itself, have been detected in microglia, astrocytes, and neurons [26,30]. Elevated levels of Kgp and Rgp were detected in AD brains compared with non-AD controls, and these proteases have been reported to hydrolyze tau and induce hyperphosphorylation [23]. Longitudinal serological studies revealed elevated anti-Pg antibody titers in individuals who later developed AD, detectable years prior to clinical cognitive decline [32]. This suggests that PD may be a high-risk factor for AD. Pg-specific genetic markers including the hmuY gene were identified in over 90% of examined AD brains using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and immunohistochemistry. These markers localized predominantly to hippocampal and cortical neurons, with significantly higher loads in AD tissues vs controls [23]. Similarly, rats orally inoculated with Pg identified the presence of RgpA and Kgp gingipain genes via qPCR [28]. The persistence of Pg DNA in neural cells further supports its potential to induce chronic neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. These findings support a significant association between PD and AD.

How does P. gingivalis contribute to central nervous system pathophysiology?

Chronic administration of Pg or LPS in animal models induces neuroinflammation characterized by microgliosis and astrogliosis in the hippocampus and cortex [27,29,[33], [34], [35]]. This is accompanied by elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), in both brain tissues and systemic circulation [[27], [28], [29],[34], [35], [36], [37], [38], [39], [40]]. These inflammatory responses correlate with AD-like molecular alterations such as increased Aβ deposition. Notably, Pg infection upregulates APP expression and enhances Aβ40/Aβ42 accumulation in the hippocampus and cortex across diverse administration routes [23,28,29,37,[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Phosphorylated tau (p-tau), another hallmark of AD, is similarly exacerbated by Pg exposure. Infected animals exhibit elevated p-tau levels at specific epitopes (e.g., Ser396, Ser404) in hippocampal regions, particularly the cornu ammonis 1(CA1) subfield, suggesting a direct link between oral pathogen invasion and tau hyperphosphorylation [28,29,38,43]. Importantly, these molecular changes coincide with functional cognitive deficits. Spatial memory impairment, as assessed by behavioral tests like the Oasis maze, has been consistently observed in Pg-infected rodents [28]. Transgenic AD mice inoculated with Pg demonstrated accelerated cognitive decline. These mice simultaneously developed heightened Aβ plaque loads and neuroinflammation in AD-prone brain areas [39].

Pg may contribute to AD progression through its virulence factors. LPS, a major component of Pg outer membrane, activates Toll-like receptors (TLRs), specifically TLR2 and TLR4, triggering nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)–mediated neuroinflammation characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. Gingipains (Rgp and Kgp) can facilitate bacterial invasion and immune evasion by degrading host defense molecules, indirectly exacerbating neuroinflammatory cascades [44,45]. In addition to LPS and gingipains, Pg-derived bacterial membrane vesicles (BMVs) represent another major virulence determinant. Lipopolysaccharide

Systemic exposure to LPS induces cognitive deficits in both wild-type and AD model mice, as evidenced by impaired spatial learning and memory in tests such as the Morris water maze [28,33,34,36,37,40,49,50]. The LPS binds to TLR4/cluster of differentiation 14 (CD14) receptors on microglia, astrocytes, and neurons, activating the IRAK1/NF-κB signaling cascade. This triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6. These cytokines amplify neuroinflammation [38,49]. Similarly, cathepsin B (CatB)–dependent NF-κB signaling can be activated when LPS binds to TLR2 on microglia. CatB promotes IL-1β maturation and secretion, and sustained NF-κB activation increases TLR2 expression. These events form a pro-inflammatory feedback loop. Additionally, CatB, activated in LPS-stimulated microglia, acts as an alternative β-secretase to cleave APP and generate Aβ [34]. β- and γ-secretase expression can be upregulated, and α-secretase activity can be suppressed by LPS. This shifts APP processing toward the amyloidogenic pathway [41]. Concurrently, LPS upregulates the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) in brain endothelial cells (BECs) via NF-κB, facilitating peripheral Aβ influx into the brain [42]. The RAGE is a key mediator of Aβ from the circulation into the CNS. Increased Aβ may trigger pro-inflammatory cytokine expression and stimulate endothelin-1 production. Peripheral Aβ inflow may play a role in the pathogenesis of AD [56]. Human Aβ can enter the brains of wild-type mice via the circulation, resulting in increased Aβ plaque deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, neurodegenerative changes, and neuroinflammation [57,58]. Moreover, mice subjected to long-term administration of pertussis toxin and Aβ42 exhibit pronounced cognitive impairment [59]. In addition, multiple peripheral delivery routes, such as intraperitoneal and intramuscular administration, have been shown to accelerate cerebral Aβ accumulation in mice displaying cerebral amyloidosis [60]. These findings provide compelling experimental support for the concept that exogenous Aβ can access the brain and drive AD-related pathology and functional deficits. Aβ deposition further activates microglia, amplifying the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. A vicious cycle of “neuroinflammation-Aβ accumulation” can be established [23,48] (Figure 1).

Pathogenic cascade linking Porphyromonas gingivalis invasion to Alzheimer’s disease via blood–brain barrier disruption

The damaged blood–brain barrier (BBB) allows Pg and its BMVs to infiltrate the brain. Once Pg enters the brain, Pg-LPS activates astrocytes and microglia, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, amplifying neuroinflammation. In LPS-stimulated microglia, activated CatB cleaves APP to produce Aβ and drives tau hyperphosphorylation, disrupting microtubule stability and promoting neurofibrillary tangles formation. Gingipains mainly show proteinolytic activity, directly cleaving tau and aberrantly cutting APP, favoring Aβ plaque formation. Meanwhile, exogenous Aβ inflow exacerbates Aβ plaque deposition. These pathological features eventually cause neuronal loss and degeneration. Furthermore, LPS enhances glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) activity via TLR4/TLR2. Activated GSK3β in microglia promotes TNF-α production via NF-κB signaling. TNF-α suppresses neuronal protein kinase B (Akt) signaling, which reduces GSK3β phosphorylation, further enhancing its kinase activity. Activated GSK3β drives tau hyperphosphorylation, leading to microtubule destabilization and promoting neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) formation, ultimately resulting in synaptic loss [50] (Figure 2).

Microglia–neuron interactions in Alzheimer’s disease

In microglia, Pg-LPS binds to TLR2/TLR4, activating the IRAK1/GSK3β pathway and leading to NF-κB activation. This results in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6. Activated microglia also release CatB, which is involved in IL-1β production and TLR2/NF-κB signaling activation, thereby specifically amplifying microglia-mediated neuroinflammation. The effects of Pg-LPS on neurons are microglia-dependent. The released cytokines bind to their respective receptors on neurons, activating NF-κB and GSK3β pathways. IL-1R signaling increases IL-1-/NF-κB-dependent production of CatB and APP. CatB in neurons directly processes APP to contribute to Aβ accumulation. TNF-α released from microglia activates Akt/GSK3β signaling, contributing to p-tau. This activation leads to Aβ production through APP cleavage and GSK3β activation, promoting p-tau and NFT formation. Thus, the interaction between microglia and neurons creates a feedback loop that amplifies neuroinflammation. Gingipains

Gingipains contribute to the pathogenesis of AD through multiple interconnected pathways. Gingipains are cysteine proteases secreted by Pg and exhibit proteolytic activity against host immune defenses and neuronal proteins. They degrade antimicrobial peptides, including complement factors C3 and C4, cathelicidins, and α-defensins. In addition, immune receptors such as CD4, CD8, and CD14 can be suppressed by gingipains. Hence, macrophage-mediated bacterial clearance and adaptive immunity are impaired [61]. This immune evasion mechanism facilitates persistent neuroinflammation, a hallmark of AD. Gingipains (RgpB, Kgp) can directly cleave tau, disrupting neuronal function and promoting tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation, ultimately leading to the formation of NFTs [23]. Additionally, gingipains interact with Aβ peptides, accelerating their aggregation [47]. Indirectly, BBB disruption, through the degradation of tight junction (TJ) proteins (e.g., ZO-1, occludin), facilitates peripheral Aβ and inflammatory factor influx [51,62,63].

Pg and gingipains further contribute to AD pathology by targeting apolipoprotein E (ApoE). Gingipains preferentially cleave ApoE isoforms in the order of ApoE4 > ApoE3 > ApoE2, correlating with the structural vulnerability of ApoE4 to proteolysis [64,65]. Fragmented ApoE, particularly neurotoxic ApoE4 derivatives, disrupts lipid metabolism and neuronal repair, contrasting with the neuroprotective role of full-length ApoE [66]. Concurrently, gingipains activate microglia and inflammasomes in the AD brain, exacerbating neuroinflammation and complement dysregulation [67]. The aberrant activation of the complement cascade, potentially mediated by gingipains, aligns with observed complement system abnormalities in AD progression [67]. More importantly, intracellular gingipains exhibit significant neurotoxicity. In vitro, SH-SY5Y cells exposed to gingipains showed a prominent decrease in length compared with the control group [68].

Another critical link associating gingipains with Aβ plaque formation is dysregulation of APP processing. By disrupting transmembrane APP, which governs synaptic stability and neuronal survival, gingipains promote aberrant cleavage pathways that favor Aβ plaque formation [10] (Figure 1). Aβ plaques, central to AD pathology, drive synaptic loss and neuronal death. Supporting these mechanisms, Dominy et al. detected gingipains in over 90% of postmortem AD brain samples, with a higher prevalence than in controls [23]. The co-localization of Pg virulence factors with AD neuropathological features underscores their potential role in disease initiation or progression. Gingipains generate reactive oxygen species (ROS). The ROS also elevate lysosomal pH by inhibiting buffering enzymes (e.g., brain-type creatine kinase [BB-CK]), stabilizing β-sheet conformations of tau, and enhancing its pathological accumulation [55].

Bacterial Membrane Vesicles

BMVs are spherical nanostructures (20–250 nm) released by bacteria. BMVs serve as carriers for diverse microbial components, including gingipains, LPS, and other bacterial constituents [69,70]. These virulence factors enable them to act as potent mediators of systemic pathogenicity. In Pg, BMVs play a pivotal role in immune evasion, biofilm formation, and host tissue degradation [71,72]. Notably, BMVs exhibit the capacity to traverse the BBB. BMVs gain entry into host cells via lipid raft-dependent endocytosis or clathrin-mediated pathways [68,69]. After tail vein injection of Pg-derived BMVs in mice, vesicle-associated signals were rapidly detected in the brain. This finding further supports the ability of these vesicles to traverse the BBB [53]. Notably, BMVs exhibit prolonged survival in lysosomes (> 24 h), evading degradation and perpetuating intracellular damage [73].

BMVs activate the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, promoting caspase-1-mediated IL-1β maturation and gasdermin D–driven pyroptosis [51,54]. BMV-derived oxysulfur anions and gingipains generate ROS, leading to lipid peroxidation and cysteine residue oxidation [55]. These oxidative modifications promote disulfide bond formation in proteins such as GAPDH. Oxidized GAPDH can aggregate and contribute to Aβ plaque formation [55]. Experimental evidence highlights the neurotoxic potential of BMVs. Consistent with BMV-driven neuroinflammation, Pg BMVs induced pronounced microgliosis and astrogliosis, and p-tau exhibited a transient increase, peaking at approximately 26 days after exposure [53]. In a murine model, oral administration of BMVs induced significant cognitive deficits including impaired spatial memory and learning abilities [51].

P. gingivalis In Vitro Cell Models

In vitro studies utilizing the hCMEC/D3 brain microvascular endothelial cell line revealed that Pg infection triggers TLR4- and NF-κB/CatB-dependent upregulation of RAGE mRNA, concomitant with augmented transcellular Aβ42 transport across endothelial monolayers [42]. This suggests that Pg compromises BBB integrity while facilitating Aβ trafficking.

Systemic inflammatory responses elicited by Pg were further demonstrated in monocyte and macrophage models exposed to LPS. Conditioned media from these cells induced IL-1β and TNF-α overexpression in primary leptomeningeal cells, mimicking cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)–blood barrier dysregulation [74]. Notably, such media also activated astrocytes derived from neuronal precursor cells. This activation was evidenced by elevated glial fibrillary acidic protein expression [75], implicating peripheral immune cells in propagating neuroinflammation [76].

Microglial activation mechanisms were delineated in BV-2, MG6, and RAW 264.7 cell lines challenged with Pg or LPS. The TLR2/TLR4-mediated NF-κB/STAT3 or NF-κB/CatB pathways drove pro-inflammatory polarization, marked by upregulated IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17, and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) [37,[75], [76], [77]]. Synergistic effects were observed when microglia were co-stimulated with Pg-LPS and Aβ42, amplifying IL-1β and TNF-α production [39]. Furthermore, macrophage–Pg co-cultures elevated APP levels, directly linking infection to amyloidogenic pathways.

Herpes Simplex Virus–P. gingivalis Interactions

AD is widely recognized as a multifactorial disorder. In addition to bacteria typified by Pg, herpes simplex virus (HSV) has also been detected in brain specimens from AD patients [78]. HSV-1, a member of the HSV family, is highly prevalent in the human oral cavity and establishes latency within the peripheral nervous system [79]. HSV-1 can likewise remain latent in neurons or glial cells, and chronic infection or intermittent reactivation may sustain microglial and astrocytic activation, driving pro-inflammatory cytokine release and exacerbating oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. In parallel, HSV-1 has been implicated in promoting Aβ accumulation and p-tau [80]. Moreover, HSV-1 may disrupt proteostatic mechanisms including the autophagy–lysosome pathway, compromise the BBB, and perturb neurotransmitter systems such as cholinergic signaling [80]. These effects may lower barriers to the trafficking of additional pathogens into the brain, thereby further aggravating infection-associated neuroinflammation. HSV has been closely linked to the severity of PD. It may synergize with periodontal pathogens to intensify inflammation and tissue breakdown [81]. Active herpesvirus infection can dampen local immune defenses, thereby promoting the colonization and expansion of pathogenic bacteria such as Pg. Conversely, bacterial components may reactivate latent herpesviruses, creating a self-reinforcing loop in which viral replication and bacterial overgrowth mutually amplify one another [82]. Recurrent reactivation of latent herpesviruses may cause episodic tissue injury; repeated or persistent episodes can compromise epithelial barrier integrity and facilitate entry into the systemic circulation [81,82].

Collectively, these findings suggest that Pg exacerbates AD through multiple virulence factors and pathogenic mechanisms. There are many mechanisms that can lead to Aβ plaque formation and tau hyperphosphorylation, such as immune evasion, dysregulated APP processing, ApoE fragmentation, and complement dysregulation, among others. However, the hierarchical relationship of these mechanisms remains elusive. This ambiguity poses significant challenges for targeted therapy development as simultaneous inhibition of multiple pathways is required. Therefore, future investigations should prioritize three critical areas: determining whether specific mechanisms exhibit temporal dominance during different stages of disease progression, elucidating potential synergistic interactions between periodontal pathogens and established AD risk factors (e.g., genetic variants, metabolic disorders), and systematically evaluating the BBB penetration kinetics of key virulence factors. Addressing these knowledge gaps will not only clarify the causal relationships underlying the Pg–AD association but also provide a robust foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

How Does P. gingivalis gain access to the brain?

The oral cavity is anatomically close to the brain. This proximity raises the possibility that microbiota and their metabolites can directly reach the CNS via multiple pathways [23,83]. Oral pathogens, especially Pg, can enter the bloodstream and cross the BBB, potentially inducing neuroinflammation and Aβ deposition [25,84].

P. gingivalis Breaches the Gingival Barrier Into the Bloodstream

How Pg breaches the local gingival defenses and enters the bloodstream is a critical step linking localized infection to systemic inflammation. The gingival tissue functions as a dynamic defensive system composed of epithelial architecture, intercellular junctions, antimicrobial molecules, and immune cells. When this system is disrupted by inflammation or mechanical stress, bacteria and their virulence factors can cross the mucosal interface through multiple routes, leading to transient or recurrent bacteremia and endotoxemia. These circulating microbial stimuli may then sustain inflammatory responses in distal organs. To better define Pg barrier-crossing mechanisms and their implications for disease progression, the following section first outlines the structural and functional basis of the gingival barrier and then highlights key nodes of pathogen-driven disruption.

Structure and function of the gingival barrier

The gingival epithelium serves as the primary line of defense against microbial invasion and plays a critical role in maintaining periodontal health. Its barrier function is multifaceted, encompassing physical, chemical, and immunologic components [85,86]. The physical barrier function of the gingival epithelium is primarily maintained by its stratified structure and intercellular junctions including TJs, adherens junctions (AJs), and gap junctions (GJs). This chemical barrier is mediated by a variety of antimicrobial peptides and proteins secreted by epithelial cells, leukocytes, and salivary glands. The immunological barrier is maintained by neutrophils that migrate through the junctional epithelium (JE) into the gingival sulcus, as well as dendritic cells and T cells located within the epithelium [85,86].

However, certain regions of the epithelium, particularly the JE, are more vulnerable to microbial and mechanical challenges, making them key sites for the pathogenesis of PD. JE, which attaches to the tooth surface via hemidesmosomes, is thinner (3–5 cell layers) than other regions of the gingival epithelium. This structural compromise, coupled with its high permeability, facilitates the continuous flow of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF). The GCF contains host factors such as plasma proteins, cytokines, immunoglobulins, and cells. Although GCF plays a crucial role in immune surveillance, it also creates a potential entry point for pathogens [87]. JE is constantly exposed to a rich and diverse microbiome, as well as mechanical stresses from chewing and oral hygiene practices. These persistent local stimuli can trigger the initiation of immune responses to address the ongoing needs for surveillance, regulation, and tissue repair [87]. However, in susceptible individuals, the inflammatory response may become dysregulated, leading to ineffective microbial control and tissue destruction. This imbalance is a hallmark of PD, with the host's immune response contributing to the destruction of the periodontium [88].

Oral bacteria can invade the gingival epithelium through both paracellular and transcellular pathways (Figure 3). Paracellular invasion occurs when bacteria disrupt intercellular junctions, either by directly degrading junctional proteins or by modulating their expression [85]. For example, Pg gingipains can degrade AJ proteins such as E-cadherin and TJ proteins such as occludin and junctional adhesion molecule 1, increasing paracellular permeability [89]. The LPS can decrease the E-cadherin, occludin, and claudin, thereby facilitating LPS translocation [90]. Additionally, bacterial metabolites such as butyrate can weaken the epithelial barrier by triggering pyroptosis and suppressing the expression of critical junctional proteins [91].

Mechanistic pathways of bacterial invasion and epithelial disruption

Pg breaks through the epithelial barrier in three ways: (1) transcellular invasion via endocytosis and exocytosis in epithelial cells; (2) paracellular invasion by damaging epithelial intercellular junction proteins AJs, TJs, and GJs; (3) neutrophil dysfunction causing overproduction of ROS, MMPs, and NETs, leading to epithelial ulceration. In addition, local inflammation increases vascular fragility, making it easier for bacteria to penetrate, allowing for the infiltration of immune cells and inflammatory mediators into the affected tissue. Abbreviations: JAM, junctional adhesion molecule; AJ, adherens junction; TJ, tight junction; GJ, gap junction; ROS, reactive oxygen species; MMPs, matrix metalloproteinases; NETs, neutrophil extracellular traps.

Transcellular invasion involves the binding of bacterial adhesins to specific host cell receptors, allowing internalization and survival within epithelial cells (Figure 3). Upon entering epithelial cells, Pg downregulates the expression of its major virulence factor, gingipains [92]. This downregulation likely aims to minimize host cell damage and prolong the survival of both the pathogen and the host. Intracellular Pg populations are transported to the endoplasmic reticulum–rich autophagosomes. Only a few persist in the hypoxic cytoplasm, predominantly localized to protein-dense perinuclear regions, which supports bacterial metabolism [93,94]. Ultimately, Pg spreads via actin-based membrane protrusion or exits cells through a vesicular recycling pathway [93]. Pg evades epithelial immune defenses through multiple mechanisms (Figure 4). It upregulates host ecto-5′-nucleotidase (CD73) activity, suppressing extracellular adenosine triphosphate (eATP)–induced ROS generation [95]. Pg-secreted nucleoside diphosphate kinase blocks eATP/P2X7 signaling, thereby reducing mitochondrial ROS production [96]. Additionally, Pg stimulates host glutathione synthesis to directly neutralize ROS [93]. Furthermore, Pg activates cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) signaling via the adenosine A2A receptor, exploiting cAMP as an energy source and suppressing pro-inflammatory responses, thereby creating a favorable niche for proliferation [97,98].

Immune evasion in the gingival barrier by Porphyromonas gingivalis

Pg evades epithelial immune defenses through multiple mechanisms. In epithelial cells, Pg upregulates host CD73 activity. This converts eATP to ADO, reducing eATP and inhibiting ROS production. ADO activates the A2A receptor–mediated cAMP signaling pathway. Pg uses cAMP as an energy source for intracellular proliferation and suppresses extracellular inflammation. Additionally, Pg blocks the eATP/P2X7 signaling pathway, reducing mitochondrial ROS production, and stimulates the host to synthesize glutathione to neutralize ROS. In neutrophils, Pg induces crosstalk between C5aR and TLR2. This decouples the TLR2-MyD88 pathway and activates the TLR2-Mal-PI3K pathway, impairing phagocytosis and enhancing inflammation. Pg can survive and breach the epithelial barrier via neutrophil movement post phagocytosis. In macrophages, Pg survives phagocytosis by macrophages, triggering excess C5a production. C5aR and TLR2 co-activation on macrophages leads to higher cAMP and lower IL-12 and NO. Also, CD14 downregulation impairs pathogen recognition. In neutrophils, Pg initiates signaling crosstalk between C5aR and TLR2 (Figure 4). This interaction leads to the uncoupling of the host-protective TLR2-MyD88 pathway, whereas it activates an alternate TLR2-Mal-PI3K pathway [99,100]. Notably, the latter pathway exhibits dual functionality by impairing phagocytic activity while simultaneously enhancing inflammatory processes. Intriguingly, even when internalized by host cells, the pathogen subverts intracellular defense mechanisms through the PI3K-mediated disruption of phagolysosome formation. This disruption allows the pathogen to evade intracellular defenses and survive within the cellular environment [101]. Even when phagocytosed by macrophages, Pg can survive (Figure 4). It triggers excessive complement C5a production, pathologically activating C5a signaling in macrophages [102]. C5aR co-activation with TLR2 elevates cAMP levels [100], while suppressing TLR4-mediated IL-12p70 synthesis [102,103]. This cascade suppresses NO production, a key bactericidal mechanism, thereby promoting Pg survival [104]. Furthermore, Pg selectively downregulates macrophage surface CD14 expression [105], impairing pathogen recognition and phagocytic efficiency.

Neutrophils, the predominant leukocytes in periodontal pockets, play dual roles in periodontal homeostasis [[106], [107], [108], [109]]. Although essential for containing pathogens, neutrophils can become hyperactivated or dysregulated when exposed to Pg-derived proteases and LPS. This pathological state leads to excessive production of ROS, MMPs, and NETs [110]. These substances directly degrade collagen, alveolar bone, and JE. Such tissue-destructive processes are strongly associated with chronic periodontitis progression [[111], [112], [113], [114], [115], [116]]. Clinically, this manifests as periodontal pocket formation, attachment loss, and ulceration of the gingival epithelium (Figure 3). These lesions can create portals for bacterial dissemination into the systemic circulation [117,118].

The integrity of the gingival epithelial barrier also undergoes continuous mechanical challenges owing to physiological functions and hygiene practices. Both periodontally healthy individuals and patients subjected to chewing, oral hygiene procedures, and dental interventions have reported transient bacteremia [[119], [120], [121], [122], [123], [124]]. Mechanical stress from chewing and toothbrushing generates intermittent pressure gradients, forcing bacteria and GCF toward the pocket epithelium and into deeper epithelial layers [[120], [121], [122], [123]]. The periodontal pocket functions as a biomechanical pump, explaining the GCF reflux phenomenon [125]. Bacteria within GCF are pushed into the gingival venules and reach blood circulation via the superior vena cava, producing both PD-related bacteremia and endotoxemia [126]. Inflamed venules, which exhibit increased fragility, permit bacterial penetration during routine mechanical stresses, creating a pathological feedback loop [123]. The oral cavity serves as the primary interface between the host and the external microbial environment [87]. Although normally in a state of equilibrium, disruptions of homeostasis—particularly within the gingival epithelium—can initiate local and systemic inflammation [88].

Cascade amplification of systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation

PD is a persistent source of systemic inflammation through multiple interrelated pathways. The ulcerated gingival epithelium facilitates pathogen translocation into the bloodstream, causing bacteremia and triggering acute-phase responses [127,128]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, are produced locally in periodontal tissues and enter systemic circulation via ulcerated gingival epithelia [129,130]. These mediators stimulate hepatic synthesis of acute-phase proteins such as C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, and serum amyloid A. Subsequently, a chronic low-grade inflammatory state develops [127,128]. Clinically, individuals with advanced periodontitis exhibit elevated serum levels of IL-6, CRP, and fibrinogen, accompanied by neutrophilia. These findings support the systemic propagation of oral inflammation [127,[131], [132], [133], [134]].

Emerging evidence has revealed indirect mechanisms through which periodontal pathogens exacerbate systemic inflammation. Oral ingestion of periodontal bacteria can ectopically colonize the gastrointestinal tract. Clinical evidence supports the presence of periodontal pathogens in the gut of inflammatory bowel disease patients [[135], [136], [137]]. Murine models suggest that Pg could alter gut microbiota composition, inducing intestinal dysbiosis and subsequent gut-derived systemic inflammation [135,[138], [139], [140]]. Conversely, intestinal inflammation may also aggravate periodontal inflammation. For example, dextran sulfate sodium–induced colitis has been reported to exacerbate periodontitis by activating the NADPH oxidase 2/ROS axis in M1-like macrophages [141]. In addition to the gut, oral microbes translocated via the oropharyngeal route have been associated with pulmonary inflammation [142]. This translocation mechanism suggests that oral pathogens amplify inflammatory burden beyond localized periodontal destruction. Dysregulated host–microbiome interactions in the oral cavity drive distal inflammation through microbial translocation, cytokine spillover, or shared immunopathological pathways [112,143,144].

The hematogenous spread of pathogens and cytokines, as well as microbial colonization of distant sites, can drive systemic inflammation. Chronic systemic diseases can be induced by this persistent inflammation. Importantly, the BBB integrity can also be negatively affected if the persistent inflammatory state is not resolved in a timely manner, ultimately leading to neuroinflammation. Pg can translocate to the brain via the bloodstream [25] or nerve pathways [21,145,146] and play a critical role in neuroinflammation. This process involves innate immune activation mediated by ROS production and complement pathways [25,63]. Studies have demonstrated that TLR4 activation by LPS in astrocytes and microglia induces a pro-inflammatory response in the brain [147,148].

In summary, PD primes the neuroimmune system through three synergistic mechanisms: direct microbial invasion of brain tissue, BBB permeability alterations, and cytokine-mediated bidirectional communication between peripheral and central immune systems. These processes establish PD as a modifiable risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases. Therefore, clarifying the temporal dynamics and synergistic interplay between these inflammatory processes is essential. This understanding is critical not only for elucidating AD pathogenesis within the oral–brain axis framework but also for identifying modifiable risk factors and developing targeted therapeutic interventions.

P. gingivalis Breaches the Blood–Brain Barrier into the Central Nervous System

The BBB is the principal line of defense between the peripheral circulation and the CNS. Its integrity determines whether blood-borne microbial components, peripheral inflammatory mediators, and immune cells can enter the brain parenchyma and trigger neuroinflammation. Within the oral–brain axis framework, Pg and its virulence factors are thought to impact BBB function through hematogenous spread or neural pathways. Under systemic inflammatory conditions, BBB stability can be further weakened, creating a permissive milieu for amplification of CNS inflammation and pathological progression. To systematically clarify this process, we first outline the structural and functional basis of the BBB. We then discuss potential routes by which Pg may invade the CNS and the mechanisms of BBB disruption driven by systemic inflammation.

Structure and function of the blood–brain barrier

The BBB is a highly selective interface. It can regulate the transport of nutrients and shield the CNS from blood-borne pathogens and harmful agents [149,150]. The BBB is composed of multiple specialized layers and ensures structural and functional integrity through a combination of physical, metabolic, and cellular components [24,[151], [152], [153], [154], [155]]. Therefore, the functional and structural integrity of the BBB is pivotal for maintaining homeostasis in the brain microenvironment. Dysregulation of this barrier is often observed in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, and accumulating evidence proposes BBB dysfunction as an early biomarker of AD and underscores its pivotal role in CNS health [[156], [157], [158], [159]]. The molecular layers of the BBB are composed of a thick glycocalyx and basement membranes (BMs) enriched with ectoenzymes, receptors, and transporters that regulate molecular trafficking [153,154]. The BMs have a two-layer composition including the vascular BMs and the glia limitans, secreted by endothelial cells and astrocytes, respectively [153,154]. Cellular components include endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes [153]. BECs are interconnected by TJs and AJs, limiting paracellular diffusion [152,154]. Together, these components constitute the physical, chemical, and metabolic barriers of the BBB [24].

There are multiple distinct pathways for Pg to invade the CNS. One common route is the transcellular pathway, where microorganisms cross microvascular endothelial cells via receptor-mediated transcytosis or hijacking vesicular transport systems [160]. Once Pg invades the endothelial cell, it inhibits phagosomes and evades lysosomes, and it can hijack the endosomal recycling pathway, spreading from one cell to another to propagate inflammation [24]. Another transcellular mechanism involves entry through a “Trojan horse” strategy, also known as leukocyte-facilitated entry. Through this mechanism, pathogens hide inside infected immune cells that cross the BBB [161]. Bacteria can either induce mutations in leukocytes, enabling them to cross the BBB endothelium, or infect leukocytes already capable of such translocation [162]. The ability of Pg to interact with leukocyte adhesion molecules at the BBB enhances inflammatory cell trafficking across this barrier. Additionally, some pathogens bypass the BBB entirely by using nonhematogenous routes such as direct invasion through cranial nerves or circumventricular organs, which lack a complete barrier [160]. Pg also exhibits neurotropic potential. Li et al. proposed that Pg could gradually spread over many years from neuron to neuron through anatomically connected pathways [163]. This suggests that neural pathways such as the olfactory or trigeminal nerves serve as potential routes for Pg to infiltrate the CNS [21,23,146,164,165]. Furthermore, Pg can compromise BBB integrity through paracellular mechanisms. Pg-LPS induces microglial O2− production, directly increasing BBB permeability [63,166,167]. Gingipains activate thrombin to disrupt endothelial TJs in vitro, facilitating paracellular passage [168]. These mechanisms established a connection between PD and AD.

Disruption of the blood–brain barrier by systemic inflammation

Under physiological conditions, the BBB maintains cerebral homeostasis by restricting the entry of peripheral immune cells and macromolecules through endothelial TJs [169]. However, this selective barrier integrity is progressively impaired in AD patients. BBB disruption has been widely reported as a critical pathological feature of early-stage AD. Dynamic-contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) has detected gadobutrol leakage and cerebral microbleeds in BBB-related brain regions. These findings demonstrate heightened BBB permeability in AD patients. Notably, gadobutrol extravasation and cerebral microbleeds localized to BBB-associated regions confirm early vascular compromise in AD pathogenesis [157]. Furthermore, Aβ can rupture the neuronal plasma membrane by forming pores, leading to cytoplasmic leakage and cell death [170], either through direct lipid disruption or through interaction with ion channels in the membrane [171]. Certainly, the integrity of the BBB is profoundly compromised under systemic inflammatory conditions. Many studies have shown that peripheral inflammatory mediators disrupt BBB functionality through multifaceted mechanisms.

Under inflammatory conditions, the glycocalyx undergoes rapid degradation, exacerbating vascular permeability and leukocyte infiltration [172,173]. Pro-inflammatory mediators including IL-1β [174,175], IL-6 [176], TNF-α [177], and cyclophilin activate endothelial receptors [178]. Concurrently, pathogen-associated molecular patterns interact with TLRs, particularly TLR2, TLR4, and TLR6 [179]. These signals increase paracellular permeability, leading to vascular leakage and extravasation of plasma-derived neurotoxic molecules. Enhanced macropinocytosis and transcytosis further exacerbate BBB dysfunction, permitting the influx of immune cells and amplifying neuroinflammation [152,180]. The BM layers become distinguishable, forming expanded perivascular spaces [153]. CD163-positive perivascular macrophages, constitutively present in this compartment, exhibit dual scavenging and immunomodulatory functions [[181], [182], [183], [184], [185]]. Pericyte–endothelial crosstalk can be impaired by pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress, leading to enhanced transcytosis, downregulation of TJ proteins, and increased leukocyte infiltration [186]. Notably, pericyte degeneration leads to neurovascular uncoupling, resulting in reduced oxygen supply to the brain and increased metabolic stress [187]. Systemic inflammation can upregulate astrocytic aquaporin-4 expression, augmenting vascular permeability [188]. Furthermore, inflamed astrocytes adopt a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by upregulated IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and prostaglandins [189]. These mediators compromise TJ integrity by inducing endothelial inflammatory cascades and oxidative stress (Figure 5). BBB leakage further permits influx of bacterial components and peripheral inflammatory mediators, amplifying neuroinflammation and neuronal death [190].

Experimental models have demonstrated that IL-1R1 signaling in BECs drives cyclooxygenase-2-dependent prostaglandin E2 synthesis [191,192], which diffuses into the parenchyma to activate neuronal and glial prostaglandin receptors [153]. This paracrine signaling cascade induces sickness behavior without overt solute leakage, underscoring the BBB’s role as a dynamic sensor-transducer of peripheral inflammation [153,191,192]. MyD88, a signaling protein in the IL-1R1/NF-κB pathway, was specifically removed from endothelial cells. Without endothelial MyD88, the previously observed neuroimmune effects disappeared. This demonstrates that intact endothelial signaling is essential for brain–immune system communication [192,193]. Sustained systemic inflammation promotes immune cell infiltration through the BBB via coordinated molecular changes. Endothelial cells increase E- and P-selectins and chemokines, which promote leukocyte rolling and adhesion [194,195]. Inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IFN-γ, and IL-17) activate matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2/MMP-9 specifically at the glial limitans. This enzymatic activity allows leukocytes to cross the barrier [196,197]. Concurrently, glycocalyx degradation and ICAM-1 overexpression further destabilize endothelial interactions [[198], [199], [200]]. These alterations permit influx of monocytes, neutrophils, and natural killer cells, perpetuating neuroinflammation and creating a permissive microenvironment for Aβ deposition [[200], [201], [202]]. According to a systematic review reported by Varatharaj and Galea [154], approximately 60% of experimental studies showed increased BBB solute permeability during systemic inflammation, particularly under endotoxemic challenges (e.g., LPS or poly(I:C) administration) [[203], [204], [205]]. Postmortem analyses of septic patients reveal loss of TJs proteins (occludin, ZO-1, and claudin-5), attributed to inflammation-driven MMP activation [206]. For example, serum from LPS-treated mice significantly reduces transendothelial electrical resistance in in vitro BBB models. Cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, induced by LPS, critically contribute to this BBB damage [207]. Importantly, the severity of BBB breakdown exhibits dose- and time-dependence. Prolonged or intense systemic inflammation not only exacerbates solute leakage but also promotes lymphocyte trafficking and innate immune cell infiltration (e.g., monocytes and neutrophils). Focal endothelial cell injury emerges as a histopathological hallmark of terminal BBB failure [153].

These multiphasic changes underscore the BBB's transition from a regulated interface to a vulnerable conduit during chronic inflammatory challenges. Altered signaling, enhanced cellular trafficking, increased solute permeability, and direct endothelial injury collectively signify an escalation in the extent of structural disruption to the BBB. The ROS generated by activated microglia and astrocytes during inflammation exacerbate BBB damage. In addition, peripheral pro-inflammatory factors can also affect the brain through neural pathways. Vagal nerve activation sends inflammatory signals to the nucleus tractus solitarius. Conversely, trigeminal nerve stimulation raises cytokine levels in the brain. These elevated cytokines drive Aβ accumulation and excessive tau protein phosphorylation [190,208].

The inflammatory response induces both astrocytes and microglia to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and CRP [[209], [210], [211]]. Additionally, in vitro studies have reported that these pro-inflammatory cytokines can also induce the production of Aβ42 and p-tau, which in turn further activate neuroinflammatory responses [209,212]. Several animal studies have shown that LPS exposure amplifies cerebral Aβ dynamics, increasing parenchymal influx while reducing efflux, coinciding with elevated IL-6, IL-10, and IL-13 levels [84,213].

In summary, PD is characterized by localized infection of periodontal tissues, predominantly caused by Pg, which leads to the sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. These pathogens and inflammatory mediators breach the gingival epithelial barrier, enter systemic circulation, and subsequently target the BBB, ultimately promoting their access to the CNS [214]. Upon crossing the BBB, cytokines activate microglia and astrocytes. This triggers a self-perpetuating cycle of neuroinflammation, marked by amplified production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS [215]. Notably, elevated systemic TNF-α levels and anti-Pg antibodies in AD patients correlate with this inflammatory crosstalk, as demonstrated by Kamer et al. [216]. This suggests that periodontal pathogens trigger systemic inflammation, leading to neural damage [36]. Pg-mediated systemic inflammation exacerbates chronic conditions such as atherosclerosis and hypertension, which are also believed to be risk factors for the development of AD [217]. Additionally, aging is another shared major risk factor for AD and PD, and both diseases are highly prevalent in older adults. Murine studies have demonstrated that aged mice exhibit stronger cytokine responses (TNF-α and IL-1β) and behavioral impairments following peripheral LPS challenge than young controls. This experimental finding parallels clinical observations showing accelerated cognitive decline in elderly patients with systemic infections [213].

The role of aging in the Alzheimer’s disease–periodontal disease axis

AD is highly prevalent among the elderly. Studies have shown that cognitive impairment occurs only in middle-aged animals following LPS exposure, compared to the young controls [34,36]. The progressive dysfunction of the BBB during aging emerges as a pivotal contributor to AD. Cellular senescence within the neurovascular unit amplifies BBB vulnerability. The age-dependent loss of pericytes disrupts endothelial integrity [180], precipitating rapid neurodegeneration and behavioral deficits in experimental models within two weeks [218]. Cerebrovascular endothelial senescence is associated with increased BBB permeability in vitro using primary mouse brain endothelial cell and pericyte co-cultures [219]. One mechanism involves a decline in Sirtuin-1 expression [220]. Enhanced endothelial expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1), driven by complement signaling, further exacerbates neuroinflammation and TJs degradation [221]. Astrocytic remodeling, characterized by pro-inflammatory activation and reduced proliferation, as well as age-related thickening of the vascular basement membrane, collectively impairs microvascular function [[222], [223], [224]]. DCE-MRI revealed age-dependent disruptions in the vascular BBB, characterized by increased permeability in hippocampal regions and cerebral white and gray matter [156,225]. These structural alterations correlate with cognitive deficits, notably impaired delayed recall, underscoring the BBB’s role in maintaining neural homeostasis [225,226].

Aging diminishes the efficiency of efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp), which is critical for clearing Aβ from the brain [227]. P-gp activity at the vascular BBB progressively declines beginning in middle age in humans [228,229], synergizing with neuropathological burden [230]. This functional impairment is also accelerated by age-associated inflammatory processes common to both physiological aging and age-related disorders [231]. Decreased CSF turnover [232] and increased caveolar transcytosis [233] in aging have also been reported. The brain’s primary clearance pathway has been linked to exchange processes involving the CSF system [232]. Therefore, the reduction in CSF turnover contributes to elevated CSF/serum albumin ratios as impaired fluid dynamics compromise macromolecule removal [234]. These changes synergistically exacerbate age-related impairments in BBB functions.

The prevalence of PD in the older population is also higher than that in younger individuals. Epidemiological data indicate a marked escalation in PD occurrence among older demographics. There is a seven times greater likelihood for individuals aged ≥ 65 years to develop periodontitis than for those aged 30–34 years. This trend becomes particularly pronounced in septuagenarian and octogenarian populations (70–81 years). This increase is reflected in higher rates of periodontal pathology. Current demographic projections suggest this pattern will intensify due to global population aging trends [235].

This age-related susceptibility to periodontal deterioration is corroborated by controlled experimental gingivitis models. When examining oral inflammatory responses across generations, experimental protocols involving 21-day oral hygiene cessation demonstrated equivalent biofilm accumulation in both young adults (< 25 years) and seniors (> 65 years). However, the elderly group exhibited distinct pathophysiological responses. Amplified inflammatory mediators, including elevated IgG titers, intensified plasma cell infiltration, and diminished polymorphonuclear leukocyte mobilization, have been observed compared to younger participants. This may explain the escalating periodontitis prevalence observed in aging populations [236]. Furthermore, age-related immune decline is associated with neutrophil dysfunction, showing a higher rate of early apoptosis [237]. A study exploring the link between neutrophil function and aging reported reduced phagocytic activity, diminished ROS generation, impaired bactericidal efficiency, and attenuated chemotaxis [238,239]. In parallel, metabolic comorbidities that increase with age may further reprogram neutrophil function and sustain chronic low-grade inflammation [240]. These compromised antimicrobial properties heighten susceptibility to pathogenic invasion in periodontal tissues [241]. Meanwhile, the elderly group exhibits a higher prevalence of systemic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. These conditions can promote the expansion of periodontal pathogens, including Pg, while reducing beneficial commensal bacteria, thereby exacerbating oral dysbiosis and periodontitis. In parallel, heightened systemic inflammation and chronic vascular injury associated with these diseases can impair the BBB and intensify neuroinflammation [22]. Oral dysbiosis and systemic comorbidities synergistically amplify the oral–brain axis, promoting the progression of AD pathology.

Beyond microbial and inflammatory mechanisms, aging acts as an independent risk factor that synergistically interacts with these processes in both PD and AD. The convergence of aging, BBB deterioration, and PD creates a self-reinforcing pathogenic cycle. Systemic inflammation originating from PD accelerates cerebral endothelial senescence and complement activation, while compromised BBB integrity allows peripheral inflammatory mediators to enter the brain, driving AD pathogenesis. This mechanistic overlap links PD to AD in the context of aging. Consequently, interventions that stabilize the BBB or reduce periodontal inflammation offer promising dual approaches for slowing age-related cognitive decline.

Therapeutic approaches

As a manageable risk factor for AD, PD treatment can reduce Pg burden and interrupt the “oral–brain axis” pathology. Compared to broad-spectrum antibiotics, therapies targeting Pg more precisely neutralize Pg-specific toxins (e.g., gingipains and LPS) while avoiding microbiome disruption. These targeted approaches also decrease antibiotic resistance risks and reduce brain oxidative stress through antioxidant activity, making them more suitable for long-term use. However, long-term use of broad-spectrum antibiotics risks disrupting commensal microbiota and promotes antibiotic resistance. This underscores the need for targeted antimicrobial strategies.

However, effective delivery of anti-Pg agents to the CNS remains a major challenge due to the restrictive nature of the BBB. Systemically administered peptides or small-molecule inhibitors often exhibit limited brain penetration, suboptimal local concentrations, and off-target accumulation in peripheral organs. Nanomedicine-based delivery systems offer a promising solution to these limitations by leveraging materials engineering to enhance BBB transport and target specificity. Shen et al. reported lipid-based nanoparticles delivering small interfering RNA (siRNA) against NLRP3 that modulated periodontal inflammation and oral microbiota while enabling downstream suppression of hippocampal neuroinflammation, thereby functionally linking peripheral nanotherapeutic intervention to CNS outcomes across the oral–brain axis [242]. To further address the challenge of direct brain delivery, Zhang et al. developed collagenase-degradable biomimetic nanocages designed to facilitate targeted intracerebral accumulation, which enabled efficient antimicrobial photothermal and gas therapy against Pg, significantly attenuating Pg-mediated neurocognitive dysfunction in vivo [243]. Moreover, biomimetic macrophage-membrane-coated platinum nanoclusters were shown to preferentially cross the BBB and accumulate at Pg-infected brain regions through inflammation-guided targeting, resulting in reduced intracerebral bacterial burden, mitigation of neuronal damage, and marked improvement of AD-like cognitive deficits in a chronic periodontitis model [244].

Against this backdrop, peptide-based Pg-targeted therapeutics with intrinsic anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities represent attractive candidates. GV1001, a 16-amino-acid peptide derived from human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT), was originally developed as an anticancer vaccine. And it has demonstrated multifunctional properties, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant, suppressing the development of PD. In murine models of Pg-induced PD, GV1001 reduced the deposition of Pg DNA aggregates and LPS/gingipains in gingival tissues by > 60%, effectively suppressing bacterial colonization and virulence. Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis revealed that GV1001 completely blocked Pg-triggered upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in brain tissues. Furthermore, GV1001 directly inhibited the expression of gingipains, the critical proteases for Pg invasion [245].

Gingipains play a pivotal role in bacterial pathogenicity and host neurotoxicity. COR286 (targeting RgpA/RgpB), COR271 (targeting Kgp), and the reversible covalent inhibitor COR119 (targeting Kgp) are representative gingipain inhibitors. Mice pretreated with a combination of oral COR271 and subcutaneous COR286 exhibited a marked reduction in hippocampal neurodegeneration induced by stereotactic injection of RgpB and Kgp. Specifically, inhibitor-pretreated mice showed 60% fewer degenerating neurons than controls, demonstrating their capacity to block gingipain-mediated neuronal damage. Notably, treatment with COR119 reduced cerebral Pg colonization by 45% and normalized Aβ to baseline levels, highlighting its dual role in mitigating microbial burden and amyloidogenesis [23].

Atuzaginstat (COR388) is also a covalent Kgp inhibitor targeting the enzyme’s active site. In murine models exposed to oral Pg for 5 weeks, COR388 administration significantly attenuated amyloid accumulation, suppressed TNF-α levels, and preserved hippocampal GABAergic interneuron viability [23]. In vitro, COR388 rescued synaptic loss and inhibited apoptosis in neuronal co-cultures exposed to Pg, demonstrating direct neuroprotection against bacterial virulence [246]. These preclinical studies provided encouraging evidence. In the Phase II/III GAIN study (NCT03823404), exploratory analyses suggested a potential signal of benefit among participants with detectable Pg DNA. However, safety concerns ultimately constrained further clinical development. These findings underscore both the promise of biomarker-guided patient stratification and the challenges of translating gingipain inhibition into a safe and effective therapeutic approach [247]. The integration of nanomedicine strategies with Pg-targeted therapies may synergistically enhance brain bioavailability and mitigate peripheral side effects. Additional studies have also demonstrated the efficacy of gingipain inhibitors. Xie et al. reported that a gingipain inhibitor could effectively restore microglial Aβ clearance and diminish Aβ deposition in a mouse model [46]. Chuang et al. showed the gingipain inhibitors, KYT-1 and KYT-36, reversed neurotoxicity [68].

Concluding remarks and perspectives

It should be acknowledged that much of the current evidence supporting oral pathogen–targeted therapies and salivary biomarkers in AD has several important limitations. The evidence is largely drawn from preclinical studies and observational epidemiological research, where residual confounding and the potential for reverse causation may influence observed associations [248,249]. In addition, differences in AD diagnostic criteria, periodontitis case definitions, and sampling and assay workflows introduce substantial heterogeneity [250]. Finally, the oral microbiome and salivary measures are dynamic. They can change with diet, circadian rhythm, medication use, and local oral conditions. This may reduce their reliability for patient stratification or diagnosis [251]. Therefore, further well-designed clinical investigations are necessary to validate these approaches and to strengthen causal inference. Future research should integrate microbial pathogenesis with host-specific factors to optimize therapeutic outcomes in AD subpopulations. Precision medicine emerges as a transformative paradigm, enabling segmentation of AD populations by risk factors or biomarker profiles. Such stratification may enhance therapeutic responses in specific patients, particularly those with evidence of Pg infection or elevated inflammatory markers. In addition to neural and circulatory pathways, the oral cavity connects to the brain through lymphatic and other routes, facilitating the exchange of molecules between saliva and brain tissue [252]. This connectivity positions saliva as a noninvasive diagnostic source for neurodegenerative diseases like AD. A meta-analysis reveals that AD patients exhibit elevated salivary Aβ42 and p-tau levels, underscoring their potential as early diagnostic biomarkers [253]. The accessibility of these salivary markers could enable routine screening, facilitating timely interventions to delay AD progression in high-risk populations. Despite existing challenges, COR388 underscores the immense developmental potential of virulence factor-targeted therapies. Beyond gingipain inhibition, targeting TNF-α pathways has attracted interest. Agents like etanercept, thalidomide, and lenalidomide aim to modulate neuroinflammation. These efforts reflect a broader shift toward noncyclooxygenase anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Future refinements in biomarker identification and safety optimization are expected to advance their clinical translation.

Multi-omics data integration, including spanning genomics, proteomics, and microbiomics, provides a foundation for precision medicine in AD. For instance, leveraging genome-wide association study (GWAS)–derived insights into ubiquitination, endocytosis, and vascular alterations enables targeted modulation of disease-relevant pathways [254]. Advancements in electronic health record analytics and machine learning provide unprecedented opportunities. These tools enable the identification of high-risk cohorts, correlation of PD severity with AD outcomes, and prediction of therapeutic responses. Such technologies, combined with longitudinal biomarker monitoring, could enable early intervention in PD to mitigate AD progression. This aligns with evidence suggesting that holistic risk factor management prevents up to 72.6% of dementia cases [255]. This approach enables personalized therapeutic regimens tailored to individual risk profiles, optimizing interventions against microbial dysbiosis or inflammatory pathways linked to AD.

Technological innovations are poised to accelerate this transition. Large-scale GWAS datasets and electronic health records enable systematic identification of AD-risk clusters linked to oral microbiome dysregulation. Concurrently, high-resolution molecular analyses (e.g., single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics) offer unprecedented opportunities to delineate microbial contributions to Aβ aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, and neurovascular compromise. Collaborative efforts across neurology, microbiology, and bioinformatics will be critical to decode these complex interactions and develop targeted therapies.

In conclusion, AD is a multifaceted neurodegenerative disorder shaped by complex interactions among genetic predispositions, systemic comorbidities, and modifiable lifestyle factors. Emerging evidence underscores PD as an influential factor in AD pathogenesis, potentially amplifying neuroinflammatory cascades and metabolic dysregulation through persistent Pg invasion and sustained systemic immune activation. While antimicrobial therapies targeting Pg show promising therapeutic prospects, their clinical efficacy remains constrained by narrow-spectrum activity, patient response variability, and insufficient characterization of host–microbe interactions within AD pathology. Thus, strategic prevention, emphasizing early PD identification and targeted intervention, is critical in mitigating the downstream risk of AD. Public health initiatives promoting rigorous oral hygiene practices, particularly among elderly and high-risk populations, represent practical and cost-effective measures to disrupt the pathogenic PD–AD axis. Ultimately, transitioning toward a precision-based therapeutic paradigm that integrates individualized patient data and comprehensive biological insights will be instrumental in reducing the global health burden posed by AD.
