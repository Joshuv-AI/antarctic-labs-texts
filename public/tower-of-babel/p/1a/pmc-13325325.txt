Delivering cervical cancer screening and management to women in remote communities of the Peruvian Amazon: a mixed methods analysis of a mobile point-of-care intervention
Authors: Valerie A Paz-Soldan, Lauren Nussbaum, Cristina Hidalgo, Daniel Lenin Del Cuadro Hidalgo, Karina Gonzales Díaz, David Ramírez, Liliana Berta Herrera, Esther Yolanda Ramírez Satalay, Nolberto Tangoa Rengifo, Hicler Napoleón Rodríguez Mashacuri, Kristopher Jean Pinto Arévalo, Victor Kerler Fatama Rengifo, Graciela Meza-Sánchez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCervical cancer disproportionately affects women in remote and underserved settings, where access to screening, timely results, and treatment is limited. HPV-based screen-and-treat (SAT) strategies can reduce loss to follow-up, but their implementation in hard-to-reach contexts remains challenging. In the Peruvian Amazon, riverine communities face extreme geographic barriers to cervical cancer prevention. This study describes the implementation, screening volume, feasibility, and context-driven adaptations of a mobile, point-of-care HPV-based SAT intervention delivered aboard government-operated ships serving remote Amazonian communities.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study using intervention campaign indicators from ship reports and semi-structured interviews. Quantitative indicators were collected for all women participating in four six-week campaigns conducted between 2023 and early 2025 on three ships: HPV screening, result delivery, visual assessment for treatment, thermal ablation, referrals, and completion of the care continuum. Qualitative interviews with health personnel and program authorities assessed intervention feasibility and adaptations required for mobile and intercultural settings.FindingsAcross four campaigns, 330 women were screened for HPV, representing a ten- to twenty-fold increase compared with prior ship-based screening approaches. Nearly all women received their results (99%), and most HPV-positive women completed same-day visual assessment and treatment with thermal ablation. Health personnel reported high feasibility and acceptability of self-sampling, same-day results, and on-board treatment. Engagement with local leaders and culturally adapted counseling were central to implementation.InterpretationA mobile, point-of-care HPV-based screen-and-treat strategy delivered aboard river ships is feasible and enabled completion of the cervical cancer care continuum in extremely remote settings, supporting context-responsive approaches to advance equitable cervical cancer elimination.FundingNational Institutes of Health/National Cancer Institute (grant ID R01/U01-CA190366).Keywords: Cervical cancer, Mobile point-of-care, Screen-and-treat, HPV, Thermal ablation, Geographically isolated populations

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Before undertaking this study, we reviewed the published literature on mobile and point-of-care HPV screening strategies, including screen-and-treat approaches, with particular attention to implementation in low-resource and hard-to-reach settings. We searched PubMed for studies published before July 10, 2025, and ran the search again on April 15, 2026, using combinations of terms including “HPV,” “screen-and-treat,” “mobile health,” “point-of-care,” and “cervical cancer,” without language restrictions, and reviewed reference lists of relevant articles. Prior studies described mobile HPV screening programs, point-of-care testing, or screening followed by delayed treatment or referral. However, we identified no published evaluations of mobile programs delivering both molecular HPV testing and same-day treatment, nor such programs within government-operated mobile service platforms serving geographically isolated populations.

Added value of this study

This study provides novel evidence on the implementation of a mobile, point-of-care HPV-based screen-and-treat strategy that integrates self-sampling, on-board molecular testing, same-day results, and thermal ablation treatment in geographically isolated Amazonian riverine communities. By embedding the intervention within an existing government mobile service platform, the program was associated with an increase in the number of women screened in a six-week campaign and enabled high completion of the care continuum among HPV-positive women. The qualitative findings offer practical insights into the operational, cultural, and workforce adaptations required to implement SAT approaches in mobile and intercultural settings.

Implications of all the available evidence

Together with existing evidence supporting HPV-based screen-and-treat strategies, these findings demonstrate that mobile point-of-care screen-and-treat delivery models are feasible and can overcome long-standing barriers to cervical cancer prevention in remote populations when adapted to local contexts. National cervical cancer programs should consider flexible, context-responsive service delivery models and diagnostic platform selection to ensure equitable access and completion of care. Mobile, single-visit SAT approaches can play a critical role in advancing global cervical cancer elimination goals for populations living in the hardest-to-reach settings.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is preventable because it can be detected and treated in its precancerous stage before progressing to invasive disease. Yet the burden remains severe in low- and middle-income countries, where over 80% of cases occur, largely due to limited access to cervical cancer screening and management (CCSM) services.1 HPV vaccination and CCSM initiatives have significantly reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality in many settings. However, weak healthcare infrastructure, procurement constraints, shortages of trained personnel, and geographic and cultural barriers continue to prevent many—particularly the most vulnerable women—from accessing early detection and treatment.1, 2, 3 HPV-based screen-and-treat (SAT) approaches offer important advantages over cytology-based screening because HPV testing is more sensitive, allows for earlier detection and longer screening intervals, and can be performed through self-collected samples, thereby reducing barriers to participation.4,5

A growing body of research from Latin America has documented both the promise and persistent challenges of implementing HPV-based cervical cancer prevention strategies in resource-constrained and geographically dispersed settings. Prior studies have demonstrated high acceptability and feasibility of HPV self-sampling among women in rural and peri-urban communities, highlighting its potential to overcome barriers related to stigma, distance, and limited clinical infrastructure.6,7 Implementation research in the region has also shown that screen-and-treat approaches can substantially improve linkage to care compared to traditional cytology-based models, though gaps in follow-up and treatment completion remain.7, 8, 9, 10 Recent evaluations have emphasized the importance of integrating community engagement, health system strengthening, culturally sensitive education, and decentralized treatment modalities to sustain gains in screening coverage and outcomes.9,11,12 Together, this literature underscores that while HPV-based SAT strategies are effective, their success depends on context-specific adaptations, particularly in settings characterized by mobility, geographic isolation, and health system fragmentation.

Proyecto Precancer is an implementation science initiative that supported the co-design and roll-out of a new HPV-based SAT approach in the largest public health network in the Amazon rainforest city of Iquitos, Peru.10,13,14 The intervention integrated self-sampling, HPV testing, visual assessment for treatment (VAT), and thermal ablation (TA) at the primary-care level for those eligible, avoiding referral for most to hospital-based specialists, where follow-up has historically been poor.15 Within months, screening tripled and follow-up more than doubled among HPV-positive women, with the largest increases in peri-urban and rural communities.10 The Peruvian Ministry of Health subsequently adopted this model for national scale-up, with Proyecto Precancer support.16

Despite these advances, women living in the most remote areas of the Peruvian Amazon remain largely excluded from cervical cancer prevention services. Many communities are accessible only by river, require days of travel to reach Iquitos, and are served only by small health posts with limited personnel, equipment, and referral capacity. Conventional screening is particularly ineffective in these settings because women frequently cannot return to public health facilities for results or treatment. This prompted the question: how do we adapt the HPV-based SAT approach for women living in the most remote areas of the Peruvian Amazon rainforest, where communities are inaccessible by road and have limited air access?

Portable HPV testing systems and rechargeable thermocoagulator devices created an opportunity to adapt HPV-based SAT for Peru's Ambulatory Platform for Social Action (Plataformas Itinerantes de Acción Social, or PIAS for its initials in Spanish) ships serving remote riverine communities. Together, these technologies enabled screening, same-day results, and treatment during a single encounter. Although mobile point-of-care HPV screening programs have been implemented,17,18 few programs have combined HPV testing with same-day results and treatment in highly remote settings.19, 20, 21, 22

The Peruvian government delivers services—ranging from civil registration to banking and health care—to the country's most geographically isolated populations through PIAS campaigns.23 In Loreto, the PIAS operate mobile six-week campaigns through ten Peruvian naval ships (and a small seaplane) that navigate the Amazon River and its tributaries approximately four times a year. Proyecto Precancer partnered with this program to pilot a mobile, point-of-care SAT intervention on three PIAS ships between 2023 and early 2025 (hereinafter Proyecto Precancer intervention).

This manuscript describes the implementation of this SAT intervention and examines its feasibility and required adaptations from the perspectives of PIAS personnel involved in four campaigns. Because the research team could not travel on the ships, we helped train clinical and laboratory personnel before departure and conducted interviews after each campaign. These findings provide insight into the implementation of HPV-based mobile SAT programs in hard-to-reach settings and may inform efforts to expand cervical cancer prevention services in other hard-to-reach settings.

Methods

Study design

This mixed-methods study drew on two data sources: quantitative indicators collected during four PIAS campaigns implementing the Proyecto Precancer intervention, and semi-structured interviews with PIAS authorities and personnel conducted after campaign completion.

Setting

This intervention was implemented in Loreto (population approximately 1·1M), Peru's largest state, which covers 28·7% of the national territory and is characterized by limited road access and extensive river networks.24 The four campaigns were conducted along the Putumayo (twice), Pastaza, and Morona river routes, serving predominantly Indigenous and mestizo communities that require several days of river travel to reach Iquitos (see Table 1).

Typically, each of the ten PIAS ships completes three to four six-week campaigns annually, providing government, banking, and health service to remote communities. Health services are delivered in small onboard consultation rooms for medicine, obstetrics, nursing, dentistry, pharmacy, and basic laboratory services, usually staffed by one provider per discipline. An intercultural facilitator accompanies the ships and assists with communication in Indigenous languages.

During routine PIAS campaigns, cervical cancer screening by Papanicolaou smear and/or visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) is offered alongside prenatal care, childhood and HPV immunizations, and other primary health-care services. However, unpublished data (Supplementary Table S1) indicate that cervical cancer screening is rarely conducted or documented, likely because of limited time in each community, resource constraints, and the difficulty of ensuring follow-up in remote settings. Pap smear results are often not available until up to 6 months later, at which point women may be difficult to locate; results are typically communicated indirectly through community leaders, residents, or local health-post staff. Among 75 campaigns with available data in Regional Ministry of Health reports, the median number of women screened was only four per campaign (range 0–58 per campaign, mean 8.3) (Supplementary Table S1).

Proyecto Precancer intervention description

Ideation, co-design, planning, and training for the Proyecto Precancer intervention were conducted in Iquitos—Loreto's capital city (estimated population 465,455)25 and the largest city in the world accessible only by air or water. A Proyecto Precancer research assistant (a midwife with extensive public health experience) and a project champion (a physician and health authority with more than 20 years of experience) met with regional Ministry of Health officials to present findings from the HPV-based SAT intervention in Iquitos and discuss adaptation of the model for PIAS ships. Proyecto Precancer subsequently worked closely with regional and national Social Inclusion Program and PIAS teams, together with the Regional Ministry of Health, to plan the intervention (see Fig. 1 for visual depiction of SAT intervention).

To support recruitment of eligible women aged 30–49 years, the Proyecto Precancer research assistant briefly oriented additional PIAS ship personnel to the HPV-based SAT intervention, including the purpose of HPV testing, the option of self-sampling, and same-day treatment for eligible women (see Fig. 1). Participants in this 3-h orientation included PIAS authorities, other health personnel (nurse), and the 10 members of the PIAS “advance team,” who travel by launch boat to communities a few days before the ship's arrival. The advance team, which includes an intercultural facilitator, informed community leaders and local health-post staff about the availability of HPV screening and encouraged them to notify eligible women. Ship personnel also announced the intervention during community meetings and routine service promotion activities to maximize participation.

Once aboard the ship, women received counseling from the midwife and could choose either self-collected or provider-collected sampling. Samples were processed on board by the laboratory technician using the GeneXpert platform. The midwife communicated results on the same day. Women with positive HPV results then underwent individual VAT by the physician. Women with no acetowhite lesions or lesions involving less than 75% of the transformation zone were considered eligible for TA and received same-day treatment with the thermocoagulator. Women with larger lesions, suspected invasive disease, or lesions extending into the endocervical canal were referred to the Loreto Regional Hospital in Iquitos.

Because Proyecto Precancer personnel were not permitted to travel on the PIAS naval ships, formal quality assurance during implementation was limited. Quality control relied primarily on competency assessment during pre-campaign training and supervised practice sessions. Only personnel judged competent in sample processing, counseling, VAT, and TA participated in the intervention. Laboratory technicians could also contact the Loreto Regional Hospital laboratory technician by telephone during campaigns if technical problems arose.

Data collection and analysis

Midwives and physicians completed one of two standardized paper forms immediately after consults for screening and, if relevant, VAT and TA. Laboratory technicians maintained a separate GeneXpert log and exported a spreadsheet of all HPV test results at the end of each campaign. Proyecto Precancer staff digitized and cross-checked all forms against the GeneXpert output and ship referral records. Quantitative data were available for all four campaigns, although information on subsequent follow-up to the hospital after referral was incomplete. Specifically, our variables of interest were (per each ship's six-week campaign, and as a total where the Proyecto Precancer intervention was employed):

Within two weeks of campaign completion, the Proyecto Precancer research assistant who assisted in the training and orientation purposively selected and conducted semi-structured interviews with all health personnel directly involved in the interventions: two physicians, two midwives, two laboratory technicians, and two PIAS program authorities. Interview guides were informed by Enola Proctor's implementation outcomes framework26 and explored perceived acceptability, feasibility, sustainability, and adaptations needed for implementation in mobile and Indigenous community settings.27

Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and coded inductively using Quirkos. Three researchers independently coded transcripts, met to compare coding, resolved discrepancies through discussion, and iteratively refined the codebook. Themes were then organized according to three main SAT model components: screening participation changes, completion of care changes, and overall remaining barriers.

Results

Across four six-week campaigns, 330 women were screened and 327 (99·1%) received their results. Forty-one women (12·4%) tested positive for HPV; of these, 38 received their results (92·7%), 37 (90·2%) underwent VAT, and 35 (85·4%) received same-day TA. Thus, 35 of 41 women with positive results completed the care continuum during the same campaign. Compared with routine PIAS campaigns, which had a median of four women screened per campaign, the intervention achieved a median of 86·5 women screened (Table 2).

Interviewees perceived self-sampling to be highly acceptable to women because it increased privacy and reduced embarrassment (Table 3, SS1–2). Personnel believed that self-sampling was especially important in Indigenous communities and contributed substantially to participation.

Interviewees emphasized that same-day results and treatment were the principal advantages of the intervention. In contrast to routine Pap smear or centralized HPV testing approaches, which often require women to be located months later, the onboard GeneXpert system enabled women to receive results and, when eligible, treatment before the ship departed (Table 3, T1–2).

Overall, as one PIAS authority described this intervention: “You could say it's the opportunity of a lifetime [for women], given the reality these populations face.” Likewise, a midwife mentioned that the responses she received from many of the women treated meant that “this has taken a huge weight off my shoulders.”

Health personnel compared the ease of HPV-based SAT to the significant barriers women experienced with obtaining Pap smear results and follow-up care (Table 3, T3).

Despite the success of the SAT model, important barriers remained. Three women could not be located to receive their results after leaving the ship despite these being available within hours, and one woman declined VAT because of her husband's opposition. Two women were ineligible for TA and faced major obstacles to referral and treatment. Health personnel informed our team that one had already died within months when they searched for her in a follow-up visit, and the other one had been referred to her local health post whose only health professional was on leave. Follow-up for women with large lesions or suspected cancer will remain a challenge due to distance and cost to reach a hospital for follow up.

After the Proyecto Precancer intervention began, the former Regional Ministry of Health cancer coordinator introduced its own HPV testing on PIAS ships in late 2023, beginning with PIAS Morona. The program used Roche's cobas© HPV testing platform, the system selected for national scale-up given its lower cost and high throughput. However, because the platform is not portable, a mobile SAT approach was not possible; results delivery, VAT, and TA for eligible women were supposed to occur later at health facilities along the PIAS route. To our knowledge, due to coordination challenges (including turnover of three cancer coordinators in one year) and resource constraints (lack of a thermocoagulator and a trained provider at health facilities along the river), these women received no follow-up. During the first PIAS campaign in 2025, seven PIAS ships carried cobas test cartridges for centralized processing; none were able to implement an SAT approach for the same reasons (See aggregate data from ship reports in Supplementary Table S1).

Suggestions for future implementation included assigning a second midwife exclusively to HPV testing and counseling because the sole midwife is often occupied with prenatal care, and extending the duration of community stops to allow sufficient time for recruitment, screening, and treatment. Interviewees also recommended recruitment messages in local languages delivered in a woman's voice. Although HPV testing offered several advantages, including self-sampling, interviewees emphasized that the principal benefit lay in the broader SAT approach, which improved service delivery in hard-to-reach settings by enabling completion of the continuum of care within hours.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that a mobile, point-of-care HPV-based SAT program was successfully implemented in remote Amazonian communities. Across four six-week PIAS campaigns, the intervention screened a median of 83 women per campaign, compared with four women during routine PIAS campaigns, and enabled 85% of women with positive HPV results to complete the care continuum through same-day VAT and TA.28 In contexts where women need days of river travel to reach a health facility and are difficult to locate once the ship departs, providing screening, results, and treatment during a single encounter is more important than increasing screening coverage alone.8,15,29

The intervention's success depended on four components: intercultural recruitment, self-sampling, point-of-care HPV testing, and same-day treatment. Advance teams, Indigenous leaders, local health personnel, and messages delivered in local languages and by women increased trust and participation. Self-sampling was perceived to reduce embarrassment and facilitated uptake, particularly in Indigenous communities. However, interviewees consistently identified same-day results and treatment as the intervention's defining advantage. Unlike Pap screening or centralized HPV testing, the onboard GeneXpert platform and portable thermocoagulator enabled women to complete care before the ship left the community.

Our findings are consistent with studies from Papua New Guinea, New Zealand, South Africa, and Malawi showing that point-of-care testing and TA can achieve high rates of same-day treatment.17,19, 20, 21, 22 However, this study extends prior work by demonstrating feasibility on naval ships serving highly dispersed riverine communities inaccessible by road, and by highlighting the importance of intercultural approaches, including Indigenous leaders and local-language messaging, in supporting trust and participation. Finally, our findings illustrate how same-day care can mitigate the extreme logistical barriers faced by women living in remote Amazonian settings.

These findings also underscore the need to monitor the full continuum of care, rather than screening alone, and strengthening follow-up care for women who screen HPV-positive throughout the country. Although Peru's Ministry of Health monitors follow-up indicators,8 most regions, including Loreto, continue to prioritize numbers screened. Same-day SAT reduced loss to follow-up, but still has limitations. VAT is subjective and depends on clinician experience. Women with lesions extending into the endocervical canal or whose endocervical canal is not fully visible, lesions involving more than 75% of the transformation zone, or suspected invasive disease could not be treated on the ships and depended on referral pathways that often failed. In addition, some overtreatment is inevitable because not all HPV-positive women with acetowhite lesions would develop clinically important disease. And, in settings where return visits are unlikely, the benefits of reducing loss to follow-up may outweigh these concerns.

There are a range of diverse tools and approaches for cervical cancer prevention and control. One approach does not work everywhere, nor does it need to. National policies and programs must allow local input and adaptation as needed by those who understand the challenges in their own regions. Providing guidelines and supplies may be insufficient when local authorities and implementors have limited time and bandwidth for planning, little experience designing new approaches or using innovative methods, and face constant turnover in both leadership and staff; in Peru, between 2016 and 2026, there have been 8 Presidents and over 15 Ministers of Health. Our findings suggest that successful implementation in remote settings requires not only appropriate technologies, but also support for local planning, adaptation, and problem solving.

This study does not seek to compare two HPV testing platforms; rather, it highlights the value of point-of-care testing and treatment in remote settings. The Proyecto Precancer intervention emerged from a locally recognized opportunity. The portable GeneXpert enabled same-day results and treatment, whereas the Roche cobas platform selected for national scale-up was designed for high-volume laboratory settings and precluded same-day care. Our findings suggest that HPV procurement and implementation decisions should consider the needs of different populations and geographies rather than prioritizing volume and cost alone. Strong coordination between mobile services, fixed laboratories, and referral facilities is essential to ensure timely results and completion of care. In this case, the discontinuation of the mobile SAT model reflected a procurement and implementation decision optimized for high-volume settings at the expense of equity.

This study has several limitations. We could not compare the intervention with prior and subsequent PIAS campaigns because PIAS and Regional Ministry of Health data were incomplete, and follow-up information was completely unavailable, a reflection of the challenging situation in cervical cancer screening and management systems in resource-limited settings.30,31 We also could not estimate screening coverage because the number of eligible women was unknown; we could only document the increase in the number of women screened. Additionally, the Proyecto Precancer team was not permitted to travel on the naval ships and therefore could not directly observe implementation or interview participating women. Interviews were conducted only with the small number of ship personnel after the campaigns, which may have resulted in underreporting of operational challenges. Finally, we were unable to verify long-term outcomes of women after referral or treatment.

Conclusion

The Proyecto Precancer intervention demonstrated that an HPV-based SAT approach delivered through mobile PIAS ships is feasible and can reduce long-standing inequities in cervical cancer prevention for women in remote Amazonian communities. By combining self-sampling with same-day results and treatment, the intervention increased screening and reduced loss to follow-up. These findings underscore the importance of adapting cervical cancer prevention strategies to local health-system capacities and geographic realities rather than relying on uniform national approaches. Scaling up such context-responsive models, while strengthening monitoring across the continuum of care, could substantially reduce cervical cancer disparities in other hard-to-reach populations.
