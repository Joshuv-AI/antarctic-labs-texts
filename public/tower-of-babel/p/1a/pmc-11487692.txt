Is palatal cavity volume affected by maxillary sinus pathologies? A CBCT study
Authors: Mediha Erturk, Melek Tassoker, Anıl Didem Aydın Kabakcı
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThis study uses cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) to determine whether pathology in the maxillary sinus (MS) affects the volume of the palatal cavity.Methods188 individuals, 95 women and 93 men, aged between 17 and 63, were included in the study. MS pathology in the patients and the open-closed status of the maxillary sinus ostium (MSO) were recorded. Palatal volume measurements were performed using open-access ITK-SNAP via CBCT images. Statistical analysis of the study was conducted using SPSS v.21 software (IBM. Chicago. IL. USA), and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe average palatal volume was 1375.29±313.38 mm3 in male patients and 1235.33±250.40 mm3 in females, and it was found to be statistically significant between genders (p=0.001). MS pathology was detected in 114 (60.6%) of the patients. It was determined that the most frequently observed pathology in both the right (n = 58, 30.9%) and left (n = 65, 34.6%) side MS of individuals was mucosal hypertrophy. It was determined that the mean palatal volume was higher when the MSO was closed (p = 0.000). As a result of the correlation analysis, it was shown that the presence of MS pathology had a positive effect in explaining palate volume by 38.6% (R2 = 0.386).ConclusionPalatal cavity volume was affected by maxillary sinus pathologies. Palatal cavity volume increases in the presence of MS pathologies and when MSOs are closed.Keywords: Maxillary sinus, Palatal cavity volume, Pathology, CBCT, ITK-Snap, Volumetric analysis

Introduction

The facial skeleton comprises orbital cavities, nasal and pneumatic cavities, oral cavity, jaws, and temporomandibular joint (TMJ) [1]. These structures support the face functionally and aesthetically. One of the essential elements of the maxillofacial structures that contribute to the facial skeleton is the hard palate (palatum durum). The hard palate, which serves critical functions such as chewing and speaking, forms the roof of the oral cavity and the floor of the nasal cavity [2]. The hard palate consists of the maxillary bone's palatal process and the palatine bone's horizontal lamina [3].

The height of the palate, also called the palatal dome in the oral cavity, is affected by various parameters. Especially in mouth-breathing individuals, the morphological characteristics of the palatal vault also change, and a more extended palatal height is observed [4]. Palate size is affected by many factors other than mouth breathing, such as tongue size, tooth inclination, occlusion, and parafunctional habits [5–8].

The central location of the hard palate and its close relationship with the adjacent nasal cavity, oral cavity, and maxillary sinus (MS) increase the likelihood that any morphological change in these structures will affect other components of the stomatognathic system [9]. Miranda-Viana et al. [10] reported in their study that there is a relationship between the width and height of the hard palate and the volume of the maxillary sinuses, and the greater the width and height of the hard palate, the greater the volume of the maxillary sinuses.

Pyramid-shaped MSs are the first paranasal sinuses to develop and have a mean volume of 6-8 cm3 at birth [11]. MS and palatum durum show two development phases between 0-3 and 7-12 years. The mean volume of MS, which reaches adult size between the ages of 14 and 18, is approximately 15 cm3 [12]. While the intrinsic diseases of MS include mucositis, sinusitis, mucus retention cyst, polyp, mucocele, and antrolith, the extrinsic diseases include periostitis, benign odontogenic cysts, tumors, and fibrous dysplasia [13].

In the literature, morphometric changes related to the palatal cavity are primarily compared before and after orthodontic treatment [14–16]. In addition, there are studies evaluating the relationship between malocclusions [17], tongue position [18], mouth breathing [6], bruxism [8], and palatal cavity morphometry. Palatal volume has been reported to increase with rapid maxillary expansion (RME) [14]. It has been determined that tongue volume has a significant positive correlation with palatal vault height [18]. Lione et al. [6] have stated that individuals with prolonged mouth breathing have different palatal morphology compared to those with normal breathing and that palatal volume is reduced in these individuals. Another study revealed that bruxists had a statistically significant more extended palate in the sagittal plane than controls [8]. However, to our knowledge, no study has investigated the effect of maxillary sinus pathologies on palatal cavity volume. While the literature offers insights into the morphometric changes of the palatal cavity related to orthodontic treatment [14–16] and other oral conditions [6, 8, 17, 18], the potential impact of MS pathologies on palatal structure remains unknown. Addressing this knowledge gap contributes to early diagnosis and intervention of potential orofacial complications. It may also enable the development of improved treatment plans for patients with MS pathologies and palatal abnormalities. Furthermore, it is crucial to understand the potential impact of pathological conditions in structures adjacent to the hard palate on craniofacial growth and development. Plaster models and 3D imaging techniques can be used when measuring the volume of the palatal cavity, which forms the superior border of the oral cavity [15, 19]. This study aims to determine whether the palatal volume is affected by the presence of specific maxillary sinus (MS) pathologies—such as mucosal hypertrophy, mucosal polyposal hypertrophy, mucus retention cysts, mucoceles, and air-fluid leveling—using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT). The study hypothesizes that these MS pathologies may contribute to changes in palatal volume, potentially leading to an increase in volume due to the impact of the pathologies on surrounding structures. This research seeks to clarify the relationship between MS pathologies and palatal volume alterations.

Methods

Sample and study design

The necessary approval for the study was received from Necmettin Erbakan University Dentistry Non-Drug and Medical Device Research Ethics Committee (approval no: 2023/350, meeting date: 28.12.2023). All procedures followed were by the ethical standards of the committee responsible for human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the 1975 Helsinki Declaration as revised in 2008. All data was anonymized, and a waiver of informed consent was obtained from the ethics committee. In the study, cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) records of 188 individuals, 95 women and 93 men, were retrospectively examined. The age range of the participants is 17-63 years. A blinded operator measured All palatal volumes twice in 60 patients (30 controls and 30 patients with maxillary sinus pathology). Intraclass correlation coefficients were highly reliable (palatal cavity volume ICC=0.992, 95% CI:0.979-0.998, p<0.001).

Data in which the right/left (MS) and right/left maxillary sinus ostium (MSO) regions were visible were included in the study. Patients with mucosal hypertrophy, mucosal polyposal hypertrophy, mucus retention cyst, mucocele, and air-fluid leveling were included in the study. Other infectious and inflammatory conditions and tumors were excluded from the study. We excluded patients who had undergone orthodontic treatment, particularly involving rapid maxillary expansion, orthodontic tooth movements, or orthognathic surgery. Cleft lip and palate patients, those who had upper jaw and/or maxillary sinus (MS) surgery, and patients with a history of head or neck trauma were also excluded. Furthermore, records with artifacts, including metal, motion, or noise artifacts, were not considered.

Image acquisition

CBCT images were acquired by NewTom GiANO HR devices (Verona, Italy, 90 kV and 10 mA, 18 seconds rotation time, voxel 0.15 mm, 160mm x180 mm, and 100mm x 130mm fields of view). CBCT data were saved in dicom file format.

Radiological examination

All evaluations were performed by the same oral radiologist (ME) with two years of oral radiology experience. MS ostia were evaluated using coronal CBCT sections, and the presence of pathologies was assessed by examining each of the three planes (axial, coronal, and sagittal) separately. The axial plane was standardized by aligning it with the Frankfort horizontal plane, and the coronal and sagittal planes were adjusted in relation to the midline structures of the skull. This standardization minimized potential variations and ensured uniformity in assessing pathologies and anatomical features. To evaluate MSO, images in the coronal section are examined; the natural opening of the MS was observed at the superior of the medial wall and inferior to the orbital floor (Fig. 1) [20]. Right/left MSOs were evaluated separately for each patient and recorded as open or closed.

Maxillary sinus pathologies were classified as present/absent. The pathological conditions observed are mucosal hypertrophy, mucosal polyposal hypertrophy, mucus retention cyst, mucocele, and air-fluid leveling in the sinus (Fig. 2). Their diagnostic criteria are given below:

Volumetric assessment

Palatal volume measurements were performed with open access ITK-SNAP (version 4.0.1) software (Cognitica, Philadelphia, PA, USA) (https://www.itksnap.org). Three points were determined to achieve standardization in palatal volume measurement [21]. These are the most anterior midline points of the maxillary alveolar process and the inferior points at the right and leftmost distal parts of the maxillary alveolar crest, respectively (Fig. 3). The inferior border of the measurement is determined by the anterior midline of the maxillary alveolar process, and the superior border is determined by the posterior nasal spine (PNS) point, the most posterior point of the palatal bone.

A, the boundaries determined in the axial section: 1, the most anterior midline point of the maxillary alveolar process; 2, the most distal and inferior point of the right maxillary arch; 3, the most distal and inferior point of the left maxillary arch, B the inferior point of the palatal volume (the most anterior point of the maxillary alveolar process on midline sagittal view), C PNS (superior border point of palatal volume)

After the ROI borders were determined, the palatal region was segmented, and the region's volume was converted to mm3, which was recorded for each patient (Fig. 4).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the study was performed using SPSS v.21 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The suitability of the parameters to normal distribution was evaluated with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Skewness and Kurtosis values. The data obtained were evaluated descriptively (mean value, standard deviation, maximum and minimum values, percentages) and quantitatively. The differences between the volumetric measurements of palatal cavities of male and female patients were compared using an independent sample t-test. Correlation analysis was used to show how adequate MS pathology was in explaining palate volume, and the chi-square test was used to analyze the relationship between categorical variables. Multiple regression analysis was used to determine how much the palatal cavity volume is affected by one or more pathologies in the maxillary sinus. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study's power for the independent sample t-test was calculated based on the sample size of two groups and an effect size equal to 0.5 (11). The power(1-β) was 0.90 at an alpha level of 0.05 (G*Power 3.1, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany). Retrospectively, CBCT images of 95 female and 93 male individuals recorded between 2020 and 2023 were analyzed. The ages of the study participants ranged between 17 and 63 (mean 29.35) years.

Volumetric measurements analysis results

Our study determined the palatal cavity volume as an average of 1449.96±264.93 mm3 in patients with maxillary sinus pathology and 1080.59±156.88 mm3 without maxillary sinus pathology. However, the average palatal cavity volume was 1497.09±284.16 mm3 in male patients with maxillary sinus pathology and 1389.62±226.78 mm3 in female patients. The average palatal cavity volume was 1106.48±179.61 mm3 in male patients without maxillary sinus pathology and 1063.91±139.92 mm3 in female patients.

Regardless of whether patients have maxillary sinus pathology, the mean palatal volume was found to be 1375.29±313.38 mm3 in male patients (min:892 mm3-max:2217 mm3) and 1235.33±250.40 mm3 in females (min:824 mm3-max:1920 mm3). This difference was found to be statistically significant between genders (p=0.001).

The changes in the palatal cavity volume were examined according to the MSOs' open and closed status. The mean palatal volume was higher when the ostium was closed (p=0.000) (Table 4).

Correlation analysis results

In our study, correlation analysis was performed to evaluate how much maxillary sinus pathologies affect the palatal cavity volume. The correlation analysis showed that MS pathology effectively explained palate volume by 38.6% (R2 = 0.386) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

The hard palate, an essential aid to speech, is an understudied area in anatomy studies [22]. Due to the close relationship of the hard palate with the maxillary sinus and nasal cavity, these structures may affect changes in its morphology. The hard palate adjusts shape and position in response to airway obstruction [10]. This adaptive response highlights the functional plasticity of craniofacial anatomy in the context of airway dynamics, which may play a critical role in managing obstructive respiratory conditions. Maxillary sinus pathologies are common and were detected in 60.6% of the individuals included in the study. This study investigated the effect of maxillary sinus pathologies and ostium closure on palatal cavity volume. The functional matrix theory suggests that breathing significantly influences craniofacial growth. Nasal breathing can encourage the lateral expansion of the maxilla, which flattens the palate and impacts the breathing process [6]. It has been shown that mouth breathers have a more profound and narrower hard palate than nasal breathers [23]. It can be inferred that chronic respiratory obstruction may affect current health and have long-term morphological consequences, potentially altering the course of craniofacial development. The clinical implications are profound, as interventions to restore nasal breathing could prevent or mitigate such structural changes, reducing the risk of developing related complications.

This study showed that the mean palatal volume was higher when the MSO was closed and that the presence of the MS path positively explained inning palate volume by 38.6%. Osteomeatal obstruction can impair maxillary sinus ventilation and increase the risk of pathology. As a result, mouth breathing may be the primary means of air intake. This highlights a critical need for early identification and management of osteomeatal complex dysfunction, which could prevent secondary changes in palatal morphology and potentially reduce the risk of associated respiratory issues.

A recent study [24] showed that palatal arch depth and width were higher in individuals with nasal septal deviation. The absence of linear measurements in the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes limits our ability to determine which specific dimension primarily contributes to changes in palatal volume. In future studies, performing linear measurements in these planes would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the dimensional changes contributing to the overall volume. Shetty et al. [25] demonstrated that the presence of nasal septal deviation (NSD) and concha bullosa (CB) have a significant effect on the palatal dimensions. These results were attributed to the fact that nasal airway passage blockage caused by CB and NSD may affect the growth and downward arching of the palate.

The present study observed that men's palatal cavity volumes were more significant than women's. Previous studies on palatal cavity height and width have reported that men show higher values [10, 26, 27]. Tsai et al. [28] found that the height of the palatal vault increases with age. In addition to age and gender, various parameters that may affect the palatal dimension have been investigated in the literature. Dolichocephalic patients tend to exhibit vertical craniofacial growth, resulting in a narrower and deeper hard palate.

In contrast, brachycephalic patients typically show horizontal craniofacial growth, with a broader and more regular hard palate [29]. These craniofacial patterns suggest that head shape may significantly determine palatal morphology. Additionally, unilateral posterior cross-bite was associated with low palatal cavity volume [30]. A recent study found that impacted canines were significantly associated with volume reduction on the affected side [31]. In another study, unilateral and bilateral cleft lip groups were reported to be smaller than controls [32]. Based on this, these patients were excluded as it could lead to bias in our study group.

Restrepo et al. [33] compared the palatal dimensions of bruxist and non-bruxist children and reported that bruxists had larger values. They stated that this may be due to bruxists' higher muscular activity. Bourdiol et al. [18] reported a strong correlation between tongue volume, maxillary inter-molar width, and palatal perimeter. These findings emphasize the importance of functional factors in craniofacial development.

Another study [22] found that the hard palate in children with Down syndrome was high-arched and narrow. In Turner syndrome [34], palatal height was higher than in the average control population. Syndromic variations in palatal morphology underscore the importance of personalized approaches in clinical practice. Individuals with genetic conditions may require tailored interventions to address their unique anatomical and functional needs. Moreover, further research into the genetic factors influencing palatal development could enhance our understanding of these conditions and improve diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a disease characterized by attacks of upper airway obstruction during sleep, resulting in recurrent hypoxia. In the study using plaster models of the maxillary arch, the palatal volume was measured as 6755.63 mm3 in the OSA group and 7254.67 mm3 in the control group. Palatal area measurements were significantly smaller in OSA [35].

Limitations

The most significant limitation of our study is its retrospective design, which inherently restricts the ability to establish causality. This methodology precludes rigorous control over confounding variables and potential biases. While considering the impact of skeletal class and growth pattern on palatal morphology, we must acknowledge that other unmeasured confounders may have influenced our findings. Nasal breathing patterns, genetic predisposition, and functional factors such as tongue posture, mouth breathing, and bruxism are potential confounders that could affect palatal dimensions. Additionally, skeletal facial type, dental malocclusions, and tooth inclination may play a role. The absence of data on these variables limits the ability to fully assess their impact on the observed relationship between maxillary sinus pathologies and palatal morphology. Future prospective studies with standardized inclusion criteria, including detailed assessments of skeletal class, growth patterns, and potential confounders, are warranted to address these limitations.

Suggestions for future research

This study showed that the presence of MS pathology positively affected palate volume by 38.6%. By addressing the following questions, researchers can better understand the underlying mechanisms and clinical implications of the observed relationship between maxillary sinus pathologies and palatal changes.

Future studies should explore the factors contributing to the remaining 61.4% palate volume variation. Specifically, the role of genetic, environmental, and developmental factors (such as skeletal class and facial growth patterns) in interaction with MS pathology should be investigated.

Roadmap for future research

Longitudinal studies may be initiated to monitor palatal changes over time in patients with different severities of various sinus pathologies. Based on the findings, clinical guidelines can be developed for the early detection and management of palatal changes in patients with maxillary sinus pathologies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a positive association between increased palatal cavity volume and MS pathologies such as mucosal thickening, mucosal polypoid hypertrophy, mucus retention cyst, and air-fluid levels within the maxillary sinuses. These findings reinforce the study hypothesis, suggesting that MS pathologies may contribute to palatal morphological changes. Clinicians, particularly dentists and oral surgeons, should remain alert to potential skeletal changes in the palatal region. This is crucial when treating patients with MS pathologies or airway obstructions. Early identification of these changes could prompt timely referrals to specialists, such as otolaryngologists or orthodontists, for comprehensive evaluation and management. This proactive approach may help prevent or mitigate complications associated with altered palatal morphology. Future research should explore the interaction between MS pathologies and other contributing factors to uncover the mechanisms driving palatal changes. This knowledge could lead to the development of more effective preventive measures and therapeutic interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
