Whole Body Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic Model to Explain A Patient With Drug–Drug Interaction Between Voriconazole and Flucloxacillin
Authors: Heshu Abdullah-Koolmees, Julia F van den Nieuwendijk, Simone M K ten Hoope, David C de Leeuw, Linda G W Franken, Medhat M Said, Maarten R Seefat, Eleonora L Swart, N Harry Hendrikse, Imke H Bartelink
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesVoriconazole administered concomitantly with flucloxacillin may result in subtherapeutic plasma concentrations as shown in a patient with Staphylococcus aureus sepsis and a probable pulmonary aspergillosis. After switching our patient to posaconazole, therapeutic concentrations were reached. The aim of this study was to first test our hypothesis that flucloxacillin competes with voriconazole not posaconazole for binding to albumin ex vivo, leading to lower total concentrations in plasma.MethodsA physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) model was then applied to predict the mechanism of action of the drug–drug interaction (DDI). The model included non-linear hepatic metabolism and the effect of a severe infectious disease on cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymes activity.ResultsThe unbound voriconazole concentration remained unchanged in plasma after adding flucloxacillin, thereby rejecting our hypothesis of albumin-binding site competition. The PBPK model was able to adequately predict the plasma concentration of both voriconazole and posaconazole over time in healthy volunteers. Upregulation of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19 through the pregnane X receptor (PXR) gene by flucloxacillin resulted in decreased voriconazole plasma concentrations, reflecting the DDI observations in our patient. Posaconazole metabolism was not affected, or was only limitedly affected, by the changes through the PXR gene, which agrees with the observed plasma concentrations within the target range in our patient.ConclusionsEx vivo experiments reported that the unbound voriconazole plasma concentration remained unchanged after adding flucloxacillin. The PBPK model describes the potential mechanism driving the drug–drug and drug–disease interaction of voriconazole and flucloxacillin, highlighting the large substantial influence of flucloxacillin on the PXR gene and the influence of infection on voriconazole plasma concentrations, and suggests a more limited effect on other triazoles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13318-024-00916-1.

Key Points

Flucloxacillin lowers voriconazole plasma concentrations with an almost immediate onset and cessation when concomitant administration is started and ended, but the cause and clinical effect of this potential drug interaction is currently unknown.Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling can be used to explore the mechanism and quantify the extent of the drug–drug interaction.This study evaluated the utility of PBPK modeling to explore and quantify plasma and tissue concentrations as a result of changes in voriconazole metabolism by infections and use of flucloxacillin.

Introduction

Antifungals and antibiotics are frequently used simultaneously in hospitalized patients [1]. Voriconazole, a triazole, is an antifungal drug used for the treatment of invasive aspergillosis, esophageal candidiasis, and candidemia [2]. Flucloxacillin is a beta-lactam antibiotic used to treat many Gram-positive bacteria such as beta-lactamase producing staphylococci and streptococci, including skin and soft tissue infections and respiratory tract infections [3]. Treatment of voriconazole is routinely monitored through therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM). The case presented here (Sect. 1.1) and other recent TDM based-case reports, suggest that a drug-drug interaction (DDI) between voriconazole and flucloxacillin exists [4–6].

Case

A 48-year-old woman with relapsed acute myeloid leukemia was admitted to the hospital with fever and a cellulitis of the arm caused by Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus). She was started on flucloxacillin therapy intravenously at a dose of 6 g daily during hospitalization. Due to persistent fever, a CT chest scan was performed which was suspect for pulmonary aspergillosis. Three days later, the patient was diagnosed with pulmonary aspergillosis and was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). The standard oral dosing regimen of voriconazole (400 mg twice daily [BID] in 24 h loading dose and continued with 200 mg BID) was initiated along with flucloxacillin therapy. As part of standard of care, a voriconazole plasma trough concentration (Ctrough) sample was taken after 2 days of treatment. The voriconazole plasma concentration was undetectable (< 0.1 mg/L). The hospital pharmacist and the treating physicians decided to replace voriconazole therapy with posaconazole at a dose of 300 mg twice a day. Flucloxacillin therapy continued and remained unchanged. Posaconazole plasma Ctrough obtained 2 days after the start of posaconazole treatment was 2.35 mg/L (target concentration: > 1.5 mg/L). Both flucloxacillin and posaconazole therapy were continued. Posaconazole plasma Ctrough levels were reassessed after 7 days. The observed plasma Ctrough was 5.21 mg/L. Two weeks later, the patient was readmitted to the hospital due to a re-infection of the S. aureus bacteremia. A therapeutic plasma Ctrough level of posaconazole was measured at a concentration of 3.35 mg/L. An overview of the patient dosing and observed concentrations are shown in Fig. 1. The patient provided written informed consent prior to inclusion in this retrospective analysis.

Drug–drug interaction observed in a patient, showing the dosing and observed concentration–time curves during concomitant administration of flucloxacillin, voriconazole, or posaconazole

Case reports show that flucloxacillin lowers voriconazole plasma concentrations with an immediate onset when concomitant administration is started and cessation when dosing has ended [4–6]. Flucloxacillin is a relatively large, polar drug which binds non-covalently to albumin in the blood pool [7, 8]. The fraction of protein-bound flucloxacillin is significantly associated with albumin concentrations in plasma [9]. In contrast, voriconazole is more lipophilic, shows a large volume of tissue distribution and the binding rate to albumin is variable (49-70%), and is influenced by plasma albumin concentrations [10–14]. Previous studies show that a lower albumin concentration results in lower voriconazole total plasma concentrations [10, 11]. Therefore, DDI may be explained by competition for the albumin binding pocket by flucloxacillin and voriconazole in plasma. This may result in a higher unbound voriconazole concentrations available for hepatic excretion, metabolism, and subsequently in a higher elimination rate [15, 16]. Furthermore, higher unbound voriconazole concentrations may lead to increased tissue uptake, where flucloxacillin has no access to compete for albumin binding. This combination of factors may lead to subsequent subtherapeutic voriconazole plasma concentrations.

The DDI may also be explained by the fact that enzyme CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19 upregulation and polymorphism has a role in (the variability in) the occurrence of the observed DDI. Flucloxacillin induces the pregnane X receptor (PXR) gene [1–3, 15, 17]. PXR is a nuclear receptor in the gut and the liver and is an important regulator for drug metabolism and efflux. Flucloxacillin binds to PXR and reaches the nucleus, where it co-activates a complex of PXR and retinoid X receptor (RXR). This complex binds to the xenobiotic-responsive enhancer molecule/PXR responsive element (XREM/PXRE) enhancer, which causes upregulation of the CYP3A4 enzymes within 2–14 days [15, 18]. This upregulation of CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP2C19 could lead to an increased metabolization of voriconazole and might explain the subtherapeutic effects.

Inflammation caused by infections, such as S. aureus bacteriemia, may also affect voriconazole pharmacokinetics [19, 20]. Cytokine modulation has been reported to significantly downregulate the gene transcription of various CYP iso-enzymes. Previous studies have demonstrated that voriconazole metabolism was decreased during inflammation, resulting in higher trough concentrations [21]. The acute phase protein CRP, reflective of inflammation, was found to correlate with voriconazole trough concentrations and metabolic ratio (P < 0.001). Flucloxacillin may reverse this effect, and therefore a potential disease–drug interaction between an infection and voriconazole pharmacokinetics should be considered to ensure safe and optimum treatment in patients with an infection and when assessing the effect of the DDI.

Recognizing the extent of the DDI, current guidelines recommend avoiding the combination treatment of flucloxacillin and azoles [5]. Posaconazole is highly protein-bound in plasma (> 98% posaconazole vs. 49–70% for voriconazole), and shows less pharmacokinetic variability, with linear pharmacokinetics, i.e. posaconazole is not significantly metabolized by the cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzyme system. Instead, it is only partially metabolized through glucuronidation by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) enzymes, particularly UGT1A4, which can also be reduced by inflammation [22, 23]. If the described DDI is related to protein binding or CYP upregulation, this may only be applicable for voriconazole, not to the total azole group. Therefore, first we determined ex vivo if there was competition between voriconazole and flucloxacillin for albumin by adding flucloxacillin to voriconazole containing blood samples, and measuring the voriconazole free fraction before and after adding flucloxacillin. Secondly, we applied physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling to explore the mechanism and extent of the drug–drug interaction (DDI) between the azoles and flucloxacillin. We hypothesize that flucloxacillin competes with voriconazole for binding to albumin and/or causes CYP3A4 upregulation through the PXR gene, leading to an increased clearance of voriconazole and thus low or even undetectable voriconazole blood concentration.

Methods

Ex Vivo Experiments

To assess the effect of albumin competition we performed two ex vivo experiments. For the first experiment, we collected residual blood samples that were taken for routine therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of voriconazole that had a total voriconazole concentration of > 2 mg/L. Three different solutions were added to these samples, i.e., a low flucloxacillin concentration of 50mg/L corresponding to the average Ctrough, a high flucloxacillin concentration of 300mg/L corresponding to the upper limit of Cmax , or a control solution of 0.9% sodium chloride [24]. For the second experiment, control plasma samples were used to which first flucloxacillin was added in the same concentrations of 0, 50, and 300 mg/L, followed by the addition of voriconazole in a concentration of 3 mg/L. All the samples were incubated for 30 min at 37°C, followed by a filtering process to be able to measure the free concentration of voriconazole in each of the three solutions. Total and unbound voriconazole concentrations were measured using LC/MS-MS and the unbound fraction was calculated. This LC/MS-MS method is a validated method at room temperature and extra validation was performed to ensure adequate recovery (> 95%) at this higher temperature of 37°C.

In Silico Experiments

A PBPK model describing whole body drug distribution in the typical patient was built based on previous publications, as presented in Fig. 2 [25–27]. Posaconazole is metabolized via glucuronidation by UGT1A4 (17%) and excreted via renal (13%) and hepatic excretion (77%), most of posaconazole is excreted unchanged (83%). In contrast to voriconazole, posaconazole is a substrate of P-glycoprotein (P-GP). Linear clearance (CLliver [27]) was used to predict posaconazole elimination. Voriconazole is metabolized via CYP2C19 (85%), CYP2C9 (14.5%), and CYP3A4 (0.5%), and experiences non-linear pharmacokinetics [25, 28]. The Michaelis–Menten constant (Km), maximum velocity (Vmax) of CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A4, and the respective enzyme concentrations were utilized to explain the non-linear liver metabolism of voriconazole [25, 28]. The tissue to blood partitioning (Kp) was predicted using a well-established PBPK-model for weak basic lipophilic drugs for all tissues [29] (Fig. 3). Reversible protein binding was introduced [7], using albumin concentrations in plasma, the literature-derived tissue to blood ratio, and drug specific values of affinity (Kd), to demonstrate the nonlinearity in voriconazole–albumin binding. Software and Model Evaluation

The simulations of the PBPK models were performed with R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using package DeSolve. As the model was based on mechanistic description of DDI and focused on quantification of variability in pharmacokinetics using specific patient characteristics such as inflammation and concomitant drug use, only single-patient simulation was performed per variable of interest. Data from literature and plasma samples from the case report were used to evaluate the PBPK models. Voriconazole plasma data were derived from Purkins et al (2002), who reported voriconazole plasma concentrations in healthy volunteers using a multiple dosing regimen [42].

Plasma steady-state voriconazole Ctrough in patients with varying stages of inflammation were derived from van Wanrooy et al. (2014). Lung and liver tissue concentrations were derived from Weiler et al. (2011), who performed tissue concentration measurements on eight deceased patients [21, 43]. The steady-state plasma pharmacokinetic profile in a typical ICU severely infected patients (patient simulation 6) at IV 300 mg BID dosing was compared to the post-mortem-observed liver and lung concentrations [43]. Posaconazole simulations were compared to plasma concentrations in healthy volunteers, as reported in the posaconazole review by Chen et al. (2020) [30]. Lung and liver tissue simulations of posaconazole were compared to tissue concentrations reported by Blennow et al. (2014) who performed data/sample collection on deceased patients, after the last dose was given at the ICU [44]. The steady-state plasma pharmacokinetic profile in a typical ICU severely infected patients (patient simulation 4) at PO 400 mg QD dosing was compared to the post-mortem-observed liver and lung concentrations (patients 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7) [44].

Model evaluation was conducted using the mean prediction error (PE) of the predicted versus observed concentrations. The model was considered accurate when the predicted plasma and tissue concentrations fell within 2-fold of the observed plasma and tissue concentrations derived from the presented patient and/or literature.

Results

For the ex vivo experiment, a total of 5 voriconazole samples from routine care were used with a mean total voriconazole concentration of 2.9 mg/L (2.2–4.1 mg/L) and mean free concentration of 1.2 mg/L (0.9–1.8 mg/L). The mean free fraction was 40% (34–47%) in the control samples, 44% (34–51%) in the low-flucloxacillin concentration samples, and 45% (36–53%) in the high-concentration samples. This shows that the increase in free fraction was only 3 and 5% in low- and high-flucloxacillin concentrations compared to the control samples. The second experiment showed similar results, with a mean free fraction of 47% (46–51%) in control samples, 48% (46–51%) in low-flucloxacillin concentration samples, and also 48% (47–50%) in high-flucloxacillin concentration samples. Therefore, we could not confirm our hypothesis of albumin competition with these ex vivo experiments.

The PBPK model predicted plasma, lung, and tissue concentration–time profiles of voriconazole and posaconazole in healthy volunteers and ICU patients are shown in Fig. 3). Six different simulations were performed, with each simulation representing a different situation: healthy, bacterial infected patients, severely diseased patient admitted to ICU and these situations after onset of flucloxacillin DDI (Table 4).

Discussion

In contrast to our hypothesis, our study showed no competition for albumin binding, but that cytochrome P450 upregulation and an effect of flucloxacillin on inflammation, was the cause of the drug–drug interaction between voriconazole and flucloxacillin. Flucloxacillin can be displaced by other albumin-bound drugs, such as imatinib [7, 8, 45], but this was not observed (ex vivo) for voriconazole. Voriconazole is metabolized mainly by CYP2C19, CYP3A4, and CYP2C9, which are saturable and may convert elimination from linear to non-linear [10–12]. Previous studies suggested that enzyme CYP3A4 upregulation and polymorphism may explain the observed DDI. Flucloxacillin induces the PXR [46], which is present in the gut and the liver and has an essential role in regulation of drug metabolism and efflux. When flucloxacillin binds to PXR and reaches the nucleus, it co-activates a complex of PXR and RXR. This complex binds to the XREM/PXRE enhancer, which causes upregulation of the CYP enzymes [47]. The interaction is specific for flucloxacillin and not observed in other beta-lactam antibiotics [36]. This upregulation of CYP enzymes could lead to rapid metabolization of voriconazole and might explain the subtherapeutic effects which are frequently seen within 2–7 days after starting flucloxacillin in patients with voriconazole. However, upregulation of the CYP enzyme usually takes between 2 days and 2 weeks [46]. Furthermore, multiple other drugs which are concomitantly used and metabolized by CYP enzymes remain unchanged during flucloxacillin treatment [6]. We used a PBPK model to demonstrate that flucloxacillin causes subtherapeutic plasma concentrations by the induction of CYP enzymes, where previously they were saturated due to the nonlinear pharmacokinetics of voriconazole. The reversal of CYP enzyme activity is further enhanced in the case of a treatment effect of flucloxacillin on the infectious disease by restoring drug metabolism. During infection and inflammation, synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) may decrease the metabolic rate by downregulation of the gene transcription of various CYP iso-enzymes [11], particularly CYP3A4, CYP2C9, and CYP1A2, which subsequently reduces drug metabolism [48, 49]. IL-6 and CRP levels exhibit high co-linearity in the context of acute inflammatory diseases. As the effect of CRP on voriconazole metabolism has been previously quantified [4–6], we included the latter correlation to show the effect of inflammation on voriconazole. As more than half of ICU patients have an infection during ICU admission [9, 34, 50], these opposing effects of induction due to the DDI and inhibition of metabolism by inflammatory cytokines may be relevant for all extensively metabolized drugs in ICU patients.

The influence of infection and inflammation, common situations on the ICU, on voriconazole clearance was taken into account by lowering the Vmax of CYP2C19. As reported, tissue validations were based on tissue samples taken post-mortem. Although all predictions fell within the 2-fold prediction error, some values fell outside the 95% CI. It is known that post-mortem drugs redistribute as a result of breakdown of cells [51], which might explain our voriconazole under-prediction in lung and liver tissue. The results demonstrate the use of PBPK models focusing on (target) tissue concentrations, but further validation is needed.

The model of this study shows differences in plasma and tissue concentrations as a result of CYP induction through PXR gene upregulation. Our aim was to pose a new explanation for the observed DDI and to support the explanation by the use of a PBPK model, which was able to support our hypothesis, although, the lack of ex vivo data to support the effect of PXR gene upregulation makes the current model less accurate. This research describes a typical patient with DDI. No pharmacokinetic variability or variance of other covariates could be included due to the research design; therefore, a one-time simulation approach was conducted. A dedicated DDI study of voriconazole and flucloxacillin is needed where unbound voriconazol and voriconazol metabolite concentrations are measured to further unravel this DDI.

To avoid the DDI between flucloxacillin and azoles, the combination is advised to be avoided, as stated in current guidelines [5]. Although posaconazole and voriconazole both are azoles, they differ in multiple aspects. As stated earlier, posaconazole is bound in plasma for > 98% versus 49–70% for voriconazole. Furthermore, posaconazole shows linear pharmacokinetics, i.e., it is metabolized to a lesser extent through the CYP enzymes and is not influenced by the amount of albumin and CRP levels [22, 23]. Although our patient had therapeutic posaconazole levels during flucloxacillin use, there have been case reports on the concomitant use of posaconazole and flucloxacillin showing a decrease in posaconazole levels, as has been reported for voriconazole [52, 53]. This shows that flucloxacillin may also lead to subtherapeutic posaconazole levels.

Future research to optimize the PBPK model should also focus on experiments to describe the upregulation of CYP enzymes through PXR genes. Another limitation was the 2.7-fold under-prediction of plasma steady-state concentrations after oral dosing. Plasma pharmacokinetics may be over-predicted due to high variability in the absorption rate and the bioavailability of posaconazole, ranging from 8 to 50% [30]. In our model, we tested the worst-case scenario of only 8% availability. Furthermore, the effect of inflammation on other proteins such as AAG was not assessed. Adding variability in PK parameters during different disease stages into the model, and performing additional tissue pharmacokinetic experiments, would help to further understand and quantify interpatient differences in the extent of the observed DDI.

Conclusion

The voriconazole unbound fraction remained unchanged after adding flucloxacillin ex vivo. PBPK modeling results suggest that voriconazole-enhanced clearance by flucloxacillin upregulation of CYP enzymes through the PXR gene is the mechanism behind the observed interaction, after accounting for the effect of flucloxacillin on inflammation. This is the first report that uses PBPK modeling to elucidate the DDI mechanism of voriconazole and flucloxacillin, and substantiates why it is safe to switch to posaconazole.
