The gut-brain-gonad axis mediates salinity adaptation in an invasive turtle: causal evidence from microbiota transplantation and metabolite supplementation
Authors: Qiongyu Zhang, Ijaz Khan, Enqi Lei, Hanru Chen, Xue Tang, Li Ding, Meiling Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreliminary studies have shown that salinity stress can impair the differentiation of spermatogonial stem cells (SSC) in red-eared slider (Trachemys scripta elegans) through the gut-brain-gonad axis, thereby affecting their reproductive ability. However, a direct causal link between salinity-induced gut microbiota alterations and reproductive suppression remains unclear. To test the hypothesis that gut microbiota and their metabolites mediate salinity adaptation by modulating the gut-brain-gonad axis, we conducted fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and metabolite supplementation experiments.ResultsResults showed that the FMT group successfully recapitulated the donor’s gut microbial profile and exhibited significant changes in intestinal metabolites. Both FMT and GABA supplementation mimicked the reproductive inhibitory phenotype observed under direct salinity stress: altered brain neurotransmitter levels (increased dopamine, decreased serotonin), downregulated expression of reproductive genes (e.g. GnRH1, FSHβ), and impaired SSC self-renewal and differentiation in testes, as evidenced by reduced marker gene expression (e.g. PLZF, Stra8) and disrupted testicular histology.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that salinity stress reshapes the gut microbiota and metabolome. This leads to increased inhibitory signaling, notably via GABA, along the gut-brain-gonad axis. Ultimately, this signaling cascade suppresses reproductive function. This study provides novel mechanistic insights into the environmental adaptation strategies of an invasive species and highlights the potential of targeting the microbiome-metabolite axis for developing innovative, environmentally friendly biocontrol approaches against this invasive species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00583-4.

Introducation

The red-eared slider, Trachemys scripta elegans, is a formidable invasive species with high environmental adaptability [1–4] and a broad dietary range [5], posing a serious threat to global freshwater ecosystems [6]. Notably, its capacity to invade and establish populations extends beyond freshwater habitats into brackish environments, significantly expanding its potential invasion range [7, 8]. However, the impact of salinity stress on its reproductive fitness remains poorly understood. Preliminary studies indicate that high salinity impairs spermatogenesis in T. s. elegans [7], specifically inhibiting the differentiation of spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) [5]. Intriguingly, this reproductive suppression appears to be regulated via the gut-brain axis, suggesting a pivotal role for gut microbiota and their metabolites in mediating the organism’s adaptation to salinity stress and its subsequent reproductive output.

The gut microbiota, often referred to as the “second brain” of the host, exerts profound direct and indirect effects on reproduction [9, 10]. It can regulate reproductive hormones [11] and capacity [12, 13] through various mechanisms, including modulating the metabolism of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), amino acids, and bile acids [14, 15]. Specific microbial taxa, such as Desulfovibrio [16], Lactobacillus [16], and Akkermansia muciniphila [17], have been implicated in the secretion of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) and the regulation of SSC differentiation. Among key microbial metabolites, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), a major inhibitory neurotransmitter [18], participates in reproductive regulation by acting on GABA receptors located on GnRH and kisspeptin neurons [19]. Environmental factors, particularly salinity, are known to reshape the gut microbial community and its metabolic output in aquatic animals [5, 20]. For instance, salinity stress alters intestinal microbiota and increases levels of inhibitory neurotransmitters like GABA in T. s. elegans, which correlates with inhibited secretion of reproductive hormones and hindered SSC renewal and differentiation [5]. Despite these correlations, conclusive evidence for a causal relationship between salinity-altered gut microbiota and reproductive impairment is lacking.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and metabolite supplementation are powerful tools to establish causality between microbial shifts and host phenotypes [21, 22]. FMT can directly test whether a donor’s microbial community can transfer a specific phenotype to a recipient [23]. Similarly, supplementing a specific metabolite identified under stress conditions can verify its functional role [22]. These approaches have been successfully applied in other models to link gut microbiota with host physiology but remain unexplored in the context of environmental adaptation in invasive reptiles [24, 25].

Therefore, this study aimed to mechanistically dissect the role of the gut microbiota and its metabolites in the reproductive adaptation of T. s. elegans to salinity stress. We hypothesized that salinity stress inhibits SSC function by reshaping the gut microbiota and elevating key metabolites (e.g., GABA), which in turn modulate signaling along the gut-brain-gonad axis. To test this, we conducted two independent experiments: (1) transplanting gut microbiota from salinity-stressed donors into healthy, antibiotic-pretreated recipients (FMT experiment), and (2) directly supplementing GABA in the diet of healthy individuals. We assessed changes in the gut microbial composition and metabolome, brain neurotransmitter and reproductive hormone levels, gene expression along the reproductive axis, and testicular histology and SSC marker expression. This work elucidates the multidimensional adaptive strategies of an invasive species to environmental stress and provides a novel theoretical framework for developing targeted biocontrol strategies based on microbiome and metabolome regulation, offering a promising avenue for non-toxic population management.

Materials and methods

Animal and experimental design

Adult male T. s. elegans (averaging 434 ± 47.2 g) were purchased from Hongwang Co., Ltd. (Haikou, China) and maintained in the Key Laboratory of Tropical Islands Ecology of the Ministry of Education at Hainan Normal University. Environmental conditions were controlled at 26 °C, pH 7.4 ± 0.2, and dissolved oxygen 8.5 ± 0.3 mg/L. Turtles were fed commercial feed twice a week, with water changed weekly. The experiment began after one week of adaptation. Nutrient levels of commercial feed in Table S1.

Experiment 1: fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

The experimental design was shown in Fig. 1. A mixed antibiotic solution was prepared with ampicillin (1 mg/mL), metronidazole (1 mg/mL), neomycin sulfate (1 mg/mL), and vancomycin (0.5 mg/mL) [21]. Hindgut contents from T. s. elegans subjected to 10‰ salinity stress for 6 months [5] were used as donor microbiota. Contents were diluted 1:5 (w/v) in sterile physiological saline, homogenized, and centrifuged (4 °C, 7000 × g, 10 min). The supernatant was collected from the bacterial suspension. Eighteen turtles were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): NS group: weekly gavage with sterile saline; ANS group: gavaged with mixed antibiotics in weeks 1–2, followed by sterile saline thereafter; FMT group: gavaged with mixed antibiotics in weeks 1–2, then received 200 μL of bacterial suspension twice weekly. We acknowledge that antibiotic pretreatment may transiently affect host physiology; however, the ANS control group was included to account for potential effects of the antibiotics themselves.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the FMT experiment and sampling

Schematic diagram of the FMT experiment and sampling

After 2 months, intestinal contents were collected for microbiota and metabolites analyses. Three testicles were sampled for tissue section, and the remaining testicles, pituitary, and hypothalamus were sampled for gene expression and ELISA.

Experiment 2: GABA supplementation

Twelve turtles were divided into two groups (n = 6 per group): CK group: fed commercial feed. GABA group: fed commercial feed supplemented with 100 mg/kg GABA twice weekly [22]. After 2 months, samples were collected as described in Experiment 1.

RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from brain and testis tissues using TRIzol reagent, followed by chloroform separation and isopropanol precipitation. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, China). Gene-specific primers (Table S2) were designed based on CDS sequences from NCBI and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). β-actin served as the reference gene. Quantitative PCR was performed with 2× SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (ABclona, USA), and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

ELISA analysis

Concentrations of neurotransmitters (GABA, DA, 5-HT) in brain and reproductive hormones (GnRH, FSH, T) in testis were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Zike, China). Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded, and concentrations were determined based on standard curves.

Testicular histology

Testicular tissues were fixed, dehydrated through an ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, sectioned (5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Sections were examined under a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100, Japan), and images were captured using CaseViewer for morphological analysis.

Gut microbiota analysis

Microbial DNA was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 338F/806 R. Amplicons were sequenced on an Illumina platform. After quality filtering, reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity. Alpha diversity (Shannon, Chao1) and beta diversity (PCoA based on Hellinger distance) were calculated. Differential taxa were identified using LEfSe (LDA > 2.0, p < 0.05).

Untargeted metabolomics

Intestinal metabolites were extracted and analyzed using a UHPLC-Q Exactive system (Thermo Scientific, USA) in both positive and negative ion modes. Raw data were processed and matched against the MJDBPM metabolite database. OPLS-DA was performed using the ropls package (v1.6.2) in R. Metabolites with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered differentially abundant. Pathway enrichment was performed using the KEGG database.

Statistical analysis

Data normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test in SPSS 26.0, respectively. For data meeting parametric assumptions, one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s post hoc test was applied. Otherwise, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Spearman’s correlation was used for association analyses. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 and FigDraw.

Discussion

FMT and GABA supplementation recapitulate the gut-brain-gonad axis dysregulation under salinity stress

This study demonstrates that fecal microbiota transplantation from salinity-stressed donors, as well as direct GABA supplementation, can effectively transfer a reproductive-suppressive phenotype to unstressed T. s. elegans. Both interventions induced convergent alterations along the gut-brain-gonad axis-reshaping gut microbial communities and metabolic profiles, modulating brain neurotransmitter systems, suppressing key reproductive hormones, and ultimately impairing SSC renewal and differentiation. These findings establish a direct causal link between salinity-induced gut microbiota/metabolite shifts and reproductive inhibition, confirming the pivotal integrative role of the gut-brain-gonad axis in environmental adaptation.

FMT-Mediated microbial rearrangement and its functional implications

The successful colonization of donor microbiota in FMT recipients indicates that salinity-stressed microbial communities are both transferable and functionally active. In this study, the community composition of the S10 group [5] was similar to that of the FMT group. The phylum-level included Firmicutes, Clostridium, Bacteroidota, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, Desulfobacterota, Patescibacteria, and Campilobacterota. At the genus level, the genera included Brucella, Cetaceus, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, Terrisporobacter, Enterococcus, Lachnospiraceae, and Lactobacillus. In addition, the dominant bacteria were Bacteroidota and Firmicutes in the FMT group, which were similar to the salinity stress experiment of T. s. elegans and Pangasianodon hypophthalmus [26] and oysters [27]. The Firmicutes can decompose dietary fiber, and their metabolite, SCFAs, is also beneficial to the host. However, the abundance of Firmicutes decreased in the FMT group, and the abundance of Romboutsia also declined, reducing beneficial effects on the host [28]. Bacteroides degrades dietary fibers and produces beneficial metabolites, including SCFAs, which regulate host energy metabolism and inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to intestinal flora stability [29]. Furthermore, the F/B ratio is an important indicator for maintaining normal intestinal homeostasis. A decrease in the F/B ratio also suggests that salt stress has led to intestinal flora imbalance [30]. The enrichment of A. muciniphila and Lachnospiraceae is particularly noteworthy. Studies have shown that A. muciniphila is known to enhance retinoic acid levels via secondary bile acid metabolism, thereby promoting SSC differentiation [17]. Its increase in the FMT group may represent a compensatory response to salinity-induced reproductive suppression. Concurrently, the rise in Lachnospiraceae, a butyrate producer associated with enhanced intestinal barrier function and anti-inflammatory effects, likely reflects an adaptive mechanism to mitigate stress-induced gut permeability and systemic inflammation [31]. Thus, the restructured microbial community under FMT appears to embody both stress-induced dysbiosis and host-directed compensatory adjustments.

GABA as a key microbial-derived signal inhibiting the reproductive axis

Elevated intestinal GABA under salinity stress emerges as a critical metabolic signal mediating gut-brain communication [32]. Our GABA supplementation experiment functionally validated its inhibitory role: dietary GABA not only altered gut microbiota but also directly suppressed the central reproductive circuit. The abundance of Proteobacteria increased in the GABA group, which is often associated with human diseases and implies intestinal microbiota dysregulation [33]. Lactobacillus is recognized as a beneficial bacterium that can alleviate ileotropic oxidative stress induced by heat stress [34], enhance intestinal integrity and intestinal barrier [35], and improve neurotoxicity along the gut-brain axis [36]. The abundance of Lactobacillus decreased significantly in the GABA group. The Escherichia-Shigella can bind to invasive Escherichia coli (EIEC), leading to intestinal epithelial inflammation and ulcers [37], and its abundance increased in the GABA group, resulting in the dysregulation of the intestinal flora. Enterobacteriaceae were closely associated with intestinal inflammation, and their reduced abundance alleviated inflammation severity. As potential pathogens, these bacteria could disrupt gut microbiota homeostasis. Their increased abundance in the GABA group suggested an imbalance in the intestinal microbiota [38]. The observed downregulation of KISS and GnRH1, coupled with upregulated GABAR expression, supports a model wherein salinity stress (or direct GABA supplementation) elevates intestinal GABA levels, which may act as a key gut-derived signal. We hypothesize that this elevated GABA activates inhibitory GABAβ receptors expressed on kisspeptin neurons in the hypothalamus. The suppression of these kisspeptin neurons subsequently attenuates the release of GnRH, ultimately leading to downregulation of the pituitary-gonadal axis (evidenced by reduced FSH and LH) and impairment of spermatogenesis in the testes. This mechanism is consistent with findings in PCOS patients [39] and rodent models [40], where GABAergic overactivity correlates with hypogonadism. Our results further extend this paradigm to an environmental context, revealing how an abiotic stressor (salinity) can be transduced into a neuroendocrine reproductive brake via a microbial metabolite.

Convergent downstream effects on spermatogenesis and SSC niche

Both FMT and GABA supplementation converged on the testicular SSC niche, impairing self-renewal and differentiation. The reduction in PLZF and Stra8 expression, along with disrupted testicular histology, mirrors the effects of direct salinity stress. The suppressed FSH levels observed in the FMT group likely play a central role. In infertile mice’s in vitro culture experiments, xenotransplantation of SSC in dogs can promote spermatogenesis, while FSH can amplify this promoting effect [41]. In this study, FSH showed a significant positive correlation with GFRα, Stra8, and PLZF. This indicates that FSH promotes SSC’s self-renewal and differentiation. Studies have shown that GDNF secreted by supporting cells and myoid cells is necessary for SSC self-renewal [42], and the number of SSC in GDNF-specific knockout mice is significantly reduced [43]. FSH stimulates the secretion of GDNF in the body [44]. After GDNF binds to GFRα-1, the classic Ras-ERK 1/2 pathway is activated, ultimately affecting the growth of SSC [45]. The decreased FSH secretion in the FMT group likely led to reduced GDNF levels, thereby failing to activate the Ras-ERK1/2 pathway and ultimately impairing SSC function. The inhibition of self-renewal leads to the inability to maintain a stable number of stem cell banks. The inhibition of differentiation reduces the number of spermatocytes, sperm cells, and mature sperm, weakening the spermatogenesis process and affecting the reproductive ability of male animals [46]. The results of tissue sections also confirmed this. The spermatogenic tubules of the testis were loosely arranged with small lumens. The spermatogenic cells at all levels were disordered and in small numbers, which was consistent with the effect of salinity on Nile tilapia [47]. The concurrent downregulation of steroidogenic genes (StAR, CYP11) and estrogen receptors further indicates a blunted hormonal milieu within the testis. These coordinated changes suggest that the gut-derived signal disrupts the pituitary-gonadal feedback loop and alters the testicular paracrine landscape, ultimately compromising the SSC niche and spermatogenic [48].

Ecological implications and novel avenues for invasive species management

From an ecological perspective, the plasticity of the gut-brain-gonad axis represents a double-edged sword for T. s. elegans. While it may facilitate short-term survival in brackish environments by diverting energy from reproduction to osmoregulation [49], it could also limit long-term population establishment in novel saline habitats [50]. This mechanistic insight refines our understanding of invasion dynamics, suggesting that microbial-mediated reproductive plasticity may influence the colonization potential of invasive species, a possibility that remains to be tested across other invasive taxa.

More importantly, this study identifies the microbiome and its metabolites as vulnerable targets for population control. The significant negative correlations between specific bacterial genera (e.g., Macellibacteroides, Edwardsiella) and SSC markers offer candidate targets for precision biocontrol. Strategies could be developed to selectively enhance these “anti-reproductive” microbes or to deliver GABA-mimetic compounds via bait, thereby suppressing reproduction in wild populations without broad environmental toxicity. This microbiota-mediated reproductive plasticity might be a conserved mechanism among invasive species facing osmotic challenges, warranting comparative studies in other invaders.

Limitations of this study

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size (n = 6 per group), while sufficient to detect major phenotypic shifts, may limit the statistical power for identifying subtler changes in microbial composition or metabolite profiles. Future investigations with larger cohorts are recommended to validate and extend these findings. Second, individual variation in donor microbiota due to genetic or subtle environmental differences cannot be ruled out. Third, while GABA supplementation and correlative receptor expression data point to the involvement of GABAβ receptors, their specific and necessary role in mediating the reproductive suppression within the proposed gut-brain-gonad axis requires direct validation. Finally, the experimental animals used in this study were male T. s. elegans. However, this conclusion may not be directly applicable to female T. s. elegans, and future studies are needed to conduct relevant experiments on females to verify this possibility. Future studies employing GABAβ receptor-specific antagonists or conditional knockdown in kisspeptin neurons would be crucial to establish this causal link. Future work should focus on: (1) identifying the exact microbial taxa responsible for GABA overproduction under salinity stress; (2) elucidating the communication routes (neural, endocrine, or immune) between the gut and the brain; and (3) testing targeted delivery of microbial consortia or metabolites in field-simulated settings to evaluate their potential as eco-friendly biopesticides.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides compelling evidence that salinity stress inhibits reproduction in T. s. elegans by reshaping the gut microbiota and elevating inhibitory metabolites like GABA, which in turn disrupts signaling along the gut-brain-gonad axis. By employing FMT and metabolite supplementation, we have moved beyond correlation to establish causality in this environmental–physiological pathway. These findings not only advance our understanding of how invasive species adjust their reproductive investment in response to environmental challenges but also pioneer a novel, microbiome-based conceptual framework for developing targeted and sustainable invasion control strategies.
