Research Progress on ROS Scavenging and Responsive Materials for Biomedical Applications
Authors: Haimang Wang, Hao Lin, Baoxuan Wei, Shunxian Gong, Jingxun Lu, Yingying Fan, Jinghua Li, Yi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) participate in essential physiological processes such as cellular signaling under normal conditions; however, their excessive accumulation under oxidative stress can induce various diseases. Addressing this challenge, the development of biomaterials capable of precisely regulating ROS levels is of great significance. This review systematically summarizes two classes of biomaterials for modulating oxidative stressROS-scavenging materials and ROS-responsive materialsfrom the perspective of material design principles, elucidating their underlying mechanisms and construction strategies, respectively. ROS-scavenging materials primarily include nanozymes, inorganic nanoparticles, natural antioxidants, and polymers rich in reactive functional groups, which exert antioxidant effects through sustained consumption of excess ROS. In contrast, ROS-responsive materials exploit responsive moieties such as phenylboronic esters, thioketals and diselenides to trigger structural disassembly or drug release within high-ROS microenvironments, thereby enabling spatiotemporally controlled on-demand delivery. Furthermore, this review also discusses the synergistic therapeutic potential of multiresponsive materials. Finally, the major challenges currently facing this field are summarized, and future directions for clinical translation are prospected.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are a class of biological oxidants generated during aerobic respiration in the human body, mainly including free radicals such as superoxide anion (O2 •–) and hydroxyl radical (•OH), as well as nonradical species like hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and singlet oxygen (1O2). , At physiological concentrations, ROS play key roles in various biological functions, including cell signaling, gene regulation, regulating cell proliferation and differentiation, modulating protein function, and mediating inflammatory responses. − However, under conditions of oxidative stress, excessive ROS production directly damages cellular proteins, lipids, and DNA, thereby inducing cell death. Currently, oxidative stress has been confirmed as a core pathological feature of multiple diseases, including cardiovascular diseases (e.g., atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction), metabolic diseases (e.g., chronic diabetic wounds), autoimmune diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis), inflammatory diseases (e.g., osteoarthritis), neurodegenerative disorders, and cancer. − (see Table )

Therefore, developing biomaterials capable of precisely regulating ROS levels is of great significance. In recent years, researchers have focused on exploring various material platforms to achieve this goal, among which nanoparticles, hydrogels, and nanofibers have attracted considerable attention due to their unique physicochemical properties and favorable biocompatibility. − For instance, Nanoparticles possess a high specific surface area and tunable size, morphology, and surface chemistry, enabling efficient ROS scavenging or triggered drug release in ROS-rich microenvironments. − Hydrogels feature a three-dimensional network with high water content, mimicking the native extracellular matrix, and can be engineered to undergo ROS-responsive degradation or swelling, allowing for ROS level-dependent drug release. , Similarly, Nanofibers offer a high specific surface area, high porosity, and anisotropic topological structures, and can be loaded with ROS scavengers or responsive components, making them suitable for applications such as tissue engineering scaffolds and wound dressings. Collectively, these materials possess distinct characteristics, offering a diverse range of options for designing precise ROS-scavenging strategies tailored to specific disease microenvironments.

To date, most reviews have summarized the literature primarily from the perspective of biomedical applications (e.g., diabetic wound healing, cardiovascular regeneration, and oncology), with few providing a principled distinction and comparison between ROS-scavenging and ROS-responsive materials based on the underlying logic of material design. The former category is primarily designed to achieve sustained scavenging of excessive ROS and alleviation of oxidative stress, specifically by introducing or constructing chemical components with antioxidant activity, with an emphasis on material stability and long-term scavenging capacity. Accordingly, from the perspective of material design logic, this article systematically reviews two types of biomaterials used for oxidative stress regulation, namely ROS-scavenging materials and ROS-responsive materials, and further discusses their mechanisms of action and construction strategies. Next, ROS-responsive materials are systematically reviewed, primarily those containing responsive moieties such as phenylboronic esters, and the design principles underlying their structural transformation, controlled drug release, or functional activation in a high-ROS microenvironment are analyzed. The Production of ROS and Its Dual Roles

ROS are a collective term for a family of reactive molecules derived from molecular oxygen, primarily including the O2 – and hydrogen H2O2. , Their production in eukaryotic cells is an inherent aspect of aerobic metabolism, arising from both enzymatic and nonenzymatic sources. The primary endogenous sources are the mitochondrial electron transport chain, where electrons leak from complexes I and III to generate O2 –, which is subsequently dismutated to H2O2. − Another key source is the NADPH oxidase (NOX) family of enzymes, which are specifically dedicated to the regulated production of O2 – and H2O2 at various cellular membranes. − As schematically summarized in Figure , the interconversion among ROS is centrally governed by the dismutation of O2 •– to H2O2, which occurs either spontaneously or is catalyzed by superoxide dismutase (SOD). H2O2 is subsequently reduced to water by the antioxidant enzymes catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and peroxiredoxins (Prx). However, in the presence of free Fe2+, H2O2 undergoes Fenton chemistry to generate the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (•OH), which indiscriminately oxidizes lipids, proteins, and DNA. Concurrently, O2 •– can react with nitric oxide (NO) at a near-diffusion-limited rate to form peroxynitrite (ONOO–), a potent nitrating and oxidizing species. The accumulation of •OH and ONOO– beyond the endogenous antioxidant capacity leads to irreversible oxidative damage to biomoleculeslipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and DNA strand breakscharacteristic of “oxidative distress”. , This equilibrium maintains steady-state concentrations of key ROS, such as H2O2, in the low nanomolar range, creating a state of “oxidative eustress.” In this context, H2O2 functions as a specific and essential second messenger. Its signaling specificity is achieved through the reversible oxidation of thiolate groups on specific cysteine residues of target proteins, a process that can be amplified via redox relay mechanisms, often involving peroxiredoxins. , This precise post-translational modification modulates the activity of numerous kinases, phosphatases, and transcription factors, thereby orchestrating a vast array of physiological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, metabolism, and adaptation to stress.

Integrated schematic of cellular ROS metabolism. Primary ROS sources include the mitochondrial ETC, where electron leakage from Complexes I and III reduces O2 to O2 •–, and NOX enzymes that catalyze regulated production of O2 •– and H2O2. O2 •– is rapidly dismutated to H2O2 by SOD; H2O2 is subsequently reduced to H2O by CAT, GPx, and Prx. In the presence of free Fe2+, H2O2 undergoes Fenton chemistry to generate•OH. Concurrently, O2 •– reacts with NO at a near-diffusion-limited rate to form ONOO–. While low levels of H2O2 serve as a second messenger via reversible thiol redox signaling, excessive accumulation of•OH and ONOO– leads to oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA, resulting in genomic instability and cell death.

The precisely controlled signaling function under eustress represents the beneficial facet of the dual roles of ROS; the opposite, pathogenic facet emerges when this homeostasis is disrupteda shift from eustress to oxidative distress. This occurs when ROS production overwhelms the antioxidant capacity, leading to a supraphysiological accumulation. Unlike the targeted signaling of H2O2, excessive ROS, particularly the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (•OH) generated via Fenton chemistry, inflict indiscriminate damage on all major classes of biomolecules. This includes DNA damage (leading to mutations and genomic instability), lipid peroxidation (generating toxic aldehydes like 4-hydroxynonenal), and protein carbonylation or cross-linking. Such damage does not merely signify collateral harm; it actively derails cellular signaling networks, converting them from homeostatic to pathological states. This is vividly illustrated in the context of cancer, where ROS play a dual, context-dependent role. In the early stages of tumorigenesis, oncogene-induced ROS can drive DNA damage and mutagenesis, promoting malignant transformation. Paradoxically, the same metabolic and signaling aberrations that make cancer cells proliferate also place them under chronic oxidative stress, rendering them heavily dependent on upregulated antioxidant systems (such as the NRF2 pathway) for survival. This dependency creates a therapeutic vulnerability, where further increasing ROS with certain chemotherapies can push cancer cells past their toxicity threshold, inducing cell death through mechanisms like ferroptosis. , Beyond cancer, the dysregulation of ROS is central to the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including chronic inflammation, neurodegeneration, cardiovascular diseases, and the aging process itself.

ROS-Scavenging Materials

Recently, biomaterials with unique ROS-scavenging properties have been engineered, offering a promising strategy to overcome the fundamental challenges posed by excessive ROS in clinical settings. Based on their diverse mechanisms of action, ROS-scavenging biomaterials can be classified into three categories: nanozymes and inorganic particles, natural antioxidants and polymers rich in reactive groups. This classification provides valuable insights into ROS-scavenging biomaterials and serves as a guide for their further understanding, design, fabrication, and evaluation.

Nanozymes and Inorganic Particles

Studies have shown that certain inorganic nanoparticles possess an inherent ability to mimic the catalytic activity of biological enzymes, leading to their designation as nanozymes. Examples include cerium oxide nanoparticles, manganese nanoparticles, and iron nanoparticles. − These materials offer several advantages, including low production cost, high modifiability, favorable surface activity, targeted accumulation in specific tissues, and excellent biocompatibility.

The antioxidant mechanism of CeO2 nanozymes is primarily based on the reversible conversion between trivalent cerium (Ce3+) and tetravalent cerium (Ce4+) on their surface, along with the resulting oxygen vacancy defects. At the nanoscale, oxygen atoms in the cerium oxide lattice tend to detach, forming oxygen vacancies and promoting the reduction of some Ce4+ to Ce3+ to maintain charge balance. Surface-exposed Ce3+ can mimic SOD, catalyzing the conversion of superoxide anion radicals into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen, while being oxidized to Ce4+. The newly generated Ce4+ can further mimic CAT, catalyzing the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen, and being reduced back to Ce3+. , Through this reversible cycle, CeO2 nanozymes can continuously scavenge ROS on the particle surface, exhibiting a regenerative capacity akin to natural antioxidant enzymes, thereby effectively protecting cells from oxidative stress damage.

As shown in Figure a, Ma et al. developed an innovative dual-functional hydrogel, CeUA@GelMA, which integrates CeO2 nanozymes and Urolithin A into a methacrylated gelatin (GelMA) matrix. In vitro experiments confirmed that CeO2 nanozymes, leveraging the redox cycle between their mixed Ce3+/Ce4+ valence states on the nanoparticle surface, exhibited sustained superoxide dismutase- and catalase-like activities, effectively reducing intracellular ROS levels in BMSCs under H2O2-induced oxidative stress, as detected by DCFH-DA fluorescence probes, and achieving significant DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging rates. Concurrently, Urolithin A released from the hydrogel specifically activated the PINK1/Parkin signaling pathway, as evidenced by upregulated Pink1 and Parkin gene expression by qRT-PCR and enhanced mitophagic flux quantified by LysoTracker/MitoTracker colocalization analysis. This coordinated mechanism effectively disrupts the pathological cycle of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby creating a hyaline-like cartilage regeneration microenvironment.

As illustrated in Figure b, Wang et al. integrated a superlubricated antiadhesion surface with a pro-healing, ROS-scavenging layer into a Janus nanofibrous patch (J-NFP). The repairing layer contains CeO2 nanozymes, which catalytically decompose H2O2 into O2, thereby effectively scavenging ROS and alleviating inflammation. In vivo, J-NFP effectively reduces tissue adhesion and accelerates healing in rat models of skin defects and intrauterine adhesions. Therefore, these nanoparticles hold great promise for applications in tissue repair, including cartilage repair and the treatment of tissue adhesion injuries.

Manganese (Mn) nanozymes act as potent ROS scavengers by mimicking the function of natural antioxidant enzymes. − They possess multivalent oxidation states (Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+) that facilitate catalytic dismutation of superoxide anions via SOD-like activity. Subsequently, the intermediate hydrogen peroxide is decomposed into water and oxygen through CAT-like activity. This cascade reaction efficiently neutralizes toxic ROS, mitigating oxidative stress while exhibiting high biocompatibility and stability under physiological conditions. For example, Xie et al. developed water-soluble Mn3O4 nanozymes to scavenge reactive oxygen species. Intranasal administration in 5 × FAD mice initially promoted M2 microglial polarization within 4 weeks by downregulating the TLR4/NOX2 pathway, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation. After 8 weeks of treatment, the nanozymes significantly reduced fibrillar amyloid-β pathology and improved cognitive deficits. In a further development (Figure c), Yun et al. developed a multifunctional oral hydrogel (PMC@Gel) coloaded with Pt/Mn3O4 nanozymes and curcumin. This system integrates dual active catalysis with autophagy induction to achieve comprehensive therapeutic intervention. The heterostructured Pt/Mn3O4 nanozymes exhibit a core–satellite architecture with Pt–O–Mn interfacial bonds. This configuration accelerates the Mn redox cycle while simultaneously exhibiting CAT-like and SOD-like activities, thereby enabling broad-spectrum ROS scavenging.

Beyond the synergistic effects within the heterostructure, Pt itself also plays a direct role in ROS scavenging. , Xue et al. developed a mitochondrial-targeted liposome loaded with platinum nanozymes (Pt@MitoLipo). The Pt nanozymes exhibit cascade SOD and CAT activities, converting superoxide anions and hydrogen peroxide into nontoxic water and oxygen. Pt@MitoLipo can penetrate the cell membrane, escape lysosomal entrapment, and target mitochondria, thereby enabling precise scavenging of mitochondrial ROS and alleviation of hypoxia. This study provides a novel strategy for treating retinal neovascular diseases by scavenging ROS and alleviating hypoxia.

In addition to the metal-based nanozymes discussed above, other nanomaterials, such as iron-based and copper-based nanozymes, have also been extensively investigated for their ROS-scavenging capabilities. Notably, iron-based nanozymes, such as Fe3O4 nanoparticles, are known for their intrinsic peroxidase-like activity. − while copper-based nanozymes, including CuO and Cu2O, demonstrate efficient superoxide dismutase-like and glutathione peroxidase-like activities. − The structural tunability and catalytic versatility of these metal-based nanozymes make them promising candidates for the treatment of oxidative stress-related diseases, further supporting the translational potential of nanozyme-based therapeutic strategies.

Natural Antioxidants

Natural antioxidants are primarily polyphenolic compounds, which are structurally characterized by one or more aromatic rings, each typically bearing one or more hydroxyl groups (−OH) that contribute to their functional activity. These phenolic hydroxyl groups possess low bond dissociation energies, enabling them to act as efficient hydrogen atom donors. By donating hydrogen atoms to free radicals, such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide anions (O2 –•), they reduce these radicals to stable small molecules, thereby interrupting free radical chain reactions. , , Meanwhile, upon losing a hydrogen atom, the polyphenolic structure forms resonance-stabilized phenoxyl radical semiquinone or quinone intermediates. Due to electron delocalization, these intermediates possess low energy and do not initiate new chain reactions, exhibiting the characteristics of a “radical terminator”. In addition, certain polyphenols can chelate transition metal ions (e.g., Fe2+, Cu2+), thereby inhibiting metal-catalyzed ROS generation pathways such as the Fenton reaction, achieving indirect antioxidant effects. ,

Among various catechol-based structures, catechol itself represents the simplest form, while dopamine, as an important natural derivative, has been widely applied in the field of ROS scavenging, owing to the strong reducing activity conferred by its ortho-dihydroxybenzene (catechol) moiety. , In practical applications, dopamine is typically utilized in the form of polydopamine particles or as side-chain-grafted dopamine within hydrogels to achieve stable antioxidant functions. , As shown in Figure a, Gu et al. incorporated polydopamine nanoparticles into a double-network hydrogel (CBGT@PDA) constructed from gelatin and chitosan derivatives. This hydrogel leverages the ROS-scavenging capacity of dopamine (with a clearance rate of 63.5%) to effectively alleviate oxidative stress in diabetic wounds. Additionally, it exhibits excellent tissue adhesion, thereby significantly accelerating the healing of infected diabetic wounds.

Meanwhile, to address the therapeutic needs of rheumatoid arthritis, He et al. developed a dopamine-modified hyaluronic acid hydrogel that integrates both bioadhesive and lubricating functions (Figure b). This system employs dopamine-grafted graphene quantum dots loaded with copper single-atom nanozymes (DAGQD@Cu@KGN). Through the synergistic effects of dopamine and copper single atoms, it achieves efficient ROS scavenging. Moreover, it mimics the mussel adhesion mechanism to provide durable tissue adhesion, while the incorporation of sulfonated hyaluronic acid enables joint lubrication. Collectively, these features contribute to full-cycle therapeutic outcomes, including inflammation suppression, mitigation of cartilage wear, and repair of cartilage damage.

Tannic acid represents another class of natural polyphenolic compounds rich in catechol and galloyl moieties, which has also garnered considerable attention in ROS scavenging and related biomedical applications. Compared with the small-molecule dopamine, tannic acidowing to its multivalent phenolic hydroxyl structure and excellent metal ion chelating capacitycan achieve efficient scavenging of multiple reactive oxygen species through synergistic mechanisms. For instance, Xu et al. constructed an immunomodulatory hydrogel (GBTF) based on natural polymers, which was loaded with a tannic acid/iron ion complex (TA/Fe3+). Through coordination with iron ions, tannic acid not only endows the system with excellent photothermal antibacterial properties but also achieves remarkable antioxidant functions by virtue of its abundant phenolic hydroxyl structures, with a DPPH radical scavenging rate exceeding 80%. These findings further expand the application prospects of polyphenolic materials in the field of complex tissue repair.

Flavonoids (e.g., quercetin, puerarin) feature a typical C6–C3–C6 skeleton, with multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups attached to both aromatic rings. This structural feature endows them with efficient hydrogen atom or electron donor capacity, thereby enabling direct scavenging of various ROS. , As shown in Figure c, Mukherjee et al. developed a dual-network injectable hydrogel loaded with quercetin@PLGA nanoparticles. Experimental results demonstrated that the sustained release of quercetin from the PLGA nanoparticles and the hydrogel matrix effectively reduced intracellular ROS levels.

Vitamin E, as represented by its most biologically active form α-tocopherol, is a potent lipophilic antioxidant. Its chromanol ring possesses a phenolic hydroxyl group that readily donates a hydrogen atom (electron donor) to lipid peroxyl radicals, effectively terminating the chain reaction of lipid peroxidation and directly stabilizing cell membranes against oxidative damage. For example, Zhang et al. developed dual-responsive NPs)­for targeted vitamin E delivery to treat ischemia/reperfusion-induced acute kidney injury (AKI). The NPs utilized a diselenide-containing amphiphilic polymer as the ROS-sensitive shell and gelatin-SH as the MMP2-cleavable component, coencapsulating liposoluble Vitamin E and self-assembling into nanoparticles. In vivo I/R kidney injury models and in vitro hypoxia/reoxygenation models confirmed that the NPs effectively scavenged ROS, reduced oxidative stress, and alleviated AKI, while also inhibiting the transition from AKI to chronic kidney disease.

In addition to the aforementioned polyphenolic compounds, other naturally derived polyphenols such as curcumin and resveratrol have also been extensively investigated for their antioxidant properties in biomedical applications. Curcumin, a diketone-containing polyphenol, not only directly scavenges ROS but also modulates endogenous antioxidant pathways such as the Nf-kB signaling axis, thereby exerting sustained cytoprotective effects. For example, Zhang et al. demonstrated that a curcumin-loaded hydrogel achieved dual ROS scavenging: extracellular scavenging via ROS-sensitive hydrogel degradation was validated by DPPH assay, while intracellular scavenging through suppressed NADPH oxidase activation was confirmed by DCFH-DA probe and inhibition of p47-phox/p67-phox translocation. Resveratrol, a representative stilbene polyphenol, exhibits potent ROS-scavenging activity and has been widely employed in the construction of drug delivery systems for the treatment of neurodegenerative and cardiovascular diseases. , Overall, polyphenolic compounds, owing to their exceptional ROS scavenging capacity and favorable biocompatibility, hold promising application prospects in the fields of related disease therapy and tissue engineering.

Polymers Rich in Reactive Groups

Thioethers are a class of sulfur-containing compounds characterized by an R–S–R′ group, where R and R′ represent alkyl or aryl groups, and these compounds are readily oxidized to sulfoxides or sulfones in the presence of relatively high concentrations of ROS. − Selenoethers and telluroethers, which share a similar structure to thioethers, exhibit analogous oxidation behavior; however, their reducing ability increases significantly with increasing atomic number. Consequently, selenoethers are more readily oxidized than thioethers, whereas telluroethers exhibit the strongest reducing ability and can undergo significant oxidation even upon exposure to air. This oxidation-responsive behavior endows these compounds with significant application prospects in smart materials, drug delivery, and ROS-related biomedical research. For instance, Jiang et al. leveraged the susceptibility of thioether groups to ROS oxidation by enriching them within the interior of dendrimers, thereby constructing a nanomaterial (Sr-G4-PEG) capable of both ROS scavenging and inflammatory cytokine capture.

Additionally, The duration of sustained ROS scavenging varies notably among different material platforms. Nanozymes (e.g., CeO2, Mn3O4) exhibit catalytic, nonconsumable activity that typically lasts from several days to weeks, with reported in vivo efficacy ranging from 14 to 21 days. Natural antioxidants (e.g., polyphenols, vitamin E) are gradually consumed during scavenging and generally provide protection for hours to a few days; however, encapsulation in hydrogels or nanoparticles can prolong their activity to approximately 3–7 days. Thioether- or selenoether-containing polymers, being sacrificial in nature, usually exert ROS-scavenging effects for 1–3 days unless embedded in sustained-release matrices.

ROS-Responsive Material

ROS-responsive biomaterials utilize the abnormally elevated levels of ROS in the pathological microenvironment as a trigger signal to induce structural dissociation or functional transformation of sensitive moieties within the material, thereby enabling on-demand and spatiotemporally controlled drug release. − Such materials thus exhibit considerable potential for clinical translation. This section provides a systematic summary of the working mechanisms of ROS-responsive biomaterials.

Phenylboronic Acid and Ester

The ROS-responsive mechanism of phenylboronic acid (PBA) is primarily based on the selective nucleophilic reaction between the unique electronic structure of its boron center and H2O2. , Specifically, the unoccupied 2p orbital of the boron atom enables it to act as an electrophilic center, accepting H2O2 as a nucleophile. In the presence of H2O2, the lone pair electrons on its peroxy bond undergo nucleophilic attack on the boron atom, forming an unstable tetrahedral intermediate. This intermediate subsequently undergoes a Baeyer–Villiger-type oxidative rearrangement, during which the boronic acid group migrates from the boron atom to an adjacent oxygen atom, followed by rapid hydrolysis. This ultimately results in the formation of phenolic hydroxyl products or further cleavage of ester bonds. Based on this mechanism, PBA is commonly employed in the construction of ROS-responsive drug delivery systems, such as in the fabrication of nanoparticles or the side-chain modification of hydrogels, enabling controlled drug release through oxidation-induced structural dissociation.

As shown in Figure a, Zhao et al. conjugated PBA to a dextran backbone via a ROS-responsive carbonate linkage to construct self-assembling nanoparticles loaded with doxorubicin (DOX). These nanoparticles exhibited remarkable ROS-triggered drug release characteristics in vitro. Notably, PBA modification significantly enhanced the cellular uptake of nanoparticles by tumor cells, effectively inhibited cell migration and invasion, and induced apoptosis, demonstrating promising potential for practical applications. Second, Huang et al. grafted epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) onto oxidized hyaluronic acid via a ROS-sensitive boronic ester linkage (Figure b). This construct was then cross-linked with sulfonated hyaluronic acid-adipic acid dihydrazide to form a dynamic acylhydrazone-based hydrogel, which enables the intelligent release of EGCG in response to ROS levels at the wound site, thereby scavenging excessive ROS.

In addition, Casillas-Popova et al. synthesized a novel phenyl diboronic acid cross-linker containing an ethylene spacer, and used it to cross-link electrospun PVA nanofibers through the formation of boronic ester bonds, thereby constructing a smart wound dressing (Figure d). In the oxidative stress microenvironment of wounds, ROS such as hydrogen peroxide induce the cleavage of these boronic ester bonds, leading to disintegration of the cross-linked network. Collectively, PBA exhibits excellent ROS-triggered drug release characteristics and the capacity to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment, positioning it as a promising candidate with broad application prospects and significant practical potential in the field of smart responsive biomaterials.

Thioketal

Thioketal (TK) bonds undergo selective cleavage upon exposure to ROS such as hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radicals. Through an electron transfer process, they convert harmful radicals into stable oxidation products, achieving “sacrificial” ROS consumption while simultaneously triggering structural disintegration or functional release of the material. − This mechanism constitutes the chemical basis for the responsive degradation and regulatory functions of TK-based biomaterials.

Zhang et al. leveraged the ROS-responsive mechanism of TK bonds to construct a factor-free injectable hydrogel based on an eight-arm PEG backbone (Figure a). Upon exposure to H2O2, this hydrogel exhibited new thiol proton signals, confirming that oxidative cleavage of TK bonds generates thiol and ketone groups, accompanied by a decrease in modulus and network degradation. Concurrently, TK bonds alleviated oxidative damage to bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) while scavenging ROS. As shown in Figure b, Xu et al. introduced ROS-responsive TK bonds into a GelMA system using a NHS-TK-NHS bifunctional linker. One end of the linker forms an amide bond with the amino groups of GelMA, while the other end conjugates the osteogenic and pro-angiogenic factor METRNL. Under H2O2 conditions, the TK cross-linkers undergo cleavage, leading to a reduction in hydrogel mass and enabling ROS-triggered matrix degradation and on-demand release of METRNL. The TK moiety alleviates inflammation by consuming local ROS, and synergizes with METRNL to promote BMSC homing and angiogenesis, thereby remodeling the vascular-osteogenic microenvironment and enhancing the quality of cranial bone defect repair.

Guo et al. incorporated TK bonds into the backbone of an amphiphilic biodegradable polymer (PHPM) to construct a ROS-sensitive polymer (Figure c). Through electrostatic interactions, this polymer coloaded copper ions and the copper ionophore elesclomol to form NP@ESCu nanoparticles. The high levels of ROS within the tumor microenvironment induced TK bond cleavage, triggering degradation of the polymer backbone and disassembly of the nanoparticles, thereby achieving site-specific release of the ES–Cu complex at the lesion site. Meanwhile, Xiong et al. modified the surface of a γ-cyclodextrin metal–organic framework with TK bonds to construct a TK-cross-linked supramolecular framework (TCOF). Dexamethasone was loaded into this framework to form TCD nanoparticles for oral targeted therapy of ulcerative colitis (Figure d). TCOF rapidly disintegrated in the presence of millimolar levels of H2O2, with TK bonds undergoing oxidative cleavage to generate small molecules such as acetone and release cyclodextrin fragments, while simultaneously exhibiting efficient scavenging capacity against multiple ROS species, including hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, and superoxide anions. The thiol groups exposed upon cleavage further undergo thiol/disulfide exchange with mucins in the intestinal mucus, significantly enhancing adhesion and retention at the inflamed colonic mucosa.

As shown in Figure e, another research team designed and synthesized a polyurethane (PFTU) with densely distributed thioketal bonds along its backbone, and further fabricated it into a fibrous patch for the treatment of myocardial infarction. Upon contact with the high levels of reactive oxygen species in the infarcted area, the thioketal bonds in the polymer backbone are oxidatively cleaved by ROS, scavenging free radicals while simultaneously triggering degradation of the material itself. This process effectively attenuates myocardial oxidative injury, cell apoptosis, and inflammation during the early stage of the disease.

Collectively, TK bonds, by virtue of their dual capability to simultaneously trigger material disassembly and functional release, have demonstrated broad application prospects in diverse biomedical fields, including tissue engineering, drug delivery, and inflammation-targeted therapy.

Diselenide and Ditelluride

Selenium is a crucial trace element that maintains redox homeostasis in living organisms. As a core component of antioxidant enzymes such as GPx, it plays an irreplaceable role in protecting cells from oxidative damage. containing polymers exhibit higher sensitivity under mild stimulation conditions compared with their sulfur-containing counterparts, and have therefore been widely employed in recent years for the construction of ROS-responsive material systems. − Such diselenide-containing materials have been reported to react with ROS and rapidly release their drug cargoes even at low concentrations of H2O2 (0.01% v/v).

Following this strategy, Xu et al. designed an orally administered diselenide-bridged hyaluronic acid nanogel (Figure a). This material achieves targeted accumulation through specific binding of hyaluronic acid to CD44-overexpressing inflammatory cells in colitis tissues. Upon ROS stimulation, the diselenide bonds undergo cleavage, enabling both direct ROS scavenging and activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, which upregulates antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, GSH, and GST while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. This system demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy in DSS- and TNBS-induced mouse models of colitis.

Furthermore, Zhou et al. incorporated diselenide bonds into a hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel (Figure b). Through remote ultrasound stimulation, the Se–Se bonds were triggered to undergo on-demand cleavage, enabling spatiotemporally controlled release of selenium. The released selenium was subsequently converted into bioactive selenoproteins within cells, which promoted cartilage matrix synthesis and inhibited catabolism via activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, while simultaneously scavenging ROS and restoring mitochondrial membrane potential. This approach effectively delayed disease progression in a rat model of osteoarthritis.

Notably, tellurium, positioned below selenium in the periodic table, possesses lower electronegativity, which typically renders ditelluride bonds more sensitive to oxidation than diselenide bonds. However, research on ROS-responsive materials based on ditelluride bonds remains relatively limited to date. As shown in Figure c, Zhang et al. introduced ditelluride bonds as reversible cross-linking units into hyperbranched polymers for tumor therapy. Upon exposure to high concentrations of glutathione within tumor cells, the ditelluride bonds undergo rapid cleavage, leading to dissociation of the nanostructure and enabling on-demand drug release.

Peroxalate Ester

Peroxalate esters represent a unique class of ROS-responsive materials characterized by the presence of a peroxy bond (−O–O−) within their molecular structure. Unlike the previously discussed boronic esters, thioethers, or diselenide bonds, peroxalate esters undergo a chemiluminescent reaction upon exposure to H2O2. The reaction mechanism proceeds via nucleophilic attack of H2O2 on the carbonyl carbon of the peroxalate ester, forming a dioxetanedione intermediate. The decomposition of this high-energy intermediate generates excited-state carbonyl species, which release energy as chemiluminescence upon relaxation to the ground state. − This unique chemiluminescent property endows peroxalate ester-based materials with dual functionality as both ROS-responsive drug delivery systems and real-time ROS detection platforms. ,

For instance, Song et al. developed a self-augmented ROS-responsive polymeric nanodrug incorporating peroxalate ester linkages for codelivery of paclitaxel (PTX) and the IDO inhibitor 1 MT (Figure a). In this system, ROS-triggered cleavage of the peroxalate ester bonds facilitated PTX release, while PTX-induced ROS generation further amplified nanocarrier disassembly, creating a positive feedback loop that enhanced immunogenic cell death and relieved immunosuppression. Similarly, Zhang et al. constructed a dual redox-responsive nanoparticle platform based on an l-cystine-derived biodegradable polymer containing both disulfide and peroxalate ester bonds (Figure b). Upon exposure to elevated GSH and ROS levels in the tumor microenvironment, the peroxalate ester bonds underwent cleavage with concomitant CO2 generation, enabling controlled release of docetaxel and significantly improving antitumor efficacy.

Ferrocene

The ROS-responsive mechanism of ferrocene is primarily based on the oxidation of its ferrous ion (Fe2+) to ferric ion (Fe3+) in the presence of ROS. , This oxidation converts ferrocene from its hydrophobic reduced state to a hydrophilic oxidized state (ferrocenium cation), thereby disrupting the structural stability of ferrocene-based nanoassemblies (e.g., micelles, vesicles) constructed through hydrophobic interactions, enabling controlled drug release or activation of probe signals. − Consequently, this mechanism has been extensively investigated.

First, Li et al. reported a ferrocene-based responsive micelle system (Figure a). Under ultrasound irradiation, the Fenton-like reaction between ferrocene and H2O2 was accelerated, significantly promoting the release of Fe2+ and the generation of hydroxyl radicals, while a sonosensitizer was incorporated to produce singlet oxygen. This combined effect not only directly induced apoptosis but also substantially depleted glutathione and thioredoxin, disrupting redox homeostasis. Moreover, the elevated Fe2+ level synergized with glutathione depletion to enhance lipid peroxidation, potently triggering ferroptosis. This synergistic sensitization between apoptosis and ferroptosis highlights the therapeutic value of ferrocene in achieving controlled Fe2+ release and ROS generation through ROS-responsive mechanisms for tumor therapy.

As shown in Figure b, Kim et al. developed ROS-sensitive ferrocene-based nanoparticles loaded with sulfasalazine for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis. In the high-ROS environment of inflamed joints, hydrophobic ferrocene (Fe2+) is oxidized to hydrophilic ferrocenium cations (Fe3+), leading to nanoparticle disassembly and enabling precise drug release. This study highlights the utility of ferrocene as a ROS-responsive switch for targeted delivery of anti-inflammatory drugs to oxidative stress microenvironments, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing systemic side effects.

Summary and Prospects

This article systematically reviews recent advances in ROS-scavenging and ROS-responsive biomaterials, with an emphasis on their design logic, mechanisms of action, and construction strategies. Despite the significant progress achieved in this field, several challenges remain to be addressed before these materials can be translated into clinical practice. First, the pathological microenvironment is often characterized by the simultaneous elevation of multiple ROS species. The interplay among these species can generate a self-amplifying “vicious cycle” of oxidative distress, leading to cumulative and often irreversible damage to proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, thereby driving the progression of a wide range of chronic diseases. A particularly alarming consequence is the oxidative burst from inflammatory cells, intended for microbial killing, inadvertently damages the surrounding healthy tissue leading to epithelial barrier dysfunction, impaired repair, and even mutational injury.

However, Current ROS-responsive systems are predominantly designed to respond to a single type of ROS, which may not fully capture the complexity of disease conditions. For example, H2O2 is the most frequently targeted ROS, appearing in over 80% of such systems, owing to its relative stability, pathologically elevated concentrations, and well-defined reactivity with boronic esters, thioketals, and peroxalate esters. Superoxide anion (O2 •–) is mainly targeted by nanozyme-based scavenging systems (e.g., CeO2 and Mn3O4 with SOD-like activity). Hydroxyl radical (•OH) often serves as a target for sacrificial scavenging materials (e.g., thioether-rich polymers). Singlet oxygen (1O2) primarily appears in photodynamic therapy. The development of multi-ROS-responsive materials capable of sensing and responding to a broader spectrum of ROS holds promise for achieving more precise and robust therapeutic outcomes.

Second, the oxidative stress microenvironment frequently coexists with other pathological cues, such as acidic pH, hypoxia, or overexpressed enzymes, which may vary dynamically during disease progression. In the tumor microenvironment, multiple stresses drive oxidative metabolic abnormalities, markedly diminishing immunotherapy response and worsening patient survival. In diabetic foot ulcers, hyperglycemia-induced ROS not only directly injure the endothelium but also impair the M1-to-M2 macrophage transition; together with superimposed multidrug-resistant infections, this cascade can necessitate lower-limb amputation in up to 20% of patients. Integrating ROS responsiveness with other stimulus-responsive mechanisms (e.g., pH, enzymes, or external stimuli such as light or ultrasound) could enable synergistic, multimodal regulation and more precise spatiotemporal control over therapeutic interventions. Such multiresponsive platforms are particularly attractive for complex diseases where multiple pathological signals are intertwined.

Finally, targeted delivery remains a critical factor for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. Although some ROS-responsive systems have demonstrated passive or active targeting capabilities, further refinement of targeting strategies, such as ligand–receptor interactions, cell-membrane camouflaging, or disease-microenvironment recognition, will be essential to enhance lesion-specific accumulation and reduce systemic toxicity. While many ROS-modulating materials exhibit favorable short-term safety profiles, their long-term fate, potential immunogenicity, and metabolic pathways remain underexplored. Standardized evaluation systems and more rigorous preclinical studies will be critical to advancing these materials toward clinical application.

In summary, ROS-scavenging and ROS-responsive biomaterials represent a versatile and powerful platform for the treatment of oxidative stress-related diseases. Future efforts should focus on integrating multi-ROS responsiveness, multistimulus synergy, and enhanced targeting capabilities into next-generation material systems, while placing greater emphasis on translational research to bridge the gap between material innovation and clinical implementation.
