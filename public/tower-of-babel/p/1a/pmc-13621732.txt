Comparative and population genomics analyses of eared pheasants inhabiting highly varying altitudes
Authors: Siwen Wu, Kun Wang, Xuehai Ge, Sisi Yuan, Dong-Dong Wu, Changrong Ge, Junjing Jia, Zhengchang Su, Tengfei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxygen pressure varies dramatically with altitudes on Earth; however, humans and animals thrive at almost all altitudes.ResultsTo better understand genetic basis underlying adaptation of closely related species to varying altitudes, we annotated and compared the genome of a white eared pheasant (WT) (Crossoptilon crossoptilon) inhabiting high altitudes and the genome of a brown eared pheasant (BR) (C. mantchuricum) inhabiting low altitudes. Moreover, we compared genetic variations in populations of WT and BR as well as of blue eared pheasants (BL) (C. auritum) inhabiting intermediate altitudes, and identified thousands of selective sweeps in each species.ConclusionsIntriguingly, the unique genes and pseudogenes in the genomes of WT and BR converge on the same set of altitude adaptation-related pathways of four functional categories as genes in selective sweeps in each species. Thus, these species appear to adapt to highly varying altitudes by diverging selection on the same traits via loss-of-function mutations and fine-tuning genes in common pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-12998-z.Keywords: Eared pheasant, Altitudes adaptation, Loss-of-function mutations, Selective sweeps, Fine-tuning genes

Background

Faunas have gone through an evolutionary process to gain physiological feature for the adaptation of highly varying oxygen abundance. Populations of humans [1–3] and many animal species that are naturally adapted to lowlands thrive in highland environments, such as Tibetan chickens [4–6], Tibetan pigs [7, 8], Tibetan goats [9], Tibetan sheep [10, 11], Tibetan mastiffs [12], Tibet cattle [13, 14], ground tits [15], and yaks [16], to name a few. The major challenge faced by these populations of humans and animals in highland environments is the low oxygen pressure-induced stress on their physiological systems [17–19]. The long-term adaptation to low oxygen pressure environments has resulted in genetic changes in these human [1–3, 14, 17, 20–27] and animal [4–7, 10–16, 19, 28–31] populations. Adaptive mutations of many genes of humans [2, 14, 17, 18, 26, 32, 33] and animals [16, 19, 30, 31, 33, 34] have been linked to highland environments. Moreover, introgression has been shown as a major mechanism for human [1, 3, 27] and domesticated animals [9, 10, 13] to adapt to highland environments. Despite these great progresses, the understanding of human and animal adaptation to highly varying altitudes is still limited [33]. Particularly, some closely related animal species can rapidly adapt to highly varying altitudes, thus, it is interesting to understand the genetic basis of their adaptation from low- to intermediate- and high-altitude environments.

Crossoptilon, belonging to the Phasianidae family in the Galliformes order, is a rare but important genus endemic in China [35]. There are four species in the Crossoptilon genus, including Tibetan eared pheasant (TB) (C. harmani), white eared pheasant (WT) (C. crossoptilon), blue eared pheasant (BL) (C. auritum) and brown eared pheasant (BR) (C. mantchuricum) [35, 36]. These species are found in coniferous forests, mixed broadleaf-conifer forests and alpine scrubs in various parts of China with very different altitudes ranging from 800 m to 5,000 m [35, 37–42]. TB and WT are believed to be conspecifics, diverging about 0.5 million years ago [43]. They sympatrically inhabit montane forest at a high altitude of 3,000–5,000 m [35, 36, 38, 40–42]. TB is distributed in southeastern Tibet and adjacent northern India (3,000–5,000 m) [35, 38, 41], while WT is found in Qinghai, Sichuan, Yunnan and Tibet (3,000–4,300 m) [35, 40]. BL and BR are closely related, diverging about 0.3 million years ago [43], but allopatrically inhabit different areas [35, 36]. BL is only encountered in the mountains of Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan and Ningxia, at an intermediate altitude of 2,000–3,000 m [38, 44], while BR is mainly distributed in mountains in Shanxi and Hebei and near Beijing, at an altitude of 800–2,600 m [37, 39, 42]. Thus, WT, BL and BR are excellent models to study the genetic basis for closely related species to adapt to highly varying altitudes.

Recently, Wang et al. [45] assembled a BR genome and re-sequenced three BR subpopulations and a BL population. Although they provide new insights into genomic changes during the course of BR’s long-term population decline [45], genetic basis underlying the adaptation of the BR and BL to low (800–2,600 m) and intermediated altitudes (1500–3000 m), respectively, were not investigated. Moreover, with a small contig N50 (0.11 Mb), this BR genome assembly has limitations as a reference genome to study the Crossoptilon species. To fill the gap, we recently sequenced and assembled the genome of a WT individual at chromosome-level with a contig N50 of 19.6 Mb, and re-sequenced 10 WT individuals and a BL individual [46]. Combining our data in WT and BL [46] with those of Wang et al. in BL and BR [45], we were well positioned to investigate the genetic bases of these closely related avian species to adapt to highly varying altitudes using comparative and population genomics approaches. We found that the three eared pheasant species might adapt to highly varying altitudes or other aspects of their distinct ecological niches by loss-of-function mutations and fine-tuning genes involved in the same sets of pathways to meet their varying demands for cardiovascular functions, energy metabolisms, neuronal functions, and immunity.

Results

Annotation of protein-coding genes in the WT and BR genomes

To see whether gene contents are related to adaption of closely related avian species to different altitudes, we first annotated the assembled WT [46] and BR [45] genomes using a combination of reference- and RNA-based methods (Materials and Methods). Using the coding DNA sequences (CDSs) of 53 well-represented Aves (Table S1) as the templates and a total of 23 RNA-seq datasets from 20 tissues of WT individuals and three tissues of BR individuals (Materials and Methods), we annotated 16,377 and 15,410 protein-coding genes as well as 1,519 and 1,976 pseudogenes in the WT and BR genomes, respectively (Table 1). The vast majority (15,565 and 14,727) of these genes and all the pseudogenes (1,519 and 1,976) in the WT and BR genomes were predicted based on the CDSs in the reference genomes (reference-based), while a small portion (812 and 683) of the genes were predicted based on the RNA-seq datasets in the two species (RNA-based) (Table 1). To evaluate the effects of different genome assembly quality on the annotation of genes and pseudogenes, we aligned the contigs of the WT assembly with those of the BR assembly using minimap2 [47]. We found that 79.5% of contigs in the WT assembly can be aligned with one or more contigs in BR, and 77.4% of contigs in the BR assembly can be aligned with one or more contigs in WT (Figure S1a). At the nucleotide level, 97.5% of sites in the WT assembly can be aligned with those of the BR assembly, and 98.4% of sites in the BR assembly can be aligned with those of the WT assembly (Figure S1b). Moreover, 78.6% and 81.2% of pseudogenes in WT and BR, respectively, are located in the middle 80% regions of their assembled contigs, and the remaining 21.4% and 18.8%, respectively, are almost evenly located in the 10% 5’-end and 10% 3’-end regions of their contigs (Figure S1c). Furthermore, 74.3% and 77.8% of missing genes in WT and BR, respectively, have parental genes located in the middle 80% regions of contigs in BR and WT, while the remaining 25.7% and 22.2%, respectively, are almost evenly located in the 10% 5’-end and 10% 3’-end regions of their contigs (Figure S1d). These results suggest that the predicted pseudogenes and missing genes are unlikely due to their biased locations to the borders of contigs that might not be well-assembled.

To evaluate the effects of the unbalanced RNA-seq datasets on the numbers of annotated genes in the two genomes, we repeated the annotation process by 100 times with each time using the CDSs in the 53 Aves genomes for reference-based annotations and a total of six RNA-seq datasets for RNA-based annotation. Of the six RNA-seq datasets, three were those from the BR tissues and the other three were randomly selected in each annotation from the 20 datasets of the WT tissues. As shown in Table S2, in each of the 100 times of annotations, the total number of annotated genes in WT is larger than that in BR, which is mainly due to the larger number of genes annotated by the reference-based approach in WT (15,565) than in BR (14,727). On the other hand, the two genomes have a similar average number (350 vs. 355) of genes annotated by the RNA-based approach (p-value = 0.33, two-tailed t-test), suggesting that different numbers of genes and pseudogenes annotated in the two assemblies are unlikely due to the unbalanced RNA-seq datasets in the two species. Moreover, in 73 of the 100 times of annotations, the BR genome has even more RNA-annotated genes than does the WT genome, suggesting that the assembly quality of the CDSs in BR is good enough for finding genes. However, when the union of the 100 annotations is taken, WT still has more RNA-based annotated genes (812 vs. 683). Taken together, these results suggest that the difference in the numbers of genes in the two species is not mainly due to the difference in the quality of genome assemblies and/or the bias of RNA-seq datasets.

Most of the reference-based genes (14,815 and 14,518) in both species have an intact open reading frame (ORF) (intact genes), while the remaining small numbers (750 and 209) contain either premature stop codon or ORF shift mutations, which are not supported by the corresponding short DNA reads, we thus called them partially supported genes, as the mutations might be caused by sequencing errors, particularly, in long reads. Vast majority of the intact genes (99.01%), partially supported genes (97.73%), and pseudogenes (95.13%) in WT were transcribed in at least one of the 20 tissues examined (Table S3). These percentages (96.54%, 93.78% and 90.84%) are smaller in BR (Table S4) as RNA-seq data from only three tissues are available [45]. Most (751 and 631) of the RNA-based genes in both species can be mapped to the NT database [48] (NT-supported new genes), suggesting that they are likely true genes. The remaining small numbers (61 and 52) that cannot be mapped to the NT database are likely novel genes (Table 1).

We compared the RNA-based genes annotated in the WT and BR assemblies, and found 450 pairs with an identity > 96.8%, comprising 55.4% and 65.9% of their RNA-based genes, respectively. They are likely true orthologous genes in both species, as the same patterns of transcriptional noise are unlikely to occur in two different species. Moreover, most of the RNA-based genes in both species (Tables S3 and S4) were varyingly expressed in different tissues with RNA-seq data available (Fig. 1a-d), indicating that they might be authentic and functional. In addition, we randomly selected 17 of the 812 RNA-based genes in WT and measured their expression levels in 16 tissues using RT-qPCR. We found that 15 (88.24%) of them were transcribed in at least one of the tissues. Of these 15 transcribed genes, five were putative novel genes (Table S5, Fig. 1e). Notably, the expression patterns of some genes were different from those seen in the RNA-seq data, which might be due to the different sensitivity of RT-qPCR and RNA-seq methods (Fig. 1f). Nonetheless, these results further suggest that most of the RNA-based genes at least in WT are likely authentic. Moreover, we annotated 12 and eight in WT and BR, respectively, of the 274 presumed missing genes in avian species [49] (Table S6).

Most pseudogenes found in the WT and BR genomes are unprocessed and unitary and thus might lose gene functions

Vast majority (for WT, 1,310 or 86.2%; for BR, 1,701 or 86.1%) of pseudogenes in both WT and BR genomes are unprocessed, i.e., they arose due to direct mutations in the parent genes. We failed to find any functional copy of these unprocessed pseudogenes; thus, they are also unitary [50]. To see whether the pseudogenization alleles in the 1,519 and 1,976 pseudogenes in the WT and BR genomes, respectively, are under selection, we mapped the short reads from 10 WT individuals and 41 BR individuals to the corresponding genomes (Materials and Methods). We found that most of the first pseudogenization alleles (the pseudogenization mutation nearest to the 5’-end of the parent gene) along the parental genes were fixed in the population of the two species (Fig. 2a and b), suggesting that these pseudogenization events might be under positive selection and thus contribute to the phenotypic traits of the two species. Interestingly, the synonymous mutations in true genes in the two species, which are generally assumed to be selectively neutral, are largely uniformly distributed along the CDSs, except at the two ends, where the numbers of synonymous mutations decrease (Fig. 2c and d), consistent with an earlier report in chickens [51]. The reduced synonymous mutations at the two ends of CDSs suggest that they might harbor functional elements not related to their coding functions, such as transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory elements [52]. In contrast, pseudogenization mutations are strongly biased to the 5’-ends and 3’-ends in both species (Fig. 2c and d). For example, 37.7% and 11.2% of the first pseudogenization mutations in BR are located in the 10% 5’-ends and 10% 3’-ends of the parental CDSs lengths, and these numbers in WT are 38.1% and 12.2% (Fig. 2c and d). The same phenomenon was also found in the other species such as human [51, 53] and chickens [51]. Therefore, the strongly biased pseudogenization mutations to the 5’-end and 3’-end in the two species also suggest that they are under positive selection. We observed there were more ORF shift mutations than nonsense mutations along the parental CDSs in both species (Fig. 2e and f). However, the 10% 5’-ends had the highest ratio of ORF shift mutations over nonsense mutations (3.03 in BR and 3.22 in WT), followed by the 80% middle (2.75 in BR and 2.78 in WT) and 10% 3’-ends (1.31 in BR and 1.66 in WT).

Of the 1,519 pseudogenes in the WT genome, 727 (47.9%) have alternative transcripts, but only 10 (1.4%) have functional alternative transcripts (Table S7). Of the 1,976 pseudogenes in BR, 1,712 have alternative transcripts, but only 17 (1.0%) have functional alternative transcripts (Table S8). These results suggest that most pseudogenes do not have functional alternative transcripts and thus have lost the functions of their parent genes. Of course, these data cannot exclude the possibility that a pseudogene may have non-protein-coding functions.

Unique genes and pseudogenes in WT and BR might be related to their adaptation to distinct ecological niches

We found that the WT and BR genomes shared 14,507 of their genes (88.6% and 94.1%) and 1,040 of their pseudogenes (68.5% and 52.6%) (Table 1). Meanwhile, the WT genome contains 1,870 unique genes and 479 unique pseudogenes, while the BR genome harbors 903 unique genes and 936 unique pseudogenes (Tables S9-S12, Fig. 3a). Of the 1,870 unique genes in the WT genome, 658 (35.2%) are unique pseudogenes and 1,212 (64.8%) are missing in the BR genome. Of the 936 unique pseudogenes in BR, 658 (70.3%) are unique genes and 278 (29.7%) are missing in the WT genome (Fig. 3a). Moreover, of the 903 unique genes in the BR genome, 173 (19.2%) are unique pseudogenes and 730 (80.8%) are missing in the WT genome. Of the 479 unique pseudogenes in the WT genome, 173 (36.1%) are unique genes and 306 (63.9%) are missing in the BR genome (Fig. 3a). There are a total of 3,357 genes that either are unique genes or become unique pseudogenes in the WT or BR genomes. To see whether the unique genes and unique pseudogenes in each species are related to their unique traits and reflect the evolutionary pressure received from their niches, we assigned GO terms to the unique genes and unique pseudogenes in each species based on their homologs in chickens or humans using the PANTHER pathway database containing 177 pathways [54]. The unique genes and unique pseudogenes in WT are involved in 69 and 25 biological pathways (Table S13), respectively, while those in BR are involved in 27 and 59 biological pathways, respectively (Table S13). Interestingly, we found that the vast majority of these pathways in WT (82 of 94, 87.2%) and BR (74 of 86, 86.0%) were involved in at least one of the four physiological functional categories including cardiovascular functions, energy metabolism, neuronal functions and immunity (Table S13, Fig. 3b), and that they were significantly enriched (p-value = 3.47e-4, test) for 133 (75.1%) pathways related to the four functional categories in the PANTHER database [54].

It has been shown that alterations in these functions are critical for animals to adapt to different altitudes [33]. Thus, the unique genes and loss of functions of the missing genes and pseudogenes in BR and WT might play a role in the adaption of the two species to their strikingly different altitudes. A few examples of such genes and pseudogenes are detailed below and more examples are given in Supplementary Note 1.

Cardiovascular functions

As shown in Fig. 3b, unique gene PIK3C2G in BR is pseudogenized in WT. PIK3C2G encodes a class II PI3-kinase involved in signaling upstream of the HIF (hypoxia-inducible factor) activation pathway [55–57] in addition to the angiogenesis pathway (Supplementary Note 1). High altitude adapted animals and humans experience chronic hypoxia [58]. Unlike acute hypoxia, excessive HIF signaling under chronic hypoxia becomes harmful [59, 60]. Thus, losing PIK3C2G could prevent overactivation of hypoxia responses, reduce unnecessary erythrocyte production, and limit oxidative damage from chronic stress. In agreement with this, high altitude adapted animals and humans show selection for attenuated, not enhanced, HIF activity though loss-of-function mutations [61, 62].

Energy metabolism

As shown in Fig. 3b, unique gene PFKM (encoding a phosphofructokinase of muscle type) involved in glycolysis in BR is pseudogenized in WT. PFKM catalyzes one of the most important rate-limiting steps of glycolysis: the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, which is essential for breaking down glucose to produce ATP. The loss of the gene in WT might be a specialized strategy for the bird to survive the extreme physiological stress at high altitudes to prevent metabolic acidosis. More specifically, under chronic hypoxia, animals typically rely more on anaerobic glycolysis to produce energy [63]. However, heavy reliance on this pathway leads to the rapid buildup of lactic acid, while clearing lactic acid is difficult, leading to muscle fatigue and metabolic acidosis [63]. Thus, by pseudogenizing PFKM, WT likely slows down the glycolytic flux in its muscles, preventing the toxic buildup of lactate and allowing it to maintain sustained physical activity. Moreover, glycolysis is relatively inefficient, producing only 2 ATP molecules per glucose molecule. Thus, the pseudogenization of PFKM forces the body to prioritize alternative metabolic pathways that may be more oxygen-efficient or better suited for the extreme cold and low-oxygen conditions at high altitudes. It is important to note that WT has not lost the ability to process glucose entirely. WT has other isoforms of this enzyme (like PFKL in the liver or PFKP in platelets). The specific loss of the muscle-type (PFKM) suggests this adaptation is specifically tuned to how their muscles operate during thin-air exertion.

Neuronal functions

As shown in Fig. 3b, unique genes CAMK2G, GRIK1, SHANK2, GRIN2C and CAMK2B in WT, which are missing (SHANK2 and CAMK2B) or pseudogenized (CAMK2G, GRIK1 and GRIN2C) in BR, are involved in the ionotropic glutamate receptor pathway [64]. CAMK2G (Calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II gamma), CAMK2B (Calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II beta) are also involved in the inflammation mediated by chemokine and cytokine signaling pathway (Supplementary Note 1). These genes are the building blocks of excitatory signaling in the brain [64]. More specifically, GRIK1 and GRIN2C are glutamate receptors (Kainate and NMDA types, respectively), and the former is involved in synaptic plasticity and the tuning of neuronal circuits [65]; while the latter is essential for learning, memory, and specialized brain functions like motor coordination [64]. SHANK2 (T-cell specific adaptor protein, TSAd) is a master scaffold protein that physically holds these receptors in place at the synapse [66]. CAMK2B and CAMK2G translate calcium signals into long-term changes in neuronal activity [67]. Maintaining these genes in WT is critical for hypoxia-tolerant neurology as in low-oxygen environments, and the brain is at risk of excitotoxicity [68]. Thus, the high-altitude specialist WT retains these genes to ensure that their brains can signal efficiently without burning out or requiring excessive oxygen. Such constraints might be relaxed in low-altitude specialist BR, and thus, their functions were lost. Alternatively, these genes were lost in BR due to genetic drift in the recent bottleneck [45].

Immunity

As shown in Fig. 3b, unique gene ARRB1 in BR, which is missing in WT, is also involved in inflammation mediated by chemokine and cytokine signaling pathway, in addition to the angiotensin II-stimulated signaling through G proteins and beta-arrestin pathway (Supplementary Note 1). ARRB1 (arrestin beta 1) normally acts as a “scaffold” or “adapter” that desensitizes G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and it essentially turns off the signal after a receptor has been activated [69]. In T cells, ARRB1 is involved in the initiation and regulation of the immune response [70]. It helps coordinate the complex signaling required for T cells to recognize and attack pathogens. The loss of ARRB1 in the high-altitude-adapted WT is likely an adaptive strategy to manage chronic inflammation and energy expenditure to prevent immune overdrive. At high altitudes, the body is under constant stress from hypoxia and UV radiation, which can lead to chronic, low-level inflammation [71]. By losing a regulator like ARRB1, WT may have modified its T cell sensitivity. It has been reported that the loss of an arrestin can lead to a more sustained, but potentially less explosive immune response, preventing the bird from wasting energy on excessive inflammation in a resource-scarce environment [70]. Moreover, ARRB1 can also regulates many other GPCRs, including those involved in heart rate and metabolism [69]. As we discussed with ADRB1 earlier, WT may benefit from “dampening” certain physiological responses. Losing ARRB1 might prevent the rapid “resetting” of specific protective pathways, allowing the bird to stay in a “hypoxia-ready” state for longer periods without the signal being terminated. In the temperate, low-altitude environment, BR faces a higher variety of pathogens (bacteria, viruses, and parasites) that thrive in warmer air, requiring precise immune response, and thus, it is essential for BR to have a fully functional ARRB1. It ensures that T cell activation is precise turning on quickly to fight infection and turning off quickly once the threat is gone. Furthermore, in a resource-rich environment, the metabolic cost of maintaining these complex regulatory switches is easily covered by available food, so there is no evolutionary pressure to get rid of them.

The populations of the three species are strongly structured

To further understand the genetic basis of altitude adaptation, we next compared the SNPs of 10 WT individuals, 41 BR individuals and 12 BL individuals (Table 2). Figure 4a shows the geographical locations where the populations were sampled. Specifically, the three BR subpopulations were from Shaanxi province, Shanxi province, and Hebei province & Beijing metropolitan, respectively [45]; the BL population were from Gansu province [45]; and the WT population were from Yunnan province. Using our assembled WT genome [72] as the reference, we identified SNPs in the BL, BR and WT populations (Table 2). Based on the bi-allelic SNPs (16 million) in the populations of the three species (Table 2), we performed principal component analysis (PCA), and found that individuals of the same species form distinct clusters (Fig. 4b). Notably, each of the three BR subpopulations forms a distinct compact subcluster, which is consistent with the previous result [45]. We also run the admixture algorithm [73] on the SNPs to infer their ancestral relationships. For K = 2, individuals of BL and BR formed a uniformly colored cluster, while WT individuals formed the other cluster sharing small portions of ancestries with the former two species (Fig. 4c), suggesting that BL and BR were derived from the same ancestor, while WT branched out earlier. This result is consistent with previous phylogenetic analysis that BL and BR are more closely related to each other [43]. For K = 3, individuals of each species formed an almost uniformly colored cluster, indicating their distinct recent ancestries.

To see possible gene introgression among the three species, we calculated the D-statistic [74] and f3-statistic [75]. We obtained a D value of 0.57 (z-score = 36.18, and p-value = 2.3e-16) using a Daweishan chicken population as the outgroup (O) on the rooted tree (((BR,BL),WT),O), suggesting introgression between BL and WT [74]. The f3 values and associated z-scores for the three choices of (target; source 1, source 2) are shown in Table 3. The significant (z-score = −26.45) negative f3 value (−0.0586) for (target = BL; source 1 = WT, source 2 = BR) suggests introgression in BL from both WT and BR [75]. However, the positive f3 values for the other two scenarios (Table 3) could not indicate gene introgression in BR from WT and BL, and in WT from BR and BL [75]. Thus, gene introgression might play a role in the evolution of BL, but this might not be the case for BR and WT.

Genes in selective sweeps of each species might be related to their distinct ecological niches

To identify genomic regions and genes that might be related to the adaptation of the three species to their distinct ecological niches, particularly, to different altitudes, we identified selective sweeps in each species by comparing its SNPs with those of the other two species using both the genetic differentiation () and difference in nucleotide diversity () parameters [76–79]. To rigorously evaluate the statistical significance of the and values, we normalized them and ) using a permutation test [80] (Materials and Methods). Figure 5a-c show the distribution of Z and as well as their values for each 40 kb genome windows with a 20 kb-step size for the three pairwise comparisons. We consider a window with > 2.33 (P = 0.01) and > 1.64 (P = 0.05) or < −1.64 (P = 0.05) as a selective sweep (Fig. 5a-c). Figure 6a-f show the Manhattan plots of Z and scores for the three pairwise comparisons. Since adjacent selective sweeps can overlap with one another, we merged the overlapping ones as a discrete selection sweep (DSS) in each species. For the BL vs. BR comparison, we identified 208 and 638 selective sweeps, and 128 and 463 DSSs containing 143 and 474 genes in BL and BR, respectively (Tables 4 and S14). For the BL vs. WT comparison, we found 92 and 274 selective sweeps, and 72 and 181 DSSs containing 67 and 167 genes in BL and WT, respectively (Tables 4 and S15). For the BR vs. WT comparison, there are 306 and 359 selective sweeps, and 184 and 188 DSSs containing 196 and 167 genes in BR and WT, respectively (Tables 4 and S16). We also used RaiSD [81] to confirm the predicted DSSs. As shown in Supplementary Table S17, from 85.2% (for BL vs. WT comparison) to 94.7% (for BL vs. BR comparison) of the DSSs overlap those predicted by RAiSD (defined as the overlapping part between a predicted DSS and that of RaiSD [81] is at least 50% of the shorter one). Thus, the results of the two methods are highly consistent.

To see whether genes in the selective sweeps are under positive or negative selections, we calculated the ratio of the number of nonsynonymous mutations over the number of synonymous mutations (dN/dS) for each gene in the selective sweeps for each pair of comparison. As shown in Table S17, for BL, of the five genes with dS > 0 in the DSSs for the BL vs. BR comparison, one (20.0%) is under positive selection (dN/dS > 1), and four (80.0%) are under negative selection (dN/dS < 1); of the 69 genes with dS > 0 in the DSSs for the BL vs. WT comparison, 14 (20.3%) are subject to positive selection (dN/dS > 1), and 51 (73.9%) are under negative selection (dN/dS < 1). For BR, of the 143 genes with dS > 0 in the DSSs for the BL vs. BR comparison, 13 (9.1%) are under positive selection (dN/dS > 1), and 114 (79.7%) are under negative selection (dN/dS < 1); of the 60 genes with dS > 0 in the DSSs for the BR vs. WT comparison, two (3.3%) are subject to positive selection (dN/dS > 1), and 55 (91.7%) are under negative selection (dN/dS < 1). For WT, of the 227 genes with dS > 0 in the DSSs for the BL vs. WT comparison, 33 (14.5%) are under positive selection (dN/dS > 1), and 165 (72.7%) are under negative selection (dN/dS < 1); of the 153 genes with dS > 0 in the DSSs for the BR vs. WT comparison, 25 (15.7%) are subject to positive selection (dN/dS > 1), and 113 (73.9%) are under negative selection (dN/dS < 1). Thus, most of the genes in the DSSs are under either positive or negative selection, and there are more genes under negative selection than under positive selection. The same results are obtained when the McDonald–Kreitman test was used to evaluate the selection modes of the genes in the DSSs for each pair of comparison (Methods), although a smaller total number of genes were detected to be under positive or negative selection (Table 17).

Genes in the DSSs of each comparison might be related to adaptation to their different ecological niches. About 55% of genes in these selective sweeps identified in each comparison do not harbor fixed nonsynonymous mutations. Thus, it is interesting to investigate whether natural selection might act upon their regulatory sequences, thereby changing their expression levels. The remaining about 45% of the genes contain fixed nonsynonymous mutations that might alter the functions of encoded proteins. For example, gene MYO3B located in a selective sweep in the BR population based on the BL vs. BR comparison (Fig. 5a) contains a fixed G-to-A nonsynonymous mutation in exon 7, leading to an Arg-to-Lys substitution. Since the AGA codon for Arg at this position is shared by WT, BL, quail (Coturnix japonica) and human (Homo sapiens) (Fig. 5d), the G-to-A mutation is BR lineage specific, which might be related to their unique traits. It is reported that gene MYO3B is required for normal cochlear hair bundle development and hearing [82]. As BR has been hunted by human for more than 2,000 years [42], it is likely that the mutation might increase their auditory sensation, thereby helping them to escape earlier when perceiving hunters approaching. It is interesting to investigate the effects of the mutation on the auditory functions of BR and its implication for natural selection and adaptation. Alternatively, the fixation of the A allele in BR might be resulted from genetic drift due to its recent population bottleneck [45]. Moreover, gene PTPRC located in a selective sweep in the WT populations based on both the BL vs. WT and the BR vs. WT comparisons contains a fixed A-to-G nonsynonymous mutation in exon 7, resulting in a Lys-to-Arg substitution. As the AAA codon for Lys at the position in PTPRC is shared by BL, BR, quail and human (Fig. 5d), the mutation is WT lineage specific. Interestingly, we found that the same mutation existed in the PTPRC genes of Lance-tailed manakin (Chiroxiphia lanceolata) and White-ruffed manakin (Corapipo altera) (Fig. 5d), both are altitudinal migrants living in subtropical Andes highlands [83], due probably to convergent evolution. It has been reported that PTPRC is related to the T cell activation [84]. Therefore, it is highly likely that the mutation in the PTPRC gene in WT and manakins might help them adapt to high altitudes where there are fewer pathogens, and thus, the pressure on the immune system in these species might be relaxed.

Genes under selection tend to be involved in the same altitude adaptation-related pathways in the three species

There are a total of 1,106 genes that are in a selective sweep predicted based on at least one of the three comparisons. They share only 105 genes with the 3,529 unique genes and unique pseudogenes in the WT and BR genomes (Fig. 7a). Genes with GO term assignments in BL selective sweeps are involved in 48 and 17 pathways for BL vs. BR (Table S14) and BL vs. WT (Table S15) comparisons, respectively; those in BR selective sweeps are involved in 56 and 45 pathways for BL vs. BR (Table S14) and BR vs. WT (Table S16) comparisons, respectively; and those in WT selective sweeps are involved in 34 and 36 pathways for BL vs. BR (Table S14) and BL vs. WT (Table S15) comparisons, respectively. The vast majority (46/48 = 95.8%, 16/17 = 94.1%; 51/56 = 91.1%, 40/45 = 88.9%; 32/34 = 94.1%, 34/36 = 94.4%) of these pathways are involved in at least one of the four functional categories (Tables S14-S16) as are the unique genes and unique pseudogenes with GO term assignments in the WT and BR genomes (Table S13), although the two sets only share four genes (Fig. 7b). These pathways are significantly enriched (p-value = 3.48e-10, test) for the 133 ones involved in the four functional categories in PANTHER pathways database [54].

For example, selection on cardiovascular function-related genes MAPK1 in BL might be related to its adaptation to intermediate altitudes, while selection on neuronal function related gene PRKAR2B might be beneficial for BL to survive in its ecological niches (Fig. 7b). Moreover, selection on cardiovascular (PLA2G4A, ITPR2), immunity (ITPR2), energy metabolism (PRKCD) and neuronal function (SLC17A6) related genes in BR might be beneficial for its adaptation to low altitude (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, selection on the hypoxia response (PPARG) related genes in WT might help it adapt to the high-altitude niches, while selection on immunity (PPP3CB, MAPK8 and PTPRC) and neuronal function (GRIN3A) related genes WT might be beneficial for its adaptation to high altitudes (Fig. 7b). More examples of genes in the selective sweeps, which are involved in altitude adaptation-related pathways, are given in Supplementary Note 1.

Discussion

To better understand the genetic bases of adaptation of closely related eared pheasant species to highly varying altitudes, we combined our own published data in WT and BL [46] with those in BR and BL published by Wang et al. [45]. We first annotated the assembled WT [46] and BR [45] genomes, and compared their gene compositions. We identified similar numbers of protein-coding genes in the WT (16,447) and BR (15,410) genomes to those annotated in other avian species [85], but surprisingly large numbers of pseudogenes in them (1,519 in WT and 1,976 in BR), not previously reported in other avian genomes, to our best knowledge. We provided evidence that most pseudogenization mutations are under positive selection and the resulting pseudogenes might have lost protein-coding functions, although our data could not exclude the possibility that some transcribed pseudogenes might have other non-protein-coding functions [86, 87].

We found that many unique genes in one species (WT or BR) were often either missing or pseudogenized in the other species (BR or WT), and they mainly affect pathways related to four functional categories: cardiovascular, neuronal, energy metabolic, and immune functions. This finding is quite interesting but not very surprising, since cardiovascular [88, 89], neuronal [90, 91], and metabolic [92] functions are highly sensitive to blood oxygen levels which is directly affected by oxygen pressures at varying altitudes. Moreover, varying pathogens and ultraviolet radiations at varying altitudes might require different immune responses to infections and DNA damages [93–95]. Physiological systems often contain both positive and negative regulatory pathways to achieve a homeostasis of the functions. Thus, both the presence of a gene involved in the positive pathway and the loss of a gene involved in the negative pathway can enhance the relevant function. Conversely, both the presence of a gene involved in the negative pathway and the loss of gene involved in positive pathway can suppress the relevant functions. Loss of a specific gene in a pathway might fine tune its function in a way that renders the species to adapt its unique enrolments. More specifically, presence or loss of genes in WT might lead to changes in these four functional categories that are beneficial to its adaptation to high altitude (Fig. 3b), as have been reported for other high-altitude adapted species and populations of humans and animals [4, 7, 11, 15, 18, 19, 22, 23, 31]. And loss or presence of the same genes in BR might result in opposite changes of these four functional categories that are beneficial to its adaptation to low altitude (Fig. 3b). For example, we found that gene ADRB1 in BR, involved in the beta1/2 adrenergic receptor signaling pathway, is pseudogenized in WT. ADRB1 is the primary receptor of adrenaline and noradrenaline in the heart [96]. The activation of the pathway by binding of ADRB1 to adrenaline and/or noradrenaline released under stress increases heart rate, and contractility and energy demand of the heart [96]. In a chronic hypoxia environment, having a highly sensitive cardiac accelerator like ADRB1 can actually be detrimental if a bird gets startled, since its heart rate might spike to a level that the available oxygen cannot support. This leads to cardiac hypoxia and potential heart failure [96]. Thus, by pseudogenizing ADRB1, WT may have more stable heart rate, and likely remains less prone to dangerous, oxygen-consuming spikes. In the oxygen-rich lowlands, the heart function is less limited by oxygen supply. Thus, BR being able to rapidly spike its heart rate is an advantage for escaping predators or engaging in high-intensity flight. Moreover, as oxygen is plentiful, there is no risk of the heart running out oxygen during these bursts of activity. Therefore, natural selection has kept the gene ADRB1 fully functional in BR.

Next, we compared the SNPs in the WT, BL and BR populations. Interestingly, we found that genes in the selective sweeps in each species are mainly involved in pathways of the four functional categories as are the unique genes and pseudogenes in the WT and BR genomes, even though the two sets of genes have little overlaps (Fig. 7a). Most genes in the selective sweeps do not have amino acid-altering mutations, although some may have missense mutations such as MYO3B in BR and PTPRC in WT (Fig. 5d), become pseudogenes such as CACNB3 in BR, or be missing such as PRKCD in BR. These results suggest that natural selection might act on their cis-regulatory sequences, thereby altering their expression levels in relevant tissues. Unfortunately, the lack of gene expression data in matched tissues in the three species prevents us from validating this possibility. Thus, it is interesting to investigate the roles of cis-regulatory sequences in evolution and adaptation of the birds. Nonetheless, it appears that natural selection on these genes might be related to the adaptation of the species to different niches, particularly highly varying altitudes. For example, in the BL vs. BR comparison, gene MAPK1 in BL is included in a selective sweep. This gene is involved in multiple pathways in the cardiovascular system and the immune system (Fig. 7b). It is likely that selection on MAPK1 might be related to the adaptation of BL to intermediate altitude. In the WT vs. BL comparison, gene PPARG in WT is included in a selective sweep, and the gene is involved in the HIF pathway [97] (Fig. 7b). Selection on the gene might be related to the adaptation of WT to low oxygen pressure at high altitudes.

It is not surprising that the unique genes/pseudogenes in WT and BR and genes in the selective sweeps of BL, BR and WT converge on the same sets of pathways involved in cardiovascular, energy metabolic, neuronal and immune functions. On the one hand, high altitude niches with low oxygen pressure, low temperature and less availability of food would pose a direct pressure on WT’s cardiovascular system and energy metabolism functions, while such pressure would be somewhat relaxed on BL and even more relaxed on BR. On the other hand, other altitude related ecological factors might exert different pressures on the three species for their neuronal functions for food foray and escaping for predators, and for immune functions for different pathogens at varying altitudes. Indeed, it has been shown that high altitude niches can have effects on the neuronal functions such as sensory perception in domestic yaks (Bos grunniens) [16], and olfaction in Tibetan pig [7], wild boars [7] and ground tit (Parus humilis) [15]. In this study, we found that a nonsynonymous mutation in MYO3B in the BR population might alter their auditory sensation. It has also been shown that high altitude niches can have effects on immune functions in ground tit [15]. In this study, we found that a nonsynonymous mutation in PTPRC in the WT population and Manakins might alter their T-cell functions. Thus, it appears that the three eared pheasant species might adapt to highly varying altitudes or other aspects of their distinct ecological niches by loss-of-function mutations and fine-tuning genes involved in the same set of pathways to meet their varying demands for the four major functions. In other words, genes in this set of pathways might play roles in the adaptation of the three pheasant species to different niches, particularly, different altitudes by complete deletion (gene missing), pseudogenization, alteration of CDSs, or alteration of cis-regulatory sequences.

Our data might also reveal a possible mechanism for nature to eliminate the functions of certain genes in a species for its adaptation to environments. It has long been assumed that synonymous mutations are selectively neutral. If this is true, then synonymous mutation rates should be the same as neutral mutation rate, and therefore are largely uniform along the full-length CDSs of all genes in a species. However, in excellent agreement with a recent report in chickens [51], we found that although synonymous mutation rate was largely uniform in roughly the 80% middle part of the CDSs in both BR and WT, it decreased rapidly toward the two ends of the CDSs (Fig. 2c, d). These results strongly suggest that both the 5’-ends and 3’-ends are generally under strong purifying selection, and thus, might contain functional elements that are not related to amino acids-coding functions. Indeed, it is well-known that the 5’-ends and 3’-ends of the CDSs of genes often harbor critical elements regulating translation or stability of transcripts [52]. Thus, arguably, the best target sites to disrupt the function of a gene with most parsimonious number of mutations might be the functional elements at the 5’-ends and 3’-ends of the CDSs of the gene. Moreover, the most effective and parsimonious way to eliminate the amino acids-coding function of a gene might be to have a point pseudogenization mutation close to the 5’-end of its CDSs. Indeed, we found that the first pseudogenization mutation (the most upstream pseudogenization mutation) in a pseudogene was strongly biased to the 5’-end and 3’-end of parent CDSs (Fig. 2c, d), which is also consistent with previous reports in human [51, 53] and chickens [51]. Although mutations at the 3’-end of the CDSs of a gene might have a limited impact on translated amino acid sequence, it may disrupt the other critical functional elements, and thus, affect the gene’s functions. We also note there are more ORF shift mutations than nonsense mutations along the parental CDSs. This might be due to the fact that the probability to have an ORF shift mutation at a given site is higher than that to have a nonsense mutation, since any indel at any site causes an ORF shift, whereas only very specific substitutions cause nonsense mutations. Interestingly, the ratio of ORF shift mutations over nonsense mutations decreases from the 5’-ends to the 3’-end along the parental CDSs. There are at least two possible not necessarily exclusive reasons. First, the 5’-ends of genes tends to have higher G/C contents, thus are more prone to polymerase slippage, resulting in ORF shift mutations. Second, the first hit effect: once a nonsense mutation occurs at the 5’-end, the gene becomes a pseudogene, and the chance for a second hit to turn it to an ORF shift mutation is higher. Additionally, the pseudogenization mutations in both BR and WT are fixed in their populations, indicating that the mutations are under positive selection. Thus, it appears that nature uses the most effective ways to eliminate the functions of certain genes in both BR and WT, which may help them better adapt to their unique ecological niches, i.e., the “less is more” hypothesis [98]. However, our data could not exclude the possibility that some genes lost functions due to genetic drift, particularly in BR that has undergone a recent population bottleneck [45].

Finally, to our best knowledge, we provided thus far the most comprehensive annotation of genes and pseudogenes in the WT and BR genomes. We also identified numerous selection sweeps in the WT, BR, and BL genomes. These results can be valuable resources for researchers to better understand the biology of these uniquely adapted species and for policy makers to design strategies for the preservation of these endangered species.

Conclusions

By annotating and comparing the genome of a WT inhabiting high altitudes and the genome of a BR inhabiting low altitudes, as well as comparing genetic variations in populations of WT and BR as well as of BL inhabiting intermediate altitudes, we found the unique genes and pseudogenes in the genomes of WT and BR converge on the same set of altitude adaptation-related pathways of four functional categories as genes in selective sweeps in each species. Thus, these species appear to adapt to highly varying altitudes by diverging selection on the same traits via loss-of-function mutations and fine-tuning genes in common pathways.

Methods

Datasets

We downloaded our previously published data [46], including the assembled genome of the WT individual from GenBank with accession number GCA_036346035.1, re-sequencing data of 10 WT individuals from NCBI SRA with accession number SRP433016, re-sequencing data of a BL individual from NCBI SRA with accession number SRP471294, and RNA-seq data from 20 tissues (chest muscle, leg muscle, liver, kidney, lung, spleen, heart, cerebellum, brain cortex, ovary, glandular, pancreas, abdominal fat, skin from chest, crops, rectum, cecum, duodenum, small intestine and gizzard) of the WT individual from NCBI SRA with accession number SRP432961. We downloaded the published datasets [45], including the assembled genome of the BR individual from China National Genomics Data Center (CNGDC) with accession number GWHAOPW00000000, re-sequencing data of 41 BR individuals from CNGDC with project number PRJCA003284, RNA-seq data from three BR tissues (blood, developing primaries, and developing tail feathers) from CNGDC with project number PRJCA003284, and re-sequencing data of 12 BL individuals from CNGDC with project number PRJCA003284. We downloaded re-sequencing data of 24 individuals of an indigenous chicken (Daweishan chicken) (Gallus gallus) breed [78] from the NCBI SRA database with project number PRJNA893352.

Ethics approval

All the experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Yunnan Agricultural University (approval ID: YAU202103047). The care and use of animals fully complied with local animal welfare laws, guidelines, and policies.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Three female adult WT individuals were collected from Diqing Tibet Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China. The birds were killed by electric shock to unconsciousness followed by neck artery bleeding. One to two grams of 20 tissues were aseptically collected from each individual bird in a centrifuge tube within 20 min after sacrifice and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80℃ until use. The collected tissues included chest muscle, leg muscle, liver, kidney, lung, spleen, heart, cerebellum, brain cortex, ovary, glandular, pancreas, abdominal fat, skin from chest, crops, rectum, cecum, duodenum, small intestine and gizzard. Total RNA from each tissue sample was extracted using TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, P. R. China) according to the vendor’s instructions. cDNA was synthetized from the total RNA by using a PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, P. R. China) following the vendor’s instructions as previously described [99]. RT-qPCR was performed using the Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR platform (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) and SYBR Green master mix (iQTM SYBRGreen® Supermix, Dalian TaKaRa Biotechnology Co. Ltd.). We randomly selected 20 putative new genes for validation, but three of them were excluded from the final batch validation as their melting temperature did not match those of the remaining 17. The primers and annealing temperatures for the 17 genes are listed in Supplementary Note. The β-actin gene was used as a reference. Primers were commercially synthesized (Shanghai Shenggong Biochemistry Company, P.R.C.). Each PCR reaction was performed in 25 μl volumes containing 12.5 μl of iQ™ SYBR Green Supermix, 0.5 μl (10 mM) of each primer, and 1 μl of cDNA. Detection of products was performed with the following cycle profile: one cycle of 95 °C for 2 min, and 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, the annealing temperature (Supplementary Note 2) for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, followed by a final cycle of 72 °C for 10 min. The 2−ΔCt method was used to analyze mRNA abundance. All tissues were analyzed with three biological replicates and each biological replicate with five technical replicates.

Protein-coding gene annotation

To annotate the protein-coding genes and pseudogenes in the WT and BR genomes, we used a combination of reference-based and RNA-based method as previously described [100]. Briefly, for the reference-based method, we used the CDSs of 53 well-represented avian genomes in NCBI (Table S1) as the templates. We mapped all the CDSs isoforms of genes in the 53 genomes to the assembly using Splign (2.0.0) [101] using default settings. For each template gene whose CDSs could be mapped to the assembly, we concatenated all the mapped parts on the assembly. If the CDSs of the template gene could be mapped to multiple loci on the assemblies, we chose the locus with the highest mapping identity and checked whether the resulting concatenated sequence formed an intact ORF (it had a length of an integer multiple of three and contained no premature stop codon). If resulting concatenated sequence formed an intact ORF, we called the locus an intact gene. If resulting concatenated sequence did not form an intact ORF, i.e. its length was not an integer multiple of three and/or it contained a premature stop codon, we mapped the Illumina DNA short reads from the same individual to the locus in the assembly allowing no mismatch and gaps using bowtie (v2.4.1) [102] with default settings. If each nucleotide position in the locus can be mapped by at least 10 short reads, we considered the locus as a pseudogene; otherwise, we called it a partially supported gene since the pseudogenization mutation might be caused by the sequencing errors in the assembly that cannot be corrected by the polishing process.

For the RNA-based method, we first mapped all the RNA-seq reads from the 20 tissues of WT (Table S3) and the three tissues of BR (Table S4) to the rRNA database SILVA_138 [103] using bowtie (v2.4.1) [102] with default settings, and filtered out the mapped reads. We then mapped the unaligned reads to each genome using STAR (2.7.0c) [104] with default settings. As the Crossoptilon species are closely related [35, 36], here, we used a total of 23 RNA-seq datasets from both species in annotating each genome to fully utilize the available data. Particularly, the datasets of BR were collected from tissues (blood, developing primaries, and developing tail feathers) that were not collected from WT, and thus, they were complementary to facilitate gene finding. Based on the mapped reads to each genome, we assembled transcripts using genome-guided method in Trinity (2.8.5) [105] with default settings. Then we mapped the assembled transcripts to their corresponding genome using Splign (2.0.0) [101] with default settings, and defined their gene structures, and removed those that partially overlap the genes and pseudogenes predicted by the reference-based method or the non-coding RNA genes (see below). For each unmapped assembled transcripts, we found the longest ORF in it and called it a protein-coding gene if it was at least 300 bp long.

For completeness, we also annotated tRNA, miRNA, rRNA, snoRNA, telomerase RNA and SRP RNA genes in each genome using infernal (1.1.2) [106] with the Rfam (v.14) database [107] as the reference using default settings.

Single nucleotide variants calling

To call the variants in the populations of BL, BR, WT and Daweishan chicken, we first mapped the short reads of each individual to the WT genome using BWA (0.7.17) [108] and SAMtools (1.9) [109] using default settings, and then called the variants for each individual using GATK-HaplotypeCaller (4.1.6) [110] using default settings and merged the variants of each individual from the same species using GATK-CombineGVCFs (4.1.6) [110] using default settings. We removed the SNPs with Quality by depth (QD) < 2, Fisher strand (FS) > 60, Root mean square mapping quality (MQ) < 40, Strand odd ratio (SOR) > 3, Rank Sum Test for mapping qualities (MQRankSum) < −12.5 and Rank Sum Test for site position within reads (ReadPosRankSum) < −8 and indels with QD < 2, FS > 200, SOR > 10, Likelihood-based test for the consanguinity among samples (InbreedingCoeff) < −0.8 and ReadPosRankSum < −20.

To calculate the fixation rate of the pseudogenes in the population of each species, we called the SNPs in the pseudogenes of each species using the DNA short reads from the species’ population (n = 10 for WT and n = 41 for BR) using the same method mentioned above. The annotated pseudogenes of each species were used as the reference for the SNP calling.

PCA, ancestry, and introgression analyses

The called biallelic SNPs in each species located in strong linkage disequilibrium blocks and those with a MAF < 0.05 were filtered. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the remaining SNPs using PLINK (1.90) [111] with default settings. The same SNPs were used to infer the ancestral relationships of the three species using ADMIXTURE (1.3.0) [73] with default settings. The results for the number of ancestral groups K = 2 and 3 were tested. The same SNPs were used to infer possible gene introgression among the three species by computing the D-statistic using Dsuite [74] with default settings and the f3-statistic using ADMIXTOOLS [75] with default settings.

Calculation of allele frequencies of pseudogenes

We computed allele frequencies of the first pseudogenization mutation of each pseudogene in each species using GATK (4.1.6) [110] based on called SNPs and indels.

Calculation of mean synonymous mutation rates along protein-coding genes

We divided the length of the full-length CDSs of each protein-coding gene into 100 bins (1, 2, …., i, …, 100) numerated from the 5’-end to the 3-end. Thus, each bin is 1% of the total CDSs length long. We counted the number of synonymous mutations in each bin, and the synonymous mutation rate in each bin is the number of synonymous mutations divided by the length of the bin (bp). In Fig. 2c and d, the mean of synonymous mutation rate in each bin i along the CDSs is the sum of the synonymous mutations in bin i over all the CDSs divided by the number of CDSs.

Calculation of the first pseudogenization positions along pseudogene

We divided the length of the full-length CDSs of the parent protein-coding gene of a pseudogene into 100 bins (1, 2, …., i, …, 100) numerated from the 5’-end to the 3’-end. If the most upstream pseudogenization mutation in a pseudogene is located in bin i of the parent gene, we consider the first pseudogenization position is located in bin i. In Fig. 2c and d, the probability in each bin i is number of pseudogenes with the first pseudogenization located in bin i divided by the total number of pseudogenes in the genome.

Identification of alternative isoforms for pseudogenes in WT and BR

We mapped the RNA-seq reads of WT and BR to their corresponding genomes using STAR (2.7.0c) [104] with default settings. Based on the mapping results and our gene annotations of the two species, we identified alternative isoforms for each pseudogene using Cufflinks (2.2.1) [114] with default settings.
