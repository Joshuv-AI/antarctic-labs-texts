Addressing sputum challenges in bronchiectasis: high-flow therapy as a game-changer for airway clearance care
Authors: Beatriz Herrero-Cortina, Victoria Alcaraz-Serrano, Héctor Sanz-Fraile, Pablo Cubero-Marín, Marta Marín-Oto, Elena Gimeno-Santos
Published: 2025

Bronchiectasis is undergoing a significant reconceptualisation, and is now viewed as a primary inflammatory disease with a highly heterogeneous endotype presentation [1]. This heterogeneity arises from both the diversity of underlying aetiologies and the distinct inflammatory profiles characterising the disease [2]. This new perspective emphasises the complex interrelationship between four fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms: airway inflammation, mucociliary system dysfunction, susceptibility to recurrent respiratory infections, and progressive destruction of lung parenchyma [2, 3].

Chronic neutrophilic inflammation of the airways plays a crucial role in the impairment of the mucociliary system in bronchiectasis [4]. Neutrophil-derived elastase in the lungs induces mucus gland hyperplasia [4] and reduces extracellular ATP concentrations [5]. These changes subsequently modify the properties of the periciliary and mucus layers, resulting in dehydration and hyperconcentration of the airway mucus layer [5]. The primary mechanism underlying these effects is an increased mucin concentration, which causes osmotic compression of the periciliary layer. This process impairs ciliary function, characterised by reduced beat frequency and increased adhesion of the mucus layer to the epithelium [5].

In people with bronchiectasis, the most prevalent respiratory symptoms, such as daily sputum production or difficulty expectorating, chronic cough episodes, and dyspnoea [6], are clinical indications of an impaired mucociliary system. These symptoms significantly impact patients’ quality of life and social interactions [7]. Not only their presence, but also the severity, of these symptoms reflects the activity of the disease, as do the patient's likelihood of exacerbation, levels of airway inflammation and evidence of mucus plugs on chest computed tomography scans [1]. Therefore, both initial and ongoing monitoring of how these respiratory symptoms impact disease progression enables clinicians to identify patients with high disease activity and to implement personalised therapeutic strategies aimed at effectively reducing and controlling the underlying airway inflammation.

Guidelines for managing bronchiectasis recommend the regular use of airway clearance techniques (ACTs) to improve respiratory symptoms associated with impaired mucociliary system [8]. However, ACTs alone may be insufficient when the mucus layer is highly hyperconcentrated and requires additional interventions such as mucoactive drugs. The addition of nebulised mucoactive drugs is time-consuming and requires meticulous cleaning after use to mitigate infection risk, presenting a substantial challenge for long-term adherence. Additionally, limited tolerability of nebulised mucoactive drugs, particularly in older patients and/or those with reduced lung function, hinders administration and continuous use of these interventions [9, 10]. This barrier is markedly intensified during acute exacerbations, when reduced tolerability can disrupt treatment continuity and compromises respiratory symptom management when interventions are most crucial. Consequently, emerging therapies with better tolerability profiles that enhance mucociliary clearance, either alone or in combination with ACTs, are highly desirable.

Considering the aforementioned context, high-flow therapy (HFT) could be a promising patient-centred intervention for improving mucociliary clearance in those with bronchiectasis [7, 11]. This therapeutic modality delivers a continuous flow of heated and humidified air, optimising mucus layer hydration and enhancing biophysical mucus properties [12–14]. Key attributes supporting the use of HFT for chronic home treatment in patients with clinically stable bronchiectasis include its non-invasive nature, adjustable settings that improve patient tolerability, low noise levels, minimal maintenance requirements, and suitability for a diverse range of patients, irrespective of their need for supplementary oxygen, disease severity and level of collaboration.

To date, the evidence supporting home-based HFT in people with stable bronchiectasis is limited and primarily derived from retrospective studies [15–17]. The findings suggest that this therapy may have the potential to modulate disease activity and improve quality of life in bronchiectasis, as demonstrated by observations of reduced dyspnoea and exacerbations, as well as enhanced ease of expectoration (figure 1) [15–19]. Notably, a positive impact on exacerbation rates has been seen across all levels of bronchiectasis severity using HFT, indicating its potential applicability to a broad range of patients. A recent systematic review further supports this rationale, demonstrating similar benefits with HFT in terms of exacerbation reduction and quality of life improvement among patients with COPD [20].

These clinical benefits may be explained by the ability of HFT to enhance mucociliary transport [11, 21]. In a recent post hoc analysis, Crimi et al. [16] demonstrated that 12 months of home-based HFT led to a significant reduction in both the overall presence of mucus plugs and the number of lung lobes affected by mucus plugging in patients with bronchiectasis. The proposed mechanism underlying these findings is that regular HFT may improve hydration of the mucus layer, thereby reducing mucus adhesion, osmotic pressure and mucin concentration, enhancing ciliary beating, and potentially modulating airway inflammation in this disease (figure 1) [11]. While this is a plausible hypothesis, it has yet to be definitively confirmed in patients with bronchiectasis or other muco-obstructive diseases.

Prescribing home-based HFT remains challenging in certain regions [22], primarily due to the need for the device itself, supplemental oxygen if required, and the ongoing replacement of consumables such as breathing tubes, autofill chambers and cannulas [23, 24]. Despite these logistical and financial barriers, previous cost-effectiveness studies in COPD [23, 24] and bronchiectasis [25] have demonstrated that HFT can lead to overall reductions in healthcare costs, primarily by lowering the frequency of exacerbations and improving quality of life.

Turning to exacerbations, current research on HFT use during these episodes in bronchiectasis has primarily focused on patients with hypercapnia and/or hypoxaemia, aiming to determine whether this therapy can prevent use of invasive techniques, improve gas exchange, and reduce hospital admissions [26]. However, this narrow approach has created a notable knowledge gap regarding the potential advantages of HFT for enhancing mucociliary clearance during this period. Indeed, HFT can play a supportive role in assisting ACTs during exacerbations, offering benefits beyond hydration of the mucus layer. By reducing inspiratory effort and prolonging the expiratory phase [12–14], HFT enables patients to improve the quality of their performance of ACTs and cough manoeuvres. Furthermore, by decreasing the overall work of breathing [12–14], HFT may mitigate the fatigue and dyspnoea commonly associated with ACTs during exacerbations, which patients report as significant barriers to effective performance during acute episodes (figure 1) [27]. Therefore, future studies exploring the clinical and physiological benefits of a combined intervention including ACTs supported by HFT during exacerbations in bronchiectasis are needed.

Despite the potential benefits described for HFT in airway clearance for bronchiectasis, uncertainty persists regarding the optimal setting parameters tailored to patient characteristics and the minimal duration required to achieve benefits, particularly when combining HFT with ACTs. Higher temperatures (37°C) provide greater relative humidity [28], improving the hydration of the mucus layer. However, long-term tolerance to this temperature may not be comfortable [29] and could vary over time, especially in warmer climates. Therefore, optimal practice involves selecting the highest patient-tolerable temperature while acknowledging potential fluctuations in tolerance.

Flow is likely the parameter that requires the most individual adaptation [30], especially when HFT is used to support ACTs. In hypoxaemic patients, both flow and fraction of inspired oxygen should be optimised to maintain safe levels of oxygen saturation. Higher flow rates can increase positive end-expiratory pressure and reduce inspiratory effort [30], but may also decrease relative humidity [28]. Consequently, selecting the optimal flow for HFT involves balancing sufficient humidification with the physiological benefits needed to maximise the effectiveness of ACTs in bronchiectasis. This flow may need to be adjusted, particularly during exacerbations or in severe cases, where greater physiological effects are required to improve ACT efficacy and tolerability.

In long-term studies, the most prescribed duration for home HFT use is about 6 h per day, typically during sleeping hours [15, 17, 20]. However, Good et al. [18] demonstrated that even an average use of 2 h daily produced clinical benefits in patients with clinically stable bronchiectasis. This is supported by a recent bench study showing that HFT improves mucus clearance compared to spontaneous breathing after just 30 min, with sustained effects after 8 h [28]. Crimi et al. [26] also observed easier expectoration after 1 h of HFT during exacerbations in bronchiectasis. While daily adherence is important for long-term benefits [17], the minimum effective daily duration for optimising mucociliary function and optimally enhancing the effectiveness of ACTs remains unclear. Determining this balance is key to supporting long-term adherence to HFT at home.

Several questions remain about HFT's role in managing bronchiectasis. Future research should focus on pragmatic trials to assess how HFT modulates disease activity, using optimised protocols for both stable patients at home and during exacerbations, regardless of oxygen requirements. To inform clinical practice and policy, these studies should also incorporate cost-effectiveness analyses to evaluate the economic sustainability and potential resource implications of broader HFT adoption. Since long-term adherence to HFT can be challenging for patients [31], further research is needed to determine the minimum effective usage, especially when combined with ACTs to maximise synergistic effects. For patients with severe disease or those experiencing exacerbations, more data on optimal HFT settings are needed to improve both efficacy and tolerability of ACTs.

HFT represents a true game-changer in airway clearance care, representing a transformative approach to addressing challenges in bronchiectasis. By providing superior physiological support and seamless integration with ACTs, HFT has the potential to improve patient tolerability and optimise clinical outcomes, marking a significant step forward in respiratory care.
