Violence against health care workers in a post-conflict context: an exploratory qualitative study in the province of South Kivu, Eastern Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Daniella Bugugu, Samuel Lwamushi Makali, Emmanuel Lurhangire, Pacifique Mwene-Batu, Armand Mulume Bisimwa, Giovanfrancesco Ferrari, Rosine Bigirinama, Jessica Ramirez Mendoza, Ghislain Bisimwa, Sonja Merten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundViolence against healthcare is a significant public health issue worldwide, yet it remains poorly documented in resource-limited countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa. This study aims to explore the causes of such violence, types, perpetrators, consequences and mechanisms for its prevention within the post-conflict context of the South Kivu province in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC).MethodsThis exploratory qualitative study was conducted from April to May 2022 in the health zones (HZ) of Kamituga, Katana and Walungu. A diverse group of participants, including health system stakeholders, community members and policy makers were interviewed, through Focus Group Discussion (FGD) and key informant interviews (KII), guided by a pre-established interview guide. A deductive thematic analysis was carried out and the verbatim quotes were used to illustrate the respondents’ comments.ResultsA total of 143 participants were interviewed for this study. The types of violence reported included physical, verbal and psychological abuse, threats through communication channels and economic abuse (misappropriation of funds, unpaid discharge). The identified causes included interpersonal dynamics within the healthcare staff and between staff and patients, cultural influences and systemic organisational issues. Aggressors varied, spanning from patients and their companions, to the healthcare workers themselves, alongside political, administrative and customary authorities. The violence has far-reaching negative consequences, affecting the individual health care workers, their colleagues and the overall quality of healthcare delivery. Pre-existing warning and prevention mechanisms include recourse to the hierarchy, silence, the utilization of suggestion boxes, regular meetings at the Central Zone Office and supervisory measures.ConclusionsViolence against healthcare professionals is a well-known but poorly documented issue that affects both their wellbeing and professional performance. Integrated health system reforms addressing accountability, governance, and working conditions are needed to prevent violence against healthcare workers, with engagement of political authorities essential for developing and implementing effective prevention strategies.Keywords: Violence, Health care worker, Post-conflict context, South Kivu, DR Congo

Background

Violence against health workers (VAHCs) has become an issue of growing global concern because of its multiple consequences and impact on public health [1]. World Health Organization (WHO) defines an attack or assault on health care as any act of verbal or physical violence or obstruction or threat of violence that interferes with the availability, access and delivery of curative and/or preventive health services during emergencies [2]. Up to 38% of healthcare workers (HCWs) report experiencing workplace violence at some point in their careers [3]. Despite widespread condemnation from the international community, such aggression intensified during the Ebola outbreaks in West Africa and in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), with a notable escalation during the recent Covid-19 pandemic [4–10]. Recognizing the severity of this issue, the World Medical Association has declared VAHCs as “an international emergency”, emphasizing its critical impact on the health of populations [11].

Studies from various high-income countries, including Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, Italy, Poland, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, and Turkey, reveal a significant prevalence of verbal assaults, physical violence, and threats against HCWs. With data suggesting that up to 74% of workplace assaults occur within healthcare environments, the critical need for effective protective measures for frontline HCWs is ever more apparent, emphasizing the urgent call to mitigate its extensive consequences on public health [1].

Africa, particularly in its fragile and conflict-affected regions, is not immune to this phenomenon, despite the limited research available on the subject [12, 13]. A 2019 systematic review found prevalence rates of VAHCs in Africa ranging from 9 to 100%, with the highest cases recorded in South Africa (54%−100%) and Egypt (59.7%−86.1%) [12]. The review found that perpetrators range from patients and their companions to colleagues and community members, with aggression taking the form of verbal attacks, physical, sexual and psychological violence, or even the destruction of healthcare facilities, especially in conflict zones. These situations can have severe repercussion for HCWs, including injury, post-traumatic stress, and death. The causes of this violence are varied, and include long patient wait times, failure to meet patient’s needs, excessive workloads, nursing errors, and staff conflicts highlighting the importance of developing effective prevention strategies.

In response to this global issue, the WHO launched the Surveillance System for Attacks on Health Care (SSA) as mandated by the World Health Assembly Resolution 65.20 [1], to systematically monitor VAHC [14]. The SSA recorded 78,642 attacks between 2020 and 2026 in 25 countries. Over the past 12 months, several of these countries—including the DRC, North and South Sudan, Mali, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Haiti, Lebanon, Israel, Iran, Syria, Ukraine, the Occupied Palestinian Territories…—have reported episodes of violence [15].

The DRC, ranking as the fourth most fragile country out of 179 globally [16], has seen an alarming rise in VAHC, particularly in the eastern regions. These areas have faced a complex humanitarian crisis for nearly three decades, adversely affecting the population’s health. Eastern DRC, particularly South Kivu, remains affected by persistent and fragmented armed violence involving many armed groups such as the Forces Armées de la RDC (FARDC), Mai-Mai militias, the Forces Démocratiques pour le Libération du Rwanda (FDLR), the Allied Democratic Forces (ADF), and, more recently, the Mouvement du 23 mars (M23) rebellion. Violence takes the form of armed clashes, attacks on civilians, kidnappings, sexual violence, looting, and forced displacement [17]. This also affects the health sector through attacks on health facilities, threats against healthcare workers, and disruptions to medical services, limiting access to essential care for vulnerable populations; as highlighted by the ICRC, such violence undermines the functioning of health systems and can deprive entire communities of timely healthcare [18]. From 2016 to 2023, the health sector in the DRC registered 572 attacks with the majority attributed to armed conflict, leading to significant harms to HCWs: 101 were killed, 151 injured, 137 kidnapped [19]. Additionally, these attacks led to the destruction of 104 health facilities. This escalation of violence is especially concerning in light of the country’s ongoing efforts to address recurrent infectious disease outbreaks, such as Ebola and COVID-19, which have pushed the healthcare system to its breaking point. Research conducted in eastern DRC has shown that violence against essential service providers, such as teachers, is driven by a combination of conflict-related factors, social positioning and institutional dynamics, suggesting that similar mechanisms may also shape violence against public service providers like HCWs in these settings [20].

While the frequency of aggression towards HCWs in eastern DRC has been poorly documented, there is a noticeable gap in research regarding the broader implications of such violence, especially in post-conflict settings [21, 22]. The current literature limits the understanding of how VAHC impacts the provision and quality of healthcare, affects the daily lives of HCWs, and influences the healthcare system’s coping and response strategies. Addressing this gap is crucial for developing effective measures to protect HCWs and foster the resilience of healthcare services in the region.

This gap is particularly marked in rural contexts and with low access to care, such as the health zones of Kamituga, Katana and Walungu, where structural and security constraints can exacerbate the effects of this violence [23].

Accordingly, this study seeks to delve into the types and causes of VAHC, ascertain the nature of violence, and understand the dynamics between the perpetrators and victimized HCWs. In addition, it aims to capture HCWs’ perceptions of workplace violence, identify their preferred methods to prevent such incidents, and assess the impact of these attacks on their professional and personal well-being, with a special focus on the unique challenges presented by post-conflict settings in rural area.

Methodology

Study design

This study is part of a larger research initiative focusing on VAHC in the post-conflict region of South Kivu, DRC [17]. The research aimed to evaluate the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of a de-escalation training program for healthcare workers combined with a facility-based Code of Conduct in reducing violence against healthcare workers in low- and middle-income settings [24]. South Kivu province was specifically chosen as the site for this investigation. In collaboration with provincial health authorities, the study strategically focused on three health zones (HZ) in different territories for a comprehensive analysis: (i) the Kamituga HZ, a rural zone, situated in the Shabunda territory, and characterized by challenging accessibility and several mining areas. It encompasses 25 health areas (HA) serving a population of 234,604 inhabitants as of 2023. (ii) The Walungu HZ, located in the Walungu territory, is also rural but more accessible. The HZ comprises 23 AS with a total population of 318,610 inhabitants in 2023. (iii) The Katana HZ, also rural and easily accessible, is located in the Kabare territory. It includes 20 HZs and a total population of 267,504 as of 2023 [25].

The South Kivu province was chosen for its relative stability compared to the conflict-ridden North Kivu, facilitating safer and more practical field research. Its post-conflict status and diverse health zones offer a unique lens to examine VAHCs in transitioning regions.

Type, period and study population

The study is cross-sectional using an exploratory qualitative approach conducted from April to May 2022.

To ensure a diverse range of participants we purposively selected a comprehensive range of stakeholders across the health system at both intermediate (Provincial Health Division, DPS) and operational (HZ) levels, who were likely to possess insights on violence against HCWs. This selection involved various key actors, including health authorities such as the provincial ministry of health, provincial health division and inspectorate. Additionally, we included members of the HZ management team and HCWs from both primary and secondary care levels (Health Centers and General Referral Hospitals). We also broadened our scope to include diverse groups within the community in order to acquire a multifaceted perspective on the issue. We chose specific health centers for our study and used the snowball method to find key participants. This approach helped us gather diverse insights by asking initial participants to recommend others who could contribute valuable information (Table 1).

Data collection

We used two data collection techniques: semi-structured interviews with key informants (KII) across various levels, and focus groups discussions (FGD) involving HCWs and community members separately. Prior to conduct these, we developed an interview guide tailored to each participant group based on similar studies [11, 15]. The interview guides, initially in French and then translated into Swahili (Appendix 1) by the language school of the Catholic University of Bukavu, included questions on the personal experiences with violence against HCWs, such as the frequency, causes and perpetrators of such violence, its consequence on the victim, and prevention mechanisms. A mixed team (2 male and 1 female) of three researchers each holding a master’s degree in public health and experienced in qualitative methods conducted the interviews.

Data analysis

The audio recordings captured on dictaphones were accurately transcribed verbatim in French into Excel for analysis. A deductive thematic analysis [26] was conducted based on a pre-established thematic framework derived from a literature review [8, 27]. This framework combines four main topics: (a) HCWs’ profile, the type and the frequency of aggression they face, (b) the causes of aggression against HCWs, and aggressor profiles, (c) the consequences of aggression on HCWs’ daily lives, and (d) mechanisms for preventing aggression against HCWs, as shown in the coding scheme (Table 2). The analysis process began with familiarizing with the content, followed by manual coding of the transcript, with sub-theme development until saturation was reached. To illustrate the responses obtained, we included direct quotes from the transcripts, carefully omitting any details that could reveal the interviewee’s identity, including the type of interview (KII or FGD), in order to guarantee anonymity. Quotes were translated into English.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of the Catholic University of Bukavu (Number: UCB/CIES/NC/001/2022) and endorsed by the provincial health division of South Kivu. Written informed consent was obtained from each respondent before each interview. At each phase of the study, all principles of confidentiality were respected.

Results

Overall, 49 KIIs and 15 FGDs were conducted at provincial and health zone levels, involving health authorities, healthcare providers, management teams, local organizations, and diverse community groups (Table 2). In total, we carried out 49 KIIs and 15 FGDs. At provincial level, we conducted 7 KIIs with members of the provincial Ministry of Health and the provincial health division (those from the inspectorate were not available). In the 3 HZs, 42 KIIs (14 with certain HGR doctors and nurses, 13 with members of the HZ management team, and 15 with representatives of local non-governmental organisations as well as political, administrative and religious leaders) and 15 FGDs (6 with groups of nurses working in the health centres, and 9 with various community groups: chronically ill people, civil society, young people, motorcyclists, village chiefs, community relays) were carried out (Table 2).

Profile of HCWs exposed and perceived frequency

The profile of the HCWs exposed to attacks is diverse, encompassing a range of roles within the healthcare system. These groups include nurses and doctors, support staff, particularly those in the administrative roles at patient reception area and community health care workers (CHWs) who serve as a vital bridge between the health system, primarily health centers and the communities they serve.

These attacks on HCWs occur not only within professional settings such hospitals, HZ central offices, primary health centers, secondary health centers and private medical centres (polyclinics) but also extend beyond the workplace. Particularly at risk are nurses, doctors and CHWs living within the same community they serve. Additionally, aggression towards HCWs also takes place remotely via various channels including telephone calls, text messages, and social media.

Frequency and underreporting of violence

According to the majority of respondents attacks on healthcare staff occur with alarming frequency. However, they assume underreporting of such cases, which respondents attributed to a number of reasons. First, there is a prevalent fear among HCWs that reporting assaults may lead to job loss. In addition, many incidents go unreported because they are not perceived as offences significant enough to warrant formal action. To make matters worse, there is no specific legal framework for dealing with cases of assaults on HCWs. Furthermore, the absence of an official body or structure for reporting assaults exacerbates this problem, meaning that many cases remain undocumented and untreated.

“First of all, you have to know that these are things that people don’t often consider to be offences, which is why, in terms of frequency, we don’t have much information on this because people don’t report it, but I know that there are care recipients who attack service providers… It’s frequent but less reported” (Decision-maker KII).

The health system is characterized by the under-prosecution of violence against HCWs. Incidents of violence are generally managed through existing administrative and internal disciplinary mechanisms at all levels of the health system. Recourse to the formal judicial system remains rare, as conflicts are typically resolved through mediation, amicable settlements, or professional bodies. This predominantly internal handling of violent incidents limits opportunities for formal redress, accountability, and justice for affected HCWs.

“In any case, when there are reports of violence, they are passed to the provincial health division. If the case can be managed at that level, all the better, but if not, the division sends agents here to the field. In some instances, it can even go up to the governorate level, but it is rare for it to reach the prosecutor’s office. But for internal problems, I know that doctors turn to their disciplinary unit, which handles disputes between colleagues, and if it goes beyond the medical order, they also consult the competent authorities. There are few cases that have actually reached the court.” (Decision maker KII).

Gendered dimensions of violence

In the context of healthcare, gender dynamics play a significant role in the exposure to violence. Women, particularly, are more susceptible to violence, partly due to their vulnerability to sexual assault or because they are less regarded and underestimated than men.

Participants’ accounts suggest that sexual violence against female healthcare workers is widely recognized and regularly reported, particularly through awareness and reporting mechanisms established by certain organizations. Such violence extends beyond acts of sexual harassment or exploitation themselves, encompassing a range of psychological and professional reprisals that often follow the refusal of sexual advances, thereby prolonging the initial act of violence. These narratives reveal a cycle shaped by gender inequalities, hierarchical power abuse, and professional dependency, which sustains this form of violence. Perpetrators are reported to include both healthcare personnel and politico-administrative authorities.

A key decision-maker noted

“… I hear about sexual abuse, from big managers to small ones. Many such cases are reported. It is often the partner’s organization who come and talk about it; the beneficiaries of their work tell them that there is often sexual abuse and even sexual exploitation, harassment, etc.” (Decision-maker KII).

Complementing the insights from the decision-maker, one nurse shared her experience:

“…towards female staff, and when you look closer, there are chases, insults, there are abuses because there was already a refusal, and in the end, you will come to realize that no, if I undergo all this violence, it’s because I had refused, and that is why they want to seek ways, at all costs, to remove me from my job.” (HCW KII)

Another HCWs reveals:

“I’ve had one or two cases …. of pharmacy trainees being assaulted by some of our doctors. It’s really a form of violence because they say: you want to succeed, or you want promotion in exchange for sex” (FGD HCWs).

This narrative emphasizes the daily reality encountered by many in the healthcare profession, stressing not only the prevalence of such incidents but also their devastating impact on the personal and professional life of those dedicated to caring for others.

Healthcare staff who are perceived as foreign to the local community and culture are often more susceptible to attacks. This vulnerability derives from the idea that external HCWs are intruders or opportunists, rather than contributing to the community’s well-being. A participant explained:

“I’ll give you an example: if you’re in an area where you’re not a native, they see you as a mercenary, they see you as someone who’s come to take what rightfully belongs to them, they see what you earn, they don’t see what you produce, your contribution, they minimize. And that can lead to hurtful comments, like when you get a new suit they say, “he comes to make money with us”” (FGD HCWs).

Conversely, certain local sociocultural norms actively protect HCWs, as they are perceived as highly respected and socially superior figures within the community, making any form of violence against them culturally unacceptable.

“…For us in our education and in our custom (“Shi”), aggression against health workers is even a taboo…Naturally in our custom, healers, traditional healers and midwives, these people existed before civilization…In any case they are respectful people in our custom…” (FGD Community).

Types of violence

Physical violence. Respondents identified several types of violence. Firstly, physical violence, either directly by beatings with hands, sometimes with weapons like knives and firearms. Such violence may be perpetrated by patients, patients’ relatives or caregivers, healthcare workers themselves, as well as mobilized state and non-state armed actors. A service provider shared a traumatic incident :

“In 2003, when I was still a permanent nurse, I was severely beaten. I even spent a week in hospital. It was a certain Monday when I was on duty, and some strangers had come in saying they were there with a patient, and when I opened the door I noticed that they had already tied up my sentinel. They came in and asked me to give them $10,000, and as I didn’t have the money, they beat me. That’s physical assault….” (KII service provider).

Or indirectly by manipulating groups to attack HCWs. This latter type of physical aggression is more likely to be perpetrated by political and administrative authorities.

Verbal and psychological violence. These are the most frequently cited form of violence, involving intimidation (directly or indirectly), death threats, accusations to the hierarchy leading to sanctions and humiliation in front of the patients. Verbal violence may extend beyond the workplace and involve the media, politico-administrative authorities, religious leaders, professional bodies, community members, and armed groups, thereby transforming individual disputes into collective mechanisms of pressure that can ultimately escalate into physical violence. Psychological violence also emerged as a continuation of other forms of violence and serves as a means of maintaining a climate of fear, control, and professional insecurity. Both verbal and psychological violence were reported to be perpetrated by healthcare workers themselves, community members, and politico-administrative authorities.

A poignant example of this is

“…in the course of my duties, I was attacked by two chiefs when I reported the misappropriation of nurses’ pay. When one manager misused the funds allocated to his staff and I reported this to his superiors, I was threatened with discourteous comments from him. It was a verbal assault, but also a psychological one, because I was threatened with suspension, reassignment, disciplinary action…”. (Decision maker KII)

A provider mentioned the potential for manipulation for personal gain:

“If I envy the position of zone chief medical officer, I can manipulate the media, the community, priests, pastors, civil society, youth movements, etc. I can manipulate everyone in the hope that I will get this position” (Provider KII).

A HCW added

“…. a colleague had registered a maternal death, and as he was not yet regularly assigned by the supervisory authorities, he [another doctor in conflict with the first] went to alert the rebels who were in the forest and they raged the hospital for 5 hours. Our lives were saved thanks to God and the young people in the area…”. (HCWs KII)

Threats of Violence through communication channels. Participants’ accounts highlight the emergence of mediated and remote forms of violence carried out through communication tools and digital platforms: threats through social media, phone messages, and leaflets thrown into the courtyard of the health facility. Because it occurs without direct confrontation, this form of violence is often anonymous and difficult to trace, creating opportunities for intimidation, harassment, and reputational attacks. Such practices may extend beyond individual healthcare workers and undermine the credibility and legitimacy of the profession as a whole. Participants reported that these acts were perpetrated by politico-administrative authorities, healthcare workers, and community members. An alarming instance of this is detailed by a provider:

“I have experienced death threats in the form of a leaflet against a doctor who intervened in the case of a woman undergoing gynaecological-obstetric care and who was taken to the operating theatre on the pretext that she had been groped and would no longer be able to conceive…” (KII HCW).

Economic violence. This includes non-payment of bills by patients (who would escape from the health facility after being treated), financial extortion, misappropriation of funds allocated to health facilities or to the community, and intimidation when claiming unpaid, irregular and uncertain salaries. The citations mentioned that economic violence, frequently precedes verbal abuse, psychological intimidation, and eventually physical confrontation. A provider describes a typical scenario:

“As we work in a system of self-payment for healthcare, there have been times when certain patients have been unable to pay the fees, and sometimes when the nurse was trying to raise awareness about the payment of fees, because the patient even wanted to escape, the patient behaved badly that day; she [the patient] even managed to pull the nurse’s blouse and other people had to intervene to separate them…” (FGD HCWs).

Underlying causes of violences

Delays in care or perceived negligence often trigger violence. A community member explains:

“…when they don’t look after their patients… the example of a lady who wanted to give birth and needed a transfusion, but the doctor who was supposed to intervene didn’t come, until the lady died…” (Community FGD).

Lack of communication and misunderstanding between the community and healthcare staff also leads to discontent and conflicts. A HCWs added in this regard:

“Sometimes people say that staff have been assaulted, but most of the attacks are worth it because there is no communication. The community thinks it’s being neglected, while we’re following the administrative process, but we have been assaulted because there’s no communication…” (HCW KII).

Also, HCWS aggress against each other because of jealousy and desire for a given position, or because of not respecting on-call rotation schedules. Additionally, HCWs misconduct in society (drunkenness) and breaches of medical confidentiality are other contributing factors.

“… we know that health workers must be professional, they must keep patients’ confidentiality, but here, when a motorcyclist has an accident, the health care workers take the blood, conduct tests and then they start saying, such a motorcyclist or such a person has HIV or an incurable disease, so all this causes problems…” (Community member KII).

Another cause of aggression is that HCWs are no longer liked by the population for a variety of reasons, including incompetence or fraudulent practices, as this quote illustrates

“… dishonesty (fraudulent practices), such as health care workers selling or administering expired medications to patients” (Community member KII).

Finally, another significant factor contributing to aggression towards HCWs is the perception of the high cost of healthcare. This is particularly prevalent among poor communities and a general low level of education of the population. A member of the community shared an example of this financial tension.

“… They [health staff] charge fixed prices, and when they see that it’s a motorcyclist and it’s an accident, they raise the price exaggeratedly, they even start to aggravate the situation to get money out of them…” (Community member KII).

Misunderstandings about nature of preventive health care activities conducted by CHWs, often leads to conflicts.

“I personally had an experience when I was administering contraceptives, a young man came up to me and raised his voice, saying: “It’s because of you that the girls in the neighbourhood are no longer getting pregnant…”” (FGD HCW).

Consequences of attacks on HCW’s daily life

These attacks on HCWs have a negative impact at several levels:

Personal impact. The assaults profoundly affect the victim’s daily lives and their families, particularly causing psychological distress. This often leads to demotivation and, at times, the development of chronic health illnesses. Physical attacks can lead to outcomes, ranging from minor injuries to death.

“When you’re assaulted, you decide to stop everything, when you don’t work you don’t survive either, with the consequence that the whole family stops living…” (HCW FGD).

Professional and healthcare quality impact. The assaults disrupt the workplace environment and the quality of care, as they induce HCWs to seek better employment elsewhere. The lack of psychological support exacerbated by a shortage of qualified staff, leaves the affected HCWs vulnerable to permanent post-traumatic stress.

“Psychologically, the victims of this violence will feel frustrated and rejected; sometimes the victims of this violence develop the desire to give up the job; I myself wanted to give up the job, because you feel inferior to others…” (KII service provider).

Another provider added:

“It should also be said that when healthcare staff are attacked, they automatically become traumatised and this can have an impact on the service they can offer their patients, especially as many facilities do not have psychologists” (KII provider).

Impact on nurse-patient relationship. Assaults by patients or their companions can severely affect nurse-patient relationship.

“… The fact of having been assaulted can lead to unintentional actions toward patients… with the risk of making mistakes even about prescribed medications” (FGD Community).

Mechanisms to prevent aggression towards HCWs

To mitigate VAHC, mechanisms had already been put in place by some international and national partner organizations, and the health zone management team sought to support and strengthen these mechanisms, however, in most cases, these efforts remained largely theoretical, with little concrete action implemented on the ground.

Standardisation of the recruitment process to ensure that health facilities have qualified personnel. This measure aims to enhance the quality of care and reduce incidents of violence attributed to professional incompetence or misunderstandings related to care quality.

“Given that the main cause of aggression is linked to the quality of care and blackmail, the logic adopted by the health zone office is to have qualified care staff through an objective recruitment process: all candidates first go through an advanced training course at the General Referral Hospital (HGR) with a trial period and are placed on a waiting list after assessment by the director of nursing, the chief medical officer, and the care staff. As soon as there is a need and depending on the skills observed, the appropriate person from the waiting list is officially assigned”. (Decision-maker KII)

Decision-making and disciplinary sanctions. Decisions about disciplinary measures are made in meetings that involve HCWs and members of the community. This process helps address any issues or misconduct. However, the decisions made are often not supported by the higher decision-making bodies, undermining their effectiveness.

“In fact, I think there are theoretical mechanisms. When we have situations of violence, we discuss them in meetings where decisions or recommendations can be made, but most of the time I have always said they remain theoretical and that the solution would come from the government to support these agents [the HCWs]” (Service Provider KII).

Strengthening communication and community engagement. Efforts to improve communication and foster a stronger relationship between HCWs and the community includes the installation of suggestion boxes at the central office of the health zone (COHZ) and within the health facility. These boxes allow the population to voice their complaints and concerns. Additionally, COHZ meetings invite community representatives to discuss healthcare issues, and the management team’s conduct supervisions in the health areas during which they engage with the community about their concerns. These meetings also serve a crucial role in clarifying health issues for the community, aiming to reduce the church’s influence in decision-making processes. A decision maker highlights this strategy:

“We have placed suggestion boxes in our facilities to collect population’s feedback to improve their care, break the church’s participation in decision-making, and regulate pricing to allow the population access to care without resorting to violence” (Decision Maker KII).

Another decision maker added:

“When we conduct supervisions on the ground… we try to gather what the community expects from us. I ask them: what do you reproach us for?” (Decision Maker KII).

Discussion

This study shows that violence against healthcare workers (HCWs) is a major and multifaceted problem in post-conflict South Kivu, DRC. HCWs are exposed to overlapping forms of violence, including verbal, physical, psychological, sexual, written threats, and economic violence, which often reinforce each other rather than occurring in isolation. These experiences reflect a continuum of violence shaped by both community interactions and systemic health system dysfunctions.

Economic violence stems from chronic underfunding, weak health financing, and poor human resource management, forcing HCWs to rely on user fees and placing them in ethically constrained positions between institutional limits and community expectations. This highlights the need for more equitable and context-sensitive health financing reforms [28–31]. Causes of violence include interpersonal tensions between HCWs and patients, long waiting times, unmet care needs, and cultural factors. Beyond harming individuals, this violence also undermines the quality of healthcare services.

In this post-conflict setting, violence against HCWs is driven by routine dysfunctions in healthcare delivery rather than isolated events, reflecting a systemic phenomenon rooted in structural, organizational, and relational constraints. It operates across macro, meso, and micro levels, where weak state capacity, poor governance, and problematic institutional practices translate into daily interpersonal conflicts between HCWs, patients, and communities. This multi-level dynamic perpetuates pervasive mistrust and further destabilizes relationships within the health system, ultimately undermining healthcare delivery [32–34].

Within this multi-level system, exposure to violence is not evenly distributed. The analysis reveals that specific healthcare staff, such as nurses and doctors, support staff (especially those working in the patient reception area) and community healthcare workers (CHWs) are particularly vulnerable. While this aligns with global trends, it is further emphasized by the unique circumstances of a post-conflict setting [12, 22].

CHWs occupy a particularly fragile position, functioning as intermediaries between the community and the formal health system while lacking institutional protection, stable remuneration, or social recognition. This “in-between” position exposes them simultaneously to downward violence from the community and upward violence from the health system [35, 36].

Assaults on HCWs can occur not only within health facilities but also outside the workplace, and increasingly, threats through remote channels such as phone calls, text messages, and social media. These incidents are often under-reported due to fear of job loss, or because such forms of violence are no longer perceived as offences. violence against HCWs becomes routinised and rendered invisible, both socially and administratively. This normalisation is sometimes interpreted as a form of resilience among HCWs, who see aggression as “part of the job”. However, our findings suggest that this apparent resilience is better understood as a symptom of chronic exposure to insecurity rather than genuine adaptation. The severity of the issue is underscored by studies conducted in eastern DRC, such as Muzembo et al. (2015) and Makali SL et al. which document prevalence rates of violence of 80.1% and 45.9% respectively, highlighting the urgent need for formal reporting mechanisms and better management strategies [21, 22, 34, 37].

In the Kivus, female HCWs are notably vulnerable to sexual violence, a situation marked by the region’s prolonged conflicts, and deep-seated gender inequalities [22]. The findings show that violence occurs in hierarchical healthcare settings with weak accountability, where women—particularly junior staff and trainees—have limited power to challenge inappropriate behaviour. Gender norms that normalise male authority and discourage reporting further reinforce silence and under-documentation. In this context, sexual violence reflects broader institutional and social vulnerabilities affecting female HCWs rather than isolated incidents, highlighting the need for gender-sensitive prevention and support mechanisms in healthcare settings, consistent with evidence from other African contexts [12, 38].

Moreover, HCWs perceived as outsiders are particularly vulnerable due to conflict, ethnic tensions, and perceptions of belonging and legitimacy, while some sociocultural norms may confer protection through social recognition and solidarity. These effects are context-dependent, as cultural identities can alternatively enable protection, exclusion, or violence depending on the setting [39, 40]. This dynamic underscores the importance of understanding and integrating local cultural and ethnic considerations into strategies aimed at protecting HCWs [37].

VAHC in the eastern DRC involves a wide array of perpetrators, including patients’ companions, community pressure groups, HCWs and political, administrative and customary authorities, reflecting a pattern seen in other conflict zones [41]. At least 40% of participants identified administrative and political authorities as aggressors, reflecting the broader influence of local political power dynamics on the health system.In the context of long-standing politico-ethnic conflicts in eastern DRC, customary and politico-administrative authorities may also interfere in the health system, influencing community decisions or creating pressure groups armed or unarmed for political ends, often to the detriment of good management and adequate provision of health services, thereby exposing HCWs to aggressions [42].

Key factors contributing to attacks include perceived poor quality of care, such as treatment delays and perceived neglect, which are often linked to the population’s high expectations and HCW’ lack of resources, including basic medical infrastructure and training [21]. These tensions are further exacerbated by the broader context of armed conflict and post-conflict instability in South Kivu, as in other provinces in the east of the DRC [43].

Violence is driven by poor communication between HCWs and communities, unprofessional conduct, service delays, and perceived high costs, further exacerbated by the lack of culturally grounded criteria for service acceptability, leading to uncertainty in access to care. Overall, inadequate communication remains a key driver of violence against HCWs [44].

Attacks have a number of consequences. Our findings show that these consequences affect HCWs’ personal wellbeing, professional functioning, and the broader delivery of care. Firstly, there is a psychological impact on the victim and their family in terms of loss of motivation. In physical terms, the consequences can range from simple injuries to death. Our findings support the findings from D’Ettore G. et al. who observed similar outcomes among HCWs subjected to abuse or assault, particularly verbal abuse. These consequences include mental health issues such as fear, irritation, anger, depression, anxiety, as well as feeling of powerlessness and disappointment [36]. These psychological sequelae, undermine HCWs ability to share and understand patients’ needs, and to sustain professional motivation over time, and are sometimes predictive of burnout, resulting in the absence of services and/or the provision of poor-quality service [45, 46].

At the service level, violence also produces cumulative effects that disrupt care delivery. These consequences may manifest in post-traumatic stress syndrome among HCWs, which can even lead to a desire to resign, as noted by other authors, including Gates et al. [47]. Furthermore, the relationship between carer and patient become compromised increasing the risk of unintended actions towards patients including errors in prescription. Studies have consistently found this relationship to be compromised after an incident of violence, leading to post-traumatic stress disorder across all categories of healthcare workers [45, 47, 48]. This cycle of deteriorating conditions ultimately fuels further violence from the population, perpetuating a vicious circle of violence, diminished work quality and violence against HCWs.

HCW safety measures include standardized recruitment and community–HCW disciplinary mechanisms, but their effectiveness is limited by weak enforcement, limited authority support, and the absence of formal reporting systems [49], limiting their effectiveness as deterrent mechanisms. Efforts to improve communication between HCWs and the community—such as suggestion boxes and inclusive meetings—have shown potential to ease tensions, as reported in other settings [50]. In South Kivu, however, these mechanisms appear to function mainly as informal tools for managing complaints rather than as structured systems for preventing violence. Reporting of assaults remains limited, and no formal training in violence prevention or de-escalation has been implemented, despite evidence of its effectiveness elsewhere [13, 51]. Despite these efforts, the reporting of assaults remains low, hindered by the lack of an official reporting mechanism and fear of reprisals from perpetrators, compounded by a culture of impunity.

Limits of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its cross-sectional qualitative design does not allow for causal inference or assessment of temporal changes in VAHC. Second, the purposive and snowball sampling strategy, while appropriate for exploratory qualitative research, may have introduced selection bias by privileging participants who were more visible, accessible, or willing to speak about violence. Third, the study was conducted in only three health zones of South Kivu, which limits the transferability of findings to other provinces of the DRC, particularly more unstable settings such as North Kivu. In addition, the strong predominance of male participants may have influenced the perspectives captured, potentially underrepresenting gender-specific experiences of violence. Finally, as data relied on self-reported experiences, responses may have been affected by recall bias or social desirability bias, especially given the sensitivity of discussing violence and institutional responsibility. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable contextual insights into VAHC in a post-conflict setting.

Conclusion

Violence against healthcare workers in South Kivu is widespread and multifaceted, encompassing physical, verbal, psychological, sexual, economic, institutional, and mediated forms of violence. These forms are often interconnected and rooted in broader structural challenges, including insecurity, governance weaknesses, poor working conditions, economic precarity, and mistrust between health services and communities. Their consequences extend beyond individual harm to undermine healthcare delivery, workforce motivation, and public trust in the health system.

Reducing violence against healthcare workers requires comprehensive interventions that address both immediate risks and their underlying determinants. Strengthening reporting and accountability mechanisms, improving working conditions, promoting respectful patient–provider relationships, and addressing institutional and governance failures should be prioritized. In fragile and conflict-affected settings such as South Kivu, violence prevention should be integrated into broader health system strengthening strategies to enhance workforce safety, service quality, and community trust.
