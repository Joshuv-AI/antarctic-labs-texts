Phenome-wide study connects behavioral genetics of odor detection dogs with temperament traits
Authors: Alexander W Eyre, Isain Zapata, Jasmin Fraire, Kevin D Coty, Heather J Huson, Katy M Evans, Cynthia M Otto, James A Serpell, Diane van Rooy, Claire M Wade, Sarah C Blott, Pamela Wiener, Marie J Haskell, Carlos E Alvarez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe conducted a Phenome-Wide Association Study (PheWAS) to investigate whether alleles previously shown to be associated with problem behaviors in Labrador Retrievers from the U.S. Transportation Security Administration’s (TSA) odor detection program also show behavioral associations in other populations. The original TSA cohort (2002–2013) consisted of dogs from a former breeding program that drew from U.S. commercial sources and the Australian Customs Service. While those data included TSA testing results from the foster period, detailed behavior profiles and reasons for elimination from the program were not recorded. To extend and validate these genetic associations in populations with richer behavioral data, we analyzed three additional Labrador Retriever cohorts with both genotype and C-BARQ behavioral questionnaire data: (1) Australian pet dogs, (2) UK dogs from a mixed pet and working background (primarily gamebird retrieving), and (3) U.S. working guide dogs. This analysis identified a total of 15 associations between 12 behavioral traits and 8 markers at 6 genome loci. Notably, we found four types of aggression and one type of fear that are directed at familiar humans or dogs, but none directed at unfamiliar ones. Other problem traits identified include separation-related behaviors, excitability, and chasing small animals. Furthermore, we utilized whole genome sequencing to identify a functional candidate associated with “aggression when approached by a household dog at a favorite resting place”. We propose this variant in an ADAMTSL1 intron results in the loss of TCF7L1 protein binding, and we highlight the evolutionary history of that conserved element, including the fixation of two mutations in the human lineage. Our PheWAS findings suggest relevance to working dog selection, breeding, and training, presenting opportunities to reduce costs while improving performance and resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42708-4.Keywords: PheWAS, GWAS, Canine, Behavior, Temperament, Working dogsSubject terms: Behavioural genetics, Aggression, Social behaviour

Introduction

Detection dogs are widely recognized as the most effective tool for detecting and alerting to diverse and evolving odors critical to security and law enforcement1. The increasing use of detection dogs in the recent past has resulted in competition for the limited numbers of available dogs from a few breeds (mainly, Labrador Retriever, German Shorthaired Pointer, Belgian Malinois, and German Shepherd)2. There is also great interest in optimizing working dog selection, training, health, and career longevity1. A rapidly growing aspect of that is research of the behavioral, genetic, and environmental effects associated with detection dog success.

Beyond the interest in working dogs, the reduced genetic complexity of dog breeds3 provides an exceptionally tractable model for understanding the genetic and environmental contributions to behavior. For instance, human complex traits have extremely high polygenicity and heterogeneity. Because many deleterious effects on behavior and cognition would have reduced evolutionary fitness, negative selection in human evolution has likely purged common variants with detrimental effects on these traits4. As a result, most behavioral and cognitive traits show few, if any, common genetic variants with sizable individual effects. Darwin noted that under domestication, artificial selection by humans dramatically relaxes the natural constraints on variation seen in the wild5. Domesticated species like dogs can accumulate countless deleterious genetic variants through several mechanisms: linkage to traits under artificial selection, insufficiently deleterious effects to be selected against in breeding contexts, or increases in frequency due to genetic drift or founder effects within subgroups of the population, such as geographic isolates or lineages from popular sires3. Thus, complex trait Genome Wide Association Studies (GWASs) in humans can yield from few to a great many variations that individually and collectively explain a small amount of the trait variance4. However, GWAS loci of the same traits in dogs can have moderate-to-large effects individually and can collectively explain most of the variance6. As this GWAS effect applies to behavioral and cognitive traits7–10, the hundreds of dog breeds offer unique and powerful approaches to dissecting brain development and function.

The field of canine behavioral genetics has relied heavily on behavioral questionnaires to measure phenotypes. The most popular questionnaire has been the C-BARQ, which was designed to capture all common problem behaviors as well as traits such as energy, excitability, and trainability11. There are several other questionnaires which capture similar domains12. Some investigators, however, have combined data from multiple instruments to assess a broader range of behaviors, including traits associated with specific breed groups, such as herding or pointing13. Genetic study designs have included interbreed mapping of breed averages of C-BARQ scores in unrelated cohorts of genotyped pedigree dogs9,10,14 and single or multiple breed mapping in dogs with individual-level data for both C-BARQ and similar questionnaires and genome-wide genotypes7,15,16. A couple of genetic studies included both pedigree and mixed breed dogs with individual-level C-BARQ17 or other questionnaire13 data. The 14 C-BARQ factors have narrow sense heritabilities in the range of h2 = 0.27–0.77 across breeds (mean 0.51 +/−0.12 s.d.)14. By contrast, a study of mostly pet Labrador Retrievers showed heritabilities of all 14 factors to be five times smaller than that on average7. While some of the highest heritabilities are shared in Labrador Retrievers and across breeds (e.g., stranger directed aggression and chasing), others are discrepant. For example, non-social fear ranks third for highest heritability in Labrador Retrievers but has the lowest heritability across breeds (ranked 14th). The heritability of trainability, a trait of great importance in working dogs, ranks ninth in Labrador Retrievers and first across breeds. The patterns of breed differences hint at selection under domestication, which is supported by Qst – Fst analysis showing all 14 C-BARQ factors are under strong positive selection14.

The Labrador Retriever is the most popular registered dog breed in the world (Federation Cynologique Internationale). They are also one of the most popular breeds used as working dogs – most notably for odor detection, guiding, and assistance work2. They have been studied for C-BARQ behavioral traits18 and heritability analysis and genetic mapping of the C-BARQ traits7. We recently performed a GWAS of elimination for behavioral reasons in Labrador Retrievers in the Transportation Safety Administration’s (TSA) canine olfactory detection program19. That GWAS provided the SNPs used to conduct the present study. Briefly, we used historical data for a breeding population with founders from US commercial breeders of Labrador Retrievers selected for field work and the Australian Customs Service. This program tested dogs four times over a one-year foster period that preceded selection for the training program or elimination for behavioral or medical reasons. The data included genome wide SNP genotypes and behavioral testing results, but not the basis for behavioral elimination. That work yielded six genome-wide significant and five suggestive loci. Haplotypes previously shown to be associated with problem behaviors across breeds and to commonly be present in pet Labrador Retrievers9,10 were absent in the population of our original GWAS19. This suggests that these behavior-associated variants may have been selectively bred against (i.e., individuals carrying them were less likely to be chosen as breeding stock) in both the U.S. hunting lines of the breed and in the Australian Customs Service, where dogs have been actively selected for odor detection work for at least 20 years20. For several loci that appeared to implicate a single gene, there was strong evidence of behavioral relevance for those genes in other species19. However, this cross-species relevance does not confirm the genetic associations identified in our study. In the second paragraph of the Discussion we note the challenges to replicating that study.

We performed separate studies of that same 2013 TSA cohort for associations of behavioral testing data and outcomes, specifically, success or elimination from the training program21. This showed that Machine Learning models could predict which dogs would succeed with reasonable accuracy but were less effective at identifying those likely to be eliminated. This aligns with other findings showing that training and performance data are more predictive of success, while temperament and personality assessments are better at identifying dogs likely to fail21–23. These findings highlight the need for working dog programs to systematically collect and integrate both types of data (work-related testing and temperament evaluations) to best understand and improve placement outcomes. For example, The Seeing Eye, an organization which breeds and trains guide dogs for visually impaired people across North America, successfully integrates both types of data – training records, C-BARQ and other behavioral assessments, and environmental factors – into their program.

The success of GWAS in identifying genetic variation associated with diverse traits presents opportunities to dissect molecular mechanisms4. It also allows a type of backwards analysis to identify additional phenotypes associated with GWAS haplotypes: Phenome-Wide Association Studies or PheWAS24. The approach starts with one or more genetic variants – often initially identified through a GWAS for a single trait – and tests their associations across a wide range of traits to better understand their broader physiological effects. These traits may be closely related to the context of the original discovery or span a broad set selected by any criteria, provided appropriate statistical correction for multiple testing is applied. The strength of observed associations can reflect how directly a variant influences core biological processes, with stronger effects typically seen for traits tied to primary biochemical pathways and weaker effects for more indirect influences. Thus, PheWAS not only identifies pleiotropic effects of GWAS variants but also helps to stratify those variants based on the diversity of associated phenotypes and underlying biochemical pathways. Here, we use PheWAS to bridge a Labrador Retriever phenotype from the original TSA odor detection cohort (i.e., used in the initial GWAS) to three additional cohorts: Australian pet dogs, UK dogs with mixed pet and gamebird-retrieving (working) roles, and US guide (working) dogs. Specifically, we use temperament and personality traits captured by C-BARQ in the three PheWAS cohorts to find associations with loci mapped for elimination for behavioral reasons in odor detection dogs (for which there is no C-BARQ data19.

Results

Description of GWAS SNPs & Labrador Retriever cohorts used for PheWAS

The SNPs used in this study were identified in a prior GWAS on a 2002–2013 TSA breeding and pretraining program using elimination for behavioral reasons as the phenotype19. This cohort included 296 genotyped Labrador Retrievers with 267 successful and 29 eliminated dogs. Because a complete pedigree for the cohort was available, we performed a family-based GWAS using the ROADTRIPS2 (version 2.0, https://faculty.washington.edu/tathornt/software/ROADTRIPS2/ROADTRIPS2_Documentation.pdf) software25. It reduces type I error due to relatedness by including the pedigree as a covariate in the association analysis and it increases power by leveraging familial relationships to impute genotypes for individuals with phenotype but no genotype data. That GWAS identified 21 SNPs. One additional SNP included here is from a GWAS of the same cohort but using GEMMA, which is part of separate ongoing work. GEMMA software generates and applies a relatedness matrix to its linear mixed model association analysis26. It was selected for inclusion in this PheWAS because it was the only finding for which both alleles were segregating in the two largest PheWAS cohorts (one of those cohorts was subsequently excluded after filtering for the minimum minor allele frequency). Both GWASs showed appropriate control of genomic inflation according to the calculated lambda values. In total, we used 22 SNPs in 11 loci here: nine genome-wide significant after Bonferroni multiple testing correction, and two suggestive (chr3 and chr4; Supplemental Table 1).

Three Labrador Retriever cohorts with both genotype data and completed C-BARQ surveys were used for this PheWAS study. The first two were pet and pet/working (gamebird retrievers housed in kennels; see Methods), respectively, populations predominately comprised of privately owned dogs from Australia (AUS)27 and the United Kingdom (UK)7. The third population was from The Seeing Eye, Inc. (TSE), which has a breeding and training program for guide dogs for blind people. Following quality control, a substantial number of SNPs within each cohort were removed due to fixed allele frequency (Table 1). Of the 22 SNPs previously identified in the 2002–2013 TSA GWAS, 13, 9, and 8 had both alleles and passed the minimum minor allele frequency of 2.5% for inclusion in the PheWAS for the AUS, UK, and TSE cohorts, respectively (Supplemental Table 1).

PheWAS and Intersection Union Test meta-analysis

A PheWAS was performed to identify which dog behaviors were correlated with each previously identified SNP. These analyses involved testing each of the 22 SNPs for correlation with each C-BARQ question. Hits were considered significant if they passed a p < 0.05 Bonferroni adjusted threshold calculated for each cohort. Figure 1 shows histograms of individual SNPs with C-BARQ scores coded by genotype. These tests resulted in a total of 15 hits spanning 8 of the 22 tested SNPs and 6 of the 10 defined SNP regions (Table 2). Seven of these hits were in the AUS cohort, five in the UK cohort, and one in TSE cohort. Additionally, a meta-analysis was conducted using the Intersection Union Test (IUT) to attempt to improve power by combining cohorts. This involved the calculation of the Fisher’s combined p-value, and findings were only considered significant if C-BARQ questions both passed p < 0.05 for each individual cohort and their combined p-value passed a Bonferroni adjusted threshold p < 0.05. In all, the meta-analyses yielded a total of two hits for chr19.21,040,815 in the combined UK and TSE cohorts (Table 2).

The C-BARQ factor that contained the largest number of significant hits was aggression, which also accounted for the individual question with the strongest p-value (chr1.24,927,539 and agg_25 in the AUS cohort). The significant questions involving social behavior are predominantly ones directed at familiar humans and dogs, except for excitability when visitors arrive at the home. However, that latter chr19 locus has several other associations - the most of any SNP - and the p-value for aggression directed at household members for taking an object away was over three orders of magnitude stronger. Chr6.76,632,282 showed associations with two questions in different factors but both questions had to do with anxiety-related behaviors, either when left alone or approached. Both SNPs in the chr36 locus had significant hits related to chasing small animals. The other two-SNP presumptive haplotype, on chr1, was also supported by both being associated with the same question.

Sequence analysis of the mapped haplotype at the ADAMTSL1 locus

As a follow-up to the original TSA GWAS and the loci supported by the present PheWAS – both conducted in Labrador Retrievers – we performed whole genome sequencing to identify candidate functional variants (Methods). This analysis was based on a random sample of seven Labrador Retrievers and seven German Shorthaired Pointers from the TSA odor detection program, enriched for dogs eliminated for behavioral reasons. These two breeds are currently the primary breeds used in the TSA detection program. Both were originally developed for hunting and are classified as Sporting Group dogs by the American Kennel Club. Among non-retriever breeds, German Shorthaired Pointers showed the third highest level of haplotype sharing with Labrador Retrievers out of 161 breeds, comparable to the second-ranking breed and to the Irish Water Spaniel, which is genetically classified in the Retriever Group28. While most mapped haplotypes are rare and absent in our small sample of randomly chosen dogs, we found evidence of the haplotype mapped on chr11. In this work, we showed it is associated with agg_33, aggression displayed when approached at a favorite resting place by a familiar dog. The risk allele of the peak SNP was present in seven carriers (four Labs and three German pointers). The two flanking SNPs in the mapping, with weaker p-values, were in imperfect LD as expected. We determined the haplotype interval shared by carriers to be chr11:38,564,807 − 38,745,391 on CanFam3.1. Most of it spans the gene ADAMTSL1, which encodes an extracellular matrix related protein. We next conducted mining of genetic and genomic data to identify ADAMTSL1 biology that may be pertinent to our study. High potential relevance to the mapped trait of elimination for behavior is implicated by human GWAS signals implicating ADAMTSL1 for brain structure, cognitive traits, and drug use (GWAS Catalog29. The strongest p-values in human GWASs are bone density, height, and myopia (p-values of 1E-300, 6E-63, 4E-51, respectively). That is consistent with strong mRNA enrichment in adult mouse osteoblasts and eye (eyecup, ciliary body, iris, and retina) (BioGPS, tissue mRNA expression30. We also found enriched mRNA expression of Adamtsl1 in the glomerular layer of the main olfactory bulb in adult mice (Allen Brain Atlas, in situ mRNA hybridization31), which could be relevant to the associated social trait, agg_33. A human normalized atlas of single cell types across the body shows predominant mRNA expression in excitatory neurons, followed by 6.3-fold lower levels in inhibitory neurons (Human Cell Atlas, single cell RNA sequencing32. That is consistent with that resource’s single cell atlas of the brain. Curiously, of the 26 excitatory neuron clusters, those with intermediate ADAMTSL1 expression were CUX2-positive33 but the lowest and by far the highest expressors were negative.

We compared the dog haplotype sequence to the matching sequence in humans, which is annotated for various evolutionary traits and deleteriousness prediction from diverse types of evidence, including human genetics (UCSC Genome Browser, hg38; see Methods). The strongest evidence of conservation at any variant sequence is in an ADAMTSL1 intron, in the middle of a 17-base pair element (PhastCons lod = 38; UCSC Genome Server, hg38). This variant position at canFam3.1 chr11:38,725,982 (C/T) has a CADD score for deleteriousness of 20.1, placing it in the top 1% of bases in the human genome (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion34). In Table 3, we show detection of the C/T alleles in canids and their frequencies if those were available. Based on these numbers, the alternative dog allele T has a frequency of ~ 7% in wolves and is much higher frequency in ancient, village, indigenous, and modern pedigree dogs, ranging 22–35%. Follow-on studies are necessary to determine the origins and history of the canid T allele.

To do so, we show the murine developmental expression of the two transcription factors in question (Fig. 2D/E) (while the rodent lineage has lost the element, we are not aware of a reason to expect that to have affected Tcf7l1 expression)31. Discussion

We report here the first phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) conducted in dogs. Using loci identified in a Labrador Retriever GWAS of behavioral elimination from the TSA odor detection program, we tested for associations with individual items from the C-BARQ questionnaire, which captures the most common problem behaviors in dogs. In three independent Labrador Retriever cohorts, the PheWAS identified six loci associated with 15 C-BARQ traits. Notably, the most frequently associated traits were four aggression items directed toward familiar dogs, overlapping with GWAS loci linked to program elimination in TSA dogs. Updated gene annotation of these GWAS/PheWAS loci revealed several novel candidate genes. To explore potential functional variation, we performed whole genome sequencing on a random sample of seven Labrador Retrievers and seven German Shorthaired Pointers currently in the TSA program. This enabled detailed analysis of a mapped haplotype spanning much of the ADAMTSL1 gene. We identified a top candidate functional variant located within a putative enhancer in an ADAMTSL1 intron, which displays a notable evolutionary trajectory across mammals. Transcription factor binding site analysis suggests this element is typically bound by the repressor TCF7L1/2 in most mammals, but by PHOX2B in simians. We hypothesize that the canine risk allele has reduced TCF7L1/2 binding. Finally, we present developmental gene expression data for these transcription factors in mice to support theory building. These findings highlight the tractability of the mouse model for functional testing of the behavioral effects of this locus and offer a testable hypothesis: that the variant alters ADAMTSL1 expression during development through changes in transcriptional regulation.

The original GWAS presents two challenges19. First, the cohort was the result of a one-time (2002–2013) TSA breeding and training program that used Labrador Retrievers from the Australian Customs Service and commercial sources in the US. Additionally, the Australian dogs were part of a breeding and training program that began in 1993 and were selected for the same work as the TSA dogs20. Second, the mapped trait was elimination for behavioral reasons, without specific phenotype data to explain the reason for elimination. Thus, replication of the GWAS was not possible.

The present work serves as an alternative to a formal replication of the original GWAS. The initial mapping was supported by strong behavioral relevance at several loci implicating single genes (e.g., CHD2, NRG3, and PDE1A); however, such evidence is not sufficient to validate the findings. Similarly, while positive PheWAS results lend support to the original associations, the nonspecific nature of the GWAS trait warrants caution in interpreting these results as formal validation. Future studies, including ongoing work in current TSA dogs with behavioral questionnaire data (e.g., CTC-BARQ45), will allow for direct comparison with the 2013 TSA GWAS and the present PheWAS findings. We anticipate that some associations will be reinforced and that their effect sizes can be more precisely estimated, ultimately strengthening the case for specific haplotypes. Beyond replication of genetic association, it is important to identify the responsible genes and variants. Below we discuss this work’s leveraging of the rich catalogs of human GWASs and of vertebrate genome sequences to nominate such functional variations. That can be followed by the creation of variant knock-ins in model species that have the relevant behaviors, possibly zebrafish or mice. Alternatively, with the owner’s consent, it would be possible to conduct veterinary clinical trials in pet dogs to treat problem behaviors using any approach available. This is possible because, unlike humans, dogs tend to have many moderate to large effect variations associated with diverse traits - as we showed in our original GWAS that led to this work19.

PheWAS results

The original GWAS for behavioral elimination in a 2013 TSA detection dog cohort of Labrador Retrievers19 identified 22 SNPs across 11 loci, which were subsequently screened in the PheWAS. Of those SNPs, only 13, 9, and 8 met inclusion criteria in the AUS, UK, and TSE cohorts, respectively. Given that all genotyping used the same SNP array platform, known to be reliable across these cohorts, the most likely explanation for the reduced number of usable SNPs is insufficient allele frequency in some populations. This may also help explain the overall low number of significant associations observed in single-cohort PheWAS analyses. For example, only one of the 13 single-cohort associations appeared in the Seeing Eye (TSE) cohort, a population that has been under long-term selection for guide work and may therefore have experienced negative selection against alleles associated with problematic behaviors. In contrast, the Australian pet cohort, which likely underwent the least behavioral selection, showed more nominal associations, albeit with consistently higher p-values, possibly reflecting higher allele frequencies but weaker effect sizes or less phenotype specificity. The UK cohort, with a mix of pet and working (gamebird retrieval) dogs, showed intermediate patterns, consistent with partial selection. Together, these observations suggest that both allele frequency and behavioral selection history shape the PheWAS signal. The most informative future PheWAS cohorts for studying common genetic variation in the TSA Labrador Retriever population may be working/hunting-line Labrador Retrievers, which are currently being analyzed in our ongoing work.

The PheWAS netted a total of 15 C-BARQ associations with 8 SNPs at 6 genome loci. The strongest p-value, in the Australian cohort, was for chr1 and agg_25, defined as aggression to a household member staring at them, which was also significant for the second SNP at that locus. The same locus in the same cohort was also associated with agg_13, aggression when an object is taken away by a household member. That same agg_13 trait also showed the second strongest p-value, this time for association with chr19, again in the Australian cohort. That chr19 marker had the most associations, including two traits each in the UK cohort and UK-TSE meta-analysis. It is discussed further in the Biological annotation section below.

Early life stressors exert a disproportionate influence on later behavior and risk of psychopathology46. While trauma has a profound impact, psychological and physical stress can also arise from neglect or deprivation. This holds true for dogs, and it is especially important that owners and foster carers of working dogs remain vigilant about the social and environmental exposures that are essential for successful working careers and those that may increase the risk of problem behaviors47. Odor detection dogs, for instance, undergo rigorous training while housed in kennels and often work in demanding environments such as busy airport terminals; their early care must prepare them for these challenges. Our PheWAS results highlight aggression, particularly when directed at familiar dogs and humans. We previously showed that common genetic variants with large effect sizes on both social and nonsocial fear were also associated with aggression9,10. However, distinct sets of variants were linked to aggression directed at familiar versus unfamiliar dogs and humans. Going forward, it will be important to determine whether this aggression reflects fear-based (reactive) behavior or non-fear-based (proactive) aggression, and to better understand its underlying causes. Identifying dogs at risk early in life could allow for tailored interventions to prevent the development of problematic behaviors. In contrast, the PheWAS finding related to food stealing is more difficult to interpret. While it may involve gene-environment interactions, it could also reflect innate survival behaviors, particularly those shaped by selection for scavenging during domestication48.

The chr6 locus was associated with urination when approached, petted, or handled, a behavior that may indicate fear or stress sensitivity. This finding may be especially relevant for working dogs, as the original GWAS was based on dogs eliminated during or shortly after the foster period in the 2013 TSA cohort. Previous studies have shown that working dogs are prone to separation-related problems and hyperactivity or restlessness17,49, especially when left alone for extended periods during early development49. The new association suggests that carriers of the chr6 risk allele may be particularly sensitive to early-life stress, including during transitions between environments. Identifying such dogs early could enable targeted strategies to mitigate these vulnerabilities, particularly during the foster period and the transition into kennel-based training environments.

The paucity of PheWAS signal in the Seeing Eye cohort invites looking at the positive traits. Both the SNP and trait were uniquely positive in this cohort: chr13 and fear_51, fear when feet are toweled by a member of the household. This may be interesting because it is the only fear trait identified. [Note: The C-BARQ item refers to a household member for dogs from a breeding and training program, like the 2013 TSA dogs in the original GWAS. That is because the questionnaires were completed by puppy raisers in the foster period.] Now it is possible to closely follow carriers of this allele. This begins with the foster period, which presents diverse grooming and health care opportunities to identify feet sensitivities and their contexts. The most common sensitivities of the feet are due to bad experiences with nail trimming. These can include stresses from restraint, unfamiliar/stress-associated environments, and extremely painful injury and associated medical complications. Another question that emerges is whether the stimulus for the dog includes aversion to towels for this association. This would be of interest to the TSA as some of their testing involves the use of scented towels. The two other signals in the Seeing Eye cohort were in the UK-TSE meta-analysis: exc_67, becoming excitable when visitors arrive in the home; and mis_74, chasing cats. These are common behaviors and are associated with the chr19 locus, which we mentioned for having the most positive traits and discussed below.

This PheWAS focused on behavioral associations. However, the original GWAS cohort consisted of TSA odor detection dogs during their year-long foster period, during which they were tested every three months. While olfactory acuity was not directly assessed, some tasks, such as locating objects by scent, relied on olfactory abilities. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that genetic variants associated with behavior may also be directly or indirectly linked to olfactory traits. While humans primarily navigate the world visually, olfaction is the most critical sense in dogs50. It was recently discovered that dogs are the only known species to have a direct and substantial connection between the olfactory bulb and the visual processing area of the brain (occipital lobe)51. The function of this connection is unknown, but it suggests direct integration of olfaction and vision. Despite these facts, we cannot yet determine to what extent smell is required to discern stimuli for traits directed at familiar vs. unfamiliar dogs and humans. Given the extreme importance of olfaction for canine discrimination, odor memory, and social interactions, it seems inconceivable that their sense of smell could be irrelevant. Conducting joint studies of olfaction and behavior will be important to answer these questions.

All four positively associated questions in the aggression factor and the one in the fear factor pertain to behaviors directed toward familiar people or dogs, typically members of the household. Consistent with that, the only positively associated item in the separation behavior factor also involves familiar individuals. Together, these items account for five of the six significant loci identified in this study. One additional significant association involved chasing small animals, which is distinct from human- or dog-directed behaviors. This finding is particularly relevant given the preferred use of retrievers and pointers as working dogs that must interact safely with the public. These breeds have a non-threatening, “floppy-eared” appearance, and there is essentially a zero-tolerance policy in the TSA detection dog program for aggression directed at anyone. Notably, in this study, aggression and fear were directed exclusively at familiar humans and dogs. This suggests a problem not with initial exposure to strangers, but with early social bonding, emotional regulation, or sensitivity in familiar contexts. These findings highlight the importance of early detection and tailored intervention during the first year of life, when dogs are developing core relationships and behavioral patterns47. Genotyping all dogs in working dog cohorts could allow identification of carriers of relevant variants, who might benefit from more individualized support during the foster period.

In addition to identifying significant hits, the C-BARQ questions were indicated as appearing or not in the new Working Dog and Canine Training Center versions of C-BARQ, referred to as WDC-/CTC-BARQ, which are slightly different from each other (Table 2; bold). Those are tailored to working dogs in general and the TSA CTC program more specifically. They were designed to monitor relevant problem behaviors and to remove questions that pertain to pets but not working dogs that are housed outside homes. The WDC-BARQ was validated in a survey of 1,117 working odor detection dogs45. Seven of the 12 C-BARQ questions significant here are captured in the WDC-/CTC-BARQ.

Biological annotation

Except for the ADAMTSL1 locus discussed below, we presented the biological annotation of candidate genes in the original GWAS paper19. The chr19 locus lacks a clear prioritization of candidate genes but merits re-examination given the new findings. This locus was the only one with signal in all three cohorts, had the second-strongest p-value, and was associated with the most C-BARQ phenotypes. Beginning with the strongest p-value, the chr19 PheWAS traits were aggression when an object is taken away by a household member (AUS), stealing food (UK), persistent barking when alarmed/excited (UK), aggression when stolen food is taken away by a household member (AUS), becoming excitable when visitors arrive at the home (UK-TSE), and chasing cats. It is possible there is a biological implication such as reactivity and excitability or excitatory-inhibitory imbalance, but further investigation is necessary.

Notably, there is no single gene implicated positionally in the chr19 locus. The nearest flanking mammalian genes are PLEKHB2 ~ 190 kb upstream and HS6ST1 ~ 940 kb downstream. These genes and others in their vicinity are enriched in the brain. We also noted previously ENSCAFG00000004229, an uncharacterized GLUD1 retrogene ~ 345 kb downstream19. Here we report its 5’ end is a single open uninterrupted reading frame of 445 amino acids that account for all exons 1–11 and the 3’ end is a poly-A sequence. It is present in the same genome location in the gray wolf and absent in the panda and cat (UCSC Genome Browser mCanLor1.2, ailMel1, and felCat9 assemblies, respectively). Based on the enhanced annotation mRNA tracks52, this retro-GLUD1 is widely expressed, including in the blood and brain. GLUD1 or Glutamate Dehydrogenase 1 is a major regulator of glutamate levels in the central nervous system (we use the HUGO official gene name and symbol; note some authors use this symbol instead of the correct GRID1 to refer to glutamate ionotropic receptor delta type subunit 1). This observation suggests the possibility of investigation of the retrogene in dogs and, if validated, testing if the mapped haplotype has altered expression and behavioral consequences.

Nomination of a functional variant at the mapped ADAMTSL1 locus and theory building

We identified the mapped haplotype overlapping ADAMTSL1 (aka Punctin1) in whole genome sequence data from our ongoing study of current TSA dogs. It is related to the ADAMTS protein family but lacks the protease activity. It is an extracellular matrix protein with both structural and signaling roles in axon guidance, cell migration, and synapse organization known to have conserved functions with those of the worm C. elegans homolog MADD-453,54. The discussion that follows regards the nomination of the most likely functional candidate and analysis of the potential biological relevance. It is important to understand these aspects are not intended to validate the mapping. Rather, these studies were done to develop testable theories.

The top-most evolutionarily conserved position that is variant in the mapped haplotype is in an ADAMTSL1 intron. According to its CADD score for deleteriousness, it is in the top 1% most constrained bases in the human genome. It lies in the middle of a 17 bp conserved element unique to mammals (PhastCons lod = 38; UCSC Genome Server, hg38). We hypothesize that the mammalian conservation of this element is due to binding TCF7L1 or 2. This is based on our prediction of transcription factor binding sites, but it is possible that another transcription factor with an unknown binding motif binds that site. As TCF7L1 is a repressor55, reduced or lost binding in the phyla mentioned - including in carriers of the dog risk allele - could increase ADAMTSL1 expression. We further hypothesize the loss or reduced binding activity in Anthropoidea, Artiodactyla, rodents, marsupial species with the deletion, and T allele dogs resulted in a reduced threshold for some aggression or fear traits.

Our analysis of Human Protein Atlas32 data showed that, on average, TCF7L1 is expressed two-fold higher than TCF7L2 in human inhibitory vs. excitatory neuron mRNA single cell RNA sequencing clusters and vice versa. We searched for evidence of their co-expression with ADAMTSL1 and found examples involving TCF7L2 in both humans and mice. Human co-expression occurs in a neuronal single-cell RNA sequencing cluster (brain cluster 38), defined by a shared expression pattern of 321 genes (Human Protein Atlas32). In mice, co-expression was observed in glucokinase-positive (Gck⁺) neurons of the anterior paraventricular nucleus of the thalamus (aPVT), characterized using a genetically modified mouse model designed to study this specific neuron type56. That study employed deep mRNA sequencing to compare Gck⁺ aPVT neurons with their Gck⁻ counterparts and conducted tract-tracing to map their connectivity. These neurons were shown to project strongly to forebrain regions involved in reward and stress processing, as well as hypothalamic regions critical for energy metabolism and feeding behavior.

The human and mouse neuron types mentioned above, as well as others, could be used to study whether TCF7L1/2 bind to the ADAMTSL1 regulatory element identified in this study. This element, along with its flanking intronic sequence, is present in rabbits but was lost in rodents after their divergence. To test whether the dog variant has behavioral effects in rodents, the corresponding intronic sequence would need to be knocked in, introducing both the reference and alternative alleles in parallel, for functional comparison. In Anthropoidea, the loss of TCF7L1 binding to the ADAMTSL1 element may have been replaced by PHOX2B binding in subcortical regions, where its effect could be either activating or repressive depending on context57). These regulatory differences, along with the other variants discussed here, could be studied in mouse knock-in models to assess their effects on behavior and underlying mechanisms.

In the same way that coding variations are catalogued and ranked by deleteriousness in diverse species, the approach presented here could be applied to genome wide scans of canine non-coding variations. This information would be collected with breed, linkage disequilibrium/haplotype data, and available genetic associations with traits. For informative sites, this circumvents the great limitation of interpreting genome sequence data of, say, hundreds of mammals with one representative of each species. Highly conserved positions may be variant in one or more species but will not be feasible to study because their variation is unknown, or phenotyping is not tractable. As we found here, conserved elements with variation segregating in dogs could be prioritized for investigation - especially when genetically mapped for an important trait - and lead to animal models in model organisms.

Mining of ADAMTSL1 biological relevance from traits mapped genetically and patterns of gene expression are consistent with our mapping for behavior in dogs. However, this evidence is so broad that it fails to rule out essentially any brain or sensory function. Traits genetically mapped in humans include visual acuity, behavior, cognition, drug use, psychopathology, and brain structure29. The highest mRNA expression in adult humans is in excitatory neurons of the brain, followed by inhibitory neurons. Adult mouse gene expression shows enrichment in the retina and other eye tissues, and the glomerular layer of the olfactory bulb. Transcript expression is present in the conceptus and in neuroectoderm during embryonic brain development, but we did not find spatial localization studies.

We recently reported that brain imaging shows that as dog body/brain size decreases, the ratio of cortex to subcortex decreases58. We proposed there that increased behavioral reactivity with decreasing body and brain size in dogs could thus be explained by that, since the cortex is generally more inhibitory and the subcortex more excitatory. Given the predominant expression of ADAMTSL1is in excitatory neurons in the brain, it will be interesting to know if this element is associated with variation in excitatory-inhibitory balance across mammals.

Conclusions

Major challenges in dog genetics include the lack of standardized phenotyping, interbreed structure, and controlled environments. Large, purpose-bred working dog cohorts can help overcome these limitations. Building on a previous GWAS of behavioral elimination in TSA odor detection Labradors, we conducted a PheWAS of mapped loci in three independent Labrador populations. The strongest signals involved four aggression traits and one fear trait, all directed at familiar people or dogs. This is notable because such behaviors are disqualifying in working dogs and may reflect issues in early development. The findings suggest that attention to behavior in familiar contexts, especially during fostering, could improve outcomes. We also highlight separation-related behaviors and excitability, traits known to influence working success. Finally, we demonstrate the utility of whole genome sequencing in identifying candidate variants and mechanisms. Together, these results extend a general GWAS of problem behavior into phenotype-specific associations that may improve prediction and prevention in working and pet dog populations.

Methods

Data acquisition and quality control

For this PheWAS study, three cohorts of Labrador Retrievers with available genotype and C-BARQ data were used. In cohorts with multiple breeds, we only used the Labrador Retrievers. The Australian (AUS) dogs were predominantly pets. The United Kingdom (UK) dogs were mixed pets and working dogs. The working UK dogs were gamebird retrievers in the structured activity of ‘shoots’ in which dogs of other breeds such as spaniels ‘flush’ gamebirds out of hiding. Such dogs are generally housed in kennels and undergo training with great attention to socialization, exercise, and health care. Data from published studies of the UK and AUS cohorts were obtained from those investigators, who are contributors here7,27. Data and DNA for The Seeing Eye cohort were originally sourced by H. J. H. from The Seeing Eye, Inc. and provided to this program in an agreement between the Battelle Memorial Inst. and The Seeing Eye, Inc. The samples used in this study are from dogs privately owned by The Seeing Eye. Because the data and DNA samples were from The Seeing Eye’s repository and a part of their normal data biological sampling routine, an IACUC protocol was not required to use samples from their repository. The Seeing Eye is a member organization of the International Guide Dog Federation (www.igdf.org.uk), meeting their standards for humane care of the dogs and veterinary standards. The samples used were previously taken by The Seeing Eye Vincent A. Stabile Canine Health Center, accredited by the American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA), for the purpose of health monitoring.

All genotyping in the original GWAS and this study was based on the Illumina CanineHD Beadchip Genotyping platform (the Embark Illumina version with ~ 220 k SNPs for the Seeing Eye cohort and the Illumina version with data for ~ 173 k SNPs for the others). Quality control of genomic data was then performed with the following settings in PLINK (version 1.9, https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink/): minor allele frequency cutoff of 2.5%, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium filter of 1e-5, maximum SNP missing rate of 10%, and maximum genotyping rate of 25%. The SNPs used in the PheWAS were identified in a GWAS of elimination from a 2002–2013 TSA pretraining program for behavioral reasons in Labrador Retrievers (n = 296)19. The top 21 SNPs identified were extracted, if available, from each cohort following quality control. One additional marker, for a total of 22 SNPs, was added from a re-analysis of that cohort using GEMMA that is part of an ongoing study. The software was utilized by first calculating a centered relatedness matrix then performing a likelihood ratio test linear mixed model on the quality-controlled data. The lambda value showed proper control of genomic inflation, and the SNP was genome-wide significant. It was chosen because it was the only finding for which both alleles were segregating in the two largest PheWAS cohorts here. The 100 C-BARQ questions14 graded on a 0–4 Likert scale were also quality controlled by first removing questions with greater than 25% missing data then removing dogs with greater than 25% missing data.

Phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) and intersection union tests (IUT)

A PheWAS was performed for each cohort in R (version 4.2.2, https://www.R-project.org/) and the PheWAS package (version 0.99.5-5.5, https://github.com/PheWAS/PheWAS). These results were uploaded into Microsoft Excel for IUT analysis using the Fisher’s combined probability test to combine p-values across cohorts, and Bonferroni correction to determine an adjusted threshold for significance. Manhattan plots, volcano plots, and relative histograms for C-BARQ scores based on genotype were made using custom scripts written in Python (version 3.9.16, https://www.python.org/) using the seaborn package (version 0.12.2, https://seaborn.pydata.org/).

Whole genome sequencing and analysis

In ongoing studies, we are collecting new data and DNA from dogs in the current TSA odor detection program, which are distinct from both the original TSA GWAS cohort and the three PheWAS populations. As part of this effort, a subset of fourteen TSA detection dogs was sampled using cheek swabs under the approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocol number 807,033 titled “Defining multiple dimensions of the working dog phenotype in client owned dogs”; Principal Investigator: Cynthia Otto (Table 4). All these methods were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations. DNA was isolated using the QIAamp 96 DNA QIAcube HT Kit (QIAGEN) and concentration measured by NanoDrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The quality of starting DNAs and sequencing libraries were assessed by capillary electrophoresis (High Sensitivity DNA Assay, Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer) and library yields were measured by Qubit fluorometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Whole genome sequencing was performed as 2 × 150 bp paired end sequencing at 20-fold coverage (Illumina NovaSeq 6000; Genomics Shared Resource, The Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center). The qualities of the reads both before and after mapping were assessed using FastQC (version 0.12.1, https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/)59. The samples had a uniform FastQC score of 37 or higher. That Phred quality score of 37 assesses the accuracy of each base call and translates to a base call accuracy of 99.98% (i.e., a 0.02% chance of an incorrect base call). Sequence data were mapped to the CanFam3.1 reference genome52 using minimap2 (version 2.28, https://github.com/lh3/minimap2) with settings for short genomic paired end reads60. The GATK (version 4.5.0.0, https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us)61 realigner tool was used to reduce the number of miscalls of indels. File conversions to a Variant Call Format (VCF) were performed using a combination of bcftools (version 1.17, https://github.com/samtools/bcftools) and samtools (version 1.19, https://github.com/samtools)62. Visualization and work with this data was performed with Integrative Genome Viewer (version 2.16.1, https://github.com/igvteam/igv)63.

For analysis of the WGS, the sequence data were converted to the VCF format. VCF formatted WGS were analyzed using the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV)63. Canine and comparative genome analyses were performed using custom UCSC Genome Browser64 tracks generated from the IGV VCF formatted data. Canine sequence analyses were primarily conducted on the UCSC CanFam3.1 assembly (Boxer breed; in some cases, compared builds and variations across the new build of the same Boxer individual, CanFam6, or the two other breeds available there, German Shepherd, Great Dane), with variants of interest checked for presence in three genome builds of Labrador Retrievers (NCBI Genome Assembly names, ASM1204487v1/ASM1204501v1, Yella_v2, ROS_Cfam_1.0). Conversion of genome positions from dog to human and other species was performed using the LiftOver function on the UCSC browser. The following UCSC tracks are curated reference datasets that were loaded for analysis of the present WGS data on the human hg38 assembly: Vertebrate and mammalian phylogenetic alignments and conservation statistics called, Conservation (100 vertebrate species)38, Cactus and Multiz (447 and 470 mammalian species, respectively)38,39; and Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion34 genome-wide annotation of deleteriousness of SNPs and insertion/deletion variants in the human genome.

Transcription factor binding site prediction

Transcription factor binding sites were predicted spanning variant positions prioritized high evolutionary conservation or other evidence of deleteriousness. Find Individual Motif Occurrences (FIMO) software (version 5.5.5, https://meme-suite.org/meme/doc/fimo.html)42 and the JASPAR 2022 Core Vertebrate set of transcription factor binding site motifs41 were used. FIMO scans DNA sequence with position specific scoring matrices, which define the binding site motifs for known transcription factors. The program’s output is a p-value based on the log-likelihood ratio score at each base in a sequence, and a q-value, which estimates the false discovery rate. Both alleles at variant positions were analyzed using FIMO with its default threshold of p < 1E-04. Motifs present only in the wild type or variant sequence, or which varied significantly in p-value, were manually analyzed by both sequence logo alignment and phylogenetic alignment analysis on the UCSC Genome Browser. Motif information and sequence logo visualization were accessed from the JASPAR database for eukaryotic transcription factor binding profiles41.
