Gene Therapy Strategies for Uveal Melanoma: Adeno-associated Virus Delivery Challenges and Translational Opportunities
Authors: Rebecca J. Wilson, Charmaine A. Ramlogan-Steel, William J. Deasy, Sarah J. Greenstein, Christopher J. Layton, Jason C. Steel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uveal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults and remains associated with poor outcomes once metastatic disease develops. Despite advances in local tumour control and molecular prognostication, effective systemic therapies capable of delivering durable benefit are limited. A range of gene-based therapeutic strategies have been explored in uveal melanoma, including immune-based approaches, gene-modified cell therapies and direct gene delivery. While these strategies have yielded important biological insights and modest clinical advances, their broader impact has been constrained by challenges related to delivery, tumour specificity, durability of effect and translational feasibility. This review surveys the current landscape of gene-based therapies investigated in uveal melanoma and synthesises lessons learned from these approaches, with a particular emphasis on the delivery platform as a determinant of therapeutic success. We examine the emerging potential of adeno-associated virus vectors as gene-delivery platforms for uveal melanoma, drawing on their safety profile and capacity for sustained transgene expression. We highlight the need for adaptation beyond native adeno-associated virus serotypes and discuss advances in capsid engineering, targeting strategies and control mechanisms that enable tumour-selective gene delivery. Finally, we consider translational challenges and future directions for integrating targeted adeno-associated virus-based approaches into therapeutic strategies for uveal melanoma.

Uveal Melanoma: The Clinical Problem

Uveal melanoma (UM) is the most common melanocyte-derived intraocular malignancy, arising from the iris, ciliary body or choroid of the eye. It represents approximately 79–81% of all ocular melanoma diagnoses and accounts for 3–6% of total melanoma cases [1]. Clinically, UM commonly presents with visual disturbances such as vision loss or distortion, photopsia, floaters, and serous retinal detachment, particularly in association with larger tumours. Additional presenting features include iris discolouration, pupil distortion and, less frequently, ocular pain [2]. Risk factors for UM include occupational or recreational sun exposure, lighter iris pigmentation, underlying co-morbidities, ethnicity and geographic location [3].

Uveal melanoma can arise anywhere within the uveal tract, with the majority of tumours originating in the choroid (approximately 80%), followed by the ciliary body (12%) and iris (7%) [4]. Iris melanoma, while less common, is often detected incidentally due to visible changes such as heterochromia or corectopia and is generally associated with a lower risk of metastatic disease [5]. These tumours are most frequently located in the inferior quadrant of the iris, although involvement of other quadrants has been reported [6]. Clinical features associated with iris melanoma include secondary glaucoma, angle seeding, ectropion uveae, hyphema and, in rare cases, extraocular extension [5].

In contrast, choroidal and ciliary body melanomas are associated with poorer prognosis owing to their anatomical location and more aggressive clinical behaviour [7]. Patients with posterior UM most commonly present with blurred vision, photopsia, floaters, visual field loss and metamorphopsia, although a substantial proportion of patients remain asymptomatic at diagnosis [8]. This asymptomatic presentation contributes to delayed detection and may facilitate early metastatic dissemination.

The incidence of UM varies markedly by ethnicity and geographic region, with the highest rates observed in populations of Northern European ancestry, including Australia, North America and parts of Europe [3, 9–11]. Australia reports some of the highest age-standardised incidence rates globally, with higher rates observed in male individuals and a clear increase in incidence with advancing age [3, 11]. Comparatively lower incidence rates have been reported in Asian populations, including South Korea [12]. These epidemiological patterns highlight the combined contribution of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure to UM development.

At the molecular level, UM is characterised by a distinct genetic landscape that differs fundamentally from that of cutaneous melanoma. Activating mutations in GNAQ [13] and GNA11 [14] are present in the majority of tumours and drive aberrant intracellular signalling pathways involved in cell proliferation and survival. Additional genetic alterations, including loss of BAP1, are strongly associated with metastatic risk and a poor clinical outcome [15, 16]. Gene expression profiling and cytogenetic analyses are increasingly used to stratify patients according to metastatic risk; however, these advances have not translated into effective targeted systemic therapies [17].

Current treatment strategies for primary UM are largely focused on local tumour control and preservation of the eye. Radiotherapy-based modalities represent the current standard of care and include episcleral plaque brachytherapy, charged-particle beam radiotherapy and stereotactic radiotherapy [17]. These approaches aim to induce tumour regression through radiation-induced DNA damage. While local control rates are generally high, these treatments are limited by their lack of tumour specificity and frequently result in damage to surrounding non-cancerous ocular tissues. Common complications include radiation retinopathy, optic neuropathy, fibrosis and progressive vision loss, and in some cases enucleation remains necessary [18].

Despite effective local tumour management, UM is associated with a high propensity for metastatic spread, most commonly to the liver [19]. At the time of primary diagnosis, approximately 2–4% of patients have detectable metastatic disease, and up to 50% of patients will develop metastases within 10 years despite treatment of the primary tumour [19]. The liver is involved in approximately 95% of metastatic cases, with additional involvement of the lung, brain and bone reported less frequently [20]. The ability of UM to form subclinical micrometastases early in tumour development, which may remain dormant for extended periods before progressing to overt metastatic disease, represents a major challenge for both surveillance and therapeutic intervention [21, 22].

Survival outcomes for patients with metastatic UM remain poor, with median survival typically limited to approximately 12 months following diagnosis of metastatic disease [23]. Systemic treatment options are limited, and UM demonstrates intrinsic resistance to conventional chemotherapeutic agents and reduced responsiveness to immune-based therapies compared with cutaneous melanoma [19, 24]. Regional liver-directed therapies can provide transient disease control in selected patients but have not resulted in durable survival benefits [25, 26].

Collectively, these clinical, biological and therapeutic challenges underscore a significant unmet need in UM management. The limitations of current local and systemic treatments, combined with the predictable metastatic pattern and early dissemination of disease, highlight the necessity for innovative therapeutic approaches. In this context, gene-based therapies and targeted delivery platforms represent a compelling strategy to improve tumour specificity, reduce off-target toxicity, and address both primary and metastatic disease.

Gene-Based Therapeutic Approaches in UM

A wide range of gene-based therapeutic strategies have been explored in UM, reflecting both the urgent need for effective systemic treatments and advances in molecular and cellular engineering technologies [27]. These approaches span immune-based therapies, gene-modified cell therapies and direct gene-delivery platforms [28]. Collectively, they provide important insights into the biological and delivery-related challenges that have constrained clinical success and inform the development of next-generation gene therapy strategies. Immune-Based and Immuno-Gene Therapies

Immune-based therapeutic strategies have been among the most extensively investigated gene-related approaches in metastatic UM, motivated by the success of immunotherapy in cutaneous melanoma and the strong rationale for immune modulation in cancer [29]. However, clinical outcomes in UM have been variable, highlighting fundamental biological differences and limitations in therapeutic delivery [30].

The most significant advance to date has been the development of tebentafusp, a bispecific fusion protein designed to redirect polyclonal T cells to tumour cells expressing the melanocyte differentiation antigen gp100 [31]. Tebentafusp comprises an affinity-enhanced soluble T-cell receptor specific for a gp100 peptide presented by human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A*02:01, fused to an anti-CD3 single-chain variable fragment that induces T-cell activation upon target engagement [32]. This approach enables targeting of intracellular tumour antigens that are otherwise inaccessible to conventional antibody-based therapies [31, 32].

In a pivotal, phase III clinical trial, tebentafusp demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in overall survival compared with investigator’s choice therapy in patients with metastatic UM [33]. Median overall survival was approximately 21.7 months in the tebentafusp-treated group compared with 16.0 months in the control arm, representing a meaningful survival benefit in a disease with historically poor outcomes [33]. Notably, objective response rates were modest, suggesting that survival benefit may reflect immune-mediated disease control rather than direct tumour cytoreduction. Treatment-related adverse events included cytokine release-related symptoms and skin toxicity, consistent with on-target activity against melanocytes [34].

Despite this success, the applicability of tebentafusp is limited by strict HLA restriction, as only patients expressing HLA-A*02:01 are eligible for treatment [35]. In addition, efficacy remains dependent on intact systemic immune function and does not involve direct delivery of therapeutic agents to tumour cells [31]. By contrast, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4, programmed cell death protein 1 or PD-L1 have shown limited efficacy in UM, with low response rates and minimal survival benefit reported across multiple studies [24, 30, 36]. This lack of responsiveness has been attributed to the distinct biology of UM, including low tumour mutational burden, reduced neoantigen load and an immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment that limits endogenous T-cell activation [16, 29].

Oncolytic virus-based therapies represent another immune-modulating gene-based strategy explored in melanoma [37]. While several oncolytic viruses have demonstrated clinical activity in cutaneous melanoma, data specific to UM remain limited [38]. Barriers to their application include challenges in achieving efficient delivery to intraocular tumours or liver metastases, potential inflammatory toxicity within the eye and pre-existing antiviral immunity [38]. As a result, oncolytic virus approaches have not yet achieved meaningful clinical traction in UM.

Together, immune-based and immunogene therapies have demonstrated that gene-informed targeting and immune redirection can yield clinical benefit in UM. However, limitations related to patient eligibility, modest response rates, and reliance on systemic immune activation underscore the need for alternative or complementary strategies capable of achieving more precise and durable tumour control [39].

Gene-Modified Cell Therapies

Gene-modified cell therapies represent a distinct class of gene-based interventions that have progressed to clinical evaluation in UM [40]. These approaches involve the ex vivo genetic modification of immune cells to enhance tumour recognition, persistence or effector function prior to reinfusion into the patient.

Several clinical studies have investigated T-cell receptor engineered T cells targeting melanoma-associated antigens, including cancer germline antigens expressed in UM [41–43]. By introducing high-affinity T-cell receptors specific for tumour-associated peptide–HLA complexes, these therapies aim to overcome limitations of endogenous anti-tumour immune responses. Early-phase trials have demonstrated feasibility and occasional clinical responses, but durable disease control remains uncommon [44]. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies have also been explored in melanoma, although their application in UM has been limited [45]. While chimeric antigen receptor T cells offer HLA-independent antigen recognition, identifying suitable surface targets that are highly expressed on UM cells while absent from essential normal tissues has proven challenging [40]. In addition, the immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment and limited T-cell infiltration characteristic of solid tumours have constrained therapeutic efficacy.

Tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy represents another adoptive cell approach evaluated in melanoma [46]. Although tumour-infiltrating lymphocyte therapy has produced durable responses in cutaneous melanoma, outcomes in UM have been less favourable, likely reflecting a lower mutational burden and reduced neoantigen availability [47].

Beyond biological constraints, gene-modified cell therapies face substantial translational challenges. Manufacturing processes are complex, time intensive and patient specific, resulting in high costs and prolonged vein-to-vein times [48]. These factors limit scalability and broad clinical adoption, particularly in UM, where patients with metastatic disease may experience rapid clinical progression [47]. Importantly, these therapies rely on systemic immune modulation rather than direct delivery of therapeutic agents to tumour cells. Traditional Viral and Non-viral Gene-Delivery Strategies

In addition to immune- and cell-based approaches, direct gene-delivery strategies have been investigated as a means to induce tumour cell death, modulate tumour behaviour or alter the tumour microenvironment in UM [49]. Early cancer gene therapy efforts predominantly employed viral vectors, most notably adenoviral and lentiviral systems, to deliver therapeutic payloads such as suicide genes, tumour suppressors and immunomodulatory factors [50–53]. These studies provided an important proof of principle that genetic manipulation of tumour cells or their surrounding microenvironment could influence disease behaviour, but they also exposed fundamental limitations related to safety, durability and delivery specificity that have constrained clinical translation.

Adenoviral vectors were among the earliest platforms explored for cancer gene therapy owing to their high transduction efficiency, large packaging capacity, and ability to infect dividing and non-dividing cells [54]. In the context of UM, adenoviral systems were evaluated for delivery of suicide genes, cytokines and pro-apoptotic factors, with the goal of inducing local tumour regression or enhancing anti-tumour immune responses [50–52]. However, adenoviral vectors are inherently immunogenic, leading to rapid activation of innate and adaptive immune responses that limit the duration of transgene expression and preclude repeat dosing [55]. These effects are particularly problematic in the ocular environment, where inflammatory responses can compromise visual function and exacerbate tissue damage. As a result, while adenoviral vectors demonstrated efficient gene transfer in experimental settings, concerns regarding safety, transient expression and inflammatory toxicity have limited their suitability for long-term or repeated use in UM.

Lentiviral vectors represent a distinct viral gene-delivery platform characterised by stable genomic integration and sustained transgene expression [56]. These features have been successfully exploited in ex vivo gene modification of immune cells, including T-cell and haematopoietic stem-cell therapies [57, 58]. However, the use of lentiviral vectors for direct in vivo delivery to solid tumours, including UM, has been far more limited. Integration into the host genome raises concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis and long-term genomic instability, particularly when delivered directly to non-target tissues [59]. In addition, lentiviral vectors exhibit relatively poor tissue penetration following local or systemic administration and lack inherent tumour specificity, further constraining their application in ocular malignancies [60]. Together, these factors have restricted lentiviral vectors largely to ex vivo applications rather than direct tumour-targeted gene delivery in UM.

Beyond viral platforms, a variety of non-viral gene delivery systems have been explored as alternatives aimed at reducing immunogenicity and simplifying manufacturing [61]. These include lipid-based nanoparticles, polymeric carriers and physical delivery methods such as electroporation [62]. Non-viral systems offer theoretical advantages in terms of payload flexibility, reduced risk of insertional mutagenesis and scalability. However, in practice, their application to solid tumours has been limited by low transfection efficiency, rapid clearance, poor tissue penetration and limited stability in vivo [63]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in UM, where effective delivery must overcome anatomical barriers within the eye or achieve sufficient accumulation within liver metastases following systemic administration [28, 64].

Collectively, experience with traditional viral and non-viral gene delivery strategies has highlighted recurring obstacles that have limited clinical impact in UM [63]. High immunogenicity, transient expression, lack of tumour specificity and inadequate delivery to both primary tumours and metastatic sites have emerged as consistent barriers across platforms. Importantly, these limitations reflect not a lack of therapeutic rationale, but rather the absence of delivery systems capable of achieving precise, durable and safe gene transfer in the unique biological and anatomical context of UM [28].

These lessons underscore the central importance of the delivery vector in determining the success of gene-based therapies. While early-generation viral and non-viral platforms established foundational concepts for tumour-directed gene therapy, their shortcomings have driven the search for alternative delivery systems with improved safety profiles, greater control over gene expression and compatibility with ocular administration [65]. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have emerged as promising candidates, offering a desirable safety profile, low pathogenicity, selective tissue tropism and enhanced transduction [66, 67]. Within this framework, AAV vectors present an opportunity to build upon prior experience while addressing the limitations that have constrained earlier approaches.

The Delivery Challenge in UM

Taken together, the gene-based therapeutic strategies explored to date in UM reveal recurring challenges that transcend individual platforms [28]. Immune-based therapies and gene-modified cell therapies demonstrate that immune modulation can influence disease outcomes, yet they are constrained by limited patient eligibility, modest response rates, systemic toxicity and substantial logistical barriers [28]. Traditional viral and non-viral gene delivery systems provide proof of principle for direct gene transfer but fall short in achieving durable tumour-specific delivery with acceptable safety profiles [63].

Across these approaches, several common limitations emerge. Achieving tumour-specific targeting without damaging adjacent healthy tissue is particularly challenging given the close anatomical relationship between UM and critical ocular structures [68]. Sustained therapeutic activity is difficult to maintain without provoking immune-mediated clearance or systemic toxicity. In addition, many strategies struggle to address micrometastatic disease, which is thought to arise early in UM progression and contributes significantly to poor long-term outcomes [19].

These considerations highlight delivery as a central determinant of therapeutic success in UM (Fig. 1). An effective gene therapy platform must combine a favourable safety profile with precise targeting, durable gene expression, and compatibility with both local ocular administration and potential systemic delivery to metastatic sites. The limitations encountered across immune-based therapies, cell therapies and traditional viral vectors underscore the need for delivery systems capable of meeting these requirements. Within this context, AAV vectors have emerged as promising candidates owing to their established safety record, ability to mediate long-term gene expression and suitability for ocular applications [69]. Importantly, advances in capsid engineering and targeting strategies now provide opportunities to address the historical lack of tumour specificity associated with wild-type AAV serotypes [70]. Adeno-Associated Virus Vectors in UM

The delivery challenges identified across immune-based therapies, gene-modified cell therapies, and traditional viral and non-viral gene delivery platforms highlight the need for vectors that combine safety, durability and precise control over gene expression. Within this context, adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have emerged as leading candidates for ocular gene delivery and represent a logical platform for further development in UM.

AAV Biology and Ocular Applications

Adeno-associated viruses are small, non-enveloped, single-stranded DNA viruses belonging to the genus Dependoparvovirus [71]. Wild-type AAVs are replication defective and require co-infection with a helper virus, such as adenovirus or herpes simplex virus, to complete their life cycle. These properties, together with the absence of known pathogenicity in humans, have contributed to the widespread adoption of AAV vectors for in vivo gene therapy [72].

The AAV genome is approximately 4.7 kb in length and is flanked by two inverted terminal repeats that are essential for genome replication and packaging [71]. In recombinant AAV vectors, viral coding sequences are replaced with a therapeutic expression cassette flanked by the inverted terminal repeats, thereby eliminating viral replication capacity while preserving efficient gene delivery [73]. Following cellular entry, recombinant AAV genomes persist predominantly as episomal concatemers, which reduces the risk of insertional mutagenesis and supports long-term transgene expression [74].

A major advantage of AAV vectors is their favourable safety profile and relatively low immunogenicity [71]. These characteristics have enabled extensive clinical evaluation, with multiple AAV-based therapies approved for inherited diseases and a large number of ongoing clinical trials across diverse indications, including ocular disorders [75–77]. The eye is particularly well suited to AAV-mediated gene delivery owing to its anatomical compartmentalisation, relative immune privilege and accessibility for local administration [77]. Intravitreal, subretinal and suprachoroidal injection routes allow high local vector concentrations while limiting systemic exposure [78].

Adeno-associated virus serotypes exhibit substantial diversity in capsid sequence, receptor usage and tissue tropism [79]. Several serotypes demonstrate natural affinity for ocular tissues, including AAV1, AAV2, AAV4, AAV5, AAV7, AAV8 and AAV9 (Table 3) [69]. This diversity has been extensively exploited in retinal gene therapy, where AAV vectors have achieved durable transgene expression with acceptable safety profiles. The clinical success of AAV-based ocular therapies provides a strong precedent for extending AAV applications to ocular malignancies such as UM. Importantly, there is a significant therapeutic overlap between oncologic and non-oncologic ocular diseases that further supports the relevance of AAV-based delivery platforms. Pathological angiogenesis is a central feature of several retinal disorders, including diabetic retinopathy and neovascular age-related macular degeneration, where dysregulated vascular endothelial growth factor signalling drives aberrant blood vessel formation [76]. Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor therapies have become a cornerstone of treatment in these conditions and have demonstrated that sustained suppression of angiogenic pathways can produce a meaningful and durable clinical benefit. Similar angiogenic mechanisms contribute to tumour growth and progression in UM, highlighting shared biological targets across malignant and non-malignant ocular diseases [80]. The extensive clinical experience with anti-angiogenic strategies in retinal disease therefore provides a strong conceptual and translational precedent for adapting gene-based delivery approaches to modulate tumour-associated angiogenesis in UM [81].

Together, the established safety profile of AAV vectors, their demonstrated success in ocular gene therapy, and the convergence of therapeutic targets across retinal disease and ocular malignancy underscore the suitability of AAV as a delivery platform for UM. These features provide a strong foundation for evaluating AAV-based strategies in this disease, while also highlighting the need to adapt native AAV tropism and control mechanisms to meet the specific requirements of malignant gene delivery.

Current AAV Studies Relevant to UM

In contrast to the extensive clinical experience with AAV vectors in inherited retinal diseases, the application of AAV-based gene therapy to UM remains at an early stage. Importantly, this reflects historical prioritisation of monogenic retinal disorders rather than biological incompatibility with UM. Early support for AAV use in this setting derives largely from retinal gene therapy studies demonstrating efficient ocular transduction, sustained transgene expression and a favourable safety profile following local administration [76].

Several AAV serotypes with established ocular tropism also exhibit features relevant to tumour targeting. In particular, AAV8 and AAV9 have been shown to utilise the laminin receptor as a co-receptor for cellular entry [82]. The laminin receptor is overexpressed in multiple malignancies and has been implicated in tumour invasion and metastatic progression, including in UM, where extracellular matrix interactions contribute to both local invasion and hepatic dissemination [83]. This observation has prompted interest in exploiting AAV–laminin receptor interactions for tumour-directed gene delivery, although direct clinical evaluation in UM remains limited [84, 85].

Specific preclinical efforts have utilised viral vectors to deliver TRAIL (tumour necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand) to induce tumour-specific cell death [86] and herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase (HSV-TK) as a suicide gene strategy to sensitise dividing tumour cells to ganciclovir [87]. Additionally, anti-angiogenic strategies, such as the AAV-mediated delivery of pigment epithelium-derived factor, have shown promise in suppressing tumour vascularisation in murine models [88]. These studies demonstrate that AAV vectors are capable of transducing melanoma cell lines and ocular tissues under experimental conditions and can elicit measurable biological effects.

Several biological and technical limitations have constrained progress toward clinical application. Wild-type AAV serotypes lack inherent tumour specificity, raising concerns regarding off-target transduction of surrounding ocular tissues [89]. The limited packaging capacity of AAV vectors restricts payload size and complexity, complicating the delivery of multi-gene or highly regulated constructs [71]. In addition, pre-existing neutralising antibodies against AAV capsids are common in the human population and can reduce transduction efficiency, particularly following systemic delivery or repeat dosing [90]. Although the ocular environment confers a degree of immune privilege, immune responses to AAV capsids and transgene products have been reported following intraocular administration [91]. As a consequence, transduction efficiency and biological effects observed in experimental systems may overestimate clinical performance, particularly for unmodified vectors lacking tumour-selective control mechanisms. Together, these studies demonstrate the feasibility of AAV-mediated gene delivery within the ocular environment, while underscoring the limitations of unmodified AAV platforms in malignant disease.

Why AAV for UM?

Despite limitations, AAV vectors offer several advantages that distinguish them from other gene-delivery platforms evaluated in UM. Compared with adenoviral vectors, AAVs exhibit substantially lower immunogenicity and support more durable transgene expression [71]. In contrast to lentiviral and retroviral systems, AAV vectors predominantly persist as episomes, reducing the risk of insertional mutagenesis while maintaining long-term expression in non-dividing cells [71].

From a delivery perspective, AAV vectors are uniquely suited to the ocular setting owing to their compatibility with local administration and favourable safety profile [77]. In addition, the predictable metastatic pattern of UM, particularly its strong tropism for the liver, raises the possibility of regional or systemic AAV-based delivery strategies for metastatic disease [92].

Importantly, the limitations identified in current AAV studies do not reflect fundamental barriers to the platform itself, but rather the constraints of wild-type capsids that were not designed for tumour targeting [93]. Advances in capsid engineering, detargeting strategies, and transcriptional control now enable precise modification of AAV tropism and transgene expression [93]. These developments provide opportunities to overcome the historical lack of tumour specificity associated with native AAV serotypes. Thus, AAV vectors should be viewed not as a finished solution for UM, but as a highly adaptable delivery platform that can be engineered to meet the stringent requirements of malignant disease. The development of tumour-selective AAV systems represents a critical next step in translating AAV-based gene therapy to UM and forms the focus of the following section.

The clinical applicability of AAV vectors in UM is fundamentally constrained by the lack of inherent tumour specificity of wild-type serotypes [93]. While AAV vectors exhibit favourable safety profiles and efficient ocular transduction, non-selective uptake by surrounding healthy tissues poses a significant barrier to their use in malignant disease [94]. As such, the successful translation of AAV-based gene therapy for UM depends on the development of targeting strategies that enable precise control over vector tropism, cellular entry and transgene expression. Engineering Tumour-Selective AAV

Extensive efforts have been directed toward engineering AAV capsids to redirect viral tropism, enhance tumour selectivity and reduce off-target transduction [94–97]. Directed evolution has emerged as a powerful approach for generating AAV variants with improved performance in defined biological contexts (Fig. 2A) [98]. By subjecting large capsid libraries to iterative rounds of selection, this strategy enables enrichment of variants capable of overcoming barriers such as inefficient cellular entry or neutralising antibody pressure without requiring prior knowledge of receptor usage.

Related approaches, including DNA shuffling, generate chimeric capsid libraries through recombination of capsid genes from multiple parental serotypes [96]. These strategies enable exploration of capsid sequence space while preserving structural compatibility and have yielded AAV variants with altered receptor usage, enhanced transduction efficiency and reduced susceptibility to neutralising antibodies. Peptide insertion represents an alternative and complementary method, in which short targeting ligands are introduced into surface-exposed regions of the capsid to confer new receptor-binding capabilities or disrupt native interactions [97].

Together, these capsid engineering strategies provide a toolkit for modifying AAV tropism at the level of cellular entry. However, capsid modification alone is often insufficient to achieve the degree of specificity required for cancer gene therapy, necessitating additional layers of targeting control [99].

Advanced Targeting Strategies

Beyond capsid engineering, advanced targeting strategies incorporate multiple layers of control to further refine vector selectivity and therapeutic precision. Dual targeting approaches combine positive targeting mechanisms that enhance tumour cell entry with detargeting strategies designed to reduce transduction of non-target tissues (Fig. 2B) [97]. Detargeting can be achieved through mutation of native receptor-binding residues or incorporation of inhibitory elements that limit off-target uptake [100].

Additional specificity can be introduced at the level of transgene expression through the use of tumour- or tissue-specific promoters and post-transcriptional regulatory elements [101–103]. While transcriptional targeting alone does not prevent vector entry into non-target cells, it provides an important safeguard against off-target gene expression when used in combination with capsid-level targeting strategies [103].

The tumour microenvironment offers further opportunities for selective activation of gene delivery [104]. Solid tumours, including UM, are characterised by hypoxia, aberrant extracellular matrix composition and dysregulated protease activity. Microenvironment-responsive AAV systems have been developed in which capsid unmasking or transgene activation is triggered by tumour-associated conditions, thereby restricting functional activity to malignant tissue [101–103].

Achieving efficient penetration within the dense architecture of solid tumours represents a formidable barrier to effective gene delivery [104]. To address this, strategies have been developed to enhance vector potency at the cellular level, including capsid surface modifications, such as tyrosine-to-phenylalanine mutations, which promote intracellular trafficking and facilitate endosomal escape [105]. Furthermore, co-administration with pharmacological agents that transiently modulate the tumour microenvironment has demonstrated the ability to significantly boost AAV transduction efficiency in preclinical models [106]. These advances are critical for clinical translation, as they may permit the achievement of therapeutic thresholds at reduced vector doses, thereby mitigating the risks of dose-dependent toxicity and immunogenicity [107].

Next-Generation Applications

The evolution of AAV targeting strategies has catalysed a paradigm shift in gene therapy, moving beyond traditional transgene replacement to enable sophisticated genomic interventions. This expansion includes in vivo gene editing and functional modulation, utilising AAV vectors to deliver compact editing machinery, such as Staphylococcus aureus Cas9, to precisely alter endogenous gene activity [108]. Although the inherent packaging capacity of AAVs imposes strict size limitations, ongoing innovations in vector engineering, including dual-vector systems and miniaturised effector proteins, continue to overcome these physical constraints [109]. Adeno-associated virus vectors also provide opportunities for combination therapeutic strategies, in which gene delivery is used to sensitise tumours to existing treatments or modulate the tumour microenvironment [110]. For example, AAV-mediated expression of immunomodulatory or anti-angiogenic factors may complement immune-based therapies or regional treatments for metastatic disease [110]. The development of multi-gene and logic-gated AAV systems represents a further evolution toward programmable gene delivery [111]. By integrating tumour-specific promoters, inducible regulatory elements, and microRNA-responsive sequences, these systems offer the potential to restrict therapeutic activity to cells that meet defined molecular criteria [112]. Such approaches are particularly relevant in the ocular setting, where preservation of surrounding healthy tissue is critical [113]. Recent advances in antigen-targeted cellular therapies also provide useful examples of tumour-associated targets that may inform future AAV targeting strategies. Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell approaches directed against melanoma-associated antigens, including targets enriched in primary and metastatic UM, illustrate how surface antigen expression can be exploited for tumour-selective recognition. Although these studies are not themselves AAV-based therapies, they highlight candidate targeting principles that could be adapted for ligand-directed capsid engineering, adapter-based targeting or layered AAV systems combining capsid tropism with transcriptional control [43, 114–116].

Collectively, these next-generation applications highlight the transition of AAV vectors from passive gene carriers to adaptable delivery platforms capable of precise therapeutic intervention. The translation of these strategies to UM will require careful integration of vector engineering, biological insight and translational feasibility, which are considered in the following section.

Translational Challenges

Manufacturing and scalability remain significant challenges for the clinical deployment of AAV-based therapies. The production of high-titre, clinical-grade AAV vectors is technically demanding and resource intensive, particularly for engineered or chimeric capsids that may exhibit reduced yields or increased batch variability [71]. These challenges are amplified when complex targeting modifications or multi-component delivery systems are incorporated, complicating process development and regulatory approval.

Immunological considerations also continue to constrain clinical translation [107]. Pre-existing neutralising antibodies against AAV capsids are prevalent in the human population and can substantially reduce transduction efficiency, particularly following systemic delivery or repeat dosing [90, 117]. Although the ocular environment provides a degree of immune privilege, immune responses to both capsid proteins and transgene products have been reported following intraocular administration [118]. These responses may limit the durability of transgene expression and restrict opportunities for re-administration, necessitating careful vector design, dosing strategies and patient selection [118].

The clinical trial design presents additional challenges in UM [17]. The rarity of the disease, heterogeneity in disease stage at presentation, and prolonged latency between primary tumour treatment and metastatic progression complicate patient recruitment and endpoint selection [17]. Moreover, the long-term follow-up required for gene therapy products places additional demands on trial infrastructure and regulatory oversight [119]. Together, these factors underscore the importance of aligning the vector design with realistic clinical development pathways from an early stage.

Future Directions

Despite these challenges, continued advances in AAV vector engineering and molecular characterisation of UM provide a strong foundation for future development. Risk stratification based on molecular and cytogenetic profiling has enabled identification of patients at high risk of metastatic progression, creating opportunities for earlier intervention using gene-based strategies [16, 17]. In this context, AAV platforms may be particularly well suited for adjuvant or prophylactic approaches aimed at delaying or preventing metastatic disease.

Personalised AAV platforms tailored to tumour-associated receptors or molecular signatures offer the potential to improve targeting precision while minimising off-target effects [89]. At the same time, refinements in capsid engineering, detargeting strategies, and regulatory control of transgene expression are expected to enhance safety and reduce immunogenicity, facilitating both local ocular delivery and potential systemic or regional approaches for metastatic disease [28].

The strong predilection of UM for liver metastasis presents additional opportunities for targeted gene delivery. Liver-directed AAV strategies, potentially combined with tumour-selective targeting mechanisms, may enable treatment of micrometastatic disease that is otherwise inaccessible to conventional therapies [120]. Importantly, such approaches may complement existing regional treatments rather than replace them.

Ultimately, the successful translation of AAV-based gene therapy for UM will depend on the integration of biological insight, delivery platform optimisation and clinical pragmatism [72]. As AAV technologies continue to evolve from broadly tropic vectors to programmable and tumour-selective delivery systems, their role in the management of UM is likely to expand [28]. Careful alignment of vector design with translational feasibility and clinical need will be essential to realise the full therapeutic potential of these approaches.

Conclusions

Uveal melanoma remains a malignancy with a substantial unmet clinical need, particularly in the metastatic setting where current systemic therapies provide limited and often transient benefit. Although advances in molecular profiling and immune-based therapies have improved understanding of disease biology and yielded incremental survival gains, these developments have not overcome the fundamental challenges associated with durable and tumour-selective treatment.

This review has examined the spectrum of gene-based therapeutic strategies explored in UM, including immune-based therapies, gene-modified cell therapies, and traditional viral and non-viral gene-delivery systems. While each approach has provided valuable insights and proof of concept, their clinical impact has been constrained by shared limitations related to delivery, specificity, durability and scalability. These experiences underscore the central importance of the delivery platform in determining therapeutic success.

Adeno-associated virus vectors offer a compelling foundation for addressing these challenges owing to their favourable safety profile, established clinical use in ocular disease and capacity for sustained gene expression. However, the application of AAV to malignant disease requires careful adaptation. Advances in capsid engineering, detargeting strategies and regulatory control now enable the development of tumour-selective AAV systems capable of more precise gene delivery than previously possible.

The evolution of AAV-based gene therapy from broadly tropic vectors to programmable and targeted delivery platforms represents a maturation of the field, informed by clinical experience and enabled by technological innovation. Continued progress will depend on integrating vector engineering with biological insight, translational feasibility and clinical need. With rigorous development and evaluation, targeted AAV-based gene therapy has the potential to contribute meaningfully to future therapeutic strategies for UM.
