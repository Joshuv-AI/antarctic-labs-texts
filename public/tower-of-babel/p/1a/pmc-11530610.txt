Integrated single-cell transcriptomic analyses identify a novel lineage plasticity-related cancer cell type involved in prostate cancer progression
Authors: Faming Zhao, Tingting Zhang, Jinlan Wei, Liang Chen, Zaoqu Liu, Yang Jin, Mingsheng Liu, Hongqing Zhou, Yanxia Hu, Xia Sheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundCancer cell plasticity is the ability of neoplastic cells to alter their identity and acquire new biological properties under microenvironmental pressures. In prostate cancer (PCa), lineage plasticity often results in therapy resistance and trans-differentiation to neuroendocrine (NE) lineage. However, identifying the cancer cells harboring lineage plasticity-related status remains challenging.MethodsBased on 13 multi-center human PCa bulk transcriptomic cohorts (samples = 3314) and 9 bulk transcriptomic datasets derived from PCa experimental models, we established an integrated lineage plasticity-related gene signature, termed LPSig. Leveraging this gene signature, AUCell enrichment analysis was applied to identify the cell population with high lineage plasticity from a comprehensive single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) meta-atlas assembled by us, which consisted of 10 public human PCa scRNA-seq datasets (samples = 93, cells = 222,529). Moreover, additional scRNA-seq dataset of human PCa, multiplex immunohistochemistry staining for human PCa tissues, in vitro and in vivo functional experiments, as well as qPCR and Western blot analyses were employed to validate our findings.FindingsWe found that LPSig could finely capture the dynamics of tumor lineage plasticity throughout the progression of PCa, accurately estimating the status of lineage plasticity. Based on LPSig, we identified a previously undefined minority population of lineage plasticity-related PCa cells (LPCs) from the human PCa scRNA-seq meta-atlas assembled by this study. Furthermore, in-depth dissection revealed pivotal roles of LPCs in trans-differentiation, tumor recurrence, and poor patient survival during PCa progression. Furthermore, we identified HMMR as a representative cell surface marker for LPCs, which was validated using additional scRNA-seq datasets and multiplexed immunohistochemistry. Moreover, HMMR was transcriptionally inhibited by androgen receptor (AR), and was required for the aggressive adenocarcinoma features and NE phenotype.InterpretationOur study uncovers a novel population of lineage plasticity-related cells with low AR activity, stemness-like traits, and elevated HMMR expression, that may facilitate poor prognosis in PCa.FundingThis work was supported by 10.13039/501100012166National Key R&D Program of China (2022YFA0807000), 10.13039/501100001809National Natural Science Foundation of China (82160584), Advanced Prostate Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Technology Innovation Team of Kunming Medical University (CXTD202216), and Reserve Talents of Young and Middle-aged Academic Leaders in Yunnan Province (202105AC160013).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

In prostate cancer (PCa), lineage plasticity frequently leads to therapy resistance and trans-differentiation to neuroendocrine (NE) lineage. To date, studies have demonstrated that lineage plasticity of PCa cells exists along a continuum, ranging from androgen receptor (AR) expressing tumors with low AR activity to AR-negative tumors that either lack or undergo NE differentiation. In this regard, in-depth characterization of the cancer cells with high lineage plasticity using single-cell-based approaches will deepen our understanding of the malignant progression of this disease.

Added value of this study

In this work, we established a lineage plasticity-related gene signature (LPSig) using 3314 PCa samples from 13 multi-center public transcriptomic datasets of human PCa. Based on this gene signature, we uncovered a previously undefined minority population of lineage plasticity-related cancer cells (LPCs) from a comprehensive single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) meta-atlas of human PCa. LPCs were characterized by low AR activity, stemness-like traits, elevated HMMR expression, and significantly correlated with PCa progression and adverse disease outcomes. Moreover, we demonstrated that HMMR was a prominent cell surface marker for LPCs. In particular, HMMR was transcriptionally repressed by AR, and was critical for NE progression of PCa.

Implications of all the available evidence

Overall, our study unveils a previously undescribed population of PCa cells with lineage plasticity potential, characterized by low AR activity, stemness-like traits, and elevated HMMR expression. Moreover, this cancer cell type may contribute to NE trans-differentiation and poor outcome of PCa.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most common cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among males worldwide.1,2 Although nearly all patients with hormone sensitive PCa (HSPC) initially respond to therapies targeting the androgen receptor (AR) signaling, the disease frequently progresses into a more aggressive phenotype known as the castration-resistant PCa (CRPC), exhibiting resistant to these therapies. The majority of CRPC tumors is still histologically classified as adenocarcinoma with reactivated AR signaling.3,4 However, with the application of AR signaling inhibitors (ARSi) like enzalutamide and abiraterone, about 40% of CRPC tumors display a more aggressive and lethal phenotype, transitioning away from AR-dependent luminal cancer cells towards alternative lineage programs with low AR activity.5,6 These AR-independent tumors further undergo trans-differentiation, loss of AR expression, neuroendocrine (NE) differentiation, and eventually result in neuroendocrine PCa (NEPC).7, 8, 9

Emerging evidence has illustrated a mechanism for PCa trans-differentiation known as lineage plasticity, suggesting the existence of a subset of AR + adenocarcinoma cells that may directly trans-differentiate into NE state.4,8,10,11 Encouragingly, advent of next-generation sequencing endows abundant depiction of potential lineage plasticity-related drivers, including genomic alterations (e.g., loss of PTEN, concurrent loss of TP53 and RB1, and biallelic loss of DNA repair genes),5,12, 13, 14, 15 epigenetic changes (e.g., EZH2 overexpression and DNA methylation),8,13 pioneer and lineage determinant transcription factors (e.g., SOX2, ASCL1 and FOXA2).8,13,16 Additionally, insights into lineage plasticity-related characteristics such as enhanced stemness and decreasing AR activity,9 lost luminal and acquired NE features,8 have further enriched our understanding in this field. However, there is still a lack of a robust gene signature applied to PCa samples to estimate the lineage plasticity status. Furthermore, these studies heavily relied on bulk tissues and snapshot comparisons of human PCa and NEPC samples, thereby missing insights into lineage plasticity-related cells and their impact on PCa progression at single-cell level. Lastly, how AR signaling inhibition modulates the early molecular events driving trans-differentiation phenotype remains to be explored.

In this study, we first established an integrated lineage plasticity-related gene signature across multi-center human PCa transcriptomic datasets, PCa experimental models, and a comprehensive single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) meta-atlas. Based on this gene signature, we identified a previously undefined lineage plasticity-related cancer cells (LPCs) characterized by low AR activity, stemness-like characteristics, elevated HMMR expression, and revealed their marked association with PCa progression. Moreover, we demonstrated that HMMR was a prominent cell surface marker for LPCs, and was correlated with aggressive progression and unfavorable prognosis of PCa. In particular, HMMR was transcriptionally repressed by AR, and was critical for the NE differentiation and aggressive behavior of advanced PCa cells. Collectively, our study uncovered a novel minority population of lineage plasticity-related PCa cells that may play important role in PCa progression and NEPC trans-differentiation.

Methods

Bulk transcriptomic dataset collection and processing

As previously described,17 we assembled 3314 human PCa samples from 13 publicly available bulk transcriptomic datasets of human PCa from corresponding databases (Supplementary Table S1). Besides, we gathered transcriptomic data for two patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, five PCa cell models, and two genetically engineered mouse models from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Supplementary Table S1). For all bulk RNA-seq data, Transcript Per Million (TPM) value was calculated. Considering the distinct dynamic ranges and batch characteristics between bulk RNA-seq and microarray data, we analyzed the aforementioned bulk datasets separately instead of conducting an integrative analysis.

LPSig generated by combing WGCNA algorithm and the NEPAL model

Co-expression mRNA networks of the PCaProfiler dataset were generated using the WGCNA package. An optimal soft threshold (β) was calculated to achieve a scale-free network. Subsequently, the weighted adjacency matrix was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), and the corresponding dissimilarity (1-TOM) was generated. The dynamic tree cutting approach was applied for module identification. To identify modules related to lineage plasticity, the module with the highest correlation to lineage plasticity-related features was chosen for further analysis. Furthermore, we utilized our recently developed robust NEPC prediction algorithm NEPAL,17 to identify potential critical genes associated with lineage plasticity. Briefly, the NEPAL model was constructed by integrating NEPC upregulated and downregulated gene signatures using single sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm to calculate NEPC risk score for each sample.17 In this study, genes with both high gene significance from the NEPAL model and high module membership from the WGCNA algorithm were designated as hub lineage plasticity-related signature genes (Supplementary Table S2).

Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data collection and processing

In this study, we collected a total of 10 previously published human PCa scRNA-seq datasets as PCa scRNA-seq meta-atlas from 93 samples covering various tumor stages (Supplementary Table S1). Additionally, we included a recently published epithelial scRNA-seq dataset based on FACS18 for validation purposes. GSE230282 stRNA-seq data was obtained from GEO database, and it had been previously annotated as NEPC coexisting with HSPC by pathologists.19 Data preprocessing, integrated analysis, and cell type annotation as previously described.17 First, we performed quality control, pre-annotation and doublets detection for each dataset. Low quality cells, defined as cells with fewer than 250 detected genes, fewer than 500 transcripts, or more than 20% mitochondrial content, and erythroid cells were excluded to mitigate potential interference with the further analyses. Genes exhibiting high dropout rates, defined as those expressed in fewer than 500 cells, along with those associated with mitochondria and ribosomes, were excluded to mitigate potential interference in subsequent analyses. After pre-annotation and merging, 222,529 high-quality cells with 20,055 genes from 93 samples were preserved for subsequent analyses. Scanpy (v1.9.1) was utilized for normalization, identification of variably expressed genes, and principal component analysis. The BBKNN algorithm was employed to mitigate potential batch effects arising from diverse platforms and protocols. This approach has recently been demonstrated to be more suitable than other classical integration algorithms for creating a PCa scRNA-seq meta-atlas.17 Clustering and single-cell distribution were visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with the Leiden algorithm. Subsequently, cell clusters were annotated based on previously reported marker genes17,20 and the combined automatic annotation method Celltypist.21 For epithelial cells re-cluster analysis, a similar procedure was applied. To identify cells related to the lineage plasticity, the clustering resolution was set to range from 0.1 to 3.0.

Identification of malignant cells

To distinguish the malignant cells from non-malignant cells, interCNVpy (v0.4.3) was employed to infer large-scale chromosomal copy number variations, following the corresponding tutorial (https://github.com/icbi-lab/infercnvpy).

Pseudotime analysis

To investigate interconversion and evolutionary trajectories of LPCs and NE cells, we applied the Monocle 3 (https://cole-trapnell-lab.github.io/monocle3/) and CytoTRACE (v0.3.3)22 algorithms following the demonstration notebook. Spearman’s correlation analyses were performed to examine the association between pseudotime and the expression level of genes or pathway activities.

Pathway enrichment analyses

As previously described,17 we conducted over-representation analysis and GSEA using the R package clusterProfiler (v4.7.1). For bulk transcriptomic data, we employed the ssGSEA method with the R package GSVA (v1.42.0). For scRNA-seq data, AUCell algorithm (v1.16.0) was employed to mitigate the impact of dropouts and inherent technical variability. Gene sets used in this study, including Hallmark (v7.2), Gene Ontology (GO, v7.2), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, v7.2), were retrieved from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB).

Deconvolution of cell-type infiltration based on single-cell expression matrix

In this study, we employed two deconvolution methods, namely ssGSEA and CIBERSORTx23 algorithms, to estimate the infiltration level of LPCs. For the ssGSEA method, we initially identified cell-specific differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of LPCs through differential expression analysis using the FindAllMarker function (avg_log2FC ≥ 0.8, p_val_adj <0.01, Bonferroni correction). Furthermore, the DEGs of LPCs were overlapped with the LPSig, resulting in 79 genes constituting the LPC gene signature (Supplementary Table S2). Lastly, the ssGSEA method was performed using the LPC gene signature for bulk transcriptomes. For additional validation, the CIBERSORTx algorithm was applied to determine the cell type infiltration proportions. To do so, we generated a reference signature matrix for all epithelial cell types from PCa scRNA-seq meta-atlas. Subsequently, we utilized the CIBERSORTx official web server (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) to estimate cell-type proportions for bulk transcriptomic datasets.

Re-analyses of published ChIP-seq data and ChIP-qPCR

Raw FASTQ files of published Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) data used in this work were downloaded from GEO database (Supplementary Table S1). The raw FASTQ files were filtered using TrimGalore software (v0.6.7). The sequencing data were aligned to the hg19 reference genome, and PCR duplicates were excluded from further analysis. MACS software (v2.2.7.1) was used for peak calling. Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files were converted to BigWig files using deepTools software (v3.5.1), which were further visualized using Integrative Genomics Viewer software (https://igv.org/doc/desktop/). Combination analyses of ChIP-seq peaks and transcriptomic data were performed using BETA software.24 Potential binding sites of AR within the HMMR gene were predicted using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net/). The predicted sites were subsequently selected for ChIP-qPCR analysis. The ChIP-qPCR assay was conducted using a ChIP assay kit (#P2078, Beyotime, China). As previously described,25 PCa cells were treated as indicated and then cross-linked with 37–40% formaldehyde at 37 °C. The cross-linking reaction was quenched with 125 mM glycine, followed by shearing of chromatin using a Bioruptor sonicator (VXC-750, Sonics, USA). The sheared chromatin was subjected to overnight immunoprecipitation with anti-AR or anti-IgG as a control. The antibody-bound chromatin complexes were immunoprecipitated using protein A + G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA and then eluted in elution buffer. Following this, formaldehyde cross-linking was reversed through overnight incubation at 65 °C, and DNA was subsequently purified. Quantification of immunoprecipitated DNA, as well as input DNA, was carried out using qPCR with designated primers. The specific primers utilized for PCR are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Clinical samples

As previously described,20 human PCa tissues, including those from patients with HSPC (Gleason scores: GS = 7, n = 2; GS = 8, n = 3; GS = 9, n = 4; GS = 10, n = 2) and CRPC (n = 2, Supplementary Table S1), were prospectively collected at Qujing Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University, and consecutive tumor sections were generated for IHC analysis. The diagnosis of PCa was confirmed through pathological examination.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and mIHC

IHC and mIHC were performed following our published protocols.25 For IHC staining, antigen heat repair was performed with 0.01 M citric acid sodium buffer solution (PH 6.0) after de-paraffinization, and 3% H2O2 was added to the tissue sections for 10 min. Then the tissue sections were blocked with 5% serum and then incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, and secondary antibody was added to tissue sections for 60 min at room temperature. Immunohistochemistry kit (G1212, Servicebio, China) was used for antigen detection. Finally, hematoxylin stained the tissue for 30 s as a nuclear indicator stain. For mIHC, human PCa tissue sections were stained with the following primary antibodies, in order: anti-HMMR, anti-CHGA and anti-AR (Supplementary Table S3). Sections were stained at each cycle using anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibody for 10 min, then reacted with corresponding Tyramide substrates (abs50012, Absin, China). Subsequently, DAPI (G1012, Servicebio, China) was used to highlight all nuclei. Finally, multispectral images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1, Japan). The protein expression in the histologic sections was quantized by the IHC Image Analysis Toolbox plugin in ImageJ 1.51j software (National Institutes of Health, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/plugins/ihc-toolbox/). Antibody details can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Cell culture and drug treatment

Human PCa Cell lines LNCaP (RRID: CVCL_0395), DU145 (RRID: CVCL_0105), and 22Rv1 (RRID: CVCL_1045) were procured from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Culture (China). All cell lines have been undergone authenticated using short tandem repeat profiling within the last three years by National Collection of Authenticated Cell Culture (China). All experiments were conducted with mycoplasma-free cells. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (C11995500BT, Gibco, USA) or RPMI 1640 medium (C11875500BT, Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS500–H, HYCEZMBIO, China), and 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Before R1881 or enzalutamide treatment, PCa cells were synchronized in the G1/S-phase using double thymidine block, as described in a recent study.26 Approximately 2 × 105 cells were seeded in 6-well plates with full medium for 12 h and then 2 mM thymidine was added for 18 h. For LNCaP cells, the medium was replaced again with 10% charcoal-stripped fetal bovine serum (C3830-0100, Vivacell, China) for 9 h and then 2 mM thymidine was added for 16 h. The cells were then subjected to 1 nM R1881 or 10 μM Enzalutamide treatment for indicated time.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol Reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, China) and then reverse transcribed by reverse transcriptase (R223-01, Vazyme, China). RNA concentration was detected by a spectrophotometer. qPCR analysis was performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q311-02, Vazyme, China) on ABI QuantStudio7 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The relative mRNA expression was determined using the 2−ΔCt method with GAPDH normalization. The experiments were performed in triplicates and repeated 3 times with consistent results. The PCR primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed as previously described.20 Briefly, cells were lysed by cell lysis buffer (P0013, Beyotime, China) added with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, G2008, Servicebio, China), cocktail protease inhibitor (G2006, Servicebio, China) and 1,4-Dithio-DL-threito (DTT, GC205010, Servicebio, China), and boiled for 5 min after adding loading buffer. 50 μg protein was added to the well of SDS-polyacrylamide gel for electrophoresis and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (88585, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The blotted membrane was then blocked in 5% non-fat milk in 0.1% Tween-20/TBS, incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S3) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated with secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Electrogenerated chemiluminescence imaging system GENE GNOME XRQ (Syngene, UK) was used for detection of the immunoreactive bands.

Colony formation assay

Colony formation assay was performed as previously described.20 Briefly, PCa cells were trypsinized, seeded on plastic dishes at an initial number of 5 × 103 cells, and cultured for 1–2 weeks. The cells were then fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde solution (G1101, Servicebio, China) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (G1014, Servicebio, China), and the total area covered by the colonies was picture by camera.

Cell proliferation assay

PCa cells were seeded at an initial number of 1.5 × 105 cells in 6-cm plates. Cell number was then counted by cell counting plate every 3 days for detecting proliferation ability.

siRNAs, plasmids and transfection

The siRNA target sequences used in this study (Tsingke Biotechnology, China) are listed in Supplementary Table S4. The human pCDNA3-Flag-HMMR-EGFP and control plasmids were obtained from Genecefe (China), while the human pCDNA3.1-CMV-AR-3xFLAG-hGHpolya-EF1a-EGFP and control plasmids were purchased from Obio (China). Cells were seeded at a number of 2.5 × 105 cells in 6-well plates and were cultured to be 60–70% confluent at transfection. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent (13778-075, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for the transfection of siRNA into cells. Lipofectamine 3000 DNA Transfection Reagent (13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for the transfection of plasmids into cells. All operations are performed according to the reagent protocol.

Lentiviral transduction

The lentiviruses for HMMR overexpression, HMMR shRNA, and control were purchased from Obio (China). Stable DU145 cells with HMMR overexpression were generated through lentiviral delivery of the HMMR overexpression construct, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Similarly, stable HMMR silencing in 22Rv1 cell lines was achieved via lentiviral delivery of the HMMR shRNA. Briefly, cells were plated at the density of 2.5 × 105 cells in 6-well plates and transfected with the respective lentiviral. According to transduction protocol, PCa cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection of 20. The cells were incubated with the lentivirus for 48 h, followed by screening with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 7 days.

Transwell assay

5 × 104 PCa cells were suspended in 100 μl serum-free medium and added to the upper chamber (8 μm pore size, #TCS003024, JET BIOFIL, USA) without Matrigel for migration and with Matrigel for invasion. The lower chamber contained 500 μl complete medium with 10% serum. After 24 h, PCa cells were fixed with polyformaldehyde for 15 min, washed twice with PBS, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. PCa cells in the upper chamber were removed, and images were captured using a Leica DM2000 microscope (Leica Camera AG, Germany).

Tumor xenograft

Male nu/nu nude mice were obtained from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China), and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a light/dark cycle (12/12 h). The xenografting and growth of HMMR stable silencing 22Rv1 cells or control cells were carried out following the protocol described in a previous study.25 Briefly, 22Rv1 cells were suspended in RPMI 1640 medium and mixed with an equal volume of matrigel matrix (#354262, Corning, USA). The cell–matrix mixture was then subcutaneously inoculated into the hind flanks of five-week-old male nude mice with 5 × 106 22Rv1 cells for each flank. Statistical analysis

The sample size for this study was determined based on previous work done in the laboratory. The cell line groups and animals were randomly assigned and subjected to blinding through a standard laboratory procedure. Only the personnel responsible for statistical analysis were aware of the group allocation. Statistical analyses were performed using the R (v4.1.0) platform, including Student’s t-test, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, one-way ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis tests, Pearson’s correlation and Spearman’s correlation. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to estimate the false discovery rate (FDR) for multiple testing. The Bonferroni correction method was applied to pairwise comparisons. The tissue samples from patients used in the survival analyses were divided into distinct groups based on the quantile expression of the proposed gene signature using the ‘surv_cutpoint’ function of the survminer R package. Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank tests was conducted to assess survival differences between the groups. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant with two-sided testing.

Results

Identification of an integrated lineage plasticity-related gene signature (LPSig) in a large-scale bulk RNA-seq cohort of human PCa

The overall design of this study is shown in Fig. 1. First, we re-analyzed a recently published comprehensive multi-centric bulk RNA-seq cohort,27 encompassing 1223 human PCa tissues ranging from non-tumor prostate, primary PCa, CRPC or metastatic CRPC (CRPC/Met), to NEPC (Supplementary Fig. S1A and Table S1). In the weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) procedure, we set the soft threshold β to 7 (scale-free R2 = 0.90), providing an optimal power value for constructing co-expression networks (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Subsequently, we identified 24 gene modules, each denoted by a different color, with the eigengene (first principal component of gene expression within a module) serving as the representative of each module (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, we calculated the correlations between modules and various clinical traits, including sample types (NEPC versus non-NEPC), sample pseudotime,27 and key lineage plasticity-related characteristics such as EZH2 signaling,13 loss of PTEN,12 dual knockout of TP53 and RB1,5 CRPCsig51 (a gene signature related to pre-exiting CRPC-like cells, castration resistance, and the evolution of CRPC/NEPC),18 elevated stemness and decreasing AR activity,9 NE differentiation, lost luminal and acquired NE features,8 as well as our recently described NEPAL model.17 Strikingly, the strongest correlation in the module-trait relationship was observed between these lineage plasticity-related characteristics and two modules (pink and tan), both of which were also associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 2A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S1C). Moreover, pathway enrichment analysis showed that genes from the pink module were predominantly enriched with terms related to lineage plasticity,4,5,14,28 including cell cycle, DNA damage and repair, as well as metabolic processes such as glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Meanwhile, genes from the tan module exhibited similar enrichment patterns as these in the pink module, with a predominant focus on terms associated with DNA damage and repair (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Furthermore, combining NEPAL model, high correlation coefficients (Spearman R = 0.94 for pink module; R = 0.66 for tan module) between gene significance and module membership (MM) were observed (Fig. 2C). Representative top genes included established genes associated with enzalutamide resistance and lineage plasticity (Fig. 2C), such as HMMR,26,29 EZH2,13 XPO1,30 AURKA,31 as well as DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs, e.g., DNMT3A, DNMT3B, and DNMT1).27 To identify hub genes derived from tumor lineage plasticity within the two modules, 327 genes with gene significance > 0.5 and MM > 0.5 were considered hub lineage plasticity-related signature genes (Fig. 2C; Supplementary Fig. S1E and Table S2). Additionally, we identified that the lightcyan module was enriched with a substantial number of classical NE markers,16,17 such as CHGB, FOXA2, POU3F2, and SYP, indicating that this gene module was associated with a terminally differentiated status of NE tumor cells (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S1F and G). Moreover, the magenta module was recognized as originated from luminal tumor cells, which showed the highest correlation with AR activity and luminal score (Fig. 2A). This module also contained many classic luminal-related markers and AR targets, such as KLK2, KLK3, and NKX3-1 (Supplementary Fig. S1H). To further validate the robustness of LPSig, we collected and analyzed 12 additional multi-centric bulk transcriptomic datasets of human PCa (Supplementary Table S1). The results demonstrated that the LPSig score was markedly correlated with the activity of lineage plasticity-related pathways in all datasets, whereas was conversely linked to AR activity and luminal features (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, samples with loss of RB1 or TP53, the critical drivers of lineage plasticity,5,13 exhibited higher LPSig score than their wild-type counterparts (Fig. 2E). This trend was consistent in PCa cells with dual knockout of TP53 and RB1 or enzalutamide-resistant status, both of which exhibited higher LPSig scores compared to their wild-type counterparts (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S1I). Lastly, in two PDX models of PCa, the LPSig score was tightly correlated with the evolution of AR/NE status (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. S1J). Together, these results demonstrate that the LPSig exquisitely captured the dynamics of tumor lineage plasticity during PCa progression.

Identification of a lineage plasticity-related cancer cell type in a large scRNA-seq meta-atlas of human PCa

Several studies have previously showed that lineage plasticity-related status in adenocarcinoma cells precedes the acquisition of NE status.18,32,33 However, accurate identification and in-depth understanding of lineage plasticity-related cells at single-cell resolution during PCa progression remains lacking. To fill in this gap, we first generated a comprehensive scRNA-seq meta-atlas from 10 published datasets of human PCa, comprising 222,529 single cells covering normal prostate (N), adjacent non-tumor tissue (Adj), primary HSPC (Pri), CRPC/Met from 93 samples (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Fig. S2A and Table S1; see Methods). Strikingly, AUCell enrichment analysis using LPSig revealed a pronounced signal in the epithelial compartment (Fig. 3A). Re-cluster analysis of 89,055 epithelial single cells further identified five well-established major epithelial cell types as denoted by key gene marker and signature expression,34, 35, 36 including basal, hillock, club, luminal, and NE cells (Supplementary Fig. S2B–D). Intriguingly, at a high clustering resolution (Leiden resolution = 3.0), a minority population of epithelial cells (1.29%) with the highest LPSig score was distinguished from NE cells (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S2C), which we termed lineage plasticity-related epithelial cells (LPCs). Differential expression analysis further indicated that LPCs expressed classic luminal cell markers and also shared markers associated with stemness, proliferation, and lineage plasticity with NE cells (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S2D). To further characterize the molecular features of LPCs, we conducted rank-based gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which revealed that LPCs and NE cells shared numerous activated pathways associated with lineage plasticity and PCa aggressive progression, such as AR-V, EZH2 targets, stemness, E2F targets, TP53/RB1-inactivated signature, and NE differentiation (Fig. 3D). These features were previously shown to be associated with AR-targeted therapy resistance and PCa progression.8,13,17,28 Importantly, LPCs showed activity of AR signaling to an extent lower than that of luminal cells and higher than that of NE cells (Fig. 3D and E). In keeping with the transition from lost luminal to acquired NE features,8 LPCs retained luminal-like identity with a moderate level of luminal activity and negative NEPC signal (NEPAL score), but exhibited the highest stemness score (Fig. 3E and Supplementary Fig. S2E). Besides, AUCell enrichment analysis of lineage plasticity-related gene sets unbiasedly confirmed the prominent lineage plasticity-related status of LPCs compared to other epithelial cell types (Supplementary Fig. S2F). Lastly, copy number variant (CNV) and cluster analysis revealed comparable high malignant rate of LPCs and NE cells (Supplementary Fig. S2G). To summarize, these results suggest that LPCs represent a lineage plasticity-related malignant subpopulation, exhibiting subdued AR signaling, elevated stemness activity, along with heightened lineage plasticity-related signaling.

LPCs are potential precursors of NE tumor cells during PCa progression

The trans-differentiation model of lineage plasticity suggests the existence of a subset of AR + adenocarcinoma cells that would directly transition to NE state.11 Considering the lineage plasticity characteristics of LPCs, we hypothesized that LPCs may be involved in the emergence of NE tumor cells. To test this, we first compared the cell states between LPCs and NE tumor cells. Interestingly, genes overexpressed in LPCs compared with NE tumor cells were enriched for lineage plasticity-related signaling, such as AR-V, NE differentiation, cell cycle pathway, and AR activity (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Conversely, genes overexpressed in NE tumor cells compared with LPCs were highly enriched for abundant NE related terms, such as NE signature, along with neuron migration and differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Furthermore, in CRPC/Met samples, LPCs exhibited loss of luminal differentiation, while aggressive characteristics were further activated compared to primary tumors, such as proliferation, stemness and NE differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S3B and C). These data suggest that LPCs may harbor NEPC trans-differentiation state.

Next, we re-clustered all LPCs and NE tumor cells, and revealed four distinct cell states characterized by unique transcriptomic profiles, including C0_LPCs, C1_NE_ASCL1, C2_NE_SOX2, and C3_NE_CHG+ (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. S3D). CytoTRACE dedifferentiation analysis22 revealed that C0_LPCs exhibited the highest degree of differentiation, while the three NE tumor clusters showed increased degree of dedifferentiation (Fig. 3F), suggesting that NE tumor cells may be derived from LPCs through trans-differentiation. Pseudotime analysis further validated the cell fates starting at LPCs, then progressing towards the three NE tumor clusters (Fig. 3F). Additionally, a significantly negative correlation was observed between pseudotime scores and luminal features, such as gene expression levels of KLK3, NKX3-1, and KLK2, as well as the activity of luminal signature and AR signaling (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S3E). Conversely, a significantly positive correlation was observed between pseudotime scores and NE features, such as FOXA2, ASCL1, SOX2, CHGB, and ENO2, along with NEPAL and CytoTRACE scores (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. S3E). Taken together, these results indicate that LPCs are likely precursors of NE tumor cells during PCa progression.

Validation of the molecular features of LPCs in an independent scRNA-seq dataset of human PCa

To further validate these findings, we collected and re-analyzed a recently published epithelial scRNA-seq dataset of human PCa based on fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).18 Employing the cell markers and features of LPCs described in this study, we successfully identified LPCs within this independent scRNA-seq dataset (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B). In keeping with the aforementioned results, LPCs constituted a minority group that could be easily mixed up with NE tumor cells. Yet, and consistently, these cells were characterized by being AR-low and NE-negative, which exhibited enhanced stemness activity and heightened lineage plasticity-related signaling (Supplementary Fig. S4B–D). These results suggest that the characteristics of LPCs are consistent in PCa tissues, but can be easily overlooked due to their limited abundance and similar expression profiles to NE tumor cells.

LPCs are correlated with PCa progression and poor prognosis

Taking advantage of the comprehensive human PCa scRNA-seq atlas we compiled, we observed a significant enrichment of LPCs in tumor tissues, especially in advanced PCa (Fig. 4A). Despite their limited abundance, LPCs were detected in almost each tumor sample, particularly in CRPC and mCRPC tissues (Fig. 4B). CIBERSORTx deconvolute analysis23 on two bulk RNA-seq datasets further confirmed a significant increase in the proportion of LPCs with the Gleason score of the primary PCa (Supplementary Fig. S5A), indicating that LPCs were pre-existing in primary HSPC, and their abundance increased with tumor progression. Meanwhile, a positive correlation between the frequency of LPCs and NE tumor cells was observed (Fig. 4C). By overlapping LPSig with cell-specific DEGs (avg_log2FC ≥ 0.8 and p_val_adj < 0.01, Bonferroni correction) of LPCs, we identified an LPC gene signature (Supplementary Table S2; see Methods). Interestingly, higher activity of LPC gene signature was significantly associated with both biochemical recurrence (BCR) in patients with HSPC and poor overall survival (OS) in patients with advanced PCa across 9 independent cohorts (Fig. 4D). Deconvolution analyses on four PCa datasets validated these observations (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S5B). According to the available ARSi treatment information from the SU2C cohort, LPCs also reliably predicted resistance to ARSi (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Additionally, the estimated abundance of LPCs consistently exhibited a negative correlation with AR activity, while a positive correlation with stemness37 and NEPAL score across four PCa datasets (Supplementary Fig. S5C–E). HMMR is a representative cell surface marker for LPCs

Next, we investigated the expression pattern of the cell surface proteins in LPCs. Intriguingly, by ranking cell surface markers expressed in LPCs, we found that HMMR (also known as CD168 or RHAMM), a novel hyaluronan-mediated motility receptor and a microtubule-associated spindle assembly factor,38 ranked as the top hit (Fig. 4F). Further analysis unveiled the specific expression of HMMR in LPCs, as depicted in our PCa scRNA-seq meta-atlas (Fig. 4G) and an independent validation scRNA-seq dataset (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Besides, the expression of HMMR was robustly correlated with the LPC gene signature and the estimated abundance of LPCs in multiple datasets (Supplementary Fig. S6B and C). Moreover, HMMR expression was markedly associated with PCa progression and adverse disease outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S6D–F). Importantly, multiplexed immunohistochemistry (mIHC) revealed an increase in the proportion of AR+/HMMR+/CHGA− cells with PCa progression (Fig. 4H). Interestingly, AR+/HMMR+/CHGA− cells exhibited significantly lower AR signal compared to AR+/HMMR−/CHGA− luminal cancer cells at each tumor grade (Fig. 4H and I). Using an additional spatial transcriptome sequencing (stRNA-seq) slice of NEPC coexisting with HSPC19 to compare the spatial distribution of HMMR expression between NEPC and HSPC sections, we observed that HMMR-positive spots was mainly enriched in the NEPC region and exhibited lower AR activity compared to the HSPC region (Fig. 4J). Collectively, these findings indicate that HMMR serves as a representative cell surface marker for LPCs.

Even though AR is known to function as a transcription activator, it occasionally plays a transcriptionally repressive role.39 To test whether AR signaling regulates the expression of HMMR, we first inquired two published transcription profiles of LNCaP cells under conditions of AR activation or suppression. The results showed that HMMR expression was down-regulated in LNCaP cells treated with 5α-dihydrotestosterone (DHT) after 16 h, but was up-regulated following AR siRNA or enzalutamide treatment (Supplementary Fig. S7E). For validation, we treated LNCaP cells with the synthetic androgen R1881, which led to activation of classical AR targets, including KLK2, KLK3, and NKX3-1, accompanied by a concurrent decrease in HMMR expression after 24 h (Fig. 5B). Conversely, treatment of LNCaP cells with enzalutamide resulted in an elevation of HMMR mRNA expression, as well as that of EZH2 and ENO2, accompanied by decreased expression of KLK2, KLK3, and NKX3-1 (Fig. 5C). Moreover, R1881 treatment robustly down-regulated the HMMR protein expression compared to DMSO control group in LNCaP cells, which was effectively reversed by enzalutamide treatment (Fig. 5D). Besides, we observed higher protein expression of HMMR in DU145 (an AR-negative and enzalutamide-resistant cell line) and 22Rv1 cells (intermediate androgen sensitivity and NEPC-like cell line40) compared to LNCaP cells (Fig. 5D). By transfecting DU145 cells with AR-overexpression (hAR) or control plasmid, we observed that re-introduction of AR resulted in a sharp downregulation of HMMR expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. S7F and G). These findings suggest the potential negative regulation of HMMR expression by AR.

To further investigate whether AR directly regulates HMMR expression, we interrogated the AR ChIP-seq and transcription profile data of LNCaP cells.41 BETA analysis24 was then employed to integrate the ChIP-seq and transcription profile data. To our surprise, HMMR emerged as one of the top direct negative regulatory targets for AR (Fig. 5E). Besides, several well-known AR direct repressive targets were identified,39,42 such as SI, OPRK1, and DDC, whereas classical AR activated targets were highly enriched at the top, such as KLK2, KLK3, and NKX3-1 (Fig. 5E). In addition to HMMR, several markers of LPCs (HMGB2 and BIRC5) and basal/hillock cells (APOBEC3A, SERPIN1, and IL1RN), along with key epigenetic regulators (SUZ12, EZH2, and DNMT3A), were also identified as direct negative regulatory targets for AR (Fig. 5E). Notably, the AR binding at HMMR intron 1 was corroborated in multiple PCa cell lines exposed to different AR agonists (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. S7H), including LNCaP cells (GSE161167 and GSE43791) and VCaP cells (GSE55062).43, 44, 45 Furthermore, a consensus androgen response element (ARE, JASPAR relative score = 0.94) within the AR-bound intronic sequences was identified, which showed high homology with the canonical ARE sequence (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. S7H and I). To validate these observations, we conducted individual ChIP-qPCR assay for AR on LNCaP cells treated with 1 nM R1881. Significantly enriched AR binding was observed at the HMMR intron 1 in R1881-treated LNCaP cells compared to control, with KLK3 promoter serving as a positive control (Fig. 5G). A sequence of the HMMR transcription start site as a negative control demonstrated no significant AR occupancy (Fig. 5G). Similar results were obtained in AR-overexpressed DU145 cells (Supplementary Fig. S7J). Taken together, these findings indicate that HMMR is transcriptionally suppressed by AR.

For further validation of these findings, we transiently repressed HMMR expression by two different siRNA (siHMMR-1 and siHMMR-2) in two advanced PCa cell lines, DU145 (AR-negative and NE-negative) and 22Rv1 (AR-positive and NE-intermediate), that have been frequently adopted to study NE phenotype and differentiation in PCa (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S8B).27,46,47 Both DU145 and 22Rv1 cells with HMMR knockdown showed decreased expression of AURKA and EZH2 (Fig. 6C and Supplementary Fig. S8B), confirming the results of the RNA-seq analysis. Conversely, HMMR ectopic expression in LNCaP cells induced the expression of AURKA and EZH2 compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. S8C). Moreover, HMMR suppression significantly elevated the level of AR targets such as KLK2, KLK3, and NKX3-1 in 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 6C), which concurrently reduced the expression of NE differentiation markers such as CHGA and SYP (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, Western blot and mIHC analyses revealed that HMMR suppression increased AR protein expression and decreased CHGA protein expression (Fig. 6E and F). These results indicate that HMMR may regulate AR signaling and NE differentiation in advanced PCa cells. Given that acquiring the ability of proliferative and invasive abilities is one of the important features of NEPC,4 we further evaluate the requirement of HMMR in advanced PCa cells by cell functional assays. First, we employed siRNA-mediated genetic inhibition of HMMR in DU145 and 22Rv1 cells, resulting in a notable reduction in cell growth and colony-forming capability (Fig. 6G and H). Conversely, ectopic expression of HMMR enhanced colony-forming capability and cell growth in LNCaP cells (Supplementary Fig. S8D and E). Furthermore, transwell assays revealed that HMMR knockdown limited cell migration and invasion in DU145 and 22Rv1 cells (Fig. 6I and J), while HMMR overexpression in LNCaP cells led to the opposite effect compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. S8F).

To further evaluate the role of HMMR in NE differentiation, we ectopically expressed HMMR in DU145 cells, an AR- and NE-negative PCa cell line, by using HMMR overexpression lentivirus (DU145HMMR) (Supplementary Fig. S8G). Consistently, DU145HMMR cells showed elevated expression of AURKA and EZH2 compared to control vector (DU145Vec) cells (Supplementary Fig. S8G). Moreover, DU145HMMR cells exhibited the emergence of CHGA protein expression after 7 weeks of cultivation (Supplementary Fig. S8H), suggesting the occurrence of NEPC differentiation. Additionally, functional assays demonstrated that DU145HMMR cells exhibited higher proliferative and invasive abilities compared to DU145Vec cells (Supplementary Fig. S8I–K). Moreover, stable overexpression of HMMR in 22Rv1 cells significantly upregulated the expression of NEPC-related genes, such as EZH2, AURKA, CHGA, and SYP (Supplementary Fig. S8L).

To investigate the long-term effects of HMMR ablation on AR signaling and NE differentiation, we stably depleted HMMR expression in 22Rv1 cells, an AR- and NE-positive PCa cell line, by lentiviral transduction of shRNA (shHMMR) (Fig. 7A and B). Consistent with previous observations, shHMMR 22Rv1 cells showed up-regulated AR and PSA expression compared to control (shNC) cells, indicating enhanced AR signaling (Fig. 7A and B). Meanwhile, mIHC staining showed that low AR expression cells exhibited concordant high HMMR expression in shNC cells (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, HMMR silencing significantly reduced the protein expression of CHGA compared to controls (Fig. 7A and B), indicating the suppression of NE differentiation. To evaluate whether HMMR supports NEPC tumor behavior in vivo, we established a nude mice xenograft model by subcutaneously inoculated 22Rv1 cells, which displayed high level of HMMR and the NE markers (e.g., CHGA).47 Strikingly, tumors derived from shHMMR cells were significantly smaller than those from shNC cells (Fig. 7C and D), without causing systemic toxicity (Supplementary Fig. S8M). By characterizing tumor samples, we observed lower HMMR protein expression in shHMMR 22Rv1 tumors compared to controls, indicating sustainable HMMR knockdown in vivo (Fig. 7E and F). We also found the number of Ki67-positive cells significantly decreased in shHMMR tumors, indicating a decrease in tumor cell proliferation after HMMR silencing (Fig. 7E and F). Meanwhile, the number of cleaved Caspase-3-positive cells significantly increased (Fig. 7E and F), suggesting enhanced apoptosis levels in tumor cells after HMMR silencing. Notably, HMMR silencing significantly upregulated the protein expression of AR and PSA compared to controls, while reduced the expression of CHGA (Fig. 7E and F), indicating that HMMR silencing upregulated AR signaling while inhibited the NE phenotype. Together, these results suggest a critical role of HMMR in supporting NEPC tumor growth in vivo.

Discussion

Lineage plasticity in PCa cells exists along a continuum, ranging from AR expressing tumors with low AR activity to AR-negative tumors that either lack or undergo NE differentiation.7, 8, 9 However, our understanding of such transition during PCa progression at the single-cell level remains inadequate. Here, we identified a minority population of luminal-like cancer cells with higher lineage plasticity that were marked by low AR activity, stemness-like characteristics, and elevated HMMR expression (Fig. 8). We further demonstrated the pivotal role of HMMR in promoting NEPC trans-differentiation in advanced PCa.Fig. 8Schematic summary of the findings from the present study.

Schematic summary of the findings from the present study.

Recent studies have reported several potential lineage plasticity-related drivers and characteristics.4,8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14,16 However, these studies may not fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of the disease, due to their reliance on typical snapshot comparison between PCa and NEPC bulk samples, and the limited sample sizes. To overcome these limitations, we first combined a large bulk transcriptomic profile of human PCa at various disease stages and multiple lineage plasticity-related gene sets, and identified a comprehensive and robust gene signature that included various gene markers associated with lineage plasticity, termed LPSig. Furthermore, using multiple bulk transcriptomic datasets of human PCa samples and experimental models, we validated that LPSig was highly correlated with numerous lineage plasticity-related pathways, such as cell cycle, DNA damage and repair, EZH2 signaling, NE differentiation, and dual knockout of TP53 and RB1.8,13 Given that LPSig does not encompass classical NE markers, we speculate that it may reflect a gene expression program associated with lineage plasticity-related cells. Indeed, recent studies have showed that lineage plasticity-related status in adenocarcinoma cells precedes the acquisition of NE status.18,32,33 In sum, we establish a lineage plasticity-related gene signature, which allows for sensitive prediction of lineage plasticity status in PCa.

To further characterize lineage plasticity-related cells at single-cell resolution during PCa progression, we first generated a comprehensive scRNA-seq meta-atlas comprising 222,529 single cells and 93 samples from multi-center datasets of human PCa, covering various disease stages. Interestingly, the LPSig gene signature led us to identify a previously undefined population of lineage plasticity-related PCa cells, termed LPCs. Detailed investigation revealed that LPCs displayed low AR activity, stemness-like traits, elevated HMMR expression, and exhibited luminal-like characteristics with the potential for NE differentiation. This is consistent with previous studies showing that a small subset of luminal-like cancer cells with high lineage plasticity could gradually reduce the dependency on AR signaling, lose their luminal identity, and acquire NE features under therapeutic pressure.8,9 Finally, by leveraging multiple PCa transcriptomic datasets and mIHC tissue staining, we demonstrated that LPCs were associated with inferior prognosis in PCa patients and significantly enriched in advanced PCa tissues. These findings provide new insights into a novel cancer cell population with high lineage plasticity at single-cell resolution, revealing its molecular characteristics and clinical relevance during PCa progression.

scRNA-seq techniques have been widely utilized to identify gene markers specific to cell types.34 To further identify potential gene markers of LPCs, we examined the expression patterns of the cell surface proteins, and identified HMMR as one of the top specific cell surface markers for LPCs. Furthermore, mIHC confirmed the presence of ARlow/HMMRhigh/CHGAnegative LPCs in PCa patient specimens, whose proportion increased with PCa pathological stages. Interestingly, we found that HMMR exhibited elevated expression in advanced PCa tissues, which correlated with poor prognosis. Besides, we also observed that HMMR expression was negatively correlated with AR signaling, while positively correlated with lineage plasticity-related markers and NEPC drivers. We further demonstrated that AR directly bound to an HMMR intronic ARE and repressed its transcription. In addition to HMMR, we also identified several markers of LPCs and basal/hillock cells, along with key epigenetic regulators as potential negative regulatory targets for AR. Indeed. The loss of basal/hillock cells36,48 and epigenetic changes17,27 are both widely known features during PCa tumorigenesis and progression but the underlying mechanism remain unclear. Our findings raise the possibility that AR-mediated transcriptional repression function may represent an overlooked yet critical mechanism for these dynamic changes under AR-targeted inhibition in advanced PCa.

Previous studies have showed that HMMR expression is associated with treatment resistance and adverse prognosis, while targeting HMMR can enhance the efficacy of enzalutamide.29,49,50 In this study, we confirmed significant upregulation of HMMR expression in advanced PCa samples, particularly in NEPC. This is likely due to the widespread use of AR-targeted therapy in advanced PCa, which could relieve the AR transcriptional inhibition of HMMR. We further demonstrated that HMMR could in turn regulate AR signaling and NE differentiation in advanced PCa cells. Indeed, emerging evidence has suggested the association of HMMR with neural development, progenitor cell fate, and NE tumors.38,51, 52, 53 Besides, how HMMR influences the expression of other NEPC drivers would be of interest for future investigation. For example, a recent study reported that HMMR could interact with AURKA, consequently upregulating the expression level of AURKA through the inhibition of its ubiquitination.49 Additionally, we found that HMMR exhibited proliferative and invasive effects in advanced PCa. In keeping with this, a recent study demonstrated that HMMR overexpression promotes lung metastasis in DU145 tumor-bearing mice.49 We showed that HMMR knockdown suppressed the growth of 22Rv1 in vivo, and might halt the adeno-to-neuroendocrine lineage transition. Our findings suggest the critical role of HMMR in the progression of advanced PCa into NEPC trans-differentiation. In the meantime, we acknowledge that the substantial body of functional and mechanistic experiments in the present study was conducted in non-NEPC cell lines, such as DU145 and 22Rv1, which represents a limitation of the study. The role of HMMR in NEPC trans-differentiation shall be further validated in NEPC cell models, such as LASPC-01 cells.54

Overall, our study uncovered a previously undescribed population of PCa cells with lineage plasticity potential, which displayed low AR activity, stemness-like traits, and elevated HMMR expression. Furthermore, we identified HMMR as a representative cell surface marker for LPCs, highlighting its critical role in NEPC progression.
