ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsLanguage is a powerful tool that shapes how people experience diabetes, their engagement with care and ultimately their outcomes. This guidance document for healthcare professionals in Ireland outlines why Language Matters in diabetes care and aims to support person‐centred, strengths‐based and non‐judgemental communication.MethodsThe guidance was developed by an Irish multidisciplinary working group, including healthcare professionals, researchers and people with lived experience of diabetes. The group drew on international Language Matters guidance, published evidence, Irish data on diabetes‐related language, stigma, distress and healthcare engagement, and insights from lived experience to develop practical recommendations for person‐centred diabetes communication.ResultsStigmatising or judgemental words can contribute to diabetes distress, disengagement and suboptimal self‐management. This guidance provides practical recommendations to foster collaboration, reduce stigma and improve quality of life. Key recommendations include using person‐first language, avoiding blame and fear, ensuring consistency across teams, and embedding reflective practice and communication training within healthcare teams.ConclusionsLanguage is a core component of diabetes care. Consistent use of respectful, empathetic and non‐judgemental communication may strengthen therapeutic relationships, support engagement with care and help create a culture in which people living with diabetes feel understood, respected and empowered.Keywords: diabetes, outcomes, psychology

INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this document is to provide concise, evidence‐based guidance for healthcare professionals (HCPs) to support person‐centred, stigma‐free communication in diabetes care. As part of this document, we will describe (and define) Language Matters in the context of diabetes, discuss why Language Matters, share evidence from both international and Irish contexts and share reflections on the importance of Language Matters from a lived experience perspective.

WHAT DOES LANGUAGE MATTERS MEAN

A broken bone can heal, but the wound a word opens can fester forever.

The Language Matters movement

Language Matters Diabetes is an international movement that promotes respectful, person‐centred communication with and about people living with diabetes (PwD). It began in Australia in 2012 and has since informed guidance in several countries. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In Ireland, a Language Matters Diabetes working group was established under the leadership of Gráinne Flynn, a person living with lived experience of diabetes and co‐founder of Thriveabetes. In 2024, Ireland published its first Language Matters Diabetes guide for the media and general public. 8 Ireland's context mirrors that of many other countries: PwD contend with significant day‐to‐day demands, and the words used about diabetes can either compound that burden or support constructive engagement. In an Irish healthcare system striving for person‐centred, integrated care, the consistent use of respectful, accurate language is a practical way to strengthen relationships and communication across services. Our previous document, aimed at the general public and media, served as the foundation and inspiration for the present Language Matters Diabetes document for HCPs and is available at this link: https://www.languagemattersdiabetes.com/_files/ugd/09baf1_fb81d6f8195f4d30a60c262f0dd5a967.pdf.

Challenges of living with diabetes

The burden of diabetes self‐management is relentless, requiring constant decision‐making, often based on incomplete or imperfect data. This continuous effort places a significant strain on individuals as they navigate complex choices daily with limited support. Approximately 0.03% of a year is spent with a diabetes HCP for a PwD. 9 For the remaining 99.97% of the time, individuals manage their condition on their own or with support from friends or family. Unsurprisingly, with this added cognitive load, diabetes distress, defined as the negative emotional impact of living with diabetes, is highly prevalent. 10 , 11 , 12 Approximately one in three adults with diabetes experiences elevated diabetes distress. Distress can lead to adverse mental health outcomes, including depression and reduced quality of life. 13 Language can affect healthcare engagement. For example, words like ‘fat’ and ‘obese’, as opposed to ‘high BMI’, when referring to people's weight, are perceived as stigmatising; use of such language has been associated with 19% of people avoiding future medical appointments and 21% of people looking for a new doctor. 14 Such stigmatising language contributes to diabetes distress. 15 Conversely, improved doctor–patient communication positively influences diabetes self‐management. 16 Interestingly, the perceived quality of communication between the PwT2D and HCP is associated with improved self‐management, well‐being, perceived personal control and self‐efficacy, as well as better diabetes care and less diabetes distress. 17

There are different types of diabetes (Figure 1, 18 , 19 ). While the treatment regimen, role of technology and nature of care provided can vary by diabetes type, what is consistent across all types of diabetes is the importance of ‘language.’ When clinically relevant, specifying the type of diabetes may improve clarity and reduce misunderstanding 20 , 21 ; however, preferences vary, and this should not be used in ways that imply hierarchy, blame or greater legitimacy of one form of diabetes over another. It is equally essential to recognise that all forms of diabetes are complex conditions influenced by a wide range of factors, many of which exist beyond the individual level. Across all types of diabetes, avoiding blame in language helps reduce stigma and fosters respectful, person‐centred care and communication.

WHY LANGUAGE MATTERS: BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE

Language Matters because it can convey support and understanding, or conversely, stigma, criticism and judgment (Figure 2). It can also help to break down stigma by validating lived experience, reducing blame, strengthening therapeutic relationships and supporting more person‐centred diabetes care. The language used around diabetes shapes individuals' self‐perception, their relationship with their condition and HCPs' attitudes. Reframing this language can shift the experience of diabetes from one associated with isolation and personal failure to one characterised by feeling supported and part of a team effort. Recognising that HCPs may not experience the daily challenges of diabetes, they should avoid words that could evoke blame, stigma, shame or judgment and instead focus on language that is respectful, collaborative and empowering. By shifting from a ‘sage on the stage’ to a ‘guide on the side’, HCPs can foster a compassionate, collaborative atmosphere that respects the complexity of diabetes management and acknowledges the active role that individuals with diabetes take in their care. Language Matters concerns not only how we communicate with PwD, but also how we speak about them. We recognise the additional challenges of communication and language use when English is not the person's first language and/or when cultural norms differ, which may affect communication, shared understanding and engagement with care.

Why does language matter?

Language shapes social perception, personal identity, intergroup bias and stereotyping. It can express bias about characteristics such as race, religion, health or gender, and in diabetes, it can reinforce stigma. People who experience or perceive diabetes stigma often report shame, guilt and social isolation. 22 The language used by HCPs contributes to the reinforcement of this stigma, with stigmatising language leading to feelings of judgment, blame and shame. 23 Such stigma leads to under‐reporting of blood glucose levels, omission of information during consultations and disengagement from care. 22 Most PwT1D (76%) and PwT2D (52%) have experienced stigma. 24 Stigma, in turn, worsens psychological distress and self‐care. 25 , 26 Stigmatising terms in medical records (‘cheat’ and ‘fails’) are more common in notes about PwD than in the notes of people living with other conditions, contributing to negative attitudes from subsequent clinicians, 27 , 28 underscoring the need for continued reflection and training among diabetes care teams to identify their biases and avoid stigmatising language.

Positive, person‐centred language fosters better outcomes, including improved self‐management, well‐being and reduced diabetes distress. 17 We acknowledge that many HCPs communicate with and about PwD with positive intentions. However, PwD frequently report that the language used by HCPs can be stigmatising. 21 , 29 , 30 To address this, HCPs can remain open to feedback, reflect on the impact of their words and continually strive to use respectful, stigma‐free, person‐centred language when communicating with and about PwD. This commitment to ongoing growth fosters a more empathetic, respectful, supportive and no‐blame environment for PwD.

The Irish perspective

In Ireland, a recent survey, including quantitative and open‐ended qualitative questions, of 183 adults living with either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, found that 37% of respondents experienced stigmatising language from a HCP ‘sometimes’ to ‘very often’ (in response to the following: ‘Thinking about your healthcare appointments in the past year, how often did you encounter language that you would consider to be stigmatising by a healthcare provider’). 29 About 50% of respondents reported that HCPs made judgments about food choices. 29 In response to a question about the extent to which language impacts the relationship with an HCP, approximately 60% of respondents reported that it had a ‘moderate’ to ‘extreme’ impact. 29

Additionally, qualitative interviews with adults (10 females, 4 males) living with type 1 diabetes (n = 10) or type 2 diabetes (n = 4) in Ireland about the use and impact of diabetes‐related language were conducted. 21 This second study found that much of the language around diabetes is experienced negatively, often conveying misconceptions and judgment, and singling out PwD as different or ‘other.’ Language does not have to be intentional to be impactful. Language from HCPs often denied or failed to acknowledge their personhood, especially words like ‘control’ and ‘compliance’; ‘you control prisoners, you control cattle, you control things, […] it doesn't feel human, almost’ [33‐year‐old; male]. Participants described wanting to avoid or actually avoiding healthcare as a result of the things said to them by their HCPs. While health data needs to be communicated, participants preferred it when HCPs centred their overall experience rather than solely focusing on their health data. Instead, HCPs often used highly critical language—for example, a person was told by her consultant that her baby ‘would be born with either anencephaly or encephalitis’ because her bloods weren't ‘controlled’ at conception, which ‘ruined the whole pregnancy.’ Participants emphasised the importance of aspects of communication beyond words/language, such as tone, intent (perceived or otherwise), attitude, manner, etc., and as such, these features of communication should not be disregarded (especially in an Irish context, where it can be culturally favourable to use more avoidant or indirect language). 21 , 31

Furthermore, Irish research has found that PwT1D or PwT2D who encountered frequent stigmatising language from HCPs reported elevated levels of diabetes distress, anxiety and depressive symptoms. 29 When responding to an open‐ended survey question, the survey respondents highlighted the effects of both positive and negative language. Positive language made participants feel they were provided with key information and were gaining valuable insights from their HCPs. In contrast, negative language was associated with feelings of judgment, guilt related to food choices, mental health struggles, feeling misunderstood and increased anxiety. Negative comments about food choices and the use of words such as ‘control’ were particularly associated with elevated diabetes distress. 29

Guidance development and co‐design

An Irish Diabetes Language Matters Working Group was established with representation from key organisations and a variety of professional backgrounds, including PwDs, HCPs and researchers, to ensure a wide range of perspectives. Three subgroups used a collaborative, iterative approach to develop the guidance. The methodological approach included reviewing international Language Matters Diabetes documents and research publications on language in diabetes care in Ireland, and drawing on insights from the lived and professional experiences of people with diabetes. The working group met regularly via virtual meetings to draw on the relevant literature and identify themes, collaborating to refine the guidance through structured discussions and written feedback on shared documents. Successive drafts were circulated for review until full agreement was reached, resulting in final guidance that reflects both evidence‐based insights and the collective expertise of all stakeholders.

‘Person‐first’ language refers to people and starts with terms like ‘person with’, ‘people with’, or ‘adult with.’ Person‐first language is intended to convey that one characteristic (in this case, diabetes) is not all that defines a person; a person has many different characteristics and strengths that are separate from their diabetes. 32 Although arguments for using person‐first language are well‐intentioned, research indicates variation in preferences for person‐first language across cultures and health and disability characteristics. While many people with health conditions like diabetes prefer person‐first language, some do use ‘identity‐first’ language. (e.g., ‘I am a diabetic’ rather than ‘person with diabetes’). For example, Litterbach et al. (2024) reported that while 74% of those surveyed found the phrase ‘person with diabetes’ acceptable, some PwT1D and PwT2D in Australia preferred the term ‘diabetic’, which they perceived to take less effort and cognitive load compared to ‘person with diabetes.’ 20 Therefore, while person‐first language is generally preferable for diabetes, it is worth considering individuals' preferences. Additionally, a person might choose to use identity‐first language to describe themselves while still preferring that healthcare providers use person‐first language in their communication.

Communication during healthcare interactions

The attitudes and communication styles of HCPs play a crucial role in shaping PwD's perceptions of their progress. It is essential for HCPs to offer support, maintain honesty and provide guidance in a way that encourages rather than discourages. 33 These points are illustrated by the following quotes from the mother of a PwT1D in a qualitative study exploring how to improve diabetes care and self‐management: ‘There is a lot of shame in diabetes. It's one of the only chronic illnesses where the people who live with it are constantly blamed for how their disease is managed.’ 33

Diagnosis

At the time of diagnosis and in the period that follows, people must both acquire knowledge about diabetes and adapt to life with it. Comments to downplay or oversimplify the reality of living with diabetes are not always helpful. Words that oversimplify or minimise the burden of diabetes, such as ‘you'll get used to it’, ‘just’, ‘should’, ‘lose weight’, and ‘at least it's not…’ can result in people feeling dismissed. However well‐intentioned, such commentary is generally not welcomed by PwD. 23 Similarly, directive words can be counterproductive. Specifically, terms such as ‘lose weight’, ‘exercise more’, ‘should/ shouldn't’, ‘can't’, ‘no’, ‘stop’, ‘not allowed to’, and ‘don't’ are not helpful and result in people feeling upset, hurt and ‘like a failure with no self‐control.’ 23 Terminology regarding the prevention of diabetes needs to be used cautiously and is not always helpful. Health‐promoting behaviours and effective management can reduce, but not eliminate, the risk of developing diabetes and its complications. The term ‘risk reduction’ is therefore preferable to ‘prevention’, as it more accurately reflects evidence, avoids blame and acknowledges both modifiable and non‐modifiable factors. 34

Scheduled follow‐up / routine consultations

During routine consultations, PwD have expressed frustration about the repeated conversations about complications and risk at every visit. Such approaches are described as scary and stressful and can be counterproductive with examples of PwDs’ thoughts including: ‘It gets old and tiring to hear about them every time’, and ‘I don't want to hear about the complications at EVERY visit. I totally heard it the first time.’ 23 Conversation tools that cover a spectrum of diabetes‐related topics, including risk management, may help guide consultations.

Conversations about reproductive health

Conversations about diabetes and pregnancy are often heavily conditioned to convey risks and adverse pregnancy outcomes, and the labelling of these pregnancies as ‘high‐risk.’ While diabetes does convey additional maternal and fetal risks, these risks can be mediated with specific and targeted care, and indeed, most pregnancies among women living with diabetes result in healthy pregnancies and pregnancy outcomes. 35 Over 70% of new and expectant mothers are overweight or live with obesity, and more than two‐thirds experience weight‐related stigma during pregnancy and postpartum. 36 For women with established diabetes, targeted care before pregnancy is recommended to support the best possible outcomes for both mother and baby. This includes early discussion of pregnancy intentions, optimising glycaemia, medication review to ensure they are safe for pregnancy, and supplementing with high‐dose folic acid, and screening for any diabetes‐related complications. 37

How HCPs communicate with women can profoundly impact their engagement with care and interactions during this important time. Yet, negative experiences have been reported, such as the account of a woman with type 2 diabetes who stated, ‘I was told by my GP that I was too fat to get pregnant, I was so hurt by what she'd said, so you can imagine my response with her in the consultation after that—I wasn't very responsive at all.’ 38 They also reported they were more likely to receive ambiguous information that they could not relate to, such as ‘They were just saying to tell them if I was thinking of a baby—they didn't tell me what the help would be, you know, it was just me telling them.’ 38 Pregnant women have reported that communication with HCPs can be ‘risk‐oriented’ with the rationale for decision‐making prefaced with comments such as ‘We have to do this or your baby's at risk of dying. You're at risk of dying.’ 39

It is suggested that HCPs assess what people know and would like to know in advance of such conversations, rather than reciting a predefined list. If asking about pregnancy or pregnancy planning, the following questions were suggested: ‘Is this something you would like me to talk about? Are you aware of this?’ 39

Conversations about obesity

HCPs should recognise and treat obesity as a complex, chronic, recurring disease 40 and conversations about weight need to take this into account. Weight is often discussed in relation to diabetes because obesity and diabetes can co‐occur, and also, weight may play a role in insulin resistance and diabetes management. It is well recognised that conversations related to weight and obesity can be both overtly and covertly judgmental and reinforcing weight bias. The strong focus on weight in type 2 diabetes narratives can contribute to stigma, where individuals may feel blamed for their condition. The UK Language Matters obesity guidelines 41 provide a comprehensive overview of the impact of language on people living with obesity. The guiding principles for good practice are similar to those for any other chronic disease and recommend seeking permission before starting a conversation about weight, being empathetic and factual, avoiding blame, generalisations and inappropriate humour and focusing on overall health and wellbeing rather than weight alone.

EVALUATION: AUDIT/KPIs/SELF‐ASSESSMENT TOOL

Reflective practice is a valuable approach for HCPs to critically evaluate their language use in diabetes‐related consultations. Through reflection in action and on action, 49 we can identify areas for improvement, adapt our communication strategies and foster more person‐centred, empowering consultations in diabetes care. Below, we propose questions for HCPs to reflect on the language they use during consultations. These are meant to provide general guidance.

A guiding principle for an HCP on the use of language is:

Do the best you can until you know better. Then when you know better, do better. Maya Angelou

The intention behind the use of any language is key — and there are ways that positive intention can be conveyed. We will not always get the preferred use of language correct every time — rather, let's set an expectation that we, as HCPs, will learn from experience and strive to do better next time. KEY TAKE‐HOME MESSAGES

Language is part of diabetes care: every word, tone and gesture can build trust or reinforce stigma. Respectful, person‐centred communication is therefore a professional responsibility for HCPs. The way clinicians communicate with and about people with diabetes influences relationships, engagement with care and potentially both service‐level and person‐level outcomes. Historical paternalistic approaches (‘doctor knows best’) should give way to collaborative, empowering care. HCPs should use person‐first, strengths‐based language that avoids labels such as ‘diabetic’ or terms like ‘non‐compliant’ and ‘good/bad control.’ Fear‐based messages about complications increase distress; provide clear, factual information and supportive strategies instead. Communication should be neutral, factual and focused on behaviours rather than blame, with clear explanations in plain language and regular checks of patient understanding (e.g., teach‐back or Ask Me 3). Clinical notes should be written without stigma, as language used in records can perpetuate bias across the wider healthcare team. Embedding the principles described above into consultations, MDT communication, written correspondence and audit processes may improve engagement, reduce distress and support better self‐management.
