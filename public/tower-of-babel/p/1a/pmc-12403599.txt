Risk factors for severe COVID-19 and development of a predictive model
Authors: Ling Zhang, Xinran Li, Ziyan Wang, Lei Zhao, Huixia Gao, Conghui Liu, Jing Bai, Tiejun Liu, Weibin Chen, Wenqiang Li, Jingshan Bai, Aishuang Fu, Yanlei Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA clinical case‒control study was conducted to identify risk factors for severe COVID-19 and to develop a predictive risk model to provide a reference for the dynamic assessment of the severity of disease in COVID-19 patients. A total of 410 patients with COVID-19 were included in the study, of whom 132 had severe or critical cases. The clinical data of the patients were collected, and the variables were subsequently screened via LASSO regression analysis and 10-fold cross-validation. The screened variables were subjected to multifactorial logistic regression analysis to screen out the independent risk factors for patients with severe or critical illnesses, and the independent risk factors were integrated to construct a nomogram. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, calibration curve analysis, and decision curve analysis (DCA), showing good predictive accuracy. Five variables, including the respiratory rate (R), systolic blood pressure (SBP), plasma albumin (ALB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and C-reactive protein (CRP), were ultimately included to construct a clinical prediction model, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.86 (CI 0.82–0.90%). The clinical prediction model constructed in this study using simple clinical indicators can assist in the clinical prediction and identification of patients with heavy or critical COVID-19.Keywords: COVID-19, SARS-CoV-2, Predictive model, Nomogram

Introduction

COVID-19 is a group of acute respiratory infectious diseases that were identified in December 2019 in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China [1]. A virus isolated from it is known as severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [2]. Similar to other RNA viruses, the SARS-CoV-2 virus exhibits a high degree of variability. Several variant strains have emerged: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron. Compared with previous variants, the Omicron strain is more accessible for transmission, has a greater ability to evade the human immune response, and is characterized by reduced natural virulence [3]. Despite the relatively low virulence of Omicron variants, some populations, such as elderly individuals with comorbidities, are at high risk of severe infection and death [4]. The highly infectious nature of SARS-CoV-2 means that it can trigger seasonal epidemic spikes, while virus mutations are unpredictable. The lack of effective monitoring mechanisms or appropriate responses may lead to the emergence of new pandemic patterns. Early and rapid identification of patients with severe COVID-19 during a COVID-19 crisis outbreak and dynamic monitoring of disease changes are essential both for treatment and assessment of prognosis. The last two years have also seen an influx of prediction models for COVID-19 patients as the virus has mutated [5, 6]. In a cohort study conducted in England [7], the researchers developed a risk prediction algorithm called QCOVID4. The algorithm is based on demographic data and is designed to assess the risk of COVID-19 patients being hospitalized or dying from the disease during the Omicron. The model has a Harrell C statistic of 0.970 (0.962–0.979). Baker [8] et al. presented a machine learning model with predictive outcomes, including acute respiratory failure, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and ventilator use. The model exceeded similar predictors, with an area under the curve of 0.83, without laboratory data for predicting patient risk. The primary outcome of the study by Weng [9] et al. was survival or death at discharge. Unlike Weng, in the study of Moon [10] et al., the prediction time for mortality was defined as 30 and 60 days, which represented the survival time of the patients while their deaths were predicted. All of the above models demonstrate good predictive efficacy, but they are all prognostic predictive models that focus more on the prognosis of patients than do diagnostic predictive models. Artificial intelligence (AI) technology has been increasingly used in the medical field, and AI-based clinical prediction models have made significant progress. For example, our team’s research on machine learning and imaging histology for community-acquired pneumonia [11] uses clinical data and imaging features to identify the current severity of a patient’s condition and provide timely treatment to prevent disease progression. Similarly, in a study of diagnostic and predictive models for COVID-19, Zhu [12] et al. used a deep learning approach to analyze CT images to differentiate between severe and mild cases. However, current research still faces several key challenges: first, some AI models have ‘black box’ characteristics, which makes it difficult to reasonably interpret the key features of the model output; second, the existing model construction process often lacks the systematic integration of medical background knowledge and does not give enough consideration to qualitative analysis. In addition, the variability of examination equipment among different medical institutions may have a significant impact on the generalisation performance of the model [13]. These factors may limit the application of AI models in real clinical scenarios. In clinical practice, considering the highly contagious nature of SARS-CoV-2, it is easy to concentrate on the outbreak, resulting in a shortage of medical resources. An ideal prediction model should have the following characteristics: low cost, easy detection, high specificity and sensitivity, and the ability to dynamically detect and reflect the severity of the disease promptly. In recent years, a large number of studies have demonstrated the important clinical value of routine blood test values (RBV) in disease diagnosis and treatment.As a cost-effective testing index, RBV is not only crucial for the diagnosis, severity assessment and prognosis monitoring of COVID-19 [14–16], but also widely used in intelligent diagnostic studies of various diseases [17–19]. Clinical evidence shows that RBV can provide a reliable means of early diagnosis and prognostic assessment for diseases such as COVID-19 [20, 21]. In terms of artificial intelligence applications, several studies have confirmed the clinical value of RBV data: Huyut’s team used supervised machine learning (e.g., LogNNet) to successfully implement COVID-19 diagnosis, prognosis, and mortality prediction and identify key biomarkers [22, 19]; Yan et al. applied RBV based on the gradient-boosting decision tree method for multiplemyeloma early diagnosis [23]. In addition, Kawakami et al. applied various machine learning models to effectively assist the preoperative diagnosis of epithelial ovarian cancer [24]; and Haider et al. achieved leukaemia through artificial neural networkearly identification [25].

Laboratory serum biomarkers show dynamic changes during disease progression in patients with COVID-19 and have important diagnostic and prognostic value for risk stratification of COVID-19. Therefore, in this study, clinical data such as demographic characteristics, vital signs, and routine serological indicators (including routine blood, liver and kidney functions, and coagulation-related indicators) were collected and analysed, and a clinical prediction model was finally constructed. Materials and methods

Study population

The Institutional Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North China University of Science and Technology approved this study. The research followed the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or similar ethical standards. Owing to the retrospective nature of our study, the Institutional Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of North China University of Science and Technology waived informed consent. Measures were implemented to ensure the confidentiality and anonymity of the data collected via appropriate privacy protection protocols.

Patients with novel coronavirus infections who were hospitalized at the Affiliated Hospital of North China University of Science and Technology from 2023.01 to 2023.05 were retrospectively selected for the study. Those diagnosed as severe or critical during hospitalization were chosen as the case group (severe group). Those nonsevere during hospitalization (mild or medium cases) were selected as the control group (Non-severe group).

According to the Chinese Diagnostic and Treatment Guidelines-Diagnostic and Treatment Guidelines for COVID-19 (10th edition of the Trial):

Diagnostic criteria: (1) There are clinical manifestations associated with COVID-19. (2) Have one or more of the following pathogenic and serological findings: (1) Positive nucleic acid test for SARS-CoV-2; (2) Positive test for SARS-CoV-2 antigen; (3) Positive isolation and culture of SARS-CoV-2. (4) SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG antibody levels are 4-fold or more elevated in the recovery phase than in the acute phase.

Clinical classification: (1) Mild cases: Upper respiratory tract infection is the primary manifestation, with dry throat, sore throat, cough, fever, etc. (2) Medium cases: Persistent high fever for > 3 days and/or cough and shortness of breath, but respiratory rate (R) < 30 breaths/minute and oxygen saturation > 93% on air intake at rest. Characteristic pneumonia with COVID-19 is seen on imaging. (3) Severe cases: Adults with any of the following that cannot be explained by causes other than COVID-19: (1) Shortness of breath with RR ≥ 30 beats/minute; (2) Oxygen saturation ≤ 93% on air inhalation at rest; (3) Arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/oxygen concentration (FiO2) ≤ 300 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), and PaO2/FiO2 should be corrected for high altitude (more than 1000 m above sea level) according to the following formula: PaO2/FiO2×[760/atmospheric pressure (mmHg)]; (4) Progressive worsening of clinical symptoms; (5) Lung imaging shows considerable progression of > 50% of the lesion within 24–48 h. (4) Critical cases: meet one of the following conditions: (1) Respiratory failure, and requires mechanical ventilation. (2) go into shock. (3) Combined with other organ failure requiring ICU monitoring and treatment.

Inclusion criteria: (1) age greater than or equal to 18 years old; (2) confirmed patients who met the diagnostic criteria.

Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with relatively incomplete medical records; (2) patients with the presence of primary lung infections caused by other well-defined pathogens were excluded; (3) patients with severe infections at other sites were excluded.

The above clinical diagnosis and clinical classification are combined with the clinical judgment of senior physicians.

Research methods

Patients eligible for the study were included through inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the following clinical data were collected: (1) general clinical information: sex, age, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, vital signs (including temperature (T), pulse (P), respiratory rate (R), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP)), clinical classification and state of consciousness; (2) disease history: combining disease categories < 2 and ≥ 2. category 1 (cerebrovascular disease, coronary heart disease, diabetes, hypertension and hyperlipidemia), category 2 (chronic lung disease: tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, COPD, etc.), category 3 (others: liver and kidney disease, benign hematological disorders, surgical trauma, etc.), and category 4 immunocompromised (malignant neoplasms, immunosuppressant drug use); (3) Laboratory tests: blood routine: white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (NEU), lymphocyte count (LYM), platelet count (PLT), and hemoglobin (HBG); blood biochemistry: liver function: aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), ALB, renal function: serum creatinine (sCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), myocardial enzymes: myoglobin (MYO), creatine kinase (CK), creatine kinase isoenzyme (CK-MB), and LDH; inflammatory markers: procalcitonin (PCT), CRP, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and interleukin-6 (IL-6); coagulation series: fibrinogen (FIB), D-dimer (D-dimer) (first fasting blood test results after diagnosis); patients with COVID-19 were then divided into severe and non-severe groups; comparisons of data between groups were performed to analyze the clinical characteristics. The screened variables were subjected to nomograms constructed by integrating the independent risk factors and the predictive efficacy of ed to multivariate logistic regression analysis to screen the independent risk factors for patients with severe or critical illnesses. The model was evaluated by plotting the ROC curve. On the basis of the maximum value of the Jordon index, The best critical value was selected, the confusion matrix of the prediction model was plotted, the sensitivity and specificity were calculated, and the kappa value was calculated to evaluate the consistency of the model and was plotted via calibration curve analysis combined with the Hosmer–Lemeshow test to assess the model’s accuracy and via DCA to evaluate its clinical utility.

Laboratory test

The routine blood analysis was performed by ABX PENTRA 120 automatic five-classified red blood cell analyser with reticulated red blood cells of ABX Company, France; the biochemical indexes were performed by Beckman AU5800 automatic biochemical analyser of the United States; the coagulation series was performed by Wolfern ACL-TOP-750 automatic coagulation analyser of Spain; PCT was measured by enzyme-linked fluorescence analysis; ESR was measured by manual method; CRP was measured by immunoturbidimetric method; IL-6 was measured by fully automatic chemiluminescence immunoanalyser method.

Statistical analysis

Data were retrospectively collected and analyzed using SPSS software (26.0) and R software (version 4.2.3). Measurement data were tested for normality and expressed as ‘mean ± standard deviation’ if they were normally or approximately normally distributed; ‘median (interquartile spacing)’ if they were clearly skewed; qualitative variables were calculated by calculating the composition ratio. Compositional comparisons between groups of count data were performed using the chi-square test; comparisons of means between groups of measured data were performed using the t-test for independent samples; and comparisons of medians were performed using the nonparametric rank-sum test. Two-sided test was used, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant difference. Lasso regression analysis and tenfold cross validation were used to screen variables. With whether the patients with new coronavirus infection were diagnosed with severe disease as the dependent variable (yes = 1, no = 0), and the factors screened by lasso regression analysis and cross-validation as the independent variables, multifactorial logistic regression analysis was performed to screen the independent risk factors for constructing the prediction model. According to the results of multivariate logistic regression analysis, meaningful indicators were selected to construct the predictive nomogram of severe COVID-19. The predictive efficacy of the model was evaluated using the ROC curve, and the critical value (cutoff value) for prediction was screened according to the maximum point of the Jordon index. The cutoff value predicted by the model was used for prediction, and the sensitivity and specificity were calculated to evaluate the veracity of the model prediction; the Kappa value was calculated to evaluate the consistency between the model prediction results and the actual results, and to judge the clinical application value of the model. Calibration curves were plotted to assess the stability of the model, and DCA was used to assess the clinical usefulness of the model. Internal validation of model performance using Bootstrap resampling methodology.

Patient characteristics

A total of 410 eligible patients were enrolled, including 132 critically ill patients and 278 noncritically ill patients, including 219 males and 191 females, with a median age of 68 years. The results revealed differences in age, coronary heart disease, hypertension, comorbidities, disorders of consciousness, temperature (T), R, SBP, WBC, NEU, LMY, HGB, ALT, AST, ALB, BUN, MYO, CK-MB, LDH, FIB, D-dimer, ESR, CRP, PCT, and IL-6 between the two groups (p < 0.05). In contrast, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, diabetes status, pulse, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), platelet (P), sCr, and CK were not significantly different (p > 0.05). LASSO regression and tenfold cross-validation

LASSO regression (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator Regression) is a regularization technique for linear regression models proposed by Robert Tibshirani in 1996. LASSO regression involves the addition of the L1 paradigm penalty term in the loss function, both to achieve the model parameters of shrinkage and to achieve variable selection. The regression coefficients of some unnecessary variables can be compressed to zero and then eliminated from the model. In high-dimensional data analysis, it can effectively reduce the data dimensions to solve the multicollinearity problem. On the other hand, cross-validation is used to fit a model via the training set by dividing the dataset into a training set and a validation set and evaluating the model performance on the validation set. The λ that gives the best performance on the validation set is selected. In this study, 6 variables were screened from the 34 variables collected on the basis of the nonzero coefficients calculated from LASSO regression analysis (Fig. 2). The 6 variables included disorders of consciousness, R, SBP, ALB, LDH, and CRP.

Screening for independent risk factors via multifactorial logistic regression analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to determine whether COVID-19 severity (yes = 1, no = 0) was the dependent variable, and the factors with significance in the above LASSO regression analysis and tenfold cross-validation were the independent variables. The results revealed that R, SBP, ALB, LDH, and CRP were independent risk factors for severe COVID-19 (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that the above factors can increase the risk of severe COVID-19 and can be used as predictors of whether a patient has severe COVID-19. In contrast, disorders of consciousness were not independent risk factors for severe COVID-19 (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

Construction of a risk prediction nomogram for patients with severe COVID-19

Logistic regression models were constructed for the predictors identified by LASSO regression analysis and multivariate analysis (R, SBP, ALB, LDH, and CRP), and a nomogram was plotted to predict the risk of a COVID-19 patient being severe or critical (Fig. 3). According to a patient’s vital signs and laboratory indicators, the corresponding points of R, SBP, ALB, LDH, and CRP were calculated on the basis of the points of the nomogram. The total score was subsequently obtained by summing the points of the five indicators. The prediction probability of the patient’s severe current condition is received by the total points line at the bottom of the nomogram and the corresponding serious risk line.

Alidation of the model

The model performance was evaluated using Bootstrap resampling method, and a total of 1000 resamplings were executed. Discussion

Since January 8, 2023, China has officially implemented the “class B and B tube” policy for patients with COVID-19. Given the high infectivity of the SARS-CoV-2 virus, which may lead to seasonal epidemic peaks, the healthcare system will undoubtedly face more serious challenges. For both patients admitted to the hospital with COVID-19 infection in the community and those infected in the hospital, dynamic assessment of their condition is critical. This study revealed that R, SBP, LDH levels, decreased ALB, and CPR were independent risk factors for predicting severe or critical COVID-19 patients. The five indicators were combined to construct a clinical diagnostic prediction model. The model is composed of common clinical laboratory test indicators and two crucial vital signs and ultimately meets the original requirements of the model—which are simple and easy to obtain—but also shows good diagnostic efficiency. Compared with other prediction models, the model constructed in this study is more concise and clear when selecting variables. Unlike those studies that rely on chest imaging examination [26, 27], imaging examination is expensive, and some patients may be unable to accept it for economic reasons, making dynamic monitoring more difficult. Second, chest CT imaging has a certain lag in reflecting the current condition of patients. In addition, for some patients with other serious diseases, leaving the ward for CT examination is restricted. In addition, the data of this study came from 2023, that is, after the prevalence of Omicron variants and the implementation of the “Class B and B tube” policy in China. Therefore, compared with studies in the early phase of the pandemic, the data in this study are more closely related to the current prevalence of Omicron variants, thus making the findings more relevant and applicable. The SARS-CoV-2 virus can cause a variety of clinical manifestations, ranging from upper respiratory discomfort to pneumonia and even multiple organ failure [28]. This pathological mechanism of multiorgan failure may be related to the unbalanced immune response during COVID-19. Therefore, in patients with COVID-19, the levels of a series of biomarkers change, some of which are critical for risk stratification, diagnosis, and prognosis assessment [29]. LDH, one of the key enzymes that catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate and lactic acid, plays a central role in the anaerobic metabolism of glucose. This process is critical when the oxygen supply is insufficient or limited. As an essential enzyme in the glycolysis pathway, LDH is widely distributed in myocardial, liver, kidney, and lung tissues, and its concentration in tissues is much greater than that in serum. When lung tissue suffers from hypoxic necrosis, a large amount of LDH is released into the blood, resulting in a sharp increase in the serum LDH level [30]. Consistent with the conclusions of this study, serum LDH has been proven to be a sensitive biomarker for assessing the dynamic changes in COVID-19 patients [30–32]. ALB is a harmful acute-phase protein synthesized by the liver and has many vital functions: it is responsible for transporting various substances and maintaining normal plasma colloid osmotic pressure [33]. Previous studies have shown that hypoalbuminemia is a practical, dose dependent, independent predictor of adverse outcomes. Each 10 g/L decrease in the serum ALB concentration was associated with a 137% increase in mortality, an 89% increase in morbidity, a 28% and 71% increase in intensive care unit and hospital stays, respectively, and a 66% increase in resource utilization [34]. In addition, a retrospective cohort study of COVID-19 patients in Spain revealed an association between low albumin (ALB) levels and poor prognosis [35]. Similarly, Beimdiek et al. [36] concluded, in agreement with the present study, that ALB levels were lower in critically ill COVID-19 patients. It is possible that during infection, the distribution rate of serum ALB from intravascular to extravascular increases significantly, as does the decomposition rate [37]. CRP is a nonspecific IL-6-induced acute phase reactant in the liver. Clinically, it is a biomarker for different inflammatory and infectious diseases. Elevated CRP levels are directly related to inflammation and disease severity [38]. As such, it is an essential biomarker for assessing the severity of COVID-19. Other studies have confirmed this conclusion [39, 40].

In conclusion, the proportion of patients with preexisting hypertension in the severe group was 52.27%, whereas that in the nonsevere group was 39.57%, indicating a significant difference but not an independent risk factor for severe COVID-19. However, elevated SBP is an independent risk factor for severe COVID-19. Hypertension is a risk factor for increased COVID-19-related deaths [41]. However, the independent role of hypertension remains controversial, as hypertension is often associated with age and cardiovascular disease, which can also increase the severity of COVID-19. In addition, antihypertensive drugs, angiotensin conversion inhibitors (ACEIs), and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) may be associated with the condition of COVID-19 patients. ACE2 is a crucial binding receptor that facilitates the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into organisms, and renin‒angiotensin system (RAS) blockers may upregulate ACE2. Therefore, these drugs may contribute to the increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19 progression, but this idea is controversial [42]. However, elevated SBP may be a marker of preexisting hypertension-mediated subclinical organ damage (HMOD) [43]. This may be due to inadequate treatment or control, so elevated SBP is a risk factor.

In this study, a novel COVID-19 severity prediction model was constructed by innovatively combining serum biomarkers reflecting systemic inflammation (LDH, CRP, ALB) with vital parameters directly characterising lung function (respiratory rate R and systolic blood pressure SBP) in a multidimensional manner. This unique biomarker-physiological parameter combination model breaks through the limitations of previous studies, which are mostly limited to one-dimensional indicators, and significantly improves the predictive efficacy of the model by integrating systemic inflammation indicators with key organ function indicators. Particularly noteworthy is that the innovative combinations of indicators, such as the synergistic assessment of ALB and respiratory rate, and the joint analysis of CRP and blood pressure, provide a new multidimensional perspective for the assessment of the severity of COVID-19, which enables our model to maintain simplicity while demonstrating superior sensitivity and specificity than traditional models. This multi-parameter organic integration research strategy opens up new avenues for the clinical assessment of COVID-19.

Notably, our study has certain limitations. First, owing to the limitations of retrospective studies, the collection of variables, such as BMI, vaccination status, and peripheral blood oxygen status, is incomplete, as is the collection time of blood indicators. Prospective studies can be subsequently conducted to dynamically observe and compare blood test results at specific time points before and after infection. Second, our study was a single-center study, and cases were not collected for external verification to verify the model’s generalizability. We can develop web calculators for clinical use after our nomogram is prospectively or externally validated.

Conclusions

When R, SBP, LDH, and CRP are increased and ALB is reduced, the risk of having severe COVID-19 is high. Combined with R, SBP, ALB, LDH, and CRP, a nomogram prediction model was established to predict the risk of patients with severe COVID-19, and this model can assist in the clinical prediction and identification of patients with severe or critically ill COVID-19.
