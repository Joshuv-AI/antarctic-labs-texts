Harnessing the dual immunomodulatory function of myeloid-derived suppressor cells to reshape the inflammatory microenvironment for osteoarthritis therapy
Authors: Zili Guo, Tao Chen, Xuehuan Wen, Qiangchang Fu, Jiabin Lai, Jiajin Li, Jiayu Chen, Jie Weng, Wang Wen, Qihua Cao, Junxiong Zhu, Zhaoming Ye, Xiaohua Yu, Lifeng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) pathogenesis is profoundly influenced by dysregulated immune dynamics, where persistent interleukin-17 (IL-17)/T helper 17 (Th17) cell mediated inflammation coordinates with failed regenerative processes to perpetuate joint destruction. Here, we unveil the role of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) as dual-phase regulators that paradoxically orchestrate both inflammatory escalation and tissue repair in OA progression. Intra-articular administration of MDSCs in OA mice amplified IL-17 dependent inflammatory cascades and chemokine-driven leukocyte recruitment, revealing a context-dependent pro-inflammatory phenotype. Unexpectedly, MDSC depletion failed to attenuate joint damage, implying their indispensable yet multifaceted role in OA pathogenesis. Mechanistically, MDSCs exhibited functional plasticity by upregulating arginase-1 to polarize M2 macrophages, fostering a regenerative niche alongside their inflammatory activity. To resolve this duality, we developed a bio-responsive hydrogel-microsphere system integrating transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) and interleukin-1 β1 antibody (anti–IL-1β) loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs). This spatiotemporally controlled platform selectively suppressed MDSC-mediated Th17 cell expansion while harnessing their intrinsic capacity to drive M2 macrophage polarization and chondrogenesis. The resultant shift from a pro-inflammatory to pro-regenerative microenvironment significantly attenuated cartilage erosion and restored joint integrity in OA models. Our findings redefine MDSCs as bifunctional immune orchestrators in OA and establish precision biomaterial guided immune decoding as a paradigm-shifting therapeutic strategy. By engineering MDSCs plasticity through antagonistic cytokine delivery, this work provides a blueprint for microenvironment remodeling in degenerative joint diseases.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), affecting more than 520 million people worldwide is characterized by the breakdown of joint cartilage and underlying bone, resulting in joint dysfunction and disability [1,2]. Such devastating consequences of OA are deeply rooted in the failure of cartilage regeneration, which is initiated by prolonged pro-inflammatory response caused by various factors including injury, age, lifestyle, etc [[3], [4], [5]]. The typical first-line pharmaceutics for OA includes anti-inflammatory drugs such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aiming to manage symptoms and maintain joint function [6]. However, such treatments can only alleviate the clinical symptoms of the disease but failed to address the underlying causes of OA progression, which inevitably drive the disease progression into a vicious circle due to the degenerative and self-worsening nature of OA [[7], [8], [9]]. Since the pro-inflammatory microenvironment of OA severely shields regenerative cues from protecting cartilage, which further intensifies the inflammation to deteriorate the progression of OA, emerging therapeutic approaches capable of restoring the pro-inflammatory reaction into pro-regenerative immune responses in the joint are highly desirable in the context of OA management.

Recent advancements in OA treatment have focused significantly on leveraging both innate and adaptive immune responses to alleviate OA symptoms and deter disease progression. For instance, macrophage phenotype polarization has been explored as innate immune modulation to suppress their pro-inflammatory counterparts [[10], [11], [12]]. The anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages are more suitable for the treatment of acute inflammatory diseases, their efficacy is considerably limited in OA due to its chronic condition [13,14]. Besides, the role of adaptive immunity, involving T cells is rapidly recognized, evidenced by the prevalence of significant population of T cells in the inflamed joint, especially T helper 1 cell (Th1) and T helper 17 cell (Th17) subsets [15,16]. These Th cells exacerbate joint inflammation and tissue degradation through the production of cytokines like interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and interleukin 17 (IL-17), which stimulate other immune cells and contribute to cartilage and bone breakdown [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. In particular, Th17 cells, as the effector subpopulations of CD4+ T cells and the primary source of IL-17, has been identified as a key mediator in the initial stages of inflammation, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory mediators, including the expression of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumour necrosis factor (TNF), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in various cell types [[21], [22], [23]]. As results, the proactive Th17 cells not only serve as driving force to breakdown cartilage by releasing more inflammation mediators and matrix metalloproteinase (MMPs), but also exacerbate subchondral bone resorption by inducing the production of receptor activator of nuclear factor-κb (NF-κB) ligand and activating osteoclasts [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Hence, targeting Th17 cells for immunomodulation shows promise for developing a comprehensive approach to restoring joint immune function in OA.

Unlike macrophages or T cells, which typically exhibit unidirectional immunological profiles in OA, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) demonstrate remarkable plasticity and can exert both immunosuppressive and pro-inflammatory effects depending on the local cytokine milieu [[28], [29], [30]]. Furthermore, MDSCs are a heterogeneous population broadly classified into two major subsets: monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs, CD11b+ Ly6Chi Ly6G−) and polymorphonuclear or granulocytic MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs, CD11b+ Ly6Clo Ly6G+). This functional and phenotypic diversity makes MDSCs a compelling target for immune reprogramming strategies in OA. On one hand, MDSCs suppress immune responses by expressing high levels of arginase 1 (Arg-1), producing immunosuppressive cytokines like interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and promoting regulatory T cell expansion, thereby mitigating excessive inflammation and maintaining immune homeostasis [31,32]. On the other hand, MDSCs also play a pro-inflammatory role in joint microenvironment by enhancing the differentiation of Th17 cells, which is largely mediated by the secretion of IL-1β [33,34]. Several studies have indicated that the proportion of circulating MDSCs positively correlates with the levels of Th17 cells in the blood, which suggests that MDSCs may facilitate the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into Th17 cells. This interaction potentially exacerbates the inflammatory processes and contributes to bone erosion. However, the exact mechanism of MDSCs in the pathogenesis of OA remains to be fully elucidated [[35], [36], [37]].

In this study, we explored the dual immune modulating roles of MDSCs in OA progression. By administering MDSCs into the joints of OA mice, we observed that MDSCs acted as pro-inflammatory mediators, accelerating OA via enhanced chemokine expression and robust inflammatory responses, particularly through the activation of IL-17 signaling pathway. In vivo experiments confirmed that MDSCs indeed promote Th17 cells differentiation and subsequent IL-17 mediated immune response. Attempts to deplete MDSCs did not yield significant impact on delaying joint degeneration, suggesting a complex role in OA. Nevertheless, the upregulation of Arg-1 suggests a potential association of MDSCs with M2 macrophages, indicating the proregenerative role of MDSCs through M2 macrophages. In response, we developed a dual-release hydrogel microsphere system encapsulating mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) loaded with IL-1β antibody (anti-IL-1β) and TGF-β1, which effectively blocked MDSCs mediated Th17 cells differentiation, reduced inflammation, and promoted M2 polarization and cartilage repair. This approach illustrates how manipulating MDSCs regulatory pathways can transition the inflammatory milieu to a regenerative state, offering a comprehensive treatment strategy for OA.

Materials and methods

Materials

Hyaluronic acid Methacryloyl (HAMA) and PDMS chip were purchased from EFL-Tech Co.,Ltd. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (size: 100 nm, pore diameter: 3–4 nm) were purchased from Ruixi Biotech Co.Ltd. EasySep™ Mouse CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit were obtained from STEMCELL Technologies. BD GolgiStop™ Protein Transport Inhibitor were obtained from Becton, Dickinson and Company. Lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP), Span80 and paraffin oil were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of MSN@TGF-β1/anti-IL-1β

1.0 mg of MSN-NH2 was dispersed in 1.0 mL of PBS, followed by the addition of 0.5 mg of anti-IL-1β (BE0246, Bioxcell, USA) and 2.5 μg of TGF-β1 (100-21C, PeproTech, USA) (concentration from literature [[38], [39], [40]]). The mixture was stirred at 37 °C for 24 h. MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β was obtained by centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 20 min. Determine the concentrations of unbound drugs (TGF-β1 and Anti-IL-1β) in the supernatant used TGF-β1 (EK0513, Boster, China) and Anti-IL-1β (ELA2031, TW-REAGENG, China) ELISA kits to calculate the encapsulation capacity.

Synthesis of HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/anti-IL-1β microspheres

HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β was prepared with modified microliquid fluidity focusing device. In short, a mixture of 4 wt% HAMA, MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β, and 0.5 wt% LAP was used as the aqueous phase and 5 wt% Span80 in paraffin oil as oil phase. Allow the droplets to spread flat into the petri dish and cross-link under blue light (405 nm, 20W, 15 min). Lastly, to eradicate surfactant and oil phase, the microspheres were washed once using acetone and three times using deionized water. HAMA-MSN microspheres and HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β microspheres were prepared in the same way.

Characterization of microspheres

Morphology and diameter of microspheres were detected with bright-field microscope (ZEISS, Germany) and the dried microspheres were observed with scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Scios2 Hivac, ThermoFisher). The mean diameter of microspheres was measured using Image J software. To quantify the release of anti-IL-1β and TGF-β1 from the microspheres, HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β microspheres were first immersed in 1.0 mL phosphate buffer (pH = 7.2) and agitated using a shaker at 80 rpm. Then, the supernatant was replaced with PBS at different time.

Swelling ability of microspheres

5.0 mg of freeze-dried microspheres was added into 1.0 mL deionized water and put in a shaker with a constant temperature of 37 °C. Microspheres were weighed (Wt) at the 0.5th, 1st, 2nd, and 3rd hour, after drying the weight was W0, swelling was calculated with Wt/W0

Biodegradability of microspheres

10.0 mg of microspheres were added into 1.0 mL PBS solution (with 500 U mL−1, Hyaluronidase) and put in shaker with constant temperature of 37 °C. The microspheres at 1st, 2nd, 4th, 6th, and 8th weeks after drying were weighed (Wt) and residual weight (%) was calculated with Wt/W0.

Cell culture

ATDC5 cells and RAW 264.7 cells were bought from Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). These cells were cultured in Dulbecco modified eagle medium (DMEM) containing 4500 mg L−1 glucose, 10 % fetal bovine serum (A5256701, Gibico) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 at 37 °C.

Biocompatibility of microspheres

The cytotoxicity of HAMA, HAMA-MSN and HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β was assessed via CCK-8 cell viability assay, live/dead staining, and fluorescence microscope imaging. ATDC5 cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL of medium for 24 h to facilitate cell adhesion to the wells. Then, the original medium per well was discarded and replaced with fresh medium made from varied materials with varying concentrations. Specifically, on days 1, 3, and 5, medium per well was replaced with 100 μL serum-free medium which contained 10 % CCK-8 solution, and then, the cells were incubated for another 1h under regular cell culture situations.

Similarly, after 1, 3, and 5 days of culture, live/dead cell staining reagent was determined by way of adding into wells for 30 min incubation at 25 °C. Fluorescence microscope was used to observe live cells on the culture dishes and microspheres, and Image J software was used to calculate cell density per unit area and per time point.

Immunofluorescence in vitro

The ATDC5 cells were inoculated into 48-well plates at 5 × 104/well. After 4 days of culture, the medium was discarded, rinsed three times with PBS, and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Treated with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 15 min, 5 % skimmed milk was used to block the cells for 2 h in a constant temperature shaker at 4 °C. After rinsing the cells three times with PBS, collagen II alpha-1 chain (COL2A1) and matrix metalloproteinase 13 (MMP-13) antibodies (Boster, China) diluted at 1:100 were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C in a constant temperature shaker. The next day, the cells were rinsed 3 times again with PBS and added with the secondary antibody (Boster, China) diluted at 1:100. The secondary antibodies were protected from light during addition and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C in a shaker followed by three rinses of PBS. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Biosharp, China). Fluorescence microscope was utilized for observation and immunofluorescence images were further analyzed by ImageJ software.

MDSCs and Th17 differentiation in vitro

The Mice (C57BL/6) bone marrow (BM) cells were isolated with 26-gauge needle from femurs and tibias. Single-cell suspensions were prepared using a syringe with 20-gauge needle, and erythrocytes were depleted. A total of 5 × 105 BM cells/mL were cultured with IL-6 (P08505, 80 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA) and granulocyte-macrophage colony stimulating factor (GM-CSF) (P01587, 80 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA) in roswell park memorial institute(RPMI)-1640 Medium (Gibico, USA) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 4 days. MDSCs differentiation was detected by flow cytometry after antibody staining.

The naïve CD4+ T cells were isolated from spleen of 6-week-old mice. Following the manufacturer's instructions, naive CD4+ T cells were isolated through magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS). After MACS isolation, naïve cells were plated under Th17 differentiation conditions (stimulation and induction) at 4 × 105 cells/well in 24 well plates. For cell stimulation, plates were coated at least 2 h before use with 5 μg/mL anti-CD3 (BE0002, Bioxcell, USA), and 5 μg/mL anti-CD28 (BE0015-1, Bioxcell, USA). For Th17 induction, TGF-β1 (P01137, 5 ng/mL; Peprotech, USA), IL-6 (P08505, 20 ng/mL, Peprotech, USA), anti-IFN-γ (BE0055, 5 μg/mL, Bioxcell, USA), anti-IL-4 (BE0045, 5 μg/mL, Bioxcell, USA). Cells were incubated in RPMI-1640 Medium (Gibico, USA) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 4 days unless otherwise stated. Th17 cells differentiation was detected by flow cytometry after antibody staining.

Regulation of CD4+ T cell differentiation in vitro

Th17 cell differentiation induction conditions were as described above, with 4 × 105 cells/mL MDSCs co-cultured with CD4+ T cells. In the transwells system, MDSCs were placed in the upper chamber, and CD4+ T cells were placed in the lower chamber. cells were incubated in RPMI-1640 Medium (Gibico, USA) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 4 days, Th17 cells differentiation was detected by flow cytometry after antibody staining.

The culture conditions were the same as above, 4 × 105 cells/mL CD4+ T cells were placed in the lower chamber with MDSC, and HAMA, HAMA-MSN, HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β and anti-IL-1β were added to upper chambers. Cells were incubated in RPMI-1640 Medium (Gibico, USA) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin in a 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 4 days. The differentiation of Th17 cells was detected by flow cytometry.

Regulation of macrophage differentiation in vitro

MDSCs and RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well in a 12-well plate. In the transwells system, MDSCs were placed in the upper chamber, and RAW264.7 cells were placed in the lower chamber and cultured in DMEM under 5 % CO2 at 37 °C for 2 days. Macrophage differentiation was detected by flow cytometry after antibody staining.

Flow cytometry

Before intracellular cytokine staining, Th17 cells were stimulated for 4 h with 1.5 μL GolgiStop™ (BD Bioscience). For surface staining, cells were stained with anti-CD4-PE (100407, Biolegend) for 30 min at 4 °C. Then cells were fixed/permeabilized for 20 min at 4 °C with polyformaldehyde/Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) and stained with anti-IL-17-APC (506915, Biolegend) per manufacturer's protocols.

MDSCs transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf (EP) tubes, and the supernatant was removed by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min). For surface staining, anti-Gr-1-FITC antibody (108405, Biolegend), anti-CD45-BV421 (147719, Biolegend), anti-Ly6C-APC (128015, Biolegend), anti-Ly6G-APC/Cy7 (127623, Biolegend) and anti-CD11b-PE antibody (101207, Biolegend) were added and incubated for 20 min at 4 °C. The cells were washed with 1 mL PBS for 3 times. Stained cells were recorded on Canto (BD Biosciences) and analyzed by FlowJo software.

The RAW 264.7 cells were collected and transferred to 1.5 mL EP tubes, and the supernatant was removed by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min). The cells were washed with 1 mL PBS after fixation and membrane breaking, and anti-CD206-APC antibody (141707, Biolegend) or anti-CD86-BV421 (745716, BD Biosciences) was added and incubated for 20 min at 4 °C. Stained cells were recorded on Canto (BD Biosciences) and analyzed by FlowJo software.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay in vitro

To elucidate therapeutic efficacy of microspheres, the cartilage metabolism-related mRNA was inspected using RT-qPCR. ATDC5 cells were treated as described above for cell immunofluorescence in vitro. Then, total RNA was isolated by RNAeasy™ Animal RNA Isolation Kit (Beyotime, China). cDNA synthesis kit was used to complementarily synthesize DNA (cDNA). cDNA, SYBR Green qPCR mix obtained as well as upstream and downstream primers of genes were blended into 20 μL reaction system as per standard protocols. Amplification reaction was conducted via 7500 fast RT-PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Warrington, UK), PCR at 95 °C for 2 min, and 40 reaction cycles at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s. Then, threshold cycle (CT) value was normalized for COL2A1, ACAN, SOX-9, and MMP-13 via CT value of control GAPDH, relative fold-change in expression of control sample in 2−ΔΔCt method was calculated. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Primers information is listed in Table S1.

Animal experiments

The experimental protocol was established and approved by the institutional animal ethics committee of Zhejiang university school of medicine the second affiliated hospital (2024–82). C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were used to create an osteoarthritis model via the DMM surgery. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium by intraperitoneal injection (30 mg/kg body weight), and then a skin incision was made from the distal patella to the proximal tibial plateau in the right knee joint. The joint capsule immediately medial to the patellar tendon was incised and spread open with scissors. Afterward, the patellar tendon was retracted, and the cranial meniscotibial ligament of the medial meniscus (MMTL) was exposed through blunt dissection of the fat pad. The MMTL was sectioned using a micro-surgical knife, and then the wound was closed cutaneously. The mice were provided with free access to food and water and allowed for unrestricted activity every day.

Eighteen mice were randomly sorted into 3 groups (Sham, Control, MDSC, n = 6). The MDSC group received intra-articular injections of MDSCs (5 × 104 cells/10 μL) twice a week, other group received injection of saline solution and the animals were sacrificed one and four weeks after surgery.

Eighteen mice were randomly sorted into 3 groups (Sham, Control, Anti-Gr-1, n = 6). The Anti-Gr-1 group received intra-articular injections of anti-Gr-1 antibodies (100 μg) twice a week, and animals were sacrificed four weeks after surgery.

Thirty mice were randomly sorted into 5 groups (Sham, Control, HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1, HAMA-MSN@Anti-IL-1β, HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β, n = 6), and intra-articularly injected with HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1, HAMA-MSN@Anti-IL-1β, HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β (50 ng TGF-β1 or 10 μg anti-IL-1β) every two weeks. Animals were sacrificed one and eight weeks after surgery.

Immune cell proportion and ELISA analysis

Mice were divided into eight groups (n = 3) and treated for one round as described above. 7 days later, the mice were sacrificed to collect the whole joints and inguinal lymph nodes. Methods for digesting joint tissue in the references [15]. In brief, joint tissue was diced and digested for 45 min at 37 °C in 0.3 mg/mL Liberase TL (Roche, USA) plus 0.2 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, USA) in serum-free RPMI-1640 Medium (Gibico, USA). Inguinal lymph nodes were ground to obtain single-cell suspension. To verify the differentiation of Th17 cells, single-cell suspensions of lymph nodes and joints were stained with anti-CD4-PE (100407, Biolegend), anti-IL-17-APC (506915, Biolegend) and anti-CD8-PB (100728, Biolegend) antibodies. To study macrophage cell differentiation, single-cell suspensions of joint were stained with anti-F4/80-APC/Cy7 (157315, Biolegend), anti-CD86-BUV395 (745716, BD Biosciences), anti-Arg-1-PE/Cy7 (25-3697-82, eBioscience), anti-iNOS-BV421 (404-5920-82, eBioscience), anti-CD206-APC (141707, Biolegend) antibodies. All of these cells were analyzed by a flow cytometer (BECKMAN, USA).

For ELISA, anesthetize the mouse and use ophthalmic curved forceps to quickly extract the mouse's eyeball. Collect the blood into a 1.5 mL EP tube and let it stand at room temperature for 2 h to allow the blood to coagulate. Centrifuge at 4 °C, 3000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the upper layer of serum. Collect and store for later use. Digest the joint tissue as described above. After 45 min of digestion, centrifuge and collect the supernatant for future use. Measure the concentrations of IL-1β (EK0394), IFN-γ (EK0375), TNF-α (EK0527), IL-6 (EK0411), and IL-17 (EK0431) in the serum and joint tissue digestion supernatant using the ELISA kit (Boster, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols.

Radiographic assessment

Mice were anesthetized and executed, the knee joints were collected. Then, isolated knee joint specimens were soaked in 4 % paraformaldehyde fix solution for 1 day, followed by storing in 70 % ethanol at room temperature. The knee joints of mice were subjected to X-ray radiography system (Faxitron X-ray, USA), and the knee space width of mice from various groups were analyzed as per scan images. Next, knee specimens were scanned via micro-CT (Scanco Medical, Switzerland) at a scanning voltage of 70 kV and scanning current of 130 μA for reconstructing subchondral bone and knee joints as well as analyzing osteophyte volume.

Histologic and immunohistochemical analysis

At predetermined time points (1, 4 and 8 weeks post-operative), treated knee specimens was collected and decalcified with EDTA decalcified solution (pH 7.4, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C for 1 month. Then, specimens were embedded in paraffin and cut into thin slices of 5 μm thickness; H&E staining and Safranin O-fast green staining. Severity of osteoarthritis per specimen via stained image evaluation as per Mankin scores modified and osteoarthritis research society international (OARSI) was analyzed. The Mankin score assesses structural integrity (0–6 points), cellularity (0–3 points), matrix staining (0–4 points), and tidemark integrity (0–1 points), with a maximum score of 14 points. Based on the above results, initial site of specimen was determined for immunohistochemistry. Paraffin sections were stained using Collagen II and MMP-13 antibodies (Servicebio, China); then, the color was developed with secondary antibody and diaminobenzidine (DAB) system and staining results were quantified with Image J software. Specimens treated with 10 % goat serum to block non-specific protein adsorption for 20 min for immunofluorescence staining. Afterward, the sections were incubated with rabbit anti-CD4 (Servicebio, 1:2000) and mouse anti-IL-17 (Servicebio, 1:3000) at 4 °C overnight for immunofluorescence staining. Thereafter, the sections were washed 3 times with PBS and then incubated with FITC conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio) and APC conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio) for 2 h at room temperature (RT) while being protected from the light. DAPI was used for staining nuclei and the sections were analyzed by using confocal laser scanning microscope.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

Mice were euthanized to collect whole joint samples after four weeks postoperatively. In the next step, samples were allocated into three groups: Sham (mice with sham operation), Control (mice treated with PBS injection), and MDSCs (mice treated with MDSCs injection). For the MDSCs groups, 5 × 104 MDSCs were injected into the knee joint postoperatively twice weekly. Each group had three replicates. The joint samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using Trizol. RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was conducted at Hangzhou Kaitai Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Hangzhou, China).

Statistical analysis

All statistical data were processed with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software and expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two groups were compared using Student's t-test, and multiple groups were compared using one-way ANOVA. p < 0.05 was set as the statistically significant standard.

Result and discussion

MDSCs intra-articular injection exacerbate knee OA

The prevailing understanding of OA has evolved from viewing it merely as a mechanical disorder due to cartilage wear to recognizing it as a multifaceted biological process involving biomechanics, inflammation, and immune system interactions [[41], [42], [43]]. Considering OA as a chronic wound emphasizes the significant role of inflammation and corresponding repair strategy, which highlights the controversial role of MDSCs in the context of immune related arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and ankylosing spondylitis (AS). However, the immune regulatory functions of MDSCs in OA, evidenced by increased levels in the peripheral blood of OA patients compared to healthy controls, call for a clearer understanding of their precise role [36,44]. To investigate the role of MDSCs in OA pathogenesis, we injected MDSCs into the joint cavity of mice in a DMM model weekly for 4 weeks and assessed OA severity through various approaches (Fig. 1A). We identified the subtypes of in vitro induced MDSCs, which were predominantly monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) (Fig. S1). Radiographic and Micro-CT analyses revealed that MDSCs injections exacerbated subchondral bone abnormalities, such as increased ossicles and irregular morphology around the tibial plateau when compared to controls (Fig. 1B). The joint space in the MDSC group decreased by 30 % (Fig. 1C), and histological staining showed worsened articular cartilage degradation in the MDSC group, with increased surface discontinuities and defects (Fig. 1D and E). Besides, the severity of OA was assessed using the Mankin scoring scale, which evaluates structural changes, cellular abnormalities, and matrix staining, with the total score used to illustrate differences between groups (Fig. 1F). The results indicated that the Mankin score in the MDSC group was significantly higher than that in other groups, with a statistically significant difference compared to the control group. This suggests that the increase in MDSCs exacerbates the severity of OA. Next, we evaluated the expression of cartilage-associated biomarkers collagen type II alpha 1 chain (COL2A1) and matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13) in each group (Fig. 1G). COL2A1 levels significantly decreased in the MDSCs group with discontinuous distribution along the articular cartilage surface (Fig. 1H). In order to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of MDSCs in promoting OA, we collected joint tissue samples from sham-operated mice and DMM model mice treated with PBS or MDSC cells for transcriptomic analysis. We analyzed RNA sequencing data from sham-operated and DMM model tissues, revealing significant upregulation of matrix metalloproteinase genes (MMP2, MMP13) and collagen biosynthesis genes (Col3a1, Col5a2) in DMM mouse joints (Fig. 1I). Gene ontology (GO) analysis indicated activation of extracellular matrix organization and collagen formation (Fig. S2A). A heatmap showed enhanced collagen synthesis and matrix degradation (Fig. 1J). These findings align with OA pathophysiology, confirming the validity of our mouse arthritis model.

Subsequently, we investigated the transcriptomic differences of joint tissues between DMM model mice alone and those that received MDSCs injection. The GO analysis revealed that after the injection of MDSCs, the inflammation-related biological processes in the joint tissues of the mice were significantly affected, particularly the production of molecular mediators of immune response, rather than the pathways related to extracellular matrix and collagen organization presented in the comparison between the DMM group and the sham group (Fig. S2B). The volcano plot indicated that MDSC injection resulted in a significant upregulation of several chemokine genes, including cxcl1, ccl17, and ccl7, which were mainly secreted by different immune cells (Fig. 1K). Existing literature supports that MDSCs can recruit substantial numbers of immune cells, particularly neutrophils, through the secretion of chemokines. The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) also supported this phenomenon, showing significant enrichment of inflammatory response and INF-γ signaling pathways after MDSC injection (Fig. 2L and Fig. S2C and D). Subsequently, we conducted Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis and found a significant upregulation of the IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 1M). Previous studies have demonstrated that the injection of MDSCs can exacerbate the progression of RA by promoting the differentiation of Th17 cells [33,45]. To further investigate this phenomenon, we analyzed genes involved in the IL-17 signaling pathway and discovered that, in addition to chemokines, MDSCs injection resulted in the upregulation of IL-1β, as well as matrix metalloproteinases Mmp3 and Mmp13. In addition, we also noted that the expression levels of various IL-17 receptors were upregulated in the joint tissues, which indirectly confirmed the role of IL-17 in the progression of OA after MDSCs injection (Fig. 1N). MDSCs promote the progression of osteoarthritis through Th17 cells and IL-17

To further validate the role MDSCs in the progression of OA, we assessed the inflammatory cytokines and the number of Th17 cells in joints and serum after MDSCs injection into the joint. Consistent with transcriptome analysis, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β and IL-6 levels were upregulated after MDSCs transfusion both in the joints and serum (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3A). More importantly, intra-articular Th17 cells rose from 3.12 % to 3.77 % after MDSC treatment while Th17 cells in healthy joints were maintained in a lower level presented as 1.10 % (Fig. 2B). Besides, the number of Th17 cells in the draining lymph nodes were also significantly elevated after MDSCs injection as compared to healthy group and control group (Fig. S3B). Correspondingly, levels of IL-17 in both intra-articular and serum samples were elevated post-injection (Fig. 2C and D). Immunofluorescence staining of joint sections confirmed increased IL-17 distribution on the cartilage surface as well as CD4+ IL-17+ T cells presence on the synovium tissue in the MDSC group as compared to the sham and OA control-operated group (Fig. 2E). These findings validate MDSCs’ involvement in exacerbating OA through the promotion of Th17 cell associated inflammatory responses.

Although increased Th17 cell number and elevated IL-17 were firmly validated in both preclinical and clinical OA studies, the exact role of TH17 in OA still needs to be addressed. We first induced Th17 cells from naïve CD4+ T cells in vitro (Fig. S3C). Then we co-cultured Th17 cells and chondrocytes in vitro. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that after co-culturing with Th17 cells, the expression level of COL2A1 in chondrocytes significantly decreased, while the expression of MMP-13 markedly increased (Fig. 2F). This observation is consistent with what we observed in joint tissues after reinfusing MDSCs into the mouse DMM model. Additionally, we successfully induced MDSCs in vitro and identified the predominant M-MDSC subtype. We also demonstrated that MDSCs were able to promote Th17 cell differentiation and IL-17A levels in vitro (Fig. 2H). These results suggest that MDSCs might promote OA progression by enhancing IL-17-related inflammatory responses through the differentiation of Th17 cells in the joint cavity.

According to these data, we hypothesized that depletion of MDSCs from the joint cavity might inhibit Th17 related inflammatory responses, and then slow down OA progression. To test this hypothesis, we used anti-Gr-1 antibodies to eliminate MDSCs in the joint cavity and observed decreased IL-17 expression and CD4+ T cells number in the synovial tissue (Fig. S3D and E), confirming that MDSCs depletion could alleviate IL-17-related inflammatory responses in OA mouse joint tissues. However, contrary to expectations, significant suppression of IL-17 following MDSC depletion did not alleviate OA symptoms. Micro-CT, alongside HE and safranin O-fast green staining of joint tissues, demonstrated that joint space and cartilage damage did not show significant improvement after the elimination of MDSCs (Fig. 2I, J, K). Based on the OARSI score, it was observed that the score in the MDSC group decreased and showed a significant difference compared to the control group. This further confirms that MDSCs depletion can alleviate cartilage loss caused by arthritis (Fig. 2L). These findings imply that MDSCs may influence osteoarthritis progression through additional mechanisms beyond modulating IL-17-related inflammation.

MDSCs presentation in joint tissue counteract OA progress via M2 polarization of macrophage

Given that the depletion of MDSCs did not significantly improve OA outcomes, we speculated that MDSCs might affect OA progression through mechanisms not previously identified. Consequently, we revisited the bulk RNA sequencing data from OA joint tissues following MDSC injection. Our findings revealed a notable upregulation of Arg1 gene, typically associated with M2 macrophages known for their roles in tissue repair and regeneration (Fig. 1K). Considering extensive research in tumor immunology shows that MDSCs can exert immune-suppressive effects by promoting M2 polarization [46], we propose that a similar modulatory effect of MDSCs might be present in OA, potentially less pronounced than in tumor environments. This observation underscores the need for further investigation into the multifaceted roles of MDSCs in the context of OA.

To validate this hypothesis, we assessed macrophage infiltration in OA tissues after MDSC injection. Our results demonstrated a significant increase in macrophage presence on the joint surface. Flow cytometric analysis indicated that both M1 and M2 macrophage populations were elevated post-injection, with a marked predominance of M2 macrophages over M1 macrophages (Fig. 3A and B). Immunofluorescence staining of joint sections also showed increased F4/80+ CD206+ macrophages on the synovium tissue in the MDSC group as compared to the sham and OA control-operated group (Fig. 3C). In vitro assays further confirmed that MDSCs promote M2 macrophage polarization and secretion of cytokines which contribute to tissue repair, including Arg1, IL-10, IL-4 and TGF-β (Fig. 3D and E). Furthermore, co-culturing M2 macrophages with chondrocytes in vitro demonstrated that M2 macrophages significantly enhance COL2A1 levels in chondrocytes while inhibiting MMP-13 expression, underscoring their cartilage repair capabilities (Fig. 3F and G). Besides, a significant reduction in macrophage numbers and Arg1 levels in joint tissues was observed upon MDSC removal (Fig. S3F and G). Collectively, based on our results regarding the roles of MDSCs during OA progression, we propose that MDSCs influence OA through two diametrically opposed mechanisms: exacerbating inflammation via the Th17-related pathway and enhancing anti-inflammatory and reparative functions through the promotion of macrophage M2 polarization. Thus, merely removing MDSCs from the joint may not be an effective strategy for modulating OA progression due to their complex dual roles.

Fabrication and characterization of HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/anti-IL-1β microspheres for the modulation of Th17 differentiation and macrophage M2 polarization

So far, we have confirmed the dual roles of MDSCs during OA progression: while MDSCs removal suppresses Th17-driven inflammation, it impairs M2 polarization. In order to leverage MDSCs as potential therapeutic targets for OA, we propose a novel immunomodulatory strategy that mitigates OA inflammation by inhibiting Th17 differentiation while addressing reduced M2 polarization by promoting macrophage pro-regenerative functions. Given that IL-1β plays a pivotal role in MDSCs induced Th17 cells differentiation, while TGF-β1 was known to promote M2 polarization and induce CD4+ T cells differentiated into regulatory CD4+ T (Treg) cells [47,48], we propose that a combination of anti-IL-1β and TGF-β1 may be an effective strategy to manipulate MDSCs toward pro-regenerative function and offer a balanced immunomodulatory treatment for OA (Fig. 4). Specifically, HAMA exhibits excellent injectability and joint lubrication properties, while MSNs demonstrate highly efficient loading capacity. Of particular significance is the nanocarrier's ability to penetrate the cartilage matrix, facilitating deep cartilage delivery [[49], [50], [51]]. The results indicate that although the individual application of TGF-β1 or anti-IL-1β can achieve partial immune modulation, only the combined use of TGF-β1 and anti-IL-1β effectively reduces MMP-13 expression levels.

HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/anti-IL-1β attenuates osteoarticular cartilage loss

Since the microspheres reduced Th17 and macrophage inflammation in vivo, next their effects on cartilage preservation under OA were assessed. Post-OA induction surgery, mice were evaluated at week 8 using radiographs and micro-CT scans. Results showed variable joint space widths among groups, with the control group being the narrowest. The HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β group showed joint spaces like normal, though this method was less sensitive for mild to moderate OA (Fig. 9A and B). We conducted Micro-CT analysis on the knee joint and subchondral bone to further evaluate OA progress. The HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β group showed improved joint morphology and less bone redundancy. Bone mineral density (BMD) analyses revealed a significant increase in BMD in this group, nearing levels of the sham group, indicating effective treatment (Fig. 9B). In summary, the HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β treatment significantly enhanced joint stability and reduced subchondral bone abnormalities. Histological examination including H&E and Safranin O-Fast green staining after 8 weeks revealed less cartilage erosion and improved morphological integrity in the treated groups compared to control group, with the HAMA-MSN@TGF-β1/Anti-IL-1β group showing the least cartilage damage, as indicated by lower OARSI score (Fig. 9C and D). Additionally, MMP-13 expression was lower and COL2A1 expression was higher in this group, suggesting effective modulation of cartilage metabolism and reduction of chondrocyte catabolism (Fig. 9E, F and G). Although TGF-β has been reported to have potential negative effects, such as causing cartilage fibrosis and hypertrophy, these effects are only observed with high-dose repeated injections [55]. Recent studies have shown that delivering TGF-β or increasing its expression can promote the repair of damaged cartilage in OA [56,57]. No significant adverse reactions associated with TGF-β1 were observed in this study, due to the use of lower concentrations of TGF-β1 and maintaining a lower injection frequency. Conclusion

This study has delineated the pivotal role of MDSCs in modulating the progression of OA by influencing Th17 differentiation and macrophage polarization. Leveraging these insights, we developed a hydrogel microsphere delivery system aiming to restore immune equilibrium within the joint microenvironment. The co-delivery of TGF-β1 and anti-IL-1β encapsulated in the hydrogel microspheres accomplished two major therapeutic objectives. First, it disrupted the MDSC-Th17 signaling axis, thereby alleviating IL-17-mediated joint inflammation. Second, it promoted the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype, thereby accelerating the repair of damaged cartilage. These mechanisms collectively shifted the pro-inflammatory microenvironment to a regenerative niche, remarkably improving joint function and endowing a conducive microenvironment for cartilage repair and extracellular matrix deposition. Collectively, this study not only reveals critical pathological mechanisms of OA but also lays the groundwork for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies aimed at immune modulation for OA treatment.

To place our findings in the context of existing literature, it is important to note that the immunological role of MDSCs has been extensively studied in autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), but their function in osteoarthritis (OA) remains underexplored. Previous studies have shown that MDSCs can exacerbate joint inflammation in RA by promoting Th17 cell differentiation through IL-1β and arginase-1–dependent pathways [58,59]. Our transcriptomic and immunological data confirm that a similar mechanism exists in OA, where MDSCs enhance IL-17 signaling and Th17 cell expansion, thereby contributing to cartilage degradation.

However, unlike in RA, our study reveals a previously unappreciated regenerative role of MDSCs in OA through the promotion of M2 macrophage polarization. This dual role has been well described in tumor biology, where MDSCs support both immune evasion and tissue remodeling [[60], [61], [62]], but its relevance in OA has not been clearly defined. Our findings thus expand the current understanding of MDSCs by demonstrating their functional plasticity in a degenerative joint disease context.

In the field of biomaterial-based immunomodulation, several recent studies have explored the use of nanoparticles or hydrogels to reprogram local immune responses in OA. For example, Shanshan Yuan et al. accurately simulated the function of efferocytosis by incorporating S1P, PS, and SPD into nanovesicles. These nanovesicles exert anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive effects through multiple mechanisms, including promoting M2 macrophage polarization, enhancing Treg differentiation, and suppressing Th17 cell levels [63], while Helena Rouco et al. developed an injectable thermosensitive hybrid hydrogel loaded with β-lapachone. By delivering the anti-inflammatory agent β-lapachone into OA joints, the hydrogel effectively reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, MMP9, MMP13, and CXCL8, thereby alleviating cartilage damage [64]. Compared to these approaches, our dual-functional hydrogel microsphere system offers a more integrated strategy by simultaneously suppressing the pro-inflammatory MDSCs–Th17 axis and enhancing the pro-regenerative MDSCs–M2 axis. This dual-targeting approach is particularly advantageous in OA, where inflammation and tissue degeneration are tightly intertwined.

Despite the promising results presented in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we identified that the majority of MDSCs in our system were monocytic (M-MDSCs), we did not perform detailed functional comparisons between M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs. Given the known functional heterogeneity of MDSCs, future studies should dissect the subtype-specific contributions to both inflammation and regeneration in OA. Second, our findings are based on a murine DMM model, and the translational relevance of our hydrogel microsphere system requires further validation in large animal models or human OA tissues. Third, while we focused on the MDSCs–Th17 and MDSCs–macrophage axes, other immune cell populations such as regulatory T cells (Tregs), dendritic cells, and B cells may also play important roles in the joint immune microenvironment and warrant further investigation. Finally, the long-term safety and potential off-target effects of repeated intra-articular microsphere injections need to be systematically evaluated in future preclinical studies.

Overall, our study provides a foundational framework for immune microenvironment remodeling in OA and opens new avenues for precision immunotherapy using biomaterial-guided strategies.
