Single-cell analysis identifies PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes in early cervical squamous cell carcinoma with HPV infection
Authors: Peiwen Fan, Danning Dong, Yaning Feng, Xiaonan Zhu, Ruozheng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC), the most common subtype of cervical cancer, is primarily caused by the high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and genetic susceptibility. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been widely used in CESC research to uncover the diversity of cell types and states within tumor tissues, enabling a detailed study of the tumor microenvironment (TME). This technology allows precise mapping of HPV infection in cervical tissues, providing valuable insights into the initiation and progression of HPV-mediated malignant transformation.Methods:We performed the scRNA-seq to characterize gene expression in tumor tissues and paired adjacent para-cancerous tissues from four patients with early-stage CESC using the 10× Genomics platform. The HPV infection and its subtypes were identified using the scRNA data and viral sequence mapping, and trajectory analyses were performed using HPV+ or HPV- cells. Interactions between different types of keratinized cells and their interactions with other cell types were identified, and pathways and specificity markers were screened for proliferating keratinized cells. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset was used to verify the prognostic correlation between tumor-specific PI3+S100A7+ keratinocyte infiltration and CESC, and the localization relationship between PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes and macrophages was verified by immunofluorescence staining.Results:Various types of keratinocytes and fibroblasts were the two cell types with the most significant differences in percentage between the tumor tissue samples and paired adjacent non-cancerous tissue samples in the early stages of CESC. We found that PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes were associated with early HPV-positive CESC, and PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes were more abundant in tumors than in adjacent normal tissues in the TCGA-CESC dataset. Analysis of clinical information revealed that the infiltration of PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes was notably higher in tumors with poor prognosis than in those with good prognosis. Additionally, multiplex immunofluorescence analysis showed a specific increase in PI3+S100A7+ expression within tumor tissues, with PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes and CD163+ macrophages being spatially very close to each other. In the analysis of cell–cell interactions, macrophages exhibited strong crosstalk with PI3+S100A7+ proliferating keratinocytes in HPV-positive CESC tumors, mediated by tumor necrosis factor (TNF), CCL2, CXCL8, and IL10, highlighting the dynamic and tumor-specific enhancement of macrophage–keratinocyte interactions, which are associated with poor prognosis and immune modulation. Using CIBERSORTx, we discovered that patients with high infiltration of both PI3+S100A7+ proliferating keratinocytes and macrophages had the shortest overall survival. In the analysis of cell–cell interactions, PI3+S100A7+ proliferating keratinocytes and macrophages were found to be involved in highly active pathways that promote differentiation and structure formation, including cytokine receptor interactions, the Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) signaling pathway, and TNF signaling pathway regulation. Further subtyping of fibroblast populations identified four subtypes. The C1 group, characterized by its predominance in tumor tissues, is a subtype enriched with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), whereas the C3 group is primarily enriched in adjacent non-cancerous tissues and consists of undifferentiated cells. Moreover, the distinct molecular and cellular differences between HPV16- and HPV66-associated tumors were demonstrated, emphasizing the unique tumor-promoting mechanisms and microenvironmental influences driven by each HPV subtype.Conclusions:We discovered a heterogeneous population of keratinocytes between tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues caused by HPV infection and identified macrophages and specific CAFs that play a crucial role during the early stage in promoting the inflammatory response and remodeling the cancer-promoting TME. Our findings provide new insights into the transcriptional landscape of early-stage CESC to understand the mechanism of HPV-mediated malignant transformation in cervical cancer.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common malignant tumor among women worldwide and poses a significant challenge to global health.[1,2] Each year, ~600,000 women are diagnosed with cervical cancer, and over 300,000 women die from it.[3] The 2018 statistics on the five-year survival rate of cancer patients in China showed that the five-year survival rate was 59.8% for cervical cancer patients diagnosed between 2012 and 2015.[4,5] In 2020, the World Health Assembly proposed a “Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem” aiming to increase human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, cervical cancer screening, and patient treatment rates.[6] Despite progress in the prevention, screening, diagnosis, and treatment of cervical cancer over the past decade, there are still significant differences in incidence and mortality rates between countries and regions. According to 2020 statistics, ~90% of deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries and regions, far exceeding those in developed countries.[7,8] More than 95% of cervical cancers are caused by HPV, which is the most common sexually transmitted viral infection. At least 14 subtypes of HPV are carcinogenic.[9] Most sexually active women and men are infected with the virus at some point in their lives, while some are repeatedly infected. More than 90% of these infections will eventually resolve on their own; however, every woman is at risk of HPV infection progressing to chronic infection, and precancerous lesions developing into invasive cervical cancer.[10] The HPV infection rate in Xinjiang is low; however, the detection rates of cervical cancer and precancerous lesions are higher than the national average. Cervical cancer is a prominent public health problem in Xinjiang, especially in southern Xinjiang.[11] According to the latest cervical cancer staging guidelines from the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO),[12] accurate staging is crucial for making treatment plans and providing prognosis consultation for patients. In early stages, such as IA–IB2 and IIA1, surgery is the preferred treatment method.[13] Early-stage cervical cancer is usually asymptomatic and is generally diagnosed only after routine screening or pelvic examinations. Therefore, understanding the origin and development of cervical cancer is essential to understanding the process of carcinogenesis, improving clinical prevention and treatment, and increasing patient survival rates.

The most common subtype of cervical cancer is cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC), which originates from the stratified squamous epithelium of the cervix.[1,14] This subtype accounts for ~70% of all cases, with most resulting from infections with high-risk HPV types, such as HPV16.[15] HPV infects proliferative basal cells of the stratified epithelium, exploiting the host’s squamous differentiation program to complete its lifecycle.[16] During the productive phase of HPV infection, the virus expresses early and late genes that play distinct roles in its lifecycle and oncogenic potential. Early genes, such as E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, and E7, are critical for viral replication and transformation. Among them, E6 and E7 act as major oncogenes, disrupting tumor suppressor pathways by inactivating p53 and retinoblastoma protein (Rb), respectively, thereby promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation and the development of precancerous lesions, such as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN).[17,18] Late genes, L1 and L2, are involved in virion assembly and are typically expressed in differentiated keratinocytes during the final stages of the viral lifecycle.[19] These distinct gene functions highlight the complexity of HPV-driven pathogenesis and its contribution to malignant transformation.

Although HPV infection is a major driver of cervical cancer, it alone does not fully account for the progression of all cases.[20] Cervical tumor tissues exhibit significant heterogeneity, both among patients and within individual tumors, which complicates the understanding of disease progression. This heterogeneity leads to continuous cancer evolution during development and treatment, often contributing to treatment failure and poor prognosis in some patients.[21] The composition of tumor tissue is complex, consisting of diverse cellular components (CCs), extracellular matrix (ECM), chemokines, and cytokines, collectively forming the tumor microenvironment (TME).[22] Key processes in the TME, including immune response regulation, ECM remodeling, and tumor angiogenesis, fundamentally influence cancer aggressiveness.[23] However, the specific molecules and signaling pathways that drive malignant transformation in CESC remain incompletely understood, necessitating further investigation. Due to the heterogeneity in the TME, studies at the bulk tissue level can provide limited information to precisely identify the key factors during this dynamic process. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) is an excellent tool for dissecting the heterogeneity of tumor tissues and investigating the interactions between various cell types in the TME.[24,25,26,27] Several scRNA-seq studies have advanced our understanding of epithelial cell dynamics in the TME of CESC,[28,29,30,31,32,33,34] particularly during tumor initiation and progression. Nevertheless, the molecular features of specific HPV-mediated epithelial cell subtypes and their roles in early malignant transformation remain incompletely understood. Moreover, the interactions between these epithelial cells, tumor-associated fibroblasts, and macrophages in promoting tumor progression require further investigation. By leveraging single-cell resolution techniques, our study aims to address these gaps and provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying HPV-mediated malignant evolution.

Methods

Patients and sample collection

The Medical Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University (K-2021005) approved the collection and use of human samples in this study. In total, four patients with pathologically confirmed cervical cancer were enrolled in the study. They were diagnosed and received treatment at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. All tumor and para-cancerous tissue samples were collected during surgery according to the 2018 FIGO stage: Two patients with IIA1, one patient with IB2, and one patient with IB1. All enrolled patients provided informed consent in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Single-cell suspension preparation

Fresh tissue specimens were immediately immersed in SCelLive tissue preservation solution (Singleron Biotechnology Co., Nanjing, China; Cat# 1010012) and stored at 2–8°C or transported on ice within 30 min post-surgery. Upon receipt, the samples were washed three times with Hanks’ balanced salt solution (HBSS) to remove residual blood and other contaminants. The tissues were minced into small fragments using sterile scissors and digested in SCelLive Tissue Dissociation Solution (Singleron Biotechnology Co., Nanjing, China; Cat# 1020066) at 37°C with constant agitation at 180 r/min for 15 min. The dissociation process was monitored microscopically, and digestion time was extended if necessary to achieve complete dissociation into a single-cell suspension. The resultant cell suspension was filtered through a 40-μm sterile cell strainer (BIOFIL) to remove undigested debris.

To lyse red blood cells, the filtered cell suspension was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (RCLB) (Singleron Biotechnology Co., Nanjing, China; Cat# 1200050002) at a 2:1 (lysis buffer: cell suspension) volume ratio. The mixture was incubated on ice for 5–8 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with 0.04% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for washing. This washing step was repeated, and the final cell pellet was resuspended in PBS (Basal Media). Cell viability and concentration were assessed using the Countstar Rigel fluorescence cell counter with acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) staining.

Droplet-based scRNA-seq

Single-cell suspensions at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/mL were converted to barcoded scRNA-seq libraries according to the protocols of the Chromium Single Cell 5′Library, Gel Bead & Multiplex Kit (10x Genomics). Briefly, the cells were partitioned into gel beads in an emulsion using the ChromiumTM Controller, where cell lysis and barcoded reverse transcription of RNA occurred. TCR-enriched libraries were generated with aliquots from each of the aforementioned Complementary DNAs using the Chromium Single Cell V(D)J Enrichment kit. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 with 150 bp paired-end reads.

scRNA-seq data pre-processing

The raw base call (BCL) files were demultiplexed into FASTQ files using bcl2fastq. Droplet-based sequencing data were quality-controlled using FastQC software. The reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome provided by Cell Ranger (version 2.0, 10x Genomics), and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts were summarized for each cell and gene. For quality control, we applied the following thresholds: Number of total UMIs/reads per cell (nCount)_RNA >500, number of detected genes per cell (nFeature)_RNA >200, and percent.mt <25%. Cells not meeting these criteria were excluded to ensure data reliability [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. This refinement process replaces the earlier filtering by gene counts below 200, top 2% gene counts, and top 2% UMI counts, and cells with more than 20% mitochondrial content. Subsequent analyses were performed using functions from Seurat v3.1.2. We used the NormalizeData and ScaleData functions to normalize and scale gene expressions and identified the top 2000 variable genes using the FindVariableFeatures function for principal component analysis (PCA) analysis. Clustering was performed using the FindClusters function with the top 20 principal components, and batch effects between samples were removed using harmony. Finally, the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) algorithm was applied to visualize the cells in a two-dimensional space.

Cell-type recognition

The cell type of each cluster was determined based on the expression of canonical markers, referencing collections from CellMakerDB, PanglaoDB, and recent literature. Cell doublets were identified by examining the expression patterns of the canonical markers. Clusters showing enrichment for multiple cell-type-specific markers were excluded from further analyses.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis

To identify DEGs, we used the Seurat FindMarkers function with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and default parameters. We selected genes that were expressed in more than 10% of cells within a cluster and had an average log-fold change greater than 0.25. To annotate cell types in each cluster, we combined the expression data of canonical markers from the DEGs with information from relevant literature. Marker expression for each cell type was visualized using heatmaps, dot plots, and violin plots created using Seurat’s DoHeatmap, DotPlot, and VlnPlot functions, respectively. Cells identified as doublets showing markers from multiple cell types were manually removed from the analysis.

Gene ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analysis

To explore the potential functions of DEGs, we conducted GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses using the “clusterProfiler” R package version 3.16.1. Pathways with an adjusted P-value (p_adj) less than 0.05 were deemed significantly enriched. GO gene sets categorized into molecular function (MF), biological process (BP), and CC were used as references for this analysis.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

The GSEA was employed to determine whether predefined gene sets are enriched in specific cell types, thereby reflecting their associated pathways. First, we downloaded hallmark gene sets from the MSigDB database (https://www.gseamsigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).[35] Subsequently, we calculated the enrichment scores and P values for the marker gene sets across different cell types using the GSEA software package in R. This analysis enabled us to evaluate potential biological functions and pathways associated with each cell type.

Trajectory analysis

Cell differentiation trajectories were reconstructed using Monocle 2.[36] Highly variable genes (HVGs) were used to order the cells according to their spatiotemporal differentiation. We used DDRTree for feature selection and dimensionality reduction using the FindVariableFeatures function. The resulting trajectory was visualized using a plotcell trajectory function.

Transcription factors (TFs) activity analysis

The R package Single-Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering was used to infer single-cell gene regulatory networks from scRNA-seq data. It starts by identifying co-expression modules from the scRNA-seq matrix, which includes various TFs and their target genes. Motif enrichment analysis revealed that motifs that were significantly enriched in these co-expression modules were retained, whereas target genes with weak associations with these enriched motifs were removed. The TFs within the co-expression modules, along with the remaining target genes, were identified as regulators. Activity scores for different regulons were calculated for each individual cell, leading to cell clustering based on these activity scores.

Cell–cell interaction analysis

Cell–cell interaction analysis was conducted using CellPhoneDB v2.1.0,[37] focusing on known receptor–ligand interactions between different cell types or subtypes. To establish a null distribution for the average ligand–receptor expression levels in interacting clusters, cluster labels were randomly permuted 1000×. The ligand or receptor expression levels were thresholded based on the average log of gene expression across all cell types. Significant cell–cell interactions were identified with a P-value <0.05 and an average log expression >0.1. These interactions were visualized using Circlize v0.4.10 R package.

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset analysis

Expression and survival data of patients with CESC were obtained from TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to evaluate the differential expression of the markers. To visualize the differences in expression levels between cancerous and adjacent noncancerous tissues, we created a boxplot using the R package ggplot2, highlighting significant differences in gene expression profiles between the two tissue types. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test. The curves were created using the R packages survival (version 3.4.0) and survminer (version 0.4.9). The optimal cutoff values for keratinocyte infiltration were determined using the surv_cutpoint function in the survminer package, which identifies the cutoff that best separates the survival curves. No regression models or adjustments for covariates were applied in this analysis. This additional information has been included to enhance clarity and reproducibility.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIHC)

The Opal 7-color Manual IHC Kit (NEL801001KT, PerkinElmer) and VECTASHIELD HardSet Antifade Mounting Medium (H-1400, Vector Labs, Newark, USA) were used for the assay. Briefly, slides were baked at 63°C for 1 h and deparaffinized using an automatic staining machine (LEICAST5020, LEICA, Deerfield, IL, USA) and antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with hydrogen peroxide for 10 min. The slides were then blocked for 10 min before incubation with one of the following primary antibodies for 1 h to perform immunofluorescence staining: Elafin/Skalp (1:50, 15963-1-AP, Proteintech), S100A7 (1:3000, ER65940, Huabio), CD68 (Ready-to-use, PA014, Abcam), CD163 (Ready-to-use, PA059, Abcam), and HLA–DRA (1:500, ET1610-66, Huabio). After washing with tris-buffered saline with tween (TBST), the slides were treated with the secondary antibody (K8000, DAKO, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for 10 min, after which the Opal dye diluent was added and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Microwave treatment was then applied to remove the antibody complexes. The subsequent markers were counterstained, and the staining process was repeated for each marker. Finally, the slides were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) for 5 min and mounted using antifade mounting medium.

Fluorescence signal quantification

Panoramic multispectral scanning of the slides was conducted using a tissue-FAXS system (TissueFAXS Spectra, TissueGnostics, Los Angeles, CA, USA). The acquired data were imported into Strata-Quest. We utilized a spectral library for spectral splitting to isolate the single-channel fluorescence signals. The DAPI channel was used to identify the nuclei. Based on the effective nuclei, we defined the distance radius according to the staining patterns of each protein channel to detect the protein-specific fluorescent signals. We established thresholds for each channel based on staining characteristics, segmented the positive cell populations, and quantified the positive cells. Additionally, we measured the number and intensity of double-positive cells. The mean fluorescence intensity of the positive cells was calculated by multiplying the intensity by the number of positive cells and dividing by the total number of cells to determine the mean gene expression.

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences in the medians of gene expression between the two groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Differences in proportions of cell types between different groups were evaluated using the chi-squared test, and Fisher’s exact test was used when the sample size was too small. The survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with results presented through survival curves. All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

Single-cell transcriptional profiling of CESC tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues

To investigate the origin and development of early-stage cervical cancer, we performed scRNA-seq on tumor tissue samples (T group) and paired adjacent non-cancerous tissue samples (P group) from four patients with CESC (FIGO stages IB1–IIA1) using the 10x Genomics platform [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C594]. This analysis aimed to elucidate the cellular composition and heterogeneity of the tissues. After data quality control and filtering, we obtained 77,061 cells with a median of 2191 genes and 6550 UMIs per cell. Of these, 43,023 cells were from adjacent non-cancerous tissues, and 34,038 cells were from tumor tissues [Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C594].

ScRNA-seq data from cancerous and adjacent noncancerous tissues were integrated after batch correction, revealing 20 distinct cell clusters. Each cluster was annotated as a specific cell type based on marker gene expression [Figure 1A and B]. The identified cell types included: 12,067 fibroblasts (PTGDS, DCN), 8423 squamous epithelial cells (KRT19, KRT16), 2600 cervical epithelial cells (WFDC2, TFF3), 5683 basal keratinocytes (COL17A1, KRT14), 3852 differentiating keratinocytes (UBE2C, STMN1), 3474 proliferating keratinocytes (S100A7, CNFN), 1427 suprabasal keratinocytes (KRT4, KRT6C), 4933 lymphatic endothelial cells (ACKR1, CCL14), 2488 endothelial progenitor cells (FLT1, RAMP2), 4747 mesenchymal stem cells (RGS5, ACTA2), 1677 myometrial smooth muscle cells (ACTG2, DES), 607 vascular smooth muscle cells (ACKR1, CCL14), 6566 cytotoxic CD8+ T cells (CCL5, NKG7), 5346 naïve CD4+ T cells (IL7R, CXCR4), 2672 regulatory T (Treg) cells (BATF, TNFRSF4), 1505 NK cells (TPSAB1, TPSB2), 2092 naïve B cells (MS4A1), 1088 IgG-plasma B cells (IGKC, IGHG1), 5722 macrophages (IFI30, LYZ), and 92 plasmacytoid dendritic cells (GZMB, IRF4) [Figure 1C, Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C594]. All cell types were present in both T and P tissue samples [Figure 1D].

Next, we conducted a comparative analysis between cancerous and adjacent non-cancerous tissues to determine differences in the absolute proportions of various cell types between the T and P groups [Figure 1E, Supplementary Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C594]. Differences in the cellular composition were observed between the tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues, particularly in keratinocytes and fibroblasts. Proliferating keratinocytes were highly enriched in tumor tissues, whereas basal and suprabasal keratinocytes were predominantly present in adjacent non-cancerous tissues. This indicates a shift in keratinocyte dynamics, characterized by increased proliferation in the TME and a loss of normal differentiation patterns. In contrast, fibroblasts exhibited a markedly lower proportion in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues, suggesting a diminished stromal presence within the TME. These results underscore the distinct roles of keratinocytes and fibroblasts in tumor development and progression.

To investigate the changes in the pathways regulated by tumor-infiltrating cell subpopulations, we utilized the molecular features database (MsigDB) to analyze the pathways of proliferating keratinocytes, differentiating keratinocytes, basal keratinocytes, suprabasal keratinocytes, and fibroblasts in cancerous and adjacent tissues. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed both shared and distinct roles for keratinocytes and fibroblasts in tumor progression. All keratinocyte subtypes were significantly enriched in interferon γ and interferon α response pathways, indicating their involvement in antiviral and inflammatory signaling. Fibroblasts demonstrated a unique enrichment in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated signaling pathways, a process that plays a vital role in ECM remodeling and facilitating tumor invasion.[38] This enrichment underscores the active involvement of fibroblasts in reshaping the TME and supporting cancer progression. In addition, fibroblasts were enriched in angiogenesis and kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) signaling pathways, emphasizing their contributions to vascularization and oncogenic signaling within the TME. Notably, inflammatory response and complement pathways were enriched across all cell types, reflecting broad immune activation within the TME. These findings underscore the distinct yet complementary contributions of keratinocytes and fibroblasts to tumor progression and TME remodeling.

HPV-driven transcriptional and cellular dynamics in keratinocyte subpopulations

In this study, a distinct distribution of keratinocyte subpopulations was observed between T and adjacent P tissues. Basal and suprabasal keratinocytes, associated with differentiation and homeostasis, were predominantly found in adjacent non-cancerous tissues, whereas proliferating keratinocytes, marked by proliferation-related genes, were enriched in tumor tissues. Differentiating keratinocytes, representing an intermediate state, were distributed across both tissue types with distinct patterns [Figure 2A].

Among the four patients whose samples were used for scRNA-seq, one patient (01) was HPV-negative, while three patients (02, 03, and 04) were HPV-positive, including two with the high-risk HPV16 subtype [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C594]. Keratinocyte subtypes and other cell compositions showed variations between HPV-positive and HPV-negative tumor tissues. In HPV-negative tissues, basal keratinocytes and differentiating keratinocytes were more enriched, whereas proliferating keratinocytes were less enriched [Supplementary Figure 2A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. In contrast, HPV-positive tissues exhibited a significant decrease in basal keratinocytes and an increase in proliferating keratinocytes, indicating altered keratinocyte dynamics associated with HPV infection [Supplementary Figure 2B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Additionally, fibroblasts were highly enriched in HPV-positive tissues, highlighting significant stromal remodeling. Similarly, monocytes (8%) were more abundant in HPV-positive tissues, whereas squamous epithelial cells were more prevalent in HPV-negative tissues (17% vs. 9%) [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. These findings emphasize the distinct cellular landscape shaped by HPV status and suggest that HPV-associated changes in keratinocyte composition and stromal components play key roles in tumor progression.

Further analysis of scRNA-seq data revealed that HPV16 early genes (E1, E2, E5, E6, and E7) and late genes (L1 and L2) were actively expressed and enriched in keratinocytes from HPV-positive samples (02, 03, and 04). In contrast, these viral genes were not expressed in cells from HPV-negative samples (01), highlighting the specificity of HPV16 gene activity in HPV-positive tissues. The enrichment of both early and late genes suggests active viral involvement in epithelial cell clusters within HPV-positive tissues [Figure 2B and C]. The analysis further revealed that the expression of E6 and E7 genes was predominantly localized within differentiating keratinocytes in HPV-positive cells, suggesting their pivotal role in disrupting normal differentiation pathways to facilitate viral infection [Figure 2D]. To assess HPV activity, we examined the expression levels of two established clinical markers, p16 and exportin-5. The analysis showed that p16 exhibited widespread expression across various keratinocyte subpopulations. In contrast, exportin-5 was predominantly overexpressed in proliferating and differentiating keratinocytes, suggesting its specific involvement in processes associated with HPV-infected cell proliferation and differentiation [Figure 2E].

To investigate the distinct gene expression profiles of proliferating and differentiating keratinocytes, differential expression analysis identified 3900 genes, with 771 upregulated in proliferating keratinocytes and 3129 in differentiating keratinocytes. The volcano plot highlights clear transcriptional differences [Supplementary Figure 3A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Further analysis using a heatmap revealed the expression patterns of the top 10 DEGs in each group [Supplementary Figure 3B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Notably, S100A7, SPRR3, CNFN, and PI3 were markedly upregulated in proliferating keratinocytes [Supplementary Figure 3C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595], whereas MT2A, NTS, STMN1, and TUBA1B showed prominent upregulation in differentiating keratinocytes [Supplementary Figure 3D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Pathway enrichment analysis showed the TNF signaling pathway as prominent in proliferating keratinocytes, reflecting its role in inflammation and cell interaction [Supplementary Figure 3E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595], whereas oxidative phosphorylation and cell cycle-related pathways were enriched in differentiating keratinocytes, underscoring their metabolic and regulatory demands during differentiation [Supplementary Figure 3F, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Moreover, GSEA results further revealed distinct pathway enrichment patterns in proliferating keratinocytes, reflecting the complex cellular responses induced by HPV infection. The interferon α/β signaling and antimicrobial peptides pathways were significantly upregulated, indicating an active antiviral immune response during the early stages of infection. In contrast, the significant downregulation of the slits and Robos pathway, which is involved in cellular communication and tissue organization, suggests a potential disruption of normal cell signaling and structural integrity [Figure 2F and G]. In addition, the analysis highlighted the significant roles of PI3 and S100A7 in proliferating keratinocytes, with their elevated expression suggesting active involvement in cellular proliferation and stress response pathways during HPV-induced processes [Figure 2H]. Using marker genes, we identified key gene sets associated with the transition of HPV-positive proliferating keratinocytes and analyzed their clinical relevance using data from the TCGA public database. Among these, PI3 and S100A7 were more enriched in tumor samples compared to non-tumor controls, according to a two-sample t-test (P = 0.099 for PI3, P = 0.04 for S100A7). Expression analysis revealed that both genes showed lower levels in early-stage tumors but were elevated in advanced stages, based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P = 0.019 for PI3, P = 0.065 for S100A7). Survival analysis showed that high expression of PI3 and S100A7 was associated with worse prognosis, as analyzed using the log-rank test, with a P-value of 0.034 for PI3 and 0.06 for S100A7. These findings underscore their potential roles as indicators of tumor progression and adverse clinical outcomes in HPV-related cancers [Figure 2I]. To evaluate the impact of HPV infection on the transcriptional regulatory landscape, SCENIC analysis was performed to identify key TFs in HPV-positive and HPV-negative samples. In HPV-positive samples, sex determining region Y-box 2 (SOX2) was identified as the most active transcription factor, aligning with its established role in promoting cervical cancer progression. Conversely, HPV-negative samples were predominantly regulated by TFs, such as bar-like home box 1 (BARX1), homeobox B13 (HOXB13), POU domain, class 3, transcription factor 1 (POU3F1), and LHX2, suggesting divergent transcriptional programs in the presence or absence of HPV infection [Figure 2J].

The presence of proliferating keratinocytes led to worse prognoses in CESC patients

To investigate the impact of HPV infection on keratinocytes, particularly in inducing their transformation into malignant cells, we performed a Monocle 2 pseudotime analysis of various keratinocyte types in HPV-positive patient tissue samples. The analysis revealed a dynamic and hierarchical progression of keratinocyte differentiation, starting from basal keratinocytes and advancing through differentiating keratinocytes, eventually reaching the suprabasal keratinocyte stage [Figure 3A, Supplementary Figure 4A and B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. HPV infects epithelial basal cells, positioning basal keratinocytes at the early part of the pseudotime sequence. Proliferating keratinocytes exhibited complex interactions with both basal and suprabasal keratinocytes but showed distinct differences from differentiating keratinocytes. This suggests that proliferating keratinocytes disrupt normal differentiation, potentially increasing proliferation to support HPV replication. Gene expression analysis along the pseudotime trajectory highlighted distinct transcriptional patterns. Key genes, such as S100A7, OR10H1, LINC01705, and IL36A, exhibited progressive upregulation along the pseudotime trajectory, indicating their roles in advanced differentiation stages [Supplementary Figure 4C and D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Conversely, early-stage markers, including SPINK2, SLC27A6, PRAC1, and HOXB13, showed decreasing expression trends, reflecting their association with basal keratinocyte functions [Supplementary Figure 4E and F, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Heatmaps further revealed cell-type-specific marker expression, with S100A7 and IL36A enriched in differentiated keratinocytes, while CDH11 and CPZ were predominantly expressed in basal keratinocytes [Supplementary Figure 4G, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Additionally, the pseudotime-associated analysis identified the activation of proliferation-related pathways, including PI3K-Akt and Wnt signaling, during the proliferating keratinocyte stage. As differentiation progressed, immune responses were enhanced, leading to the suppression of tumor proliferation [Figure 3B].

To validate the specificity of PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes as a potential subtype associated with early-stage HPV-positive CESC, we utilized publicly available data from the ArrayExpress database (EMTAB11948, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress/studies/E-MTAB-11948). Raw data were downloaded, followed by cell clustering analysis to identify and confirm the expression of selected markers in HPV-positive early-stage cervical cancer samples. The results demonstrated that PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes were enriched in HPV-positive early-stage cervical cancer tissues, further supporting their potential role as a disease-specific keratinocyte subtype. Notably, this conclusion is consistent with findings validated using the publicly available EMTAB11948 dataset [Figure 3C]. Given the limited sample size of the scRNA-seq dataset, we utilized the CIBERSORTx deconvolution algorithm to infer cell-type-specific gene expression profiles and estimate their relative abundance across bulk RNA-seq samples from the TCGA-CESC dataset. This approach enabled large-scale quantification of keratinocyte subpopulations. Notably, PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes were more abundant in primary tumor samples compared to adjacent normal tissues, as analyzed using the two-sample t-test (P = 2.22e−16) [Figure 3D]. Clinical analysis demonstrated that the infiltration of PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes was higher in tumors associated with poor prognosis compared to those with favorable outcomes according to the log-rank test (P = 0.049) [Figure 3E]. Furthermore, mIHC confirmed the co-localization of S100A7 and PI3 proteins within PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes analyzed by non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P <0.05), providing direct evidence of their enrichment in tumor tissues [Figure 3F].

Tumor-associated macrophages regulated the shaping of the TME in CESC

To investigate the interactions shaping the TME, we used receptor–ligand data from the CellPhoneDB database to analyze intercellular communication in scRNA-seq data. Macrophages were significantly enriched in the tumor tissue compared to the adjacent non-cancerous tissue [Figure 4A]. Heatmap analysis of cell–cell communication revealed that immune cell interactions were notably reduced in tumor tissues, with macrophages being the only immune cell type among the 13 most interactive cell types, highlighting their pivotal role in the TME [Figure 4B]. Receptor–ligand analysis identified strong CD74-mediated interactions, including CD74-MIF (migration inhibitory factor), CD74-COPA, and CD74-APP, which underscore the role of macrophages in modulating the TME via CD74-related pathways [Figure 4C]. Further analysis using CellChat revealed high expression of CD74 in macrophages, confirming its significant role in the MIF signaling pathway [Figure 4D].

To investigate the role of macrophage–keratinocyte interactions in the TME and their potential contribution to HPV-related cancer progression, we analyzed cell–cell communication patterns in HPV-positive cancers and adjacent non-cancerous tissues. The analysis of cell–cell interactions in HPV-related cancers and adjacent non-cancerous tissues revealed significant differences in the interactions between macrophages and proliferating keratinocytes. The heatmap demonstrated that in the cancer microenvironment, macrophages exhibited the strongest interaction with proliferating keratinocytes, representing the highest interaction intensity among all analyzed cell pairs [Supplementary Figure 5A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. Conversely, in adjacent non-cancerous tissues, the interaction between macrophages and proliferating keratinocytes was notably reduced, indicating a tumor-specific enhancement of these interactions [Supplementary Figure 5B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. The ligand–receptor network analysis identified TNF as a central mediator of macrophage–keratinocyte interactions in cancer tissues, along with key signaling molecules, such as C-C motif chemokine ligand-2 (CCL2), which recruits macrophages via C-C chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2), and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CXCL8) and interleukin 10 (IL-10), which contribute to immune modulation and inflammation [Supplementary Figure 5C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. In contrast, the network in adjacent non-cancerous tissues showed a reduced prominence of these interactions, with EGFR emerging as a central node, reflecting a shift in the interaction landscape [Supplementary Figure 5D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C595]. These findings highlight the dynamic, context-dependent nature of macrophage–keratinocyte interactions, emphasizing their potential role in shaping the TME in HPV-related cancers.

To explore interactions between proliferating keratinocytes and macrophages, we identified PI3+S100A7+ proliferating keratinocytes as predominantly infiltrating tumor tissues. CIBERSORTx analysis using the log-rank test revealed that patients with high infiltration of both PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes and macrophages (PI3+S100A7+High_CD163+High_CD68) had the poorest overall survival (OS) (P = 0.04), indicating a potential collaborative role of these cell types in promoting tumor progression [Figure 4E]. mIHC analysis further demonstrated the spatial proximity between PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes and CD163+macrophages within tumor tissues, suggesting physical interactions and potential crosstalk between these cell types [Figure 4F]. Pathway analysis of these interactions identified cytokine receptor interactions, NF-κB signaling, and TNF signaling pathways as highly active, which are known to promote differentiation and structural remodeling of the TME [Figure 4G]. These findings suggest a stromal cell-mediated network linking macrophages with PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes, collectively contributing to TME deterioration and immune evasion.

Characterization of fibroblasts and keratinocytes using scRNA-seq data

Fibroblasts emerged as a key cell population in the TME of CESC, showing variations in abundance and heterogeneity between the tumor and adjacent non-cancerous tissues. Fibroblasts were more abundant in adjacent non-cancerous tissues compared to tumor tissues [Figure 5A], suggesting structural and compositional changes in the TME during tumor progression. To explore fibroblast heterogeneity, we re-clustered all fibroblasts and assessed their degree of malignancy based on established CAF markers, including α-SMA/ACTA2 and POSTN. Most marker-expressing fibroblasts were enriched in tumor tissues, suggesting a transformation toward CAF phenotypes during early carcinogenesis [Figure 5B]. Further subtyping revealed four distinct fibroblast subtypes [Figure 5C]. Among these, the C1 subtype, predominating in tumor tissues, was enriched with CAFs, while the C3 subtype, found primarily in non-cancerous tissues, consisted of undifferentiated fibroblasts. GSEA of CAFs identified the pathway wp_cells_and_molecules_involved_in_local_acute_inflammatory_response as highly activated [Figure 5D]. This activation highlights the role of acute and chronic inflammation in promoting cervical cancer progression. Persistent infection and inflammation can lead to cytotoxic effects, DNA damage, and eventual malignant transformation of normal cells, underscoring the contribution of fibroblast-mediated inflammation to the TME and tumor evolution.

The comparison of cell type compositions between HPV16- and HPV66-infected tumor tissues revealed distinct differences in the TME. HPV16-infected tissues showed a higher abundance of proliferating keratinocytes and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, while HPV66-infected tissues were enriched in fibroblasts and squamous epithelial cells, reflecting distinct tumor-promoting mechanisms [Figure 6A].

In HPV16-infected tissues, proliferating keratinocytes represented the most abundant cell type, characterized by the upregulation of KLK5, a well-established marker of HPV16-positive keratinocytes, and WFDC2, a gene implicated in tumor invasion and metastasis [Figure 6B]. Pathway enrichment analysis identified significant involvement of the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and HPV infection pathway, indicating the influence of inflammatory and immune mechanisms on keratinocyte behavior and HPV-associated cancer progression [Figure 6C]. Cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, the second most abundant cell type in HPV16-infected tissues, demonstrated high expression of cytotoxicity-associated genes, including GZMB, GZMA, and PRF1, indicating enhanced cytotoxic activity [Figure 6D]. Upregulation of CTLA4, a key immune checkpoint molecule, further underscores the active immune regulatory environment in HPV16-associated tumors [Figure 6D].

In HPV66-infected tissues, fibroblasts were the most abundant cell type, exhibiting high expression of genes, such as CCN5, APOE, CFD, and PTGDS, suggesting active roles in ECM remodeling and lipid metabolism [Figure 6E]. Squamous epithelial cells, the second most abundant cell type, showed high expression of KRT13, CXCL14, and CALML3, indicating epithelial differentiation, immune modulation, and tumor progression [Figure 6F].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate distinct molecular and cellular differences between HPV16- and HPV66-associated tumors, emphasizing the unique tumor-promoting mechanisms and microenvironmental influences driven by each HPV subtype.

Discussion

CESC is primarily driven by HPV infection and genetic susceptibility. Recent advances in scRNA-seq have enabled unprecedented resolution of cellular heterogeneity and TME dynamics in CESC, yet most studies are limited by focusing on single patients or malignant tissues alone, restricting the evaluation of inter-patient variability and the broader interplay between cell types, particularly keratinocytes and immune cells under HPV infection.

In this study, we utilized gene marker-based cell typing rather than platform barcoding, which improved the biological relevance and functional annotation of key cell populations such as PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes and CD163+ macrophages. This strategy allowed us to better capture the functional and phenotypic diversity in the CESC TME and provided deeper insights into the interactions underlying HPV-driven oncogenesis.

Keratinocytes are a primary target of HPV infection and play a central role in the TME of CESC. These cells not only serve as the site of initial viral entry but also influence other components of the TME through the secretion of inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic factors.[39] Studies have shown that HPV specifically infects basal cells of the stratified squamous epithelium, disrupting the normal differentiation program and driving precancerous lesions.[40] The lifecycle of HPV is closely tied to keratinocyte differentiation, with viral genome replication and late-stage gene expression dependent on this process.[41,42] HPV delays keratinocyte differentiation while promoting abnormal proliferation, decoupling these two processes to ensure viral replication and the assembly of virions as epithelial cells progress toward shedding.[43] Our scRNA-seq analysis supports these findings, identifying keratinocytes as one of the most enriched cell types in CESC tissues. Within the keratinocyte population, we observed proliferating keratinocytes were predominantly enriched in tumor tissues, whereas basal and suprabasal keratinocytes were more prevalent in adjacent non-cancerous tissues. This shift reflects a disruption in the normal differentiation program and an increase in unregulated proliferation within the TME, consistent with previous studies linking keratinocyte dysregulation to cancer development.[41,43] Monocle 2 pseudotime analysis further revealed that basal keratinocytes, the initial targets of HPV infection, progress through differentiation into two fates: Differentiating keratinocytes and suprabasal keratinocytes.[44] However, proliferating keratinocytes disrupt this normal trajectory, exhibiting abnormal interactions with both basal and suprabasal keratinocytes. This disruption is accompanied by the activation of PI3K-Akt and Wnt signaling pathways, along with upregulation of cell proliferation and DNA repair pathways, driving tumorigenesis and supporting HPV replication.

S100A7 and PI3 were notably upregulated in proliferating keratinocytes, highlighting their significant association with tumorigenesis. S100A7, also known as Psoriasin, is a member of the S100 family of calcium-binding proteins that influence critical processes in tumor progression, including cell migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and EMT.[45,46,47,48,49] Similarly, PI3, an elastase-specific inhibitor and antimicrobial peptide, has been linked to poor prognosis in multiple cancers, such as ovarian cancers, breast cancers, and esophageal squamous cell carcinomas (OSCC), suggesting a shared mechanism that connects inflammation and carcinogenesis.[50,51,52] In our study, we identified PI3 and S100A7 as significantly overexpressed in HPV-positive CESC samples compared to non-tumor controls, as validated by TCGA data. Through mIHC, we further confirmed the co-localization of S100A7 and PI3 within keratinocytes, defining a distinct subpopulation of PI3+S100A7+ cells. This co-expression suggests a synergistic role for these proteins in tumor progression, potentially through co-regulated pathways that enhance the malignant phenotype. Similar findings have been reported in other cancers, where S100A7 promotes migration, invasion, and EMT via RAGE-mediated signaling in breast cancer, osteosarcoma, and OSCC.[47,48,49] Concurrently, PI3 has been shown to enhance proliferation and resistance to genotoxic stress in ovarian and breast cancers.[52,53,54] These parallel mechanisms underscore the broader relevance of S100A7 and PI3 in cancer biology, supporting their roles in facilitating tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance.

Expression analysis revealed that PI3 and S100A7 exhibit lower levels in early-stage tumors but are markedly elevated in advanced stages. This suggests that PI3 and S100A7 might play a dual role: supporting early-stage transformation and later promoting aggressive tumor behaviors. Interestingly, the involvement of PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes is not limited to CESC. Similar patterns of upregulation have been observed in other HPV-associated malignancies, such as OSCC.[55] Survival analysis in our study demonstrated that high expression levels of PI3 and S100A7 are significantly associated with poor prognosis in HPV-related cervical cancers. This association underscores their potential as prognostic biomarkers, indicating more aggressive disease and adverse clinical outcomes. Additionally, clinical analysis revealed that tumors with poor prognosis exhibited higher infiltration of PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes compared to those with favorable outcomes. This increased infiltration may contribute to a more supportive TME, promoting tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion.

Macrophages are pivotal immune modulators within the TME of CESC, where they contribute to tumor growth, angiogenesis, and immune suppression.[56,57] In our study, macrophages were enriched in tumor tissues and exhibit strong crosstalk with keratinocytes, mediated by TNF, CCL2, CXCL8, and IL10. TNF and CCL2-CCR2 signaling pathways are known to recruit tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and promote an immunosuppressive environment, facilitating tumor progression and metastasis.[58,59,60] Notably, immune cell profiling by CIBERSORTx revealed that patients with high infiltration of both PI3+S100A7+proliferating keratinocytes and CD163+macrophages exhibited the shortest overall survival, highlighting the adverse clinical impact of this cellular combination. mIHC further confirmed the close spatial proximity of PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes and CD163+macrophages within tumor tissues, suggesting that their interaction may be key to the formation of a pro-tumorigenic niche.

The close proximity of PI3+S100A7+keratinocytes to CD163+ macrophages, as revealed by our findings, suggests a synergistic interaction that further amplifies pro-tumorigenic signaling pathways. S100A7 and PI3 may collaborate to enhance the inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties of the TME, thereby facilitating immune escape and tumor advancement. This interplay underscores the potential of targeting these interactions as a therapeutic strategy. Disrupting the crosstalk between PI3+S100A7+ keratinocytes and macrophages, either by inhibiting key cytokine pathways or reprogramming macrophage polarization, could restore immune surveillance and improve treatment outcomes in HPV-related cervical cancer.

Moreover, the MIF–CD74 interaction plays a crucial role in this context. CD74, the high-affinity receptor for macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), was found to be highly expressed in macrophages within the TME. Engagement of MIF with CD74 activates downstream signaling cascades, including ERK and PI3K/AKT, which enhance tumor cell survival, angiogenesis, and immune evasion.[61] This signaling axis not only supports the immunosuppressive function of macrophages but also contributes to the maintenance of an inflammatory niche that promotes tumor progression.[62]

Our scRNA-seq data revealed fibroblasts as the most abundant cell type, with a markedly lower proportion in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-cancerous tissues. This suggests a dynamic alteration of the stromal landscape during tumor progression. Fibroblasts in the TME were enriched in angiogenesis and KRAS signaling pathways, underscoring their role in promoting tumor vascularization and growth.[63] Subsequent comparison of fibroblast populations between tumor and non-tumor groups revealed substantial heterogeneity, with most fibroblast subpopulations being unique to each group. This indicates the presence of tumor-specific fibroblasts that may possess distinct functional properties supporting malignancy. Re-clustering of all fibroblasts and scoring based on CAF markers α-SMA/ACTA2 and POSTN demonstrated that CAFs were predominantly located in tumor tissues. Further subtyping identified four distinct fibroblast subtypes, with the C1 group being enriched with CAFs and primarily present in tumor tissues, while the C3 group consisted of undifferentiated fibroblasts predominantly found in adjacent non-cancerous tissues. GSEA of CAFs identified the activation of pathways involved in local acute inflammatory responses, underscoring the role of CAFs in orchestrating inflammation within the TME.

Taken together, our study revealed the presence of specific cell subtypes in early-stage cervical cancer, suggesting a complex interaction between tumor cells, stromal cells, and immune infiltration in the TME. We hypothesized that this mechanism could contribute to blocking the invasion of extracellular pathogens into HPV-infected tissues. In conclusion, this study provides further evidence for the crucial role of keratinocytes and HPV infection in the early malignant transformation of cervical cancer and reveals its significance for clinical translation and disease treatment.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was small, involving only four patients, three of whom were HPV-positive and one HPV-negative. This limited cohort restricts the generalizability of our findings and reduces the statistical power needed to detect rare cell populations or subtle transcriptional differences. Particularly, the small number of HPV-negative samples limits our ability to identify distinct molecular or cellular features that differentiate HPV-positive and HPV-negative cases. Second, although our study is the first to elucidate the pro-tumorigenic roles of S100A7 and PI3 in keratinocyte proliferation and their interaction with macrophages, it lacks animal models or functional assays to further validate these molecular mechanisms. Additionally, the study is limited by the lack of extensive clinical data, which constrains the ability to correlate our molecular findings with broader clinical outcomes and patient characteristics. Third, technical biases inherent to scRNA-seq must be considered, as tissue dissociation and cell capture methods may selectively impact certain cell populations. Finally, the reliance on scRNA-seq alone limits the ability to capture the full spatial organization and functional diversity of the TME. In the future, spatial transcriptomics or multi-omics approaches can be conducted to offer complementary insights, enabling a more integrated analysis of cell–cell interactions and signaling networks within tumor tissues.
