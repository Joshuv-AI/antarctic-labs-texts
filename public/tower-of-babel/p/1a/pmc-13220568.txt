A gene signature derived from leukemia associated macrophages provides a compelling risk stratification for human AML patients
Authors: Deedra Nicolet, Ting Zhang, Christopher J. Walker, Justice Ameyi, Stephanie F. Mori, Xuexiu Fang, Litao Zhang, Jorge Cortes, Catherine C. Hedrick, John K. Cowell, Ann-Kathrin Eisfeld, Tianxiang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In a mouse model of FGFR1-driven leukemia, we demonstrated a role for circulating non-conventional monocyte-derived macrophages in the peripheral blood, which suppressed T-cell function and promoted leukemogenesis. A single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) analysis of these leukemia-associated macrophages (LAMs) identified LAM-specific dysregulation of gene expression associated with leukemogenesis. Based on the top markers identified in these LAMs, we generated a LAM score based on the expression levels of a 32 gene signature. This scoring system was then applied to the transcriptomic data from a cohort of 838 newly diagnosed patients treated on Alliance/CALGB protocols, who were similarly treated with intensive cytarabine/daunorubicin-based chemotherapy on the CALGB/Alliance for the Clinical Trials in Oncology protocol. Patients were subclassified as those with high and low LAM scores. Patients with a high LAM score had shorter overall survival, disease-free survival and event-free survival compared to those with a low LAM score. We also noted a strong association of FLT3-ITD, RUNX1 and TP53 mutations with high LAM scores. Applying the LAM score to the current European Leukemia Network risk group criteria, independent prognostic implications and a refined prognostic significance of each subgroup were provided, indicating the value of including immune microenvironment data into AML risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40364-026-00944-w.

To the Editor,

The Stem Cell leukemia/Lymphoma syndrome (SCLL) presents in heterogeneous forms, including myeloproliferative neoplasm, T- or B-lineage lymphoblastic leukemia/lymphoma, and even mixed phenotype acute leukemia but in most cases progresses to acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1]. SCLL is unique within the spectrum of cytogenetic sub-groups of myeloid neoplasms, in that 100% of patients present with chromosome rearrangements that result in constitutive activation of the FGFR1 kinase [2]. We have developed representative mouse models using retroviral transduction of hematopoietic stem cells [3, 4] and, in a recent study of these models using single cell proteomic and transcriptomic analysis of hematopoietic cells involving in the circulatory microenvironment, we demonstrated a significant increase in circulating macrophages in the peripheral blood [5], which were shown to be derived from non-conventional monocytes. This transition into leukemia associated macrophages (LAMs) was marked by a pronounced transcriptional reprogramming. To determine the clinical significance of these LAMs, correlative analysis within 838 newly diagnosed patients treated on Alliance/ Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) protocols was performed [6, 7]. The top 50 genes that were most highly upregulated in murine LAMs were converted to human homologues using the nichenetr package and manual queries on NCBI/gene. For the LAM genes, one of the 50 (AW112010) was excluded as a suitable human homologue was not identified. To construct a robust combined prognostic risk score from the remaining 49 genes, a machine learning technique was used to eliminate weak predictors (Additional File 1). In brief, we implemented a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-penalized Cox proportional hazards model using the cv.glmnet function from the glmnet R package [8, 9]. To protect the model against overfitting and ensure the stability of gene selection, we executed 1,000 iterations of leave-out-10% (10-fold) cross-validation. During this iterative process, the LASSO penalty shrank the coefficient values (CV) of weaker, redundant, or non-predictive variables to exactly zero. Genes were rigorously filtered based on their selection frequency across these simulations. 17 of the 49 genes showed a median CV = 0 in at least 900 of 1000 iterations demonstrating a lack of consistent prognostic power and were subsequently excluded. For the other 32 genes (Additional File 2), a weighted average was calculated using the mean CV of 1000 iterations for the weight of each gene, to derive an RNA expression, LAM score. This score was calculated for each of the 838 AML patients who were similarly treated with intensive cytarabine/daunorubicin-based chemotherapy on CALGB/Alliance for the Clinical Trials in Oncology protocol using databases CALGB 8461(cytogenetic studies), CALGB 9665 (leukemia tissue bank) and CALGB 20,202 (molecular studies). All the clinical and molecular characteristics for those patients are provided in Additional File 3. Using the expression levels of the 32 genes, patients with a high LAM score were compared with those with a low LAM score (Fig. 1A).

When analyzing survival endpoints, we found that high LAM score patients had shorter overall survival (OS; 3-year rates, 23% vs. 64%, P < 0.001), disease-free survival (3-year rates, 23% vs. 52%, P < 0.001) and event-free survival (3-year rates, 14% vs. 47%, P < 0.001) compared to those with a low LAM score (Fig. 1A). We then leveraged the extensive clinical and molecular information available for the AML patient cohort to understand any correlation patterns. When comparing patients with high LAM scores to those with low scores, we noted a strong association of FLT3-ITD, RUNX1 and TP53 mutations with high LAM scores (Table 1 and Additional file 3), whereas core-binding-factor (CBF) aberrations, which represent a subtype of AML that has a relatively good prognosis compared to other forms of AML [10], associated with low LAM scores (Table 1 and Additional File 3). Consequently, high LAM score patients belonged more often to the adverse genetic risk group of the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 classification [11, 12]. However, the prognostic association of the LAM score cannot be fully explained by its association with established prognostic features. In fact, applying the LAM score to the current ELN risk group, provided independent prognostic implications and refined the prognostic significance of each subgroup (Fig. 1B). As such, 23% of favorable risk patients were in the high LAM score group and had significantly shorter survival than the favorable risk patients in the low LAM score group (OS; 3-year rates, 49% vs. 72%, P = 0.002; Fig. 1B). This observation has important clinical and therapeutic significance, as favorable risk patients would not routinely be considered for more intensive consolidation, such as allogeneic stem cell transplantation in first complete remission.

In summary, correlative analysis in newly diagnosed AML shows that patients expressing high levels of our macrophage gene signature are associated with poor prognosis, suggesting that the presence of high levels of leukemia-induced macrophages is associated with poor clinical outcome, further confirming the role of these cells in immune suppression and leukemia promotion. In addition, this work highlights the value of data derived from representative mouse models of leukemia in providing a translational relevance to a broad range of leukemia subtypes.
