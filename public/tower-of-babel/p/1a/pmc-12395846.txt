A systematic analysis assessing epidemiological characteristics and surgical outcomes of posterolateral tibial plateau fractures
Authors: Qinglin Qiu, Zeyu Zhang, Zhongpei Lin, Xiaoyue Wen, Shouwen Su, Bo He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyze the Schatzker classification and related epidemiological situation of posterolateral tibial plateau fractures (PTPFs), and analyze the curative effect of different surgical approaches and internal fixation methods according to the subtype of PTPFs, to provide a strategic basis for clinicians to treat posterolateral plateau fractures.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases to identify PTPFs between 2000 and 2024. The epidemiological data related to PTPFs, including its injury reason, Schatzker and OTA/AO classification, combined injury, complication, fixation methods, function evaluation methods, and other data, were manually screened and retrieved for comparative analysis. The data were analyzed by SPSS 27.0, and the quality of evidence was evaluated according to the MINORS (Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies) evaluation tool.ResultsAmong PTPFs, the common causes of injury were traffic accidents and falls (71.2%, 238/334 vs 23.9%, 80/334), and the most common complications were ligament (ACL, etc.) and meniscus injury (33.6%, 110/327 vs 28.7%, 94/327). Schatzker type II accounted for 50.5–73.7% and type III accounted for 22.8–43.9%. More than half of PTPF fixation can be completed by placing an L-shaped rafting plate (+ screws) through an anterolateral approach. In some situations, fibula neck osteotomies were performed and all healed. The overall excellent and good rate of all internal fixation systems was 0.82 [0.69, 0.95], the excellent and good rate of plates was 0.9 [0.82, 0.98], and the excellent and good rate of absorbable screws was 0.85 [0.75, 0.96]. HSS score was most widely used in postoperative functional evaluation, and reduction loss (22.2%) was the most common postoperative complication.ConclusionSchatzker type II is the most common type in PTPFs. More than half of PTPF fixation can be completed by placing an L-shaped rafting plate (+ screws) through an extended anterolateral approach, especially for PTPFs involving Zone I–II.Level of evidenceLevel I.

Background

Tibial plateau fracture is one of the most common intra-articular fractures around the knee joint, accounting for about 1%−2% of the total body fractures. Epidemiological studies have shown that there are significant differences in the incidence rate of such fractures in different regions, which is closely related to the aging of the population, the incidence of traffic accidents, and sports injury patterns. In developed countries, high-energy injuries (such as car accidents and falls from heights) are the main cause of tibial plateau fractures, while in developing countries, low-energy injuries (such as falls) represent a higher proportion in elderly osteoporosis patients. With the increase in the number of motor vehicles and the participation rate of sports activities, the incidence rate of such fractures is on the rise worldwide [1–3]. Especially among the young and middle-aged labor force, its disability rate and socio-economic burden cannot be ignored.

Posterior tibial plateau fractures (PTPFs) are one of the most common types of tibial plateau fractures, which are characterized by small fracture blocks, and most of the fracture lines are found in the coronal position [5–10]. The posterolateral structure of the tibial plateau is complicated, where there is not only the obstruction of the fibula head and lateral collateral ligament, but also important structures such as the popliteal blood vessels and common peroneal nerves nearby. Improper operation may lead to vascular and nerve damage [11]. In recent years, PTPFs have received widespread attention from the field of orthopedics due to their unique anatomical characteristics and treatment difficulties. The discovery of various surgical approaches and the emergence of internal fixation devices have not only reduced operative time, but also improved the surgical outcomes of PTPFs. However, for different subtypes of PTPFs, the choice of surgical approaches and internal fixation methods varies greatly. At present, there are few studies on the epidemiological data of PTPFs, especially the correlation between PTPF subtypes and surgical approach selection and efficacy.

The position of the knee and the force direction and magnitude applied determined the morphology and degree of fracture subtypes. At present, tibial plateau fractures are commonly divided into six types according to the Schatzker classification. Type I to III refer to lateral tibial plateau fractures, while type IV to VI refer to high-energy fractures involving the medial plateau [1–3]. The injury mechanism of PTPFs was mostly caused by violence of knee flexion and valgus [8]. Therefore, PTPFs can only be Schatzker I to III types, which are simple and lower-energy fractures, exhibit higher functional scores [1].

To better understand the selection of surgical approaches and internal fixation methods for PTPFs, this paper intends to search the literature on the surgical treatment of PTPFs from 2000 to 2024, analyze the corresponding epidemiological situation, as well as compare and analyze the efficacy of different surgical approaches and internal fixation methods, which provides a strategic basis for clinicians to treat PTPFs.

Materials and methods

Source of materials

English literature on PTPFs was searched from PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases, in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guideline [12]. Search strategy: (tibial plateau fracture[MeSH Terms]) or (posterolateral tibial plateau fracture [MeSH Terms]) Limits Activated: Human, Male, Female, English, Publication Date from 2000/01/01 to 2024/12/31. At the same time, the (Tibial plateau [Title]) AND (posterior [Title]) retrieval strategy was used for secondary screening to avoid omissions. Synchronized research projects on the platforms of Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/) and United States Clinical Trial Registry (https://clinicaltrials.gov/) were searched.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Literature inclusion criteria: 1. Published English literature on clinical studies of PTPFs; 2. Cases involving only posterolateral tibial plateau; 3. Tibial plateau was classified into Schatzker or OTA/AO classification types I–III. 4. Individual information retrieved from the literature included: approach, fixation, surgery position, sex, affected side, injury reason, Schatzker, OTA/AO classification, combined injury, complication, fixator, function evaluation, bone graft, and excellent and good rates. Exclusion criteria: 1. not following the inclusion criteria; 2. non-clinical research; 3. case reports; 4. literature and cases inconsistent with the research purpose.

Study identification

Two evaluators searched the data according to the unified criteria and independently read the titles and abstracts of the obtained literature. After excluding the studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria, the full text of the literature that met the inclusion criteria was read to determine whether the inclusion criteria were truly met. The two researchers (QQ & ZZ) cross-checked the results of the included trials. Ambiguous studies, which were difficult to determine whether they should be included, will be decided by discussion or by a third researcher (BH).

Quality assessment

Based on the non-randomized research methodology index (MINOR) scoring tool for quality assessment, eight items are included, each of which is scored on a 3-point scale (0–2). The grading of quality is as follows: research without a control group. High quality: ≥ 13 points (≥ 75%), medium quality: 8–12 points (50%−74%), low quality: < 8 points (< 50%). Research with a control group (all 12 items, out of 24 points). High quality: ≥ 18 points (≥ 75% out of 24 points), medium quality: 12–17 points (50%–74%), low quality: < 12 points (< 50%).

Result retrieval

According to the pre-designed data retrieval form, one researcher (QQ) retrieved and entered data, and the other (ZZ) checked it. Finally, the data were summarized by BH, SS, and XW. The primary outcome and endpoint were used for the postoperative assessment of knee joint function. The secondary outcome refers to the postoperative complication.

Postoperative functional evaluation criteria were: Hss and Kss scores were excellent (≥ 85 points), good (70–84 points), fair (60–69 points) and poor (< 60 points). Rasmussen radiological scores were excellent (18 points), good (12–17 points), fair (6–11 points), and poor (< 6 points). Rasmussen functional scores were excellent (≥ 27 points), good (20–26 points), fair (10–19 points), and poor (6–9 points).

Statistical methods

For the primary and secondary outcomes and endpoints, the rates of excellence/good or efficiency were compared among the groups. For the secondary observation indicators, the percentage was calculated. The data were analyzed by STATA ver.18.0 statistical software. The I2 rangewas divided into low heterogeneity (<50%), moderate heterogeneity (50%~75%), and high heterogeneity (>75%). I2 indicates insufficient number of studies.

The excellent and good rate of cement filling of bone defect after reduction reached 100% (26/26). Other commonly used filling methods included autograft, allograft, and synthetic products, accounting for 41.2%, 52.9%, and 29.4%, respectively. Allogenetic bone was the most commonly used one.

In terms of plate fixation, compared with fibular osteotomy, the excellent and good rate was higher in surgery without fibular osteotomy (1/5, 20.00% VS 180/186, 96.77%) (p < 0.05). There were two methods of fibular osteotomy: fibula head osteotomy (42.9%) and fibula neck osteotomy (57.1%). All cases with osteotomy were healed. The fixation methods for osteotomy included long screws and tension bands. All fibula neck osteotomies were fixed by long screws, 66.6% (2/3) of fibula head osteotomies were fixed by tension bands, and 33.3% of fibula head osteotomies were fixed by long screws (p > 0.05).

Common postoperative complications included infection (9.3%), traumatic OA (5.5%), delayed union (5.5%), numbness (9.3%), tissue necrosis (1.8%), reduction loss (22.2%), fixation failure (5.5%), venous thrombosis (14.8%), common peroneal nerve injury (1.8%), incision dehiscence (5.5%), and others (18.5%), including flexion limitation, claudication, knee pain, and valgus deformity.

Discussion

PTPFs, either isolated or combined with other column fractures, are more common than previously reported, with the widespread use of computed tomography (CT) for identification. Yang et al. studied the CT images of 525 PTPF patients and found that the incidence of an independent fragment of the posterior column in tibial plateau fractures was 28.8% (151/525) [4]. At the same time, the incidence of independent PTPFs accounted for about 7% (split fractures) to 15% (posterolateral cortex, 36/242) of all tibial plateau fractures [5–7]. The injury mechanism was mostly caused by the violence of knee flexion and valgus [8]. Myint reported a case that suffered common peroneal nerve palsy due to the posterolateral displacement of a fractured lateral tibial plateau in 1980 [9]. Waldrop et al. found that these fractures occurred in a younger population and generated significant disability in activities requiring a stable knee in flexion, and recommended open reduction and bone grafting in acute cases to eliminate instability in flexion [10].

Selection of surgical approaches for PTPFs

In general, the commonly used surgical approaches for PTPFs include anterolateral, direct lateral, posterolateral, posteromedial, and combined approaches [59]. When choosing the surgical approaches, the most important thing to consider is the fracture pattern, which allows direct visualization of the posterolateral fragment and the application of the fixator, besides the soft tissue condition around the fracture.

The position of the knee and the force direction and magnitude applied determined the morphology and degree of fractures [8]. Kerschbaum et al. collected the imaging data of PTPF patients and found through the Schatzker classification that among 140 cases, type I, II, III, IV, V, and VI accounted for 19.1%, 21.6%, 9.7%, 11.5%, 5.8%, and 32.4%, respectively [1]. The secondary data processing of the above cases showed that in lateral tibial plateau fractures, type I, II, and III accounted for 37.9%, 42.8%, and 19.3%, respectively. Currently, there has been no specific epidemiology data on PTPFs. This study found that the proportion of type II was 50.5–73.7%, and that of type III was 22.8–43.9%. For the isolated PTPFs, Schatzker type I, II, and III accounted for about 5.6%, 50.5%, and 43.9%, respectively. That is to say, the main types of PTPFs were Schatzker type II and type III.

We emphasized the concept of three pillars and four zones here. According to CT examination, Luo et al. divided the tibial plateau into three columns, namely anteromedial column, anterolateral column, and posterior column (BOE area in Fig. 7A) [60]. Accordingly, tibial plateau fractures were divided into anteromedial column fractures, anterolateral column fractures, and posterior column fractures, of which posterolateral column fractures refer to the BOD area in Fig. 7A. The posterolateral tibial plateau described by Sim et al. is different from that described by Luo. The posterolateral tibial plateau described was the AOD area in Fig. 7A, and it was divided into three areas, namely Zone 1 (BOA area), Zone 2 (COB area) and Zone 3 (DOC area) (Fig. 7). Zone 1 used an extended anterolateral approach, Zone 2 used an anterolateral fibula osteotomy approach, and Zone 3 used a posteromedial approach [45]. This study found that more than half of PTPFs can be treated with the anterolateral approach (54.5%), including traditional anterolateral approach, supra-fibular-head approach, extended anterolateral approach, transfibular approach, and lateral femoral epicondyle osteotomy (Fig. 7C). The anterolateral approach usually requires fenestration or cleavage of the anterolateral cortex, or fibula osteotomy to expose and reduce the posterolateral plateau, and finally fixation by placing an inverted L-shaped anterolateral plate. The traditional anterolateral approach, extended anterolateral approach, and supra-fibular-head approach are essentially the same. Moreover, there is no difference in the approach to expose the posterolateral tibial plateau besides the difference in length and shape of the skin incision. The transfibular approach and lateral femoral epicondyle osteotomy are the osteotomy approaches in which part of the fibula or lateral femoral condyle is turned upward and downward, respectively, which directly shows the lateral and posterolateral tibial plateaus (Fig. 7B, C) [61]. The transfibular approach was first described by Lobenhoffer in 1997, in which the posterolateral angle of the tibia could be fully exposed [62]. Solomon reported that all fractures and osteotomies were healed after the transfibular approach [24]. At that time, the surgical design overcame the difficult problem of exposing the front from the rear, but it is still controversial. Many structures need to be peeled off, resulting in larger surgical trauma, and it also brings about iatrogenic injury [30].

If the fracture accumulates the COD area (Zone 3), a direct posterolateral approach (Frosch/Carlson approach) is often needed for reduction and support. Frosch introduced a new posterolateral approach without fibula osteotomy for the treatment of tibial plateau fractures in 2010, which can expose posterolateral and lateral fracture fragments by one incision [35, 52]. At first, the Frosch approach is a longitudinal straight incision, and later it is modified into a long and thin S-shaped incision (Fig. 2). Then, Carlson introduced a posterolateral approach as one of the combined approaches for posterior bicondylar fractures in 2005. This approach is later modified and applied to isolated PTPFs (Fig. 3) [63]. The structure shown by the Carlson approach is similar to the posterolateral window of the Frosch approach, except that the former exposes more subplateau structures than the latter (COB area, Figs. 3 and 5), which is more conducive to the placement of the plate.

In this study, the posterolateral approach accounted for 29.5%, including (modified) Frosch approach, (modified) Carlson approach, posterolateral transfibular approach, and general posterolateral approach. Except for the (modified) Frosch approach, other approaches, whether the incision is straight or inverted L, are essentially the Carlson approaches.

Selection of internal fixation methods for PTPFs

The most common postoperative complication was reduction loss (22.2%) after PTPF fixation. The results demonstrated that plate fixation was better than screw fixation, and cancellous screw fixation was better than absorbable screw fixation. While the literature did not strictly combine the accurate location of PTPFs with Schatzker classification to select the corresponding fixation method, so the conclusion that “ plate fixation is more effective than screw fixation” cannot be obtained, especially unable to draw a conclusion of a specific fracture type in a particular location. Nevertheless, clinical doctors generally believe that plate fixation is the first choice for PTPFs, and screws or bone cement can be considered for localized contained depression Schatzker type III fractures. This requires further clinical controlled studies to confirm.

For those involving Zones 1–2 (AOC area of Fig. 7A) without obvious displacement splitting Schatzker type I–II fractures, the traditional anterolateral plate could be dealt with. If the fracture involves the back of Zone 2, no screw or only one screw of the plate could hold the posterolateral fracture [16]. Sassoon et al. found that the distance between the last row of nails (the fourth screw from the front to the back in Fig. 2F) and the posterior edge of the tibial plateau was 16 mm, accounting for 42% of the anteroposterior diameter of the entire lateral plateau, and that the mechanical strength of a single transverse screw fixation was relatively limited [64]. Hu et al. found that the incidence of articular surface collapse was 29.167% with one screw fixation. In case of this situation, it is recommended to use He’s type 1 fixation, that is, the L-shaped rafting plate and rafting screw. Different from type I and II fractures, Schatzker type III fractures include contained pure depression and non-contained depression. For Schatzker type III fracture with contained pure depression of Zones 1–2, type 2 fixation can also be used. In this way, the single screw is implanted obliquely from the anteroinferior medial to the posterosuperior lateral side.

For those involving Zone 3, especially for fractures with splitting displacement, the fracture should be reduced and fixed with a posterolateral buttress plate. It was concluded that the posterolateral buttress plate was biomechanically the strongest fixation method for the posterolateral shearing tibial plateau fracture [65]. Therefore, for Schatzker type I and II isolated PTPFs involving Zone 3 (COD area of Fig. 7A), it is recommended to use He’s type 3 fixation through the posterolateral approach [66]. If the fracture type is non-contained depression Schatzker type III, type 5 fixation is recommended. If the fracture type is contained depression Schatzker type III, such a fracture is a bit difficult to deal with. Clinically, arthroscopic bone cement (He’s type 8 fixation) or rafting/magic screw (He’s type 7 fixation) can be considered, or type 5 fixation can be used after posterior wall fenestration. Here, the excellent and good rate of cement filling of the bone defect can be found after reduction reaches 100%.

For those involving Zones 1–3 (AOD area of Fig. 7A), the splitting displacement is obvious in Zone 3 (Schatzker type I–II); He’s type 4 fixation can be adopted. Rarely, for complex non-contained depression Schatzker type III fractures with Zones 1–3 (AOD area of Fig. 7A) [56], He’s type 6 fixation can be used.

Furthermore, the most combined injuries were ligament (ACL, etc.) and meniscus injury among PTPFs. Therefore, some scholars believe that for PTPF patients, arthroscopic techniques should be actively used to assess the relevant soft tissue conditions when surgery is performed [19, 35, 37, 39].

Functional evaluation reliability

HSS score, Rasmussen scoring, and ROM are the most common for the postoperative functional evaluation. The HSS score was proposed by the Hospital for Special Surgery in 1976 and is a standardized tool for assessing knee joint function. Its core purpose is to reduce subjective judgment biases through quantitative indicators. The Rasmussen functional scoring system is of great significance in postoperative evaluation. It not only considers the subjective feelings of patients but also combines objective clinical examination, which can fully reflect the functional recovery of the knee joint. The disadvantage of this system is that if one item is scored poor, the patient’s final functional score is poor regardless of the other item scores. The radiological Rasmussen score includes factors such as posterior inclination angle, knee space width, varus angle, and plateau collapse, which are closely related to the efficacy of internal fixation of tibial plateau fractures. The combined detection of these parameters can be used as a prediction scheme for surgical efficacy. Therefore, all the postoperative functional scores in this study are peer-reviewed, and the results of the efficacy evaluation are reliable.

Summary and conclusion

Schatzker type II is the most common type in PTPFs. More than half of PTPF fixation can be completed by placing an L-shaped rafting plate (+ screws) through an anterolateral approach. For Schatzker type I and II isolated PTPFs in Zone 3, the buttress plate is often placed through the Carlson approach. It is difficult to treat the contained depression Schatzker type III fractures, while the non-contained depression Schatzker type III fractures can be fixed with a (horizontal) T-shaped plate.

This study conducted a meta-analysis on the epidemiology, surgical approaches, and internal fixation methods of PTPFs in the past 20 years. The included literature was of moderate quality; therefore, the results of this study have certain scientific and clinical significance.It is hoped that this study may bring up this issue into the perspective of more clinical doctors, trigger more high-quality RCTs, and clarify the accuracy of our recommendations regarding the selection of PTPF surgical approaches and internal fixation methods.

In conclusion, more than half of PTPF fixation can be completed by placing an L-shaped rafting plate (+ screws) through an extended anterolateral approach, especially for PTPFs involving Zone I–II.
