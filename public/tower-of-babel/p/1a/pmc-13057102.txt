Randomized controlled trial protocol of ASSIP and ACT for suicide prevention among Iranian suicide attempters
Authors: Hadiseh Yaghoobi, Azadeh Choobforoushzadeh, Marjan Fathi, Konrad Michel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide attempts are a major global health concern, and more effective interventions are needed. This study aims to compare the effectiveness of the Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program (ASSIP) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) in reducing suicidal ideation and mental pain among suicide attempters.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial (RCT) will recruit 60 participants (20 in each group) who have attempted suicide and whose contact information has been obtained from the Mental Health Unit of Eslamshahr Health Network. Participants will be randomly assigned to three groups: one receiving ASSIP in addition to treatment as usual (TAU), one receiving ACT alongside TAU, and a control group receiving TAU only. We will also compare the effectiveness of the two interventions.ConclusionsThis study will provide valuable insights into the comparative effectiveness of ASSIP and ACT interventions for suicide attempters. The results are expected to inform clinical practice and contribute to evidence-based suicide prevention strategies. Background

Suicide, a deep-rooted societal affliction, remains a paramount concern in mental health. This crisis endangers countless lives, ranking as the fourth leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds [1]. Suicide refers to a self-initiated act that leads to death, carried out by an individual with awareness that the behavior could be fatal. The degree of intent can vary and, in some cases, may be unclear or not explicitly expressed. On the other hand, Suicide attempt describes a self-injurious act performed with at least some intent to die, where the individual survives the event [2]. Recent statistics from the World Health Organization [3] indicate that more than 720,000 individuals worldwide die by suicide each year.

A complex interplay of factors contributes to suicidal behavior, with prior suicide attempts and suicidal ideation emerging as the most potent risk factors. The presence of psychiatric disorders, chronic physical illnesses, sociodemographic variables, and parental suicide further elevate the risk of suicide mortality [4].

Suicide attempts arise from a range of complex and interacting motivations rather than a single, uniform cause. These motives may include intrapersonal factors such as unbearable psychological pain, hopelessness, or the wish to escape, alongside interpersonal motives such as a desire for help or an effort to communicate distress. Empirical evidence suggests that intrapersonal motivations are often associated with stronger suicidal intent, whereas interpersonal motivations may reflect a need for connection and support rather than a definite wish to die [5].

Suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and death by suicide are distinct concepts; some individuals may contemplate suicide for extended periods without taking any action, while others may attempt suicide suddenly and without any prior history [6].

Suicidal ideation, which refers to thinking about, planning, or wishing for one’s own death, whether or not accompanied by suicidal intent or behavior [2], is recognized as a significant predictor of suicide. These thoughts exist on a continuum, ranging from a general desire for death without any specific method, plan, intent, or action, to active suicidal ideation, which involves a detailed plan and a firm intention to carry out these ideas. Suicidal ideation is closely linked to suicide attempts and death and serves as a substantial risk factor for future suicide attempts [7]. According to Mojahedi et al. [8] in 60% of cases, the transition from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts occurs within one year.

One element closely linked to suicidal ideation and behavior is ‘mental pain’ or ‘psychological pain’. Mental pain is a pervasive, complex, and intense subjective emotional experience focused on feelings of distress arising from negative self-appraisals [9]. According to Pompili [10], it is one of the most common triggers for suicide. In this process, suicide occurs as an attempt to stop the flow of mental agitation and create emotional distance due to unbearable suffering. Shneidman [11] first coined this term to describe intolerable psychological suffering. According to him, this pain arises from the failure to meet fundamental human needs such as the need for love and belonging, control, self-esteem, avoidance of shame, and a sense of security. As a result, individuals experience dissatisfaction and a desire to discontinue their lives, leading to various negative emotions such as sadness, guilt, shame, humiliation, as well as hopelessness and anger. Psychological pain compromises physical, psychological, and social functioning and interferes with daily activities. Pompili et al. [12] further demonstrated in a study that individuals who reported more severe symptoms of mental pain exhibited higher frequencies of suicidal ideation and even past suicide attempts.

Despite the global burden, the challenge of recurrent suicidal behavior is particularly pronounced in the Iranian clinical context [13, 14]. While standard care, primarily pharmacotherapy and supportive counseling (i.e., TAU), is widely utilized in centers [15] like the Eslamshahr Health and Treatment Network, the implementation of structured, evidence-based psychological interventions (such as those based on ASSIP and ACT protocols) remains limited. Suicide attempts and self-harm behaviors represent a leading cause of emergency psychiatric hospitalization and mortality risk in the region [15]. This clinical gap—the high rate of recurrent risk combined with the lack of access to brief, highly focused psychological treatments—underscores the urgent need for local efficacy trials. This study directly addresses this gap by comparing the incremental benefit of two leading brief protocols within a real-world Iranian clinical setting.

Given that suicide is a gradual process with often a significant time lapse between ideation and action, it appears that providing appropriate interventions during this period can prevent individuals from reaching the action phase. Specialized training programs have been developed for this purpose; one such program, rooted in Michel’s research [16], is designed for therapists working with individuals with a history of suicide attempts. The Attempted Suicide, Short Intervention Program (ASSIP) equips therapists with the necessary training to significantly reduce the risk of repeated suicide attempts. This model views suicide as an action and argues that suicide is experienced as an alternative to life-oriented goals. That is, when an individual faces a serious threat to their higher-level personal goals, such as being loved, respected, or successful, due to adverse life events. In personal crises, individuals experience deep pain, hopelessness, shame, failure, worthlessness, and even self-hatred, ultimately viewing suicide as the only solution [17]. ASSIP aims to prevent future suicidal crises primarily by facilitating the reconstruction of the personal narrative surrounding the suicide attempt and by establishing a strong therapeutic alliance. Through video-recorded narrative interviews, collaborative case formulation, and individualized safety planning, ASSIP enables patients to gain insight into their suicidal process, identify early warning signs, and develop adaptive coping strategies [16].

Studies have shown that ASSIP has been effective in reducing suicidal ideation and suicide attempts in individuals with a history of suicide attempts [18, 19]. Gaily-Luoma’s study [20] also found that clients who received ASSIP treatment rated it as effective in creating positive and life-enhancing changes. Despite this, research on the effectiveness and application of ASSIP in Iran has been scarce!

On the other hand, one of the psychological interventions that has recently attracted a great deal of attention and has one of the proven applications in suicide prevention is Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy is considered one of the third-wave behavioral psychotherapies, the ultimate goal of which is to improve psychological flexibility [21]. Psychological flexibility refers to being in the present moment and changing or stabilizing behavior, behavior that is consistent with one’s values, given the situation [22]. From a behavioral science perspective, the philosophical foundation of ACT is that almost all suicidal crises are somehow related to experiential avoidance [23], in a way that individuals often engage in repeated attempts to escape from unpleasant thoughts, feelings, and experiences. These attempts may manifest as isolation, substance use, and other avoidance behaviors. Intense efforts to avoid painful experiences can come at the cost of losing supportive resources and meaningful interpersonal relationships. If these attempts fail, suicidal ideation develops and the likelihood of suicidal behavior increases; in this case, suicide is seen as an ultimate solution to escape from suffering and pain [24]. It also appears that individuals who attempt suicide have difficulty finding appropriate solutions to difficult situations due to a lack of psychological flexibility [25]. In fact, ACT therapists, by targeting psychological flexibility, help patients to consider alternative solutions through this element, which can lead to a reduction in suicidal ideation [26].

In this regard, one of the Acceptance and Commitment Therapy protocols, specifically designed to prevent suicide, is the ‘ACT For Life’ protocol; in this protocol, therapists, by involving clients in the main processes of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, try to avoid dictating behavior and instead encourage them to focus on internal events that are in line with their own life values [24]. Previous studies have shown that Acceptance and Commitment Therapy is effective in reducing self-criticism and suicidal thoughts [27]. Also, Alizadeh’s research [28] indicated the effectiveness of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy on the subscale of suicide readiness. The study by Ghadam et al. [29] also showed that Acceptance and Commitment Therapy has a significant impact on suicidal thoughts.

Given that survivors of suicide attempts often find it difficult to engage in long-term therapies [20], brief interventions that enhance motivation and reduce mental pain are of particular importance. In this study, we compare two theoretically grounded approaches. ASSIP, based on narrative and attachment theories, views suicidal behavior as a response to unbearable mental pain and aims to restore meaning and self-coherence through narrative reconstruction, therapeutic alliance, and individualized safety planning [16]. In contrast, ACT grounded in the psychological flexibility model, targets experiential avoidance and cognitive fusion—two core processes contributing to suicidal ideation. Through mindfulness, acceptance, and values-based behavioral activation, ACT helps individuals develop the ability to tolerate painful emotions without avoidance, detach from self-critical thoughts, and re-engage with meaningful life directions. By increasing psychological flexibility [23, 30], ACT is expected to reduce the intensity of suicidal ideation and mental pain. Both interventions therefore aim to alleviate suicidal ideation and psychological pain through distinct but complementary mechanisms.

Despite evidence supporting ASSIP and ACT in Western contexts [17, 18, 20, 24], few studies have evaluated these interventions among non-Western populations, particularly within Middle Eastern cultural settings. Moreover, there is a lack of randomized controlled trials directly comparing these two structured, short-term therapies in suicide attempters. This study aims to address these gaps by testing and adapting ASSIP and ACT protocols in an Iranian clinical context. Therefore, this protocol for implementing the psychological interventions of this research has been developed under the supervision of Ardakan University and the Mental, Social, and Addiction Health Unit of the Eslamshahr Health Network.

Methods/design

Research aims

The primary objective of this study is to compare the effectiveness of ASSIP and ACT as add-on interventions to TAU on mental pain and suicidal ideation (in adult) suicide attempters. Sub-objectives include determining the individual effectiveness of ASSIP and ACT on mental pain and suicidal thoughts, and comparing their effectiveness against each other on these outcomes.

Hypotheses

Based on the research aims and the comparative nature of the trial, the following hypotheses will be tested:

Primary hypotheses

H1 (ASSIP vs. TAU)

The Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program (ASSIP) will result in a significantly greater reduction in mental pain (OMMP scores) and suicidal ideation (BSSI scores) compared to the Treatment as Usual (TAU) control group at post-treatment and 2-month follow-up.

H2 (ACT vs. TAU)

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) will result in a significantly greater reduction in mental pain (OMMP scores) and suicidal ideation (BSSI scores) compared to the Treatment as Usual (TAU) control group at post-treatment and 2-month follow-up.

Secondary hypotheses

H3 (ASSIP vs. ACT - Mental Pain)

There will be a significant difference in the effectiveness between ASSIP and ACT in reducing mental pain (OMMP scores) at post-treatment and 2-month follow-up.

H4 (ASSIP vs. ACT - Suicidal Ideation)

There will be a significant difference in the effectiveness between ASSIP and ACT in reducing suicidal ideation (BSSI scores) at post-treatment and 2-month follow-up.

Additionally, data on self-harm behaviors, re-attempts, and hospitalizations will be descriptively monitored to complement the understanding of treatment effects, although these are not considered formal outcome variables within the present hypotheses.

Study design

The study is designed as a quantitative, quasi-experimental research with a pre-test-post-test follow-up design, involving two experimental groups and one control group. The two intervention groups will receive either the Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program (ASSIP) or Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), respectively, in addition to treatment as usual (TAU), while the control group will receive TAU only. All groups will be assessed at three time points: before the start of treatment, immediately after the completion of treatment, and tow month after the intervention. A follow-up assessment is conducted two months after completion of the intervention to evaluate short-term maintenance of treatment effects. The two-month interval was selected to balance the need for meaningful outcome assessment with the feasibility of retaining participants in this high-risk population, given the typically low adherence rates among suicide attempters. Longer-term follow-up assessments (e.g., at 6, 12, and 24 months) are planned in future extensions of this study, contingent upon securing additional resources.

In the participating clinical network (Eslamshahr Health and Treatment Network), TAU for suicide attempters typically comprises standard psychiatric evaluation and pharmacotherapy when indicated, in outpatient clinics or hospital services. The content and intensity of TAU may vary between individuals according to clinical need (e.g., type and dose of psychotropic medication, brief supportive counselling, social work follow-up). To document and later control for this heterogeneity, a structured baseline clinical interview will be conducted prior to randomization, and detailed information will be recorded on prior and current treatments, including: (a) current psychotropic medication (agent, daily dose, start date), (b) any psychotherapy or counselling received after hospital discharge (excluding structured, manualized protocols) (type, provider, number of sessions), (c) frequency of clinical contacts or follow-up calls, and (d) other psychosocial supports (e.g., family interventions, social services). These TAU components will be prospectively logged in the study database for each participant.

Study site, participants, and recruitment

The study population will comprise individuals who have attempted suicide in Eslamshahr County, Iran and the study will be conducted at the Imam Hossein District Health Center in Eslamshahr, Tehran. To this end, a list of individuals discharged from the hospital following a suicide attempt is obtained from the local health network. According to the ASSIP protocol, the intervention should ideally be initiated within a short time frame following a suicide attempt, typically within days or weeks. However, based on the structure of the Iranian health system, records of suicide attempts are made available to local health networks through monthly reports issued by Tehran University of Medical Sciences, in accordance with the protocols of the Ministry of Health. Recruitment feasibility has been evaluated based on these official records. Over the past two years (2023–2024), approximately 600 individuals with a history of suicide attempts have been registered within the Eslamshahr Health Network. Because all discharged patients are routinely referred to this network for follow-up psychological services, sufficient access to eligible participants is ensured. Thus, recruiting 60 participants (20 per group) is considered feasible within the planned timeframe.

Participants will be recruited using the most recent records available at the time of sampling. Due to limited immediate availability and variations in participants’ willingness to engage in the study, recruitment will sometimes involve contacting individuals from previous months’ reports. As a result, the time lapse between the suicide attempt and the beginning of the intervention may vary. In the current sample, this time lapse will range from 2 to 16 weeks (M ≈ 8 weeks). A total of three groups of 20 individuals will be planned, randomly selected from the eligible participants.

Clinical trial registration and registration date

The study is currently ongoing and was registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07132099, registered on August 20, 2025).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria for this study include: being 18 years of age or older, being able to read and write, providing informed consent, and having a history of at least one recent suicide attempt.

Exclusion criteria include severe physical or psychiatric illnesses that may interfere with treatment participation, such as psychotic disorders (e.g., schizophrenia spectrum or other psychotic disorders as defined by DSM-5-TR), acute manic episodes, or severe cognitive impairment. The presence of these conditions will be determined through a brief structured clinical interview and review of medical and psychiatric records at baseline. Participants receiving concurrent structured, manualized psychological interventions (other than TAU), those who miss more than two treatment sessions, or those who withdraw consent will also be excluded. Furthermore, participants with a current severe substance use disorder will be excluded. The exclusion of other structured interventions is necessary to maintain the study’s internal validity and prevent confounding of the specific therapeutic effects of ASSIP or ACT.

Sample size

The total sample size of 60 participants (20 per group) was determined based on a priori power analysis using G*Power 3.1 software for a one-way multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) with three groups (ASSIP, ACT, and TAU), two dependent variables (mental pain and suicidal ideation), and an alpha level of 0.05. Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25, equivalent to η² = 0.06) and a statistical power of 0.80, the required total sample size was estimated at 57 participants. To account for an expected attrition rate of approximately 5%, the final target sample size was set at 60 participants. This effect size assumption is consistent with prior clinical trials evaluating short-term psychological interventions for suicide prevention (e.g. [18]).

Randomization and blinding

The random allocation sequence is generated by a third party (the health center social worker) using a computer-generated random sequence to create a list of group assignments in an Excel spreadsheet. This list is kept confidential and is disclosed to the principal investigator only after each participant’s enrollment is completed. This procedure ensures that the assignment of participants to groups is not known in advance, thereby preventing selection bias.

Participants are unaware of their group allocation, as randomization occurs after the baseline assessment and before the first intervention session. Because of the limited size of the research team, the same clinical psychologist serves as both the therapist and the principal investigator; therefore, full double-blinding is not feasible. However, outcome measures are standardized self-report questionnaires (BSSI and OMMP), and data entry and statistical analyses will be performed by an independent researcher who is not involved in the treatment process. This design minimizes the risk of bias arising from the single-therapist setup.

Interventions and procedures

Participants will be contacted to obtain consent for participation in the study. All participants, including the control group, will continue to receive Treatment as Usual (TAU) (the detailed description of which is provided in the Study Design section). Both ASSIP and ACT are implemented as add-on therapies to the participants’ standard TAU, consistent with evidence from seminal trials [18, 24]. The study’s design, therefore, compares the incremental efficacy of each structured intervention (ASSIP + TAU vs. ACT + TAU vs. TAU alone). The interventions will be conducted in person. The ASSIP intervention will be delivered over three essential sessions, with an additional session if needed. Each session will last between 60 and 90 min. The ACT intervention will be delivered over an average of five individual sessions, each lasting approximately 60 min. All groups will be assessed at three time points: before the start of treatment, immediately after the completion of treatment, and one month after the intervention.

Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program (ASSIP)

The Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program (ASSIP) is a brief, structured therapy designed for individuals following a suicide attempt. It is administered as an add-on to usual clinical management and is based on a patient-centered model of suicidal behavior, emphasizing therapeutic alliance. The standard protocol consists of three face-to-face sessions, ideally completed within three weeks, followed by a series of regular follow-up letters [31, 32]. Each session typically lasts between 60 and 90 min, and a fourth session may be added if clinically necessary [17]. In the present study, the intervention was implemented by the researcher, who received training through a webinar organized by Iran University of Medical Sciences and followed the official ASSIP manual. This approach is consistent with the options described on the official ASSIP website, where implementation based on the manual is accepted in a clinical context. Outcome measures

In this study, two primary outcome measures will be used to assess the effectiveness of the interventions:

Orbach and Mikulincer Mental Pain Scale (OMMP)

The OMMP is a 44-item self-report scale developed by Orbach and colleagues [33]. It measures the intensity of mental pain and its nine subscales include inevitability, lack of control, narcissism/worthlessness, emotional turmoil, numbness, alienation, confusion, social withdrawal, and emptiness (meaninglessness). The scale has demonstrated high reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha for the overall OMMP reported as 0.966 [34].

Beck Suicide Ideation Scale (BSSI)

The BSSI is a 19-item self-report measure developed by Beck and Steer [35]. It is used to assess suicidal thoughts, attitudes, and behaviors, and consists of three subscales: desire for death, suicide ideation, and suicide plans. Each item is rated on a Likert scale from 0 to 2, with a total score ranging from 0 to 38. In Iran, the BSSI has shown high reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.95 and a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.88 [36].

In addition to assessing suicidal ideation and mental pain as the primary outcomes, information on any self-harm behaviors, suicide re-attempts, or hospitalizations occurring during the follow-up period will be recorded through participant self-report and clinical documentation. Although these behavioral indicators are not included as formal outcome variables in the current protocol, they will be descriptively reported to provide a more comprehensive understanding of treatment effects.

Data management, analysis and security

Demographic data, clinical history, and pre-, post-test, and follow-up scores will be collected and entered into a password-protected Excel database to ensure data confidentiality and prevent unauthorized access. All analyses will be performed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and frequencies) will be used to summarize participant characteristics and baseline equivalence between groups. For inferential analyses, Multivariate Analysis of Covariance (MANCOVA) will be employed to compare post-test and follow-up outcomes (BSSI and OMMP scores) among the three groups, controlling for baseline values. If a significant multivariate effect is found, univariate ANCOVAs will be conducted for each dependent variable, followed by pairwise post-hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction to identify specific group differences (ASSIP vs. TAU, ACT vs. TAU, and ASSIP vs. ACT). Because TAU may vary between participants, detailed information on medication use, psychotherapy received, and frequency of clinical visits will be collected at baseline and during follow-up. These variables will be entered as covariates in ANCOVA/MANCOVA models to statistically control for the potential influence of TAU on study outcomes. All analyses will follow an intention-to-treat (ITT) approach. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation, and the level of statistical significance will be set at p < 0.05.

Study oversight and monitoring

The leadership committee for this study will consist of a supervisory professor, a consulting professor, and a clinical psychologist from Imam Hussein District Health Center. Ardakan University and the Eslamshahr Health Network will serve as the leadership committee and research partner, respectively. This team will be responsible for the research design, guidance, ensuring the correct implementation of the research, overseeing data recording, and supervising the writing of the research content.

Therapist qualifications and responsibilities

The interventions will be implemented by the researcher, a master’s student in psychology, who has received specialized training in both the Attempted Suicide Short Intervention Program (ASSIP) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) from certified instructors. The researcher is supervised directly by the leadership committee, which includes a supervisory professor, a consulting professor, and a clinical psychologist. The researcher possesses knowledge and expertise in suicide crisis intervention and is proficient in establishing a strong therapeutic alliance with participants.

The researcher’s responsibilities will include establishing contact with individuals who have attempted suicide, inviting them to participate, and conducting the ASSIP and ACT interventions. The researcher will also be responsible for accurate data entry, ensuring the security and confidentiality of all collected data, and adhering to ethical and legal obligations throughout the study.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial examines the effectiveness of two brief psychotherapeutic interventions—ASSIP and ACT—in reducing mental pain and suicidal ideation among adults with recent suicide attempts. By comparing these treatments with a TAU control condition, the study aims to clarify their added therapeutic value beyond standard care and generate evidence to inform suicide-focused clinical practice. A key strength of the trial is its focus on a high-risk group that is rarely included in controlled studies. The comparative design provides an opportunity to evaluate the unique and shared effects of ASSIP and ACT, offering insights into how different therapeutic approaches may influence recovery following a suicide attempt.

In the context of this trial, both ASSIP and ACT are expected to influence mental pain and suicidal ideation through distinct, theory-driven mechanisms. ASSIP is designed to reduce psychological fragmentation and emotional overwhelm by helping patients construct a coherent narrative of the suicidal crisis through a structured narrative interview [16]. Rewatching the recorded interview and collaboratively developing an individualized case formulation are expected to enhance self-reflection, increase awareness of triggers, and reduce emotional reactivity. The formulation, safety planning, and follow-up contacts aim to strengthen therapeutic alliance and reduce feelings of isolation and abandonment, which are strongly associated with mental pain and recurrent suicidal crises [17, 18]. Through these mechanisms, ASSIP may help participants gain a more predictable understanding of their suicidal state, reduce psychological pain, and enhance perceived control.

ACT is expected to exert its effects through a different pathway, primarily by increasing psychological flexibility and reducing experiential avoidance [23]. Techniques such as cognitive defusion, self-as-context, mindfulness, and values clarification allow participants to reinterpret internal experiences as transient mental events rather than literal truths, thereby reducing the emotional weight of suicidal thoughts [22, 30]. ACT encourages participants to shift from suppressing or fighting painful thoughts toward accepting them as part of the human experience, while committing to meaningful action aligned with core values. This combination of acceptance, defusion, and value-driven action may reduce the perceived necessity of suicide as a problem-solving strategy and lead to clinically meaningful reductions in both mental pain and suicidal ideation.

Several limitations of the current study design warrant consideration. Firstly, although necessary to ensure internal validity and reduce confounding effects on the primary outcomes, the exclusion of participants with current severe substance use disorder restricts the generalizability of the findings, as substance use problems are highly prevalent among individuals who attempt suicide. Future research should prioritize adapting and testing these interventions in this co-occurring population. Secondly, the study utilizes a geographically localized sample drawn solely from the Eslamshahr clinical network, which may further limit the generalizability of the results to other communities with different demographic or healthcare characteristics. Thirdly, due to resource constraints and the specialized nature of the interventions, the same individuals serve as both research assessors and treatment providers (therapists). We acknowledge that this potential for allegiance and assessment bias is a significant limitation. To mitigate this risk, all data analysis and statistical modeling will be performed by a separate, independent expert statistician who is blind to group assignment and the clinical aspects of the study.
