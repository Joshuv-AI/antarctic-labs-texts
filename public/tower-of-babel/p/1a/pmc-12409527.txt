Magnetically Recoverable WO3/Fe3O4 Catalyst for the Selective Oxidation of Styrene to Benzaldehyde
Authors: Yoga Saputra, Ado Ibrahim Yargaya, Ahmad Muhammad Sani, Abbas Yusuf Abdurrahman, Vannapa Luckanawat, Kittichai Chaiseeda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A magnetically recoverable catalyst, WO3/Fe3O4, was successfully synthesized via a coprecipitation and calcination method and applied for the selective oxidation of styrene to benzaldehyde. Structural and surface characterizations (XRD, XRF, SEM, TEM, BET, VSM, and XPS) confirmed the successful integration of both components, mesoporosity, strong magnetic properties, and the presence of W6+ and Fe2+/Fe3+ species. Catalytic studies identified optimal conditions: 33 wt % WO3 loading, 40 mg catalyst, 10 equiv H2O2, 70 °C, and 24 h, resulting in a styrene conversion of 74.21% and a benzaldehyde selectivity of 82.10%. The catalyst exhibited excellent recyclability, maintaining high activity and selectivity across five cycles. These findings highlight WO3/Fe3O4 as a promising, cost-effective, and easily recoverable catalyst for sustainable industrial oxidation processes.

Introduction

The oxidation of styrene is a crucial reaction in both industrial applications and academic research, as it leads to the production of styrene epoxide, phenylacetaldehyde, benzaldehyde, and benzoic acid. Among these products, benzaldehyde is particularly valuable due to its widespread use in the production of fine chemicals, dyes, fragrances, pharmaceuticals, and sweeteners. − Traditionally, benzaldehyde has been produced via the direct oxidation of benzyl alcohol or toluene under liquid–gas phase conditions, requiring high temperatures and pressures. , However, this method is associated with toxic byproduct formation, harsh reaction conditions, and complex procedures, necessitating the development of more sustainable alternatives.

Recent research has explored various catalytic systems for the oxidation of styrene, including photocatalytic, thermal, and electrocatalytic approaches. These systems often utilize heterogeneous catalysts such as MOF-based, , carbon-based, , silica-based, , metal oxide, , and polymer-supported metal catalysts. , Heterogeneous catalysts are particularly favorable due to their reusability, ease of separation, and mild reaction conditions. , For instance, photocatalysts such as porphyrin MOF, V2O5/TiO2, P16PySO/WO3, and NH4Cl-modified g-C3N4/BiOBr have demonstrated potential under mild conditions, utilizing light energy to activate oxygen and selectively oxidize styrene to benzaldehyde. However, this type of catalyst often faces critical limitations that hinder industrial viability, including narrow light absorption spectra and rapid recombination of charge carriers. − Similarly, electrochemical catalysts like MnO2/(Ru0.3Ti0.7)­O2/Ti, single-atom Cr1/CoSe2, and thermal catalysts like polyoxometalate based supramolecular compounds have revealed excellent styrene conversion and selectivity. , Yet, these multicomponent catalysts often prove difficult to reproduce consistently or scale up effectively due to their inherent structural complexity and demanding synthesis methods. Consequently, achieving scalable styrene oxidation to high-value products like benzaldehyde remains a significant challenge when relying solely on these emerging catalytic approaches.

To address these limitations, the development of magnetically recoverable catalysts has significantly enhanced the practicality of thermal catalytic systems. , These catalysts have gained significant attention as a promising alternative to conventional catalyst supports, exhibiting high recoverability, high surface area, chemical stability, economic feasibility, and excellent dispersibility in various solvents. , A key advantage of these magnetic catalysts lies in their easy separation from the reaction mixture using an external magnet, thereby eliminating the need for additional procedures like filtration or centrifugation, which are crucial for efficient industrial scale-up. ,

In the context of oxidation of styrene, several studies have reported magnetic catalysts demonstrating variations in product selectivity based on catalyst composition and structure. For example, Xie et al. investigated the catalytic performance of Fe3O4, observing benzaldehyde as the sole oxidation product. Meanwhile, Wu et al. synthesized porous Fe3O4@CuSiO3 core–shell structures, where styrene epoxide was identified as the primary product. Conversely, Yang et al. fabricated Fe3O4@SiO2@mSiO2–Fe nanocomposites, and Bera et al. developed polymer-conjugated Fe3O4 nanoparticles, both of which favored benzaldehyde formation with styrene oxide as a side product. , Huang et al. synthesized gold nanoparticle-immobilized Fe3O4, where styrene oxide was the predominant product and benzaldehyde appeared as a side product. Yang et al. synthesized Fe3O4/g-C3N4–CeO x and revealed that 92% styrene conversion with 70.0% selectivity was achieved. These findings highlight that the catalytic properties of iron oxide-based materials are strongly influenced by the nature of the supporting material. Additional studies on iron oxide-based catalysts for styrene oxidation are summarized in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Despite extensive efforts to optimize iron-based catalysts, the pursuit of an efficient, scalable, and recyclable system for selective styrene oxidation to benzaldehyde remains ongoing. In parallel, tungsten oxide (WO3) has attracted increasing attention in oxidation chemistry due to its robust redox properties, high oxygen mobility, and catalytic efficiency in reactions such as alcohol oxidation, glycerol oxidation, and oxidative desulfurization. − However, to the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have explored the use of WO3 specifically for the selective oxidation of styrene. To address this gap, we report herein the synthesis of a novel WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst via a simple coprecipitation method and its application in the selective oxidation of styrene to benzaldehyde (Scheme ). The integration of WO3 with Fe3O4 is designed to leverage the oxidative strength of WO3 and the magnetic recoverability of Fe3O4, resulting in a catalyst system that combines good activity, selectivity, and recyclability under mild thermal conditions, positioning it as a promising alternative for industrial applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Sodium tungstate dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O, Carlo Erba Reagents), nitric acid (HNO3, 65%, RCI Labscan), iron­(III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, Tian-Nam Chemical Industrial Trade), iron­(II) chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl2·4H2O, Loba Chemie), and ammonia solution (NH4OH, 60%, Thomas Baker) were used in the synthesis of Fe3O4 and WO3/Fe3O4. Tungsten­(VI) oxide (WO3, Tokyo Chemical Industry) was used as a reference material for both physicochemical characterization and catalytic performance in styrene oxidation. Styrene (stabilized with TBC, C8H8, Tokyo Chemical Industry), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%, Quality Reagent Chemical), tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBHP, 70%, C4H10O2, Tokyo Chemical Industry), potassium peroxodisulfate (K2S2O8, Quality Reagent Chemical), 3-choloroperoxybenzoic acid (m-CPBA, C7H5ClO3, Sigma-Aldrich) and acetonitrile (CH3CN, RCI Labscan) were employed in the catalytic oxidation reactions. Benzaldehyde (C7H6O, Loba Chemie), phenylacetaldehyde (C8H8O, Sigma-Aldrich), and benzoic acid (C7H6O2, KemAus) were used as a standard compound, while biphenyl (C12H10, Thomas Baker) was used as the internal standard for GC analysis.

Synthesis of Fe3O4

In a separate beaker, 8.15 g (30 mmol) of FeCl3·6H2O and 1.5 g (75 mmol) of FeCl2·4H2O were dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and stirred until a homogeneous solution was obtained. The two solutions were then combined and heated to 60 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 20 mL of 25% ammonia solution (267 mmol) was added, and the pH was adjusted to approximately 10. The reaction mixture was allowed to proceed for 30 min under continuous stirring. After completion, the reaction was cooled to room temperature, and the resulting Fe3O4 precipitate was separated and washed thoroughly with deionized water. The obtained Fe3O4 was dried in an oven at 70 °C overnight and used in the subsequent synthesis steps.

Synthesis of WO3/Fe3O4

The synthesis of WO3/Fe3O4 was adapted from the method reported by Stan et al. with minor modifications. An appropriate volume of 0.05 M Na2WO4·2H2O solution was heated to 80 °C, and concentrated HNO3 was added dropwise until the pH reached approximately 0.65. The resulting solution was allowed to react for 2 h under continuous stirring. In a separate beaker, 2.32 g of Fe3O4 (corresponding to 1 mmol) was dispersed in 20 mL of deionized water and stirred at 80 °C for 10 min. The two mixtures were then combined and stirred together for an additional hour. The resulting WO3/Fe3O4 composite was washed thoroughly with deionized water and dried at 65 °C. It was then calcined at 550 °C for 2 h. The final product was weighed and used for further characterization and catalytic application.

Characterizations

The materials Fe3O4, WO3 (commercial), and WO3/Fe3O4 were characterized using various analytical techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller, BET), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM). The crystallinity of the materials was analyzed using a powder X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku SmartLab SE) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. Diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 20–70° with a scanning rate of 0.01° per step. Elemental composition and metal ratios were determined by X-ray fluorescence (Fischerscope X-ray XUV 773). The surface morphology, microstructure and elemental composition were examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEOL JSM-IT800) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (HR-TEM/EDS, JEOL JEM-ARM200F). Specific surface area and pore structure were evaluated using nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms on a surface area analyzer (Quantachrome Autosorb-iQ3-MP/Kr). Magnetic properties were measured using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, Lake Shore Cryotronics 8600 Series) with a maximum magnetic field of 20 kOe, a field step size of 10 Oe, and a magnetic strength set at 2.76 T. The oxidation states of elements were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS Supra) under monochromatic conditions, with an emission current of 15.00 mA.

Catalytic Oxidation of Styrene

A specified amount of styrene, catalyst, oxidant, and acetonitrile was added to a 50 mL two-neck round-bottom flask equipped with a condenser and an overhead stirrer. The reaction mixture was stirred and heated at the designated temperature for a specified duration. Upon completion, the mixture was cooled to room temperature, and 50 mg of biphenyl was added as an internal standard. The resulting products were then filtered and analyzed by gas chromatography (GC) using a Shimadzu SH-I-5MS capillary column.

Recyclability Test

The recyclability of a catalyst is a critical factor contributing to its practicality and cost-effectiveness. In this study, recyclability tests were performed under the optimized reaction conditions. The catalyst was subjected to five consecutive reaction cycles. After each cycle, the catalyst was recovered using an external magnetic field, washed three times with acetonitrile, and then dried at 200 °C for 2 h before being reused in the subsequent cycle.

Results and Discussion

XRD Analysis

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the synthesized Fe3O4 exhibited diffraction peaks at 2θ = 30.35°, 35.59°, 43.31°, 57.34°, and 62.97°, which correspond to (220), (311), (400), (511), and (440) planes. This pattern match the standard magnetite phase with a cubic crystal system (ICDD PDF No. 01-084-2782). However, there is a minor peak at 33.2° indicates the possible presence of a small amount of α-Fe2O3, likely due to partial oxidation of Fe3O4 during synthesis or handling.

Commercial WO3 was also analyzed, revealing three high-intensity peaks at 23.09°, 23.59°, and 24.32° corresponding to (002), (020), and (200) planes. These peaks are closely spaced, with the most intense peak at 23.09°. Additional peaks were observed at 26.60° (120), 28.87° (−121), 33.24° (022), 33.52° (−202), 34.12° (202), and 49.90° (223), consistent with the monoclinic phase of WO3 (ICDD PDF No. 01-071-2141).

The XRD pattern of the WO3/Fe3O4 composite displayed characteristic peaks of both WO3 and Fe3O4. The three high-intensity WO3 peaks appeared at 2θ = 22.93°, 23.43°, and 24.04°, similar to those of the commercial WO3 sample, but with slight peak shifts and broader features, suggesting partial amorphization of WO3 when supported on Fe3O4. This structural modification may result from the interaction between the WO3 phase and the Fe3O4 surface during synthesis.

To estimate the average crystallite sizes, the Debye–Scherrer equation was applied using selected diffraction peaks. The full width at half-maximum (FWHM) values were obtained through Gaussian multiple peak fitting. The calculated crystallite size of WO3/Fe3O4 (16.99 nm) lies between that of pure Fe3O4 (9.48 nm) and commercial WO3 (38.79 nm), reflecting the nanoscale nature of the composite and possible suppression of WO3 crystal growth due to interfacial interaction.

The crystallographic stability of the WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst after multiple catalytic cycles was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. As shown in Figure , the XRD pattern of the reused catalyst after the fifth cycle displays diffraction peaks corresponding to both WO3 and Fe3O4 phases, indicating the retention of the WO3/Fe3O4 structure. Although the intensity of the peaks slightly decreases and shows minor broadening after the fifth cycle, no new peaks indicating degradation or phase transformation were observed. These results indicate that the WO3/Fe3O4 maintains its phase and crystallinity even after repeated use.

XRF Analysis

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis was performed to determine the elemental composition and to estimate the ratio of WO3 to Fe3O4 in the WO3/Fe3O4 composite. The XRF results showed that the material contained 64.99% Fe3O4 and 34.63% WO3, confirming the successful incorporation of both components in the catalyst system. Based on this composition, the turnover number (TON) and turnover frequency (TOF) under the optimized reaction conditions were calculated, yielding values of TON = 12.36 and TOF = 0.52 h–1. The detailed calculations of TON and TOF are provided in the Supporting Information.

SEM and TEM Analysis

The morphologies of Fe3O4, WO3, and WO3/Fe3O4 were examined using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) and transmission electron microscopy. The FE-SEM images (Figure ) revealed that Fe3O4 nanoparticles possessed a predominantly spherical shape, with particle diameters ranging from 10 to 30 nm. The average particle diameter and particle diameter distribution was evaluated based on 100 measurements. An average particle diameter of 20.76 nm was obtained, and the data fit a Lorentzian distribution model (Figure S1), indicating that the Fe3O4 nanoparticles had a relatively uniform size. However, agglomeration of Fe3O4 particles was observed, likely due to magnetic dipole–dipole interactions, the polarity of water used during synthesis, and the high specific surface area of the nanoparticles. For commercial WO3, the particles exhibited a mixture of faceted and angular morphologies, with diameters ranging from 80 to 400 nm. In contrast, the WO3/Fe3O4 composite showed a morphology characterized by a combination of angular WO3 structures and clustered spherical Fe3O4 particles. Notably, the WO3 phase in WO3/Fe3O4 appeared as flake-like structures and displayed greater uniformity compared to the commercial WO3, with particle sizes ranging from 80 to 300 nm. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) analysis confirmed the spherical morphology of Fe3O4 nanoparticles, with an average diameter of approximately 14 nm (Figure a–c). Upon incorporation into the WO3/Fe3O4 composite, the Fe3O4 particles retained their original morphology, indicating that the deposition of WO3 did not significantly alter their shape or size (Figure d–f). The lattice fringes of Fe3O4 exhibited an interplanar spacing of 0.19 nm, corresponding to the (331) plane. In contrast, the WO3/Fe3O4 composite showed lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.13 nm, which is attributable to the (053) plane of monoclinic WO3, confirming the successful incorporation of WO3 onto the Fe3O4 surface. Furthermore, TEM-EDS elemental mapping revealed a homogeneous distribution of tungsten (W) across the Fe3O4 surface, suggesting a uniform and successful deposition of WO3 (Figure ). This uniformity is crucial for optimal catalytic performance and stability, as it enhances the availability of active sites and minimizes potential aggregation during reactions.

BET Analysis

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis was performed on Fe3O4, WO3, and WO3/Fe3O4 to investigate their surface area and porosity characteristics. The resulting isotherms (Figure ) for all three materials displayed features typical of Type IV isotherms, which are characteristic of mesoporous materials. Among the samples, WO3 exhibited the lowest adsorption–desorption capacity, indicating a low surface area and limited porosity, likely due to its larger particle size, as previously observed in the SEM analysis. In contrast, WO3/Fe3O4 showed mesoporous behavior and an adsorption profile similar to Fe3O4, suggesting that the porous nature of Fe3O4 was partially retained in the composite. Effect of WO3 Loading on WO3/Fe3O4 for the Oxidation of Styrene

The catalytic performance of WO3/Fe3O4 was evaluated in the oxidation of styrene, focusing on several reaction parameters, including the WO3 loading, type of oxidant (H2O2, m-CPBA, TBHP, and K2S2O8), reaction time, reaction temperature, oxidant-to-substrate ratio, catalyst loading, and recyclability, to determine the optimum conditions. To investigate the influence of WO3 loading, various amounts of WO3 were deposited onto Fe3O4, and their catalytic activities were compared. The blank reactions were also performed with Fe3O4 alone set as 0% and WO3 alone as 100%. The results showed that increasing the WO3 content from 5 to 33% led to a gradual increase in both styrene conversion and benzaldehyde selectivity, indicating that the presence of WO3 enhances the number of active sites on the catalyst surface (Figure ). Although catalysts with WO3 content greater than 33% were also synthesized, they exhibited a significant loss of magnetic properties, making magnetic separation difficult. As a result, 33% WO3/Fe3O4 was selected as the optimum catalyst composition, balancing high catalytic performance with sufficient magnetic recoverability.

Effect of Oxidants

Four types of oxidantsH2O2, m-CPBA, TBHP, and K2S2O8along with a blank control (no oxidant), were tested to evaluate their effects on the oxidation of styrene using WO3/Fe3O4 as the catalyst (Figure ). In the absence of an oxidant, no product formation was observed, confirming that the presence of an oxidant is essential for initiating the oxidation reaction. Among the tested oxidants, K2S2O8 gave the lowest conversion, with only 16.81% styrene conversion and 8.56% benzaldehyde yield, resulting in a benzaldehyde selectivity of 50.92%. No additional oxidation products were detected with this oxidant. The use of TBHP led to the highest styrene conversion (79.81%) and resulted in the highest selectivity toward phenylacetaldehyde (11.65%). However, benzaldehyde selectivity was low, indicating that TBHP promoted the formation of undesirable byproducts. When m-CPBA was used, a styrene conversion of 62.25% was achieved. However, benzaldehyde selectivity was only 8.01%, while notable amounts of benzoic acid (35.10%) and styrene oxide (13.46%) were also detected. These results suggest that m-CPBA favors overoxidation and the formation of multiple byproducts. In contrast, the use of H2O2 resulted in a styrene conversion of 54.74% with a benzaldehyde selectivity of 76.34%, the highest among all tested oxidants. The selectivities for other products, including benzoic acid, styrene oxide, and phenylacetaldehyde, remained low (in the range of 2–5%). Based on these results, H2O2 was identified as the optimum oxidant for styrene oxidation using the WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst, due to its high selectivity for benzaldehyde and minimal formation of undesired byproducts.

Effect of Reaction Time

The effect of reaction time on styrene oxidation was investigated over a period of 8–36 h (Figure ). From 8 to 12 h, both styrene conversion and benzaldehyde selectivity were relatively low. A sharp increase in conversion was observed at 16 h, followed by a plateau between 16 and 20 h, and a slight increase at 24 h. Benzaldehyde selectivity also increased steadily from 16 to 24 h, indicating that longer reaction times favor the formation of the desired product. However, at 36 h, both styrene conversion and benzaldehyde selectivity slightly decreased, while small increases in byproducts such as styrene oxide, phenylacetaldehyde, and benzoic acid were observed. These results suggest that extending the reaction beyond 24 h may lead to overoxidation and reduced selectivity. Therefore, a reaction time of 24 h was chosen as the optimal reaction time, offering a balance between conversion and product selectivity while minimizing byproduct formation.

Effect of Reaction Temperature

Increasing the reaction temperature typically enhances the collision frequency between the substrate and catalyst, which can improve the conversion of styrene. In this study, an increase in temperature led to a corresponding increase in styrene conversion, rising from 54.74% at 60 °C to 76.20% at 90 °C (Figure ). However, benzaldehyde selectivity remained nearly constant between 60 and 70 °C, then decreased at higher temperatures. This suggests that elevated temperatures are not favorable for selective benzaldehyde formation in this system. Interestingly, the selectivity for styrene oxide, benzoic acid, and phenylacetaldehyde remained relatively constant over the temperature range of 60–90 °C. This indicates that side reactions, such as epoxidation of styrene, oxidation to phenylacetaldehyde, and further oxidation of benzaldehyde to benzoic acid, did not significantly occur under the studied conditions. However, the decline in benzaldehyde selectivity at higher temperatures could be attributed to the formation of additional byproducts, such as 1-phenyl-1,2-ethanediol, , or to the thermal instability of benzaldehyde and styrene oxide, which may decompose or convert to more oxygenated species. Moreover, higher temperatures may promote competing side reactions, including isomerization, overoxidation, and even polymerization, which complicate the reaction pathway. , Therefore, 70 °C was selected as the optimal reaction temperature for subsequent optimization studies.

Effect of Amount of Oxidant (H2O2)

The influence of H2O2 quantity on the oxidation of styrene was investigated over a range of 1 to 20 equiv (Figure ). The results showed that both styrene conversion and benzaldehyde selectivity progressively increased progressively with higher H2O2 amounts. A significant improvement in conversion was observed at 6 equiv, reaching 46.21%, while the benzaldehyde selectivity reached 66.95%. Beyond this point, the conversion continued to increase, peaking at 59.13% with 10 equiv. Similarly, benzaldehyde selectivity reached a maximum of 76.12% at 10 equiv, although a slight fluctuation was observed at 7 equiv. Importantly, side products such as styrene oxide, phenylacetaldehyde, and benzoic acid remained minimal throughout the range, generally under 7%. These results confirm that increasing H2O2 promotes both conversion and selectivity, with 10 equiv providing the best performance under the studied conditions. At 20 equiv of H2O2, styrene conversion and benzaldehyde selectivity both decreased compared to their peak values at 10 equiv. In contrast, benzoic acid selectivity increases, indicating that excessive H2O2 promotes overoxidation. These results confirm that 10 equiv of H2O2 provide the best performance under the studied conditions. Thus, 10 equiv of H2O2 were selected as the optimum amount for further reactions.

Effect of Catalyst Loading

The effect of catalyst loading on the oxidation of styrene was studied by varying the amount of WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst from 20 to 100 mg under otherwise identical reaction conditions. The results, shown in Figure , indicate that increasing the catalyst loading from 20 to 40 mg slightly increases the conversion of styrene from 72.37 to 74.21%, while selectivity toward benzaldehyde remains nearly unchanged at around 82%. However, further increases in catalyst loading from 60 to 100 mg result in a more pronounced decline in both conversion and selectivity. This phenomenon can be attributed to excessive catalyst particles causing aggregation, leading to reduced availability of active sites and mass transfer limitations. Therefore, the optimum catalyst loading is determined to be 40 mg, which offers a balanced performance with a conversion of 74.21% and a selectivity of 82.10%.

Recyclability

The recyclability of the WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst was evaluated through its performance in styrene conversion and benzaldehyde selectivity over five consecutive reaction cycles. After each 24-h reaction, the catalyst was recovered using a magnetic bar, thoroughly washed with acetonitrile, and dried at 200 °C for 2 h before reused. The results demonstrate that the catalyst retained a high level of catalytic activity and selectivity, with only minor variations observed across the cycles. Styrene conversion remained consistently high, and benzaldehyde selectivity showed minimal fluctuation, confirming the catalyst’s stability under repeated use. The TON and TOF of each reaction cycle were also calculated (Table ) reveals slight changes in catalyst’s performance. These findings highlight the excellent reusability and robustness of the WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst, making it a strong candidate for sustainable and cost-effective catalytic processes. Its consistent performance over multiple cycles supports its industrial applicability, where catalyst durability and recyclability are essential. Proposed Mechanism

The catalytic cycle for the selective oxidation of styrene to benzaldehyde over the WO3/Fe3O4 composite is likely to resemble the mechanism proposed by Liu et al. The reaction proceeds via a multistep pathway involving the activation of hydrogen peroxide to generate electrophilic oxygen species and the subsequent transformation of styrene. A schematic representation of the proposed mechanism is shown in Scheme . In the first step, H2O2 reacts with surface tungsten species (W6+) on WO3 to generate peroxotungstate intermediates (W–OOH). These reactive oxygen species are stabilized on the catalyst surface via coordination with lattice oxygen and surface oxygen vacancies. Styrene molecules adsorb onto the catalyst surface, where they undergo electrophilic attack by the W–OOH species, forming a 3-phenyl-1,2-dioxetane intermediate. This unstable intermediate decomposes to yield benzaldehyde and formaldehyde. Further oxidation of these compounds may lead to the formation of benzoic acid and formic acid, respectively. In a parallel pathway, styrene oxide can also form via epoxidation of styrene. Hydrolysis of styrene oxide yields 1-phenylethane-1,2-diol, while 1-phenylacetaldehyde may arise via an acid-catalyzed hydride shift and subsequent deprotonation of styrene oxide. Together, these pathways explain the formation of the major product (benzaldehyde) and minor byproducts (styrene oxide, benzoic acid, 1-phenylethane-1,2-diol, and 1-phenylacetaldehyde) observed in the reaction.

Conclusions

In this study, a magnetically recoverable WO3/Fe3O4 catalyst was successfully synthesized via a coprecipitation and calcination method and applied for the selective oxidation of styrene to benzaldehyde. The catalyst exhibited well-defined structural, magnetic, and surface characteristics, as confirmed by XRD, SEM, BET, XPS, and VSM analyses. Under optimal conditions33% WO3 loading, 40 mg catalyst, 10 equiv of H2O2 at 70 °C for 24 hthe catalyst achieved a styrene conversion of 74.21% with 82.10% selectivity toward benzaldehyde. Notably, the catalyst retained its activity and selectivity over five consecutive cycles, demonstrating good recyclability and cost-effectiveness.

The synergy between WO3 and Fe3O4 enables a combination of oxidative functionality and magnetic recoverability, providing a practical and scalable catalytic system. Compared to conventional systems based on noble metals or complex supports, WO3/Fe3O4 offers a simpler, more sustainable, and economically viable alternative for oxidation reactions.

However, several areas warrant further investigation.
