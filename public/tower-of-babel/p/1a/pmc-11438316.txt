Pulp calcification identification on cone beam computed tomography: an artificial intelligence pilot study
Authors: Li Ye, Shangxuan Li, Chichi Li, Cheng Wang, Xi Wei, Wu Zhou, Yu Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to verify the effectiveness of a deep neural network (DNN) in automatically identifying pulp calcification on cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images.MethodsThis study retrospectively analysed 150 CBCT images. Pulp calcification was identified and manually annotated by three endodontists with 10 years of experience. A DNN model based on the U-Net architecture was constructed to identify pulp calcification, and ten rounds of fourfold cross-validation were conducted. The model performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsThe model achieved a sensitivity of 75.91 ± 2.84% in automatically identifying pulp calcification, with a specificity of 68.88 ± 2.35%, an accuracy of 72.78 ± 2.13%, and an AUC of 73.68 ± 3.09%. According to the ranking for diagnostic tests, the proposed method achieved a “good” grade for sensitivity, accuracy, and AUC and a “fair” grade for specificity.ConclusionsThe results indicate that the proposed method shows promise for identifying pulp calcification on CBCT images. Future research aims to expand the dataset and refine the model, thereby enhancing its clinical applicability. The integration of artificial intelligence into diagnostic and treatment systems is anticipated to increase the efficiency of diagnosing pulp calcification and assist dentists in assessing the difficulty of root canal treatment cases preoperatively.Clinical registrationRegistration was performed on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/) (Registration number: ChiCTR2300077078, 27/10/2023) and National Medical Research Registry Information System (https://www.medicalresearch.org.cn/, 30/10/2023) (Number: MR-44–23-039664).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04922-2.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Deep neural network, Cone beam computed tomography, Pulp calcification

Introduction

Root canal treatment (RCT) is the primary method for treating pulpitis and apical periodontitis, aimed at eliminating infection and preventing reinfection by chemical and mechanical cleaning of the infected root canal system [1]. The difficulty of cases in RCT is influenced by various factors, including pulp calcification, which is a frequently encountered and complex situation [2–4]. Pulp calcification, causing narrowing or obstruction of the pulp chamber and root canals, presents a significant challenge in RCT operations and increases the risk of procedural errors [5, 6]. Dentists commonly utilize preoperative imaging techniques to identify pulp calcification, which is characterized by a decrease in radiolucency within the pulp chamber or root canals. This assessment helps to determine the case's difficulty and whether it is necessary to refer to an endodontic specialist [7]. However, identifying pulp calcification accurately based solely on preoperative imaging is challenging for inexperienced dentists, given the complex and variable morphology and size of these calcifications [8, 9].

Currently, the clinical identification of pulp calcification commonly relies on imaging methods, including X-ray radiography (periapical, bitewing, panoramic radiography, etc.) and cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Two-dimensional X-ray radiography offers advantages such as ease of use and a low radiation dosage. However, it is limited in identifying pulp calcifications smaller than 200 µm on X-ray radiographs. Additionally, the superimposition of anatomical structures can obscure the identification of calcifications within the root canals of multirooted teeth [7, 10]. Conversely, CBCT is widely employed due to its ability to provide high-resolution, three-dimensional spatial structural information. It offers detailed visualization, enabling the depiction of the location, size, and extent of pulp calcification from axial, coronal, and sagittal perspectives [11–13].

In recent years, deep learning (DL)-based image analysis methods have gained significant attention and have been successfully applied in various medical imaging fields, demonstrating excellent performance [14]. DL models rely on algorithms to automatically extract image features and learn to distinguish between normal and diseased structures by learning many cases, which is somewhat like the learning process of doctors. One popular DL architecture, the U-Net model, has been widely used due to its U-shaped structure, which effectively integrates contextual information. The U-Net model offers several advantages, including a relatively small number of parameters, fast training speed, and the ability to achieve high accuracy even with limited labelled samples [15]. The U-Net model has been widely used in oral image analysis [16, 17]. In the identification of pulp calcification, Selmi et al. [9] combined DL models with machine learning classifiers to classify periapical films as “with” or “without” pulp stones, while Yuce et al. [18] employed an object detection network to detect pulp calcification on bitewing films automatically. The DL model is trained on many data annotated by professional doctors, therefore understanding the characteristics of pulp calcification and automatically identifying calcified teeth in practical applications. Compared with traditional methods, the DL model can automate the identification process, reduce the workload of doctors, and mitigate inconsistencies arising from subjective differences among doctors. The DL model possesses strong generalization capabilities and can continuously learn new data to achieve continuous optimization. Although studies have shown promising results for DL models in identifying pulp calcification, there remains a need for comprehensive assessments that consider the three-dimensional anatomical information of the root canal.

Despite the limitations in generalizability across diverse patient populations, this pilot study aimed to propose and assess a potential method for the identification of pulp calcification on CBCT images using a U-Net-based model, integrating segmentation and classification tasks. It is expected that the DL model can meet or even exceed the judgment of professional doctors, assisting in the diagnosis of pulp calcification.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study followed the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. The study adhered to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidelines (2015) [19]. The calculation of sample size is provided in supplemental materials due to word count limitation. A retrospective collection of 150 anonymized CBCT images was obtained from Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. The images were taken using a CBCT machine (NewTom VGi, Italy) between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2022. The imaging parameters included an image resolution of 0.150 mm, a tube voltage ranging from 80 to 120 kV, a tube current between 5 and 12 mA, and a field of view (FOV) of 10 cm × 10 cm.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for the study were as follows: (1) aged between 10 and 75 years; (2) had CBCT images with clear visibility, a complete FOV, and no significant metal artifacts. The exclusion criterion was fewer than 20 remaining natural teeth. The baseline characteristics of the patients were obtained from electronic medical records. All CBCT images were stored in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, with a volume of 672 × 688 × 688.

Data preprocessing

CBCT data annotation

To establish the ground truth labels for model training and performance evaluation, three endodontists with 10 years of experience examined each CBCT image and manually annotated the data. Pulp calcification was identified when the root canal or pulp chamber exhibited narrowing, blurring, or became non-visible, or when pulp stones were present [7]. One endodontic expert utilized MITK (The Medical Imaging Interaction Toolkit) version 2022.04 for manual annotation and tooth segmentation [20]. The other two endodontists reviewed and confirmed the annotations. Interrater reliability between the two reviewers was assessed using Cohen’s kappa test. In cases of disagreement, the two reviewers discussed the issue together and reached a consensus, which was considered the ground truth.

CBCT data preprocessing

To balance computational efficiency and accuracy, all CBCT images were first normalized to an isotropic resolution of 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.4 mm3 [21].To mitigate the impact of metal artifacts, the voxel intensity values of the CBCT images were limited to a range of [0, 2500]. Following the standard image processing protocol in deep learning, voxel intensities were then normalized to the range of [0,1] [21].

Algorithm structure

In this study, we employed a U-Net-based model to automatically identify pulp calcification in CBCT images. The model’s architecture is depicted in Fig. 1.

The convolutional neural network architecture used in this study. The architecture is composed of an encoder, a decoder, and a classifier

The overall model comprises three interconnected parts: an encoder, a decoder, and a classifier. The encoder and decoder are linked through skip connections, enabling the integration of low-level features from the encoder with high-level contextual information from the decoder. This integration enhances the model’s ability to accurately segment teeth and identify calcification. The encoder acts as a feature extractor, extracting relevant features and anatomical details related to tooth segmentation and pulp calcification identification from the raw CBCT data. The decoder generates a precise voxel-level segmentation map of the teeth based on these extracted features. The classifier receives both the feature representation from the encoder and the segmentation results from the decoder. The proposed method provides colour coding in teeth segmentation, with each colour representing a different tooth. And output of the classifier includes the tooth number and the classification of identification result.

Model training and validation

The 150 CBCT images underwent ten rounds of fourfold cross-validation, with 60 CBCT images containing pulp calcification evenly distributed among the four folds. In each cross-validation iteration, one fold was designated the validation set, while the remaining three folds were used for training. This process was repeated ten times, and the average of the test results was considered the outcome.

The initial learning rate was set to 1e-3, and the model was trained for 60,000 iterations. The Adam algorithm was employed to minimize the objective function. Two RTX3090 GPUs (NVIDIA GeForce), each with 24 GB of memory, were used for training. The learning rate decayed by a factor of 0.99 every 500 iterations. All parameters (including weights and bias values) were initialized using a truncated normal distribution with a standard deviation of 0.1.

Statistical analysis

A total of 150 CBCT images were included in this study. The characteristics of pulp calcification were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25, considering sex, age, and tooth distribution. Chi-square tests were conducted to explore differences in the incidence of pulp calcification. The performance of the deep neural network (DNN) model was assessed using the following statistical parameters. Sensitivity measures the DNN’s ability to correctly identify cases with pulp calcification. The specificity of the DNN indicates its ability to accurately identify cases without pulp calcification. Accuracy is defined as the ratio of correct identifications to the total number of cases. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) reflects the accuracy of the method in identifying patients with pulp calcification. According to the ranking for diagnostic tests, scores above 80% are considered “excellent”, 70%-80% are considered “good”, 60%-69% are considered “fair”, and scores less than 60% are considered “poor” [22].

Chi-square tests revealed no statistically significant differences in pulp calcification incidence by age or sex but revealed significant variations by tooth position. Pairwise z-tests confirmed higher calcification rates in molars compared to other regions (P < 0.05), with no difference between anterior and premolar regions (P > 0.05). Furthermore, Cohen’s kappa test yielded a κ = 0.793 (P < 0.001), indicating very good agreement between the two reviewers. This suggests that the ground truth used in this study is reliable.

Discussion

In the medical field, Artificial intelligence (AI) models have been developed for automated disease diagnosis, disease risk prediction, and prognosis assessment. DL has exhibited exceptional performance in analysing complex image data structures, rendering it highly promising for oral imaging analysis [14]. DL-based oral imaging analysis can enhance the efficiency of image diagnosis and aid less experienced dentists in making accurate diagnoses. Currently, a few studies have applied DNNs for the radiographic identification of pulp calcification [9, 18]. These studies demonstrate the potential of DNN models for detecting fine and subtle pulp calcifications on X-ray radiography. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations associated with these models. First, they may not be able to identify small areas of pulp calcification that are less than 200 μm in size. Second, bitewing radiographs do not provide images of the root canal, which means that pulp calcification occurring within the root canal cannot be detected using this method. Furthermore, the superimposition of anatomical structures in two-dimensional X-ray radiography hinders the detection of calcifications within the root canals of multirooted teeth. In this study, we employed a U-Net-based model to automatically identify pulp calcification in CBCT images and validated its performance. The model can assist dentists in assessing the complexity of RCT cases preoperatively, making appropriate referral decisions, and reducing procedural errors during RCT [4].

Our pilot study suggests the potential of DL in the identification of pulp calcification on CBCT images. The developed model, based on the U-Net architecture, achieved results with a "good" sensitivity, accuracy, and AUC, and a "fair" specificity. During training, the DNN learned standard tooth anatomy, enabling it to identify and classify similar data. However, when faced with variations in the test data, such as uncommon anatomical variations or significant artifacts, the model’s performance may be affected.

There are several limitations to consider in our study. First, CBCT provides valuable information about the location and extent of the calcification, which is crucial for dentists. The model's specificity may be influenced by factors such as root canal fillings and anatomical variations, necessitating refined data categorization for performance enhancement. The single-centre dataset and limited sample size necessitate multicentre data and semi-supervised learning for dataset expansion and reduce manual annotation, enhancing AI's generalizability. Additionally, preprocessing for GPU memory constraints may have affected image detail and feature extraction. Future work will minimize information loss and improve model performance with algorithmic refinements like attention mechanisms and multiscale fusion within the U-Net framework. Future work will also leverage the 3D information of CBCT to improve RCT procedures.

Conclusion

The results indicate the proposed method is promising for identifying pulp calcification on CBCT images. Future research will aim to expand the dataset and refine the model to improve its clinical applicability. Incorporating AI into diagnostic and treatment systems is anticipated to enhance the efficiency of diagnosing pulp calcification and assist dentists in preoperatively assessing RCT complexity.
