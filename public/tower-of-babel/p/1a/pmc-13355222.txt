Sleep period noise induces wakefulness via the paraventricular thalamic lateral septum circuit in mice
Authors: Tingting Wang, Junjie Hu, Xiaojun She, Fenghan Wang, Cui Gu, Xinyu Dai, Yanjie Zheng, Yingwen Zhu, Xiujie Gao, Kefeng Ma, Honglian Yang, Hong Xie, Yan Li, Bo Fu, Bo Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEnvironmental noise exposure disrupts sleep architecture by inducing sleep-wake state transitions (SWSTs) or reducing continuity. This study examined patterns of noise-induced SWST and underlying neural circuit mechanisms. White noise (45 dB SNR) induced SWST and increased paraventricular thalamic (PVT) neuronal activity. In vivo fiber photometry revealed increased calcium signaling in PVT glutamatergic neurons prior to noise-induced arousal. Optogenetic/chemogenetic PVT inactivation prolonged latency to arousal and reduced arousal probability. Viral tracing and immunofluorescence revealed dense glutamatergic projections from the PVT that are in close spatial apposition to GABAergic neurons within the intermediate part of the lateral septum (LSI). Projection-specific optogenetic inhibition of PVT terminals in the LSI successfully suppressed noise-induced SWST. These results identify the LSI as a critical functional downstream target of PVT glutamatergic neurons in mediating acoustic arousal, providing a potential neural target for intervening in noise-induced sleep fragmentation.Keywords: noise, sleep-wake state transition (SWST), Thalamic paraventricular nucleus, Glutamatergic neurons

Introduction

Noise pollution is a major public health concern worldwide. A growing body of evidence from both domestic and international studies has shown that noise exposure during sleep can trigger sleep-wake state transitions (SWSTs), resulting in sleep fragmentation or reduced total sleep time.1,2 According to current world health organization (WHO) guidelines for community noise, to minimize sleep disturbance, bedroom sound levels should ideally not exceed 30 dB (LAeq) of continuous background noise3 However, in modern urban environments, nocturnal outdoor noise levels commonly exceed these recommended standards. Specifically, nighttime noise levels adjacent to major traffic corridors commonly reach 70 dB(A) or higher,4 establishing a clear environmental relevance to the 45-dB SNR noise stimulus employed in the present study. Under our experimental conditions (using a 36 dB(A) background), this stimulus corresponds to 81 dB(A), effectively modeling high-intensity urban acoustic perturbations. As such, elucidating the biological mechanisms by which sleep-period noise induces SWST is imperative.

The noise parameters selected as the stimulus factor to induce SWST are inconsistent.5 To address this gap, the present study adopted a titration strategy using a range of signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels to systematically model varying environmental noise intensities. By establishing an intensity-response relationship, we sought to identify a reliable behavioral threshold that effectively triggers SWST, while remaining within ecologically relevant noise limits. While the behavioral consequences of sleep-period noise are well-documented, our understanding of the specific neurobiological mechanisms mediating these sudden state transitions is still evolving.

Recent investigations have elegantly begun to map the distributed network governing sound-induced awakenings. These studies have identified several key neuronal populations involved in sensory-evoked sleep-to-wake transitions, including noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus,6 dopaminergic neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus,7 and parvalbumin neurons in the basal forebrain,8 alongside specific thalamic and brainstem nodes such as the medial sector of the auditory thalamus9 and the mediodorsal thalamic nucleus.10 Despite these significant advances, how specific midline thalamic hubs integrate acoustic perturbations to gate environmental noise-induced arousal remains incompletely understood. The initiation and maintenance of wakefulness primarily depend on the brain’s endogenous arousal systems, for which the thalamus serves as a central hub. Within the thalamus, the paraventricular nucleus (PVT) has emerged as a critical multimodal hub, showing high activity during spontaneous and sustained wakefulness.11 Notably, recent anatomical and functional evidence indicates that the pontine central gray (PCG) transmits rapid acoustic information to the PVT, thereby implicating this thalamic nucleus as a potential gatekeeper for sound-induced arousal.12 Prior studies have shown that the optogenetic or chemogenetic activation of PVT glutamatergic (Glu) neurons can rapidly trigger transitions from sleep or anesthesia to wakefulness.11,13 However, whether the PVT is required for gating environmental noise-induced arousal remains unclear, and the specific downstream pathways involved remain unidentified.

In the present study, auditory stimulation combined with electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG) was applied to record the SWST in mice exposed to noise of varying parameters. To examine the underlying circuit mechanisms, c-Fos immunofluorescence staining and fiber photometry were employed to assess neuronal activity. Furthermore, optogenetic and chemogenetic approaches were conducted to selectively manipulate neural activity, while viral anterograde tracing was used to identify downstream brain regions. Subsequently, site-specific optogenetic suppression of PVT axonal terminals was applied to verify the functional significance of these projections. Overall, our findings reveal that the PVT-to- lateral septum (LSI) glutamatergic circuit plays a crucial role in mediating noise-induced arousal, providing novel insights into the neural mechanisms through which environmental noise disrupts sleep architecture.

Results

Noise-induced SWST depends on 45-dB SNR relative to background noise

To monitor sleep-wake states in real time, custom-made EEG/EMG electrodes were implanted into the cerebral cortex and posterior cervical muscles of the experimental mice. Following a 7-day postoperative recovery period, animals were exposed to white noise of 2–16 kHz (60 s, 4–12 repetitions) during sleep, with the ambient cage noise maintained at 36 dB(A) (Figure 1A). To verify whether different SNRs had an effect on SWST induced by white noise, mice were exposed to 51 dB(A), 66 dB(A), and 81 dB(A) white noise (equivalent to SNR of 15-dB, 30-dB, and 45-dB, respectively). To minimize individual variability, a self-controlled (repeated measures) design was employed, in which each subject was sequentially exposed to the gradient intensities while EEG/EMG was recorded (Figure 1A). The white noise 1/1 octave map revealed that noise intensity was relatively consistent in each frequency band (Figure 1B).

In accordance with EEG/EMG waveform characteristics, sleep stages were classified into non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and REM sleep. During the auditory stimulation trials, the majority of noise-induced transitions were initiated from the NREM sleep state; transitions from REM sleep were notably rare, likely due to the relatively short duration of REM episodes in mice. Therefore, our subsequent analyses primarily focused on transitions from NREM sleep to wakefulness. Upon exposure to 45-dB SNR white noise, the representative EEG changed from a high amplitude of sleep to a low amplitude of wakefulness, and its power spectral density decreased significantly, while the EMG changed from a synchronous and low-amplitude state of sleep to a desynchronized and high-amplitude state of wakefulness (Figure 1C).

The statistical results showed that the latency to arousal (LTA) of the 45-dB group was significantly lower than that of either the 15-dB or 30-dB groups (Figure 1D). Furthermore, the arousal probability was analyzed to more comprehensively characterize the acoustic arousal response. Further, the 45-dB group exhibited a significantly higher arousal probability compared to the 15-dB and 30-dB groups, while the 40-dB group showed a strong upward trend (p = 0.0668), approaching statistical significance (Figure 1E). In conclusion, these results show that 45-dB SNR white noise reliably induced SWST in mice.

To refine the threshold, a 40-dB group was supplemented between the 30-dB and 45-dB groups. The white noise 1/1 octave map showed that noise intensity was relatively uniform for each frequency band (Figure 1B). The LTA of the 40-dB group was lower than that of the 30-dB group and significantly higher than that of the 45-dB group (Figure 1D). In addition, the arousal probability of the 40-dB group showed an intermediate level between the 30-dB and 45-dB groups (Figure 1E). These results show that the effect of the 40-dB SNR white noise-induced SWST was weaker than that of the 45-dB SNR white noise.

To further study the effect of 45-dB SNR white noise on SWST in mice under enhanced ambient noise conditions, the ambient noise was increased to 42 dB(A) (Figure 2A). During 45-dB SNR white noise exposure, the EEG changed from high amplitude in the sleep state to low amplitude in the wakefulness state, while its power spectral density decreased significantly, with the EMG changing from synchronization and low amplitude in the sleep state to desynchronization and high amplitude in the wakefulness state (Figure 2B). LTA in the Stim group was lower than that in the Pre group (Figure 2C). Moreover, consistent with the observed reduction in latency, the arousal probability exhibited a clear upward trend (p = 0.0634) during the noise stimulation period (Figure 2D). In conclusion, 45-dB SNR white noise can induce SWST and shorten arousal latency in mice while ambient noise is enhanced.

Frequency is a critical acoustic parameter. To investigate the effect of noise frequency on SWST, mice were exposed to 45-dB-SNR low-frequency noise (<200 Hz; Figures S1A and S1B), with an ambient background noise maintained at 45 dB(A). Consistent with the known auditory frequency sensitivity of mice—which are largely insensitive to sounds below 1 kHz14,15—during 90 dB(A) (45-dB SNR) low-frequency noise exposure, the EEG showed that mice maintained a high amplitude of sleep with no obvious changes in its power spectral density, while the EMG maintained a synchronous, low-amplitude sleep state (Figure S1C). Statistically, there was no significant difference in the LTA or arousal probability between the stim and pre groups (Figures S1D and S1E). Thus, the 45-dB SNR low-frequency noise failed to elicit SWST.

Glutamatergic neurons in the PVT are activated during noise induced SWST

To explore the neural circuit mechanisms underlying noise-induced SWST, c-Fos immunofluorescence staining was performed as an immediate-early gene marker of neuronal activation.16,17 Mice in both the control and noise groups were housed under a controlled environment with a consistent ambient background noise of 36 dB(A). Mice in the control group remained quiet and undisturbed, whereas those in the noise group were exposed to 45-dB SNR white noise to induce SWST. Brain tissue was collected and fixed 150 min following exposure, dehydrated, and cut into sections for c-Fos immunofluorescence staining. (Figure 3A). We first examined the auditory cortex (Acx), a primary hub for auditory processing. Following white noise exposure, quantitative analysis using high-magnification confocal imaging revealed that the density of c-Fos+ neurons in the Acx significantly increased compared to the control group (Figure S2; n = 4 mice/group, p < 0.01). We subsequently focused on the paraventricular thalamic nucleus (PVT), a critical node implicated in arousal and wakefulness. Immunofluorescence staining with three-dimensional confocal reconstruction revealed a dramatic increase in the density of c-Fos+ cells in the PVT of noise-exposed group (Figures 3B–3D; n = 4 mice/group, p < 0.0001). The PVT is a midline thalamic nucleus predominantly composed of glutamatergic (Glu) neurons.18,19 To directly verify the identity of activated cells, we conducted double immunofluorescence staining for c-Fos and the vesicular glutamate transporter 2 (VGLUT2), a specific marker for glutamatergic neurons, to address the cell-type specificity of noise-evoked activation. Remarkably, quantitative analysis revealed that the vast majority (97.0% ± 2.6%) of noise-activated c-Fos+ neurons in the PVT were VGLUT2-positive (Figure 3E; n = 4 mice). These results provide compelling anatomical evidence that 45-dB SNR white noise preferentially and robustly activates PVT glutamatergic neurons during SWST.

To verify the functional involvement of PVT glutamatergic neurons in noise-induced SWST, a viral mixture of rAAV-VGLUT2-Cre and rAAV-DIO-GCaMP6s was co-injected into the PVT to induce the expression of calcium indicators specifically in glutamatergic neurons. Following viral expression, optical fibers and EEG/EMG electrodes were implanted into mice (Figure 4A) and connected to a simultaneous auditory stimulation and fiber photometry recording system (Figure 4B). Histological analysis confirmed the successful expression of GCaMP6s in PVT glutamatergic neurons, with fiber channels observed above the PVT (Figure 4C). To ensure that the recorded signals represent biological calcium transients rather than motion artifacts, a 405 nm isosbestic control channel was used in all recordings.

We recorded calcium dynamics during noise exposure at different intensities. While low-intensity noise (15-dB and 30-dB SNR) did not elicit any significant changes in calcium signals (Figures S3A–S3D), 45-dB SNR white noise exposure induced a robust increase PVT glutamatergic neuron activity (Figures 4E and 4F). Notably, as shown in the simultaneous EEG/EMG and calcium recording, the calcium signal in PVT glutamatergic neurons increased prior to behavioral arousal onset (indicated by the EMG burst and EEG desynchronization) during 45-dB SNR exposure (Figure 4D, red and black arrows). Quantitative analysis showed that the ΔF/F after stimulus onset was significantly higher in the 45-dB group than in the Pre group (Figure 4G; n = 4 mice, p < 0.0001, paired t test). These findings indicate that 45-dB SNR white noise induces SWST in mice by activating PVT glutamatergic neurons rather than the activation being a consequence of wakefulness.

Inhibition of PVTGlu suppresses noise-induced SWST

To investigate the role of PVT glutamatergic neurons in noise-induced SWST, we applied optogenetic manipulation to immediately inactivate PVT glutamatergic neurons. AAV-VGLUT2-CRE was mixed with AAV-DIO-eNpHR3.0-mCherry or AAV-DIO-mCherry (as a control), and then transfected into glutamatergic neurons. Optical fibers were implanted at the transfection site, and the EEG/EMG electrodes were implanted (Figure 5A). Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining confirmed the specific expression of the viral constructs and their co-localization with VGLUT2 (Figures 5B and 5C). Quantification revealed that 95.9% ± 5.0% of mCherry-expressing cells co-expressed VGLUT2 (Figure 5D). As shown in the representative continuous recording, the mCherry control group showed typical transitions to wakefulness regardless of laser stimulation (Figure 5E). In contrast, for the eNpHR3.0 group (Figure 5F), during the light-off condition, EEG and EMG transitioned from sleep to wakefulness during noise exposure; however, when PVT glutamatergic neurons were subsequently inactivated by laser light (light-on), EEG and EMG were successfully maintained in the sleep state during the next noise exposure. To account for trials where mice failed to awaken, a maximum latency of 60 s was assigned, and each data point represents the average of multiple trials per mouse. Statistical analysis showed that the LTA of the eNpHR3.0 light-on group was significantly higher than that of the light-off group, which did not occur in the mCherry group (Figure 5G). Furthermore, the arousal probability was significantly reduced in the eNpHR3.0 light-on group (Figure 5H).

The role of the PVT glutamatergic neurons was further corroborated by chemogenetic inactivation. AAV-VGLUT2-CRE was mixed with AAV-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry (an artificially designed G protein-coupled receptor inhibited by CNO) or AAV-DIO-mCherry and transfected into PVT glutamatergic neurons implanted with EEG/EMG electrodes (Figure 6A). Histological verification confirmed the expression of hM4Di-mCherry and its co-localization with VGLUT2 (Figures 6B and 6C). Quantification from immunofluorescence staining revealed that 98.29% ± 3.2% of mCherry-expressing cells co-expressed VGLUT2 (Figure 6D). Mice were repeatedly exposed to 45-dB SNR white noise for 60 s while asleep, 30 min following intraperitoneal injection of CNO (2 mg/kg) or saline (Figure 6A). As illustrated in representative EEG and EMG recordings of mice in the hM4Di group (Figure 6E), under the saline condition, noise exposure readily triggered a transition from sleep to wakefulness. However, following the inactivation of PVT glutamatergic neurons by CNO, this noise-induced arousal was markedly suppressed, with mice frequently maintaining a sleep state during the stimulus. In the mCherry control group, the sleep-to-wake transitions occurred normally across both conditions. Consistent with these representative traces, the LTA of the hM4Di group under CNO treatment was significantly higher than that in the saline condition and mCherry controls (Figure 6F), while the arousal probability was significantly decreased (Figure 6G).

Noise-induced SWST mediated by PVTGlu→LSI circuit

To identify downstream targets and investigate their functional role, we co-injected AAV-VGLUT2-CRE and AAV-DIO-eNpHR3.0-mCherry into the PVT (Figure 7A). This approach allowed both the visualization of axonal projections and site-specific activity manipulation. After three weeks of viral expression, robust mCherry fluorescence was successfully observed in the somata of PVT glutamatergic neurons (Figure 7B). By examining the downstream encephalic regions, we identified dense mCherry-labeled axonal terminals enriched in the intermediate part of the LSI. Because the LSI is widely reported to be composed primarily of GABAergic neurons,20,21 we subsequently conducted immunofluorescence co-staining with the GABAergic marker GAD65 to determine the identity of target cells. This analysis revealed that the mCherry-positive axonal terminals originating from the PVT were in close spatial apposition to GAD65-positive neurons in the LSI (Figure 7C). While this optical microscopy lacks the resolution to directly confirm functional synapses, this anatomical proximity strongly suggests that the PVT glutamatergic neurons likely innervate the inhibitory framework of the LSI.

To determine whether this specific PVT-to-LSI projection is functionally required for noise-induced SWST, we implanted optical fibers over the LSI to specifically silence these PVT axonal terminals during noise exposure (Figure 8A). Histological verification confirmed the robust viral expression in the PVT somata (Figure 8B) and the precise placement of the optical fiber above the mCherry-labeled PVT axonal terminals in the LSI (Figure 8C). As illustrated in the representative continuous recordings, the mCherry control group showed typical transitions to wakefulness regardless of laser stimulation (Figure 8D). In contrast, for the eNpHR3.0 group (Figure 8E), during the light-off condition, EEG and EMG transitioned from sleep to wakefulness during noise exposure; however, when PVT axonal terminals in the LSI were subsequently inactivated by laser light (light-on), the mice were successfully maintained in a sleep state during the next noise exposure. Statistical analysis revealed that terminal inhibition significantly prolonged the LTA (Figure 8F) while markedly decreasing arousal probability (Figure 8G) compared to controls. Together, these anatomical and functional results strongly indicate that the PVT-to-LSI glutamatergic pathway is a critical circuit for modulating noise-induced SWST.

Discussion

Currently, the neural circuits underlying noise-induced SWST remain largely unexplored. In the present study, we demonstrated that white noise at a 45-dB SNR can induce SWST through the activation of the PVT glutamatergic neurons. Specifically, we found that PVT glutamatergic neurons are activated during white noise-induced SWST, whereas the artificial inactivation of these neurons or their specific projections to the LSI prolongs the LTA and significantly reduces arousal probability. Our results, which are supported by robust sample sizes and rigorous viral controls (mCherry), establish a causal link between the PVT-to-LSI circuit and acoustic arousal.

Noise-induced SWST is influenced by multiple factors, including intensity, frequency, noise type, and each individual’s sensitivity, physiological state, and mental state.22,23 The present study showed that exposure to 45-dB SNR white noise can reliably evoke SWST, even under varying ambient noise conditions, indicating that the SNR is a more critical determinant of state transitions than absolute sound pressure level alone. Interestingly, low-frequency noise did not elicit SWST. This result not only agrees with the insensitivity of mice to low-frequency sounds,14,15 but also indicates that low-frequency stimulation may not be sufficient to activate the PVT glutamatergic neurons required to trigger downstream arousal circuits. These results thus support the idea that the PVT functions as a 'salience hub' that selectively gates arousal in response to biologically significant acoustic signals or potential threats.24

Although the PVT circuitry involved in wakefulness has been extensively investigated in prior studies,11,25 its specific role in noise-induced SWST remains poorly examined. Indeed, a recent study identified a distributed auditory network mediated by the central gray matter (PCG) of the pontine brain that analyzes ultra-rapid awakenings in response to alarm sounds. This study reported that while the PCG transmits auditory signals to the PVT, optogenetic activation of PCG terminals within the PVT is, by itself, insufficient to induce a sleep-to-wake transition.12 Combined with these findings, our results indicate a potential hierarchical framework in which the PCG may act to relay rapid acoustic information to the PVT, while the execution of the subsequent behavioral arousal command may specifically depend on the recruitment of the PVT-to-LSI circuit. Our fiber photometry recordings indicate that calcium signaling from PVT glutamatergic neurons is significantly increased prior to behavioral arousal, further supporting the role of the PVT as a driver node upstream of state transition.

Regarding the effector pathway, we prioritized the LSI as one of the primary downstream targets of PVT glutamatergic neurons. This choice was informed by the classic efferent mapping identifying the LSI as one of the most prominent subcortical targets of the PVT,26 a finding strongly corroborated by the results of our own anterograde tracing analyses, which revealed a dense plexus of mCherry-labeled axonal terminals distributed throughout the LSI. Crucially, our histological analysis revealed that these PVT glutamatergic terminals are in close spatial apposition to markers for LSI GABAergic neurons, indicating that the PVT likely provides direct excitatory input to the inhibitory framework of the LSI. The LSI is known to provide extensive GABAergic innervation to core sleep-wake regulatory centers, including the lateral hypothalamus (LHA) and ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO).27,28 This inhibitory interface makes the LSI an ideal gating hub, capable of transforming salient environmental stimuli into rapid state transitions by modulating the activity of these hypothalamic nuclei. Our functional experiments confirmed that the specific inhibition of the PVT terminals in the LSI is sufficient to markedly suppress noise-induced arousal, establishing the PVT-to-LSI pathway as an indispensable functional circuit for acoustic arousal.

It is worth noting that the PVT also targets other brain regions, such as the nucleus accumbens (NAc) and central amygdala (CeA).29 While the NAc and CeA are primarily implicated in motivational valence and emotional salience,30,31,32 the PVT-to-LSI circuit nevertheless provides a direct subcortical route for modulating the canonical sleep-wake switch via its output to the hypothalamus. Our pathway-specific functional experiments further demonstrate that inhibiting PVT terminals within the LSI suppresses noise-induced arousal and provide direct evidence for the necessity of this circuit.

In summary, the findings of the present study establish that mice exposed to 45-dB SNR white noise exhibit distinct SWST patterns compared to controls, an effect mediated by the recruitment of a specific thalamoseptal circuit involving the activation of PVT glutamatergic neurons and their functional projections to GABAergic neurons within the LSI. Through combined optogenetic, chemogenetic, and circuit-specific manipulations, we provide evidence that the PVT-to-LSI glutamatergic pathway is essential for noise-induced arousal. Overall, these findings highlight that exposure to noise with specific parameters can induce SWST, presenting a potential public health concern. Future research should systematically investigate how distinct acoustic parameters drive SWST and exploit animal models that closely mirror human auditory characteristics to delineate the underlying neural circuits and molecular mechanisms, thereby providing an evidence-based foundation for public-health standards and potential therapeutic interventions for noise-induced sleep fragmentation.

Limitations of the study

Despite the study’s important findings, it has several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, as our study used only male mice, future validation in female cohorts will be necessary to ensure the generalizability of the PVT-to-LSI mechanism. Second, as our investigation focused on NREM-to-wake transitions, the involvement of this circuit in REM sleep—which shows distinct arousal thresholds and neural regulation—remains to be elucidated. Third, while our functional data strongly implicate a PVT-to-LSI circuit, immunofluorescence imaging demonstrates spatial apposition rather than direct monosynaptic connectivity. Future studies employing electrophysiological approaches or trans-synaptic viral tracing are required to definitively map this microcircuit. Nevertheless, the study establishes the PVT-to-LSI pathway as a pivotal substrate for noise-induced sleep disruption, thus providing a mechanistic framework for understanding how environmental stimuli perturb sleep.

Experimental model and study participant details

Male C57BL/6J wild-type mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China). In accordance with existing protocols established in seminal studies of PVT-mediated arousal,11 only male mice were used in the experiments, to ensure a stable hormonal background and minimize variability in sleep architecture associated with the estrous cycle. Therefore, the influence of sex on the results was not evaluated, which is considered a limitation of this study. Mice were individually housed in custom-made polycarbonate cages under controlled conditions (ambient temperature: 21°C–23°C; relative humidity: 30–50%; 12-h light/dark cycle, lights on 08:00–20:00), with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. All experimental procedures conformed to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences (protocol no. IACUC of AMMS-04-2022-012).

Method details

Fabrication and implantation of EEG/EMG electrodes

A 4–5 cm segment of chromium–nickel alloy wire was used to form at least one complete, tensioned coil around the corresponding 6-pin female header. The central pair of pins served as the EEG leads, the leftmost pins functioned as ground/reference electrodes, and the rightmost pins connected to the EMG leads. To create the electrodes, a constant-temperature welding platform (SBK936b; Shenzhen Bakon Electronic Technology) was preheated to 250 °C, an aluminum wire was melted to secure the wound portions, and the wire was pulled to ensure stability. This procedure was repeated until all six pins were wrapped and welded. Short circuits or incomplete welds were excluded using a multimeter. A hot-melt adhesive gun (JSD-E; Jingsida Trading) was subsequently used to seal and fix the six pins, which were allowed to completely solidify before implantation.

Mice were anesthetized intraperitoneally using avertin (200 mg/kg) and placed in a stereotaxic apparatus (ZS-FD; RWD Life Science). Following aseptic preparation, the scalp was incised to expose the skull and neck muscles, after which the periosteum was removed. Stainless steel screws were inserted into the skull at the bregma and lambda points. The ground and reference electrodes were established using screws at the left and right lambda, while EEG electrodes were placed at the left and right bregma. EMG electrodes were inserted into the cervical musculature. Finally, the electrodes were secured to the skull with dental cement. Following complete solidification, the mice were returned to their home cages; their postoperative condition was monitored 2 h later.

Auditory stimulation and calibration

The sound delivery system (SER-016; SKC Acoustics Technology) comprised a noise signal generator, power amplifier, and loudspeaker, housed in a soundproof room. Noise was generated by the signal generator, amplified, and transmitted through the loudspeaker. To ensure a uniform sound field across the enclosure while accommodating the EEG/EMG commutator and optical fiber tethers, the loudspeaker was positioned 30 cm directly above the center of the recording cage within a soundproof room.

The intensity of the white-noise stimulus was defined by the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). To align our stimuli with internationally accepted environmental noise standards, the absolute sound pressure level in A-weighted decibels [Lp,A, in dB(A)] was calculated using the following formula:

The sound levels were measured in situ for each experimental condition using a high-precision Class 1 sound level meter equipped with an A-weighting filter (BSWA308; BSWA Technology, Beijing, China). All measurements were calibrated at the level of the mouse’s head.

To determine the arousal threshold, we conducted a behavioral titration by systematically increasing the SNR across various ambient background levels. These pilot experiments identified 45-dB SNR as a robust minimum effective threshold for reliably inducing SWST across multiple conditions. These values fall within the range of urban environmental noise defined by the World Health Organization,33 and are below the threshold for nociceptive responses in mice. For frequency-specific experiments, low-frequency noise with a spectral range primarily of below 200 Hz was utilized.

In the noise exposure–induced SWST experiment, mice were exposed to 4–12 noise bouts (60 s each) during sleep. For the c-Fos immunofluorescence staining experiment, animals were assigned to either a control group or a 45-dB SNR white noise group. In the fiber photometry recording experiment, mice were subjected to multiple 5-s exposures of different intensities (15, 30, and 45 dB SNR) to assess neuronal activity in the PVT. In all subsequent functional manipulation experiments (optogenetics and chemogenetics), 45-dB SNR white noise (60 s) was used as the standard stimulus. Across all experimental paradigms involving repeated noise presentations, an inter-trial interval of at least 60 s was strictly maintained.

EEG/EMG signal acquisition and analysis

At least one week after EEG/EMG electrode implantation, mice were transferred to recording cages and connected to the recording line for 1–2 days of adaptation. EEG signals from the cerebral cortex and EMG signals from the neck muscles were recorded using a multichannel electrophysiological recording system (Neurokey; Subsidiary of LEPU Surgical). Data were digitized and analyzed using NeuroScope software. Sleep–wake states were initially scored automatically in 4-s epochs as “wake,” “NREM,” or “REM,” with manual checking and correction performed as necessary. Different states were defined according to EEG and EMG waveform characteristics: NREMS was identified by synchronous, high-amplitude, low-frequency δ (0.5–4 Hz) activity, with minimal EMG activity compared with wakefulness; REMS was defined by a distinct θ (4–10 Hz) rhythm accompanied by low EMG activity; arousal was characterized by desynchronized, low-amplitude EEG dominated by θ rhythm, together with increased EMG activity and periodic bursts.

Noise exposure–induced SWST was conducted between 09:00 and 19:00. In the experiment assessing different SNR levels, mice were exposed to white noise at frequencies of 15-dB, 30-dB, 40-dB, and 45-dB SNR. Two primary outcome measures were quantified: (1) the latency to arousal (LTA), defined as the time from noise onset to the first epoch scored as wakefulness; and (2) the arousal probability, calculated as the percentage of the successful sleep-to-wake transitions within the 60-s noise exposure period. In the experiment testing different ambient noise and noise frequencies, mice were exposed to 45-dB SNR noise, with the readings 1 h before exposure serving as the baseline control group (Pre) and the exposure period serving as the stimulation group (Stim). EEG/EMG signals were recorded continuously from 1 h before exposure until the end of the experiment. Similarly, each 60-s noise exposure was considered one trial. If SWST was not induced during a trial, the LTA was recorded as 60 s for latency calculations and scored as a failed arousal for probability calculations.

Stereotaxic surgery and viral injection

Mice were anesthetized using tribromoethanol (avertin, 200 mg/kg, i.p.) and positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus. Avertin was used to ensure rapid induction and stable anesthesia, while minimizing the suppression of baseline respiratory and cortical activity.11,34 To ensure safety and efficacy, all Avertin solutions were freshly prepared, and shielded from light prior to use. Ophthalmic ointment was applied to prevent corneal desiccation. After shaving and sterilizing the scalp with 70% ethanol, a midline incision was made to expose the skull, after which the periosteum was carefully removed with sterile cotton swabs. Stereotaxic coordinates were defined as dorsoventral (DV) from the brain surface, anteroposterior (AP) from the bregma, and mediolateral (ML) from the midline. Small craniotomies (∼1 mm in diameter) were drilled, and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors were injected into the paraventricular thalamus (PVT; AP: −1.50 mm, ML: 0.00 mm, DV: −2.40 mm) at a rate of 0.1 μL/min using a glass micropipette attached to a 5 μL Hamilton syringe. Following injection, the glass pipette was retained in place for 10 min before slow withdrawal. For anterograde projection experiments, craniotomy sites were sealed with quick-drying adhesive, and the scalp was closed with interrupted sutures. For chemogenetic experiments, EEG/EMG electrodes were implanted. For fiber photometry and optogenetic experiments, an optical fiber (AP: −1.50 mm, ML: 0.00 mm, DV: −2.40 mm) was implanted following EEG/EMG electrode placement and secured with dental cement. Behavioral or in vitro experiments were conducted at least three weeks post-surgery.

For fiber photometry experiments, mice were administered injections of plasmid rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-GCaMP6s- WPRE-hGH polyA (200 nL; BrainVTA) and recombinant rAAV-VGLUT2-CRE-WPRE-hGH pA (200 nL; BrainVTA). For optogenetic experimental and anterograde projection groups, recombinant rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-eNpHR3.0-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA (200 nL; BrainVTA) was co-injected with recombinant rAAV-VGLUT2-CRE-WPRE-hGH pA. For chemogenetic experiments, plasmid rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry-WPRE (200 nL; BrainVTA) was co-injected with recombinant rAAV-VGLUT2-CRE-WPRE-hGH pA. To control for the potential effects of viral transduction and experimental manipulations, a dedicated control cohort was injected with a fluorescent reporter virus, AAV-Ef1α-DIO-mCherry-WPRE (200 nL; BrainVTA), combined with AAV-VGLUT2-Cre. This control group was utilized across optogenetic and chemogenetic experiments to ensure that observed behavioral changes were not attributable to the viral vector or delivery procedures. All viral vectors utilized AAV serotype 9 to ensure efficient pan-neuronal transduction and robust axonal labeling. The specificity of the VGLUT2-Cre-mediated expression was histologically verified by immunofluorescence for endogenous VGLUT2 in the PVT.

Fiber photometry and Data Analysis

The fiber photometry system (QAXK-FPS-LED; Thinker Tech Bioscience) consisted of separate fluorescence excitation and fluorescence collection modules. A 488 nm laser served as the excitation light source. The emitted light was attenuated by a 1% neutral density (ND) filter and directed vertically by a dichroic mirror (DM). After passing through a 10× objective lens, the beam was focused onto the core end of an optical fiber jumper. The jumper was connected to the ceramic ferrule fixed to the animal’s head, allowing the excitation light to reach the target brain region to elicit a green fluorescence signal.

Fluorescence signals were collected via the implanted optical fiber, transmitted back through the fiber jumper and objective lens, and finally passed through a GFP bandpass filter at the dichroic mirror. The filtered signal was detected by a photomultiplier tube (PMT), converted into a photocurrent, and subsequently transformed into a voltage signal by the built-in amplifier. The signal was low-pass filtered at 40 Hz and digitized at 200–500 Hz using a data acquisition system. To rigorously correct for motion-induced artifacts and fluorescence bleaching without the need for a separate GFP-expressing control cohort, a 405 nm LED was utilized simultaneously as an isosbestic control channel. Because fluorescence emission under 405 nm excitation is insensitive to calcium fluctuations, it serves as a highly validated intra-subject reference widely used in recent high-impact neural circuit studies.35,36 Data quality was strictly validated by inspecting the 405 nm traces; any trials exhibiting significant motion-induced fluctuations in the reference channel were excluded from the final analysis.

Mice were assigned to the 15-dB, 30-dB, or 45-dB groups for exposure to corresponding SNR white noise for multiple 5-s trials. For each trial, the 5 s prior to exposure served as the baseline control (Pre), and the 5 s during exposure constituted the stimulation phase (Stim). Fluorescence signals were processed in MATLAB and further analyzed using the Thinker Tech Fiber Photometry software. During data processing, the calcium-dependent 488 nm signal was strictly normalized against the corresponding 405 nm isosbestic signal. The relative fluorescence intensity (ΔF/F) was calculated using the formula (F − F0)/F0, where F represents the current fluorescence intensity and F0 represents the mean baseline intensity during the pre-stimulus period.

Results were visualized as heat maps and line graphs. The heat maps displayed all recorded events aligned to the stimulus onset, while line graphs represented the average response across events. For statistical analysis, the mean ΔF/F was calculated for each mouse within a ±5 s window around stimulus onset.

Optogenetic inactivation during polygraphic recordings

Three weeks following viral transfection and implantation of optical fibers and EEG/EMG electrodes, mice were placed in freely-moving cages within a soundproof room and allowed one day of adaptation to the multichannel electrophysiological recording system and the fiber-optic interface connected to a 589 nm yellow laser (QAXK-LASER-589; Thinker Tech Bioscience). Based on the viral construct, mice were assigned to the mCherry control group or the eNpHR3.0 experimental group to ensure that any behavioral effects were specifically due to opsin-mediated neuronal inhibition, and not the viral vector or laser light.

Prior to optogenetic inactivation, the laser output was calibrated to 6 mW at the fiber tip using an optical power meter. During the experiment, mice were exposed to 45-dB SNR white noise for 60 s while asleep. In the light-off condition, the laser remained off during noise exposure to serve as an intra-subject control. In the light-on condition, continuous laser illumination (70 s) was delivered, beginning 10 s prior to noise onset and continuing until the end of the noise stimulus to ensure stable pre-emptive inhibition of PVT glutamatergic neurons. These two conditions were alternated throughout the experiment, with an inter-trial interval of at least 120 s between consecutive light stimulations.

Chemogenetic inactivation during polygraphic recordings

Three weeks after viral transfection and implantation of the EEG/EMG electrodes, mice were assigned to either the hM4Di experimental or mCherry control groups. Mice were placed in freely moving cages in a soundproof room one day before the experiment where they were allowed to adapt to the multichannel electrophysiological recording system. This dual-group design was employed to rigorously control for any potential non-specific effects of the viral vector or CNO metabolites on sleep-wake architecture.

The following day (saline condition), experiments were conducted between 09:00 and 19:00. Each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL saline, and 30 min later was repeatedly exposed to 45-dB SNR white noise for 60 s during sleep. On the third day (CNO condition), mice received an intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 0.1 mg/mL, 2 mg/kg; 34233-69-7; MedChemExpress), followed 30 min later by the same noise exposure protocol as for the saline condition. All results were validated post-hoc through histological examination to ensure that hM4Di-mCherry/control mCherry expression was strictly localized within the PVT.

Immunohistochemistry and histological verification

Following the behavioral experiments or recording sessions, mice were deeply anesthetized with avertin (200 mg/kg, i.p.) and transcardially perfused with 10 mL of saline followed by 10 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for at least 48 h, and then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose.

To investigate neuronal activation and neurochemical identity, 40-μm-thick coronal sections were prepared using a freezing microtome (CM1900; Leica). Sections were rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Non-specific binding was blocked by sequential incubation in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin and 0.5% Triton X-100. For functional mapping, sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The primary antibodies used were as follows: rat anti-c-Fos (1:2000; Synaptic Systems, Cat# 226 017), rabbit anti-VGLUT2 (1:500; HUABIO, Cat# HA723218), and rabbit anti-GAD65 (1:500; HUABIO, Cat# HA722750). After thorough washing in PBS, sections were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rat (Invitrogen, Cat# A-21208), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (Abcam, Cat# ab150065), or Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit (Abcam, Cat# ab150076) for 2 h at room temperature.

To quantify neuronal activation, representative coronal sections at matched anteroposterior coordinates were selected for each mouse. The anatomical boundaries of the target regions (e.g., PVT and Acx) were delineated according to the mouse brain atlas. The number of c-Fos positive cells within these defined regions was counted using Imaris software (Bitplane), and the activation level was quantified as cell density (number of c-Fos positive cells per mm2). All imaging and quantification were performed using high-resolution confocal microscopy to ensure accuracy.

For verification of viral transduction and hardware placement, 40-μm-thick sections were examined using both a fluorescence microscope (DM4B; Leica) and a confocal microscope. Data inclusion was strictly limited to mice in whom viral expression (e.g., GCaMP6s, hM4Di, or eNpHR3.0) was localized within the anatomical boundaries of the PVT. For projection experiments, mCherry-labeled axonal terminals—distinct from the somata—had to be confirmed within the LS. Finally, the anatomical precision of all viral transduction and hardware implants (optical fibers, cannulas, and electrodes) was verified post-hoc. Subjects with significant off-target viral spread (e.g., into adjacent midline thalamic nuclei) or misplaced hardware were excluded from all final analyses to ensure data stringency.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism Software (ver. 10.0). Statistical details of experiments, including the exact value of n and what n represents, can be found in the figure legends and Results section. In all experiments, n represents the number of individual animals. Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean), unless otherwise specified. To represent the responses of individual subjects across different experimental conditions, the individual data points and their corresponding paired trajectories are displayed in the arousal latency and probability plots.

For quantitative comparisons involving more than two groups (e.g., the SNR gradient experiments), one-way RM ANOVA analyses followed by Tukey’s post-hoc tests were conducted to identify specific group differences. For experiments involving repeated measurements or multiple factors (e.g., light-off vs. light-on conditions across different viral groups), two-way RM ANOVA analyses were employed to identify significant interactions between treatments and conditions.

Simple comparisons between two independent groups or within-group comparisons (e.g., Pre vs. Stim periods) were conducted using unpaired and paired t-tests, respectively. Normality and variance homogeneity were checked prior to choosing the appropriate test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (two-tailed).
