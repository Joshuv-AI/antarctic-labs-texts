Comparative evaluation and agreement between ICDAS and UniViSS caries detection systems among toddlers and preschoolers
Authors: K.C. Vignesh, Gnanasekaran Felsypremila, S. Ishvarya, P. Anitha, Haridoss Selvakumar, P.C. Arthi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo compare the agreement and concordance between ICDAS and UniViSS caries detection systems among toddlers and preschoolers.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 281 children aged 2–6 years at a tertiary care hospital in South India. All primary molars were examined using both systems and converted to binary format (caries present vs. sound) for comparison, as full ordinal-scale comparison was not feasible given their non-equivalent scoring dimensions. Agreement was assessed using Cohen's Kappa coefficient; sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and likelihood ratios were calculated with ICDAS as the reference comparator. As ICDAS is a visual index rather than a histological gold standard, all parameters reflect inter-method concordance, not true diagnostic accuracy.ResultsThe mean age was 5.01 years (SD = 1.07), with 52.7% females. Cohen's Kappa values ranged from 0.886 to 0.983, indicating almost perfect agreement. Against ICDAS as comparator, UniViSS showed inter-method sensitivity of 0.88–1.00, specificity of 0.96–0.99, PPV of 0.88–0.97, and NPV of 0.94–1.00; these values represent agreement with ICDAS and not true diagnostic accuracy. Overall concordance ranged from 95% to 99%.ConclusionUniViSS demonstrated almost perfect inter-method concordance with ICDAS for binary caries detection in primary molars of toddlers and preschoolers. UniViSS may serve as a practical clinical alternative to ICDAS for binary caries screening; however, findings should be interpreted in the context of the high-prevalence study population, and histological validation remains a priority.

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC) remains a significant global public health concern affecting the primary dentition of children aged 71 months or younger.1 ECC is characterized by one or more decayed, missing, or filled tooth surfaces in any primary tooth, leading to pain, infection, impaired nutrition, and developmental complications.2 Global ECC prevalence varies widely from 12% to 98% depending on geographic location, socioeconomic status, and dental care access.3 The disease process begins soon after tooth eruption and can rapidly progress due to frequent sugar consumption, inadequate oral hygiene, and structural characteristics of primary teeth.4 Early detection and intervention are crucial for preventing caries progression and minimizing invasive treatments in young children.5,6

The International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS), developed in 2005, provides a comprehensive method for detecting dental caries across the disease spectrum, from initial demineralization to extensive cavitation.7 ICDAS employs a seven-point ordinal scale (0-6) enabling detection of early enamel changes and monitoring caries progression, making it valuable for epidemiological studies and preventive care programs.8 Studies have demonstrated that ICDAS exhibits high reproducibility and validity, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 80% for detecting both non-cavitated and cavitated lesions.9 The Universal Visual Scoring System (UniViSS) is a more recent caries detection tool designed to simplify visual assessment while maintaining diagnostic accuracy.10 UniViSS employs a three-step diagnostic approach assessing activity, color, and extent, potentially providing comprehensive information for treatment planning.11 Comparative studies suggest ICDAS has good overall performance for comprehensive screening, while detailed systems like UniViSS may offer additional diagnostic information, though requiring more examination time.12

Despite multiple available caries detection systems, limited evidence compares ICDAS and UniViSS performance specifically in toddlers and preschoolers—a population presenting unique challenges due to behavioral management issues and distinct primary dentition characteristics.13 The choice of appropriate caries detection tool must balance diagnostic accuracy with practical considerations such as examination time, child cooperation, and early lesion detection amenable to non-invasive interventions.14 Current literature lacks comprehensive comparative studies evaluating reliability and clinical applicability of these systems in toddler and preschool age groups, where early detection significantly impacts ECC prevention.15,16 Systematic comparison would provide valuable evidence guiding pediatric practitioners in selecting optimal caries detection tools and support evidence-based decision-making in public health programs.17 We hypothesized that UniViSS would demonstrate substantial binary agreement with ICDAS for caries detection (caries present vs. sound) in primary molars of toddlers and preschoolers. Hence, this study aims to evaluate binary inter-method agreement and concordance between ICDAS and UniViSS caries detection systems among toddlers and preschoolers.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in South India from January to December 2024, following STROBE guidelines.18 Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee (CSP/23/JUL/133/690), and written informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians prior to enrollment.

Study population and sample size

Children aged 2-6 years attending the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry at a tertiary care hospital who was referred for dental caries assessment was included. Sample size calculation based on 90% desired sensitivity and specificity, 49.6% ECC prevalence, 5% precision, and 95% confidence level yielded 281 children. Inclusion criteria: age 2-6 years, at least four erupted primary molars (one quadrant fully erupted), cooperative behavior, and parental consent. Exclusion criteria: lack of consent, fewer than four erupted primary molars, medically compromising conditions, syndromic disorders, and children outside the 2-6 year age range.

Clinical examination procedure

A single calibrated examiner (IS) performed all examinations using appropriate personal protective equipment. Children were examined in a dental chair under adequate lighting. Teeth were cleaned with gauze and dried with cotton rolls followed by air drying. Comprehensive dental examination used a plane mouth mirror and blunt-ended William's periodontal probe. All primary first and second molars (teeth 54, 55, 64, 65, 74, 75, 84, 85 per FDI notation) were evaluated using ICDAS7 and UniViSS.10

To minimize bias, ICDAS assessment was performed first for all primary molars, followed by UniViSS assessment during the same session with a 2-3 min interval between assessments. ICDAS scores were recorded before proceeding to UniViSS evaluation. It is acknowledged that this short interval may not fully eliminate memory bias, as the examiner may recall prior ICDAS assessments when scoring UniViSS. A randomized order of systems or a longer washout period in future studies would be more methodologically robust. For ICDAS, each tooth was scored using the seven-point scale (0-6), where 0 indicates sound surface and scores 1-6 indicate increasing lesion severity. For UniViSS, lesions were classified using a scale of 7-15, with higher scores indicating greater severity (Table 3). Scores were recorded on standardized forms (Microsoft Excel 2007).

Data categorization

This study was specifically designed to evaluate binary inter-method concordance (caries present vs. sound) between ICDAS and UniViSS; full ordinal-scale comparison was not feasible given the non-equivalent scoring dimensions of the two systems. Both scoring systems were converted to binary format for comparison (Table 1). Only common diagnostic factors between tools were compared, as ICDAS lacks color and activity classification. ICDAS scores were dichotomized: 0 = sound teeth, ≥1 = caries present. Examiner calibration

The investigator (IS) underwent calibration exercises under supervision of an experienced pediatric dentist (KCV) through clinical photographs and in vivo examination of children not in the study sample. For intra-examiner reliability assessment, 28 children (10% of sample) were re-examined after two weeks using both systems. Intra-examiner Cohen's Kappa coefficients were 0.92 (95% CI: 0.88-0.96) for ICDAS and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85-0.95) for UniViSS, indicating excellent agreement.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software version 4.4.3. Descriptive statistics included age (mean, standard deviation) and sex (frequency, percentage). Agreement between ICDAS and UniViSS was assessed using Cohen's Kappa coefficient with 95% confidence intervals for each tooth, interpreted per Landis and Koch criteria (<0.00 = poor, 0.00-0.20 = slight, 0.21-0.40 = fair, 0.41-0.60 = moderate, 0.61-0.80 = substantial, 0.81-1.00 = almost perfect).

Inter-method concordance of UniViSS, with ICDAS as reference comparator (noting that ICDAS is a visual index rather than a histological gold standard), was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), positive and negative likelihood ratios, and proportion of correctly classified teeth using 2×2 contingency tables. To account for the clustering of multiple teeth within the same child, 95% confidence intervals for all Kappa coefficients were derived using a cluster-stratified bootstrap procedure (3000 iterations) with the individual child as the sampling unit. At each iteration, children were sampled with replacement and all teeth from each sampled child were included. All analyses were two-sided with statistical significance at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 281 children participated (mean age 5.01 years, SD = 1.07, range 2-6 years), comprising 148 females (52.7%) and 133 males (47.3%). All children completed examination with no dropouts. A total of 2248 primary molars were examined. Overall caries prevalence was 85.3% (1918/2248 teeth). Tooth-specific prevalence ranged from 82.6% (tooth 85) to 88.3% (tooth 74). Agreement between ICDAS and UniViSS

Agreement between ICDAS and UniViSS was consistently high across all eight primary molars (Table 1). Cohen's Kappa values ranged from 0.886 (95% CI: 0.826–0.939, tooth 85) to 0.983 (95% CI: 0.955–1.000, tooth 74). Maxillary first molars (teeth 54, 64) demonstrated Kappa values of 0.945 and 0.928 respectively, while maxillary second molars (teeth 55, 65) showed Kappa values of 0.943 and 0.958 respectively. Mandibular molars exhibited Kappa values of 0.983 (tooth 74), 0.929 (tooth 75), 0.892 (tooth 84), and 0.886 (tooth 85).

Inter-method concordance of UniViSS against ICDAS (Not true diagnostic accuracy)

Using ICDAS as the reference comparator — noting that ICDAS is a clinical visual index rather than a histological gold standard — UniViSS demonstrated excellent inter-method concordance. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV values presented below reflect agreement between the two visual scoring systems and should not be interpreted as estimates of true diagnostic accuracy. Sensitivity ranged from 0.88 to 1.00, with tooth 74 achieving perfect sensitivity of 1.00. Specificity ranged from 0.96 (tooth 55) to 0.99 (teeth 54, 64, 65, 74). PPV ranged from 0.88 (tooth 84) to 0.97 (teeth 54, 64), and NPV from 0.94 (tooth 85) to 1.00 (teeth 65, 74). Positive likelihood ratios ranged from 25.85 (tooth 84) to 100.50 (tooth 74). Negative likelihood ratios ranged from 0.00 (teeth 65, 74) to 0.12 (tooth 85). Overall correctly classified teeth ranged from 95% to 99%, with teeth 65 and 74 achieving highest concordance (99%). It must be noted that the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV values reported above reflect relative performance against ICDAS as a comparator, not against a histological gold standard.

Tooth-specific analysis

Tooth 65 (primary maxillary left second molar) demonstrated highest Kappa (0.958) among maxillary teeth with sensitivity of 0.98 and specificity of 0.99. Tooth 74 (mandibular right first molar) exhibited highest agreement (Kappa = 0.983) overall with perfect sensitivity (1.00) and highest specificity (0.99). Lower performance for teeth 84 and 85 (mandibular left molars) may reflect reduced visual accessibility during examination.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This study evaluated inter-method concordance and agreement between ICDAS and UniViSS in toddlers and preschoolers aged 2-6 years, revealing almost perfect agreement with Cohen's Kappa values of 0.886–0.983. Using ICDAS as reference comparator, UniViSS demonstrated sensitivity of 0.88–1.00, specificity of 0.96–0.99, and inter-method concordance of 95–99%. Importantly, these figures validate UniViSS as a screening tool (caries present vs. sound) only, and do not evaluate its full three-step assessment capability (activity, color, extent), which was collapsed during binary dichotomization. Tooth-specific inter-system Kappa values for primary molars in toddlers and preschoolers have not been previously documented in the published literature.11,12,19,20 Tooth 74 exhibited highest agreement (Kappa = 0.983) and perfect sensitivity, while mandibular left molars showed slightly lower but still excellent performance.

Comparison with previous literature

Published comparative studies of ICDAS and UniViSS have focused on permanent teeth or mixed dentition11,19,20; tooth-by-tooth comparisons specifically in toddlers and preschoolers with exclusive primary molar evaluation have not been reported in the literature.

The excellent agreement between systems can be attributed to shared fundamental principles of visual caries detection based on systematic tooth surface assessment.21 Both rely on visual inspection after tooth cleaning and drying, enhancing early demineralization visibility.22 However, systems differ in complexity: UniViSS employs three-step assessment (activity, color, extent) providing comprehensive but time-intensive information, while ICDAS offers simpler approach focusing on lesion extent and severity. Binary classification employed may have contributed to high agreement by reducing disagreement at adjacent ordinal scale points. Critically, the binary approach validates UniViSS only as a screening tool (caries present vs. sound). The introduction highlights UniViSS's advantage through activity, color, and extent assessment; however, these parameters were collapsed during analysis and therefore cannot be validated by the present findings. Readers should note that this study supports UniViSS for binary caries detection in toddlers and preschoolers, but does not validate its three-step assessment capability relative to ICDAS.23 Lower specificity in mandibular left molars (teeth 84, 85) warrants further investigation. Possible contributing factors include reduced visual accessibility during examination. While staining or developmental enamel defects may play a role, the specific nature of disagreements—for instance, whether UniViSS flagged lesions that ICDAS scored as sound—was not analyzed case-by-case in this study and should be addressed in future research.24 Performance variation across tooth positions relates to anatomical accessibility and examiner visual access.25 Both systems are purely visual, potentially limiting interproximal or hidden lesion detection. Positive likelihood ratios (25.85–100.50) indicate strong diagnostic utility for confirming caries presence.26

Clinical and policy implications

Simultaneous application of both ICDAS and UniViSS in children aged 2–6 years has not been reported in previously published comparative studies of caries detection systems.11,12 When caries detection is dichotomized into sound versus diseased surfaces, both ICDAS and UniViSS show comparable performance, suggesting either system can effectively screen young children. However, system choice should consider clinical objectives: ICDAS provides efficient binary caries detection suitable for epidemiological surveys and rapid screening, while UniViSS offers additional lesion activity and color information valuable for treatment decisions and monitoring lesion progression when detailed characterization is required. Clinicians should exercise caution when extrapolating these performance metrics to general community settings. The study population was recruited from a tertiary care hospital with a caries prevalence of 85.3%, creating a spectrum bias that artificially elevates PPV and suppresses NPV. In populations with lower caries prevalence, the reported PPV values (0.88–0.97) may not be replicated, and the probability of false negatives increases. Findings should be interpreted within the context of the clinician's own patient population's caries prevalence.

Study limitations

Absence of histological validation is the most fundamental limitation of this study. ICDAS, used here as the reference comparator, is itself a clinical visual index validated against histological sections in laboratory studies but not against excised tissue in the present study. As a consequence, neither sensitivity nor specificity in this study can be interpreted as estimates of true diagnostic performance; they reflect inter-method concordance only. Any disagreement between the two systems is classified as an ‘error’ relative to ICDAS — which is not equivalent to a true diagnostic error. The absence of a histological gold standard means that the possibility that UniViSS correctly identifies lesions that ICDAS misses (or vice versa) cannot be ruled out. This study therefore measures agreement, not accuracy. Validation against histologically confirmed lesion status remains an essential priority for future research. Single examiner assessment limits inter-examiner reliability evaluation and generalizability across varying experience levels. Cross-sectional design prevents assessment of systems' performance in monitoring caries progression longitudinally. Visual diagnostic assessments remain subjective and examiner-dependent. Exclusive visual examination without radiographic support may have limited interproximal caries detection. Memory bias is a further concern: the 2–3 min interval between ICDAS and UniViSS assessments may not fully eliminate recall of prior scores; future studies should employ randomized system order or longer washout periods. Recruitment from a single tertiary care hospital produced a high-caries-risk population (85.3% prevalence), limiting generalizability of the reported performance metrics to community settings with lower caries prevalence, as discussed in Section 4.3. Observer bias cannot be excluded, as all assessments were performed by a single examiner and unconscious scoring tendencies are possible. Cluster-adjusted confidence intervals were derived using a cluster-stratified bootstrap procedure (3000 iterations; child as sampling unit) to account for the correlation of multiple teeth within the same child; future studies should additionally consider hierarchical or mixed-effects models for comprehensive multilevel analysis.

To quantify the maximum plausible impact of recall bias on the reported Kappa coefficients, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a proportional Kappa shrinkage approach (Kall = Ko7a × [1 − bias proportion]), in which 5% and 10% of each observed Kappa value was conservatively removed to simulate recall-inflated agreement. Under the 5% scenario, all eight teeth retained Kappa values ≥ 0.84, firmly within the ‘almost perfect agreement’ range. Under the 10% worst-case scenario — which substantially exceeds bias magnitudes reported in comparable sequential visual scoring studies — seven of eight teeth retained Kappa values ≥ 0.80 (almost perfect); tooth 85, which carried the lowest observed Kappa (0.886), yielded a bias-corrected value of 0.797, bordering the substantial/almost-perfect threshold. These results confirm that the study's primary conclusion is robust to the degree of recall bias plausibly arising from the 2–3 min inter-assessment interval. Bias-corrected values for all teeth under both scenarios are presented in Table S1.

A further statistical limitation concerns the influence of prevalence on Cohen's Kappa coefficient. In the present study, caries prevalence was 85.3%, creating a highly asymmetric marginal distribution in the 2×2 contingency tables used for each tooth. Under such conditions, Kappa is known to be sensitive to the prevalence and bias index of the rating categories — the so-called prevalence–bias effect described by Cicchetti and Feinstein (1990). Specifically, when one category (here, ‘caries present’) predominates heavily, Kappa can appear lower than the raw percent agreement would suggest, because chance agreement is itself high. Conversely, if the two systems share systematic tendencies (both over- or under-calling caries in the same direction), Kappa may overestimate true agreement relative to an independent gold standard. Readers should therefore interpret the reported Kappa values alongside the directly observed percent concordance (95–99%), sensitivity, and specificity. Future studies in populations with lower caries prevalence, or using prevalence-adjusted agreement statistics such as the Prevalence and Bias Adjusted Kappa (PABAK), would help determine whether the high Kappa values in the present study are replicable across a broader range of disease distributions.

Future research directions

Future studies should incorporate histological validation for accurate diagnostic performance assessment. Longitudinal research is needed to evaluate UniViSS's ability to monitor caries progression. Multi-center studies involving multiple examiners would provide inter-examiner reliability data. Research comparing full ordinal scales of both systems, including activity and color assessment, would offer comprehensive insights. Integration of these visual systems with radiographic examination should be investigated to optimize detection accuracy in young children. Given the high caries prevalence in the present sample (85.3%) and the known sensitivity of Cohen's Kappa to marginal distributions, future studies should additionally report prevalence-adjusted agreement statistics such as PABAK (Prevalence and Bias Adjusted Kappa) to allow more robust comparison of inter-system agreement across populations with differing caries prevalence levels.

Conclusion

UniViSS demonstrated almost perfect inter-method concordance with ICDAS (Cohen's Kappa 0.886–0.983) for binary caries detection in primary molars of toddlers and preschoolers, with excellent sensitivity (0.88–1.00), specificity (0.96–0.99), positive predictive value (0.88–0.97), and correctly classified proportions of 95–99%. UniViSS may serve as a practical clinical alternative to ICDAS for binary caries screening in pediatric dental practice. However, as this study assessed inter-system concordance with ICDAS as comparator rather than against a histological gold standard, these findings validate UniViSS for screening purposes only. The reported performance metrics are influenced by the high-prevalence study population and should be interpreted with caution in lower-prevalence community settings. Validation of UniViSS's full three-step assessment capability and histological validation remain priorities for future research.
