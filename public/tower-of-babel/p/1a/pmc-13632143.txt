Effect of continuous wave of condensation on postoperative pain using bioceramic versus resin sealers: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Merve Defişet, Merve Yeniçeri Özata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical behavior of bioceramic sealers under heat-based obturation techniques remains insufficiently explored, despite their widespread clinical use. Although these materials are generally recommended for single-cone techniques, their performance when exposed to heat during warm obturation procedures is still controversial. This randomized clinical trial aimed to evaluate postoperative pain following the use of bioceramic and epoxy resin-based sealers in combination with the continuous wave of condensation technique (CWCT), addressing a clinically relevant gap in the literature.MethodsSeventy-two patients with single-rooted teeth diagnosed with asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 24): AH Plus, TotalFill BC Sealer, and Bio-C Sealer. All treatments, including post-endodontic restorations, were completed in a single visit by a single experienced operator using the CWCT. Postoperative pain intensity was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS; 0–10), and pain incidence was recorded at 6, 24, 48, and 72 h, and 7 days. Statistical analyses included Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation, Cochran’s Q test, and binary logistic regression analysis (α = 0.05).ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed among the three sealer groups regarding postoperative pain incidence or intensity at any time point (P > 0.05). Pain incidence was highest within the first 24 h (16.7% at 6 h and 11.1% at 24 h) and decreased significantly over time (P < 0.001), with complete resolution after 48 h. None of the evaluated variables, including age, gender, jaw, or sealer type, significantly influenced postoperative pain outcomes.ConclusionNo significant difference in short-term postoperative pain was detected among the three root canal sealers when used with the continuous wave of condensation technique.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06726837, 10 December 2024.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09200-x.Keywords: Asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis, Postoperative pain, Root canal sealers, Visual analog scale, Warm obturation

Introduction

Postoperative pain following endodontic treatment is defined as a dental experience characterized by discomfort or pain, with prevalence rates reported in the literature ranging from 3% to 58% [1]. Postoperative pain is influenced by various clinical and individual factors, including age, gender, number of treatment visits, analgesic use, periapical status, tooth type, and preoperative pain, as well as instrumentation, irrigation, and obturation protocols [2].

The etiology of postoperative pain is multifactorial, arising from a complex interplay of mechanical, chemical, and microbiological factors [3]. Procedural factors, such as mechanical trauma from instruments or the extrusion of irrigation solutions and infected debris into the periapical region, can trigger tissue damage and predispose patients to pain [3]. However, beyond mechanical and microbial factors, the chemical interaction of obturation materials with periapical tissues also plays a decisive role in pain development [4]. Contact between filling materials—particularly sealers—and tissue fluids at the apical foramen, or their extrusion into the periapical space, can initiate a local inflammatory response or exacerbate existing inflammation depending on the material’s chemical composition [5].

Since root canal sealers have the potential for direct contact with periapical tissues, their constituents can modulate the inflammatory process [5, 6]. For many years, epoxy resin-based sealers such as AH Plus (Dentsply DeTrey, Konstanz, Germany) have been considered the “gold standard” due to their dimensional stability and superior sealing properties [6]. Nevertheless, it has been reported that resin-based sealers may exhibit slight cytotoxicity during the polymerization process or when freshly mixed, potentially influencing early-stage pain perception [7].

In recent years, the use of bioceramic-based (calcium silicate-based) root canal sealers has become increasingly widespread due to their biological advantages. These materials are characterized by their ability to promote hydroxyapatite formation during setting, high biocompatibility, and antibacterial effects resulting from their alkaline pH. These properties are attributed to the increase in pH via hydroxyl ion release and the promotion of bone formation through the osteoblastic differentiation induced by calcium ions [8]. It is hypothesized that the bioactive properties of bioceramic sealers may reduce postoperative pain by causing less inflammation in periapical tissues [8]. In current practice, premixed injectable bioceramic sealers, such as TotalFill BC Sealer (FKG Dentaire SA, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland) and Bio-C Sealer (Angelus, Londrina, Parana, Brazil), are frequently utilized [9].

The single-cone technique is often preferred due to its relative ease of application, cost-effectiveness, and lower dependence on operator experience [10]. However, in oval-shaped root canals, the larger area filled by the sealer may increase void formation, potentially leading to inadequate obturation [11]. Therefore, warm gutta-percha methods, such as the CWCT, are preferred to enhance obturation quality and achieve more effective penetration into canal irregularities [12]. This trend is supported by a recent multi-center survey involving endodontic clinicians from Portugal, Italy, and Türkiye, which identified warm obturation techniques as the most commonly used method [13].

While numerous studies in the literature evaluate the effect of sealers on postoperative pain using lateral condensation and single-cone techniques [14–16], data regarding the performance of bioceramic sealers in heat-applied techniques and the clinical outcomes of this combination on postoperative pain remain limited [5, 17, 18]. To date, no randomized controlled clinical trial has compared these three different chemical types of sealers (one resin, two different bioceramics) in vital teeth with AIP, specifically using warm obturation techniques.

A critical gap exists in the clinical validation of bioceramic sealers when subjected to high-temperature obturation. While laboratory data suggest that some bioceramics maintain their physicochemical properties up to 200 °C, the clinical reflection of this—specifically regarding postoperative pain—remains largely unexplored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to directly compare the clinical outcomes of a gold-standard resin sealer with two different types of premixed bioceramic sealers using the CWCT in teeth with asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis (AIP).

The aim of this study was to compare the effects of an epoxy resin-based sealer (AH Plus) and two bioceramic-based sealers (TotalFill BC Sealer and Bio-C Sealer) on postoperative pain when used with the CWCT in vital teeth undergoing single-visit root canal treatment. Furthermore, this study sought to provide clinically relevant evidence regarding the use of bioceramic sealers under heat-based obturation conditions. The study hypotheses were as follows:

Null Hypothesis (H0): The type of root canal sealer used will have no statistically significant effect on the level of postoperative pain.

Null Hypothesis (H0): The type of root canal sealer used will have no statistically significant effect on the level of postoperative pain.

Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Regardless of the sealer type, the incidence and severity of postoperative pain will be significantly higher within the first 24 hours following treatment compared to other measurement time points.

Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Regardless of the sealer type, the incidence and severity of postoperative pain will be significantly higher within the first 24 hours following treatment compared to other measurement time points.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This parallel, single-blind, randomized controlled clinical trial was conducted at the Dicle University Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Endodontics, in accordance with the PRIRATE (Preferred Reporting Items for Randomized Trials in Endodontics) guidelines (Fig. 1) [19] and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Harran University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Ref: 04/26.08.2024) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06726837, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06726837; First posted: 10 December 2024). Patient recruitment was conducted between December 2024 and August 2025. The reporting of this study adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for clinical trials.

PRIRATE flowchart of patient randomization, allocation, and follow-up. Adapted from: Nagendrababu V, Duncan HF, Bjørndal L, et al. Patients aged 18–65 years who presented without preoperative pain were screened for eligibility. Inclusion criteria were: (i) diagnosis of AIP in single-rooted and single-canalled permanent teeth, (ii) absence of periapical radiolucency, (iii) prolonged pain response to cold testing (Super Spray Endo Ice, Seçkin Dental, Istanbul, Türkiye) and electric pulp testing (C-Pulse Pulp Tester, Coxo, Foshan, China), (iv) absence of tenderness to percussion or palpation, and (v) good systemic health [ASA I or ASA II (controlled hypertension and controlled type II diabetes)]. AIP was diagnosed based on the absence of symptoms, increased and/or prolonged response to cold testing, and the presence of deep caries extending to the pulp chamber on radiographs [20]. If pulpal perforation occurred during caries removal and bleeding did not stop within 5 min despite the application of a NaOCl-soaked cotton pellet, this was considered an additional diagnostic criterion.

Teeth with open apices, root resorption, or calcification; previously treated teeth; teeth with periodontal pockets > 4 mm; patients who used analgesics/antibiotics within the last 7 days; or pregnant/lactating patients were excluded.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power version 3.1.9.6 (Heinrich-Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a similar study [21], with an effect size of 0.412, a 5% margin of error (α = 0.05), and 95% power, a minimum of 20 patients per group was required. Accounting for a potential 20% attrition rate, a total of 72 patients (24 per group) were included.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 24) using computer-based block randomization (www.randomizer.org). Randomization was performed by a second investigator who was not involved in the clinical procedures. Participants were enrolled by the treating operator. The assignment sequence was concealed from the operator until the obturation stage. Due to the different physical forms of the sealers (powder/paste mixing vs. syringe), blinding the operator was not possible. However, a single-blind design was implemented by ensuring patients remained unaware of the material used.

Clinical procedures

All treatments were completed in a single visit by one experienced endodontist. Following local anesthesia (2% lidocaine, 1:80,000 epinephrine; Onkain, Onfarma, Istanbul, Türkiye) and rubber dam isolation, caries were removed, and access cavities were prepared. Pulp vitality was confirmed by observing bleeding from the canal orifice. A glide path was established with a #10 K-file (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland), and apical patency was ensured. Working length was determined using a #15 K-file with an endodontic motor’s integrated apex locator (EndoRadar Pro; Woodpecker, Guilin, China) and confirmed radiographically at 0.5 mm short of the radiographic apex.

Root canal shaping and irrigation

Canals were shaped using the T-Endo MUST reciprocal file system (Dentac, Istanbul, Türkiye) with an EndoRadar Pro motor, operating at 160° counter-clockwise and 40° clockwise, at 300 rpm and 2.0 Ncm torque. Based on the initial apical diameter, canals were enlarged to size m25 (25/0.06), m40 (40/0.04), or m50 (50/0.04). A new file was used for each patient. Irrigation was performed using a 30-gauge side-vented closed-ended needle (TruNatomy; Dentsply Sirona, Tulsa, OK) placed 2 mm short of the working length. 2 mL of 2.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) was used after each file change, with a total volume of approximately 20 mL per tooth. The final irrigation protocol included 5 mL of 17% EDTA (1 min), 5 mL of 2.5% NaOCl (1 min), and 10 mL of distilled water. During final irrigation, both EDTA and NaOCl were activated using a sonic device with a polymer tip (Scope; Gtech Dizayn Dental, Yozgat, Türkiye) for three 20-second cycles (total 1 min) each.

Obturation protocol

Canals were dried with sterile paper points corresponding to the final apical preparation size, namely m25-, m40-, or m50-matched paper points according to the final file used. In the bioceramic groups (TotalFill BC Sealer and Bio-C Sealer), canal moisture was standardized by inserting one corresponding paper point to the working length for 5 s. Drying was then terminated after this single paper point. The canal was considered “slightly moist” when no visible free fluid remained and the final paper point showed slight moisture without being completely wet. In the resin-based sealer group (AH Plus), corresponding paper points were inserted for 5 s each until the final paper point emerged completely dry. All drying procedures were performed by a single calibrated operator. All canals were filled using the CWCT. A master gutta-percha cone corresponding to the final apical preparation size was seated at the working length. The down-pack procedure was performed using an eHeater device (Bomedent eHeater; Bomedent Medical Technology, China) set at 200 °C. For the down-pack procedure, heated plugger/heating needle tips of #40/0.04, #45/0.04, #50/0.04, and #55/0.08 were used. Before activation, these tips were passively tried in each canal. The appropriate tip was selected according to the final canal preparation size and taper, based on its ability to passively advance to 5 mm short of the working length without binding. After confirmation of the appropriate tip fit, the device was activated and the master gutta-percha cone was severed at this level. According to the manufacturer’s automatic heating cycle, the heating needle is activated for a maximum of 5 s per cycle and then enters the cooling mode. In most canals, the gutta-percha cone was severed with a single activation; in rare cases, a second activation was required. Since no canal required more than two activations, the cumulative active thermal exposure time was limited to a maximum of 10 s per canal. The remaining apical gutta-percha was then vertically compacted using appropriate hand pluggers. The remainder of the canal was back-filled using a warm gutta-percha gun (Bomedent eFiller) set at the same temperature. Additional vertical compaction was performed with appropriate hand pluggers to ensure homogeneous adaptation of the warm gutta-percha to the canal walls. Immediately after obturation, all teeth were restored during the same visit with a glass ionomer base and composite resin (Palfique Estelite LX5; Tokuyama, Japan). Occlusal adjustments were made to prevent premature contact. Postoperative radiographs were taken to verify quality. All patients were prescribed 600 mg ibuprofen and were instructed to take it only in cases of severe pain and to record any analgesic intake on the VAS form.

Postoperative pain assessment

Patients were given a 10 cm Visual Analog Scale (VAS) form (0: no pain, 10: unbearable pain) to record pain intensity at 6, 24, 48, 72 h, and 7 days. Automatic SMS reminders were sent at each time point to enhance data reliability.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v23.0 (IBM Corp). Normal distribution was checked. Categorical variables were examined using Fisher’s exact test and Monte Carlo simulation. Cochran’s Q test assessed pain changes over time within groups. Binary logistic regression was used to determine the impact of independent variables (age, gender, jaw, tooth type, sealer) on pain presence. Significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 100 patients were assessed for eligibility; 28 were excluded due to criteria or refusal. Seventy-two patients [50 females (mean age: 38.2), 22 males (mean age: 41.4)] were randomized into three groups (n = 24 each). All participants completed follow-ups; no data were lost. The patient flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1.

Demographic characteristics (gender, age, jaw, tooth type, region) are summarized in Table 2. No significant differences existed between groups regarding gender, age, tooth type, or region (P > 0.05). However, the distribution of teeth between the upper and lower jaws differed significantly (P = 0.042), with more mandibular teeth in the TotalFill BC Sealer group and more maxillary teeth in the AH Plus group. Discussion

The primary aim of this randomized controlled clinical trial was to evaluate the effect of an epoxy resin-based sealer (AH Plus) and two bioceramic-based sealers (TotalFill BC Sealer and Bio-C Sealer) on postoperative pain following single-visit root canal treatment in teeth diagnosed with AIP. Results showed no statistically significant difference between groups in terms of pain incidence or intensity at any time point. Therefore, the null hypothesis was accepted.

The lack of significant difference between resin-based and bioceramic-based sealers in the present study is consistent with various recent randomized clinical trials [5, 14, 15, 18]. In line with these findings, Graunaite et al. [5] compared AH Plus and TotalFill BC Sealer using the warm vertical condensation technique in asymptomatic teeth and reported no significant difference in postoperative pain. Similarly, Drumond et al. [18] found no significant differences in early postoperative pain levels when evaluating EndoSequence BC, Bio-C Sealer, and AH Plus.

In another randomized clinical trial [14] involving single-visit treatment of asymptomatic vital and non-vital teeth, bioceramic sealers (MTA-Bioseal, GuttaFlow Bioseal, and NeoSealer Flo) were compared with AH Plus; no significant differences in pain levels were found between sealer types in either vital or non-vital groups. Furthermore, another study [15] compared AH Plus with ADseal, EndoSeal, and CeraSeal using the single-cone technique; despite differences in chemical compositions and obturation protocols compared to our study, no significant differences were observed in pain incidence or severity.

To better understand this clinical consensus, the underlying biological mechanisms must be considered. Postoperative pain is viewed as an indicator of the local inflammatory response in periapical tissues. This process is associated with the release of biochemical mediators, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the irritating effects of chemical components released during the sealer’s setting phase [8]. While freshly mixed epoxy resin sealers like AH Plus exhibit limited cytotoxic effects that diminish significantly after setting [7], bioceramic sealers are noted for their high biocompatibility and bioactivity [8]. The lack of difference in our study may be attributed to strict procedural controls: maintaining apical patency, using side-vented needles to prevent irrigant extrusion, and the absence of sealer extrusion in all cases. Given the limited contact area with periapical tissues and the dilution of toxic components by tissue fluids, potential irritants likely failed to manifest as clinically significant pain [22].

Literature suggests that sealer extrusion is an exacerbating factor rather than the sole determinant of postoperative pain. Some studies comparing sealers reported that while extrusion occurred in both bioceramic and resin groups, pain was more frequently associated with the latter. For instance, Ensinas et al. [23] reported higher 24-hour pain levels in the AH Plus group, attributing this to a more pronounced inflammatory response following extrusion in cases with high bacterial loads. Similarly, Abada et al. [24] observed higher pain in the AH Plus group at 12 and 48 h, explaining that the cytotoxic potential of resin sealers becomes clinically evident upon extrusion. In our study, no extrusion was observed, likely due to the inclusion of only primary treatments without periapical radiolucency, minimizing risks of inflammatory root resorption or over-instrumentation [25]. Furthermore, despite maintaining patency, preserving the anatomical integrity of the foramen and ensuring master cone “tug-back” likely served as barriers against extrusion.

The biological effects on trigeminal nociceptors also play a role. Fresh epoxy resin sealers can stimulate CGRP release, whereas both resin and bioceramic sealers suppress it after setting [26]. Since our first assessment was at 6 h—coinciding with the average setting time of AH Plus—this may explain the similar pain levels across groups. This is further supported by in vivo histological evidence showing similar intraosseous biological responses to both resin and calcium silicate sealers [27]. Confining filling materials within the canal system likely minimized chemical irritation.

A significant finding was the temporal pattern of pain. Incidence peaked at 6 h (16.7%) and 24 h (11.1%), with all symptoms resolving by 48 h. This confirms the incidence component of the alternative hypothesis. This transient model is consistent with temporary periapical inflammation triggered by ROS production during the initial 24 h of setting [8]. The complete disappearance of pain after 48 h highlights the efficacy of the single-visit protocol and chemomechanical cleaning, paralleling other studies [5, 23]. However, the “pain severity” component of H1 could not be directly tested; since most patients reported zero pain at later intervals, statistical comparison of mean VAS scores was not feasible. Instead, pain was analyzed as a dichotomous “presence/absence” variable, demonstrating the protocol’s clinical effectiveness.

Premixed calcium silicate–based root canal sealers require moisture to perform their hydration and setting reactions [28]. Accordingly, the manufacturers of both TotalFill BC Sealer and Bio-C Sealer recommend that the canal should not be excessively dried before obturation and indicate that residual dentinal moisture contributes to the setting process [29, 30]. The literature also reports that calcium silicate–based sealers can benefit from residual dentinal moisture due to their hydrophilic nature, and that slightly moist canal conditions may support the hydration reaction of these materials and their interaction with dentin [31, 32]. Therefore, in the present study, visible free fluid was removed in the bioceramic groups; however, complete drying was avoided in order to preserve residual dentinal moisture.

A distinctive feature of this study was the use of the continuous wave of condensation for all groups, including bioceramics. Although the single-cone technique has traditionally been recommended for bioceramic sealers, laboratory studies have reported that some premixed calcium silicate–based sealers remain stable after heating up to 200 °C without undergoing permanent chemical degradation, indicating a certain degree of tolerance to heat application [17]. Furthermore, their use in conjunction with warm vertical compaction has been shown to enhance sealer penetration and improve adaptation to dentinal walls [33]. The present study addresses an important clinical concern regarding this technique: the potential effect of bioceramic sealers on postoperative symptoms when used under warm obturation conditions. Unlike the single-cone technique, the CWCT involves the application of thermal energy, which could theoretically accelerate the setting reaction or affect the chemical structure of the sealer. However, in the present study, under the defined CWCT protocol, short-term postoperative pain in the Bio-C Sealer and TotalFill BC Sealer groups was not increased compared with the AH Plus group.

In this context, thermoplastic obturation approaches, such as warm carrier-based obturation, warm vertical compaction, continuous wave compaction, and thermomechanical compaction, should be considered together in terms of their effects on the clinical performance of bioceramic sealers, postoperative pain, and sealer extrusion. Spinelli et al. used a premixed tricalcium silicate-containing bioceramic sealer with a warm carrier-based obturation technique and reported that postoperative pain, assessed using the VAS, was mostly absent or mild despite a sealer extrusion rate of 47%. Moreover, the association between pain-free cases and higher 12-month periapical healing rates suggests that early postoperative symptoms may also be clinically relevant during follow-up [34]. In the study by Drumond et al., performed using the warm vertical compaction technique, EndoSequence BC Sealer and Bio-C Sealer showed postoperative pain scores similar to and as low as those observed with AH Plus in cases with apical extrusion. This finding suggests that the use of bioceramic sealers with warm vertical compaction may not pose an additional risk in terms of postoperative pain compared with resin-based sealers [18]. In a recent randomized clinical trial, the C-Root SP bioceramic sealer was evaluated using single-cone, warm vertical compaction, and cold lateral condensation techniques. Although the pain rate on the first day was higher in the warm vertical compaction group than in the C-Root SP/single-cone group, pain decreased over time in all groups, and no significant differences were reported among the groups in terms of 1-year success rates or changes in PAI scores [35]. Regarding the thermomechanical compaction technique, de Oliveira et al. compared Bio-C Sealer and AH Plus Jet and reported no significant difference between the sealers in terms of postoperative pain or 12-month healing outcomes. However, the finding that extrusion of filling material increased the risk of postoperative pain indicates that apical control is important for patient comfort when warm obturation techniques are used [36]. Taken together, the available findings support that the use of CWCT with bioceramic sealers does not create an additional disadvantage in terms of postoperative pain and that low pain levels should be interpreted mainly in relation to material properties and controlled apical boundary conditions.

Recent literature clarifies that warm obturation does not significantly increase pain incidence or severity [25, 37], as actual contact temperatures in the canal are often lower than nominal settings and periapical blood flow dissipates heat [38]. Thus, the results here are primarily attributed to the sealers’ properties rather than the obturation technique. Regarding demographics, younger patients (< 30 years) showed a non-significant trend toward higher pain (OR: 2.682). This might be linked to wider, more permeable dentin tubules in young teeth and a denser pulpal nerve network that diminishes with age [39].

The study minimized bias by including only pain-free patients with AIP, limiting the age range, focusing on single-canalled teeth, and utilizing a single operator. However, limitations include the single-blind design (operator blinding was impossible), the subjective nature of pain assessment, the lack of direct measurement of the actual intracanal temperature during CWCT, the absence of a split-mouth design for ethical/practical reasons, and the fact that the results are specific to vital teeth. Although the device was set at 200 °C and the heat application protocol was standardized, the actual temperature reached at the sealer–gutta-percha–dentin interface was not recorded; therefore, thermal exposure should be interpreted according to the defined clinical protocol rather than the nominal device temperature alone.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, no statistically significant difference in short-term postoperative pain during the 7-day follow-up period was detected among AH Plus, TotalFill BC Sealer, and Bio-C Sealer when used with the CWCT. However, since no sealer extrusion beyond the apex was observed in any case, the findings should be interpreted with caution and may not be directly generalizable to cases involving sealer extrusion.
