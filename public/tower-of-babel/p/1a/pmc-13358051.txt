Tumor heterogeneity: development, mechanisms, and therapeutic implications
Authors: Jianhong Zhang, Heng Li, Shuo Ru, Hui Feng, Junsong Guo, Danlei Sun, Tingxiu Xiang, Daqian Xu, Yang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tumor heterogeneity is a fundamental hallmark of cancer that drives progression, metastasis and therapeutic resistance. This diversity originates from persistent genomic instability, dynamic clonal evolution, and cancer stem cell plasticity, which are further amplified through complex crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment. While conventional therapies effectively eliminate certain tumor cell populations, residual resistant subclones frequently lead to disease relapse. Recent breakthroughs in single-cell multi-omics, spatial transcriptomics, and liquid biopsy now enable a comprehensive, multidimensional dissection of tumor heterogeneity across molecular, cellular, spatial, and temporal scales. These approaches reveal real-time dynamics of tumor evolution, providing new opportunities for therapeutic intervention. This review synthesizes key advances in understanding tumor heterogeneity: from its cellular origins and molecular mechanisms to its multidimensional manifestation in the tumor microenvironment and from metastatic heterogeneity to the fundamental causes of treatment resistance. We critically examine how these insights are informing novel therapeutic paradigms—including targeting clonal cooperative networks, modulating epigenetic plasticity and reprogramming metabolic adaptations. Finally, we outline future pathways toward precision medicine through the integration of multi-omics data and dynamic monitoring technologies. Ultimately, overcoming tumor heterogeneity necessitates reconceptualizing cancer not as a static collection of cells but as a dynamically evolving ecosystem.

Introduction

The molecular nature of cancer is highly variable, with cellular differences not only between patients but also within the same patient and within the same tumor. Detailed mapping of genetic, epigenetic, and transcriptional alterations across diverse tumors can reveal the full extent of this variation.1,2 As a highly dynamic disease characterized by plasticity, cancer evolves rapidly, becoming increasingly heterogeneous at the genetic, phenotypic and pathological levels.3 Heterogeneity thus represents one of the key features of most tumors, reflecting the diverse subpopulations of tumor cells with distinct characteristics and posing a significant challenge in cancer ecosystems.4

Previous studies identified distinct subpopulations of malignant cells within a single tumor, suggesting that carcinogenesis follows a complex and divergent evolution of genetic mutations.5 Nonetheless, this concept has evolved significantly with advancements in cancer research over time.6 Recent findings suggest that tumor heterogeneity arises from the clonal expansion of genetically altered cells interacting with the tumor microenvironment (TME), giving rise to genetic and epigenetic diversity shaped by both Darwinian and non-Darwinian evolutionary trajectories.7 Accordingly, tumor heterogeneity is currently defined as the differences between cancer cells within a single tumor, between primary and metastatic tumors, or even between tumors of the same type in different patients.7 It arises primarily from cancer cell-intrinsic genetic variation, as well as variations in cellular properties and intrinsic plasticity.8 These differences contribute to distinct cellular subpopulations, each exhibiting unique biological behaviors.9 Such complexity and diversity drive tumor heterogeneity and exacerbate metastasis and treatment resistance,10 highlighting the critical roles of heterogeneity in cancer research and the advancement of personalized treatment strategies.11

Heterogeneity within tumors manifests in different forms, including intratumor heterogeneity (ITH).12 ITH consists of both spatial and temporal heterogeneity, collectively referred to as spatiotemporal heterogeneity.13 The former describes genetic differences across various anatomical regions of the same tumor or between primary and metastatic lesions, as revealed by multi-region genome sequencing.14 The latter, by contrast, involves significant genetic changes that occur within a tumor over time, as demonstrated by accelerated longitudinal studies.15 Variations in heterogeneity can also occur among tumors of the same histopathological subtype, termed inter-tumor heterogeneity.16 This form of variability manifests as either interpatient heterogeneity, where tumors of the same histopathological subtype exhibit molecular differences across distinct individuals, or intra-patient heterogeneity, characterized by divergent genomic or phenotypic features among spatially or temporally separated tumors within a single patient.

Therefore, tumor heterogeneity is a multilevel phenomenon spanning molecular, cellular, tissue, and clinical scales, arising from genetic instability, epigenetic dysregulation, and transcriptional reprogramming.17 These molecular events shape the diverse phenotypes and functional states of cancer cells.18 At the cellular level, stem-like and differentiated malignant cells, together with diverse immune and stromal components of the microenvironment, form a dynamic “tumor ecosystem” that drives adaptive evolution and underlies immune evasion, metabolic reprogramming, and therapy resistance.19,20 Tissue- and organ-level heterogeneity is reflected in regional differences in angiogenesis, hypoxia, and stromal composition, imposing selective pressures that foster clonal selection and phenotypic plasticity.21,22 Collectively, tumor heterogeneity defines an evolving cancer ecosystem that not only limits the efficacy of uniform therapeutic strategies but also underscores the necessity of precision oncology. A detailed understanding of its molecular mechanisms, evolutionary dynamics, and clinical implications is therefore essential for improving diagnosis, prognostication, and personalized treatment strategies. In this review, we discuss tumor heterogeneity at the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels and its impact on progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response. We emphasize how interactions between cancer cells and the microenvironment drive the emergence and evolution of heterogeneity and synthesize key insights from multi-omics studies that illuminate its mechanistic basis, inform precision diagnosis and prognostication and guide the development of novel, individualized therapeutic strategies.

Tumor heterogeneity: a century of evolving understanding

The concept of tumor heterogeneity has been recognized for more than a century, representing a continuous deepening of our understanding of cancer’s intrinsic complexity (Fig. 1). In the late 19th century, with the advent of microscopy, pathologists such as Rudolf Virchow documented the morphological diversity within tumors through microscopic examination, challenging the notion of tumors as homogeneous entities.23 This morphological diversity laid the foundation for recognizing tumors as complex, multicellular systems. By the mid-20th century, the work of Sajiro Makino and others provided direct evidence of genetic heterogeneity by identifying chromosomal abnormalities (aneuploidy) in cancer cells, linking these alterations to tumorigenesis and progression.24 In 1976, Nowell’s clonal evolution model conceptualized tumors as dynamic ecosystems evolving under selective pressures, establishing the theoretical basis for genetic heterogeneity.25 With the rise of molecular biology and large-scale genomic projects such as the Human Genome Project and TCGA, research has moved from morphology to molecular mechanisms. However, with the advent of high-throughput technologies, it has become evident that genetic mutations alone cannot fully account for the complex phenotypes of tumors. In the second decade of the 21st century, single-cell and multi-omics technologies sparked a paradigm shift in the study of tumor heterogeneity. In 2009, the single-cell mRNA transcriptomics analysis technology was developed, ushering in a new era of single-cell sequencing. This breakthrough revealed the existence of multiple functional subpopulations within tumors, driven primarily by epigenetic regulation and transcriptional plasticity rather than fixed genetic alterations.26,27 Concurrently, spatial transcriptomics, spatial metabolomics, and imaging mass spectrometry expanded the field from the “single-cell” to the “spatial tissue” dimension, revealing the organized architecture of the TME.28 Gradients of oxygen tension, nutrient availability, and immune cell distribution shape distinct evolutionary trajectories across spatial niches. Longitudinal sampling and lineage tracing further demonstrated that therapeutic interventions reshape clonal architectures and induce new resistant subclones, defining the concept of temporal heterogeneity. Today, the integration of artificial intelligence with multi-omics analytics is propelling heterogeneity research toward prediction and intervention. In the future, the ability to precisely capture and modulate tumor heterogeneity across time and space will determine whether precision medicine can truly be achieved.

The origins of tumor heterogeneity

As mentioned above, the concept of tumor heterogeneity has been investigated for centuries. However, the origins of tumor heterogeneity remain incompletely understood. To address this complexity, several prominent theories have been proposed. Genomic instability

Genomic instability, a widely recognized characteristic of cancer, drives tumor proliferation, metastasis, and oncogenic transformation while acting as a central engine of genetic heterogeneity through the multilineage evolution of unstable cancer cells.29,30 It includes a spectrum of aberrations, ranging from structural abnormalities such as mutations and chromosomal rearrangements to numerical alterations that involve the gain or loss of entire chromosomes, all rooted in distinct mutational processes.30 At the macroscale, centrosome anomalies that result in numerical and/or structural chromosomal alterations contribute to chromosomal instability (CIN).31 At the microscale, genomic instability is further reflected in mutations, copy number variations (CNVs), single nucleotide variants, microsatellite instability and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs).32

Tumors are composed of genetically diverse subclones, and CIN contributes to tumor heterogeneity by driving genetic diversity and aneuploidy.31,33–35 CIN enhances malignancy by fostering karyotype diversity, wherein aneuploid tumors maintain a dominant karyotype while harboring heterogeneous chromosomal complements that favor adaptation under selective pressures.36 Specific karyotypes provide selective advantages by altering gene dosage or expression, supporting tumor evolution and progression. Aneuploidy, long recognized as a hallmark of tumorigenesis,37 is observed in approximately 90% of solid tumors and 50% of hematopoietic cancers,38,39 making it more prevalent than any specific gene mutation. Although aneuploidy is typically deleterious during development, leading to spontaneous abortion or disorders, cancer cells paradoxically exploit it to sustain clonal expansion and therapy resistance.40 Beyond aneuploidy, CIN accelerates heterogeneity by inducing chromosomal rearrangements, micronuclei formation and epigenetic reprogramming.31

Genomic instability, through the continuous generation of new genetic variants, provides an evolutionary basis for tumor cells to adapt to different microenvironments and therapeutic pressures. Recent single-cell multi-omics studies have shown that even within the same TME, neighboring tumor cells may have significantly different genomic instability profiles, explaining why therapeutic responses are heterogeneous even under uniform treatment regimens.41 Meanwhile, CNVs arising from recombination, replication errors and repair-associated processes42 exhibit strong associations with clinical outcomes, as exemplified in ovarian,43,44 endometrial45 and breast cancer.46 Similarly, SNVs are the most common genetic variants in the genome, occurring at specific locations in the genome and playing a key role in the evolution of tumor heterogeneity.

Genetic polymorphisms are key determinants shaping both intertumor and intratumor heterogeneity. Polymorphisms, defined as variations in DNA sequences, are the most frequently occurring genetic variation in the human population.47 Unlike rare mutations, these variants exhibit distinct patterns of inheritance and functional consequences. SNPs located in various gene regions can affect gene expression through mechanisms, such as promoter activity alterations,48 transcription factor binding,49 DNA methylation,50 and histone modifications,51 thereby directly influencing tumor susceptibility and disease progression. Recent studies have also highlighted noncoding SNPs as key regulators of cancer susceptibility, particularly in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma.52 Mechanistically, these SNPs regulate gene expression by influencing chromatin conformation, miRNA binding and long noncoding RNA activity, such as HOTAIR.52 They not only determine cancer risk but also act as “seed events” that shape the accumulation pathway of somatic mutations, ultimately driving macroscopic heterogeneity in molecular subtypes, TME and treatment responses among patients.53 Therefore, genetic polymorphisms play a foundational role in promoting the formation and evolution of tumor heterogeneity.

Microsatellites, also known as short tandem repeats, consist of 1–6 nucleotide motifs scattered across the genome.54 They arise from DNA slippage during replication or strand mismatches, which are normally corrected by mismatch repair. Deficiency in mismatch repair generates microsatellites instability, characterized by accumulated replication errors.55 Microsatellites instability markedly increase the somatic mutation burden and contribute to neoplastic transformation, particularly in colorectal and other cancers with this distinctive phenotype, and they now serve as both a diagnostic biomarker and a predictor of response to immunotherapy.56

Clonal evolution hypothesis

Tumor heterogeneity is shaped by clonal evolution—a dynamic process in which cancer cells originating from a single progenitor accumulate genetic and epigenetic alterations over time. These alterations generate subclonal populations with varying fitness, leading to Darwinian selection favoring adaptive traits such as drug resistance or metastatic potential. This framework, rooted in Darwin’s theory of natural selection, was formalized in oncology by Peter Nowell to explain ITH,25 a hallmark of cancer characterized by coexisting genetically distinct subclones. ITH therefore represents not only tumor adaptability but also a central challenge for therapy, as it fosters treatment resistance and phenotypic plasticity.

Following tumor initiation, cancer remains highly dynamic, exhibiting diverse evolutionary patterns across tumor types, individual patients and metastatic sites,57 including linear, branching, neutral, punctuated, convergent, parallel and cancer stem cell (CSC) evolution.58 Linear evolution describes sequential mutations leading to the dominance of a single clonal lineage,59 while branching evolution reflects the coexistence and divergence of multiple subclones.60 Recent studies underscore the role of neutral evolution in certain cancers—including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), colorectal cancer and breast cancer (ER⁺/HER2⁻ subtypes)—where genetic alterations accumulate without selective advantages, enabling the coexistence of subclones with comparable fitness.61 In addition, macroevolutionary jumps,7,62 incongruent genetic patterns,63 cellular plasticity,64 and the TME65,66 collectively illustrate that clonal dynamics are shaped by both intrinsic genomic events and extrinsic tumor–host interactions, thereby strengthening the conceptual link between evolutionary trajectories and clinical heterogeneity.

Although liquid biopsy and histological analysis provide insights for tumor assessment, a single indicator alone cannot fully reflect the complex dynamics within a tumor. Tumor heterogeneity complicates these assessments, as genetically distinct subpopulations, or clones, within the same tumor exhibit diverse proliferation rates and evolutionary trajectories.67,68 In the TME, proliferative clones gain dominance through competition for resources and evasion of immune surveillance, with PD-L1 regulation and TNF-α signaling as key examples of adaptive advantage.69,70 Highly proliferating clones also shed more circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), serving as a marker for liquid biopsy.71 However, conventional pathological assessments often average across bulk samples, masking subclonal differences in proliferation. This averaging obscures the dynamic nature of tumor growth, limiting precision oncology by neglecting proliferative hierarchies and their therapeutic vulnerabilities.

In summary, clonal evolution provides a robust framework for understanding the complexity of cancer biology. It integrates principles of evolutionary theory with molecular insights, forming a foundation for therapeutic innovation.72 However, the translation of ITH concepts into clinical strategies—such as preventing the competitive release of resistant subclones—remains underdeveloped. To address this, multidimensional approaches are needed: dynamic monitoring via ctDNA and single-cell multi-omics,73,74 targeted disruption of clonal cooperation and epigenetic reprogramming, and adaptive therapies such as metabolism- or ecDNA-directed interventions combined with immune microenvironment remodeling.75–77 At the infrastructure level, establishing cross-cancer ITH mapping databases, AI-based prediction models and open-source computational pipelines (EvoFreq, PhyloWGS), together with validation in PDOs and rapid autopsy samples, is essential. Standardized clinical assays (liquid biopsy) and adaptive trial designs (basket/umbrella trials) will further enable the incorporation of ITH into therapeutic decision-making, providing concrete pathways for translating evolutionary theory into clinical impact.

Cancer stem cell plasticity

CSCs constitute a minor but critical subpopulation within tumors, playing a central role in driving cellular heterogeneity in cancer.78 These CSCs are notorious for being capable of self-renewal to maintain the stem cell pool and differentiation to generate tumor cell heterogeneity, thereby driving tumor diversity and expansion.79,80 CSCs demonstrate asymmetric division, enabling the generation of both self-renewing CSCs and differentiated progeny.81 They can also undergo epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and its reversible counterpart,82 mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET), which enhances their plasticity and stem-like properties.83 Moreover, although CSCs are typically quiescent in the G0 phase, they can shift to a proliferative state, as observed in glioblastoma post-chemotherapy, underscoring their role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance.84,85 Although they share some markers with normal stem cells, CSCs exhibit distinct features, including enhanced migratory and adhesive properties, and are often described as tumor-initiating cells.86 Additionally, some theories suggest that CSCs revert to an atavistic state under stress, adopting ancestral cellular behaviors that contribute to tumor plasticity.87

The hierarchical organization theory posits that tumors are structured with CSCs at the apex, capable of both self-renewal and differentiation into the diverse cell types that constitute the tumor.88 CSCs reside at the top of a cellular hierarchy and contribute to the tumor ecosystem through two primary mechanisms: 1) direct differentiation into heterogeneous populations of differentiated tumor cells89,90 and certain vascular-associated cells91,92; and 2) active remodeling of the TME by secreting factors that recruit and “educate” host-derived mesenchymal/stromal cells93 and immunomodulatory cells,94 which in turn provide structural support, mediate immunosuppression, and facilitate nutrient supply. Together, these cell types constitute the complex ecosystem of the tumor, and their interactions determine the malignant phenotype and treatment resistance of the tumor. This theory highlights CSCs as the primary drivers of tumor growth and long-term progression, presenting them as critical therapeutic targets. Complementarily, the clonal evolution model emphasizes genetic heterogeneity and tumor adaptability, underscoring the complexity of CSC-targeted therapies.25

In recent studies, CSCs are commonly identified using surface markers such as CD133,95 CD44,96 and EpCAM97, together with functional markers such as ALDH,98,99 which vary across cancer types. Well-established examples include CD34⁺/CD38⁻ in acute myeloid leukemia (AML)100 and LGR5 in colorectal and gastric cancers.101 Transcription factors, such as OCT4,102 Nanog signaling,103 and SOX2,104 also serve as markers, maintaining CSC properties and promoting chemoresistance. Despite their critical role in tumor maintenance, controversies remain about the applicability of the CSC model across all cancers, primarily due to heterogeneity arising from variability in CSC markers and the challenges of accurately estimating tumorigenic cell frequencies.85

CSCs are closely associated with therapy resistance, which is categorized into intrinsic and acquired mechanisms.105,106 Intrinsic resistance originates from preexisting cellular factors, whereas acquired resistance emerges as a consequence of therapeutic exposure.107 By expressing stemness-associated markers, CSCs contribute to tumor recurrence and metastatic progression, aligning with the “seed and soil” hypothesis.108 Their plasticity, regenerative capacity and ability to evade conventional therapies underscore the necessity of targeting CSCs for more effective cancer treatments.109 Therapeutic strategies under investigation include targeting key CSC signaling pathways, cell surface markers110 and metabolic vulnerabilities, as well as utilizing immunotherapy and viral therapies. However, CSCs’ phenotypic diversity and adaptive capabilities present significant challenges.111 Emerging technologies, such as single-cell sequencing112 and CRISPR-Cas9 screening,113 are advancing our understanding of CSC biology, offering novel avenues for therapeutic intervention.

In conclusion, CSCs represent a cornerstone of tumor biology, where their unique features drive tumor heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance and recurrence. Despite substantial progress, challenges remain, including the identification of robust CSC markers, the development of assays to accurately measure tumorigenic potential and the integration of CSC-targeted therapies with existing treatment regimens. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms governing CSC function and plasticity will enhance the development of targeted therapies, paving the way for more effective and durable cancer treatments.

Tumor heterogeneity mechanisms and types

Tumor heterogeneity is manifested not only at the cellular level but also at the molecular, tissue and organ levels. This reflects the diversity of tumor cells in terms of genes, epigenetics, metabolism and signaling pathways, and it is maintained through interwoven mechanisms that preserve the tumor’s biological characteristics. At the molecular level, tumor heterogeneity is primarily driven by gene mutations, epigenetic changes and dynamic interactions between signaling pathways, all of which influence tumor initiation, progression and drug resistance. In contrast, cellular heterogeneity is exhibited through lineage diversity, CSC transdifferentiation and metabolic pathway differences. The complex interaction between CSCs and the TME increases the possibility of tumor invasiveness, adaptability and therapeutic tolerance. Thus, the multilevel heterogeneity of tumors is a complex biological phenomenon involving interactions across molecular, cellular and microenvironmental levels. A deeper understanding of tumor heterogeneity at each level and the underlying mechanisms provides new strategies for personalized tumor treatment and offers theoretical support for overcoming tumor drug resistance and treatment failure.

Tumor heterogeneity at the molecular level

The molecular heterogeneity of tumors is primarily manifested at the genomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic levels, with its complexity directly governing tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Such molecular-level heterogeneity constitutes the very foundation of tumor heterogeneity and represents a central challenge in precision oncology.

Genetic heterogeneity

Genetic heterogeneity refers to variations in DNA sequences among tumor cells within a single tumor or across different patients, which primarily arises from the process of clonal evolution in cancer.114 Early mutations may establish the founding clone, while subsequent branching mutations further generate subclones, resulting in a multilayered, polyclonal architecture. This subclonal structure profoundly influences tumor invasiveness, metastatic potential, and drug tolerance. For instance, in non-small cell lung cancer, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) driver mutations may reside in the dominant clone, whereas T790M resistance mutations are often confined to subclones, thereby leading to localized failure of targeted therapies.115

Early cancer research has predominantly focused on its genetic foundations, particularly tumor suppressor gene inactivation and oncogene activation. Clinicians increasingly use tumor mutations and gene expression profiles to diagnose cancer, guide treatment and make prognostic decisions. The effectiveness of targeted therapies depends on specific omics events across a patient’s tumor and robust methods to detect these events. Representative genetic drivers involved in the regulation of tumor heterogeneityand therapeutic response

Tumor suppressor gene-driven tumor heterogeneity

The tumor suppressor p53, the most frequently altered gene in human cancers, is a master regulator of apoptosis, senescence, DNA repair and cell cycle progression.116–118 TP53 influences the TME through secretome modulation.119 Mutations in TP53, whether leading to gain-of-function or loss-of-function activities, can drastically alter tumor cell population dynamics and drive tumorigenesis, progression and drug resistance. TP53 mutations are observed in various malignancies, including high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC). Notably, loss-of-function TP53 mutations represent the primary driver of chromosomal instability in HGSOC.120 This genomic instability subsequently facilitates tumor heterogeneity development, as extensively documented in foundational studies of cancer biology. The influence of mutant TP53 on cellular pathways, such as NF-κB signaling, and its suppression of immune responses further support its role in driving malignancy and therapeutic resistance.121,122

The tumor suppressor PTEN, a critical negative regulator of the PI3K pathway,123,124 fosters tumor heterogeneity primarily by shaping the immunosuppressive TME.125 For example, PTEN loss in melanoma elevates inflammatory mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), reducing CD8 + T cell infiltration and diminishing the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.126 In KRAS-driven pancreatic cancer, partial PTEN loss activates NF-κB, recruiting Treg and immunosuppressive myeloid cells and inducing inflammatory cytokines.127 The ability of PTEN loss to both enhance immune checkpoints and reprogram cytokine networks explains its association with reduced immune checkpoint blockade efficacy in multiple cancers.

Loss-of-function mutations in the tumor suppressor serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11), which encodes liver kinase B1 (LKB1), are frequently observed in various solid tumors, particularly LUAD.128 These mutations contribute to tumor heterogeneity by fostering distinct cellular populations and influencing tumor progression, therapeutic resistance and immune evasion. In KRAS-mutant lung cancer models, STK11/LKB1 inactivation drives the expression of chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines, such as granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), CXCL7 and IL-6, inhibiting T cell infiltration and promoting immunosuppressive neutrophil recruitment.129,130 This immunosuppressive microenvironment reduces the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade and hampers antitumor immunity.

Beyond PTEN, STK11 and TP53, the loss of function in other tumor suppressor genes, such as RB1,131 CDKN2A,132 ZBTB7A,133 KISS1,134 and TET2,135 has also been implicated in the regulation of tumor heterogeneity. In summary, beyond their cell-intrinsic roles, a mechanism by which diverse tumor suppressor genes (TP53, PTEN, STK11) drive heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance is through the profound and often immunosuppressive remodeling of the TME.

Oncogene-driven tumor heterogeneity

Oncogenic pathways in tumor cells regulate the TME and disease progression through various mechanisms. For example, missense mutations resulting in oncogenic activation of KRAS proteins are associated with altered expression of signaling molecules, including cytokines, chemokines and growth factors.136–138 In pancreatic tumor models, KRAS-G12D mutations enhance the expression of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), promoting the expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and inhibiting apoptosis.139 Research has revealed that silencing GM-CSF in pancreatic epithelial cells expressing KRAS-G12D increases intratumoral apoptosis, thereby suppressing the formation of precancerous lesions.139 Currently, targeted therapeutic strategies aimed at key downstream pathways induced by KRAS mutations, such as MEK inhibitors combined with immune checkpoint blockade, are being evaluated in several clinical trials.140 Further investigation into the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of these strategies in KRAS-mutant tumors will provide critical insights into tumor heterogeneity and its treatment.

Another key oncogene implicated in tumor heterogeneity is MYC. It is often deregulated in cancer and plays a key role in cell proliferation, differentiation, metabolism and apoptosis.141–143 Beyond regulating cancer cell behavior, MYC is crucial for shaping the TME. MYC plays a multifaceted role in coordinating signaling pathways and cellular dynamics that drive tumor heterogeneity. As a long-standing therapeutic target, MYC has proven challenging to target directly with drugs. However, recent advances in small-molecule inhibitors show promise. Compounds such as MYCi975 and MYCi361 have demonstrated the ability to suppress tumor growth in preclinical models by disrupting MYC activity.144 Recent advances have uncovered extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) as a pivotal genetic determinant of MYC transcriptional diversity in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. This non-chromosomal genomic architecture enables dynamic MYC dosage fluctuations through rapid ecDNA copy number variations, thereby equipping malignant cells with reversible phenotypic plasticity to adapt to microenvironmental stresses. Critically, this ecDNA-MYC axis operates as a bidirectional modulator: environmental pressures (chemotherapy) trigger MYC amplification cascades (>200 copies/cell), while stress alleviation prompts ecDNA shedding to mitigate replicative burdens. Such dynamic equilibrium underscores ecDNA’s role in balancing adaptive advantage against genomic instability costs.145

Aberrations in the ERBB receptor tyrosine kinase family, such as amplifications of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and mutations in EGFR, have been implicated in tumor heterogeneity through diverse molecular mechanisms.146 Therapeutic advancements have targeted HER2-driven cancers with monoclonal antibodies, often combined with chemotherapy, leading to significant improvements in patient outcomes with HER2-positive cancers.147,148 In addition to HER2, EGFR mutations are negatively correlated with antitumor response gene signatures across multiple cancer types. In EGFR-mutant lung cancer samples, this phenomenon is attributed to factors such as activation of the CD73-adenosine pathway, reduced PD-L1 expression and low mutational burden, which promote extracellular adenosine production and regulatory cell proliferation.149

Epigenetic heterogeneity

In addition to genomics-related factors, epigenomic modifications, including histone modifications, DNA methylation and the involvement of noncoding RNAs, play a crucial role in contributing to tumor heterogeneity.150–152

DNA methylation and histone modifications

DNA methylation and histone modification, as core epigenetic regulatory mechanisms, work together through dynamic interactions to shape tumor heterogeneity and influence cancer progression and drug resistance. DNA methylation, an essential epigenetic modification in mammalian cells, primarily occurs at cytosine residues within CpG dinucleotides. Aberrant DNA methylation patterns have been observed in cancer, particularly in the early stages of tumor development, characterized by focal gains and significant losses in promoter regions across various genomic sites.153 Early-stage cancers with such irregular methylation patterns tend to exhibit more aggressive behavior, leading to worse clinical outcomes.154 In breast cancer, hypermethylation in CpG islands often silences key DNA repair and tumor suppressor genes, such as BRCA1 and LRIG1, while hypomethylation in other regions can activate oncogenes, contributing to tumorigenesis.155,156 Histone modification is another key aspect of epigenetic regulation and involves the removal or addition of functional motifs on histones encapsulated by DNA. These modifications, including phosphorylation, acetylation and methylation, can significantly impact gene expression and chromatin structure, thereby influencing various cellular processes, including drug response. Since chromatin serves as the structural framework for the genome, alterations in chromatin remodeling complexes, such as changes in nucleosome positioning through ejection, sliding, or histone eviction, can directly affect the tumor cellular phenotype.157

Altered DNA methylation patterns in cancer play a crucial role in driving molecular and cellular heterogeneity, contributing to the diversity of phenotypes within tumor subpopulations. For instance, in non-small cell lung cancer, hypermethylation of the insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-3 gene decreases sensitivity to cisplatin, while hypermethylation of MGMT is associated with the response to temozolomide in glioblastoma.158,159 In contrast, global DNA hypomethylation, often occurring alongside localized hypermethylation, can also contribute to drug resistance. Hypomethylation may activate oncogenes and enhance the expression of genes involved in drug efflux mechanisms. In cancer patients, variable and widespread histone modifications contribute to chromatin accessibility heterogeneity across different tumor subpopulations, which in turn leads to variability in the expression of genes governing cell differentiation, proliferation and survival, increasing TME heterogeneity.160,161 Aberrant regulation of histone-modifying enzymes is also frequently implicated in cancer and contributes to therapeutic resistance. For example, elevated levels of histone deacetylases (HDACs) are commonly detected in various malignancies and correlate with poor clinical outcomes.160,161

Histone modification and DNA methylation are the two major epigenetic mechanisms of tumor heterogeneity formation. They promote heterogeneity between and within tumor cells by affecting gene expression and chromatin structure. These mechanisms not only affect the biological behavior of tumors but may also influence the response to therapy and prognosis. Therefore, understanding these mechanisms is important for developing new therapeutic strategies and improving patient prognosis.

Noncoding RNAs

Aberrant expression of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), including circular RNAs (circRNAs), long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) and microRNAs (miRNAs), resulting from disrupted epigenetic mechanisms significantly influences gene regulation and is closely associated with cancer metastasis, development and TME remodeling.162,163 Cancer often triggers highly heterogeneous and elevated ncRNA expression.164,165 Depending on the context, ncRNAs may function as tumor promoters or suppressors, contributing to diverse roles across different cancer types and thus driving heterogeneity.

CircRNAs, produced through introns, back-splicing exons, or both, form covalently closed circular structures. They regulate gene expression by sequestering miRNAs, binding to RNA-binding proteins, or influencing transcription and splicing. Dysregulated circRNA expression in tumors drives phenotypic heterogeneity by altering these processes.166,167 CircRNAs are also implicated in tumor malignancy and chemoresistance by influencing tumor heterogeneity. For instance, circEZH2 was reported to promote angiogenesis and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, further driving tumor heterogeneity and resistance.168

LncRNAs regulate gene expression at the epigenetic, posttranscriptional and transcriptional levels. Dysregulated lncRNA expression in cancer impacts genes involved in tumor metastasis, invasion, apoptosis, angiogenesis, differentiation and proliferation by interacting with RNA-binding proteins, mRNA and DNA.169 LncRNAs also play significant roles in modulating drug resistance. By interacting with proteins, RNA and DNA, lncRNAs regulate chromatin structure and gene expression. For instance, the lncRNA HOTAIR associates with polycomb repressive complex 2, directing it to specific genomic regions to mediate histone methylation and gene silencing.

MiRNAs regulate tumor cellular processes such as immune response, apoptosis, differentiation and proliferation by binding to mRNA, leading to its translational inhibition or degradation.170 Dysregulated miRNA expression influences tumor heterogeneity by modulating the function and differentiation of CSCs.171,172 Acting as either oncogenes or tumor suppressors, miRNAs fine-tune the activity of critical signaling pathways, including those driven by KRAS and p53. Taken together, ncRNAs significantly contribute to cancer drug resistance by modulating gene expression, chromatin structure and TME dynamics, thereby promoting tumor heterogeneity, survival and therapy evasion.

Tumor heterogeneity at the cellular level

Cellular heterogeneity represents the phenotypic manifestation of molecular alterations and is a hallmark of tumor evolution. Within a single tumor mass, cancer cells exhibit distinct morphological, functional, and behavioral properties, reflecting their diverse cellular origins, differentiation states, and adaptive responses to microenvironmental cues. This layer of heterogeneity determines tumor progression, metastatic potential, and therapeutic response, thereby shaping clinical outcomes.

EMT drives tumor heterogeneity

EMT is one of the central drivers of tumor heterogeneity formation and evolution. By orchestrating reversible molecular networks, fostering genomic instability and integrating microenvironmental signals, EMT generates phenotypic and functional diversity that underlies metastasis, drug resistance and immune evasion. Two independent trajectories of EMT programs exist within tumors: invasive EMT (embryonic-like) and anti-inflammatory EMT (reparative).173 The former is often located at the tumor edge and promotes metastasis by activating transcription factors such as Prrx1; the latter is often located at the tumor center and maintains tumor microenvironmental homeostasis by recruiting macrophages to suppress inflammatory responses. This division of labor produces spatially distinct subpopulations, reinforcing intratumoral heterogeneity. EMT is not a simple binary transition but contains multiple intermediate transition states (partial EMT, p-EMT).174 Cells in these intermediate states, marked by conserved genes such as SFN and NRG1, exhibit high plasticity that facilitates early invasion and adaptive phenotypic oscillations via ERBB2/3 signaling.175 Recent studies have revealed that EMT activation induces CIN and chromothripsis through genetically engineered mouse models and single-cell sequencing, driving genomic instability, and clonal evolution and leading to large-scale structural genomic variation.174 These genomic alterations accelerate clonal evolution, fostering transcriptional and phenotypic diversification that supports tumor progression.

In addition to genetic mechanisms, EMT is also regulated by physical cues within the microenvironment. Cell elongation enhances cytoskeletal contractility to promote EMT, while nuclear deformation triggers mesenchymal programs through Wnt/β-catenin signaling.176 Such morphology-dependent regulation underscores the physical basis of spatial heterogeneity. Clinically, EMT-associated heterogeneity significantly affects prognosis and treatment response. Patients with high EMT signatures often show poor survival and resistance to chemotherapy or immunotherapy, underscoring the need for strategies that exploit EMT reversibility or plasticity. Current approaches include targeting transcriptional regulators such as Snail/ZEB1 or combining epigenetic drugs (DNMT inhibitors) to blunt adaptive responses.177–179 Organoid models generated by CRISPR-Cas9 further enable patient-specific EMT profiling, offering a platform for precision therapy.180

In conclusion, EMT is a central driver of tumor heterogeneity through multi-trajectory differentiation, intermediate state plasticity, genomic instability and microenvironmental interactions. Recent studies have emphasized that targeting EMT dynamic processes, such as intermediate state retention or genomic stability, may be more effective in suppressing tumor evolution. For example, inhibition of the SFN-NRG1 axis or modulation of mitochondrial nucleotropic distribution (Oct4-Wnt pathway) are potential strategies.174,181 In the future, it is necessary to combine spatial transcriptome and organ-like models to resolve the spatiotemporal evolution of EMT heterogeneity, which will provide a basis for precision therapy.

The TME is not a passive bystander but an active ecosystem that co-evolves with cancer cells to foster heterogeneity. Immune cells drive tumor heterogeneity

The immune system plays a dual role in cancer, a process conceptualized as immunoediting, which culminates in the immune escape phase, where tumors evade destruction.183 This escape is mediated by a complex repertoire of immune cells within the TME that collectively sculpt an immunosuppressive landscape and drive tumor heterogeneity.184

Importantly, distinct immune cell subsets do not act in isolation but rather establish dynamic crosstalk that drives tumor heterogeneity. T cells promote the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment through the secretion of cytokines and chemokines, which in turn affect the proliferation, migration and metastatic capacity of tumor cells and increase tumor heterogeneity. Within the TME, CD4 + T cells can polarize into diverse functional subsets—including Tregs, Th9, Th2, and Th17 cells—underscoring the profound plasticity of helper T-cell programs in malignancy. Among these, Treg cells are one of the most immunosuppressive CD4 + T cell subpopulations in the TME, supporting tumor immune escape and progression. Treg cells not only suppress T cells, NK cells and antigen-presenting cells through their surface molecules CD25, CD39, and CD73 but also promote immune suppression by secreting inhibitory cytokines such as IL-10, IL-35, and TGF-β.185–187 In addition to mediating anti-tumor cytotoxicity through the secretion of effector molecules such as TNF, GZMB, and IFN-γ, CD8 + T cells are also governed by TOX, NFAT, and CTLA-4. These drive the aberrant expression of COX-2, PD-L1, and TGF-β, ultimately precipitating the loss of anti-tumor efficacy and the terminal differentiation toward an exhausted state. Exhausted CD8 + T cells are involved in the formation of the immunosuppressive microenvironment. Collectively, both Tregs and exhausted CD8 + T cells enhance immune tolerance, thereby amplifying intratumoral heterogeneity.188,189

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) exhibit profound heterogeneity that extends beyond the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy.190 Single-cell technologies have revealed a spectrum of states, including inflammatory TAMs (IL-1β, TNF-α), regulatory TAMs (PD-L1, IDO1, ARG1) that secrete immunosuppressive factors to inhibit T cells, and interferon-primed TAMs (ISG15, IRF1).191,192 Inflammatory TAMs are characterized by high expression of inflammation-related genes such as IL-1β and TNF-α. Regulatory TAMs are characterized by high expression of immune-regulatory genes such as PD-L1, IDO1, and ARG1. By secreting immune-suppressive factors that inhibit T cell activation and proliferation and promote Treg infiltration, regulatory TAMs create an immunosuppressive microenvironment that facilitates immune evasion.193,194 Interferon-mediated regulatory TAMs express high levels of genes related to the interferon signaling pathway, such as ISG15 and IRF1. They are found in the tumor nest and stroma, where they modulate immune responses via interferon signaling. Of course, the TAM subsets summarized here are by no means exhaustive or exclusive. TAMs not only promote phenotypic plasticity and the maintenance of stemness in tumor cells through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β, but also orchestrate the differentiation of Tregs via the synthesis of IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby reinforcing an immunosuppressive landscape. In addition, TAMs can be derived either from tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs) or differentiated into monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) through monocyte migration to the tumor region. Together, TRMs and MDMs constitute a complex ecological niche for TAMs, which play key roles in tumor growth and progression.195 The distribution and aggregation patterns of TRMs and MDMs within tumors differ significantly, and these cell populations show dynamic changes as the tumor progresses, affecting tumor treatment response and prognosis and exacerbating tumor heterogeneity.

The B cell lineage within tumors also exhibits substantial diversity, representing an important component of cellular heterogeneity in the TME.196,197 Activated memory B cells, germinal center B cells, plasma cells (PCs) and their intermediates198,199 collectively reflect their adaptability and heterogeneity in different TME. Memory B cells and PCs are the most common subpopulations of TIL-Bs, which are clustered according to class switching and CD27 status.200 Research has found that TIL-Bs can be clustered into ten distinct subpopulations, across multiple cancer types.197 This phenotypic diversity underlies functional plasticity. While some TIL-Bs exert antitumor activity, others can promote an immunosuppressive environment directly or indirectly by secreting cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β.201–204 For instance, c-Maf signaling in a B-cell subset can drive tumor progression by modulating regulatory B cells and inflammatory responses, directly linking B cell heterogeneity to tumor evolution.205

Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) also exhibit significant phenotypic heterogeneity in cancer.206 Traditionally, TANs have been classified into protumor (N2) and antitumor (N1) types, but recent studies suggest that this classification is overly simplistic.207 Researchers have identified five TAN subsets in lung cancer by scRNA-seq, some of which differ from neutrophils in healthy tissues and are associated with tumor growth and poor prognosis.208 For example, N5 TANs, which highly express CTSB and CCL3 (detected by immunohistochemistry using peptidase inhibitor 3), are linked to tumor growth and poor prognosis in lung cancer.208,209 In addition, cancer cells orchestrate the expansion and recruitment of TANs via G-CSF and GM-CSF signaling, a regulatory axis that significantly contributes to the promotion of a pro-tumorigenic environment. In early-stage tumors, TANs can acquire antigen-presenting and T cell-stimulatory functions under specific contexts, thereby contributing to antitumor immunity.210–213 However, as malignancy progresses, the initial anti-tumor activation of TANs gradually wanes. These cells undergo a functional shift toward an immunosuppressive phenotype, secreting TGF-β and IL-1β or orchestrating angiogenesis through the release of NETs, thereby promoting tumor advancement.214 Studies have shown that TANs secrete immunosuppressive factors that enhance tumor cell proliferation and metastasis. For instance, exosomes from N2-polarized tumor-associated TANs containing miR-4745-5p/3911 regulate SLIT2 to promote gastric cancer metastasis.215 Future therapies could aim to exploit this plasticity, transforming TAN heterogeneity from a driver of disease into a therapeutic opportunity.

CAFs promote tumor heterogeneity

CAFs are a major stromal component with remarkable heterogeneity and plasticity, critically regulating tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance.216,217 For example, CAFs can switch between quiescent and activated states in pancreatic cancer through dynamic regulation by PRRX1, thus adapting to the different needs of the tumor.218 Rather than being a uniform population, CAFs diversify into distinct functional subsets that collectively shape tumor heterogeneity. Recent studies have revealed several classical CAF subtypes. These subtypes are categorized as myofibroblast-like CAFs (myCAFs), inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs) based on their transcription profiles and functional markers. MyCAFs increase the stiffness and adhesion of the tumor stroma by remodeling the ECM and depositing collagen while inhibiting immune cell infiltration, thereby forming an “immune barrier” that hinders effective antitumor immune responses.219,220 iCAFs predominantly secrete cytokines (IL-6 and CXCL12) that modulate the inflammatory environment of tumors and promote immunosuppression.221,222 apCAFs are prominently expressed in major histocompatibility complex class II molecules (MHC-II) in tumors such as pancreatic cancer and interfere with T cell activation by modulating antigen presentation.223,224 Together, these subtypes act in a complementary but heterogeneous manner, generating diverse immune-regulatory niches within tumors. CAFs further contribute to TME heterogeneity by secreting immune-modulating cytokines such as TGF-β, IL-6, CXCL1 and CXCL12, promoting an immunosuppressive TME.223,225 For example, CAF-S1, a subpopulation of myofibroblasts, recruits CD4 + CD25 + T cells to create an immunosuppressive microenvironment through CXCL12 and expresses B7H3, CD73 and DPP4 to promote CD4 + CD25 + T cells differentiation into Tregs, thus promoting tumor growth.226 Meanwhile, the secretion of various chemokines by CAFs not only leads to EMT but also restricts the mobilization of immune effector cells (CD8 + T cells) into tumor tissues and promotes spatial heterogeneity of the TME.227 Moreover, CAFs secrete a diverse repertoire of angiogenic factors, including VEGF, PDGF, CXCL12, and HGF, which collectively stimulate the proliferation and recruitment of endothelial cells and pericytes, thereby orchestrating tumor vascularization to drive invasion and metastasis. These mechanistic insights highlight that CAF-driven heterogeneity is not merely descriptive but is directly linked to therapeutic resistance, suggesting that stratifying CAF subtypes may guide more precise interventions.

Endothelial cells promote tumor heterogeneity

Within the tumor microenvironment, tumor endothelial cells (TECs) are not merely passive structural components of the vasculature, but active regulators with marked lineage diversification and functional heterogeneity. Distinct TEC subsets, including PLVAP+ tip-like cells and IGFBP3+ states, may contribute to spatial heterogeneity in blood perfusion, oxygen and nutrient availability, and mechanical stress, thereby providing a material basis for intratumoral heterogeneity. At the same time, TECs can promote malignant progression through metabolic reprogramming and ESM1-associated positive feedback signaling, cooperatively reinforcing invasive and metastatic traits in tumor cells. Moreover, by engaging TIM-3- and Delta-Notch-related pathways, TECs contribute to local immunosuppression and macrophage polarization, establishing a vascular niche that supports immune evasion and therapeutic resistance. Collectively, TEC heterogeneity represents a critical vascular axis driving tumor progression and heterogeneity.

Metabolic heterogeneity

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, enabling tumor cells to survive and proliferate under environmental stresses, adapt to challenges and continue to evolve during tumor progression.228 A central aspect of cancer metabolism is the ability of malignant cells to acquire enhanced self-feeding capacity through the activation of nutrient uptake mechanisms.229 Metabolic reprogramming is often linked to the hypoxic tumor core, where it activates hypoxia-inducible factors and regulates genes associated with metabolic adaptation.230,231 According to the Warburg effect, tumor cells favor aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation; glucose is converted to lactate even in normoxia, enabling rapid ATP production to fuel rapid proliferation. Metabolic heterogeneity is primarily observed in enhanced glycolysis, upregulated glutamine metabolism, increased fatty acid synthesis, and inhibition of fatty acid oxidation in tumors (Fig. 4), contributing to pronounced metabolic diversity.232,233 Cancer-specific metabolic changes provide resistance to external stresses and provide a molecular basis for understanding heterogeneity and drug resistance.234,235 This rewiring allows cancer cells to survive and proliferate under diverse microenvironmental stresses, thereby exacerbating tumor heterogeneity.Fig. 4Metabolic reprogramming as a driver of tumor heterogeneity. Due to their dependence on the glycolytic pathway, cancer cells favor rapid, albeit less efficient, ATP production. As a result, tumor tissues exhibit increased glucose uptake and lactate production.236 Notably, although lactate is considered a metabolic waste product in the TME, it can also serve as an important metabolic intermediate and fuel source.237,238 Mitochondria-associated lactate dehydrogenase converts lactate to pyruvate, which serves as a fuel for the TCA cycle.239 In addition, lactate can lead to histone lactylation, a modification that drives oncogenesis and promote tumor immune escape and metastasis.240–242 Glycolysis not only supplies ATP but also enhances cellular biosynthesis and antioxidant defenses through its metabolites, such as intermediates in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). Specifically, glucose-6-phosphate (G-6-P) is converted to ribose-5-phosphate (R-5-P) and NADPH during glycolysis,243 where R-5-P provides raw material for nucleic acid and amino acid synthesis and re-enters the glycolytic pathway through the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate (F-6-P) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G-3-P).244 NADPH, on the other hand, acts as a key antioxidant, helping to scavenge intracellular ROS.245 These reactions not only mitigate ROS-induced damage in cancer cells but also provide the necessary materials for DNA synthesis to support cancer cell growth and reproduction.

Beyond glycolysis, lipid and amino acid metabolism form an intertwined network that further amplifies metabolic heterogeneity. Lipid metabolism is widely dysregulated in the early stages of tumors.246 Tumor cells often adapt to their rapid growth by increasing fatty acid synthesis and decreasing fatty acid β-oxidation.247 CD36 is one of the major receptors for inducing fatty acid uptake.248 It is highly expressed in a variety of tumor types, especially in CSCs, and is closely associated with poor tumor prognosis and metastasis. The transmembrane glycoprotein CD147 also plays an important role in fatty acid synthesis. It triggers the transcription of key fatty acid-related genes, including fatty acid synthase and acetyl coenzyme A carboxylase, through activation of the downstream AKT/mTOR signaling pathway to promote fatty acid synthesis.249 In addition, AMPK, a key molecule in metabolic sensing, can also enhance fatty acid uptake and inhibit SREBP signaling by upregulating CD36 and other lipid transport proteins.250,251

Amino acid metabolism is critical for tumor cell growth and survival, especially during protein synthesis, nucleic acid synthesis and energy production. Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are important in the metabolic network of tumor cells through their fatty branched aliphatic side-chain structure. These amino acids are catalytically converted to α-keto acids by branched-chain amino acid transaminase (BCAT), which further enters the TCA cycle through the dehydrogenase reaction and participates in the energy metabolism and energy supply process of tumor cells. The metabolic regulation of BCAAs shows significant heterogeneity in different types of tumors. The accumulation of BCAA promotes the growth and survival of tumor cells, mainly through the activation of the mTORC1 pathway, which further exacerbates the heterogeneity of tumors.252 Glutamine is one of the important amino acids in tumor cells, participating in energy metabolism and contributing to tumor cell detoxification.253 Tumor cells take up glutamine from plasma through a series of specific amino acid transport proteins and convert it to glutamate via glutaminase (GLS) in the mitochondria.254 Glutamate is converted to α-ketoglutarate by glutamate deaminase, which enters the TCA cycle and provides ATP to tumor cells to support their continued proliferation and survival.255,256 Meanwhile, glutamate metabolites regulate the process of cellular autophagy by modulating amino acid metabolism and making adaptive adjustments to the internal environment of tumor cells (pH) to further enhance tumor adaptability and drug resistance.257 This dual role of glutamine metabolism underpins both metabolic reprogramming and the emergence of tumor heterogeneity, reflecting the dynamic changes in the TME and its close interaction with tumor cell metabolism.

Tumor heterogeneity at the tissue and organ levels

Tumor heterogeneity at the tissue and organ levels reflects spatial and functional diversity beyond the cellular scale. It arises from the complex interplay among malignant cells, stromal components, vascular networks, and immune infiltrates across distinct tumor regions or metastatic sites, leading to differences in morphology, perfusion, oxygenation, extracellular matrix composition, and immune distribution that collectively influence tumor evolution, therapeutic response, and clinical outcomes. At the tissue level, spatial heterogeneity is driven by gradients of nutrients, oxygen tension, and stromal remodeling. At the organ level, heterogeneity is further shaped by organ-specific microenvironments and metastatic tropism, where distinct metabolic and immune adaptations emerge at different metastatic sites. Overall, tissue- and organ-level heterogeneity highlights cancer as a dynamic ecosystem shaped by both intrinsic genetic evolution and extrinsic host context, underscoring the need for spatially informed and organ-adaptive therapeutic strategies.

Spatial heterogeneity

Under histological examination, areas of cell-rich and stroma-rich tissue are evident, indicating that the tumor is not a homogeneous entity. In fact, there is a difference in the structure, function and biological properties of cells and their microenvironment in different regions of tumors. Heterogeneity is manifested not only in the genetic and phenotypic differences of tumor cells but also in the heterogeneity of nontumor cells (fibroblasts, endothelial cells, immune cells). Multiregional sampling is a common method to study spatial heterogeneity and clonal evolution, but if few regions are sampled in a specimen, more than 70% of subclonal mutations will be misidentified as clonal.258 In addition, spatial transcriptomics has advanced rapidly, but many current methods lack the resolution to parse the microenvironment at the single-cell level. ScRNA-seq combined with spatial transcriptomics is a cutting-edge method to study spatial heterogeneity in cancer. Therefore, the development of technologies that can achieve single-cell and subcellular resolution while balancing broad transcriptome coverage, high spatial resolution, and gene detection efficiency represents a key future direction for spatial transcriptomics research.

The spatial heterogeneity of tumors is the result of a combination of several factors, among which vascular distribution and oxygen gradient play a crucial role in promoting the spatial heterogeneity of tumors (Fig. 5a). Irregular blood vessels cause local hypoxia. This prompts tumors to meet their metabolic needs through VEGF-mediated angiogenesis and simultaneously activates signaling pathways associated with invasiveness.259,260 Previously, it was thought that solid tumors had to undergo neovascularization to meet their nutritional and metabolic needs.261 However, researchers have come to realize that not all tumors rely on angiogenesis for growth and survival, but rather that tumor cells “hijack” existing vascular networks in host tissues.262 Oxygen gradients are another important driver of spatial heterogeneity, with a comprehensive impact on the biological performance of various cells in the TME.263 Greenwald et al. stratified human GBM by spatial neighborhood analysis, starting from the tumor core, and identified five distinct cellular state layers, including hypoxic ecotopes, hypoxia-adjacent ecotopes, immune- and angiogenesis-associated ecotopes and neurodevelopmental GBM states, as well as normal brain.264 In addition, the uneven distribution of environmental nutrients further promotes the spatial heterogeneity of tumors. The invasive margin constitutes another specialized niche, where tumor immune interactions are highly active but frequently dominated by immunosuppressive programs, including enrichment of CD11c+/CD11b+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), M2-like macrophages and immunoregulatory molecules such as PD-L1 and IDO. Together, these features reinforce local immune evasion and spatial heterogeneity. b Organotropism of tumor metastasis. Organ-specific metastatic tropism represents a higher-level manifestation of tumor heterogeneity, reflecting the distinct capacities of tumor cell subpopulations to adapt to and colonize distant organs. This is illustrated by the preferential bone metastasis of estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer and the lung tropism of triple-negative breast cancer. Similarly, metastatic dissemination in pancreatic cancer is influenced by P120CTN status, whereas lung or liver colonization can also be shaped by specific signaling programs and genetic alterations, such as DKK1-mediated regulation of immune-cell recruitment or mutations associated with colorectal cancer liver metastasis. These findings indicate that tissue- and organ-level heterogeneity not only reflects tumor evolution, but also helps determine metastatic route and organ preference

Tumor spatial heterogeneity is reflected in the high degree of compartmentalization and functional differentiation of various cells in the TME. Immune cells such as T cells primarily aggregate in lymphocyte-rich zones and stromal zones, while neutrophils and B cells exhibit distinct spatial preferences.5,267 The distribution of immune cells is closely related to angiogenesis. Tie2+, CD68+, Lyve-1+ and CCR2+ TAMs, which are enriched in vascular or stromal regions, respectively, forming specialized niches that drive angiogenesis, modulate vascular remodeling and establish immunosuppression, and enhance tumor invasion and drug resistance.268–273 The invasive margin is considered the frontline of the battle between tumors and the immune system, where the interactions between tumor cells and immune cells are particularly complex and intense.274 The application of spatial multi-omics technology has revealed spatial heterogeneity at the invasive front, providing new insights into the biological characteristics of this region. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) are most highly enriched at the invasive margin through spatial transcriptomic analysis, indicating their crucial role in the antitumor immune response.275,276 Moreover, the infiltration of immune cells such as natural killer T cells, CD8+ effector memory T cells, T helper 1 cells, CD4+ naive T cells and memory B cells at the invasive margin is closely associated with better patient survival prognosis.274 Spatial heterogeneity is not only reflected in the distribution of cells but also involves functional interactions between cells. The spatial layout and interactions of immune cells, stromal cells, and tumor cells are key determinants of tumor progression and immune escape in the TME. For instance, in prostate cancer, chemokines expressed by tumor cells and stromal cells are involved in myeloid differentiation and recruitment through ligand‒receptor interactions, spatially interacting with each other.277 This suggests that bidirectional communication between spatially neighboring cells to support tumor growth and progression promotes the recruitment and differentiation of immune-suppressing cells, thereby enhancing the ability of tumors to escape immune suppression and progression.278

Although the invasive margin is a region of active immune response, its local immune microenvironment usually exhibits strong immunosuppressive properties and is closely associated with macrophages. In this region, M2 macrophages were more densely distributed,279 while a large number of CD11c+/CD11b+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells280 expressing immunosuppressive factors (PD-1, PD-L1 and IDO) were detected, which led to the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment through the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors and the suppression of immune cell activity. Further studies have shown that invasive tumor cells induce high expression of serum amyloid A1 and A2 (SAA1, SAA2) by activating the JAK/STAT3 pathway through secretion of CXCL6.281 These proteins promote the recruitment of FPR1+ macrophages and M2 polarization through the SAAs/TLR2 axis, thereby further intensifying local immunosuppression. More complexly, under the influence of mucosal-associated invariant CD8 + T-secreted CSF1 and CCL5, a subpopulation of FOLR2+ macrophages with intact phagocytic function gradually shifts toward SPP1+ macrophages. These SPP1+ macrophages, together with vascular endothelial cells and POSTN + FAP+ fibroblasts, form a unique “triad structure”,282 which further exacerbates immunosuppression in the TME. These findings reveal the immunosuppressive microenvironment at the invasive margin and provide new targets and ideas for tumor immunotherapy.

In conclusion, understanding how spatial organization and cellular interactions influence tumor behavior will be crucial for advancing cancer treatment and immune interventions. There remain several issues to be addressed, particularly in the space context. For example, what intrinsic or extrinsic factors enable cancer cells to evade the immune system? How do vascular endothelial cells and cancer cells interact to promote growth and metastasis?

Organotropism of tumor metastasis

Tumor metastasis is considered the ultimate event in cancer evolution, essentially a Darwinian selection process that traverses multiple survival bottlenecks. The complex genetic and non-genetic heterogeneity within the primary tumor provides a “pool of variation” for clonal adaptive evolution, enabling specific subclones to acquire the potential to escape, migrate and colonize distant organs.283 Recent studies tracking the lineage of paired primary and metastatic tumors have revealed that tumor metastasis can follow two patterns: linear evolution (i.e., late dissemination), parallel evolution (i.e., early dissemination),284–286 or even both simultaneously.287 The coexistence of these patterns reflects the uncertainty and plasticity of clonal fate in heterogeneous ecosystems. The multidimensional manifestations of heterogeneity constitute a potential “preference map” for metastasis occurrence (Fig. 5b). For example, estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer (ER⁺/LumBC) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) exhibit significant differences in metastasis trends, with the former tending toward bone metastasis288 and the latter more commonly spreading to the lungs,289 reflecting the highly specific biological compatibility between heterogeneous clones and organs. Thus, intratumoral heterogeneity not only determines which clones are “selected” to enter the metastatic pathway but also lays the biological foundation for their subsequent successful colonization.

Beyond intrinsic mutations, the reciprocal crosstalk between disseminating clones and host-organ niches ultimately dictates metastatic tropism. More importantly, the subclones driving metastasis are not always dominated by the primary tumor but are often associated with EMT characteristics, transcriptional reprogramming, epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming, among others.290 Clones that have undergone EMT, for example, display both heightened metastatic potential and superior stress adaptability.291 The heterogeneous expression status of P120CTN in tumor cells not only regulates EMT but also influences organ-selective metastasis. Pancreatic cancer cells lacking P120CTN preferentially metastasize to the lungs, while the retention of at least one allele allows tumor cells to metastasize simultaneously to both the lungs and liver.292 In colorectal cancer, stem-like clones that coexpress PTPRO and ASCL2 function as the core drivers of organ-specific metastasis to the liver and ovaries.293 Metabolic heterogeneity is also an important determinant of metastatic potential. For example, loss of PHGDH expression enhances metastatic potential by increasing cell migration capacity, suggesting that metabolic heterogeneity may serve as an important means of adapting to environmental stress.294

This adaptation is not accidental but driven by a series of regulatory factors. For example, cancer cells secreting DKK1 are prone to lung metastasis, as they antagonize the WNT/PCP-RAC1-JNK signaling axis, thereby inhibiting PTGS2-mediated immune cell recruitment and promoting lung metastasis.295 In breast cancer, the feedback loop between FOXF2 and the NF-κB or TGF-β signaling pathways determines breast cancer subtype-specific organ tropism by controlling metastasis dormancy or reactivation.296 Mutations in genes such as NELL1, ADAMTS10 and RXFP3 have been reported or enriched in CRC with liver metastasis, suggesting that they may promote tumor cell adaptation to the liver microenvironment. Therefore, tumor heterogeneity not only determines whether metastasis occurs but also shapes where it occurs and how it colonizes and evolves in new organs. Organ selectivity is the result of ongoing adaptation between heterogeneous clones and the organ microenvironment during evolution. It challenges the traditional “late dissemination” hypothesis and instead emphasizes the ecological evolutionary logic of “early branching and clonal adaptation,” potentially becoming a key breakthrough in precision medicine for anti-metastasis.

Impact of tumor heterogeneity on targeted therapies

Tumor heterogeneity represents not only a central challenge for targeted therapy but also a fundamental barrier in the era of precision oncology. Genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, and phenotypic diversity within tumors limits the efficacy of single-target therapies, allowing resistant subclones to escape drug pressure and drive relapse. In EGFR-mutant lung cancer, the emergence of secondary mutations such as T790M and C797S exemplifies the dynamic evolution of resistant clones.297 Moreover, epigenetic reprogramming, compensatory signaling, and microenvironmental remodeling—including hypoxia, immune suppression, and fibroblast activation—further attenuate therapeutic efficacy.298 Addressing this multidimensional complexity requires a paradigm shift from single-target inhibition to systemic intervention, integrating multitarget combination strategies, real-time liquid biopsy monitoring, and single-cell spatial multi-omics to anticipate and intercept resistance evolution.

Although tumor heterogeneity poses serious challenges to therapy, it also provides opportunities for predicting disease progression and guiding treatment. Different regions of the same tumor may carry distinct driver mutations (KRAS, BRAF),299,300 and targeted therapies may only be effective against some subclones, with residual resistant clones leading to relapse.301 Identification of dominant clones through single-cell sequencing or spatial transcriptomics can predict drug sensitivity. In NSCLC, tumors with activating EGFR mutations are initially sensitive to EGFR-TKIs, but acquired resistance can emerge through mechanisms such as T790M, C797S, or MET amplification.302,303 Monitoring circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) by liquid biopsy enables early detection of resistance and timely adjustment of therapy.

Beyond genetic diversity, intratumoral variability in transcriptional states, metabolic dependencies, and immune landscapes shapes therapeutic responses in complex ways.304 Tumor cells can adopt drug-tolerant persister phenotypes via chromatin remodeling, reversible quiescence, or altered redox balance, sustaining minimal residual disease.305,306 Interactions within the tumor microenvironment—including cytokine-mediated signaling among cancer cells, fibroblasts, and immune cells—create adaptive niches promoting clonal selection. Intratumor heterogeneity is also reflected in tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs),307 which in tumors show disorganized architecture, chronic immune activation (PD-L1⁺ dendritic cells),308 and enrichment of immunosuppressive subsets such as exhausted CD8⁺ T cells and regulatory B cells.309 These features underscore TLSs as both prognostic biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets, highlighting the need for integrated strategies that combine targeted, epigenetic, metabolic, and immune-modulatory therapies, guided by dynamic, single-cell-resolved monitoring of tumor evolution.

Targeting genomic instability and clonal evolution

Targeting DNA damage repair pathways

While genomic instability drives rapid clonal evolution in cancer cells, it creates exploitable defects within the DNA repair network, offering opportunities for targeted therapeutic interventions.310,311 For instance, BRCA1/2 mutations compromise homologous recombination repair (HRR), forcing tumor cells to rely on alternative pathways (single-strand break repair) for survival.312,313 These distinct repair dependencies represent a molecular basis of heterogeneity that can be selectively targeted.

Early animal studies using BRCA-deficient mouse models demonstrated that PARP inhibitors reduced tumor burden by over 70% compared to controls, with sustained suppression even after treatment cessation.314 For example, the Ledermann clinical study showed that Olaparib significantly extended progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with BRCA-mutated ovarian cancer, demonstrating the clinical feasibility of a synthetic lethality approach.315 The SOLO-1 trial led by Moore et al. further demonstrated the efficacy of Olaparib as a first-line treatment for newly diagnosed advanced ovarian cancer, improving the 2-year PFS rate from about 35% in the control group to 74%, underscoring its value in frontline settings.316 Importantly, these trials illustrate how clonal heterogeneity arising from BRCA/HRR deficiencies can be therapeutically exploited by tailored interventions. These findings paved the way for the approval of additional PARP inhibitors (Talazoparib, Rucaparib, Niraparib) across multiple BRCA/HRR-deficient cancers.317–320 Nonetheless, real-world clinical practice shows that some patients often develop resistance within 6–12 months through BRCA reversion mutations or upregulation of alternative DNA repair pathways.313,321 Recent investigations thus emphasize sequential or combination approaches to delay resistance.322 For example, combining PARP inhibitors with platinum drugs (carboplatin or cisplatin) has improved progression-free survival in patients with BRCA-like metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, albeit with increased bone marrow suppression and nephrotoxicity.323 Balancing efficacy with these potential toxicities, however, remains an ongoing challenge in clinical practice.

Beyond PARP inhibitors, targeting other key DNA damage response network molecules (ATR, CHK1, and WEE1) can also orchestrate cell cycle checkpoints, and their inhibition prevents damaged cells from pausing for repair.324–326 This expands the therapeutic scope to additional heterogeneous tumor contexts where the HRR is intact but other vulnerabilities exist. This approach shows particular synergy with radiotherapy or platinum agents, yielding greater-than-additive effects. A phase I trial in NSCLC and TNBC showed that combining ATR inhibition with cisplatin demonstrated preliminary antitumor activity, albeit with enhanced bone marrow suppression and gastrointestinal toxicities.327,328 Identifying patients most likely to benefit from combined therapeutic intervention is thus crucial for expanding its therapeutic window. Indeed, recent research has proposed combining DDR inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors to exploit newly exposed neoantigens from DNA damage, enhancing T-cell recognition.329,330 Future progress will depend on integrating genomic profiling with adaptive trial designs to connect specific DDR vulnerabilities to defined patterns of tumor heterogeneity, rather than relying on broad drug lists without a mechanistic context.

Controlling multiclonal resistance

Polyclonal evolution is characterized by the emergence of resistant subclones that gain dominance through resistance mutations or pathway bypass mechanisms under therapeutic pressure. Addressing this challenge entails real-time mutational monitoring, sequential therapies and multi-pathway combination interventions to maintain sustained control of resistant clones.331,332

In EGFR-mutant NSCLC, first- or second-generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) often lead to significant tumor shrinkage in the initial treatment of naïve patients.333–335 However, T790M-mediated resistance emerges within approximately 6 months.336,337 Although third-generation Osimertinib specifically targets the T790M mutation and extends median PFS by approximately 22.1 months, its application is usually accompanied by the emergence of new mutations such as C797S or MET/HER2 amplifications.338–340 These newly acquired resistance mechanisms can then be detected through tissue biopsies or circulating tumor DNA assays, guiding the addition of MET or HER2 inhibitors to mitigate disease progression.

A similar “multi-pathway blockade” strategy has shown promise in other solid tumors, such as melanoma.341–344 For instance, compared to single-agent BRAF inhibitors that typically extend survival by only a few months,345 dual BRAF and MEK inhibition has been shown to prolong median PFS to approximately 14.9 months.346,347 More recently, small-scale trials have attempted triple-combination regimens that add PD-1 inhibitors in addition to BRAF plus MEK inhibitors, which further boost response rates but increase immune-related toxicities such as hepatitis and colitis.342,348,349 For example, a phase Ⅲ study reported an objective response rate of nearly 70% in patients with BRAF^V600-mutant melanoma, albeit with grade ≥3 adverse events in approximately 55% of patients.350 These findings highlight the need to refine dosing and scheduling in high-intensity regimens to balance efficacy with tolerability.

Overall, with dynamic molecular testing serving as an “early warning system” to target resistant subclones at different disease stages, sequential or combination therapies are increasingly fundamental to personalized management of solid tumors. As single-cell sequencing and spatial genomics mature, clinicians may detect the emergence of resistant clones at earlier stages and initiate “preemptive” strategies for more durable disease control.

Targeting CSCs and phenotypic diversity

Targeting CSCs

Targeting CSCs represents a promising strategy to disrupt the clonogenic potential of tumors at their root. Vismodegib, a small antagonist of the hedgehog pathway component smoothened, is the first systemic drug approved for basal cell carcinoma.351–353 Moreover, inhibitors of the Notch and Wnt pathways have been applied in phase I/II trials for pancreatic and breast cancer.354,355 However, many CSC-targeting drugs have failed in clinical translation, largely due to the difficulty of accurately identifying CSCs across tumor subtypes and the unintended toxicity caused by simultaneously affecting normal stem cells.356 Despite the remarkable progress in targeted therapy research, clinical success remains limited because of the biological heterogeneity of CSCs and their dynamic adaptation to treatment. Recent advances highlight strategies that attempt to overcome these barriers. For instance, near-infrared (NIR) light-triggered temperature-sensitive hydrogels (ATHs) combining photothermolysis with thioridazine (THZ) were developed to precisely target CSCs and induce apoptosis. In a mouse model, this strategy significantly inhibited breast cancer recurrence and lung metastasis while reducing off-target toxicity.357 Similarly, mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos) can deliver chemotherapeutic drugs or siRNA to CSCs, enhancing treatment specificity and minimizing systemic exposure.358 Nanoparticles (PEA-CD133/shRNA-CCL5) inhibit ovarian cancer metastasis and prolong survival by targeting CSC surface markers such as CD133.358 Moreover, metabolic interventions—such as restricting methionine metabolism in TNBC—have been shown to reduce CSC enrichment, and combining this approach with PARP inhibitors may further improve efficacy.359 Efforts to identify more precise CSC markers (CD133, CD44, ALDH1) have also yielded translational opportunities. CD133 monoclonal antibody-based regimens showed antitumor activity in colorectal cancer, while hyaluronic acid (HA)-coated nanoparticles suppressed stemness markers in breast cancer by targeting CD44.360 ALDH1 inhibitors demonstrated activity in ovarian cancer and melanoma, but dynamic changes in marker expression remain a challenge.361 Importantly, temporal and spatial heterogeneity of CSC subgroups often limits the durability of single-targeted therapies.362 Although CSC-targeted therapy still faces obstacles, emerging approaches such as single-cell analysis and immune–CSC interaction studies offer concrete directions for future research.363 Rather than broad drug lists, the focus should shift toward integrating marker heterogeneity, microenvironmental cues and immune modulation to design CSC therapies with durable efficacy.

Epigenetic and metabolic interventions

Epigenetic modification and metabolic reprogramming are distinct factors driving polyclonal resistance. Epigenetic plasticity alters transcriptional states to foster adaptability, whereas metabolic rewiring supplies bioenergetic and biosynthetic resources that sustain survival and immune evasion. Importantly, these mechanisms frequently converge to reinforce tumor heterogeneity, underscoring their value as dual therapeutic targets. Recent advances have focused on reversing abnormal epigenetic changes as a therapeutic strategy.364,365 One notable example is the DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) inhibitor 5-azacytidine, which achieves an overall response rate as high as 73% in high-risk myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS).366 Its hematologic efficacy has stimulated broader investigations in solid tumors, where DNMT or HDAC inhibitors restore silenced tumor suppressors, enhance antigen presentation, and sensitize tumors to immune clearance.367,368 Notably, these epigenetic agents often exhibit synergistic effects in combination therapies. For instance, co-administration of 5-azacytidine or HDAC inhibitors with PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade or chemotherapy has been shown to counteract resistance phenotypes and bolster T-cell cytotoxicity.369–371

Likewise, tumor metabolism has emerged as a complementary axis of vulnerability. Solid tumors frequently display glycolytic addiction or glutamine dependency to sustain their rapid growth.372 Accordingly, glutaminase inhibitors such as CB-839 have reduced tumor burden by 20–30% in preclinical models and further amplified responses when combined with chemotherapy or mTOR inhibition.373–375 However, early-phase trials in renal cell carcinoma demonstrated that resistance can still develop through compensatory fatty acid oxidation,376 highlighting the challenge of adaptive metabolic escape routes.

These insights suggest that therapeutic strategies should shift from single-pathway inhibition toward rationally designed multitarget combinations that directly address tumor plasticity. For instance, in a hepatocellular carcinoma model, dual inhibition of GLS1 (glutaminase) and FASN (fatty acid synthase) not only disrupted amino acid and lipid synthesis routes but also produced superior antiproliferative effects compared to single-agent therapy.377 Similarly, a recent phase I study (NCT02071862) investigated telaglenastat in combination with standard-of-care regimens in advanced solid tumors. An ongoing phase II trial is evaluating the fatty acid synthase (FASN) inhibitor TVB-2640 (denifanstat) in combination with bevacizumab for patients with recurrent high-grade astrocytoma. Preliminary results from this trial reported a promising overall response rate (ORR) of 56%, including a complete response rate of 17%, along with a significant improvement in 6-month progression-free survival (PFS6) to 31.4% compared to historical controls receiving bevacizumab alone.378 Collectively, integrating epigenetic and metabolic targeting with immunotherapy or chemotherapy represents a more coherent strategy to counteract resistance, directly linking clinical interventions to the heterogeneity that underpins therapeutic failure.

Tumor heterogeneity and therapy resistance

Tumor heterogeneity encompasses genetic, phenotypic, and behavioral diversity, thereby contributing to heterogeneous responses to immunotherapy. For instance, some tumor areas may have higher immunogenicity and respond well to treatment, while others may resist treatment due to immunosuppressive mechanisms.379 Therefore, tumor heterogeneity has a significant impact on the efficacy of immunotherapy and needs to be addressed through strategies such as individualized treatment, combination therapy and enhancing the function of the immune system.

Immune checkpoints and signaling blockade

In terms of the immune evasion challenge, immune checkpoint targets such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 have emerged as pivotal strategies for cancer immunotherapy. They have the capacity to restore T cell activity, thereby supporting more efficient recognition and elimination of tumor cells. Nonetheless, the efficacy of ICIs remains limited, with clinical evidence showing that resistance rates vary by therapy type and cancer context. For example, resistance occurs in 60–85% of patients treated with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 for NSCLC and in 70–80% of patients treated with CTLA-4 inhibitors in melanoma.380 Mechanistic studies have revealed that defective neoantigen presentation, compensatory upregulation of checkpoints such as LAG-3 and TIM-3, and increased immunosuppressive cytokines often underlie these resistant phenotypes, further illustrating the heterogeneous nature of immune escape.381,382

To overcome these barriers, researchers have developed rational combination strategies designed to modulate multiple immune-evasion pathways simultaneously, thereby amplifying T-cell infiltration and restoring cytotoxic function.383,384 Among these strategies, combining ICIs with anti-VEGF agents or other small-molecule drugs has shown particular promise. These combinations not only reduce tumor-induced immunosuppression but also enhance local immune cell trafficking. In unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma, for example, the IMbrave150 trial reported that atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) plus bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) significantly outperformed sorafenib, improving both overall survival and progression-free survival.385 Impressively, the 12-month survival rate of those patients who received the combination treatment was also superior to that of the control group. However, this intensified immune response was also accompanied by higher incidences of immune-related hepatitis, proteinuria and hypertension, underscoring the importance of vigilant, multidisciplinary adverse event monitoring. Moving forward, the refinement of combination strategies will likely depend on integrating biomarker-driven insights into tumor heterogeneity—for instance, recognizing emergent LAG-3 expression or altered cytokine landscapes early in treatment could allow clinicians to tailor regimens dynamically and preserve therapeutic benefit.386

CAR-T and cellular therapies

In addition to ICIs, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy has emerged as one of the most rapidly advancing fields. For instance, CD19-targeting CAR-T therapy has shown great potential for treating hematologic malignancies. It can induce complete remission rates exceeding 80% in certain cohorts with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and B-cell lymphomas.387 This transformative efficacy has been attributed to a relatively uniform expression of CD19 on malignant B cells, leading to a robust expansion of CAR T cells upon encountering their antigen. However, when applied to solid tumors, the intrinsic heterogeneity of antigen expression, the abundance of immunosuppressive cytokines and metabolic barriers in the TME severely limit efficacy, underscoring the direct link between therapeutic challenges and tumor heterogeneity. Researchers have thus engineered multi-specific and modular CAR-T platforms to target multiple tumor-associated antigens simultaneously and reduce immune escape.388 Additional modifications include knocking out PD-1 or incorporating genes for cytokines such as IL-12 or GM-CSF, enabling CAR-T cells to resist inhibitory signals and potentially reshape the TME to favor an antitumor response.

Encouraged by these preclinical findings, early-phase clinical trials have begun assessing novel CAR-T constructs in various solid tumors. In recurrent glioblastoma, IL-13Ra2-targeting CAR-T cells have shown transient regression of intracranial lesions in small patient cohorts, despite inconsistent durable responses.389 Another example comes from pancreatic cancer trials investigating CAR-T cells directed against mesothelin or Claudin 18.2. While a subset of patients demonstrated partial responses or prolonged stable disease for several months (NCT03323944, NCT04581473, NCT03874897, and NCT01935843), their overall response rate was modest. Importantly, these outcomes highlight that intratumoral heterogeneity and immune suppression remain dominant resistance mechanisms, rather than a simple failure of CAR-T design. Moreover, such a limited improvement in treating solid tumors is accompanied by a propensity for severe toxicities such as cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity. To address both efficacy and safety, localized delivery strategies—such as intraperitoneal or intraventricular infusion—have been developed to concentrate CAR-T activity at tumor sites, thereby mitigating systemic toxicity while partially overcoming spatial heterogeneity. For instance, studies in preclinical models of advanced gastric and ovarian malignancies have demonstrated significant tumor regression and prolonged survival with intraperitoneal infusion of CAR-T cells, supporting the feasibility of localized delivery.390 Clinical trials have thus begun translating this approach to solid tumors such as glioblastoma, where intraventricular delivery of bivalently linked CAR-T cells targeting EGFR and IL-13Ra2 resulted in tumor size reduction in all six treated patients, with a median PFS of 2.5 months and stable disease in four patients.391,392 For Monitoring tumor heterogeneity

While the above therapeutic innovations are reshaping cancer management, their effectiveness remains constrained by tumor heterogeneity—a fundamental driver of malignant progression that fuels resistance mechanisms. This biological complexity underlies the persistent clinical challenges in overcoming therapeutic resistance and optimizing treatment outcomes. Advanced monitoring techniques, including single-cell sequencing, multi-region tissue sampling, representative biopsy approaches and longitudinal liquid biopsy, now enable comprehensive investigation of tumor complexity and clonal dynamics. These innovations provide deeper insights into the molecular and cellular diversity within tumors, supporting more effective and personalized treatment strategies.

scRNA-seq

Traditional bulk sequencing analyses examine cell populations collectively, reconstructing tumor phylogenies through computational inference of somatic mutation variant allele frequencies.7 These methods often average signals across subgroups, potentially missing minor subclone mutations and introducing biases due to tumor purity and CNVs.396 Furthermore, the lack of standardization among computational tools adds additional challenges to results interpretation. In contrast, owing to the rapid development of single-cell isolation, single-cell approaches provide unparalleled resolution in reconstructing tumor subclonality and reveal subtle cell-to-cell variations. Single-cell RNA sequencing is one of the most widely used technologies that allows for detailed gene expression analysis, thus enabling the identification of rare residual or drug-resistant cells.397,398 Despite challenges such as technical noise and false negatives, innovations such as unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) have improved data accuracy by reducing errors.399 Recent advancements in multi-omics single-cell approaches have further enhanced the understanding of tumor heterogeneity and evolution.400,401 By integrating data on multiple macromolecules within individual cells, these methods offer a more holistic view of cellular states and dynamics. However, the inherent technical complexity of single-cell sequencing techniques, including single-cell isolation, low-input library preparation and massively parallel sequencing, continues to impose substantially higher costs than bulk-cell analyses.

Tissue biopsy sampling

For many years, tissue biopsy has been the standard method for tumor diagnosis, although it remains an invasive procedure with potential risks. Additionally, it may not be readily feasible in certain situations, particularly when the tumor is in an inaccessible location. Moreover, a single biopsy often represents only a tiny fraction of the evolutionary trajectory of cancers, including melanoma and ovarian, renal and lung cancers. The TRACERx project, a large-scale study focused on tracking cancer evolution, also uses multiregional sampling to investigate tumor progression in several malignancies, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), melanoma and lung cancer.402,403 It has been reported that whole-exome sequencing of 1206 tumor regions from 101 ccRCC patients revealed higher levels of genetic diversity and mutational load than previously seen in single-biopsy studies.404 By analyzing somatic events and their timing, the researchers not only identified distinct evolutionary trajectories in tumors but also associated them with different clinical outcomes. Interestingly, their analysis highlighted the genetic differences between metastases and primary tumors, showing that metastases tend to exhibit fewer driver mutations and are more genetically homogeneous.57 These findings suggest that certain clones are selected for metastatic dissemination, while others are excluded.

While multiregion sampling provides valuable insights into tumor evolution, it remains impractical for routine clinical use due to its high costs, invasiveness and logistical difficulties involved in processing multiple samples simultaneously. A more cost-effective approach could involve pooling RNA or DNA samples from multiple tumor regions before sequencing, thus improving mutation detection and clonal characterization.405 However, this strategy still carries the risk of missing critical tumor regions. The representative sequencing (Rep-Seq) protocol, proposed by Litchfield et al., offers a more feasible alternative for clinical applications.406 It involves homogenizing all residual tumor material, which was not used for standard pathology and analyzing it via next-generation sequencing (NGS). This method has been shown to provide more reproducible results than single-biopsy sequencing, with a 95% similarity rate across Rep-Seq replicates in ccRCC.406 It also successfully detects variants at frequencies as low as 0.15%, allowing for a more accurate reconstruction of clonal architecture.406 In comparison to multiregion sequencing, Rep-Seq avoids the issue of “clonal illusion,” where a mutation may appear clonal in a single biopsy but is subclonal in the broader tumor. In addition, a proof-of-concept study demonstrated that Rep-Seq could detect driver mutations in lung, colorectal and breast cancer samples,407 further supporting its potential for routine clinical use. Although there are still limitations, Rep-Seq presents a promising, more accessible approach to assessing tumor heterogeneity and clonal evolution in clinical settings.

Liquid biopsy

Liquid biopsy, which involves analyzing tumor markers present in bodily fluids, such as tumor-derived extracellular vesicles, nucleosomes, circulating tumor nucleic acids (ctRNA, ctDNA) and CTCs, is gaining recognition as a viable alternative to traditional tissue biopsy408–410 (Fig. 6). This minimally invasive approach has demonstrated significant potential across multiple applications, including cancer screening, prognosis, diagnosis, treatment stratification, monitoring disease progression, evaluation of minimal residual disease and identifying resistance mechanisms early on. While plasma remains the most extensively studied medium for detecting tumor-derived markers, emerging research underscores the potential of other bodily fluids, such as cerebrospinal fluid, pleural effusions, stool, saliva and urine, for cancer-related clinical investigations.411 Unlike tissue biopsies, liquid biopsies can capture molecular alterations from diverse tumor clones, reflecting broader tumor heterogeneity. Additionally, serial analysis of plasma samples offers a straightforward, non-invasive means to monitor the dynamic evolution of cancer over time. Circulating tumor cells

Research on CTCs has expanded significantly in recent years, although their study remains less developed than that of ctDNA. CTCs exhibit phenotypic and genetic heterogeneity that evolves alongside tumor progression.414 Originating from both primary tumors and metastatic sites, CTCs provide a comprehensive snapshot of the molecular landscape of the disease at a given time. Studies have established a correlation between CTC counts and cancer progression, as well as patient survival.415,416 Beyond their prognostic utility, analyzing CTC heterogeneity holds clinical value in elucidating underlying resistance mechanisms, predicting drug resistance, guiding treatment decisions and monitoring tumor responses.417–419 Remarkably, CTCs can form clusters, either alone or in association with nonmalignant cells.420 These clusters contribute to tumor heterogeneity and enhance the metastatic potential by 23- to 50-fold compared to individual CTCs. Understanding the biology of metastatic seeding could benefit from the characterization and identification of these clusters. CTC-based approaches typically require their isolation from blood samples before conducting multi-omics or single-cell analyses, including proteomic, transcriptomic, epigenetic and genetic studies.421 Single-cell analysis of CTCs offers high-resolution insights into tumor heterogeneity. Despite their promise, isolating CTCs poses significant technical challenges due to their scarcity in the bloodstream (ranging from 0.1 to 10 CTCs per milliliter of blood, representing just 1:106–1:108 blood cells).422 Current enrichment methods leverage biological characteristics (lack of CD45 staining and epithelial cell marker expression) or physical properties (electric charge, density, deformability and size) for selective isolation.423 The CellSearch system, the first FDA-approved technique for CTC detection, identifies cells based on markers such as cytokeratins and EpCAM, with the exclusion of CD45, and applies to metastatic colorectal, prostate and breast cancers.424,425 However, the absence of tumor-specific markers in some cases may lead to false positives or negatives in CTC-based assays.

Circulating tumor DNA

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA), released into bodily fluids through necrosis, apoptosis and active secretion (sometimes within nucleoprotein complexes or extracellular vesicles), originates from both nonmalignant and malignant cells.426,427 Among cfDNA, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) comprises a small subset derived specifically from tumor cells, including those at metastatic and primary sites. Typically, ctDNA is fragmented (approximately 146 base pairs) and harbors tumor-specific epigenetic and genetic alterations. In recent years, ctDNA has garnered significant attention for its potential applications in cancer prognosis, diagnostics, tracking clonal evolution, monitoring therapeutic responses, treatment planning and detecting resistance mechanisms at early stages.428,429 For example, the Rep-Seq study evaluated plasma samples across time points to explore tumor heterogeneity in ccRCC.406 However, reproducibility between plasma samples was limited, with only 24% similarity between pairs, suggesting challenges in capturing comprehensive clonal structures using plasma-derived ctDNA due to temporal tumor evolution and missed truncal mutations. Similarly, the TRACERx study investigated ctDNA’s clinical utility in monitoring cancer evolution in NSCLC.430 Tumor phylogenies were reconstructed using multiregion exome sequencing, followed by patient-specific ctDNA assays targeting clonal and subclonal variants. Pre-surgery plasma samples detected variants in nearly half of the patients, with higher variant allele frequencies for clonal mutations correlating with tumor volume. Subclonal variants were more prevalent in patients experiencing tumor relapse, often appearing in plasma long before radiological detection (average lead time: 70 days).430 This study underscored ctDNA’s capacity to predict relapse, monitor treatment resistance and track metastatic subclones over time. Despite its promise, ctDNA’s utility is constrained by its low concentration in total cfDNA, often constituting less than 0.01%. Recent advances in ultrasensitive detection methods and silico error-correction tools have improved ctDNA analysis.431,432 Additionally, profiling ctDNA-specific characteristics, such as fragment length and methylation pattern, facilitates discrimination from nontumor-derived cfDNA.433,434 However, challenges remain in differentiating tumor-specific mutations from age-associated clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), a condition marked by somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cells.435 CHIP can lead to mutations in genes related to signaling pathways (GNAS, JAK2), DNA damage repair (PPM1D, TP53) and epigenetic regulation (TET2, DNMT3A).436–438 Such mutations may mimic tumor-specific alterations, especially in individuals exposed to therapies that select for clones with DNA repair deficiencies.

Tumor-derived exosomes

Exosomes are ~30–150 nm nanovesicles secreted by almost all mammalian cells (adipocytes, dendritic cells, epithelial cells, mast cells and endothelial cells) and play an irreplaceable role in communication between different cells.439 Exosomes carry a wide range of phenotypically relevant proteins, RNA, DNA and lipids that are closely associated with tumor angiogenesis, tumor metastasis, tumorigenesis and drug resistance. This intricate heterogeneity poses significant challenges for developing exosome-based cancer therapeutics.

Exosomes circulating in body fluids provide physiological and pathological information about an individual’s tumor status. In recent years, exosomes have been recognized as an emerging minimally invasive tool for cancer diagnosis and prognosis. For example, high expression of the serum exosome AnxA2 in patients with TNBC was associated with tumor grade, low overall survival and low disease-free survival.440 Serum exosomal phosphatidylcholine glypican-1 levels are significantly higher in pancreatic cancer patients than in healthy subjects, distinguishing pancreatic cancer patients from healthy subjects and patients with benign pancreatic diseases.441 Elevated circulating exosomal miR-21 has been associated with a variety of tumors (pancreatic, colon, liver and breast cancers, among others), and elevated urinary exosomal miR-21 has been associated with prostate cancer.442 Exosomal miR-34a and miR-146a tumor suppressor miRNAs were associated with breast, liver and pancreatic cancers. Exosomal DNA in body fluids provides information on cancer-specific mutations.443 Circulating exosomal DNA can be used to detect mutations in the pancreatic cancer markers KRAS G12D and TP53 R273H.444

Given that exosomes play an important role in tumor progression, reducing exosome production may be a viable approach to tumor suppression. A number of molecules involved in exosome biogenesis may serve as targets for intervention. For example, ceramide promotes the growth of exosomal vesicles into multivesicular endosomes. Therefore, siRNA-mediated inhibition of neutral sphingomyelinase 2, which is involved in ceramide synthesis, could indirectly reduce exosome secretion.445 However, exosomes have the potential to be used to improve the accuracy and optimize the diagnosis of cancer. Multiple uncharacterized biomarkers should be present on the surface or inside exosomes that can be used as accurate biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and prognosis. Therefore, the use of exosomes in cancer diagnosis and prognosis requires further research.

Summary and outlook

Tumor heterogeneity is one of the most challenging and central issues in tumor biology research, involving multiple levels of complexity.446 From genetic mutations and epigenetic alterations to the cellular composition of the TME and its dynamics, tumor heterogeneity presents multidimensional and complex features at all levels. These features are not only drivers of tumor evolution but also key factors affecting clinical outcomes, especially during therapy resistance, metastasis and recurrence of tumors.447 Despite the growing understanding of tumor heterogeneity in basic research, its dynamics and complexity have constrained the precision and long-term effectiveness of therapeutic strategies.

At the molecular level, tumor heterogeneity arises from genetic alterations such as gene mutations, gene CNVs and chromosomal rearrangements, which result in different molecular characteristics of different clonal populations within a tumor.448 In addition to gene mutations, epigenetic changes (DNA methylation and histone modifications) also play an important role in the maintenance and promotion of tumor heterogeneity.449 These epigenetic alterations may affect tumor biology by altering gene expression patterns and cellular phenotypes, and they show dynamic changes in space and time.450 The evolution of the genome and epigenome of tumor cells at different time points may be influenced by microenvironmental changes, therapeutic interventions and selective pressures from the tumor itself. For example, different patterns of genetic variation may be exhibited between primary tumors and metastases, and this spatiotemporal heterogeneity is a manifestation of the adaptive responses and growth strategies of tumors at different sites.293 In particular, during the course of treatment, tumor cells may evade drug inhibition through genetic mutation, epigenetic reprogramming, or remodeling of the TME, which can lead to relapse and drug resistance.451 These dynamic changes make tumor therapy a great challenge, as conventional therapies are often unable to track and respond to the heterogeneous changes that occur in tumors during treatment.

The dynamics of tumor heterogeneity are one of the biggest technical challenges of current research. The evolution of tumor cells involves not only the accumulation of genetic mutations but also a complex process based on the interaction between the microenvironment and therapeutic pressure. It is difficult for us to capture the evolution of tumors at different time points and different treatment stages through traditional tumor tissue sections and genomic analysis. Therefore, how to monitor the dynamic changes in tumors in real time, especially cellular adaptation during drug treatment and drug resistance, is an urgent need for current research.

Another challenge is sample heterogeneity, especially the large differences between tumor samples obtained at different sampling sites and time points, which makes a systematic panoramic analysis of tumor heterogeneity more difficult. In addition, integration of multi-omics data (genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, etc.) is a great challenge. Multi-omics data are highly dimensional and heterogeneous, and how to standardize, integrate and analyze these massive data and then refine meaningful biomarkers and therapeutic targets is a key issue currently faced.

In future studies of tumor heterogeneity, the application of innovative technologies will be key to driving breakthroughs. For example, multi-omics integration technologies that can combine ST with orthogonal or direct correlation imaging of the proteome, metabolome, lipidome and genome for comprehensive analysis will enable a more comprehensive understanding of shared and distinct spatial ecological niches in heterogeneous tumors, providing a more holistic perspective for revealing tumor heterogeneity. In addition, advances in spatial histology techniques allow us to observe the interactions between the TME and tumor cells at the tissue level, thereby revealing the distribution and dynamics of tumor heterogeneity at the microenvironmental level. It provides new ideas for the study of tumor heterogeneity.
