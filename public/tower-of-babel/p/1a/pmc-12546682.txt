Morphology-regulated millet bran nanocellulose-zein nanoparticles complexes: stabilization of Pickering emulsions and β-carotene delivery☆
Authors: Yulian Zhu, Ziqi Wei, Qian Shi, Qin Dan, Mengqing Li, Xiuzhu Yu, Shuang-kui Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study revealed how the morphology of millet bran nanocelluloses, including cellulose nanocrystals (MCNCs), cellulose nanofibrils (MCNFs), and cellulose nanospheres (MCNSs), regulated the complexation with zein nanoparticles (ZNPs), thereby influencing structure and emulsifying performance of the resulting complexes. Electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interaction induced ‘chain-bead-like’ structures in MCNCs-ZNPs and MCNFs-ZNPs, while MCNSs-ZNPs exhibited a ‘bead-bead-like’ structure. The ‘chain-bead-like’ structures conferred better emulsifying ability, producing emulsions with smaller droplets, higher viscosity, and better stability due to dense continuous phase networks, while ‘bead-bead-like’ structure induced a looser network. Structural advantages also persisted during in vitro digestion of β-carotene-loaded emulsions. MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion preserved droplet integrity under gastric conditions and promoted lipid digestion in intestinal phase, achieving higher β-carotene chemical stability (27.83 %) and bioaccessibility (9.93 %) than MCNFs-ZNPs (21.15 % and 8.02 %) and MCNSs-ZNPs (20.87 % and 6.56 %). Overall, this work highlights morphology-regulated nanocellulose-ZNPs complexes as effective emulsifiers for improving emulsion stability and delivering β-carotene.

Introduction

Nanocellulose, a nanoscale derivative of cellulose, has attracted widespread interest in food and materials science due to its excellent biodegradability, biocompatibility, and thermal stability (Dai et al., 2023). It includes various morphologies, such as cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), cellulose nanospheres (CNSs), and cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs), with each morphology affecting properties such as rheology, viscosity, and emulsifying ability (Zhu et al., 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that nanocellulose reinforces the continuous phase of Pickering emulsions by forming viscous networks, thereby enhancing emulsion stability (Feng et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2022). Notably, nanocellulose can be sustainably extracted from agro-industrial byproducts such as cereal bran, aligning with circular economy principles and enabling high-value utilization of agricultural waste. However, the inherent hydrophilicity of nanocellulose limits its effectiveness as a Pickering emulsion stabilizer. A promising and environmentally friendly approach is the construction of amphiphilic complex emulsifiers by combining nanocellulose with other food-grade proteins (Meng et al., 2024). Protein complexation introduces hydrophobic domains onto the cellulose, imparting amphiphilicity to the complexes, which promotes irreversible interfacial adsorption and steric droplet stabilization (Dai et al., 2023). Among the proteins, zein nanoparticles (ZNPs) is considered an ideal candidate for designing plant-based emulsifiers, owing to the unique hydrophobicity and near-neutral isoelectric point, which facilitate electrostatic assembly with negatively charged nanocellulose (Wan et al., 2023). The complexes formed via electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged components are expected to enhance emulsion stability while improving particle dispersion and hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance (Zhang et al., 2024). Although the potential of nanocellulose-protein complexes for emulsion stabilization and delivery has been reported, systematic comparisons of different nanocellulose morphologies, particularly the distinct roles in regulating emulsion properties, gastrointestinal stability, and the delivery of bioactive substances, remain limited.

β-Carotene is a natural lipophilic antioxidant and an important precursor of vitamin A, widely used in functional food development due to its health benefits. However, its hydrophobic nature leads to extremely low solubility in aqueous systems, and it is highly susceptible to degradation by environmental factors such as light, oxygen, and metal ions, significantly limiting its direct incorporation into water-based foods. Moreover, its polyene backbone is highly susceptible to isomerization and oxidative degradation under acidic and enzymatic conditions, resulting in substantial loss during gastrointestinal transit (Molet-Rodríguez et al., 2025). Previous studies have demonstrated that oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions can serve as effective carriers, encapsulating, protecting β-carotene, and improving its solubility and bioaccessibility in the digestive tract. For instance, Zhou et al. (2023) used oxidized protein-based O/W emulsions to deliver β-carotene, demonstrating that moderately oxidized protein exhibited the optimal protective effect, inhibiting oxidation and degradation of β-carotene during simulated gastric digestion. Habtegebriel et al. (2024) reported that β-carotene encapsulation in O/W emulsions stabilized by milk proteins enhanced its chemical stability and reduced degradation. Nevertheless, most studies have employed single food components, particularly proteins, to stabilize β-carotene-loaded emulsions. Although proteins exhibit a certain degree of interfacial stability, exposure to gastric acid and bile salts can induce conformational changes, oxidative degradation, and collapse of the interfacial film, ultimately causing emulsion instability, phase separation, and premature release of active compounds (Dai et al., 2023). Consequently, developing nanocellulose-protein complexes as emulsifiers with enhanced gastrointestinal stability has become a key strategy for functional emulsion systems. Such complexes can form dense, resilient interfacial films, thereby preserving emulsion integrity during digestion and enabling effective encapsulation, sustained release, and targeted delivery of lipophilic bioactive substances.

In our previous work, nanocelluloses with distinct morphologies (millet bran cellulose nanocrystals, MCNCs; nanofibrils, MCNFs; and nanospheres, MCNSs) were successfully prepared and exhibited marked differences in structure and properties. Specifically, MCNCs possessed a needle-like structure with good dispersibility and higher surface charge, while MCNFs displayed a tangled fibrous structure with the largest aspect ratio, and MCNSs showed a spherical shape with the best dispersibility (Zhu et al., 2024). Due to these structural differences, the rheological properties and network-forming abilities of the three nanocelluloses varied significantly. These structural variations may influence interactions with proteins, thereby affecting the emulsifying properties of the complexes and the digestive behavior of the corresponding emulsions in the gastrointestinal tract.

Hence, it was hypothesized that negatively charged nanocelluloses with different morphologies extracted from millet bran can be electrostatically complexed with positively charged ZNPs to yield stable and amphiphilic particles. Moreover, morphology-regulated complexation was expected to dictate emulsion structure and gastrointestinal stability. Accordingly, this study systematically investigated and compared the roles of MCNCs, MCNFs, and MCNSs in the complexation mechanism and functional properties of nanocellulose-ZNPs complexes, as well as the effects on the emulsifying performance and digestive fate of β-carotene-loaded emulsions (Fig. 1). Materials and methods

Materials

Millet bran was provided by the Shenmu Agricultural Technology Promotion Center (Yulin, China). Ammonium persulfate (APS) and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([BMIM]Cl) were purchased from Macklin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Zein (from corn, purity = 92 %), medium chain triglyceride (MCT) and Nile Red were obtained from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Calcofluor White stain was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation of millet bran nanocelluloses and zein nanoparticles

Millet bran cellulose nanocrystals, nanofibrils, and nanospheres were prepared via APS oxidation, ball milling combined with [BMIM]Cl, and phosphoric acid dissolution/regeneration, as described by Zhu et al. (2024).

ZNPs were prepared using the anti-solvent precipitation method, according to Zhang et al. (2024). Briefly, zein (10 g) was dissolved in 300 mL of 80 % (v/v) ethanol, stirred overnight at 25 °C, and then poured into 600 mL of distilled water. After 30 min of stirring, ethanol and part of the water were removed under vacuum at 45 °C to obtain a 1.5 wt% ZNPs suspension.

Self-assembly of complexes

The complexes were prepared by mixing suspensions of MCNCs, MCNFs, or MCNSs with a ZNPs suspension (both 1.5 wt%, pH 4.0) at mass ratios of 8:1, 4:1, 2:1, and 1:1 (v/v), followed by stirring at 800 rpm for 30 min at 25 °C. The resulting complexes were designated as MCNCs-ZNPs (CZ1–4), MCNFs-ZNPs (FZ1–4), and MCNSs-ZNPs (SZ1–4), respectively. All samples were stored at 4 °C for subsequent analyses, and portions were freeze-dried for structural characterization.

Morphology observation

Following Zhu et al. (2024), scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Nova Nano SEM-450, FEI, USA), transmission electron microscopy (TEM; HT7800, Hitachi, Japan), and atomic force microscopy (AFM; Multimode-8, Bruker, USA) were employed to characterize the microstructure and morphology of the complexes (CZ4, FZ4, and SZ4).

For SEM, the complex suspension (0.01 %, w/v) was placed onto a silicon wafer, dried overnight, mounted on a sample stage, and sputter-coated with gold before imaging at an accelerating voltage of 50 kV.

For TEM, the complex suspension (0.01 %) was dropped onto a copper grid, stained with uranyl acetate (0.25 %), dried overnight, and imaged at 80 kV.

For AFM, the complex suspension (0.001 %) was deposited onto freshly cleaved mica, dried overnight, and scanned in tapping mode.

Molecular docking

To predict potential non-covalent interactions between cellulose and zein, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. The three-dimensional structure of zein (UniProt ID: Q41844) was obtained from the UniProt database (Lei & Lee, 2024), while cellulose and nanocellulose were modeled using 20 and 2 repeating β-d-glucose units, respectively, based on previous studies (Liu, Ying, Sanguansri, Cai and Le, 2018; Shahzadi et al., 2023). Zein was designated as the receptor, while cellulose or nanocellulose was defined as the ligand, with the docking grid encompassing the entire protein. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was regarded as the most favorable, and the interactions were visualized using PyMOL software.

Physicochemical characterization

Zeta potential

Following Ma et al. (2023), sample suspensions (3 mg/mL, diluted with distilled water) were analyzed in triplicate at 25 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer (ZEN 3600, UK).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Following Han et al. (2022), freeze-dried complexes and ZNPs powders were mixed with KBr at a ratio of 1:100 (w/w) and pressed into pellets. Spectra (400–4000 cm−1, 4 cm−1 resolution, 16 scans) were collected using a VERTEX 70 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR, Bruker, Germany). The secondary structure of the protein was further analyzed using PeakFit software (v4.12).

Raman spectra

Raman spectra (200–3000 cm−1) were recorded using an inVia Raman microscope (Renishaw, UK) equipped with a 785 nm laser, as described by Lu et al. (2024). Spectra were collected within the range of 200–3000 cm−1 with a resolution of 1 cm−1. The phenylalanine band at 1003 cm−1 was used as an internal standard for normalization.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

Referring to Wan et al. (2023), the intrinsic fluorescence of samples (2 mg/mL) was measured using an F-7000 spectrofluorometer (Hitachi, Japan). Emission spectra were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm, within the emission range of 290–450 nm, using a 5 nm slit bandwidth and a scan speed of 6000 nm/min.

Rheological property

Following Ma et al. (2023), the apparent viscosity of the complexes was measured using a DHR-1 rheometer (TA Instruments, USA) equipped with a 20 mm parallel plate (1 mm gap) at 25 °C, covering a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1.

Emulsifying performance

Preparation of Pickering emulsions

According to Zhu et al. (2024), O/W Pickering emulsions were prepared by gradually mixing 5 mL of MCT with 10 mL of complex suspension, followed by homogenizing at 10000 rpm for 3 min using a high-speed homogenizer (Ultra-Turrax IKA T18, Staufen, Germany). Control emulsions stabilized solely by nanocellulose suspensions or a ZNPs suspension were also prepared under the same conditions.

Creaming index measurement

Referencing Liu et al. (2024), creaming stability was evaluated by calculating the creaming index (CI) using the following equation:CI(%)=HCHT×100where HC stands for the height of the cream layer (cm), and HT represents the total height of the emulsion (cm).

Microstructure observation

Emulsion microstructures were observed using an optical microscope (IX71, Olympus, Japan) based on the method of Feng et al. (2023). A 10 μL sample was placed on a concave glass slide and covered with a coverslip. Images were captured at 10× magnification.

Droplet size and zeta potential measurement

The average droplet size of the emulsions was determined using a laser diffraction particle size analyzer (LS13320, Beckman Coulter, USA) based on the Fraunhofer model in triplicate at 25 °C (Ma et al., 2023). Zeta potential was measured in triplicate at 25 °C using the Malvern Zetasizer after diluting the emulsions 500-fold with distilled water (Zhu et al., 2024). MCT was used as a control.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy measurement

The microstructure of emulsions was further observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) equipped with a 20× objective lens. Referencing Ni et al. (2022) with some modifications, 1 mL of emulsion was mixed with 40 μL of mixed stain (0.1 % Calcofluor White and 0.1 % Nile Red mixed at a 1:1 ratio) to label the complexes and oil phase, respectively. The mixture was vortexed for 10 s and mounted on a slide with a coverslip. Excitation wavelengths were set at 405 nm and 488 nm, respectively.

Rheological behavior

Following Feng et al. (2023), the rheological behavior of emulsions was evaluated using a rotational rheometer equipped with a 20 mm parallel plate (1 mm gap). Apparent viscosity was measured over a shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1.

Evaluation of β-carotene delivery using complexes stabilized Pickering emulsions

β-Carotene-loaded emulsions were prepared as described in Section 2.8.1, using 0.05 % (w/v) β-carotene dissolved in MCT as the oil phase.

The digestive properties of emulsions loaded with β-carotene were assessed according to a previously reported method with some modifications (Zhou et al., 2023). Simulated digestive fluids, including simulated salivary fluid (SSF), simulated gastric fluid (SGF), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF), were prepared in advance and stored at 4 °C.

Oral phase: 7.5 mL of β-carotene-loaded emulsion was mixed with 7.5 mL of SSF and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with constant shaking.

Gastric phase: 15 mL of the oral digest was combined with 15 mL of SGF (containing 0.045 % w/v pepsin), and the pH was adjusted to 2.5 with 1 M HCl. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 120 min with constant shaking.

Intestinal phase: An equal volume of SIF was added to the gastric digest (with pH adjusted to 7.0), followed by sequential addition of 1.7 mL of salt solution (3.67 % w/v CaCl2 and 21.91 % w/v NaCl), 3.5 mL of 5.36 % w/v bile salt solution, 5 mL of 3.2 % w/v pancreatic lipase, and 5 mL of 0.36 % w/v trypsin. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 120 min with constant shaking. All reagents in this stage were prepared using phosphate-buffered saline (0.2 M, pH 7.0).

Physicochemical characterization

At each digestion stage, droplet size, size distribution, and microstructure were determined as described in Section 2.8.

Determination of fatty acid release rate

During the intestinal phase, the pH was maintained at 7.0 by continuously adding 0.25 M NaOH. Free fatty acid (FFA) release was calculated based on NaOH consumption, using the following equation according to a previous study (Gomes, Costa, Cardoso, Furtado and Cunha, 2020):FFA%=VNaOH×CNaOH×MlipidWlipid×100where VNaOH is the volume of NaOH added (L), CNaOH is the concentration of NaOH (M), Mlipid is the average molecular weight of MCT (492 g/mol), and Wlipid is the total amount of lipid in the emulsion system (g).

Chemical stability and bioaccessibility of β-carotene

Based on the methods reported by Sorasitthiyanukarn et al. (2022) and Zhang et al. (2021), samples (2 mL) were collected before and after each digestive phase. Final digesta were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 30 min) to isolate the micellar phase. Digesta and micelle were extracted with n-hexane/ethanol (3:2, v/v), vortexed for 1 min, stored overnight at 4 °C, and centrifuged (5000 rpm, 10 min). The absorbance of the supernatant at 450 nm was used to calculate the β-carotene concentration based on the calibration curve (y = 7.8056× + 0.1111, R2 = 0.9996). Chemical stability and bioaccessibility were calculated using the following equations:Chemical stability%=CdigestaCinitial×100Bioaccessibility%=CmicelleCinitial×100where Cdigesta, Cmicelle, and Cinitial are the β-carotene concentrations in the digesta after whole digestion, in the micellar phase, and the initial emulsion, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All trials in this study were performed independently in triplicate. The obtained data were expressed as mean±standard deviation. The one-way fixed-effects analysis of variance (ANOVA) test with Duncan's Multiple Range test was conducted to evaluate significant differences between means (p<0.05). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, New York, USA).

Results and discussion

Morphological observation of complexes

The morphology of the complexes was comprehensively characterized using SEM, TEM, and AFM (Fig. 2), revealing considerable variations among the different samples. ZNPs prepared via the antisolvent precipitation method exhibited smooth, spherical particles with an average size of 204.0±0.5 nm (Fig. S1 and Fig. S2). MCNCs, featuring a needle-like structure, served as effective anchoring substrates for ZNPs. SEM, TEM, and AFM images (Fig. 2 A1-C1) showed that MCNCs uniformly anchored ZNPs, yielding compact and well-dispersed complexes. In MCNFs-ZNPs (Fig. 2 A2-C2), a more entangled, fibrous structure was observed. MCNFs formed a continuous web-like network that supported ZNPs attachment, producing a compact, cross-linked structure. Localized ZNPs aggregation was also evident, likely driven by physical entrapment and electrostatic attraction within the nanofibrillar mesh. The high aspect ratio and viscosity of MCNFs may facilitate interparticle bridging, thereby promoting network formation and partial clustering (Ni et al., 2022). MCNSs-ZNPs (Fig. 2 A3-C3) exhibited spherical morphologies for both components, leading to strong particle-particle adhesion and the formation of large, irregular aggregates. The interactions within MCNSs-ZNPs may be governed mainly by nonspecific surface contacts, resulting in poor dispersibility, low uniformity, and pronounced aggregation. These structural variations were dictated by the intrinsic morphology of the nanocellulose and its interaction behavior with ZNPs. According to the electrostatic complexation model proposed by de Kruif et al. (2004), polysaccharide-protein complexes typically form ‘chain-bead-like’ assemblies, in which needle-like or flexible polyanionic chains bind oppositely charged globular proteins through electrostatic interactions. This mechanism was evident in the MCNCs- and MCNFs-based systems, in which ZNPs were embedded like ‘pearls’ on the chain-like structures of MCNC or MCNF ‘necklaces’. As for MCNSs-ZNPs, the spherical ZNPs and MCNSs adhered to each other to form aggregates, which can be described as a ‘bead-bead-like’ structure. Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking simulations (AutoDock Vina, 10 runs) were performed to predict non-covalent interactions between zein and cellulose-based ligands. The optimal docking results, characterized by the lowest binding energies of −4.5 kcal/mol for zein-cellulose and − 5.6 kcal/mol for zein-nanocellulose, are presented in Fig. 3A. A lower binding energy indicates a more stable conformation and a greater likelihood of spontaneous complex formation (Lei et al., 2024). These results indicated that nanocellulose exhibited a stronger tendency to form complexes with proteins compared with native cellulose. Multiple hydrogen bonds were identified between zein and both cellulose and nanocellulose molecules. For cellulose, nine hydrogen bonds were formed with four zein residues: 3.2 Å to Asn169, 2.9 Å, 3.1 Å, and 3.3 Å to Asn211, 2.0 Å to Thr208 and 2.0 Å and 3.3 Å to Tyr173. For nanocellulose, seven hydrogen bonds were observed with six residues in zein, with bond lengths of 2.1 Å to Asn211 and 2.6 Å to Thr208, 3.3 Å to Gln130 and 3.0 Å to Gln134, and 3.1 Å and 3.3 Å to Gln175. These results revealed that multiple binding sites on zein were involved in hydrogen bonding with both cellulose and nanocellulose. Notably, the smaller molecular size of nanocellulose may facilitate access to a greater number of protein residues, thereby enhancing its interaction potential. Structure characterization

Zeta potential

As shown in Fig. S3, the zeta potential of ZNPs varies with pH, exhibiting an isoelectric point at approximately pH 5.25 and a relatively high positive charge of +37.0 mV at pH 4.0. All complexes exhibited negative zeta potentials, with absolute magnitudes lower than those of the corresponding nanocelluloses alone (Fig. 3B). With increasing proportions of ZNPs in the complexes, the absolute zeta potential values decreased, indicating partial charge neutralization due to electrostatic attraction between the oppositely charged components (Guldiken et al., 2023). This observation supported the formation of complexes primarily through electrostatic attraction, which was consistent with previous findings on nanoparticle complexation mechanisms (Yang et al., 2023). Moreover, the distinct zeta potential values were strongly correlated with the morphological differences of the complexes. Among them, MCNCs-ZNPs exhibited the highest absolute zeta potential, providing sufficient electrostatic repulsion to maintain dispersion stability and prevent aggregation, consistent with the morphological observations in Fig. 2. In contrast, MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs exhibited lower absolute zeta potential values, which may be attributed to the inherently lower surface charges of MCNFs and MCNSs. Additionally, the lower absolute zeta potential values may result from the more clustered and aggregated structures of MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs, which shield surface functional groups within the bundles or aggregates, thereby reducing the measurable surface charge (Zhang et al., 2023).

FTIR analysis

Fig. 3 C1—C3 and Fig. S4 present the FTIR spectra of the complexes and pure ZNPs. Compared with the spectra of the three nanocelluloses reported in our previous study (Zhu et al., 2024), no new characteristic peaks appeared after complexation, and the typical cellulose peaks were retained, indicating that the interaction was primarily driven by non-covalent interactions. The intensity of several cellulose bands decreased with increasing ZNPs content, implying partial encapsulation of surface functional groups. All complexes exhibited a shift or broadening of the -OH stretching vibration band near 3300 cm−1, indicating the formation of additional intermolecular hydrogen bonding between nanocellulose and ZNPs (Zhang et al., 2023). These results were consistent with the hydrogen bonding predicted by molecular docking (Section 3.2), further confirming that hydrogen bonding was a key interaction in complexes. In the complexes, slight shifts in the amide I (1654 cm−1 to1654–1660 cm−1) and amide II (1541 cm−1 to 1539–1541 cm−1) bands were observed, suggesting hydrophobic interaction between nanocellulose and ZNPs (Lin et al., 2024). Deconvolution of the amide I region (Fig. 3D) revealed a decrease in α-helix and β-turn contents, together with an increase in β-sheet content and random coil structure, compared with pure ZNPs. These results suggested that nanocellulose incorporation altered the polarity of the microenvironment within ZNPs, leading to conformational changes (Yang et al., 2024). The reduction in α-helix content in the complexes suggested that interactions between nanocellulose and ZNPs induced partial unfolding of the protein, exposing previously buried residues (Yang et al., 2023). This result also aligned with the molecular docking predictions, which suggested that small-sized nanocellulose effectively interacted with protein residues, potentially inducing structural changes and enhancing intermolecular interactions. Additionally, these findings indicated that the incorporation of nanocellulose promoted the relaxation and unfolding of the native protein structure, facilitating the formation of intermolecular interactions within the complexes. The increase in β-sheet content, which is typically associated with protein aggregation (Qu et al., 2024), further supported that complexation with nanocellulose enhanced hydrogen bonding within protein molecules. These structural changes are also corroborated by the microstructural observations in Fig. 2, where ZNPs aggregate within the complexes.

Raman spectra analysis

The normalized Raman band at 760 cm−1 (I760), attributed to the indole-ring stretching of tryptophan, is highly sensitive to hydrophobic microenvironments (Kuhar et al., 2021). As depicted in Fig. 3 E1-E2, ZNPs exhibited the lowest I760 intensity, while all complexes showed higher values, indicating that tryptophan residues were more deeply buried in hydrophobic domains upon complexation. The highest I760 was observed for CZ4, possibly due to additional -COOH groups introduced via APS oxidation on MCNCs (Zhu et al., 2024), suggesting stronger hydrophobic interaction. Raman bands near 830 cm−1 and 850 cm−1 reflect the microenvironment of tyrosine residues. The intensity ratio I850/I830 indicates hydrogen bonding states involving phenolic hydroxyl groups in tyrosine side chains (Wang et al., 2023). Ratios between 1.0 and 2.5 typically indicate that tyrosine residues are exposed to and engaged in weak hydrogen bonding, whereas values below 1.0 suggest that tyrosine residues are buried and act as hydrogen bonding donors (Zhao et al., 2023). The I850/I830 ratios (Fig. 3 E2) of all complexes were lower than that of pure ZNPs, suggesting that tyrosine residues were more embedded within the complexes, thereby enhancing hydrogen bonding. Among the complexes, FZ4 exhibited the lowest I850/I830 value, indicating the strongest hydrogen bonding, likely due to extensive encapsulation of ZNPs by the long fibrous structure of MCNFs (Fig. 2). The Raman band near 1450 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of aliphatic residues (Lu et al., 2024). The increased intensity (I1450) in all complexes compared with ZNPs alone indicated that the interaction between nanocelluloses and ZNPs promoted deeper burial of aliphatic side chains and further structural changes in proteins (Zhao et al., 2023), consistent with the FTIR analysis. Notably, the aliphatic residues (Thr, Asn, and Gln) and the tyrosine residue, which exhibited spectral changes, were also the residues predicted by molecular docking to be involved in the interaction between nanocellulose and zein. These results were consistent with the prediction, highlighting Thr, Asn, Gln, and Tyr residues as crucial contributors to the interactions between nanocellulose and ZNPs. Structural changes in these residues further supported the presence of both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interaction within the nanocellulose-protein complexes. Together with FTIR results, it can be concluded that nanocellulose engaged in both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interaction with ZNPs, thereby inducing the formation of stable complexes.

Fluorescence microscopy image

The fluorescence emission of complexes (Fig. 4 A1-A3) originates from tyrosine (Tyr) and tryptophan (Trp) residues (Cai et al., 2022). Variations in fluorescence intensity therefore reflect alterations in the polarity and hydrophobicity of the aromatic microenvironments (Zhang et al., 2024). At a nanocelluloses to ZNPs mass ratio of 8:1, limited protein loading and extensive nanocellulose coverage resulted in weak fluorescence despite strong interfacial interactions. Increasing the ratio to 4:1 produced the highest intensity, indicating that enhanced nanocellulose-ZNPs binding and chain entanglement partially unfolded zein, thereby exposing buried Trp residues (Cai et al., 2022). Further increases in ZNPs content (2:1 and 1:1) promoted ZNPs aggregation, and the lower nanocellulose contents cannot fully interact with excess ZNPs, leading to shielding of aromatic residues and a corresponding decrease in fluorescence intensity. Similar results were also reported by Liu et al. (2022) for protein-carboxymethyl cellulose complexes, where strong encapsulation resulted in weak fluorescence. Among the three nanocellulose morphologies, the MCNCs-ZNPs series exhibited the highest fluorescence intensities, implying the most effective binding and protein unfolding. In contrast, the MCNFs-ZNPs series exhibited the lowest intensities, consistent with dense encapsulation of ZNPs within a fibrous network. Rheological property analysis

Fig. 4 B1—B3 shows the apparent viscosities of the complexes. All samples exhibited shear-thinning behavior characteristic of pseudoplastic, non-Newtonian fluids, with viscosity decreasing over the shear rate range of 0.1–100 s−1, reflecting the gradual disruption of the nanocellulose-ZNPs network. Among the three types, MCNFs-ZNPs showed the highest viscosities, followed by MCNCs-ZNPs, whereas MCNSs-ZNPs exhibited the lowest. The elevated viscosities in MCNFs-ZNPs systems may be attributed to the fibrous structure and larger particle size of MCNFs, which promote network entanglement and aggregation (Feng et al., 2023). In contrast, the higher surface charge and better dispersion of MCNCs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs reduced interparticle associations, resulting in lower viscosities (Cai et al., 2021). Apparent viscosity increased with rising ZNPs content, indicating progressive network reinforcement. This effect was dose-dependent in the MCNCs-ZNPs and MCNFs-ZNPs systems but was less pronounced in the MCNSs-ZNPs series. These differences may be attributed to morphology-regulated network formation. Fibrous MCNFs-ZNPs formed extensive entanglements that generated a dense network structure with high viscosity but also promoted potential aggregation. Needle-like MCNCs, with a large surface area, strongly adsorbed ZNPs and built a uniform ‘chain-bead-like’ structure that balanced viscosity and uniform dispersion. Aggregated MCNSs-ZNPs cannot form continuous networks, resulting in a weak ‘bead-bead-like’ structure and minimal viscosity enhancement.

Particle size and zeta potential characterization

The morphology of complexes significantly influenced the average droplet size and particle size distribution of emulsions (Fig. 5 E2 and Figure F1-F3). Emulsions stabilized by MCNCs and MCNCs-ZNPs exhibited the smallest droplets (51.89±2.77 μm and 32.74±0.90 μm to 49.90±3.64 μm, respectively), while emulsions stabilized by MCNSs (45.01±0.61 μm), MCNSs-ZNPs (49.83±0.75 μm to 73.65±3.56 μm), MCNFs (65.87±5.48 μm), and MCNFs-ZNPs (56.21±5.92 μm to 89.56±8.78 μm) showed progressively larger sizes. The increase in droplet size observed in MCNSs-ZNPs (SZ2-SZ4) was attributed to particle aggregation within the ‘bead-bead-like’ structure, which became more pronounced as the ZNPs content increased. Conversely, MCNCs-ZNPs formed compact amphiphilic complexes via the ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure, yielding smaller and more uniform droplets. All complexes exhibited narrower, left-shifted size distributions compared with the nanocellulose-only controls, confirming that incorporation of ZNPs enhanced emulsifying efficiency and promoted the formation of finer, more stable droplets.

All emulsions, including the MCT control, exhibited negative surface charges (Fig. 5 E3). Notably, emulsions stabilized by complexes showed higher absolute zeta potential values than MCT, likely due to enhanced surface charges resulting from polarization and ionization during homogenization (Badar et al., 2024). The zeta potential values closely followed those of the corresponding stabilizers, indicating that the electrostatic properties of the complexes predominantly governed the droplet surface charge (Liu et al., 2024). Among the samples, MCNFs-ZNPs emulsions exhibited the lowest absolute zeta potential values, likely due to the inherently lower surface charge (Fig. 3B) and the shielding effect of the entangled fibrous network, which may encapsulate MCT molecules and mask interfacial charges. Together with the particle size data, these results confirmed that emulsion stability was regulated by the morphology of complexes. Specifically, the ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNCs-ZNPs produced smaller and more uniform emulsion droplets, whereas the ‘long chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNFs-ZNPs, despite exhibiting strong entanglement, showed reduced electrostatic stabilization and produced larger droplets. MCNSs-ZNPs formed a loose ‘bead-bead-like’ structure, resulting in unstable emulsions. These morphology-regulated differences in particle size and surface charge aligned with microstructural observations, confirming that nanocellulose morphology governed droplet refinement and electrostatic stabilization.

CLSM observation

To further investigate the spatial distribution and structural behavior of the complexes within emulsions, CLSM images were captured and presented in Fig. 6A. Emulsion stabilized by MCNCs-ZNPs exhibited uniformly distributed and closely packed droplets, with visible dispersion of complexes in the continuous phase. This observation suggested effective interfacial film formation and network construction, which contributed to enhanced emulsion stability. Conversely, emulsion stabilized by MCNFs-ZNPs displayed irregularly shaped droplets and evidence of coalescence into larger clusters, likely due to flocculation and insufficient interfacial barriers. The entangled fibrous structures of MCNFs-ZNPs likely hindered effective interfacial adsorption, with the complexes contributing predominantly to continuous phase thickening rather than to interfacial reinforcement (Ni et al., 2022), which explained the larger droplet sizes. Emulsion stabilized by MCNSs-ZNPs showed loosely packed droplets and scattered aggregates in the continuous phase, indicating poor structural integrity. Although MCNSs-ZNPs can absorb at the interface, the low intrinsic viscosity may hinder network formation, thereby reducing resistance to droplet motion and accelerating phase separation (Feng et al., 2023). These observations further indicated the morphology-regulated stabilization behaviors of the complexes. The ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNCs-ZNPs may facilitate effective interfacial adsorption and robust network formation, aligning with the smaller droplet sizes and higher absolute zeta potentials (Fig. 5). In contrast, ‘long chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNFs-ZNPs may have contributed mainly to filling in the continuous phase but failed to provide sufficient interfacial coverage. The ‘bead-bead-like’ structure of MCNSs-ZNPs may lead to weak adsorption and limited network formation, thus explaining the poor emulsion stability. Rheological behavior analysis

Fig. 6B illustrates the apparent viscosities of the emulsions. All emulsions exhibited shear-thinning behavior over 0.01–100 s−1, characteristic of pseudoplastic fluids, owing to progressive disruption of the nanocellulose-ZNPs network under shear. Viscosity increased with rising ZNPs content in all nanocellulose systems, confirming that the complexes reinforced the continuous phase network (Feng et al., 2023). The viscosity of emulsions was strongly influenced by the morphology of the complexes. Emulsions stabilized by MCNCs-ZNPs and MCNFs-ZNPs exhibited higher viscosities than those stabilized by MCNSs-ZNPs, reflecting the intrinsic viscosity differences of the corresponding complexes (Fig. 4 B1—B3). The higher viscosity of MCNCs-ZNPs emulsions can be attributed to the higher absolute zeta potential values (Fig. 3B) and the uniform ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure, which may facilitate rapid dispersion, interfacial adsorption, and partial thickening of the continuous phase. These effects inhibited droplet coalescence, resulting in finer droplets and contributing to the formation of a three-dimensional network among the emulsion droplets. These behaviors corresponded with the microstructural observations, in which MCNCs-ZNPs produced uniformly distributed and closely packed droplets, indicating effective interfacial film formation and the construction of a dense network that collectively inhibited droplet coalescence. In contrast, MCNFs-ZNPs generated the highest viscosities due to the dense and adherent fibrous network that thickened the continuous phase but simultaneously limited interfacial coverage (Ni et al., 2022), thereby explaining the larger droplet sizes and lower absolute zeta potentials observed. This interpretation was further supported by CLSM images, which displayed irregularly shaped droplets and flocculated clusters, consistent with inadequate interfacial stabilization despite the continuous phase thickening effect. For MCNSs-ZNPs, the ‘bead-bead-like’ structure contributed minimally to emulsion viscosity, resulting in larger and less stable droplets. This was consistent with the larger droplet sizes, loosely packed microstructures, and low CI values. Collectively, these results demonstrated that the morphology of nanocellulose governed the stabilization behavior of the complexes. MCNCs-ZNPs achieved stability through balanced interfacial adsorption and network formation. MCNFs-ZNPs improved the viscosity of the emulsions but led to limited interfacial coverage. MCNSs-ZNPs through weak adsorption and limited network formation. The enhanced stability of emulsions stabilized by MCNCs-ZNPs and MCNFs-ZNPs was further supported by the higher viscosities, which facilitated the formation of dense and robust networks within the continuous phase. Consistently, Wan et al. (2023) also reported that fixing zein nanoparticles onto fibrillar carboxymethyl cellulose effectively stabilized Pickering emulsions and improved the viscosity by promoting network formation within the continuous phase of emulsions.

In vitro digestion evaluation of β-carotene-loaded emulsions

Particle size detection and microstructure observation

As shown in Fig. 7A-B, the particle size distribution and microstructure of the three emulsions exhibited distinct differences during the digestion process. Initially, emulsion stabilized by MCNCs-ZNPs displayed the most uniform droplet distribution with well-defined boundaries, whereas emulsions stabilized by MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs contained larger and looser droplets. After the oral phase, droplet sizes in all formulations remained virtually unchanged, and the structures were preserved, indicating that the complexes conferred resistance to dilution and α-amylase activity. During the gastric phase, droplets in MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion remained largely intact, with only minor aggregation, demonstrating strong tolerance to acidic conditions and pepsin hydrolysis (Feng et al., 2024; Molet-Rodríguez et al., 2025). By contrast, emulsions stabilized by MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs showed pronounced aggregation and flocculation, forming large, irregular clusters and losing the original droplet morphology, providing evidence of inferior gastric stability. In the intestinal phase, extensive droplet breakdown was observed in all systems, in which the original morphology had largely disappeared, with only a few unhydrolyzed oil droplets remaining, indicative of mixed-micelle formation and advanced lipid digestion (Han et al., 2022). Consistently, particle size distributions shifted toward larger apparent sizes, likely representing aggregated micelles or digestion residues. The superior gastric stability of MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion can be attributed to the uniform ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNCs-ZNPs with high surface charge, which may promote dense interfacial adsorption and strong electrostatic repulsion between droplets. The fibrous ‘long chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNFs-ZNPs mainly thickened the continuous phase but provided insufficient interfacial coverage, thereby explaining the partial coalescence and flocculation during gastric digestion. FFA release

During the intestinal phase, all emulsions showed time-dependent increases in FFA release (Fig. 7 C1). All emulsions released more FFA than bulk MCT, reflecting rapid displacement of interfacial materials by bile salts, which facilitated lipase adsorption. The digestion rate was positively correlated with the specific surface area of the droplets, as smaller droplets provided more surface area for enzyme interaction, thereby promoting more efficient hydrolysis (Han et al., 2022). Among them, the MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion exhibited the smallest droplet size and maintained structural integrity after gastric digestion. These fine droplets were readily coated by bile salts, forming mixed micelles that facilitated lipase penetration and accelerated hydrolysis of the oil core (Molet-Rodríguez et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023). Consequently, the MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion demonstrated a significantly higher FFA release rate during the first 60 min of intestinal digestion (Fig. S6). In contrast, FFA diffusion in the MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs emulsions was hindered by the denser interfacial layers and lower specific surface areas. The larger specific surface area of MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion further enhanced enzyme-substrate interactions, thereby accelerating lipid degradation. Moreover, the interfacial layer stabilized by MCNCs-ZNPs prevented droplet coalescence during intestinal digestion and facilitated rapid interfacial replacement by bile salts, thereby improving lipid accessibility. Although lipase can access the oil surface in bulk MCT, the limited specific surface area of large oil droplets restricted lipolysis efficiency (Zhang et al., 2025). These findings highlight the critical role of complexes in enhancing lipid digestion by increasing the effective interfacial area for enzymatic action. The superior FFA release of MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion was strongly related to the morphology-regulated microstructure. The uniform structure of MCNCs-ZNPs may promote uniform dispersion and effective interfacial adsorption, which facilitated bile salt replacement and rapid lipase access to the oil core. Conversely, MCNFs-ZNPs, due to fibrous entanglement, formed a thicker but less permeable interfacial layer, thereby restricting enzyme penetration and slowing hydrolysis. The aggregated MCNSs-ZNPs may provide weak interfacial adsorption, leading to unstable droplet structures with lower surface area, thereby limiting lipid digestion efficiency.

Chemical stability and bioaccessibility of β-carotene

Emulsions stabilized by complexes with different morphologies exhibited significant differences in β-carotene protection and release (Fig. 7 C2). Among them, the MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion showed the highest chemical stability of β-carotene (27.83±3.12 %), significantly higher than that of MCNFs-ZNPs (21.15±0.22 %) and MCNSs-ZNPs (20.87±1.65 %), and markedly superior to MCT (13.36±1.11 %). These results indicated that the interfacial structure formed by MCNCs-ZNPs provided strong gastrointestinal resistance, effectively encapsulating β-carotene and minimizing its degradation under acidic and enzymatic conditions during gastric digestion. Although MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs emulsions offered partial structural protection, the larger and less stable droplet morphologies were more susceptible to disruption during gastric digestion, resulting in increased leakage and degradation of β-carotene. It is well established that digestive products such as FFA, glycerol, and bile salts can form mixed micelles that solubilize lipophilic bioactives and enhance the absorption (Han et al., 2022; Paredes-Toledo et al., 2024). Hence, higher FFA release is typically associated with greater bioaccessibility. Consistent with this trend, the MCNCs-ZNPs group also exhibited the highest β-carotene bioaccessibility (9.93±0.99 %), significantly exceeding that of MCNFs-ZNPs (8.02±0.72 %), MCNSs-ZNPs (6.56±0.09 %), and the MCT control (6.42±0.13 %). These findings suggested that the MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion not only protected β-carotene during the gastric phase but also facilitated its efficient release and incorporation into mixed micelles during the intestinal phase, thereby enhancing intestinal absorption potential (Molet-Rodríguez et al., 2025; Sorasitthiyanukarn et al., 2022). Overall, the MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion demonstrated superior gastrointestinal resilience, preserved droplet integrity, and promoted efficient micellar transfer of β-carotene. While MCNFs-ZNPs stabilized emulsion mainly through continuous phase thickening, the insufficient droplet integrity led to leakage and degradation. By contrast, MCNSs-ZNPs generated large, aggregated droplets with poor structural stability, which were highly susceptible to enzymatic disruption and resulted in reduced β-carotene retention and release.

Mechanism of formation and emulsion stabilization of the complexes

Based on the above results, Fig. 8 schematically illustrates the complex behavior of nanocelluloses with distinct morphologies and the role in stabilizing Pickering emulsions. Nanocelluloses primarily interacted with ZNPs through electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interaction, thereby serving as amphiphilic bridges at the oil-water interface. This complexation significantly reduced the inherent hydrophilicity and improved the emulsifying ability of nanocelluloses. The interaction behavior was strongly driven by the morphology of nanocelluloses. Needle-like MCNCs densely anchored ZNPs along short chains, forming a ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure that efficiently adsorbed at the interface, resulting in small and uniformly distributed droplets. A portion of these complexes was also dispersed in the continuous phase, thereby inhibiting droplet aggregation. In contrast, long fibrous MCNFs formed an entangled ‘long chain-bead-like’ structure with embedded ZNPs, creating a viscous network that increased system viscosity. However, the larger size and fibrous structure reduced interfacial coverage, leading to droplet coalescence. Spherical MCNSs loosely co-assembled with ZNPs to form an irregular ‘bead-bead-like’ structure, which exhibited the weakest network formation and the poorest emulsion stability. With increasing ZNPs content, additional hydrophobic groups promoted higher viscosity and more robust network formation. The amphiphilic complexes formed interfacial films that resisted Ostwald ripening, while excess complexes dispersed in the continuous phase established a viscous network that limited Brownian motion and droplet aggregation. Thus, ZNPs contributed to Pickering emulsion stabilization via a synergistic mechanism involving interfacial bridging and continuous phase network formation. Among all systems, the ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure of MCNCs-ZNPs achieved the optimal balance of droplet size control, interfacial coverage, and viscosity, thereby offering superior stabilization and delivery potential. This structure-property relationship was further confirmed by in vitro digestion, in which MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion resisted gastric acid, preserved droplet integrity, and protected β-carotene. During the intestinal phase, displacement by bile salts exposed a large interfacial area, facilitated lipase adsorption and lipid digestion, and improved β-carotene bioaccessibility. In contrast, emulsions stabilized by MCNFs-ZNPs and MCNSs-ZNPs collapsed under gastric conditions, resulting in oil leakage, β-carotene degradation, and reduced chemical stability. Overall, incorporating ZNPs into nanocellulose matrices imparted amphiphilic interfacial activity, and the resulting morphology-regulated complexes governed emulsion stability, gastrointestinal resistance, and nutrient delivery efficiency. Conclusion

This study established a morphology-regulated strategy for fabricating amphiphilic complexes by combining oppositely charged nanocelluloses with ZNPs, and revealed how these complexes stabilized Pickering emulsions. Electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interaction synergistically drove the formation of distinct structures, including the ‘short chain-bead-like’ structure for MCNCs-ZNPs, the ‘long chain-bead-like’ structure for MCNFs-ZNPs, and the ‘bead-bead-like’ structure for MCNSs-ZNPs. These morphology-regulated complexes significantly influenced the structural characteristics and emulsifying properties, with ZNPs incorporation enhancing the emulsifying capacity of nanocelluloses in a dose-dependent manner, thereby reducing droplet size and improving dispersibility. Among them, uniform MCNCs-ZNPs produced small emulsion droplets with high structural integrity, fibrous MCNFs-ZNPs generated larger emulsion droplets through strong entanglement, and aggregated MCNSs-ZNPs yielded loose and unstable emulsions. Furthermore, MCNCs-ZNPs and MCNFs-ZNPs established continuous network structures that markedly enhanced emulsion stability and inhibited phase separation, whereas MCNSs-ZNPs failed to form such networks, resulting in low viscosity and poor stability. Importantly, the structural advantages of MCNCs-ZNPs were preserved during in vitro gastrointestinal digestion. The MCNCs-ZNPs emulsion withstood the harsh gastric environment, maintained droplet integrity, facilitated lipid digestion in the intestinal phase, and achieved the highest β-carotene bioaccessibility and chemical stability. Collectively, this study highlighted the pivotal role of nanocellulose morphology in regulating the structural characteristics, emulsion stability, and bioactive delivery of nanocellulose-ZNPs complexes, thereby guiding the design of clean-label emulsifiers in food systems. Future studies can focus on evaluating the structural and stability changes during processing, as well as assessing the feasibility of nanocellulose-ZNPs based emulsions in real food matrices.
