Consumption Patterns of NOVA Food Groups and their Association with Body Mass Index among Jordanian Adults
Authors: Lana M. Agraib, Buthaina Alkhatib, Ahmad Suleiman Aslaih, Asem Taher Shawabkeh, Rahma Aldahabi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNOVA food group consumption has received significant attention recently for assessing the quality of dietary intake and has been linked to weight gain and obesity.Aimto examine the ultra-processed foods (UPFs) and different NOVA food groups among Jordanian adults in various body mass index (BMI) categories.MethodologyA cross-sectional study was conducted among 537 Jordanian adults (aged 19–64) from February to April 2024. Anthropometric data were collected, and habitual dietary intake was assessed using a validated Arabic food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). Foods were classified according to the NOVA system into minimally or unprocessed foods (MUPF), processed culinary ingredients (PCI), processed foods (PF), and UPFs. The total energy from each group was analyzed using ESHA’s Food Processor software.ResultUPFs contributed approximately 52% of daily energy intake; however, total energy intake was not significantly different across BMI categories (p = 0.870). Obese individuals had a higher MUPF intake (% of total Kcal) (31.97% ± 10.63%) and a lower UPF intake (50.57% ± 12.17%) compared to normal-weight individuals (MUPF: 29.85% ± 9.78%; UPF: 53.52% ± 10.99%; p < 0.05). Excess-weight participants also showed a higher MUPF intake (32.32% ± 10.03 vs. 29.74% ± 9.64) and lower UPF intake (50.27% ± 11.32 vs. 53.65% ± 10.86) than those with normal weight (p < 0.01). No significant differences were found for PCI or PF intake.ConclusionDespite generally high UPF consumption, individuals with higher BMI consume more energy from MUPF and less from UPF, suggesting a potential shift toward healthier food choices as body weight increases. Therefore, interventions should also focus on energy balance and portion control in normal-weight people within MUPF, not just UPFs.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex, chronic disease defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an excessive accumulation of fat that affects human body health. Around two billion adults are overweight with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 globally, and approximately 650 million adults are obese with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (Fallah 2024). The prevalence is continuously increasing globally due to low physical activity, changes in food consumption, and a sedentary lifestyle. Approximately half of the world’s adult population is expected to be overweight or obese by 2030 (Ajlouni et al. 2020). Moreover, the change in global food systems has a significant impact on the obesity rate globally (Machado et al. 2020). This led to the replacement of traditional diets with Westernized ones, which are rich in ultra-processed foods (UPFs) (Monteiro et al. 2019).

The NOVA categorization method classifies reported single food items into minimally or unprocessed foods (MUPF), including cereals, legumes, vegetables, fruits, milk, and meat; processed culinary ingredients (PCI), such as table salt, sugar, honey, vegetable oils, and butter; processed foods (PF), which include canned fish, canned vegetables in brine, salted or sugared nuts, freshly made bread, and cheeses; and UPF, such as mass-produced packaged bread, carbonated soft drinks, confections, cookies, breakfast “cereals,” flavored yogurts, reconstituted meat products, ready-to-heat meals, and packaged instant soups and noodles. (Monteiro et al. 2019; Rauber et al. 2018). Additionally, in UPFs, changes to their structural and physical characteristics, as well as the removal of water during processing, and the addition of colors, enhancers, and cosmetic additives, all contribute to the nutritional imbalance of UPFs (Machado et al. 2020).

Numerous global cross-sectional and cohort studies have confirmed the relationship between UPF consumption and weight gain and obesity (Machado et al. 2020). A recent study has shown that UPFs increase the daily calorie consumption of approximately half of Canadian adults (45%), particularly among younger adults, smokers, men, individuals with lower educational levels, those with low physical activity, and those born in Canada (Nardocci et al. 2019). In Brazilian individuals aged 10 years or older, it was found that 30% of total calorie intake came from UPFs, and this was significantly associated with a higher BMI compared to individuals with the lowest levels of consumption (da Louzada et al. 2015). It has been found that participants who consumed UPFs gained 1.1 kg more weight, with 813.4 kcal more per day, compared with those consuming minimally processed or unprocessed foods (MUPFs) (Hamano et al. 2024).

In Jordan, only a single study conducted on children and adolescents concluded that UPF consumption accounted for 40.0% of daily energy intake (Al Hourani et al. 2025). However, there are still concerning studies about UPF consumption and its association with excess weight in Middle Eastern regions. Therefore, we hypothesized that UPF consumption could be higher as BMI increased. This study aims to examine the UPFs and different NOVA food groups among Jordanian adults in various BMI categories.

Materials and Methodology

Study Design and Data Collection

To conduct a cross-sectional study, 537 randomly selected, non-hospitalized adults (aged 19–64) who were Jordanian, not vegetarian, and not following a reduced diet during the study period, and living in urban areas, were recruited. A response rate was 28%. Vegans, vegetarians, and nursing and pregnant women were not included. Those who underreported or overreported, as well as those with an incorrect food frequency questionnaire (FFQ), were also excluded. Data was gathered through in-person interviews with a qualified nutritionist between February and April of 2024. The Institutional Review Board at The Hashemite University (No. 6/1/2023/2024) reviewed and approved the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants who chose to take part provided written consent.

Anthropometric Measurements and Indices

According to established measurement procedures, interviewers used digital scales, vertical stadiometers, and a metal tape measure to obtain and record height (in centimeters) and weight (in kilograms) measurements for the surveys. According to the WHO, particles were divided into various categories, and the BMI was calculated (WHO 2025).

Food Consumption Data

A validated Arabic version of a semi-quantitative FFQ with 157 items was used to measure habitual food consumption (usual) (Aoun et al. 2019), for the past 12 months. The Photographic colorful Jordanian Food Atlas was used to determine the exact amount and quantities of food intake, featuring over 100 foods, to help participants estimate their intake of various foods (Al Hourani et al. 2023). Additionally, Lebanon Food Composition Data: Traditional Dishes, Arabic Sweets, and Market Foods (Food Composition Data 2021), Composition of Local Jordanian Food Dishes (Takruri et al. 2020) were used to identify Jordanian and Arabic dishes not found in the food processor. An automated nutrition analysis program was used to analyze the food intake that was gathered (ESHA’s Food Processor, Nutrition Analysis Software, version 11.0; ESHA’s Food Processor® Nutrition Analysis Software (version 11.15.94, database structure 11.15.1).

The following four groups (and subgroups within these groups) were created using the NOVA categorization method to classify reported single food items and the key components of mixed plates in FFQ: Examples of minimally or unprocessed foods (MUPF) include cereals, legumes, vegetables, fruits, milk, and meat; processed culinary ingredients (PCI) include table salt, sugar, honey, vegetable oils, and butter; processed foods (PF) include canned fish, canned vegetables in brine, salted or sugared nuts, freshly made bread, and cheeses; and ultra-processed foods (UPF) include mass-produced packaged bread, carbonated soft drinks, confections, cookies, breakfast “cereals,” flavored yogurts, reconstituted meat products, ready-to-heat meals, and packaged instant soups and noodles (Monteiro et al. 2019; Rauber et al. 2018). Next, the total calories from each NOVA group were calculated, along with the macronutrient intake across the different groups.

The ratio of reported daily energy intake (EI) to basal metabolic rate (BMR) was calculated to determine the extent of underreporting. According to Black and Coli (2000), participants with an EI: BMR ratio of less than 1.2 were categorized as under-reporters, those with a ratio of 1.2–2.4 as plausible, and those with a ratio of > 2.4 as over-reporters (Black and Cole 2000; Lopes et al. 2016). Starting from 627, the overall total, including participants, was 537, comprising 274 females and 263 males.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 software (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp, USA). Dnormality was tested (Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) and frequencies with percentages (%) were used for categorical descriptive statistics. The independent t-test and ANOVA test were used to compare the means of continuous variables, while the Chi-square (χ2) was used to detect differences among categorical variables.

Discussion

NOVA food classification is now taking considerable attention, especially the UPFs, which are a common component of daily energy consumption in many societies, marking a significant shift in dietary preferences over the past few decades. Due to their well-established link to weight gain, poor nutritional content, and obesity-related health effects, the increasing reliance on UPFs—known for their hyperpalatable composition, ease of use, and extended shelf life—has raised concerns throughout the world. The goal of the current study was to investigate the pattern of NOVA-classified food group intake among Jordanian individuals across different BMI groups. Jordan seems to be no exception in this changing nutritional landscape. In the current study, UPFs contributed the largest share of total daily energy intake in all BMI groups, with a mean contribution of 51.93%. This is significantly greater than reported in Brazil (30.0%) (da Louzada et al. 2015), Korea (25.1%) (Shim et al. 2021), and China (28.5%) (Pan et al. 2023), and is similar to levels in the USA (57.0%) (Cordova et al. 2021), the UK (48.6%) (Rauber et al. 2018), and Lebanon (46.7%) (Khattar et al. 2024).

Contrary to international trends where UPF consumption generally rises with BMI, our results show the opposite pattern. Normal-weight individuals had a greater proportion of energy consumed from UPFs than overweight and obese participants, despite their absolute consumption being similar. Approximately 29.2% of normal-weight individuals fell into the highest quartile of UPF (≥ 59.74%), whereas 28.0% of obese subjects were overrepresented in the lowest quartile (< 44.17%). This finding aligns with research conducted in Malaysia, which revealed that obese adults consumed more MUPF (37.6%) than their normal-weight counterparts (32.8%), and that their energy intake from UPFs was only 42.0% compared to 55.0% for their normal-weight counterparts (Ganesrau et al. 2023). Similarly, Asma et al. (2019) found that UPFs accounted for 36.4% of energy in adults of normal weight and 31.0% in those who were obese. Additionally, obese subjects had higher MUPFs (39.3% vs. 34.5%) (Asma et al. 2019).

These patterns differ from those observed in Western populations. In the United States, UPFs accounted for 49.0% of the energy intake of normal-weight individuals and 58.0% of that of obese adults (Baraldi et al. 2018). According to Marrón-Ponce et al. (2018), UPF consumption rose in Mexico as income and BMI increased, rising from 25.7% in lower-income categories to 30.3% in more overweight groups (Marrón-Ponce et al. 2018). The disparity in our sample may be due to underreporting by people with higher BMIs, dietary behavoiral adaptation, or lagged metabolic effects. The latter is well-established; Huybrechts et al. (2020) found significant inaccuracy, especially among obese individuals, when comparing FFQ data to biomarker validation (Huybrechts et al. 2020). In addition to obesity, the current study found that all BMI groups had poor dietary quality, as evidenced by their low consumption of protein, carbohydrates, and fiber. This is consistent with global trends, where consuming more UPF replaces healthier foods, leading to micronutrient deficiencies, decreased fiber intake, and higher consumption of salt, sugars, and saturated fats (Askari et al. 2020; Moubarac et al. 2017; Rauber et al. 2018). Poor diet diversification and nutritional imbalances were also shown in the Lebanese study by Khattar et al. (2024) among high UPF consumers (Khattar et al. 2024).

Weight gain linked to UPF has been demonstrated to develop over time by longitudinal studies. In a French cohort, Beslay et al. (2020) showed that a 10% increase in UPF intake was associated with a 0.26 kg/m² rise in BMI, with individuals in the top quintile having a 26% higher risk of obesity (Beslay et al. 2020). Rauber et al. (2021) found that the greatest UPF consumers had a 79% increased chance of being obese in the UK Biobank cohort (Rauber et al. 2018). Additionally, Cordova et al. (2021) found that people in the highest UPF tertile had a 9% higher risk of obesity and gained + 0.12 kg more annually. These findings raise questions about Jordan’s nutritional habits, particularly among its younger citizens (Cordova et al. 2021).

Age trends in our sample further support these worries. The higher UPF intake in these groups may be attributed to the fact that younger individuals (19–35 years old) comprised 90.0% of the underweight group and 83.7% of the normal-weight group. These patterns are consistent with data from Korea, where UPF intake rose from 20.1% to 33.2% between 1998 and 2022, reaching a peak of more than 38.0% among those aged 19 to 29 (Lee et al. 2025). In the same vein, Sekyi et al. (2024) found that young adults in Ghana consumed more UPFs due to inadequate dietary literacy, price, and convenience (Sekyi et al. 2024). According to these results, a high UPF intake in individuals of normal weight may represent a dietary shift that occurs early on, before symptoms of clinical obesity appear.

Consumption of MUPF showed the opposite pattern; overweight people consumed more MUPFs. Only 20.4% of normal-weight participants and 15.0% of underweight people, 30.4% of overweight participants, and 29.0% of obese participants were in the highest quartile of MUPF. These patterns align with a Brazilian study, which found that overweight adults consumed more traditional staples, such as rice and beans, which are MUPFs (Cattafesta et al. 2020). This could be a sign of increased health consciousness or a shift in behavior following a diagnosis.

PF and PCI consumption were consistent across groups and did not show a significant correlation with BMI. In Mexico, PCIs and PFs contributed 5.8% and 10.2% of energy intake, respectively, while in the United States, they contributed 7.0% and 9.4%, respectively (Baraldi et al. 2018; Marrón-Ponce et al. 2018). These are often culturally based staples that do not vary significantly within BMI categories, such as sweets, oils, and canned vegetables.

All of these results suggest that BMI alone may not accurately represent the dietary risk associated with excessive UPF intake. A potential trajectory toward obesity and metabolic impairment may be indicated by a high UPF intake in normal-weight individuals, particularly in young persons. Evidence suggests that even in non-obese individuals, early exposure to UPF can cause systemic inflammation, poor nutritional quality, and gradual fat accumulation (Askari et al. 2020; Beslay et al. 2020; Moubarac et al. 2017; Rauber et al. 2018).

Public health action is desperately needed given the high frequency of UPFs in the Jordanian diet and the observed sociodemographic trends. Culturally tailored dietary guidelines could help mitigate the growing burden of diet-related non-communicable diseases. Additionally, more informative food labels, fiscal measures such as UPF taxation, and nutrition education for youth could also play a role. In-depth body composition assessments, molecular markers, and longitudinal study designs should all be used in future research to elucidate the relationship between food processing and health in Jordan.

The strengths of the present study are that it provides a comprehensive understanding of typical and current dietary consumption, as it is one of the first in Jordan to evaluate NOVA food group intake by BMI category, using both FFQ. The reliability of the results is increased by using validated tools and a representative adult sample. However, causal interpretation is limited by the cross-sectional design. The precision of relationships may be impacted by unmeasured confounders, including physical activity and socioeconomic level, as well as the absence of biochemical and body composition data. Furthermore, there may have been some degree of NOVA categorization error despite efforts to separate traditional and mixed foods, which could have impacted the precision of intake estimations. Moreover, social desirability bias could be found.

In conclusion, an unexpected result was observed contrary to previous data expectations. The study shows significant differences in the relative intake of NOVA food groups among Jordanian adults across BMI categories, despite no notable variation in total energy intake; weight gain leads to dietary modification. Overweight and obese participants consumed a higher proportion and quantity of MUPFs, while their intake of UPFs was lower compared to individuals with normal or underweight BMI. These findings suggest an inverse relationship between UPF consumption and BMI, as individuals with normal or lower BMIs tended to be in the higher quartiles of UPF intake. Conversely, individuals with excess weight were more commonly found in the lower UPF quartiles and higher MUPF quartiles. However, regardless of BMI classification, most participants obtained the largest part of their energy intake from UPF, followed by MUPF. This pattern raises a crucial and alarming public health concern, as it may contribute to the rising rate of obesity in the future. Given that UPFs account for over half of total energy intake among Jordanian adults, public health strategies should target reducing UPF consumption and promoting minimally processed foods.
