Evaluation and management of incomplete ovarian function suppression in premenopausal breast cancer patients receiving anti-hormone therapy
Authors: Ruchi Patel, Elizabeth Weil, Sara Bugamelli, Emma Carroll, Kylie Steinke, Erinn Stockhausen, John Burfeind, Colin Mooney, Janet Retseck, Deepika Sriram, Jutta Deininger, Angela Halbach, Maressa Sweeney, Sailaja Kamaraju, Lubna N. Chaudhary, Yee Chung Cheng

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer is the most common malignancy among females. The Tamoxifen and Exemestane Trial (TEXT) and the Suppression of Ovarian Function Trial (SOFT) were two landmark studies, which showed adjuvant therapy with the aromatase inhibitor (AI), exemestane, or tamoxifen combined with ovarian function suppression (OFS) improved disease-free survival (DFS) among premenopausal women (Pagani et al. in N Engl J Med 371(2):107–118, 2014). NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology: Breast Cancer. Version 2.2024. www.nccn.org). Background

Female breast cancer is the most common malignancy [1, 2]. Approximately 70% of the reported new cases are hormone receptor-positive (HR +) [3]. Studies have shown that estrogen and other hormones promote breast cell proliferation, and estrogen metabolites may initiate DNA damage and mutations; therefore, anti-hormone therapy is commonly used for breast cancer treatment [1].

In 2003, the International Breast Cancer Study Group initiated two randomized, phase 3 trials, the Tamoxifen and Exemestane Trial (TEXT) and the Suppression of Ovarian Function Trial (SOFT), involving premenopausal women with HR + early breast cancer [4]. The trials were designed to determine the impact of ovarian function suppression (OFS) in women receiving adjuvant tamoxifen and whether adjuvant therapy with the aromatase inhibitor (AI), exemestane, with OFS improved disease-free survival, as compared with tamoxifen, among premenopausal women [4]. In both studies, OFS was achieved by choice of triptorelin at a dose of 3.75 mg intramuscular (IM) every 28 days, bilateral oophorectomy, or ovarian irradiation. Approximately 95% of patients used the gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonist, triptorelin, as the OFS method [5]. The studies found that the 8 year disease free survival (DFS) was 78.9% with tamoxifen alone, 83.2% with tamoxifen plus OFS, and 85.9% with AI plus OFS, highlighting the benefits of ovarian suppression [4, 6]. Notably, there is low utilization of triptorelin as the GnRH agonist agent of choice in the United States with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) citing the use of leuprolide and goserelin as their agents of choice for OFS [7–9].

Based on the results of the SOFT and TEXT trials, OFS added to tamoxifen or an AI has become the mainstay of anti-hormone adjuvant therapy for high-risk premenopausal women with HR + breast cancer [4]. However, data has emerged regarding incomplete OFS or higher mean estrogen levels than those found in postmenopausal women [3, 4, 7, 10]. The SOFT Estrogen Substudy (SOFT-EST) was a prospective substudy of SOFT, which evaluated estradiol (E2) levels to determine if patients on an AI and OFS experienced suboptimal estrogen suppression. In this study, 66% of patients treated with exemestane plus triptorelin 3.75 mg IM every 28 days showed a persistent reduction in E2 levels during the first 12 months of treatment; however, at least 17% of patients had an E2 level greater than 2.72 pg/mL at each time point, which was determined to be ovarian escape (OE) or incomplete OFS [3]. This study called into question whether E2 levels should routinely be monitored in women on GnRH agonists. Similarly, Burns et al. evaluated the proportion of premenopausal women who experience persistent OE while receiving OFS for HR + breast cancer treatment [3]. They found that a quarter of premenopausal women do not attain adequate OFS within the first three months of GnRH agonist therapy based on E2 levels [3]. In premenopausal patients on GnRH therapy combined with an AI, suboptimal E2 levels are a concern, as the reduced estrogen levels caused by the AI can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis [11]. Among the premenopausal women who did not attain complete ovarian suppression, young age was a significant risk factor for likelihood of OE [3]. Body mass index (BMI), previous chemotherapy, and drug used (tamoxifen versus AI) did not correlate with the likelihood of OE in the patient cohort evaluated in Burns et al.; however, SOFT-EST did find an increased incidence of OE in patients with a higher BMI and those that did not receive prior chemotherapy [3, 5].

The NCCN and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) are two organizations that have comprehensive guidelines which detail the management of oncologic disease states. NCCN states that serial assessments of circulating estradiol is “mandatory” in premenopausal patients “to assure a true postmenopausal state” if they are to be considered for adjuvant endocrine therapy with an AI [7]. ASCO also states the following, “clinicians should be alert to the possibility of incomplete ovarian suppression with GnRH agonist therapy and should evaluate patients in whom there is concern for residual ovarian function.” [10] These organizations do not provide specific laboratory values to determine if residual ovarian function is present. Froedtert and the Medical College of Wisconsin (MCW) currently did not have a standardized protocol in place for monitoring OFS. Additionally, Froedtert & MCW preferred formulary GnRH agonist is leuprolide acetate (Eligard) 7.5 mg subcutaneous (SQ) every month or 22.5 mg SQ every 3 months, which is not included in the SOFT/TEXT trials and is not as widely studied for use in breast cancer patients [4, 8]. As previously mentioned the NCCN recommends leuprolide or goserelin as the GnRH agents of chose [7]. Leuprolide acetate (Eligard) was chosen as the preferred formulary agent of chose as it is a more comfortable injection for patients and a more cost efficient option for the patient. A review of protocols at other institutions revealed that standardized methods for evaluating incomplete OFS are not consistently implemented across different health systems. The aim of this study was to develop and implement a protocol for monitoring E2 levels at our institution based on the NCCN and ASCO recommendations (Appendix A). Retrospective analysis of the E2 levels drawn per the protocol were used to evaluate if patients were experiencing incomplete OFS on GnRH agonists.

Methods

Study design

This single-health system, retrospective study reviewed electronic health records of female patients with HR + breast cancer receiving a GnRH agonist with tamoxifen or an AI within Froedtert & MCW health-system from October 1, 2022 to May 1, 2024. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the health system. Based on the retrospective nature of the study, Human Ethics and Consent to Participate declarations were not applicable. The ambulatory oncology pharmacist was responsible for ordering and monitoring E2 levels on included patients per the institutions E2 monitoring protocol (Appendix A). This protocol was developed by the project team and was reviewed and approved by the Pharmacy & Therapeutics Committee of the health system. A baseline E2 and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) level was drawn on all new GnRH agonist patients. Tamoxifen therapy was initiated at the time of the GnRH agonist or approximately 28 days after. Aromatase inhibitors were initiated at least 28 days after the start of the GnRH agonist. After 28 days on concurrent therapy with GnRH and tamoxifen or an AI, subsequent E2/FSH levels were drawn approximately 28 days apart for patients on monthly injections and 84 days apart for patients on every three-month injections. If the E2 level was ≤ 2.72 pg/mL for those on AI therapy or ≤ 21 pg/mL for those on tamoxifen therapy for three consecutive draws, the pharmacist would notify the primary oncologist the definition of OFS was met and no further E2 monitoring was required. If the E2 level was > 2.72 pg/mL for those on AI therapy or > 21 pg/mL for those on tamoxifen the provider would be notified of the high level. After the second consecutive high level the pharmacist notified the primary oncologist that the patient had met the definition of incomplete OFS and the oncologist may consider changing the GnRH agonist dose, frequency, or formulation as clinically indicated. (Appendix A). E2 levels were completed by ARUP Laboratories in Salt Lake City, Utah, to allow for increased sensitivity measuring down to 2 pg/mL.

Patients

Patients were eligible for inclusion if greater than 18 years old, female, deemed premenopausal by their treating oncologist, and diagnosed with HR + breast cancer receiving a GnRH agonist with tamoxifen or an AI, with the plan to continue for a minimum of three months. Patients younger than 18 years old, assigned male sex at birth, or female patients on concurrent chemotherapy and a GnRH agonist for fertility preservation were excluded.

Endpoints

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients identified per the E2 monitoring protocol that failed to achieve OFS for three consecutive levels. Secondary outcomes included number of provider interventions initiated based on patient failing to achieve OFS per the protocol, and proportion of patients that failed to achieve OFS for three consecutive levels stratified by age, BMI, GnRH agonist received, and prior receipt of chemotherapy.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive data was analyzed using measures of central tendency. Nominal data were compared using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous data using the Mann Whitney U or student’s t-test, where appropriate. Statistical analysis was completed in Microsoft Excel and SSPS Statistics version 29.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 85 patients were reviewed at the time of analysis. The average age was 41.2 years (26–55) and the average body mass index was 28.5 mg/m2 (17.4–49.9). Fifty-seven patients (67.1%) received chemotherapy for their breast cancer compared to 28 patients (32.9%) with no prior receipt of chemotherapy prior to starting a GnRH agonist. Prescribed GnRH agonist agents included leuprolide 7.5 mg monthly SQ injection (81.2%), leuprolide 22.5 mg every three months SQ injection (5.9%), leuprolide 3.75 mg monthly IM injection (4.7%), leuprolide 11.25 mg every three months IM injection (1.2%), goserelin 3.6 mg monthly SQ injection (5.9%), and goserelin 10.8 mg every three months SQ injection (1.2%). For adjuvant anti-hormone therapy, 78 patients (91.7%) were on an AI and seven patients (8.3%) were on tamoxifen. Discussion

In this single-health system, retrospective evaluation of premenopausal patients with HR + breast cancer, 17 patients (20%) demonstrated incomplete OFS per our study definition over an 18-month evaluation period. Previous studies have shown that younger age, higher BMI, and chemotherapy naïve patients are all associated with higher incidence of incomplete OFS. In our study, the average age in patients with complete vs incomplete OFS was similar; however, the age range was lower for patients with incomplete OFS. The average BMI was higher in the group with incomplete OFS, and the range was also broader. Chemotherapy naïve patients also had a higher incidence of incomplete OFS. Our study was consistent with the SOFT-EST study which found an increased incidence of incomplete OFS in patients with a higher BMI and those that were chemotherapy naïve. Froedtert & MCW’s preferred formulary GnRH agonist is leuprolide acetate (Eligard) 7.5 mg SQ monthly injection, which was not included in the SOFT/TEXT trials and is not as widely studied for use in breast cancer patients [4, 8]. The authors of this study hypothesized rates of incomplete OFS would be lower than reported in the SOFT-EST study since the dose of leuprolide acetate in Eligard is higher (7.5 mg) than used in Lupron (3.75 mg), which are more widely studied in breast cancer. However, our study found similar rate of incomplete OFS (20%) to those reported in SOFT-EST (17%) and in Burns et al. (23.9%) [3, 5].

Our study also evaluated if switching GnRH agonist agents would improve patients’ ability to achieve OFS for those patients with incomplete OFS with their initial agent. Half of those patients (n = 7) were able to achieve complete OFS which is clinically meaningful and highlights the effectiveness of changing GnRH agents. Notably, one patient did not switch agents and went on to meet the definition of complete OFS with additional monitoring. Patients who had continued incomplete OFS despite switching agents were found to be younger on average and with a higher average BMI, which is again consistent with the findings from the SOFT-EST study. Additionally, patients who had never previously received chemotherapy were significantly more likely to have continued incomplete OFS group compared to those who had complete OFS after intervention (p = 0.010). The role of chemotherapy in suppressing ovarian function is well known, but it remains unclear how much this impacts the clinical outcomes for HR + breast cancer patients. This controversy has been highlighted following the results of the RxPONDER trial which demonstrated a benefit of chemotherapy in node-positive premenopausal patients, sparking debate whether the benefit stems from the chemotherapy itself or the OFS that occurs as a result of chemotherapy [12]. This question will be further answered once the study results from the NRG-BR009 OFSET trial (NCT05879926) are published which is further examining the benefits of chemotherapy in patients who have hormonal therapy specifically with AI and OFS [13]. Uniquely our study has highlighted that those patients who have not previously received chemotherapy have a harder time achieving OFS with GnRH agonists compared to those patients that have received prior chemotherapy.

NCCN mandates the serial assessments of circulating estradiol in premenopausal patients on an AI to assure a true postmenopausal state. Their definition does not include a specific E2 cutoff but goes on to state that “FSH and estradiol levels are used to support the diagnosis of menopause; however, clear criteria to guide interpretation of FSH and estradiol in this population is lacking.” [7] Therefore, at this moment, it remains unclear what E2 level is a true definition of menopause or OFS. This lack of clarity has created significant variability in the management of anti-hormone therapy. A recent survey conducted through the ASCO Research Survey Pool [14] revealed both consistencies and inconsistencies with the practice at our institution compared to the respondents. For example, respondents widely initiated monthly goserelin (54%) as their GnRH agent of choice compared to our preferred agent of leuprolide 7.5 mg SQ monthly. Also, 43% of respondents were checking hormone levels “regularly” while patients were receiving OFS and 27% checking levels “sometimes,” mainly if clinically indicated due to symptoms that were suggestive of uncertain menopausal status. This further highlights the inconsistencies in the frequency with which institutions monitor E2 levels. Notably, only 11% of respondents chose to switch GnRH agonist agents if incomplete OFS was found following monitoring levels, which in contrast, was the main form of intervention at our institution. Finally, the survey found that 92% of respondents would like guidance from ASCO regarding the use of OFS and many “expressed a need for standardization while simultaneously appreciating the complexity of this therapy” [14].

Per our institution guideline, complete OFS is defined by three consecutive E2 levels ≤ 2.72 pg/mL to mirror the cutoff used in the SOFT-EST study. Of note, the SOFT-EST study did not elaborate on how this cutoff level was chosen, but also reported data for less stringent cutoffs. Therefore, we evaluated two less stringent E2 cutoffs in our patient population as well for incomplete OFS per our institutional guideline. Raising cutoff to ≤ 5 pg/mL or ≤ 10 pg/mL significantly increased the number of patients that met the definition of complete OFS. This highlights the opportunity for continued investigation into rates of OFS on GnRH agonist and the exact definition of OFS on GnRH agonist therapy.

Our study was limited by a single-health system design, small patient population, early discontinuation of lab monitoring, as well as uncertainty in clinical correlation with E2 levels. Our data collection and analysis was also dependent on chart review, thus may be subject to reporting bias. In addition, some providers chose to continue to monitor levels after patients had incomplete OFS, rather than switch GnRH agonist agents, if they felt the levels were close to 2.72 pg/mL, patients were non-adherent to AI therapy, or if patients were late for an injection. This did not align with our established guidelines and lacked consistency among providers, a common challenge when translating guidelines into practice in real-world settings.

Future directions

Since implementing our protocol, we have identified several potential directions for future research. The first is to re-determine Froedtert & MCW’s institution definition of OFS. We mirrored the cutoff used in the SOFT-EST study but found several other definitions of E2 cutoffs utilized in other studies which explore higher ranges of < 5 pg/mL and < 10 pg/mL. Second, we would like to evaluate patients’ adherence to their concurrent anti-hormone therapy in those experiencing incomplete OFS and fluctuating E2 levels to see if this plays any role in their higher E2 levels. Adherence to AI or tamoxifen therapy was not consistently evaluated for all patients as a standard practice. Lastly, we would like to evaluate progression free survival (PFS) in patients experiencing incomplete OFS to assess the true correlation between E2 levels and clinical outcomes. The current plan for our study is to continue data collection for an additional one year and then re-evaluate the data.

Conclusion

The TEXT and SOFT landmark studies demonstrated adjuvant therapy with an AI or tamoxifen combined with OFS improved DFS among premenopausal women; however, the SOFT-EST substudy showed 17% of patients in these studies had incomplete OFS defined as E2 levels > 2.72 pg/mL. The aim of this study was to develop a protocol for standardized monitoring and managing E2 levels at Froedtert & MCW based on the NCCN and ASCO recommendations regarding monitoring E2 levels in premenopausal HR + breast cancer on GnRH agonists. Our study has shown a trend toward the need for checking E2 levels to assess for incomplete OFS and that changing GnRH agonist agents may improve rates of complete OFS for some patients. Additionally, it is has shown that chemotherapy naïve patients may have a harder time achieving OFS; however, further data is needed to validate these results.
