Contiguous spread of melioidosis to the brain: a rare case of subdural empyema following odontogenic infection. Illustrative case

Melioidosis is an opportunistic infection caused by the environmental bacterium Burkholderia pseudomallei, a gram-negative bacillus commonly transmitted via inoculation, inhalation, or ingestion of contaminated soil or water.1 Endemic to Southeast Asia, melioidosis is reported annually in Hong Kong, although the incidence rate is lower than that in other endemic regions, with only sporadic cases.2,3 Clinical presentations vary widely and may include pneumonia, visceral abscesses, and sepsis.4 CNS involvement is uncommon, with reports in fewer than 5% of melioidosis cases, but carries significant morbidity and mortality.5

Neurological melioidosis can present with brain abscess, cranial nerve palsies, or subdural empyema.6 Diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific symptoms and radiological similarity to other infections. Here, we describe a rare case of intracranial melioidosis in an immunocompromised 50-year-old male, despite early aggressive antimicrobial therapy. Surgical drainage and prolonged antibiotic treatment were ultimately required. Most commonly, subdural empyemas originate from infections of the paranasal sinuses, middle ear or mastoid, or metastatic spread.7 Our case represents an unusual instance of odontogenic origin with subsequent contiguous spread. We illustrate the aggressive nature of B. pseudomallei, its potential for intracranial spread, and the need for increased clinical suspicion and multidisciplinary management in endemic regions.

Illustrative Case

A 50-year-old male with an unremarkable past health history presented with progressive right facial swelling, fever, and dizziness 1 week following the extraction of his right upper molar. He was also newly diagnosed with diabetes mellitus with a hemoglobin A1c of 12.7% and was given insulin and oral gliclazide. There were no identifiable causes for immunocompromised state, including organ transplant, human immunodeficiency virus, or steroid use.

On physical examination, there was notable right facial and neck swelling, which were tender to palpation. Contrast-enhanced CT of the orbit, face, and neck revealed extensive facial cellulitis with a right masticator space abscess extending into the parotid and extraconal orbital spaces. Empirical antibiotics, specifically intravenous augmentin, was given initially for 3 days and was changed to intravenous meropenem and co-trimoxazole after blood culture isolating B. pseudomallei. This confirms the diagnosis of melioidosis septicemia.

Despite intravenous meropenem and co-trimoxazole for 26 days, the infection progressed and the patient experienced a seizure episode. MRI of the head and neck revealed a 2.5 × 1–cm right temporal lobe abscess with an adjacent subdural empyema, along with subperiosteal and lateral rectus muscle abscesses, and a 2.5 × 1.2–cm frontal abscess (Fig. 1).

The patient underwent right-sided craniotomy with drainage of the subdural abscess and anterior right temporal lobectomy. Intraoperatively, extensive granulation tissue and viscous turbid yellowish pus were observed over the subdural space, and a localized right frontal subdural abscess was identified in proximity to the precentral gyrus (Fig. 2). Gram stain of the abscess only showed a large number of white cells, but no organism was identified. Subsequently, culture of the same specimen revealed B. pseudomallei.

Intraoperative photograph of melioidosis empyema in the brain. A loculated subdural abscess is identified and excised.

Postoperatively, intravenous co-trimoxazole and meropenem were given every 8 hours for 7 weeks, followed by a year of oral co-trimoxazole of the same dosage. The patient experienced breakthrough seizures requiring escalation of antiepileptic therapy, including levetiracetam, phenytoin, and lacosamide for 6 months, after which seizure activity terminated. During follow-up checks, repeated MRI of the brain demonstrated interval reduction in the size of intracranial collections and an improvement in the inflammatory changes of the right craniofacial region (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Observations

In the presented case, the rapid progression from an odontogenic source to facial cellulitis, orbital abscesses, and ultimately intracranial empyema underscores the virulence of B. pseudomallei. Although CNS melioidosis typically results from hematogenous dissemination, particularly in patients with underlying immunosuppression or hematological abnormalities,8,9 previous case reports have documented contiguous extension from infected adjacent structures such as the orbit10 or parotid gland.11 The current case adds to this body of evidence, supporting local invasion from facial and orbital abscesses into intracranial compartments. While atypical, this underscores the potential for localized infections to breach anatomical barriers, especially when host immune defenses are compromised, as reflected in our patient with newly diagnosed diabetes mellitus.

Neuromelioidosis remains diagnostically challenging due to its nonspecific clinical manifestation and neuroimaging features overlapping with tuberculosis,12 fungal infections, or malignancies.13 T2-weighted MRI of the brain remains the primary diagnostic modality, with radiological findings demonstrating multiloculated abscesses with ring enhancement, subdural empyema, dural thickening, or diffuse hyperintensities. Additional imaging, for example, diffusion-weighted imaging and MR spectroscopy, may also facilitate diagnosis as they distinguish abscesses from neoplasm.14 The gold standard for diagnosing neuromelioidosis involves a combination of positive pus or blood cultures and evidence from MRI. In our case, neuroimaging revealed ring enhancement and dural thickening, which along with a positive blood culture confirmed the diagnosis.

Moreover, despite interval resolution of primary infection in follow-up imaging in our case, seizure activities persisted and required multiagent antiepileptic therapy.

Lessons

This case highlights several lessons relevant to the diagnosis and subsequent management of neuromelioidosis. Foremost, clinicians should maintain high awareness and suspicion for CNS involvement in melioidosis patients and the potential for progressive craniofacial infection. Prompt diagnosis and escalation of care are essential to prevent irreversible neurological damage.

First, the management of neuromelioidosis is particularly challenging due to the capacity of B. pseudomallei for relapse and the poor CNS penetration of many standard antimicrobials.15 Table 1 summarizes recently published cases of neuromelioidosis, highlighting the spectrum of antibiotics used and clinical outcomes. A recent systematic review supported the findings from our literature search that most cases adopted a two-phase antimicrobial management, consisting of intravenous ceftazidime or meropenem, sometimes with cotrimoxazole during the initial intensive phase, typically for 3–8 weeks, followed by an oral eradication phase with cotrimoxazole or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for 12–48 weeks to ensure elimination of persistent infection and reduce relapse risk.13 Most cases showed prompt clinical improvement with intravenous antibiotics in the intensive phase, with further clinical improvement in the oral eradication phase.

Compared with brain abscesses of other etiologies, a key distinction in the treatment of neuromelioidosis lies in the longer duration of both intravenous and oral antibiotic administration.1 This is reflected by the intracellular survival of B. pseudomallei, its ability to cause latent recrudescence, and the high morbidity of relapse in the CNS. In contrast, standard pyogenic brain abscesses are generally managed with 4–6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics, with shorter oral courses if any are given.16

Second, although antibiotic therapy remains fundamental to melioidosis management, this case also highlights the limitations of antibiotic monotherapy in managing melioidosis with CNS involvement. Despite high-dose intravenous meropenem, the patient’s condition progressed, necessitating neurosurgical intervention. Concomitant surgical drainage offers several advantages, including the removal of necrotic and purulent materials, decompression of the mass lesions, and provision of samples to guide further antibiotic treatment.17,18 In this illustrated case, the patient underwent right anterior temporal lobectomy and subdural empyema drainage, which led to subsequent clinical and radiological improvement. A multidisciplinary approach incorporating neurosurgeons, infectious disease specialists, and radiologists is essential in managing such complex cases to optimize outcomes for patients.

Moreover, postoperative seizures are a well-recognized complication of cerebral abscesses19 and were particularly challenging in this patient. Persistent seizures in this patient reflect possible ongoing cortical irritation or postinfectious gliosis, perhaps as a glial response to melioidosis, emphasizing the necessity for prolonged neurological follow-up.20 Furthermore, B. pseudomallei is known for its intracellular survival and potential for relapse.21 Therefore, strict adherence to prolonged oral eradication therapy and careful monitoring are essential for full recovery.

In summary, this case illustrates the uncommon but serious potential for B. pseudomallei to spread contiguously from odontogenic infection to the CNS, resulting in subdural empyema and brain abscess. Our case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high level of vigilance for its neuroinvasive complications and anticipating prompt management escalation when warranted. Rigorous follow-up and adherence are essential to achieve sustained clinical recovery and minimize the risk of relapse.
