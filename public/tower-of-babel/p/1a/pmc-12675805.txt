A Modified Posterior Approach to the Nerve Transfer of the Spinal Accessory Nerve to the Suprascapular Nerve
Authors: Lenny L. Rivera-Rosario, Ruben Tresgallo Parés, José I. Acosta-Julbe, Alexandra Claudio Marcano, Joseph Salem-Hernández, Gerardo S. Caussade Silvestrini, Norman Ramirez, Christian Foy-Parrilla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeRestoration of shoulder abduction and external rotation is critical in patients with upper brachial plexus injuries (BPIs). The transfer of the spinal accessory nerve to the suprascapular nerve is a well-established surgical technique; however, the optimal approach remains debated. The traditional posterior approach may compromise muscle integrity because of trapezius detachment and splitting of the supraspinatus. This study introduces a muscle-sparing modification to the posterior approach and evaluates functional outcomes in pediatric and adult patients with obstetric or traumatic BPIs.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted on patients who underwent spinal accessory nerve to suprascapular nerve transfer using a muscle-sparing posterior approach from September 2018 to November 2022. Inclusion criteria comprised pediatric patients (aged ≤18 months) with obstetric BPIs and adult patients with traumatic BPIs with a minimum postoperative follow-up of 24 months. Pediatric patients were evaluated using the Toronto Muscle Grading System, Modified Mallet Grading System, and Birch Triple System. Adults were assessed using range of motion and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand questionnaire scores.ResultsFourteen patients met the inclusion criteria (six pediatric and eight adult). Pediatric outcomes demonstrated Modified Mallet Grading System scores of grade IV and Birch Triple System stages IV–V, indicating good shoulder function. Toronto Muscle Grading System scores of 3 reflected active movement against gravity. Among adults, mean forward flexion improved from 35° to 53°, abduction improved from 28° to 47°, and external rotation improved from 12° to 23°. The mean Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand questionnaire score improved from 91 to 48, exceeding the minimal clinically notable difference. No complications were observed.ConclusionsThe muscle-sparing posterior approach for spinal accessory nerve to suprascapular nerve transfer is a safe and effective technique that yields favorable functional outcomes in both pediatric and adult BPI populations. Further comparative studies are warranted.Type of study/level of evidenceTherapeutic IV.

Brachial plexus injuries (BPIs) are rare but devastating orthopedic conditions.1 Their incidence has increased in recent years, mainly because of an increase in high-energy trauma, particularly motor vehicle accidents.2 These injuries most commonly affect young men aged 15–25 years, with a prevalence of approximately 1.2% and an annual incidence of 1.64 per 100,000.3,4 In contrast, obstetric trauma—such as traction injuries during delivery—can result in total or upper BPIs in 1–4 per 1,000 newborns.5,6

The brachial plexus is typically formed from C5 to T1 nerve roots, with possible contributions from C4 and T2.7 It originates in the posterior triangle of the neck and terminates in five peripheral nerves: axillary, median, musculocutaneous, radial, and ulnar.7 Depending on the level of injury (root, trunk, division, or cord), BPIs may impair any of these terminal branches.7

Regardless of etiology, BPIs often lead to severe shoulder dysfunction.8 Consequently, restoring shoulder function is a primary surgical goal in upper BPI management.9 Radial nerve transfer to the axillary nerve is a well-established procedure to restore shoulder abduction (ABD) with minimal controversy.10,11 In contrast, the transfer of the spinal accessory nerve (SAN) to the suprascapular nerve (SSN) remains a topic of debate, particularly regarding approach, timing, and outcomes.12

Spinal accessory nerve to SSN transfer can improve shoulder stability, ABD, and external rotation (ER).12 Both anterior and posterior approaches are used for this procedure, with the anterior approach historically being preferred. However, the posterior approach, as described by Colbert and Mackinnon,13 offers advantages such as preserving the upper trapezius and anatomical proximity to the target muscle.14,15 Yet, limitations of the traditional posterior route include patient positioning, detachment of the trapezius from the scapular spine, and splitting of the supraspinatus muscle.13, 14, 15, 16, 17

To address these concerns, we propose a muscle-sparing modification to the posterior approach. Although traditional techniques have demonstrated favorable outcomes, this specific modification has not been previously studied.17 It offers quicker nerve identification and minimizes tissue disruption, an advantage particularly relevant in pediatric and delayed-repair cases, where preserving already compromised muscle is essential.

This study aims to describe our modified technique and retrospectively assess its functional outcomes in pediatric and adult patients with BPI. Although both groups underwent the same surgical procedure, outcome analyses were stratified owing to their differing anatomical, clinical, and prognostic profiles. Distinct evaluation tools were used to maintain clinical relevance. Our primary objective was to assess the feasibility and safety of this technique across the spectrum of BPI presentations.

Materials and Methods

Study design

We retrospectively reviewed outcomes of patients who underwent SAN to SSN transfer using a modified posterior approach. Institutional review board approval was obtained. Inclusion criteria were (1) pediatric patients aged ≤18 months with obstetric total or upper BPIs, (2) adult patients with traumatic total or upper BPIs treated between September 2018 and November 2022, (3) use of the modified posterior surgical approach, (4) a minimum of 2 years of postoperative follow-up, and (5) documented informed consent from the patient or legal guardian. Surgical time was defined as the interval from skin incision to final closure, as recorded in intraoperative anesthesia records.

Exclusion criteria included (1) patients considered poor surgical candidates because of delayed presentation (>18 months after injury), significant comorbidities (eg, uncontrolled systemic illness), or preexisting severe musculoskeletal deformities; (2) those requiring concomitant vascular repair or fracture fixation; (3) cervical spine fractures or known peripheral neuropathies; (4) loss to follow-up or nonadherence to the rehabilitation protocol; and (5) absence of informed consent.

Surgical technique

Anatomical landmarks and incisions followed the approach described by Mackinnon and Colbert.13 Unlike the classic posterior technique, our muscle-sparing modification avoids complete detachment of the trapezius and intramuscular splitting of the supraspinatus. Instead, the trapezius fascia is sharply elevated, and the supraspinatus fascia is released from its medial origin while preserving the muscle belly and neurovascular bundle.

Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in prone position with the arm adducted. A 1:100,000 adrenaline-to-saline solution was injected subcutaneously and periosteally. Following subcutaneous dissection, the trapezius fascia was elevated from the scapular spine, exposing the supraspinatus fascia. Dissection proceeded from medial to lateral along the inferior border of the supraspinatus, lifting the fascia without violating muscle fibers (Fig. 1). Fascia insertion points were tagged for reattachment.Figure 1Elevation of the trapezius fascia and exposure of the supraspinatus muscle fascia. Sharp dissection along the scapular spine revealed the supraspinatus fascia, preserving the neurovascular supply during medial-to-lateral and caudal-to-cranial release.

Elevation of the trapezius fascia and exposure of the supraspinatus muscle fascia. Sharp dissection along the scapular spine revealed the supraspinatus fascia, preserving the neurovascular supply during medial-to-lateral and caudal-to-cranial release.

The dissection continued along the superior border of the fossa to identify and medially release the superior transverse scapular ligament, exposing the SSN (Fig. 2). This medial approach avoids the suprascapular artery laterally. A nerve stimulator confirmed no electrical activity before blunt dissection exposed the SSN proximally.Figure 2Medial release of the superior transverse scapular ligament and identification of the SSN. The ligament was released medially to minimize arterial injury and expose the SSN for proximal dissection and preparation.

Medial release of the superior transverse scapular ligament and identification of the SSN. The ligament was released medially to minimize arterial injury and expose the SSN for proximal dissection and preparation.

The SAN was identified at the superomedial scapular border, just beneath the trapezius. Reflecting the supraspinatus fascia facilitated direct access to the SAN, which was confirmed via stimulation and dissected distally.

The SSN was transected proximally, and the SAN distally, and then redirected laterally to the suprascapular notch. Nerves were coapted tension-free using 9-0 nylon and fibrin glue. Trapezius and supraspinatus fasciae were reapproximated with 3-0 Ethibond (Ethicon, Inc) in pediatric patients and Ethibond 0 in adults (Fig. 3).Figure 3Final coaptation of the SAN to the SSN and reattachment of trapezius and supraspinatus fasciae. Tension-free nerve coaptation was performed using microsutures and fibrin glue, followed by anatomic reattachment of the dissected fasciae.

Final coaptation of the SAN to the SSN and reattachment of trapezius and supraspinatus fasciae. Tension-free nerve coaptation was performed using microsutures and fibrin glue, followed by anatomic reattachment of the dissected fasciae.

Clinical evaluation

Active range of motion (ROM) was measured in degrees using a standard goniometer at 6-month postoperative intervals during routine outpatient visits. A board-certified hand therapist or the attending surgeon (L.R.R.) performed measurements. In pediatric patients, both active and passive ROM were assessed to monitor for glenohumeral dysplasia and shoulder contracture development. All ROM data reported reflects active motion unless otherwise specified.

Data collection

Demographic and injury-related data were collected for each participant. The senior author (C.F.P.) performed all physical examinations. Patients were grouped by etiology as having obstetric (pediatric) or traumatic (adult) BPIs and then further classified as having total or upper trunk involvement on the basis of clinical and intraoperative findings. This classification aided interpretation of functional outcomes, especially Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) questionnaire scores in adults, which reflect global upper extremity function rather than isolated shoulder recovery.

Pediatric patients were assessed postoperatively using age-appropriate functional grading systems. Standard ROM measurements were not used because children—especially those under 3 years of age—often cannot reliably follow commands. Instead, observational tools were used. Children aged ≤3 years were evaluated using the Toronto Muscle Grading System, which assesses spontaneous or reflexive movements in infants and toddlers.18

Children older than 3 years were assessed using a combination of the Modified Mallet Grading System (MMGS), the Modified Medical Research Council (MRC) grading system, and the Birch Triple System Pediatric Functional Grading Systems

Adult patients with traumatic BPI were evaluated using preoperative and postoperative shoulder ROM and the DASH questionnaire.22 The DASH is a 30-item self-reported measure of upper extremity disability scored from 0 (no disability) to 100 (most severe).22 For interpretability, scores were stratified into three categories: 0–29 (mild), 30–54 (moderate), and 55–100 (severe limitations).23 Although described in the literature, the clinical utility of these thresholds remains debated and was not the focus of this study.

The minimal clinically significant difference for DASH score in upper limb conditions is estimated to be between 11 and 15 points, representing the threshold at which patients perceive meaningful improvement or decline.24,25

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, and range) were calculated for all continuous and ordinal variables. Owing to the small sample size and nonnormal distribution, nonparametric tests were used. Group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by pairwise Mann-Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate changes in DASH scores from preoperative to postoperative periods in adults. A two-tailed Student t test was also applied to compare mean DASH scores before and after surgery. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were conducted using Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation) SciPy v1.11.1.

Results

A total of 14 patients met the study’s inclusion criteria and underwent SAN to SSN transfer using the modified posterior approach. Group 1 included six pediatric patients with obstetric BPIs, and group 2 included eight adult patients with traumatic BPIs. Among the pediatric patients, four had upper BPI and two had total BPI. Among adult patients, five were classified as having upper BPI and three as having total BPI on the basis of clinical and intraoperative findings. The average surgical time across the cohort was 67 minutes. All patients were followed for a minimum of 24 months, with an average follow-up time of 34 months in both the pediatric and adult groups.

Among the four pediatric patients evaluated using the MMGS and the BTS, the most frequent MMGS score was grade IV. In the MMGS, grade IV indicates active shoulder ABD greater than 90°, ER greater than 20°, and the ability to easily perform tasks such as bringing the hand to the mouth, neck, and back. Three of these patients were classified as having stage IV function on the BTS, which reflects good functional recovery in shoulder, elbow, and hand movements. In contrast, one patient achieved stage V, denoting near-normal function across these joints. Two children under 3 years of age were assessed using the Toronto Muscle Grading System, scoring grade 3. In the Toronto Muscle Grading System, grade 3 corresponds to active movement against gravity, indicating fair muscle strength. See the Table for the completed grading criteria used in each pediatric evaluation system.

Among the adult patients in group 2, the mean preoperative shoulder forward flexion was 35° (range, 10° to 90°), ABD averaged 28° (range, 5° to 80°), and ER was 12° (range, 0° to 30°) (Fig. 4). Postoperatively, the mean forward flexion improved to 53° (range, 15° to 140°), ABD increased to 47° (range, 10° to 120°), and ER reached 23° (range, 5° to 60°), reflecting average gains of 18°, 19°, and 11°, respectively (Fig. 1). Muscle strength, as assessed by the MRC grading, was documented in five of six pediatric patients and six of eight adult patients. Among pediatric patients, four of five achieved MRC grade 4 or higher in supraspinatus and infraspinatus function. In the adult cohort, two of six patients reached MRC grade 4, whereas the remaining showed strength ranging from grade 2 to 3. These results paralleled the active ROM findings and reinforced the differential recovery capacity between pediatric and adult patients.Figure 4Functional outcomes among group 2 patients.

All eight adult patients completed preoperative and postoperative DASH assessments (Fig. 2). The mean DASH score improved from 91 preoperatively to 48 postoperatively, representing a 43-point reduction. This change exceeds the established minimal clinically significant difference for the DASH questionnaire, which ranges between 11 and 15 points. Notably, five of eight patients (63%) achieved postoperative DASH scores under 55, corresponding to minimal-to-moderate disability levels. The mean DASH score improved significantly from 91 preoperatively to 48 postoperatively (P = .0078) (Fig. 5). Discussion

This study presents a retrospective analysis of 14 patients who underwent SAN to SSN transfer using a modified posterior approach. All pediatric patients demonstrated moderate-to-good shoulder function, as indicated by MMGS and BTS scores, suggesting favorable recovery in ABD and ER. Adult patients showed improvements in forward flexion (53°), ABD (47°), and ER (23°). Pediatric and adult outcomes were analyzed separately using distinct evaluation tools, allowing for the assessment of feasibility across age groups without conflating functional baselines. All eight adults had significant reductions in DASH scores postoperatively, with a mean improvement of 43 points. This not only exceeded the minimal clinically significant difference (11–15 points) but also moved most patients from severe disability (DASH score, >75) to moderate or mild disability (DASH score, <55), indicating a meaningful clinical impact.24, 25, 26, 27, 28

Our findings align with prior studies, which show that nerve transfers can restore function in patients with BPI, particularly when performed early.29 Haldane et al29 reported functional gains but noted that patient satisfaction may lag because of persistent limitations. Although satisfaction was not directly assessed in our study, improved DASH scores and ROM suggest relevant functional recovery. Compared with the findings of Czarnecki et al,30 who reported 68° gains in shoulder ABD using the traditional posterior approach, our ABD gains were more modest, likely reflecting the complexity of our cohort, which included infants and delayed adult presentations.

Wells et al31 demonstrated that 83% of patients undergoing radial-to-axillary nerve transfers achieved clinical improvement. Kostas-Agnantis et al32 reported similar outcomes across different transfer strategies. Shoulder ABD results were generally comparable with tendon transfers, although ER recovery remained less robust, as echoed by our findings.

A novel aspect of our approach is the muscle-sparing technique, which avoids complete detachment or splitting of the supraspinatus. Although we release its fascia, we preserve the muscle belly and neurovascular bundle. This may offer advantages in bleeding control, structural preservation, and recovery, which are particularly important in pediatric cases. Although not fully validated, this fascia-only release may balance access and muscle integrity.

Pediatric outcomes in our study align with prior reports. Chueh et al33 demonstrated that SAN to SSN transfers resulted in superior ER recovery and reduced secondary procedures in infants compared with nerve grafting. Noor et al34 reported excellent ER outcomes and resolution of glenohumeral dysplasia in infants undergoing reverse end-to-side SAN to SSN transfer. Similarly, Chomiak et al35 noted better ER and ABD outcomes in patients under 16 months of age using this approach. Our pediatric patients consistently achieved MMGS grade 4 or BTS stage IV–V, supporting the feasibility of this technique in early-life reconstructions.

Compared with the findings of Jimulia et al,12 who reported average ER gains of 61° in adduction and 44° in 90° ABD using a traditional posterior approach, our mean adult ER gain of 23° is more modest. This may reflect the supraspinatus fascia release in our technique, which avoids muscle splitting but may alter the vector of pull or delay activation. Similarly, Hyttinen et al36 reported better outcomes with conventional dissection in patients with obstetric BPI, further supporting the need for comparative studies to assess the trade-offs of fascial preservation.

Segal et al37 demonstrated that SAN to SSN transfers alone can restore meaningful shoulder motion and help patients avoid the need for secondary tendon transfers. Although none of these studies directly examine our muscle-sparing modification in a mixed-age cohort, they support the broader utility of SAN to SSN transfers and reinforce the potential benefits of our approach in younger patients.

This study has limitations. Its retrospective design introduces potential bias, and the small sample limits statistical power. Although each was analyzed separately, combining pediatric and adult cases may complicate interpretation; thus, this approach is not recommended. The absence of a control group prevents definitive comparisons with traditional methods. Additionally, postoperative DASH scores may reflect improvements from other nerve transfers (eg, elbow or hand), especially in total BPIs, and should not be interpreted as shoulder-specific metrics. Blood loss was not objectively measured and should not be treated as a validated outcome.

In conclusion, this study supports the feasibility of a muscle-sparing posterior approach for SAN to SSN transfer across pediatric and adult BPI populations. It offers a safe and anatomically favorable alternative, particularly where muscle preservation is a priority. Future prospective studies with control groups, longer follow-up, and patient satisfaction data are needed to determine its long-term role in nerve reconstruction algorithms.
