Soluble factors released from fibroblast-like synoviocytes of JIA patients with future extended course can condition persistent JIA synovial fibroblasts for increased IL-6 production
Authors: Megan M. Simonds, Lina Al-Jaberi, Hermine I. Brunner, AnneMarie C. Brescia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundJuvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA) includes a subset of oligoarticular patients who later extend to a polyarticular course (extended-to-be, ETB), resulting in worse outcomes. Predictors of extension remain poorly defined. We investigated whether interleukin-6 (IL-6) and fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) contribute to this progression.MethodsSynovial fluid samples from persistent oligoarticular patients who remained persistent (≤ 4 joints involved) throughout their disease course and ETB patients were analyzed using protein antibody arrays and ELISA. Importantly, ETB samples were collected prior to clinical extension, and persistent samples were from patients who never extended during the course of their disease. FLS were isolated from synovial fluid and cultured to assess IL-6 secretion. Conditioned media experiments were performed to evaluate whether factors secreted by ETB FLS could influence IL-6 expression in persistent FLS.ResultsMANOVA revealed that (Wilks’ λ = 0.5446, F(9, 31) = 2.88, p = 0.0136) IL-6 and multiple IL-6–related cytokines were significantly elevated in synovial fluid from ETB patients compared to persistent patients. Quantitative ELISA confirmed higher IL-6 concentrations in ETB samples (1.46-fold increase, p = 0.047). FLS isolated from ETB patients prior to extension secreted significantly more IL-6 than persistent FLS (1.6-fold increase, p = 0.0239). Conditioned media from ETB FLS induced IL-6 production in persistent FLS to levels comparable to ETB FLS, demonstrating that soluble factors secreted early by ETB FLS can reprogram persistent FLS toward an inflammatory phenotype, even though these persistent FLS came from patients who remained clinically persistent throughout their disease course.ConclusionsOur findings suggest factors secreted by ETB FLS including IL-6 protein may contribute to disease extension. These data highlight the potential role of early synovial signaling in shaping disease trajectory and support further investigation into IL-6 and FLS as possible therapeutic targets to prevent progression.

Background

This work focuses on the contribution of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) to the increased production of interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the pathogenesis of Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA). JIA is classified into seven subtypes of disease based on onset [1]. Here, we propose that a subset of patients with oligoarticular arthritis who eventually extend to a polyarticular disease course, termed extended-to-be (ETB), have increased production of IL-6 and that FLS are directly involved in the amplified secretion of this cytokine. Patients who extend have poorer clinical outcomes and lower health-related quality of life compared to oligoarticular patients who remain persistent in their disease course [2]. We propose that ETB FLS have unique cytokine expression related to IL-6 and that conditioned media from this cell type can influence FLS from more benign subtypes of JIA to increased IL-6 expression. We show that FLS are a direct source of increased IL-6 in synovial fluid of patients who eventually extend to a polyarticular disease course.

There are critical gaps in our knowledge of the drivers of extension to polyarticular course of JIA and how to mitigate the poorer outcomes in patients who have extended. Since disease progression increases the risk for disability, identifying indicators of the pathogenesis of this course is essential. Unfortunately, no reliable clinical or laboratory markers have been established. Extension to a polyarticular course carries a high incidence of joint destruction. Long duration of elevated ESR, longer time from diagnosis to start of disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARD), and the presence of IgM rheumatoid factor, are predictors of joint erosions [3]. Active disease at 5 years after onset is a predictor of disability. Clearly, identification of risk factors early in the course of illness is more beneficial to avoid clinical progression to extended oligoarthritis. Studies in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) suggest that FLS are disease initiators and key to the understanding the pathogenesis of RA; there is evidence that targeting disease directly at the synovium could have therapeutic benefits [4–6]. Here, we show that, through increased IL-6 signaling, ETB FLS have the potential to drive disease progression in JIA.

Many studies have determined that IL-6 plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of RA [7–10]. The inhibition of this pathway has proven effective in the treatment of RA and JIA [11, 12]. Several mononuclear cell types are found in synovial fluid, including macrophages, lymphocytes, and synoviocytes, but recent studies in RA suggest FLS are responsible for the perpetuation of disease, demonstrating a need for studies on this specific cell type [13–16]. Production of IL-6 is enhanced at sites of inflammation and can contribute to disease development [17]. The pathogenesis of JIA is not as well-defined as that of RA. However, studies have shown that IL-6 elevation correlates to disease progression in all subtypes of JIA [18]. Mangge et al. performed a 2-year prospective study of serum cytokine patterns in JIA and found that increased CRP and ESR levels correlated with increased IL-6 levels in all JIA subtypes examined [19].

We show that JIA FLS are a source of IL-6, as in RA. We propose that IL-6 and soluble factors secreted by FLS play a critical role in the extension of disease course in JIA, possibly allowing for intervention at the site of the synovium. Discovering the pathogenesis of this extended disease course could allow for earlier initiation of course-altering therapies to prevent extension of disease and ultimately lower the risk for joint destruction and disability.

Materials and methods

Synovial fluid sample selection

Synovial fluid samples were selected from a repository approved by our Nemours Foundation BioMedical Research Institutional Review Board (IRB#84709) using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria described in our previously published work [20–23]. Briefly, inclusion into the repository was offered to participants with clinically indicated arthrocentesis, and written consent was obtained. All selected synovial fluid samples analyzed in this study were from patients who were at most taking NSAIDs at the time sample was obtained, and none of the patients had received advanced medications, including disease modifying anti rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) or biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs). For all inclusion and exclusion criteria please refer to our methods found in our previously published work [21, 22]. In the described synovial fluid studies in which we measure IL-6 using ELISA, there are two cohorts of samples: 41 synovial fluid samples from our patient repository (23 persistent and 18 extend-to-be synovial fluid samples) and an additional 30 samples (12 persistent and 18 ETB) were obtained from Cincinnati Children’s Hospital. We established a material transfer agreement and data transfer agreement with this institution. Medical record review was performed at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital and samples were de-identified, prior to shipping to Nemours. Full clinical characteristics for these additional samples were not available, all were collected from patients who were either on no medications or receiving only NSAIDs at the time of sample. For consistency between our cohort and these additional samples, for ETB group, samples were collected before patients extended to a polyarticular disease course.

Cell culture

Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) were isolated from synovial fluid, following previously published protocols, synovial fluid was spun at 500 rpm for 5 min, and cell pellets were plated in T-25 culture flasks with 15% fetal bovine serum/Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium. At confluence, cells were passed into T-75 flasks and further passed three more times to ensure FLS were the predominant cell type in culture [22–25]. FLS were isolated from synovial fluid of persistent oligoarticular patients and synovial fluid of patients prior to their disease extending, termed extended-to-be (ETB) oligoarticular JIA were grown in medium containing 15% FBS. At sub-confluence, media were removed, and cells were incubated overnight in growth medium containing 0.2% serum. The serum-starved cells were then seeded on six-well plates for experiments.

For conditioned-media experiments, we followed our previously published protocols [24, 25] except that media was pooled from ETB FLS samples. Briefly, ETB FLS were grown to confluence. Media was replaced and cells were incubated for 48 h. Media was then pooled from three ETB FLS cultures. Persistent FLS were grown until confluence then washed with PBS, trypsinized, and resuspended in the pooled conditioned media from ETB FLS. Normal media controls were also collected. Cell culture supernatants were collected at 48 h after exposure to conditioned media from 6-well plates.

Protein antibody arrays

Cell culture supernatants were packed on dry ice and shipped to RayBiotech. Arrays were performed (Human Inflammation Array C3 - AAH-INF-3) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Samples were processed based on our previously published protocols [21, 22, 26]. Briefly, Bradford assay was used to calculate protein concentrations in synovial fluid or cell media samples. The array membranes were processed, and a chemiluminescent readout was obtained using densitometry. Background correction was performed. Averages were calculated between technical duplicates for each sample. Scaling was performed by converting intensity measurements or relative protein amounts, to the log10 value.

We analyzed cytokine profiles using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), an approach appropriate for correlated biological variables such as cytokine panels [27, 28]. Cytokine concentrations were log10-transformed to stabilize variance and improve multivariate normality. For each cohort, all nine IL-6–related cytokines were entered jointly as dependent variables, with group (Persistent vs. Extended-to-be; No Meds/NSAID vs. DMARD/Biologic) as the fixed factor. Multivariate significance was evaluated using the standard omnibus statistics (Wilks’ Lambda, Pillai’s Trace, Hotelling–Lawley Trace, and Roy’s Largest Root) each reported with its corresponding F statistic, degrees of freedom, and p value. When the MANOVA indicated a significant group effect, we performed follow-up Welch’s unequal-variance t-tests, a recommended approach when groups differ in variance or sample size, on each individual protein. Multiple testing across the nine cytokines was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure (q = 0.05). All analyses, including MANOVA, Welch’s t-tests, and FDR correction, were conducted using computational scripts in Python.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ELISA was performed following our previously published methods [20, 23–26, 29] except they were performed using the following assay purchased from R&D Systems: IL-6 (D6050B). All ELISAs were performed according to manufacturer’s protocols for sample dilution, technical replicates, and procedures. Data and statistical calculations were completed following methods previously published by our laboratory [24, 25]. Briefly, absorbance readings were obtained using an Epoch plate reader at 450 nm for signal detection and 570 nm for background correction, as the 570 nm wavelength accounts for optical imperfections in the plate. Corrected values were calculated by subtracting 570 nm readings from 450 nm readings. Averages and standard deviations were computed for each sample across technical replicates. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, and unadjusted p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Luminex assays

Cell culture supernatants were collected from fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) isolated from synovial fluid of patients with persistent JIA and extended-to-be (ETB) JIA. All ETB samples were obtained prior to clinical extension, and persistent samples were from patients who remained persistent throughout their disease course. Luminex assays were performed using the Luminex xMAP Intelliflex System with the ProcartaPlex multiplex immunoassay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, magnetic beads conjugated to capture antibodies were incubated with cell culture supernatants, followed by detection antibodies and streptavidin-PE. Plates were washed using a magnetic plate washer to remove unbound reagents. Data acquisition was performed on the Intelliflex instrument, and analyte concentrations were calculated using a five-parameter logistic (5PL) standard curve generated from known standards provided in the kit. All samples were run in technical duplicates. Averages and standard deviations were calculated for each condition, and comparisons between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, with unadjusted p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

IL-6 and IL-6–related cytokines are significantly upregulated in synovial fluid from ETB compared to persistent JIA patients

We analyzed synovial fluid from 23 persistent oligoarticular JIA patients and 18 patients who eventually extended to a polyarticular disease course, termed extended-to-be (ETB). Clinical characteristics of the synovial fluid patient cohort are described in Tables 1 [21]. All samples were collected before patients began disease-modifying medications and those on no medications or on NSAIDs only were included in this experiment. For the ETB group, all synovial fluid samples were obtained prior to clinical extension.

Using protein antibody arrays, we measured relative protein expression of 40 well-characterized chemokines and cytokines. To evaluate whether synovial fluid cytokine profiles differed between persistent and extended-to-be (ETB) JIA, we performed a Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) including nine IL-6–related cytokines: IL-6, IL-1α, TNF-α, LT-α, GM-CSF, G-CSF, IL-10, IL-11, and TGF-β1. The multivariate effect of disease group was significant (Wilks’ λ = 0.5446, F(9, 31) = 2.88, p = 0.0136), indicating that ETB and persistent patients differ in their overall inflammatory cytokine signature. This means that, when all the inflammatory markers are considered as a group, cytokine expression can differentiate between the two JIA subtypes. To help visualize this, we used a method called canonical variate analysis (CVA), which takes all nine cytokines and combines them into a single score that best highlights how the groups differ. In the CVA plot, individual samples had little overlap between the two JIA subtypes in their confidence intervals. This visual pattern shows significant separation between the two JIA subtypes (Fig. 1). This data suggests that when taken together, all nine IL-6 related cytokines can differentiate between patients who eventually extend to a more severe disease course and those who remained persistent throughout their disease course.

Specifically, IL-6 was increased in ETB samples (fold change = 1.17, q = 0.0395), supporting its role as a primary inflammatory mediator in JIA. IL-1α, a strong inducer of IL-6 expression in FLS and other immune cells [30], was also elevated (fold change = 1.62, q = 0.0013). TNF-α, a potent stimulator of IL-6 production in FLS [31], showed higher expression in ETB synovial fluid (fold change = 1.29, q = 0.0487). G-CSF, which can be induced by IL-6 [32], demonstrated the greatest increase (fold change = 3.20, q = 0.0013), while GM-CSF, often co-expressed with IL-6 during inflammation [33], was similarly elevated (fold change = 2.54, q = 0.0013). IL-11, which shares the JAK-STAT signaling pathway with IL-6 [34], was upregulated as well (fold change = 1.69, q = 0.0048). IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine known to suppress IL-6 under normal conditions [35], was increased (fold change = 2.32, q = 0.0004), suggesting a compensatory feedback response within the inflammatory milieu of synovial fluid from ETB patients. Finally, TGFβ1, which modulates IL-6 signaling [36], was also significantly elevated (fold change = 1.33, q = 0.0148).

Together, these findings demonstrate that patients predisposed to develop a more severe JIA phenotype exhibit a coordinated elevation of IL-6 and multiple cytokines and growth factors that regulate or are regulated by IL-6. This pattern highlights the central role of IL-6 in distinguishing patients who remain persistent throughout disease course and patients who eventually extend to a polyarticular disease course.

IL-6–related cytokine expression differs by disease subtype but not by eventual need for advanced treatment therapies

We analyzed synovial fluid from all 41 patients in our cohort, dividing them into two groups based on whether they eventually required a disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) or biologic agent. This grouping was done regardless of JIA subtype, such that each category contained both persistent and extended-to-be (ETB) samples. 16 patients never required advanced therapy, while 25 ultimately did. All synovial fluid samples were collected before initiation of any DMARD or biologic therapy, patients were either on no medication or NSAID only at the time of injection, and for the ETB group, samples were obtained prior to clinical extension to a more severe disease course.

Using protein antibody arrays, we measured relative expression levels of the nine proteins directly involved in IL-6 signaling or regulation (IL-6, IL-1α, TNF-α, LTα, GM-CSF, G-CSF, IL-10, IL-11, and TGF-β1). A MANOVA comparing patients who never required advanced therapy (n = 16) to those who eventually did (n = 25) showed no significant multivariate effect (Wilks’ λ = 0.7110, F(9, 31) = 1.40, p = 0.231). The CVA plot illustrates significant overlap between the two groups (Fig. 2). These findings indicate that IL-6 production and signaling are not predictive of eventual need for advanced therapies.

Taken together, these findings suggest that IL-6–associated synovial inflammation is not a marker of future treatment escalation, but rather a marker of underlying disease phenotype. When patients were grouped only by their eventual need for DMARD or biologic therapy, the absence of a significant MANOVA indicates that the IL-6 pathway does not distinguish patients who will or will not require advanced therapy later. In contrast, once patients were separated by JIA subtype, a strong multivariate signature emerged, driven by coordinated up-regulation of several IL-6 related cytokines in the ETB samples. These differences were already present before clinical extension occurred, supporting the idea that ETB patients have an intrinsically heightened inflammatory milieu rather than one that develops only when disease worsens. Thus, the overall pattern indicates that IL-6 pathway activity reflects subtype-specific biology, not treatment trajectory, and that early cytokine production may signal an inherent propensity toward disease extension rather than impending therapeutic escalation.

Quantitative ELISA confirms IL-6 overexpression in ETB synovial fluid samples

For this analysis, we examined a total of 71 synovial fluid samples. 41 samples were from our previously characterized cohort, for which detailed clinical information has been published by our laboratory [21]. An additional 30 synovial fluid samples (12 persistent and 18 ETB) were obtained from Cincinnati Children’s Hospital to strengthen the statistical accuracy of our analysis. Although full clinical characteristics for these additional samples were not available, all were collected from patients who were either on no medications or receiving only NSAIDs at the time of sample. For consistency between our cohort and these additional samples, for ETB group, samples were collected before patients extended to a polyarticular disease course.

Quantitative ELISA was performed to measure total IL-6 protein concentration in synovial fluid (Fig. 4). These data were analyzed using a two-tailed t-test to compare IL-6 levels between ETB and persistent samples. IL-6 concentrations were significantly higher in ETB synovial fluid, showing a 1.46-fold increase compared with persistent samples (p = 0.047). These results confirm that IL-6 levels are higher in ETB patients even when analyzed in a larger, partly independent sample set using a quantitative method. This supports our earlier findings from the semi-quantitative protein array data and further suggests that increased IL-6 expression is a consistent characteristic of patients who go on to develop a more severe disease course in juvenile idiopathic arthritis.

IL-6 is produced at elevated levels by ETB FLS in vitro

We aimed to evaluate whether FLS are a source of increased IL-6 levels observed in synovial fluid from patients with ETB JIA compared to those with a persistent course. To do this, we cultured three FLS samples isolated from synovial fluid of patients who remained persistent throughout their disease course and compared them to FLS isolated from synovial fluid of ETB patients. Selecting samples prior to disease extension allowed us to determine if differences in IL-6 expression exists before clinical progression. All FLS samples were obtained from joints without prior steroid injections, and patients were only on NSAIDs or no medications at the time synovial fluid was collected.

Using ELISA, we measured total IL-6 protein expression in media from three persistent FLS and three ETB FLS cultures. FLS isolated from ETB patients exhibited significantly elevated IL-6 protein expression compared to FLS from patients with a persistent disease course, with a 1.6-fold increase (p = 0.0239) (Fig. 5). Our findings demonstrate that FLS are a source of elevated IL-6 protein in patients who eventually extend to a polyarticular disease course, distinguishing them from FLS in patients who remain persistent. The observation that ETB FLS secrete significantly more IL-6 suggests a potential role for these cells in shaping the inflammatory environment that may contribute to future changes in disease course.

Conditioned media from ETB FLS induces IL-6 production in persistent FLS

Conditioned media refers to culture media collected from FLS after growth in vitro. For this experiment, ETB FLS were cultured in standard DMEM with 15% FBS, and media were collected after 48 h according to our lab’s established protocols [24, 25]. Media from three independent ETB FLS cultures were pooled to generate ETB conditioned media. This pooled media containing many soluble inflammatory factors including IL-6 secreted by ETB FLS was then used to culture persistent FLS to test whether exposure to ETB-derived soluble factors could induce increased IL-6 expression in persistent FLS.

Total IL-6 protein expression was quantified using Luminex multiplex analysis (Fig. 6). Untreated ETB FLS produced significantly more IL-6 than untreated persistent FLS (fold change = 2.12, p = 0.0477). Likewise, persistent FLS cultured in ETB conditioned media showed a significant increase in IL-6 production compared to untreated persistent FLS (fold change = 2.34, p = 0.0292). Most notably, there was no significant difference in IL-6 levels between untreated ETB FLS and persistent FLS exposed to ETB conditioned media, indicating that ETB-derived soluble inflammatory factors were sufficient to cause increased IL-6 expression in persistent FLS to levels comparable to ETB FLS. These findings suggest that soluble factors present in ETB conditioned media, including IL-6, can reprogram persistent FLS to produce IL-6 protein to levels similar to ETB FLS. This supports the concept that ETB-derived soluble factors can cause elevated IL-6 expression and this inflammatory milieu of the ETB FLS contributes directly to IL-6 activity in persistent FLS.

Furthermore, in our methods, we removed the ETB-derived conditioned media after the initial 48-hour exposure. Therefore, after those first two days, no ongoing supply of ETB-derived soluble factors, including IL-6, remained in the culture. We then collected the supernatant from the persistent FLS and serum-starved the cells for 24 h before adding any new conditioned media from the ETB FLS. Because proteins naturally break down over time, any soluble factors derived from persistent JIA FLS, including IL-6, would be reduced to less than 3% of their original amount based on half-lives, and would no longer have meaningful biological activity. This is because IL-6 has a relatively short half-life in the body (serum t 1/2 ~15.5 h), so after this serum starvation, any residual IL-6 would be considered biologically negligible [37]. The experimental setup therefore ensures that any increase we observe in IL-6 production reflects new production triggered by the ETB-derived soluble environment, rather than reflective of IL-6 from original persistent FLS culture media.

Discussion

In adult rheumatoid arthritis (RA), early aggressive treatment can lead to remission, suggesting that there is a window of time when RA can be cured. This may also be true for JIA in children; however, treatment paradigms in JIA remain reactive rather than proactive due to lack of reliable clinical or laboratory parameters that can predict disease course. Current treatments of JIA, including arthrocentesis with intra-articular steroid injections, systemic non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs), non-biologic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (nbDMARDs), biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs), and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDS), have revolutionized the treatment of JIA as they target specific inflammatory molecules and pathways. However, despite improvement in treatment guidelines and advancing therapy, fewer than half of patients achieve long-term remission [38]. It is imperative to identify predictors of disease course to enable a targeted, early introduction of DMARDs in an effort to improve longer-term outcomes of those patients with JIA who are at risk for disease progression and therefore, damage development.

Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) are emerging as potential therapeutic targets in inflammatory arthritis given their pathogenic properties that can perpetuate disease severity [4, 39, 40]. FLS have also been studied to define the mechanisms through which FLS contribute to disease development, primarily through their contribution to the inflammatory environment in affected joints, changes in gene expression in immune response, and an adapted, aggressive phenotype that leads to increased cell metabolism [5, 41–44]. In a recent study, Qian et al. describe the pathogenic characteristics of FLS, and propose targeting cell surface markers and specific signaling pathways in FLS in the treatment of RA [4].

In RA, IL-6 plays a critical role in disease pathogenesis [45]. Specifically, lymphocytes and monocytes infiltrate the synovium, where they trigger increased secretion of IL-6; Likewise FLS release IL-6, perpetuate inflammation, and ultimately contribute to joint destruction [45, 46]. IL-6 is a pleiotropic cytokine with many downstream influences in RA. It is thought to act both locally, promoting joint inflammation, and to cause extra-articular manifestations of RA [47]. Given their role in RA progression, there are several new studies focusing on FLS as a possible therapeutic target [4, 48]. Hammaker et al. suggests that local microenvironmental factors influence IL-6 cytokine signaling dynamics, and contribute to variability in disease manifestations and therapeutic responses [49]. In RA, it is well-established that FLS are capable of secreting increased IL-6 protein which recruits downstream immune mediators that lead to joint destruction [42, 50–52].

While the contribution of FLS to increased IL-6 levels has been well-studied and determined to be a critical contributor to disease severity in RA, similar studies have not been conducted in JIA. IL-6 gene and protein expression are detectible in blood and tissues of all JIA subtypes, but expression of IL-6 was markedly higher in synovial fluid [53]. IL-6 expression is directly related to the number of affected joints and the degree of disease progression in JIA [54, 55]. With such compelling evidence suggesting IL-6 as a key contributor to JIA pathogenesis, combined with our studies described here, FLS emerge as a source of the IL-6 protein. We submit that FLS are a key effector cell in disease course progression and soluble inflammatory markers contribute directly to increased expression of IL-6 protein and cytokines and growth factors directly related to IL-6 signaling. Here, we establish that, as in previous studies, synovial fluid of patients who eventually extend to a more severe disease course have elevated IL-6 protein levels compared to those with persistent JIA. Indeed, we show that differential IL-6 levels between JIA groups (persistent, extended) can be directly attributed to FLS. This evidence is particularly critical for patients who extend to a polyarticular course. These patients present with oligoarticular involvement at onset, then after 6 months of disease, recruit more joints, and evolve to a polyarticular disease course [1].

Our most compelling data comes from the conditioned media experiments. We demonstrate that ETB FLS secrete IL-6 and multiple IL-6 related cytokines and growth factors at significantly higher levels than persistent FLS. When persistent FLS derived from patients who remained clinically persistent throughout their disease course were cultured in conditioned media from ETB FLS, their IL-6 production increased to levels comparable to ETB FLS. This finding is striking because it suggests that even cells from patients predetermined to remain persistent can be reprogrammed, once exposed to ETB-derived soluble inflammatory factors, to express IL-6 proteins at levels similar to ETB FLS.

Importantly, the conditioned media used in these experiments were collected from extended-to-be FLS, prior to clinical extension, indicating that these cells produce IL-6 and related inflammatory mediators early in disease course. This early cytokine signature may distinguish ETB patients from other JIA subtypes and could represent a critical window for therapeutic intervention. The observation that ETB-derived soluble factors can enhance IL-6 production in persistent FLS highlights a potential link between early synovial signaling and later disease course, although additional mechanistic studies will be needed to determine whether IL-6 pathways directly drive progression.

While our findings clearly demonstrate that ETB FLS produce more IL-6 than persistent FLS and that ETB-derived soluble factors, including IL-6, can induce increased IL-6 production in otherwise stable persistent FLS, these data do not establish a causal role for IL-6 itself. Because conditioned media contain many cytokines and growth factors, additional mechanistic studies are necessary to determine which specific mediators are responsible for this effect. Other limitations we plan to address with future studies involve IL-6 gene expression measured in parallel with protein assays. As part of our future work, we plan to directly test IL-6 pathway involvement by pharmacologically inhibiting IL-6 or IL-6R signaling in FLS using clinically available DMARDs and biologics, including IL-6/IL-6R blockers and JAK/STAT pathway inhibitors. These experiments, including cytokine neutralization, receptor blockade, and rescue/add-back approaches, will allow us to determine whether IL-6 signaling is required for ETB-derived soluble factors to enhance IL-6 production in persistent FLS and will help establish a causal relationship.

Although causality cannot yet be confirmed, our current results provide strong preliminary evidence that ETB FLS possess an intrinsically heightened inflammatory phenotype and can influence persistent FLS toward a similar IL-6 favored profile. These findings support the broader concept that FLS themselves may represent a direct therapeutic target in JIA, particularly for patients at risk of extending to a more severe disease course. Early identification of patients with elevated IL-6 and related cytokines may ultimately enable more targeted use of IL-6–blocking or JAK/STAT therapies before extension occurs, with the potential to improve long-term outcomes.
