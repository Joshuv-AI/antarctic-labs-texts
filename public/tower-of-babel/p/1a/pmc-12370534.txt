Effects of natural aging on hydrophilicity and mechanical properties of PDMS in various storage environments
Authors: Shuyu Zhang, Anne E Staples
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is a synthetic elastomer widely used in biomedical and industrial applications. Despite its widespread use, the natural evolution of its mechanical and surface properties over time remains poorly understood. In this study, we fabricated PDMS samples with base-to-curing agent mixing ratios from 5:1 to 30:1 and aged them for up to 8 weeks under six non-harsh conditions at room temperature. Contact angle measurements revealed increasing hydrophobicity with aging, with maximum increases up to 16.5°. Mechanical testing showed up to 130% increases in Young’s modulus and 60% changes in flexibility after 5 weeks. Storage in mineral oil best preserved surface hydrophilicity, while storage in water best maintained mechanical integrity. These results provide a framework for optimizing PDMS storage conditions in microfluidic and biomedical device applications.Subject terms: Materials science, Biomaterials, Materials for devices, Soft materials, Biomedical engineering, Engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) is a soft synthetic elastomer commonly used in a wide range of applications, most notably in the manufacturing of microfluidic devices1. PDMS-based microfluidic devices can be used for cell cultures2, drug screening and delivery3,4, DNA sequencing5, disease diagnosis3, and chemical synthesis6. Besides microfluidic devices, PDMS is commonly present in devices such as catheters, microvalves, and implants1. Industrially, PDMS can be used as membranes for chemical separation7, optical lenses8, and protective coatings, among many other applications9. Due to its advantages, including flexibility10, optical transparency11, non-toxicity12, biocompatibility12, gas permeability13, functionalizability14, mechanical and chemical tunability10, ease of fabrication15, and low cost15, PDMS has become one of the most important elastomers in scientific and industrial fields16.

Although PDMS is known to be mechanically and chemically stable10,17,18, it has been found to change in mechanical and chemical properties over time, known as aging. Among the mechanisms of aging, thermally accelerated aging has been the most well-studied for PDMS19–24. Other aging mechanisms include radiation-induced aging24–26, artificial seawater- and motion-induced aging14, chemical-induced aging27,28, and unaccelerated natural aging. Studies that have investigated the natural aging of PDMS in non-harsh environments have been relatively few. One of the earliest studies on the natural aging of PDMS, Pfleiderer et al. (1995) investigated the chemical changes of PDMS parts implanted in rats using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxation and spectroscopy29. Agaoglu et al. (2017), Kurian et al. (2010), and Placet and Delobelle (2015) are some previous studies that examined mechanical changes in PDMS resulting from natural aging30–32. However, due to the manufacturer’s recommendations, most scientists only examined PDMS at the standard base-to-curing agent mixing ratio of 10:1, or “on-ratio” PDMS33. Although “off-ratio” PDMS (i.e., mixing ratios other than 10:1) has been associated with adverse effects on cells30,34, it has advantages including strong PDMS-PDMS bonding, high responsiveness, cavity resistance, and tunable mechanical properties and gas permeability13,33,35. As a result, off-ratio PDMS can be used as precise-control valves33, cavity-resistant polymer networks35, cell-mimetic microcapsules36, and gas-permeable microfluidic devices13. Therefore, it is necessary to thoroughly characterize PDMS elastomers at a wide range of mixing ratios. Moreover, changes in hydrophilicity and mechanical stiffness could alter the performance of microfluidic devices containing cell cultures, since cells respond differently to PDMS of different hydrophilicities and stiffnesses37,38. In addition, there is very little information in published sources regarding how PDMS should be optimally stored to minimize undesirable aging. Therefore, there is a critical need to characterize changes in hydrophilicity and mechanical properties of PDMS aged in different environments over time.

It is known that the properties of PDMS can be affected by oxygen when exposed to air at elevated temperatures, but it is still not established whether exposure to oxygen at room temperature can cause the properties of PDMS to change39. Furthermore, water is known to maintain the hydrophilicity of plasma-treated PDMS and suppress the activity of free curing agent30,40. The use of mineral oil in the storage of PDMS or similar polymers has not been examined in previous studies, to our knowledge, but mineral oil is known to protect materials from air and moisture and is widely used for the storage of reactive metals such as sodium and potassium41,42. Therefore, we leveraged five storage environments that could help protect the PDMS samples from oxygen, including Petri dishes, plastic wrap, a vacuum-pulled desiccator, deionized (DI) water, and mineral oil, and investigated the extent of natural aging of PDMS in the five environments over several weeks.

In this study, we fabricated PDMS samples of five mixing ratios (parts base:1 part curing agent), 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1, and aged the samples in the five aforementioned storage environments and in direct exposure to air. We measured the contact angle of the samples every two weeks for up to eight weeks and performed a tensile failure test to determine changes in mechanical properties of fresh PDMS samples and samples aged in each environment for five weeks. We believe that such a preliminary investigation of PDMS will lead to a better understanding of how properties of PDMS change over time, and how such changes may impact PDMS-based devices. Thus, these findings can be used to guide how PDMS should be stored and handled for various applications.

Results and discussion

Contact angle measurements

Most of the fresh PDMS (Sylgard 184) samples were found to exhibit slightly obtuse contact angles, with average contact angles of 94.4°, 101.3°, 101.5°, 102.6°, and 108.3°, for PDMS with mixing ratios of 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1, respectively (Fig. 1a). This finding indicates that PDMS is a mildly hydrophobic material, which is consistent with previous studies that characterized the contact angle of unmodified PDMS43–46. It is noteworthy that there was a mild increase in the contact angle as the mixing ratio increased, confirming the finding of Seghir and Arscott (2015)10. Esteves et al. (2009) suggested that different mixing ratios between the vinyl-terminated prepolymer base and the curing agent could result in slight differences in the PDMS chemistry, most notably due to the presence of side reactions that consume C = C bonds at high concentrations of the curing agent47. However, they used a PDMS formulation different from Sylgard 184, although the prepolymer base of their formulation was vinyl-terminated, similar to that of Sylgard 18420,47. This suggests that further studies on the molecular chemistry of Sylgard 184 are required to understand the relationship between the molecular crosslinking and the surface hydrophilicity48.

As the PDMS aged for 8 weeks, the average contact angle increased by 12.8°, 7.61°, 9.27°, 6.29°, and 3.54°, for mixing ratios of 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1, respectively (Fig. 1b). The changes were statistically significant for all mixing ratios except for 30:1 (Fig. S1). The contact angle of PDMS samples aged in each storage environment was compared with that of fresh PDMS samples of the corresponding mixing ratio (Fig. 1b–g). An increasing trend in the contact angle was observed over the 8 weeks in most experimental groups (Fig. 1b). Statistically significant increases in the contact angle were observed from most storage environments for mixing ratios of 5:1, 10:1, and 15:1, except for 5:1 samples submerged in water and mineral oil and 10:1 samples exposed to air (Fig. 1c–e). Samples in mixing ratios of 20:1 and 30:1, however, only showed a statistically significant increase in some storage environments, including storage in air, the Petri dish, and the desiccator for 20:1, and storage in the Petri dish, the desiccator, and water for 30:1 (Fig. 1f, g). Considering all mixing ratios, the average contact angle increased by 6.27°, 10.8°, 9.20°, 9.83°, 7.35°, and 4.01° for samples stored in air, the Petri dish, plastic wrap, the desiccator, water, and mineral oil, respectively (Fig. 1b). Together, these results suggest that the surface hydrophobicity of PDMS increases upon aging, and storage in mineral oil can best resist this increase, followed by storage in air and water. The other three storage environments (Petri dish, plastic wrap, and desiccator) all involve contact between the PDMS surface and a plastic surface, and such contact could potentially modify the chemistry of the PDMS surface31. This might be what caused a more pronounced increase in the PDMS contact angle in these three environments. Although less noticeable, an increase in contact angle also resulted from aging in the other environments. Aging in air may promote surface oxidation or chain scission, slightly increasing surface polarity39. On the other hand, submersion in mineral oil or water may lead to surface reconstruction or the leaching of unbound oligomers, altering the surface energy49,50. Conversely, storage in water or high-water environments has been found to preserve the hydrophilicity of PDMS and deactivate PDMS crosslinkers30,40. Storage in mineral oil or oil-like solvents has apparently not been performed for elastomers such as PDMS, suggesting that further studies are necessary to investigate the effects of such solvents on PDMS.

Tensile failure test

From Fig. S7, it is obvious that different sample replicates of the same mixing ratio at week 0 followed very similar curves before their Young’s moduli reached a maximum. Phothiphatcha and Puttapitukporn (2021) modeled the hyperelastic behavior of PDMS in which stress is a polynomial function of strain in PDMS samples51. The stress-strain profile of PDMS can also be fitted with more sophisticated hyperelastic models, including the Ogden model, the Mooney-Rivlin model, the Neo-Hookean model, and the Yeoh model51–56. In this study, we have selected the polynomial model for simplicity, but other models can be validated in future work. The regressions shown in Table 1 exhibit very high R2 values (R2 > 0.9600 for 30:1 samples, and R2 > 0.9900 for 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, and 20:1 samples), validating the model of Phothiphatcha and Puttapitukporn (2021)51. Furthermore, our study expanded the aforementioned model by modeling the hyperelasticity of PDMS in mixing ratios ranging from 5:1 to 30:1 and experimentally determining the coefficients in the model.

The average initial Young’s modulus of 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1 samples were found to be 1760, 2150, 980, 542, and 97.9 kPa, respectively (Fig. 2b), suggesting a gradual decrease in the Young’s modulus with an increase in the mixing ratio from 10:1 to 30:1. This finding lines up well with the model by Chen et al. (2023), who modeled the Young’s modulus of Sylgard 184 PDMS as a logarithmic function of the mixing ratio using a Mooney-Rivlin model36. This model does not apply to samples with mixing ratios lower than 10:1. We found an increase in the Young’s modulus as the mixing ratio was increased from 5:1 to 10:1, which confirmed the findings of Khanafer et al. (2009) and Hocheng et al. (2010), both of whom suggested that PDMS achieves a peak Young’s modulus at a mixing ratio between 8:1 and 10:157,58. According to Agaoglu et al. (2017) and Khanafer et al. (2009), PDMS mixed in a ratio of around 10:1 is considered most efficiently crosslinked, while PDMS mixed in higher and lower mixing ratios exhibits lower stiffness due to excessive prepolymer base and curing agent, respectively30,57. Similarly, PDMS with the 10:1 mixing ratio exhibited the highest maximum Young’s modulus in all five compositions at an average of 6270 kPa, compared to 5080, 5090, 872, and 191 kPa for 5:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1 PDMS, respectively.

We found that the 15:1 samples exhibited the highest flexibility, as these samples were able to stretch for an average strain ratio of 1.79 before reaching their ultimate strength (Fig. 2d), compared to average strain ratios of 1.09, 1.14, 0.915, and 1.08 for 5:1, 10:1, 20:1, and 30:1 samples, respectively (Figs. 2e, S7). It is expected that PDMS with lower mixing ratios (10:1 and below) and higher crosslinker content would be less flexible because the flexibility and crosslinking density are typically inversely related for rubber-like polymers10,59,60. However, we found that 20:1 and 30:1 samples were more brittle than 15:1 samples, although they were significantly softer. This might be due to the scarcity of the curing agent limiting crosslinking of PDMS30,55. Due to the hyperelastic characteristic of PDMS, higher flexibility is expected to result in higher ultimate strength. As expected, the 15:1 samples exhibited the highest ultimate strength in all five compositions at 4280 kPa, compared to 3240, 4080, 557, and 146 kPa for 5:1, 10:1, 20:1, and 30:1, respectively.

Similarly, since the toughness is approximately proportional to the product of ultimate strength and flexibility (Fig. S19), 15:1 samples also exhibited the highest average toughness in samples of all five mixing ratios as expected. More specifically, the average toughness of 15:1 samples was determined to be 2960 kPa, compared to 1660, 2210, 295, and 92.3 kPa for 5:1, 10:1, 20:1, and 30:1 samples, respectively (Fig. 2f). For a linearly elastic material, the coefficient k in Eq. (5) is expected to be 0.5 or higher (if failure does not occur immediately after reaching ultimate strength), but this value is lower than 0.5 for all five mixing ratios of PDMS, as shown in the slope of the linear regression in each subfigure of Fig. S19. This confirms that PDMS is a non-linearly elastic polymer whose stress-strain curve has an upward concavity. This coefficient is the lowest, only at 0.3106, for 15:1 samples, while it is between 0.4 and 0.5 for all four other mixing ratios. This confirms the finding in Fig. 2a, where 15:1 samples deviate the furthest from the linearly elastic scheme.

In PDMS samples in a mixing ratio of 5:1, statistically significant increases in the initial Young’s modulus were exhibited by samples stored in all environments at week 5 compared to week 0. The flexibility was also significantly compromised in samples stored in plastic wrap (p = 0.0001). The toughness, on the other hand, was enhanced after exposure to air or storage in the Petri dish. From Fig. 8a, it is obvious that 30:1 samples exhibited large increases in the initial Young’s modulus, which more than doubled (increased by 118%, 107%, and 130%, respectively) upon aging in air, Petri dishes, and plastic wrap, and almost doubled in the desiccator and water (increased by 99.7% and 74.9%, respectively). The initial Young’s moduli also indicated an increasing trend in 5:1 and 20:1 samples, except for 20:1 in mineral oil. The 10:1 and 15:1 samples, however, showed very little increases or slight decreases in the initial Young’s moduli.

The maximum Young’s moduli and the ultimate strengths, however, plummeted by more than 50% for all 15:1 groups except for the samples stored in water (Fig. 8b, c). These drops were also observed for 5:1 and 10:1 samples stored in mineral oil. The aged 20:1 samples, on the other hand, showed noticeable improvements in strength compared to fresh samples by an increase of 55.8%, 60.4%, 48.8%, 65.0%, and 51.9% in the maximum Young’s modulus and an increase of 105%, 102%, 85.5%, 122%, and 93.8% in the ultimate strength in the Petri dish, plastic wrap, the desiccator, water, and mineral oil, respectively. Exposure to air for 5 weeks, however, sharply decreased the maximum Young’s modulus and ultimate strength of the 20:1 samples by 59.3% and 34.2%, respectively. The 30:1 samples showed an increase in the maximum Young’s modulus in all six environments but a decrease in the ultimate strength in all environments except for water, which can be attributed to a decrease in flexibility that will be discussed in the next paragraph.

Results for flexibility were very similar to those for the ultimate strength, which is expected because a higher strain at failure also likely leads to a higher stress at failure (Fig. 8d). At low mixing ratios of 5:1 and 10:1, the flexibility showed a 41.5% and 32.6% decrease by aging in mineral oil, respectively. Similar to the maximum Young’s modulus and the ultimate strength, the flexibility of 15:1 samples decreased in all storage environments except for water. The 20:1 samples showed an increase in flexibility except for those directly exposed to air, similar to the Young’s moduli and the ultimate strength. However, unlike the other parameters, the flexibility of 30:1 samples showed a noticeable decrease upon aging in all storage environments, with the least decrease in water (Fig. 8d).

The mixing ratio of 10:1 is considered the standard mixing ratio for PDMS, and therefore PDMS mixed in such a ratio is called “on-ratio,” because it is claimed to be fully crosslinked and has the highest mechanical stiffness30,33,61. The fully crosslinked nature might be the reason why the 10:1 samples experienced the least changes in all five mechanical properties upon aging (Figs. 4, 8). On the other hand, mixing ratios other than 10:1 are considered “off-ratio” and are not fully crosslinked30, and therefore are less stable and more susceptible to aging-related changes. Placet and Delobelle (2015) suggested that aging at room temperature may increase crosslinking of PDMS32. If this theory holds true, then the increase in the initial Young’s moduli of 5:1, 20:1, and especially 30:1 samples could be explained by an increase in crosslinking. Also, most 15:1 samples experienced large decreases while the 20:1 samples experienced large increases in the ultimate strength and the flexibility, possibly because a critical amount of crosslinking is typically necessary for polymers to achieve maximum flexibility (such as the fresh 15:1 samples in our study), yet more crosslinking past the critical balance would cause a decrease in flexibility10,62. The ultimate strength is expected to be dependent on the flexibility because an increase in strain typically corresponds to an increase in stress before samples start to fail (Figs. S7, S9–S13).

A summary of the findings of this mechanical failure study can be stated as follows: In PDMS samples with lower mixing ratios (5:1 and 10:1), the most noticeable deterioration was observed in the mechanical strength and flexibility upon aging in mineral oil. Interestingly, aging in mineral oil increased the ultimate strength and flexibility in 20:1 PDMS, while other compositions showed decreases. This suggests a composition-dependent interaction with oil, where intermediate crosslink density may allow plasticizer-induced toughening, while higher or lower crosslink densities lead to embrittlement or softening.63 In samples with higher mixing ratios (15:1, 20:1, and 30:1), however, most noticeable deteriorations in strength and flexibility were seen in samples directly exposed to air, instead. Notably, in samples aged in all six storage environments, samples submerged in water showed the highest ultimate strength in all five mixing ratios and the highest maximum Young’s modulus for all mixing ratios except for 30:1. Also, in all the aged 15:1 and 30:1 samples, the samples submerged in water retained their flexibility much better than samples aged in other environments. All these results indicate that, to maximally retain mechanical strength and flexibility, PDMS samples should be stored underwater, especially for those at higher mixing ratios. According to Agaoglu et al. (2017), exposure of the PDMS curing agent in water-rich environments may cause the curing agent to deactivate, preventing additional crosslinking from occurring30. On the other hand, exposing the samples directly to oxygen in air could result in increased chain scission and crosslinking, reducing the flexibility of the material. Although there is no direct evidence for this, Zaghdoudi et al. (2021) suggested that such reactions could occur when PDMS is exposed to oxygen and high temperatures39. For samples in mineral oil, further investigations are necessary because apparently no previous studies investigated the interaction between PDMS and mineral oil or similar organic liquid environments.

In summary, we have thoroughly characterized the natural aging of PDMS for a wide range of mixing ratios and storage conditions. Changes in contact angle and mechanical characteristics of PDMS at five mixing ratios were characterized over eight and five weeks, respectively, of aging in six storage environments. We found that PDMS at all mixing ratios was mildly hydrophobic, and an increase in hydrophobicity was exhibited over time, especially in samples with lower mixing ratios. Similar significant changes were found for the mechanical properties of the PDMS samples, including the stiffness, ultimate strength, and flexibility of freshly fabricated PDMS. We found that the 10:1 samples exhibited the highest stiffness, while the 15:1 samples exhibited the highest ultimate strength, flexibility, and toughness. Changes in these quantities during aging were vastly different for different mixing ratios, which can be attributed to different extents of crosslinking. We concluded that storage in mineral oil, water, and air best preserved the hydrophilicity of PDMS, whereas storage in water best preserved the mechanical strength and flexibility of the samples. Therefore, we recommend storing PDMS samples in water or aqueous solutions.

We believe that this study is a significant advancement in the aging characterization of PDMS because previous studies mostly focused on accelerated aging in harsh environments instead of unaccelerated natural aging. Furthermore, we filled a gap in research by thoroughly characterizing the natural aging of PDMS at a wide range of mixing ratios, instead of only on-ratio PDMS, and the effects of storage environments on PDMS to minimize undesirable aging. Future studies may examine the characteristics and natural aging behavior of PDMS with a wider range of mixing ratios, or with more focus on the crosslinking characteristics. Furthermore, while this study focused on inert and common storage conditions, future work may investigate the effects of aging of PDMS in biologically relevant media, such as cell culture media, given its importance in biomedical and organ-on-a-chip applications. In addition, we can investigate further optimization of the storage environments by including potential preservatives and varying storage temperatures.

Methods

PDMS sample fabrication and storage

To fabricate PDMS samples, the elastomeric base and curing agent of PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow) were mixed in ratios of 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1, with each achieving a total weight of 29.3 g so that the layer thickness would be approximately 3 mm when it was cast molded onto a 100 × 100 mm silicon wafer. Each PDMS precursor mixture was poured onto a leveled, plain 100 × 100 mm silicon wafer (University Wafer) and cured in a baking oven (Falc Instruments) at 90 °C for two hours. After baking, 24 rectangular pieces of 20 × 10 mm were cut from each PDMS layer. Four of the 24 pieces were stored in each the six following environments: directly exposed to air, stored in Petri dishes (Kord-Valmark), wrapped in plastic wrap (Kroger), stored in a vacuum-pulled desiccator (United Scientific Supplies), submerged in DI water (MaxTite), and submerged in mineral oil (Innovating Science), all at room temperature. The fabrication and storage steps were repeated for three batches of 120 PDMS slices each, with one batch used for contact angle measurement and two batches used for mechanical failure tests. Week 0 measurements were performed before storage.

Contact angle measurement

To examine the hydrophilicity of PDMS surfaces, the static water contact angle of each PDMS slice was measured every two weeks for up to eight weeks using an optical tensiometer (Biolin Scientific Theta Flow). The sessile drop mode was chosen on the graphic user interface (OneAttension). PDMS samples were leveled on the tensiometer platform, and a full spherical droplet of DI water (MaxTite) was gently dropped against the sample surface. The instantaneous contact angle was measured as the contact angle averaged between the left and right sides of the water droplet at any instant. The contact angle measurement was continued for 10 s. A contact angle vs. time plot was automatically generated by the interface, and a screenshot of the plot was taken for each of the samples. After the measurement, the plotted data were digitized (WebPlotDigitizer), and a simple trapezoidal rule was used to calculate the average contact angle over 10 s.

Tensile failure test

To examine the changes in mechanical characteristics of PDMS samples due to natural aging, a tensile failure test was performed for fresh and five-week-old PDMS samples using an Instron 5944 Universal Testing System. Two batches of PDMS with 24 pieces each were fabricated for each of the five mixing ratios: 5:1, 10:1, 15:1, 20:1, and 30:1. One batch was subjected to a tensile failure test at week 0, and the other batch was aged in the six environments with four samples each and subjected to the tensile failure test at week 5, with four samples per mixing ratio stored in each of the six environments, before testing. During the failure test, the displacement on the Instron 5944 graphic user interface (Bluehill Universal) was zeroed when the distance between the upper and lower pneumatic clamps was 10.0 mm. The thickness of each PDMS sample was measured by a caliper (VWR Traceable) and input into the graphic user interface. The sample was placed between the two clamps so that 10.0 mm of the material was stretched at a strain rate of 5 mm/min until failure. The failure criterion was when the stretching force on the PDMS was reduced past 30% of the maximum force experienced on the PDMS.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed for the Week 0 data using the Shapiro–Wilk test (normality was assumed if p ≥ 0.05). Contact angle data for all five mixing ratios indicated no significant deviation from normality in either group. Therefore, an unpaired, two-sample, two-tailed t test with Welch’s correction (α = 0.05) was performed to compare the contact angle of aged samples at each mixing ratio from each storage environment against fresh samples at that mixing ratio. For the mechanical properties (initial Young’s modulus, maximum Young’s modulus, ultimate strength, flexiblity (strain at ultimate strength), and toughness), results from Shapiro-Wilk and Brown-Forsythe (equal variance assumed if p ≥ 0.05) indicated that a portion of the data could not be assumed to be normally distributed or equal in variance. Therefore, an unpaired, two-sample, two-tailed Brunner-Munzel test (α = 0.05) was used to compare all five mechanical properties of aged samples at each mixing ratio from each storage environment against fresh samples at that mixing ratio. Significance stars were labeled based on the statistical significance of each group of aged samples compared against samples at week 0 (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001).
