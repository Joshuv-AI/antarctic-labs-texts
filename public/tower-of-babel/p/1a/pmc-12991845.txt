Low-dose biochar and nitrogen fertilizer reduces nitrogen loss in acidic tea soils
Authors: Cenwei Liu, Jing Ye, Yi Lin, Bangwei Zhang, Weixi Shu, Yixiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExcessive nitrogen fertilizer in red-soil tea plantations reduces quality and yield in southern China and causes environmental issues. This study employed 15N isotope tracing to evaluate the effects of co-applying biochar (0%, 1%, 2%, and 4%) with nitrogen fertilizer on tea growth, nitrogen use efficiency (NUE), and soil microbial communities in acidic red-soil plantations. The application of 1–2% biochar showed positive outcomes, including increased tea biomass, reduced fertilizer residue and loss, and enhanced soil microbial diversity. In contrast, a high dose of biochar (4%) decreased NUE in whole plants. This reduction was accompanied by an 82.1% increase in urease activity and a 49.5% decrease in nitrate reductase activity. Our findings suggest that co-applying 1–2% biochar with nitrogen represents a promising strategy to optimize root nitrogen uptake, improve tea biomass, and regulate soil microbial communities, thereby supporting more sustainable nitrogen management in tea plantations.

Introduction

Tea plantations (Camellia sinensis) are a major land use type in the low-hilly red soil regions of southern China. Tea prefers acidic soils (pH 4.5–5.5), and is highly dependent on nitrogen (N) fertilizer, and improper management can directly affect both tea yield and quality.1 To increase the production of tea, excessive N fertilizer is often applied in tea plantations, with average nitrogen application rate in China tea gardens reaching 491 kg ha−1, far exceeding the recommended range of 250–375 kg N ha−1.2 Such overuse not only reduces nitrogen utilization efficiency (NUE) by tea, but also leads to a series of environmental issues, including nitrogen leaching, ammonia volatilization, and nitrous oxide (N2O) emissions, thereby accelerating soil acidification and increasing the risks of N loss and greenhouse gas emissions.3,4,5 Long-term excessive nitrogen application markedly accelerates the acidification of tea garden soils, sharply reducing soil pH and thereby increasing the solubility and availability of exchangeable aluminum.6 High levels of Al enrichment have adverse effects on both tea growth and quality.7 This not only affects nutrient availability and tea plant nutrient uptake, but also strongly inhibits the diversity and functional activity of soil microorganisms, particularly those key functional groups involved in organic matter decomposition, nitrogen cycling (e.g., nitrification, denitrification, and nitrogen fixation), and organic transformation.8 This disruption of microbial community structure and function further weakens soil fertility and nitrogen cycling efficiency, raising environmental risks and exerting a dual negative impact on tea yield and quality.9 Hence, developing optimized nitrogen management strategies to balance tea yield and quality, while minimizing environmental pollution, has become a critical scientific and practical goal for achieving ecological and economic benefits.

As a promising alternative, biochar is produced from agricultural waste by pyrolysis at 350°C–700 °C under anaerobic conditions.10 Its porous structure significantly enhances soil adsorption of NH4+, thereby reducing leaching losses.11 Application rates over 5% can significantly raise soil pH and lower exchangeable Al content.12 Additionally, the slow-release properties of biochar can regulate NO3− release by influencing nitrification enzyme activity to match tea plant growth cycles.13 Biochar also increases abundances of ammonia-oxidizing archaea and bacteria, suppresses denitrification, raises soil pH in acidic soils, and reduces N2O production by competitively occupying electron acceptor sites.14,15 These microorganisms further promote organic matter decomposition, improve soil structure, and enhance nitrogen assimilation through symbiotic interactions with tea roots.16 Previous studies have shown that biochar reduces nitrogen loss by adsorbing inorganic nitrogen and altering soil physicochemical properties and microbial processes.17 Biochar-nitrogen co-application can improve NUE and crop growth.18,19 However, in acidic tea garden soils, direct 15N tracing of the pathways by which biochar regulates nitrification-denitrification and mineralization remains limited, and the mechanisms for enhancing tea growth and nutrient availability are poorly understood. In particular, the effects of biochar on nutrient transformation, microbial community dynamics, and their synergistic regulation of tea growth and NUE in the soil-tea system remain unclear. Most previous studies have focused on individual or general crops, with limited systematic investigation into biochar-nitrogen interactions in tea gardens, including the impacts of varied application rates on soil nutrients, N transformations, and microbial structures, and their relevance for “reduction-efficiency” strategies and practical effectiveness evaluations.

To address these gaps, we employed 15N isotope tracing to examine how biochar-nitrogen combinations affect soil nutrients, N transformations, and microbial communities, and how these effects translate into tea growth and NUE. We hypothesize that low-dose biochar enhances soil nitrogen retention and microbial function, thereby synergistically improving NUE and tea growth. The findings will provide a theoretical basis for optimizing tea garden management and for the scientific evaluation of biochar applications in tea soils.

Results

Tea plant growth indicators

Co-application of biochar and fertilizer significantly influenced plant growth and physiological responses. Compared to the BC0 treatment, leaf dry matter mass significantly increased in both BC1 and BC2 (p < 0.05), and stem dry matter mass showed a non-significant decrease of 22.1% in BC1 (p > 0.05), followed by significant reductions of 36.7% and 44.5% in BC2 and BC3 (p < 0.05), respectively (Figure 1A). Biochar application rate exhibited a dose-dependent negative effect on tea plant stems and roots, as evidenced by the progressive reduction in total dry matter mass. This suppressive effect extended to plant growth parameters, with both plant height and stem diameter showing significant downward trends with higher biochar rates. Nitrogen uptake and contribution

Nitrogen uptake in plant tissues exhibited a trend close to the dry matter mass pattern across biochar treatments (Figure 2A). Compared to the BC0 treatment, significant stimulatory effects were observed in the leaves of BC1 and BC2 (p < 0.05), which increased by 82.0% and 146.2%, respectively. In contrast, the higher biochar application of BC3 treatment yielded only 14.3% nitrogen uptake elevation (p > 0.05). Nitrogen uptake demonstrated a consistent negative response to increasing biochar applications in both stem and root tissues. This downward trend was further corroborated at the whole-plant scale, with total nitrogen uptake showing a decreasing trend consistent with that observed in individual stem and root tissues. The highest proportion of fertilizer nitrogen contribution was observed in each tissue in the BC0 treatment (Figure 2B). With increasing biochar application levels, the proportions in leaves and stems exhibited a decreasing trend. Compared to BC0 treatment, the fertilizer nitrogen contribution decreased by 36.8%–47.5% across BC1-BC3 treatments. A negative correlation was also observed between the fertilizer nitrogen contribution to the root and the biochar application rate. The fertilizer nitrogen contribution was higher in leaves than in stems and roots. Soil enzyme activity

Compared to the CK, both the BC0 and BC1 treatments significantly decreased urease activity (UA) by 29.1% and 19.8%, respectively (Figure 3A). In contrast, the highest urease activity was observed in the BC3 treatment, reaching 349.9 μg d−1 g−1, which is an 82.1% increase compared to the BC0 treatment. Overall, UA increased with increasing biochar application when compared to BC0. Meanwhile, nitrate reductase activity (NR) was significantly suppressed by all treatments compared to CK, with reductions ranging from 18.0% to 58.6%. The NR decreased with increasing biochar amount. Soil nitrogen-cycling functional microbial genes

The Shannon index at the KEGG level quantified the diversity and evenness of metabolic pathways across treatments. The highest Shannon index was observed in the BC0 treatment, indicating a greater diversity of metabolic pathway types and a more uniform distribution. In contrast, biochar treatments exhibited fewer metabolic pathway types (Figure 3B). Compared with BC0, biochar treatment significantly decreased the abundance of soil nirK gene, but showed no significant difference among biochar application rates (Figure 3C). The treatment combining 2% biochar with nitrogen fertilizer reduced the abundance of nosZ gene by 22.3%, while other biochar treatments had no significant effect on nosZ gene abundance (p > 0.05). All biochar combined with nitrogen fertilizer treatments significantly increased the abundance of the nirS gene (p < 0.05). Biochar combined with nitrogen fertilizer effectively suppresses soil denitrification and reduces denitrification intensity.

Microbial diversity and community structure

At the phylum level, bacterial abundances in tea plant soils differed among different biochar application rates (Figure 3D). The dominant bacterial phyla across treatments were Actinomycetota (22.8%–30.9%), Pseudomonadota (17.6%–38.1%), Acidobacteriota (5.1%–21.5%), Chloroflexota (9.3%–11.9%), and Planctomycetota (3.6%–4.9%). Biochar addition altered the proportional distribution of bacterial phyla in soils. Compared with CK, the abundances of Actinomycetota significantly increased in all treatments. However, it decreased in all biochar treatments (BC1, BC2, BC3) compared with BC0. The abundance of Pseudomonadota and Acidobacteriota showed a consistent decrease compared with CK, but it increased with biochar addition. Chloroflexota abundance showed no significant difference among treatments (p > 0.05). Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) demonstrated that microbial communities under biochar treatments and CK clustered similarly but distinctly from the BC0 control (non-biochar) at the genetic level. PC1 and PC2 accounted for 81.68% and 7.41% of bacterial community variation, respectively, totaling 88.09% of the explained variance (Figure 3E).

Environmental factors dominate the structure of the microbial community

Overall, environmental factors effectively explained the variation in microbial community structure at the phylum level, with a cumulative variance explanation rate of approximately 88.34% (Figure 3F), and the RDA1 alone accounted for 87.28% of the explained variance. TN, NO3−, and UA are the key environmental factors driving changes in microbial abundance and 15N utilization efficiency. Specifically, higher NO3− concentrations correlated with an increased relative abundance of certain microbial communities, whereas elevated NH4+ levels had the opposite effect. This pattern was further supported by enzyme activities. UA showed a positive correlation with these microbial groups, while NR was negatively correlated. This negative correlation with NR may reflect the inhibitory effect of an ammonium-dominated nitrogen metabolic pathway on the community. Additionally, lower pH levels were linked to reduced microbial community diversity.

Discussion

Nitrogen absorption dynamics and fertilizer use efficiency

Long-term application of nitrogen fertilizer alone significantly reduces soil pH, which leads to a significant rise in nitrogen loss via leaching and runoff.20 Biochar has the potential to enhance soil adsorption capacity for inorganic N, inhibit soil N mineralization, and reduce N leaching.21 Meanwhile, the inherent properties of biochar and its alteration of soil physicochemical properties also affect the migration and transformation of soil N. The experimental data illustrated clear differences in N absorption and NUE in response to varying biochar application rates. Under the co-application of low-dose biochar (1%) and nitrogen fertilizer treatment, the proportion of fertilizer N absorbed by tea leaves increased significantly. The data from Figures 1A and 1B indicate that BC1 and BC2 treatments resulted in significantly higher tea plant biomass, stem diameter, and plant height compared to other treatments. This suggests that the appropriate biochar amendment with nitrogen fertilizer enhances nitrogen absorption from the soil and its transport to the leaves during the growth period of tea plants. Conversely, nitrogen uptake data (Figure 2A) reveal that BC0 treatment showed the highest fertilizer-derived nitrogen absorption, while BC1 and BC2 treatments demonstrated a lower proportional contribution of fertilizer nitrogen. Plants in the BC1 and BC2 treatments exhibited higher biomass despite lower fertilizer nitrogen uptake, which suggests that biochar improved soil structure and microbial mineralization, thereby increasing the availability of native soil nitrogen to plants. Biochar may also enhance nitrogen metabolism enzyme activities to promote nitrogen absorption, while reducing NH3 volatilization and NO3− leaching and thereby indirectly increasing the total soil nitrogen pool. When considered alongside previous studies, our results suggest that biochar application combined with nitrogen fertilizer effectively decreases both the fertilizer residue ratio and the fertilizer loss ratio (Figure 3).20 In tea garden soils, urea is rapidly hydrolyzed under the action of soil urease, releasing a large amount of NH4+-N, which is then converted into NO3−-N, resulting in significant N loss and reduced NUE. However, biochar can inhibit this conversion, thereby reducing the leaching and denitrification losses of NO3−-N.22 Corn biochar contains carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups and has a high cation exchange capacity (CEC), contributing to increased SOM.23 The increase in SOM leads to an increase in exchangeable base cations and the effective cation exchange capacity that enhances NH4+-N fixation in the soil. This result aligns with evidence from a previous study, which found that straw return practices can not only increase SOM but also enhance the fixation of fertilizer N.24 The activity of soil nitrogen transformation enzymes is a critical indicator of nitrogen metabolic dynamics, influencing both nitrogen conversion and plant uptake.25 As the sole extracellular enzyme converting nitrogen fertilizers, urease catalyzes urea breakdown into ammonium and CO2, directly reflecting soil inorganic nitrogen availability.26 Our study found biochar combined nitrogen application significantly enhanced urease activity, likely due to its porous structure providing more reaction sites and the slow-release fertilizer maintaining substrate supply. This synergy improved the soil's capacity to supply nitrogen, supporting higher tea yields.

Responses of microbial communities to biochar application

Soil microorganisms play an important role in the soil ecosystem, influencing the formation and transformation of nutrient cycling.27 In this study, microbial diversity (Shannon index) indicates that biochar addition can enhance soil species richness and community diversity (Figure 3B). However, these differences were not statistically significant among treatments, indicating that the biochar application rate had no significant effect on alpha diversity indices. Biochar application improves soil porosity and provides microhabitats that protect microbes from predation, further enhancing microbial community diversity.28,29 The most predominant bacterial phyla across all treatments were consistent with previous studies.30 These bacterial communities displayed superior adaptability under biochar-amended conditions. The large proportion of shared communities across treatments indicates that the inherent microbial communities in tea plantation soils are relatively stable, with only a small fraction of taxa responding to environmental changes induced by biochar.31,32 Biochar application has been demonstrated to enhance the population levels of diverse soil microbes that play pivotal roles in nitrogen cycling processes. A high dosage of biochar may increase the soil system’s carbon content, thereby raising the C:N ratio, which subsequently affects soil microbial function and reduces plant-available nitrogen.33 Our findings agree with previous studies, which showed that a high dose of biochar (BC3 with 4% biochar) decreased the abundance of Actinomycetota and Chloroflexi, but increased Pseudomonadota. Biochar combined with nitrogen fertilizer also affected the abundance of denitrification (nirK, nirS, and nosZ) genes. The reduction in nirK gene abundance induced by biochar may weaken denitrification, thereby reducing the loss of soil nitrogen in gaseous forms (such as N2O and N2). This reduction in gaseous nitrogen loss helps retain more nitrogen in the soil, making it available for plant uptake and utilization.34

Soil characteristics and tea biomass response to biochar application

The degree of enhancement or reduction in plant growth and development reflects the physiological response of plants to individual or combined applications of biochar and N fertilizer. Numerous studies suggested that biochar can increase plant biomass, which is in agreement with our results.35 In this study, biochar application increased the contents of SOM, TN, AP, and pH value, with the degree of these changes varying depending on the biochar application rate. The observed soil pH increase brought soil conditions closer to the optimal range for nitrifying bacteria.36 These results corroborate earlier findings, which reported that biochar enhances N content through mechanisms including N immobilization, improved soil aeration, and reduced nitrification rates via its porous structure and surface functional groups.37,38 Nevertheless, our results showed that tea plant growth and biomass were negatively impacted when the amount of biochar application increased from 2% to 4% (Table 1). These results are consistent with previous studies indicating that biochar can promote root growth by increasing soil N content and altering soil physicochemical properties.39 For example, rice straw biochar serves as a nutrient reservoir, enriching soils with essential elements such as N, phosphorus, and substantial potassium reserves.40 This enhancement stems from biochar’s inherent capacity to retain diverse organic and inorganic mineral forms, whose composition is influenced by feedstock sources and pyrolysis conditions.41,42 Our results revealed that the application of low-dose biochar (BC1) promoted the synergistic growth of plants’ aboveground and belowground biomass. In contrast, high-dose biochar (BC3) might impede root development. These findings align with a previous study that highlighted that biochar enhances root N absorption and regulates functional microorganisms.43 Specifically, the presence of carboxylate groups in biochar can inhibit nitrification, a process crucial for efficient N utilization. Although the pot-experiment results show statistically significant treatment effects, these findings should be validated through field-scale trials to confirm their robustness under real tea-plantation conditions. Notably, a high biochar application rate did not significantly increase the nitrogen use efficiency over a 6-year cycle.44 This supports the view that biochar has a limited effect on improving overall soil nitrogen availability during the early stages. In addition, biochar has been reported to act as an “electron shuttle,” promoting the microbial reduction of N2O and thereby altering soil nitrogen transformation pathways and availability. Biochar application promotes the growth of electrophilic nrfA-type bacteria and the expression of their functional genes, and these bacteria catalyze the final step of the dissimilatory NO3− reduction to ammonium (DNRA) pathway.45 Thus, while altering the electron flow, biochar also fundamentally shifts the soil redox balance. Consequently, when its application rate exceeds a certain threshold, the positive effect of biochar on nitrogen retention may be counteracted or even reversed by its pronounced reducing disturbance to the entire ecosystem.

Taken together, this study used the 15N isotope tracing method to explore the effect of different biochar application rates (0%, 1%, 2%, and 4%) combined with nitrogen fertilizer on N fertilizer utilization and soil ecosystem functions in tea plantations. The results revealed that co-application of low-dose biochar (1%–2%) exhibited a synergistic effect, which enhanced fertilizer residue, reduced fertilizer loss, and promoted biomass of tea leaves. Conversely, co-application of high-dose biochar (4%) had a negative impact on the environment. It disrupted the stability of the soil ecosystem, leading to a reduction in N availability and inhibiting microbial activity. In the short-term study, co-application of biochar and nitrogen fertilizer did not significantly improve the NUE of plants. However, it effectively reduced the loss rate of N fertilizer and increased N retention in the soil. These properties make biochar, combined with fertilizer, a suitable candidate for long-term application of a slow-release fertilizer, which can continuously supply nutrients to plants over time. The combined application of 1%–2% biochar with N fertilization emerged as an optimal strategy for “reduction and efficiency” management in red soil tea plantations. This integrated approach provides valuable theoretical support for sustainable N cycling and pollution mitigation, helping to maintain the long-term production and ecological balance of tea plantations. Moreover, the combined application of biochar and N provided a higher nutrient supply than the single application of N fertilizer. Importantly, it can effectively reduce N fertilizer loss in tea plantations and protect the surrounding environment. These findings highlight the critical role of precisely controlled biochar dosage in optimizing plant growth and physiological performance in the soil-tea system.

Limitations of the study

This study was a short-term pot experiment, and whether its conclusions can be stably reproduced under long-term field conditions requires multi-year field trials. Since the experiment was conducted in a controlled greenhouse environment, it was unable to fully simulate the complex and variable environmental factors of actual tea plantations, thus introducing uncertainty when extrapolating the findings to real-world conditions. In future research, we could establish long-term field trials in representative red-soil tea plantations, in order to assess the dynamic process of soil acidification, nitrogen accumulation, and nitrogen loss under different treatments. Process-based models could be developed using the long-term monitoring data to simulate and predict the long-term effects of biochar under various climate and management scenarios, thereby providing a decision-making basis for regional promotion.

Experimental model and study participant details

The experimental soil was collected from the Guangxing Tea Garden in Banmian Town, Youxi County, Sanming City, Fujian Province (26.03°N, 118.12°E). The region has a humid subtropical climate, with an average annual temperature of 15 °C and an average annual precipitation of 1600 mm. The soil is classified as an Ultisol, with a sandy loam texture (62% sand, 29% silt, and 9% clay). Biochar was produced from corn straw using a tubular vacuum furnace at 500°C under anaerobic conditions. One-year-old tea seedlings of the cultivar Mingguan 0306D were obtained from a local tea garden. To minimize experimental errors, three vigorous and uniformly sized tea seedlings were transplanted into each pot. A sprinkler irrigation system was employed to maintain soil moisture content at 60%–70%, ensuring a stable water supply during the tea seedling growth period. The physicochemical properties of the soil and biochar are as follow: total carbon, total nitrogen, total phosphate of soil and biochar are: 26.5 g kg−1, 1.67 g kg−1, 0.51 g kg−1, 4.5 and 564.8 g kg−1, 10.2 g kg−1, 2.5 g kg−1, 9.31, respectively. Specific surface area of biochar is 19.5 m2 g−1.

Method details

Greenhouse experiments

The experiment was conducted from early February to late October 2024. A completely randomized pot experiment was designed with 5 treatments, each treatment had three biological replicates, and the data are presented as mean ± standard error (mean ± SE). Based on our previous study, the four treatments included: N fertilizer without biochar (BC0), N fertilizer combined with 1% biochar (BC1), and N fertilizer combined with 2% biochar (BC2), N fertilizer combined with 4% biochar (BC3).20 Biochar was thoroughly mixed with the soil at rates calculated based on the dry weight of 15 kg of soil per pot. Each pot (38 cm in diameter × 30 cm in height) was filled with 15 kg of the soil-biochar mixture and maintained at 60% of its water-holding capacity. N was applied at a rate of 3 g N pot−1 using 15N-labeled urea (Shanghai Chemical Research Institute, purity ≥98%, 15N enrichment 10.15%, N content 46%). Phosphorus and potassium fertilizers were applied as superphosphate (P2O5 12%) and potassium chloride (K2O 50%), at rates of 1.5 g P pot−1 and 1.5 g K pot−1, respectively. All fertilizers were uniformly mixed into the soil and applied as a single basal application. The nitrogen, P2O5 and K2O application rate is equivalent to a field fertilization rate of approximately 239.2 kg N ha−1, 113.4 kg P ha−1, 215.8 kg K ha−1, respectively.

Sample collection

At the end of the experiment, the height and basal stem diameter of tea plants were recorded. Fresh leaves, branches, and roots were harvested separately from each pot, and their masses (g pot−1) were measured. The harvested plant materials were then oven-dried at 70°C to a constant weight and subsequently weighed to determine dry biomass. Soil samples were collected using at a depth of 0–10 cm and air-dried at room temperature for the analysis of physicochemical properties and nutrient indices. The rhizosphere soil was collected by digging out the whole plant, shaking it to remove large soil clumps, and gathering the soil particles adhering to the root surface within 1–2 mm zone. Then, the soil from the root was dislodged by immersion in 5 mL sterile water and vortexing to obtain the rhizosphere soil for subsequent microbial metagenomic analysis.52

Analysis of soil physicochemical properties and soil enzyme analysis

Soil organic matter (OM) content was determined using the potassium dichromate oxidation-reduction titration method. The soil pH was measured using a digital pH meter (model FE20, Mettler-Toledo Instruments, China) following the standard 1:5 (weight/volume) soil-to-water extraction method. Available phosphorus (AP) was determined using the sodium bicarbonate extraction-molybdenum antimony resistance colorimetric method. Total nitrogen (TN) and total phosphorus (TP) were determined by digesting soil samples with the Kjeldahl method and sodium hydroxide fusion method, respectively.53 The contents of TN and TP in the soil were measured by continuous flow colorimetry (Skalar San++SA 2000, Netherlands). NO3−-N was determined using the dual-wavelength ultraviolet spectrophotometry method. For NH4+-N, soil samples were extracted with 0.01 mol L−1 calcium chloride, and NH4+-N concentrations were measured using a continuous flow analyzer (Bran+Luebbe, Germany). Urease activity (UA) was determined using Soil Urease ELISA Kit, which purchased from Suzhou Mingke Biotechnology CO., Ltd. The indophenol blue colorimetric method was used to determine the ammonium N produced by the hydrolysis of urea by urease at wavelength of 578 nm.54 Nitrate reductase activity (NR) was determined by the sulfanilic acid-acetic acid-α-naphthylamine colorimetric method.55 The enzyme activity unit was expressed as the production of 1 μmol of NO2− per gram of soil sample within 24 h.

DNA extractions, library construction, and metagenomic sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of fresh soil sample using the Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, U.S.) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were measured separately using the TBS - 380 and the NanoDrop 2000. The quality of the DNA extract was assessed by running it on a 1% agarose gel. To construct a paired - end library for subsequent analysis, the DNA extract was fragmented to an average size of approximately 350 base pairs (bp) with the Covaris M220 (Gene Company Limited, China). The paired - end library was then built using the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA - Seq kit (Bioo Scientific, Austin, TX, USA). During this process, adapters with the complete set of sequencing primer hybridization sites were attached to the blunt ends of the DNA fragments. The paired - end sequencing was carried out on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) at Majorbio Bio - Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit v1.5 (300 cycles) was used in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions available at http://www.illumina.com.

Sequence quality control and genome assembly

The data analysis was conducted using the online platform provided by Majorbio Cloud Platform (http://www.majorbio.com). Initially, the paired-end Illumina reads underwent adapter trimming, followed by the removal of low-quality reads-specifically those with a length shorter than 50 bp or a quality score below 20. This preprocessing step was efficiently carried out employing fastp (version 0.23.0) (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp, version 0.23.0).46

Gene prediction, taxonomy, and functional annotation

The open reading frames (ORFs) within each assembled contig were identified through the application of Prodigal and MetaGene (http://metagene.cb.k.u - tokyo.ac.jp).47,48 Subsequently, those predicted ORFs that measured 100 bp or longer were selected and translated into amino acid sequences, utilizing NCBI translation table (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Taxonomy/taxonomyhome.html/index.cgi?chapter=tgencodes#SG1). A non-redundant gene catalog was built by applying CD-HIT (version 4.6.1; http://www.bioinformatics.org/cd-hit) using a 90% sequence identity and 90% coverage threshold.49 Then, high - quality reads were mapped to this non - redundant gene catalog, and gene abundance was calculated with a 95% identity criterion by using SOAPaligner (version 2.21; http://soap.genomics.org.cn/).50 Representative sequences from the non-redundant gene catalog were annotated taxonomically by aligning them to the NR database (e-value cutoff: 1e−5) using Diamond (version 0.8.35; http://www.diamondsearch.org/index.php).51 KEGG annotations were performed by matching sequences to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (e-value: 1e−5) using Diamond (version 0.8.35; http://www.genome.jp/kegg/).51

Quantification and statistical analysis

The experimental data were organized using Excel 2016 and subjected to comprehensive statistical analysis using R 4.5.2. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to assess the impact of diverse biochar treatments on key parameters including tea plant growth indicators, soil physicochemical properties, and nitrogen utilization efficiency (NUE). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons among treatment groups were conducted using Tukey’s test significant difference (HSD) test, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05.
