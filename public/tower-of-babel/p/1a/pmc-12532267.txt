Incorporating Lung-involved Malignancies into Pediatric Intensive Care Unit Risk Models
Authors: Wenhao Li, Binxue Xia
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The authors are to be commended for evaluating pediatric risk of mortality IV (PRISM IV) and pediatric index of mortality (PIM 3) in a pediatric oncology cohort.1 This study addresses an important gap in mortality risk prediction for critically ill children with cancer. While the discussion notes several limitations, additional study-specific considerations may further clarify interpretation and strengthen future validation.

First, the heterogeneity of underlying malignancies deserves closer attention. Pediatric oncology spans acute leukemias, lymphomas, and diverse solid tumors, each with distinct clinical courses and complication profiles. Without stratification or weighting for diagnosis-specific mortality, over-representation of certain cancers could bias performance estimates and reduce applicability to less-represented subgroups.

Second, the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission pathway may have introduced selection bias. In Tertiary Oncology Centers, transfer decisions often reflect factors beyond physiological severity, including triage norms, bed availability, family preferences, and anticipated prognosis. Such dynamics may yield a study cohort systematically different from the broader population of critically ill pediatric oncology patients, influencing both discrimination and calibration.

Third, consistency in timing of variable capture is critical for accuracy. Pediatric risk of mortality IV and pediatric index of mortality rely on defined time windows to record peak physiological derangements. Variations in monitoring frequency, laboratory timing, or early intervention can alter severity estimates. Rapid correction of hypotension or metabolic derangements before sampling, for example, may mask initial instability.2

Fourth, the resource setting may affect model validity.3 Both scores were developed in high-resource environments with ready access to advanced supportive therapies, including continuous renal replacement and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. In lower-resource PICUs, the absence of such modalities can shift determinants of mortality toward factors not adequately weighted by the original models.

Fifth, treatment-related toxicities—such as chemotherapy-induced cardiomyopathy, severe mucositis with sepsis, or acute pulmonary injury—were not separated from baseline disease severity. These events may not be fully captured by current variables, and model underperformance could reflect this omission rather than intrinsic design flaws.

Finally, patients with primary pulmonary malignancies or major lung involvement represent a distinct high-risk subgroup. Rapid hypoxemia, airway obstruction, and treatment-induced pneumonitis can dramatically alter prognosis within hours. If deterioration is not recorded at its peak—due to delayed transfer or early stabilization—severity may be underestimated. Incorporating lung-specific variables or performing targeted subgroup analyses could improve predictive accuracy and broaden generalizability.

Addressing these issues through diagnosis-specific calibration, transparent admission pathway data, standardized time-window capture, resource-context adaptation, and inclusion of oncology-specific variables would enhance both the validity and the clinical utility of mortality prediction models in pediatric oncology PICU populations.
