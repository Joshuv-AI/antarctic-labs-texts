Internal Suture Closure of the LAA: Ineffective and out of Date
Authors: Luca Aerts, Mariusz Kowalewski, Bart Maesen
Published: 2025

We welcome the study by Nakayama et al that retrospectively evaluated 125 patients undergoing robotic-assisted mitral valve surgery with left atrial appendage (LAA) closure using an internal suture technique. Postoperative CT-scan was performed to evaluate LAA closure, and the authors found—despite the use of robotic assistance—an incomplete closure of the LAA in 24% of patients. These results confirm previous concerns that internal suturing is not a safe and sufficient technique for LAA exclusion.

Robotic surgery plays an increasingly important role in minimally invasive cardiac surgery. It offers technical advantages, including precision and enhanced visualization. However, technical preference should not come at the cost of incomplete or ineffective treatment. The 2024 ESC/EACTS AF guidelines give a Class I, Level A recommendation for concomitant surgical AF ablation in AF patients undergoing mitral valve surgery—as it increases freedom AF symptoms and recurrences and may prevent death—and a Class I, Level B recommendation for surgical LAA exclusion in patients with AF undergoing cardiac surgery, based on solid evidence from the LAAOS III trial.1,2 This large trial randomized over 4700 patients between closure and no closure of the LAA and demonstrated that adding surgical LAA closure to cardiac surgery resulted in a 33% relative reduction in stroke or systemic embolism. As such, there is no longer a discussion about whether AF and the LAA should be addressed during cardiac surgery; both should.1,2 Choosing a surgical platform or approach should never compromise these essential treatment components.

Several surgical techniques have been described for the management of the LAA, including clipping, suture ligation, stapler resection, and surgical excision. Among these, suture ligation and stapler resection have been most frequently associated with high rates of incomplete closure or the formation of residual stumps. Kanderian et al3 reported a success rate of only 23% for suture ligation, 0% for stapler resection, and 73% for surgical excision. In contrast, Petersen et al4 demonstrated markedly higher success rates with clipping (98.4%), compared with 39.2% for suture ligation, and 76.6% for stapler resection. Complementing these findings, Toale et al5 performed a large multicentre systematic review focusing exclusively on the AtriClip device, which demonstrated a pooled success rate of 97.8%, thereby confirming both its efficacy and reproducibility across diverse patient populations. Finally, Lee’s recent review of surgical and epicardial strategies for LAA closure highlighted that clipping consistently provides the most reliable results, whereas suture ligation proved to be the least effective technique, with highly variable success rates ranging between 23% and 100%.6

The findings by Nakayama et al, using ECG-gated CT and 3D imaging, confirm that closing the LAA from within the atrium with a suture often results in residual stumps or leaks—commonly at the junction with the mitral valve or at the start of the suture line, producing the characteristic “speech bubble” appearance. These incomplete closures, clearly documented in this study, underline how LAA morphology and its proximity to the mitral annulus can hinder complete closure. In addition, the pronounced haemodynamic forces in the left atrium during diastole are concentrated at the junction between the LAA orifice and the mitral valve, which, over time, can loosen the sutures and further contribute to residual leaks.7 Taken together, these findings confirm once again that this technique is not suitable for routine clinical use.

The only effective treatment of the LAA is one that results in complete exclusion or removal. Currently, surgical amputation or external clipping are the only solid, proven techniques to achieve reliable LAA closure, and should be preferred over internal suturing.
