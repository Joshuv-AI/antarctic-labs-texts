Ancient wheat DNA: How and what for?☆
Authors: Caroline Pont, Pierre-Louis Stenger, Carol Moreau, Cécile Huneau, Marie Lelièvre, Jerome Salse
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In complement to classical approaches currently used in agricultural science in characterizing and exploiting in breeding traits- or phenotype-driving genes and genetic variants from modern genetic diversity, knowledge of past evolution and adaptation processes from ancient plant DNA can be mobilized to develop varieties adapted to current environmental challenges. The article reviews the current scientific and technical insights from the investigation of ancient DNA from plant remains with a focus in wheat.

Wheat shaped from a complex evolutionary history

Wheat hybridization history − Wheat and its wild cousins (often referred to as Triticeae) are the product of several million years of evolution signed by recurrent inter-species hybridization. Well before their domestication, nearly 10,000 years ago, the ancestors of our modern wheats were the result of a complex history of hybridization between ancestral species, leading to the superposition of several genomes within the same individual, a process known as allopolyploidy (Fig. 1). Recurrent hybridization and genetic exchanges between diploid species of Triticeae over several million years even before the polyploidization events founded modern wheats as described in Pont and Salse1. These diploid species have 7 pairs of chromosomes, making a total of 14. The emmer lineage evolved via hybridization event, which gave rise to the hexaploid bread wheats. By convention, the genomes of these wild diploid ancestors are designated by a double letter: AA for the genome of Triticum urartu and BB for that of a presumably extinct species whose closest current form belongs to the genus Aegilops, Ae. speltoides. These two species hybridized spontaneously around 800,000 years ago and by addition allowed the emergence of wild allotetraploid form (AABB) T. turgidum dicoccoides, discovered on the shores of Lake Tiberias in 1906 by A. Aaronsohn2. Involved in a complex time series of domesticated forms, it has been identified as the ancestor of the modern durum wheat T. turgidum durum used nowadays for the production of pasta, semolina and therefore very important in Mediterranean culinary culture. The first domesticated form of Triticum dicoccoides (Fig. 1), T. turgidum dicoccum, migrated eastwards from the domestication zone3, giving rise to a form that could be threshed more easily. This form then hybridized with another wild diploid species, Ae. tauschii, with a DD genome, originated from south-east of the Caspian Sea4. This resulted in a new accumulation of genomes, giving rise to the hexaploid line BBAADD, known as T. aestivum or 'bread wheat'. This species therefore has three sets of seven pairs of chromosomes, totalling 42. Thus, there is no ancestral wild forms of hexaploid wheat that further colonized much of the planet since this last polyploidy event, and is used today for the production of flour, bread, and many other uses, including animal feed.Fig. 1Wheat Evolutionary model. In contrast to the emmer (AABB) lineage, the T. timopheevii lineage has a genomic constitution GGAA (Fig. 1) and its wild ancestral form is T. armeniacum from Northern Iraq3, 5. The origin of T. timopheevii (found in Georgia) remains unclear, but was probably introduced from Turkey6, 7 with potential inter-group relationship of the prehistoric and widespread ‘New Glume Wheat’ (NGW), with the oldest known records being from Turkish origin8.

Thus, successive hybridizations and genomic exchanges are at the heart of the evolution and genesis of modern wheat genetic diversity. They are considered as a driving force of wheat adaptation allowing recurrent mixing of genetic diversity which can thus lead to the appearance of new lines and phenotypes on which selection can act and produce new adaptation capacities to different environments. Beyond the contribution of the D genome to cold-adaptive properties and numerous disease resistances, polyploidy allows for the instant fixation of favorable allelic interactions between genomes (fixed heterosis) and also confers the capacity to buffer mutations by the triplication of gene copies and to use the diversity thus created for new adaptations9. Geographical and cultural expansion varies with the ploidy level: some diploid forms have not spread much out of the center of origin and remain confidential today despite a recent resurgence of interest. Tetraploid forms (emmer then durum wheat) historically colonized the Mediterranean region and central Europe in two successive waves, while the hexaploid bread wheat has experienced a more global and world-wide spread.

Wheat domestication genetic legacy – The first archaeological traces of domesticated wheat were found in a string of Early Neolithic peasant villages10 dating from 10,500 to 10,100 BP. The oldest remains of hexaploid wheat (8,800 to 8,400 BP) were discovered at Can Hassan III in southern Turkey and Abu Hureyra in Syria, which overlaps with the habitats of wild emmer in the Fertile Crescent. Later records of hexaploid wheat have been found southwest of the Caspian Sea, where the D genome donor, Ae. tauschii, is identified. Domesticated emmer was abundant in the Middle East during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B (PPNB, 10,800–8,500 years BP) and persisted as the main crop for a few thousand years until it was largely replaced by durum wheat (around 3,000 years BP), probably due to its ease of use due to the facilitated release of the grains during threshing. This archaeological evidence reflects historical contact between bread wheat and wild populations.

Comparison of grain morphology from archaeobotanical sites demonstrates the impact of domestication on grain typology11. Wheat domestication is characterized by the acquisition of numerous physiological and morphological traits12, 13, such as erect plants, increased number of flowers per spikelet, larger and less protein-rich seeds, synchronization of stem emission, non-dispersal of seeds (for harvesting by threshing), reduced seed dormancy for optimal germination, and a shortened flowering period2. All of these traits define the domestication syndrome10, 14, from which the genetic basis is now reported15. Both archaeological and genetic evidences support that from the centre of origin, the Fertile Crescent, the spread of wheat cultivation followed two routes, first towards Western Europe by an inland route (via Anatolia and the Balkans to Central Europe) and a coastal route (via Egypt to the Maghreb and the Iberian Peninsula), and then towards the east by routes to the northeast and along the Inner Asian mountain corridor, later other secondary colonization events in the American, Oceanian and African territories followed.

Ancient remains seminal to investigate wheat domestication and cultivation

During the last 10,000 years of domestication, wheat has been a crucial crop in human history, transitioning societies from hunter-gatherer lifestyles to sedentary agricultural communities. Overall, while the understanding of the origins and introduction routes of free-threshing wheats across western Asia and into Europe is improving, still our understanding of past wheat cultivation remains partial.

Morphological identification of ancient wheat remains − The study of plant macro-fossils is a subdiscipline of archaeobotany which focuses on fruit and seed remains from archaeological samples16. Archaeobotanists work on wild and cultivated plant remains, to reconstruct past environments, plant use and agricultural practices through time. Identification of cultivated plant species is also used to retrace domestication ‘hotspots’ and pathways, as well as identify trade routes10, 17. Archaeological wheat remains are preserved in a different way depending on the domesticated form and conservation: carbonized free-threshing wheat mostly yields grains and rachises (but rachis segments are, however, more susceptible to destruction during charring18) while hulled wheat leaves only spikelet bases and grains (Fig. 2); in waterlogged contexts, many spikelets bases typically subsist, with grains rarely preserved. These biases can distort the precise identification of species based on morphological analysis, with hulled wheat dominating waterlogged assemblages due to differential preservation (no grain and few rachis). Charred grains of free-threshing wheats are more commonly recovered than rachises, but identifying their ploidy level remains difficult. Advanced analyses, such as geometric morphometrics, can help to correct these biases by distinguishing wheat types and revealing the true diversity of ancient wheats. The majority of archaeobotanical remains are preserved in a carbonized state, and many studies exploited these resources because the morphology of such remains is often remarkably well preserved, but morphological identifications of some remains become impossible for the human eye. For example, distinguishing between different naked wheat species such as Triticum durum and Triticum aestivum cannot reliably be achieved morphologically, and genetic assignation is impossible, as there is no ancient DNA conserved from charred samples19. In this context, the geometric morphometric analysis would offer a promising avenue to address these taxonomic uncertainties and future developments in AI-based morphometric analysis could further enhance our ability to resolve species-level identification of ancient remains (Fig. 2). This approach relies on the creation of reference image banks of known accessions experimentally carbonized, analysed through elliptical Fourier transform of grain outlines followed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) using the R package momocs20. Morphometric analysis of archaeological cereal grains is affected by several biases. Charring can cause shrinkage and deformation, often in unpredictable ways, while fragmentation and selective preservation reduce the representativeness of assemblages. In addition, substantial overlap in grain morphology between closely related wheat species and strong intra-taxonomic variability complicate species-level identification. Finally, the limited scope of modern reference collections, which may not capture the diversity of ancient landraces, further constrains reliable classification. Despite this, the initial results are very promising where studies on wheat18 and barley21 report already correct classification rates of 71–90%, but, depending on the taxonomic groups considered, and 86.1% to naked (sub)species (i.e. charred modern specimens of bread wheat (T. aestivum), club wheat (T. compactum), durum wheat (T. durum) and rivet wheat (T. turgidum)) can be properly assigned. Geometric morphometrics study supports the theory of a more northerly hexaploid and more southerly tetraploid expansion route into western Europe18. Moreover, these methods shed new light on the identification and domestication status of ‘new glume wheat’ at Neolithic Çatalhöyük22. In addition to grain-based analyses, morphometry can also be applied to spikelet remains with automated measurements carried out on archaeological NGW spikelets, focusing on parameters such as abscission scar length and U-shaped profiles, and compared with experimentally charred modern spikelets and archaeological remains. Several metrics were adapted from previous work23, with modifications introduced to allow consistent measurements from photographs, and additional parameters were developed specifically to investigate the comparison with modern T. timopheevii and T. dicoccum. To account for natural size discrepancies between modern and archaeological specimens, the measurements were normalized prior to being compared with those of modern T. timopheevii and T. dicoccum. Morphometric analyses revealed that NGW is more similar to T. timopheevii than to T. dicoccum.

Genetic identification of ancient wheat remains − Ancient DNA from remains often exhibits high rates of nucleotide damage, short DNA fragment lengths, and low endogenous DNA content, making it difficult to obtain reliable aDNA data24, 25. There is a risk of modern contamination and incorrect assignments which can lead to false interpretation of the data26. Ancient plant genomic data, particularly that from wheat specimens, presents a significant challenge, making it a growing scientific area5, 24, 25.

Dedicated Facilities − Ancient plant DNA (aDNA) requires strict laboratory protocols to minimize contamination and maximize recovery of highly fragmented DNA. These specialized facilities typically feature physically separated pre- and post-PCR laboratories with positive air pressure systems in a confined lab, HEPA-filtered air supply, and UV decontamination capabilities to prevent cross-contamination with modern DNA27, 28. Critical to this process is the meticulous identification of archaeobotanical remains under a binocular microscope within the same confined, over-pressurized environment, ensuring that both morphological (see previous section) and genetic analyses are protected from external contaminants. All laboratory procedures are conducted within sterile laminar flow cabinets using comprehensive decontamination protocols, including bleach treatment of surfaces, UV sterilization of equipment, and strict waste disposal procedures, as contamination poses the greatest risk to aDNA authenticity29, 30.

DNA extraction challenges − When working with ancient DNA, two major challenges are degradation of the molecules and contamination, mostly from environmental microorganisms. Ancient DNA extraction methods have been optimized for the recovery of short fragments that are characteristic of degraded DNA49. Various pre-treatments exist to reduce contamination, but most have been developed for hard tissues such as bones and teeth. Non-destructive methods have even been proposed for bones50 and herbarium specimens51. Plant remains introduce additional methodological challenges because they contain polyphenols and secondary metabolites that co-extract with DNA and act as inhibitors during library preparation and amplification. The types of inhibitory compounds vary with taxon and tissue, which is why no single DNA extraction protocol is suitable for all plant samples. The nature of the preserved remains depends on the time and environment. For example, desiccated wheat is often preserved as grains, which can be surface-sterilized with bleach24. By contrast, waterlogged wheat is typically represented by glumes and spikelet forks, which are porous, soft tissues saturated with water. In addition, waterlogged contexts often introduce humic substances and fine sediment particles into the tissue, which not only increase the level of exogenous DNA contamination but can also co-extract as enzymatic inhibitors during downstream processing. Surface decontamination seems particularly difficult for those soft samples, and mild enzymatic incubation that would partially digest the outermost material and release surface contaminants while leaving the inner tissue intact for subsequent extraction may be attempted. Waterlogged wheat remains are also extremely small (Fig. 4). Pooling is one strategy to increase input, but it relies on accurate morphological identification, which can be tricky as seen in a previous section and therefore not always reliable, creating risks of mixing different taxa and making the final identification difficult5. To avoid such uncertainties, methods could be adapted to work with very small amounts of material but the drawback is a higher level of contamination, resulting in a very low endogenous DNA rate. With very small inputs (one grain or one spikelet base for example), any aDNA loss has proportionally greater consequences. Therefore, methods developed to optimise steps where the amount of DNA is crucial, would be very useful, such as micro-volume, capillary library preparation methods52 or single-tube library preparation53. Finally, once extracted, aDNA is prepared for high-throughput sequencing where methods are designed to retain ultrashort and highly degraded DNA. Single-stranded aDNA library preparation methods can further improve the recovery of highly degraded DNA54, 55. Optional enzymatic repair of damaged cytosines may be applied, though deamination signals are also useful for authentication56.

Bioinformatic challenges in ancient wheat DNA analysis

Ancient plant genomic data, particularly from wheat specimens, present computational challenges that require sophisticated bioinformatic approaches (Fig. 3) markedly distinct from modern genomic analyses24. The degraded nature of aDNA creates a complex landscape of short, damaged fragments with extremely low coverage and elevated error rates, necessitating specialized methodological frameworks to extract meaningful genetic information from these precious archaeological samples25, 57. Read mapping represents the foundational challenge in ancient wheat genomics, where degraded DNA fragments must be aligned to modern reference genomes despite substantial temporal and evolutionary distances. The inherent difficulties are due to wheat's exceptionally large, and repetitive genome structure with repetitive elements, which create significant mapping ambiguities58. Ancient DNA fragments, typically ranging from 25 to 150 base pairs due to post-mortem degradation, struggle to achieve unique mapping positions within these repetitive landscapes26, 59. The situation is further complicated by reference genome divergence, where mapping ancient sequences to phylogenetically distant genomic references dramatically reduces the detectability of authentic damage signals and can obscure genuine ancient DNA signatures57. This challenge is particularly acute in wheat species complexes, where the hexaploid bread wheat genome (AABBDD) contains multiple homeologous regions (see previous section) that can confound mapping algorithms when processing degraded ancient sequences60, 61, 62. Kistler et al.63 report that for a simulated maize aDNA readset, only 21% of the reads are uniquely and unambiguously aligned, highlighting the substantial information loss using current approaches. Although the BWA tool is considered the most used for mapping ancient DNA, different parameters are classically used that can result in a variation in the percentage of mapped reads of between 7 and 8%5, 24. Aspecific mapping on the D genome is also reported when ancient emmer wheat is investigated24. Phylogenetic investigation from ancient wheat DNA requires fundamentally different approaches compared to contemporary genomic studies due to the extremely low coverage typical of aDNA datasets25, 64. Standard genotyping (i.e. SNP for Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms) calling methods, which rely on sufficient read depth to confidently distinguish heterozygous from homozygous positions65, fail for ancient DNA samples where coverage often falls below 1x per position on the modern reference genome used. To address these challenges, researchers have developed complementary analytical strategies, including single-read sampling per genomic position to minimize stochastic error, genotype likelihood estimation methods that explicitly incorporate uncertainty from low-coverage data, and preferential utilization of transversion substitutions which are inherently less susceptible to cytosine deamination artifacts characteristic of ancient DNA (detailed in the following section, Scott et al.24, Ausmees et al.66). The challenge is intensified in wheat by the species' complex polyploid nature, where distinguishing between homeologous chromosomes and identifying true genetic variants requires sophisticated statistical models that can handle both low coverage and genome complexity simultaneously24, 65. Damage patterns authentication constitutes perhaps the most critical bioinformatic challenge in ancient wheat DNA analysis, as the ability to distinguish authentic ancient sequences from modern contamination depends entirely on detecting characteristics of post-mortem damage signatures26, 67, 68. Ancient DNA exhibits distinctive cytosine-to-thymine transitions at fragment termini due to spontaneous deamination processes, creating a temporal fingerprint that validates ancient sequence authenticity67, 68, 69. However, several factors dramatically influence damage detection reliability, including reference genome divergence, read depth, fragment length, and the underlying damage levels in the sample57. When ancient sequences are mapped to distant reference genomes, the characteristic damage patterns become increasingly difficult to detect, as sequence divergence can mask or randomize the deamination signal57. This creates a methodological paradox where the most appropriate references for ancient wheat samples may be unavailable, forcing researchers to use phylogenetically distant genomes that compromise authentication capabilities. Advanced computational approaches now incorporate correction matrices that adjust damage profiles based on reference divergence, improving signal recovery and authentication accuracy57. Recent advances, such as the ngsBriggs method, further improve authentication by providing rapid and statistically robust inference of damage model parameters, enabling more accurate read-level discrimination between authentic ancient fragments and modern contaminants, even in highly contaminated samples70.

Markers and genomic targets in ancient wheat studies present strategic decisions that profoundly impact data recovery and analytical power19, 71. Organellar genomes, including chloroplast and mitochondrial DNA, offer substantial advantages due to their high copy numbers per cell, potentially improving DNA recovery from degraded samples72, 73, 74. However, these genomic regions present unique challenges, including frequent rearrangements, gene transfer events between organelles, and maternal inheritance patterns that limit population genetic inferences73, 74, 75. Chloroplast genomes in wheat relatives show complex evolutionary patterns with varying rates of sequence evolution and structural modifications that complicate comparative analyses74. Nuclear genomic data, while more informative for population genetic studies, remains extremely difficult to recover from ancient wheat samples due to the single-copy nature of most sequences and the challenges posed by large, repetitive genomes24, 58, 74. Some approaches have employed targeted capture techniques to enrich specific genomic regions of interest, enabling focused analysis on genes relevant to domestication, adaptation, or other traits of evolutionary significance19, 24, 76.

Three studies are considered as references in wheat paleogenomics research, following the previous standards now required in any plant aDNA studies: Chunxiang Li et al.77 established the spread of wheat to East Asia evidenced by the presence of bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) in the Xiaohe cemetery in Xinjiang, China, during the Bronze Age, showing sequence similarity to hexaploid bread wheat. Scott et al.24 study sequenced the genome of 3,000-year-old emmer wheat chaff excavated from Egypt, revealing that Egyptian emmer wheat was genetically distinct from earlier Near Eastern varieties, then suggesting local adaptation and multiple dispersal waves from the Fertile Crescent. Sow et al.5 have reviewed the analysis of this sample to show that this ancient Egyptian sample genetically clustered with Durum wheat from the Horn of Africa and T. carthlicum genotypes, supporting the role of hybrids or gene flow in the emergence of cultivated wheats. Moreover, Sow et al.5 also proposed that a Neolithic New Glume Wheat sample from Lake Chalain (France, 3200–2600 BCE) genetically resembles the GGAA genome group (like domesticated Zanduri wheat T. timopheevii), rather than the more common emmer T. dicoccum, with DNA analyses suggesting NGW as either a GGAA-type wheat, a BBAA-type emmer, or a hybrid of both. This challenges the assumption that GGAA wheats disappeared by 1000 BCE, as these findings suggest their persistence—or hybridization—in Western Europe during the Late Neolithic. Successful studies on ancient maize and emmer wheat have revealed crucial insights into local adaptation, admixture patterns, and the temporal dynamics of domestication trait evolution24, 74. These analyses demonstrate that major domestication transitions, including non-brittle rachis and seed dispersal mechanisms, were largely complete by 6,000 years ago in the Near East, while revealing ongoing gene flow between wild and cultivated populations throughout the domestication process24. The integration of ancient genomic data with archaeobotanical evidence and paleoenvironmental reconstructions promises to revolutionize our understanding of wheat (and crops in a broad sense) evolution, agricultural origins, and the co-evolutionary dynamics between humans and crops across temporal and spatial scales24, 74.

Promises from waterlogged and herbarium samples

Wheat waterlogged remains as unique source of ancient DNA − Waterlogged samples, such as those recovered from ancient pile dwellings in lakes5, 78, 79, have been preserved in water-saturated, anaerobic environments, slowing down organic decay. Such conditions are rare, since plant remains are most commonly preserved through carbonization, when ‘fresh’ organic matter is replaced by carbon and escapes decomposition, although it loses its genetic information19. Ancient plants can also be found desiccated, usually from dry environments (e.g. Egypt, the Middle East), however they are very rare because they are subject to degradation by micro-organisms. Although uncommon, waterlogged conditions offer excellent preservation of a wider variety of taxa (Fig. 4) and their morphological characteristics, and in some cases with preservation of ancient DNA. In waterlogged contexts, the prolonged stay in water results in the dissolution of the endosperm, leaving only a thin, flattened envelope that scarcely retains the original shape of the cereal caryopsis80. For this reason, cereal taxa are mostly identified using chaff remains (spikelet bases, glume bases, rachises) which are only a few millimeters wide. While criteria exist for species identification from this type of biological material81, archaeobotanists sometimes face limits in the identification at the species or even subspecies level (Fig. 2), depending on the quality of the preservation. Some chaff remains are also rarer, such as that of naked wheats which are rarely found on site82. They are difficult to link to a specific species, other than determining whether it is tetraploid or hexaploidy wheats, based on the morphological traits of the rachis81. Therefore, the extraction and analysis of ancient DNA from these remains can offer additional support for more specific identifications at the species or even subspecies level of ancient waterlogged remains.

Despite this exceptional preservation, the extreme fragility of waterlogged remains often makes DNA extraction more challenging. The recent successful extraction and analysis of ancient DNA from 40 waterlogged fragments5 is an exceptional achievement for reconstructing the history of wheat domestication. These results highlight the very high potential of waterlogged remains for future archaeogenomic studies, despite their rarity. As mentioned in the previous section, Sow et al.5 studied NGW from waterlogged remains from the Neolithic site of Lake Chalain (Jura, France, 3200–2600 BCE) and delivered genetic evidence (Fig. 5) showing that these remains were tetraploid wheats, positioned genetically between GGAA-type wheats (T. timopheevii) and BBAA-type wheats (T. dicoccum). Admixture analyses indicated 25–59% GGAA ancestry. This suggests that NGW was either a hybrid form or reflects the fact that T. timopheevii and T. dicoccum were cultivated simultaneously, allowing genetic flow between them. The results highlight that multiple wheat lineages contributed to domestication history beyond the classic “founder crops” model. The clear presence of T. timopheevii ancestry in the ancient DNA of NGW from Chalain demonstrates that this lineage contributed to Neolithic NGW, which was cultivated alongside (and possibly hybridized with) wheats of the dicoccum group. The persistence of T. timopheevii-related landraces in modern cultivation (notably the ‘Zanduri’ mixture, grown in Western Georgia for local consumption) further indicates that this lineage was not completely lost. The apparent extinction of NGW around 1000 BCE therefore likely reflects the progressive disappearance of NGW as a cultivated form, rather than this complete loss of the lineage. Sow et al.5 illustrate the prospect of studying Pile-Dwelling Sites on a large scale, from a site located at an elevation of 500 msl on a western slope of the Jura mountains (Chalain, UNESCO site, France) consisting of 29 villages each spread over an area of 0.5–1 ha and inhabited from 3,200 to 2,600 BCE. More broadly, the UNESCO Prehistoric Pile-Dwelling Sites comprising 111 archaeological sites selected from 937 known sites across six Alpine and sub-Alpine countries (Fig. 5), spanning the period from 5,000 to 500 BCE, undoubtfully represent a unique resource for waterlogged samples investigation. Herbaria, as a unique resource to conduct museomics approaches – Herbaria hold around 350 million plant specimens collected over the past ∼ 400 years, making them invaluable archives for ancient and historical DNA studies83. With digitization and high-throughput sequencing, these collections are increasingly used to explore biodiversity, evolution, ecology, and plant diseases. Genomic approaches applied include shotgun sequencing, genome skimming84, targeted DNA capture, SNP assays, and organellar genome assembly, opening the field of Museomics. Herbarium samples are especially useful when in situ collection is impossible (e.g., extinct or endangered species, remote areas). They enable population genomic studies across time and space (Fig. 6) but also morphometrics, microbiome, stomatal, and isotope analyses85 offering insights into species’ historical ranges, genetic diversity, and adaptation. Recent work has also demonstrated that century-old herbarium plants preserve authentic ancient microbial DNA showing characteristic damage signatures, and that these microbiomes cluster with modern soil microbial communities, thus faithfully reflecting past soil and rhizosphere biodiversity86. In parallel, methodological advances have enabled the recovery of long DNA fragments (>1 kb) from relatively recent herbarium specimens through optimized size-selection protocols, greatly improving the potential for more complete genomic analyses87. Barcoding success, however, depends strongly on sample age: recent specimens (>2000) yield high success rates, whereas older ones (from the 1800 s) are more degraded. New techniques such as targeted capture further enhance recovery from historical material.
