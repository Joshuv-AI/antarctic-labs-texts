Spatially varying coefficient generalized distributed lag models for binary response with MCEM estimation
Authors: Ali Hadianfar, Omid Karimi, Azadeh Saki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe adverse health effects of environmental exposures often manifest after a time delay. Distributed lag models (DLMs) are well-suited for modeling these delayed effects and play a central role in identifying critical exposure windows in environmental epidemiology. However, traditional DLMs frequently fail to account for potential spatial dependence and spatial non-stationarity, limiting their accuracy and generalizability across diverse geographical contexts. To address this gap, we propose a spatially varying coefficient generalized distributed lag model (SVCGDLM) that integrates spatial heterogeneity directly into the DLM framework. To estimate model parameters, we develop an efficient Monte Carlo Expectation–Maximization (MCEM) algorithm employing Pólya-Gamma data augmentation and Gibbs sampling to approximate the conditional expectations in the E-step. Through a comprehensive simulation study, we show that the proposed model significantly outperforms standard generalized linear models (GLMs), generalized geographically weighted regression (GGWR), and Generalized Linear Mixed Model using the INLA Approximation (GLMM-INLA) in terms of parameter estimation accuracy and predictive power. Finally, we apply our model to a real-world dataset from Mashhad, Iran, to estimate the spatially varying association between short-term exposure to PM2.5 and cardiovascular mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-026-02886-1.Keywords: Cardiovascular mortality, Distributed lag models, MCEM algorithm, PM2.5, Pólya-Gamma augmentation, Spatial non-stationarity

Introduction

In epidemiological and environmental health research, the effects of exposures or interventions on health outcomes often do not manifest immediately but instead occur with a temporal delay—a phenomenon known as the delayed effect. Distributed lag models (DLMs) have emerged as essential analytical tools for effectively modeling such delayed responses [1]. DLMs quantify these effects by distributing the impact of an exposure across multiple time lags and employing distinct parameters to capture contributions at each lag period. This approach provides a comprehensive framework for analyzing associations between time-varying environmental exposures and their corresponding health outcomes.

Traditional DLMs typically assume linear exposure–response relationships, but recent methodological advancements have relaxed this constraint. Generalized additive distributed lag models (GADLMs) employ splines to flexibly model lag structures [2], and distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNMs) allow for simultaneous non-linear exposure and lag effects [3]. Bayesian distributed lag models (BDLMs) incorporate prior information to regularize lag coefficients [4], while penalized methods achieve smoothness through difference penalties [5].

Despite these advancements, most existing models have overlooked the potential for spatial heterogeneity in the exposure–response relationship. Such spatial variability may arise from geographic differences in environmental composition, population susceptibility, or exposure measurement error [6]. While some studies have incorporated spatial dependence using, for instance, spatial statistical models [7], spatiotemporal DLMs for zero-inflated data [8], or Markov Random Field (MRF) approaches within GADLMs [5], few have explicitly addressed spatial non-stationarity in lag structures themselves. Spatially varying coefficient (SVC) models naturally accommodate such heterogeneity [9]; however, they are rarely integrated with distributed lag methodologies. A recent study proposed a spatially varying DLM to assess the relationship between air pollution and low birth weight at term [6]. However, this model has computational limitations with large datasets and lacks comprehensive, simulation-based validation. Moreover, it fails to account for spatial dependence in the model residuals, potentially leading to biased inference.

These methodological shortcomings underscore the need for models that jointly address both spatial non-stationarity and spatial dependence within distributed lag frameworks. By integrating both features, such models can enhance the accuracy of effect estimation and improve the interpretability and predictive utility of the results.

To bridge this methodological gap, we propose the Spatially Varying Coefficients Generalized Distributed Lag Model (SVCGDLM). Our model unifies DLM and SVC frameworks by incorporating spatially varying coefficients to capture non-stationarity and Conditional Autoregressive (CAR) random effects to account for spatial dependence.

For the parameter estimation, we develop an efficient Monte Carlo Expectation–Maximization (MCEM) algorithm that leverages numerical optimization techniques within its M-step and relies on maximum likelihood principles for parameter estimation. We use Pólya-Gamma data augmentation to facilitate closed-form conditional distributions in the E-step via Gibbs sampling. The M-step then optimizes the expected complete-data log-likelihood over both fixed and spatial parameters using numerical optimization techniques. This strategy circumvents restrictive linear assumptions and leverages sparse matrix computations to ensure computational scalability.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 introduces the proposed SVCGDLM in detail. Section 3 reports the results of simulation studies evaluating its performance. Finally, Sect. 4 provides a real-world application, exploring the spatial variation in the lagged association between PM2.5 exposure and cardiovascular mortality in Mashhad, Iran.

To address potential concerns regarding double-counting of spatial variation between spatially varying coefficients and spatial random effects, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. This analysis assessed whether our model appropriately distinguishes true spatial heterogeneity in the exposure-lag relationship from residual spatial noise. Data were generated under two scenarios: (i) Setting 2 with lag length 5 but excluding spatial random effects , and (ii) a scenario with spatial random effect while relaxing the spatial dependence in the coefficient process. By fitting the full model to these datasets, we evaluated its ability to accurately attribute spatial signals to the correct source.

In addition, distributed-lag models with high inter-lag correlation can pose identifiability challenges for temporal dependence parameters. To directly assess the model's ability to estimate the temporal correlation parameter ϕ across different temporal smoothness regimes, three distinct true values: (ϕ =0.1, ϕ =0.3, and ϕ =0.75) included in the simulations.

For each setting, we considered three lag lengths L and two sample sizes n . For every combination of setting, lag length, and sample size, 100 independent datasets were generated. The proposed SVCGDLM was then fitted to each dataset and its performance was compared with the following benchmark models:

Generalized Linear Model (GLM): as a global model, a classical logistic regression model was fitted to the pooled data, with a single set of lag coefficients shared across all regions.

For a fair comparison, all models included the same set of lagged exposure variables and covariates .

In the simulation study, the proposed SVCGDLM was fitted using our PG-MCEM algorithm. For each simulated dataset, initial values for the fixed effects were obtained by fitting a standard logistic regression via maximum likelihood. The variance parameters () were set to 1.0, and the spatial and temporal correlation parameters (γ, ρ, ϕ) were initialized at 0.5. In the E-step, we used M = 5,000 Gibbs draws per each EM iteration. The first 1,000 samples were discarded as burn-in, and the remaining 4,000 were thinned by retaining every 5th sample, to reduce autocorrelation, yielding approximately 800 effective samples for Monte Carlo integration.

Convergence was assessed by monitoring the absolute difference in the parameter estimates between successive iterations. The algorithm was considered converged when or when a maximum of 20 EM iterations was reached. In practice, convergence was typically achieved well before the maximum iteration limit across most simulated datasets.

Average runtime per simulated dataset ranged between 3 and 5 min on a standard desktop computer (12th-gen Intel® Core™ i5-12,400, 2.50 GHz, 16 GB RAM), increasing with lag length and sample size as expected.

In addition to RMSE and RMSPE, we evaluated model discrimination and calibration using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), Brier score (BS), calibration‑in‑the‑large (CIL), and calibration slope (CS). The calibration slope quantifies the agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities, with values closer to 1 indicating better calibration. All performance metrics were computed on independently generated hold-out datasets, providing an internal validation of model performance across repeated realizations.

In summary, the simulation results across Settings 1–3 demonstrate that the proposed SVCGDLM provides a robust and competitive framework for modeling spatially varying distributed lag effects. The model demonstrates high accuracy in estimating fixed effects and uses temporal information effectively to improve estimation of variance and temporal dependence parameters. It reasonably captures spatial patterns when a true spatial signal is present, and estimation behavior remains stable and interpretable when no spatial structure exists. These results, supported by larger‑sample analyses (n = 5,000) in Supplementary Figures S5–S6 and Tables S7–S9, suggest that the SVCGDLM is a promising approach for complex spatiotemporal data analysis.

To assess potential overlap between spatially varying coefficients and residual spatial random effects, an additional sensitivity analysis focusing on lag length L = 5 was conducted. This analysis examined whether the model can, in most cases, distinguish covariate‑specific spatial heterogeneity from residual spatial dependence, thereby reducing the risk of double‑counting spatial signal. Specifically, three model specifications were evaluated: (i) a model including only spatially varying coefficients (SVC) without a residual spatial CAR effect; (ii) a model including only a residual spatial CAR effect without spatially varying coefficients; and (iii) the full model including both components. Data were generated under contrasting scenarios where either the spatial dependence of lag coefficients (γ) or the residual spatial correlation (ρ) was set to zero, allowing us to evaluate parameter recovery under partial misspecification. Results (Tables S10–S11) show that when only one source of spatial structure is present, the model appropriately attributes spatial variability to the correct component, with limited inflation of the other parameter. Under the full specification, both spatial components remain identifiable, though estimation variability naturally increases due to the complexity of jointly modeling multiple spatial processes.

Overall, the results from three simulation settings provide empirical evidence that the SVCGDLM can effectively disentangle spatial heterogeneity in exposure effects from residual spatial autocorrelation. By separating the spatially varying structure attributable to the exposure–outcome relationship from the remaining spatial dependence captured by the error component, the model avoids redundant attribution of spatial signal. Consequently, the SVCGDLM is better able to characterize complex spatial phenomena in a coherent and interpretable manner, while improving the reliability of inference under realistic spatial settings.

Analysis of PM2.5 and cardiovascular mortality data

The association between short-term exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and adverse cardiovascular outcomes is well-established in epidemiological literature [14–16]. However, a key limitation of many existing studies is the frequent assumption of spatial stationarity in exposure–response relationships. This assumption may mask important local variations in risk influenced by demographic, environmental, or infrastructural factors, potentially leading to biased effect estimates at regional levels [17]. To address this gap, the application of the proposed SVCGDLM was demonstrated through an analysis of the delayed association between short-term PM2.5 exposure and cardiovascular mortality in Mashhad, Iran. This analysis explicitly accounts for both spatial heterogeneity in exposure–response relationships and temporal lag structures.

Study design and exposure assessment

The dataset for the application study was derived from a historical cohort that utilized routinely collected hospital admission data. Cardiovascular patient records from January 2017 through December 2021 were extracted from the Hospital Information System (HIS) of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences. The dataset included all cardiovascular-related admissions from seven major tertiary hospitals affiliated with the university, covering all 17 administrative districts of Mashhad. The population at risk consisted of residents of Mashhad who were hospitalized with a primary cardiovascular diagnosis during the study period. The outcome of interest was in-hospital cardiovascular mortality, defined as death occurring during the index hospitalization, with the underlying cause coded as cardiovascular disease according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Specifically, cardiovascular diagnoses included ischemic heart disease (I20–I25), atrial fibrillation (I48), heart failure (I50), ischemic stroke (I63, I65–I67, G45–G46), and atherosclerotic diseases (I70–I77.1). To avoid over-representation of frequently hospitalized individuals, only the first eligible admission per patient was retained. Records with non-residential addresses, incomplete demographic information, or inconsistent diagnostic coding were excluded. After applying these criteria, a total of 42,267 unique hospitalizations were included, with an overall in-hospital cardiovascular mortality rate of 9.1%. Details regarding data collection, geocoding procedures, and ethical approvals have been described previously [18].

Ambient exposure to PM2.5 was assessed by linking each patient’s geocoded residential address to daily average PM2.5 concentrations. Daily PM2.5 data were obtained from 22 fixed Air Quality Monitoring (AQM) stations distributed across Mashhad. For each patient, exposure was estimated for the day of admission (lag 0) and the preceding 10 days (lags 1–10) using Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) spatial interpolation. The resulting dataset has a two-level hierarchical structure, with individuals (level 1) nested within administrative districts (level 2). This structure is typical of environmental epidemiology studies examining short-term exposure–outcome associations and allows simultaneous modeling of distributed-lag effects and spatial heterogeneity. The SVCGDLM adjusted for individual-level covariates (age and sex) and time-varying confounders (daily mean temperature, relative humidity, day of the week, holiday indicator, season, and COVID-19 period). District-level spatial random effects were incorporated to account for unmeasured spatial variability not explained by covariates or spatially varying lag coefficients. Given the observational nature of the data and the absence of a counterfactual exposure assignment, all results are interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

The proposed model was fitted using the PG-MCEM algorithm detailed in Sect. 2.2. Initial values for the fixed effects were obtained from a standard logistic regression fitted via maximum likelihood. Variance and dependence parameters were initialized at moderate values (e.g., 1.0 for variances, 0.5 for correlation parameters) to promote stable convergence. At each EM iteration, the E-step was approximated using M = 5,000 Pólya–Gamma draws. The first 1,000 draws were discarded as burn-in, and every fifth draw was retained to reduce autocorrelation, yielding 800 approximately independent samples for Monte Carlo integration. The M-step then updated the model parameters by maximizing the resulting Monte Carlo approximation of the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The estimated covariance parameters revealed a distinct spatial structure in the data. The spatial correlation parameter γ, governing the correlation of the spatially varying coefficients, was estimated at 0.57 (95% CI: 0.35, 0.80). This indicates a moderate spatial dependence, meaning that the PM2.5–cardiovascular mortality association is similar across neighboring districts. The correlation parameter for the random effects (ρ) is estimated to be 0.98 (95% CI: 0.96, 0.99), indicating substantial remaining spatial structure not fully captured by spatially varying effects or measured covariates.

The results demonstrate considerable spatial and temporal heterogeneity in the exposure-lag-response association between PM2.5 and cardiovascular mortality across Mashhad. Most districts showed ORs close to 1.0 with confidence intervals including the null value, indicating no strong overall association. However, statistically significant positive associations were observed in Districts 2, 3, and 15 at lag 4, where an IQR increase in PM2.5 concentration four days prior to admission was associated with increased odds of in-hospital cardiovascular mortality.

These findings highlight the importance of accounting for spatial non-stationarity, as the association between PM2.5 and cardiovascular mortality varies markedly across districts and lag periods.

To assess potential collinearity among the 11 lagged PM2.5 exposure variables, the condition indices, variance decomposition proportions, and variance inflation factors (VIF) were computed. The condition number of the correlation matrix was 5.11, well below the conventional threshold of 30 indicating severe collinearity. All condition indices were below 30, and no pair of variables showed variance decomposition proportions greater than 0.5 under the highest condition index. In addition, VIF values ranged from 1.76 to 2.49, far below the typical cutoff of 5 or 10 (see Supplementary Tables S11 and S12). These diagnostics suggest that collinearity among the lagged exposures is moderate and unlikely to fully explain the estimation difficulties observed for the temporal dependence parameter ϕ at shorter lag lengths.

Model performance was assessed in terms of discrimination (AUC), overall accuracy (Brier score), and calibration (calibration‑in‑the‑large, CIL, and calibration slope, CS). To ensure a fair comparison, all models were fitted using the same set of predictors. In addition to the GLM, GGWR, and GLMM‑INLA models, a spline‑based distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) implemented within a logistic generalized additive model (GAM) framework was included as an additional benchmark. The DLNM-GAM incorporated a cross-basis function for PM2.5 exposure over lags 0–10, using natural splines for both the exposure–response and lag dimensions, and adjusted for the same covariates and district-level random effect.

As shown in Table 5, the proposed SVCGDLM achieved a favorable balance between discrimination, calibration, and predictive accuracy relative to the conventional benchmark models and the DLNM-GAM approach. To evaluate temporal predictive performance, a chronological split-sample validation was conducted. Specifically, models were trained using the first 80% of the observations ordered by date, while the remaining 20% of the study period was reserved as an independent temporal validation set. This approach ensured that predictions were generated for future observations not used during model fitting.

Discussion

Spatial distributed lag models (SDLM) are essential tools for quantifying the short- and long-term impacts of environmental exposures, natural events, or policy interventions on health outcomes. By explicitly modeling the temporal evolution of exposure–response relationships, SDLMs provide a principled framework for capturing latency patterns well-documented in environmental epidemiology. However, conventional SDLMs typically assume spatial homogeneity in the exposure–response relationship—an assumption that is often violated in real-world settings where population vulnerability, environmental conditions, and healthcare access vary substantially across space.

In health economics and social epidemiology, the spatial heterogeneity of delay association presents a prevalent challenge, influenced by varying political and geographical contexts. Such variability may lead to substantial differences in health outcomes across regions, as local policies, resource availability, and cultural factors shape the way populations respond to external influences. Consequently, understanding these spatial disparities is crucial for developing targeted interventions and ensuring equitable health outcomes. Researchers must account for these complexities to draw meaningful conclusions and effectively address the diverse health needs of different communities [19, 20]. To address this gap, the SVCGDLM was proposed. The proposed model simultaneously incorporates spatial non-stationarity in lag structures and spatial dependence through CAR random effects.

From a methodological perspective, spatial dependence and spatial heterogeneity pose substantial computational challenges, particularly for non-Gaussian outcomes. Spatial dependence often makes the use of standard maximum likelihood methods challenging, since the conditional distributions do not have closed forms. The Monte Carlo EM (MCEM) algorithm, first introduced by Dempster et al. (1977), addressed this challenge by approximating conditional expectations in the EM framework using MCMC samples [21]. However, the MCEM algorithm suffers from slow convergence and heavy computational demands. To overcome these limitations, Chen et al. [22] proposed a Pólya-Gamma–based MCEM approach for generalized linear mixed models [23]. By introducing latent variables from the Pólya-Gamma distribution, the conditional distributions attain closed Gaussian forms, making the sampling process straightforward and highly efficient. Empirical findings by Song et al. [24] demonstrated that the Pólya-Gamma–augmented MCEM algorithm achieves faster convergence compared with first-order gradient ascent methods [23].

This Pólya-Gamma–based MCEM algorithm, implemented within a Gibbs sampling framework to estimate the proposed SVCGDLM parameters in the present study. This approach provided a reliable approximation of the log-likelihood function and allowed efficient inference. To our knowledge, our methodology represents the first fully implemented approach for estimation and prediction under the SVCGDLM. Notably, previous research has shown that Pólya-Gamma augmentation is highly effective for hierarchical models with logit links, often outperforming alternatives such as Stan in computational efficiency [23].

In present study, extensive simulation results demonstrated that the SVCGDLM outperforms or performs comparably to standard alternatives—including GLM, GGWR, and GLMM-INLA—in terms of parameter recovery (particularly for spatially varying lag coefficients), predictive accuracy, and calibration. The model showed clear advantages when spatial heterogeneity and moderate-to-strong spatial dependence were present. Performance improved markedly with longer lag structures, highlighting the importance of sufficient temporal information for reliable estimation of temporal dependence parameters. These findings were robust across various sensitivity analyses examining potential double-counting of spatial variation and different levels of temporal correlation.

In the real-world application to PM2.5 exposure and in-hospital cardiovascular mortality in Mashhad, Iran, the SVCGDLM revealed important spatiotemporal heterogeneity. We observed a statistically significant positive association at lag 4 in Districts 2, 3, and 15, while most other districts showed null or weaker associations. This delayed association is consistent with previous studies [16, 25, 26] and the known pathophysiological pathways of PM2.5, which involve systemic inflammation and oxidative stress that can precipitate acute cardiovascular events after a short latency period [27]. More importantly, the analysis uncovered substantial spatial non-stationarity in the exposure–lag–response relationship. The elevated risk in specific northern and northeastern districts likely reflects a combination of higher local exposure levels, socioeconomic vulnerability, and differences in healthcare access. This elevated risk may be attributable to a confluence of factors, including higher baseline PM2.5 levels [28] due to prevailing wind patterns and industrial location, socioeconomic factors that increase susceptibility, or differences in access to healthcare [29].

These findings underscore the limitations of city-wide average estimates, which can mask localized high-risk areas [21, 30–32]. By providing district-specific insights, the SVCGDLM offers actionable information for targeted environmental and public health interventions and contributes to environmental justice efforts.

Several limitations of the present study should be noted. First, the amount of heterogeneity and spatial correlations in lag effects. Second, needs for approximately large sample sizes for variety in spatial–temporal coefficient estimations. Third the real-data application was conducted in a single city; external validation in other geographical settings is essential to evaluate the generalizability and transportability of the framework.

In conclusion, the SVCGDLM represents a flexible and computationally feasible approach for jointly modeling spatial non-stationarity and distributed lag effects in binary health outcomes. By integrating spatial heterogeneity directly into the lag structure, the model provides deeper insights into place-based vulnerabilities and offers a valuable tool for environmental epidemiology and precision public health. Future extensions could include nonlinear exposure–response functions, multivariate outcomes, or integration with high-resolution spatiotemporal exposure surfaces.
