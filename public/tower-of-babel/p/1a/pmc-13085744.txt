Addressing organizational risk factors and interventions during transitions of care for older patients: a systematic umbrella review
Authors: Britt Wagenaar, Bo Schouten, Johannes CF Ket, Cordula Wagner, Hanneke Merten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global aging population presents significant challenges for healthcare systems, with projections indicating that by 2050, individuals aged 65 and above will total 1.6 billion. Older adults, often experience chronic conditions and face frequent transitions in care, which can result in fragmented care and increased risks. Communication issues, poor discharge planning, and lack of standardized procedures contribute to patient safety concerns during these transitions. Addressing such risks through effective organizational interventions is crucial for improving patient outcomes. Existing research often isolates organizational risks and interventions, overlooking their combined impact on patient safety across care settings. Therefore, this systematic umbrella review aims to identify and synthesize organizational risk factors and interventions related to patient safety outcomes in transitions of care, including a hospital admission or an emergency department visit, for older adults aged 65 and above.MethodsThis systematic umbrella review reported following the PRISMA 2020 statement criteria. A literature search (in Medline, Embase, CINAHL and Scopus) was performed from inception up to November 18, 2025, in collaboration with a medical information specialist. Two screening strategies (Excel and ASReview) were utilized for study selection and data extraction was guided by a predefined data extraction tool. Quality of studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist. Data were analysed thematically and narratively synthesized.ResultsA total of 29,587 records were identified, with 18 reviews meeting eligibility criteria for inclusion. Three reviews examined organizational risk factors, highlighting issues in communication, discharge documentation, and coordination. Fifteen reviews focused on interventions, most often discharge planning and medication management, with two reviews also examining organizational risk factors. Other frequently mentioned interventions included patient education, interdisciplinary collaboration, and home-based interventions. Primary outcome categories included readmissions, medication-related outcomes, and health service use.ConclusionThis systematic umbrella review identifies key organizational risk factors and interventions influencing patient safety outcomes during care transitions for older adults. Background

Worldwide, the population is aging at an unprecedented rate, presenting challenges in healthcare systems. By 2050, the number of people aged 65 and older is projected to double, reaching 1.6 billion [1]. Older adults are more likely to experience multiple chronic conditions and functional impairments, requiring continuous and complex care [2]. As a result, the frequency of transitions in care, defined as the coordination and continuity of the movement of patients between different healthcare settings or levels, has increased substantially [3]. During transitions, patients, and especially older patients, are at risk of receiving fragmented care and exacerbation of underlying illness due to multimorbidity [4]. For example, breakdowns in the process that lead to poor transitions and fragmented care can increase diverse adverse effects like, exacerbation of chronic illness, falls, medication errors, increased healthcare costs and specifically hospital readmissions [5–7].

Although many types of risks affect patient safety outcomes, organizational factors such as communication failures, lack of standardization, and poor discharge planning are common [8]. One of the most commonly cited risks is inadequate communication between healthcare providers during patient handovers [9]. Studies have shown that the likelihood of medication errors and adverse outcomes increases when providers fail to communicate essential patient information during transitions (e.g., due to the absence of well-structured digital information exchange systems) [10, 11]. In addition, the absence of standardized procedures for care transitions has been associated with communication breakdowns and increased error rates [12]. Another critical risk is poor discharge planning. Older adults discharged without clear instructions, follow-up appointments, or access to appropriate post-discharge care are more likely to experience complications and readmissions [13–15]. In addition, older adults are particularly vulnerable to the adverse events and safety incidents related to multiple transitions of care [16].

Therefore, targeted organizational interventions are essential to “ensure the coordination and continuity of health care” during care transitions [3]. A substantial body of literature identifies several key organizational interventions, including early transition planning, multidisciplinary teams, electronic tools that enhance communication among providers, patient and caregiver coaching/education, medication reports and reconciliation, the implementation of standardized discharge protocols/letters, home visits and follow-up care [13, 14, 17, 18]. Evidence suggests that such organizational interventions enhance patient safety during care transitions. In addition, evidence-based transitional care models (e.g., Transitional Care Model (TCM), Care Transitions Intervention (CTI), Project RED (Re-Engineered Discharge), Project BOOST (Better Outcomes by Optimizing Safe Transitions) and Interventions to Reduce Acute Care Transfers (INTERACT)) have been developed to support healthcare systems and providers in optimizing transitional care processes [19–24]. These models play a critical role in improving continuity of care and reducing adverse events during transitions, such as hospital-to-home (TCM, CTI, RED) and nursing home-to-hospital (INTERACT) transfers [19–24].

However, despite the availability of numerous transitional care models and a growing body of literature on organizational risks and interventions, there is still a lack of understanding of how interactions between different organizational factors might affect patient safety outcomes across care transitions [20].

This systematic umbrella review aims to identify organizational risk factors and interventions related to patient safety outcomes in transitions of care, including a hospital admission or an emergency department (ED) visit, for older adults aged 65 and above.

Methods

A systematic umbrella review was conducted to identify reviews that examine organizational risk factors and/or organizational interventions regarding safety outcomes for older adults. This umbrella review was reported according to PRISMA 2020 statement criteria [25] (see Additional file 1) and conducted in accordance with an a priori protocol that has been registered at Prospero, with the registration number CRD420251034128.

A systematic umbrella review was selected because it follows a rigorous, structured process to systematically appraise and synthesize international evidence on organizational risks and interventions in care transitions for older adults, minimizing bias and providing reliable, evidence-based conclusions that can guide clinical practice, policy, and future research [26–30]. Given the extensive body of primary research literature on individual risks and interventions, the focus was on reviews to obtain a comprehensive and targeted overview of the available evidence across a range of care transitions (including a hospital admission/ED visit) and outcomes for older patients.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were screened for assessment against the inclusion criteria. Studies were eligible if the study was a review (except for umbrella reviews and review of reviews to prevent overlap in evidence), if their primary aim was to examine organizational risk factors and/or organizational interventions and if the review evaluated the effect of interventions against patient safety outcomes related to transitions of care. The care process had to include a hospital admission or an ED visit but the transition may also have extended to and from various other settings, including primary, secondary, and intermediate healthcare, as well as ambulatory care, long-term care facilities, and home care. ED visits and hospital admissions frequently indicate an acute change in a patient’s situation, during which clear, timely communication and high-quality handover are essential. Furthermore, studies must have focused on older adults, aged 65 years and above.

Full-text reviews published in any language were included. Additionally, reviews up to November 18, 2025, were considered, with no minimum date requirement. To assess whether these inclusion criteria are met, an eligibility verification form has been used (see Additional file 2).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed in the databases: OVID/Medline, Embase.com, Ebsco/CINAHL Plus with Fulltext and Elsevier/Scopus from inception to November 18, 2025 in collaboration with a medical information specialist (JCFK). The full search strategies in all database can be found in Additional file 3.

The search included controlled and free text terms for synonyms of ‘aged’ or ‘geriatric’ and ‘patient transfer’ or ‘discharge’ or ‘handover’ and ‘patient safety’ and ‘hospitals’ and excluding ‘children’ or ‘animals’ or ‘cancer’ or ‘diabetes’. Reviews that focused on diabetes and cancer were excluded, because our study does not focus on specific care pathways or diseases. The search was performed without restrictions for methodology, date or language. Duplicate articles were excluded by a medical information specialist (JCFK) using Endnote X20.0.1 (Clarivate Tm), following the Amsterdam Efficient Deduplication (AED)-method [31] and the Bramer-method [32].

Study selection/source of evidence screening

Following the search, all identified citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote X9 to manage the references and to remove the duplicates. Two strategies of source of evidence screening were conducted in parallel, both consisting of two phases of screening.

Strategy 1: Excel

First, a screening strategy in Excel was conducted, which focused exclusively on narrowing down to reviews by filtering the articles that include the term ‘review’ in their abstract, type and/or title. The first researcher (BW) screened all reviews on their abstract and title against the inclusion criteria. Thereafter, the full text screening was conducted by two independent reviewers (BW & HM). Any articles that were potentially relevant for inclusion in the full-text screening, were discussed among the two reviewers (BW & HM) until consensus was reached.

Strategy 2: ASReview

Second, all references (not restricted to reviews) were imported into ASReview for screening on title and abstract. This strategy ensured no review was overlooked, since some reviews did not include the term “review” in their title, type or abstract and allowed to identify relevant articles and potentially uncover additional reviews that may provide further insights. Due to the large volume of primary research studies, ASReview was used to assist in the process. ASReview is an Artificial-Intelligence tool that ranks articles by predicted relevance, based on prior knowledge given by the researchers [33]. This study showed, in accordance to literature, that the algorithm detected 95% of relevant articles within the first 15% of publications screened [33]. First, a pilot test was conducted in which titles and abstracts were screened by three independent reviewers (BW, HM and BS) for assessment against the inclusion criteria. Any articles that were questionable for inclusion were discussed until consensus was reached. Next, the primary researcher (BW) used the algorithm to begin screening the articles on title and abstract until the stopping criteria was reached (excluding hundred studies consecutively). Once the stopping point was reached, the first (BW) and a second researcher (HM) independently continued screening another hundred studies on title and abstract. Ultimately, also these hundred studies were excluded by both reviewers and the full-text screening phase started. The citations were assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (BW & HM). Again, any articles that were potentially relevant for inclusion in the full-text screening, were discussed among the two reviewers (BW & HM) until consensus was reached.

Quality assessment

The JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses was used to assess the quality of the studies that met the inclusion criteria. The tool has been developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) and collaborators and approved by the JBI Scientific Committee following extensive peer review [34]. The aim of this appraisal tool is to evaluate the methodological quality of a study and assess how effectively it has addressed potential biases in its design, conduct, and analysis [34]. This tool consists of a series of critical appraisal questions and ends with an overall appraisal decision. Each study was assessed independently by two researchers (BW & HM). Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction, analysis and synthesis

Data were charted from included studies by one reviewer (BW) and checked by a second researcher (HM) using a predesigned data extraction tool in Excel developed by two researchers (HM & BW) and iteratively updated. The data extraction form included detailed information about the author(s), year of publication, objective(s) of the study, type of transition, context, total number of patients included, total number of studies reviewed, organizational risk factors, organizational interventions and transitional safety related outcomes relevant to the review question (see Additional file 4). Organizational risk factors and outcomes were categorized based on their content and organizational interventions were structured into different categories based on the foundational evidence-based care transition care models (TCM, CTI, INTERACT and Project RED and BOOST). Consequently, the impact of organizational interventions on outcome measures was systematically synthesized and presented using a table.

Results

Search results

The search identified 29,587 records. After the first screening phase on title and abstract using the first strategy in Excel, 37 full-text reviews remained for eligibility assessment, while 354 full-text publications were left for eligibility assessment using the second strategy of ASReview. The selection process and reasons for exclusion during the second screening phase on full-texts are presented in a PRISMA flow diagram [35] (see Fig. 1). Ultimately, after removing duplicates, 18 reviews were eligible for this review (see list of included reviews in Additional file 5).

PRISMA flow diagram illustrates the selection process

Quality appraisal results

Using the JBI Critical Appraisal approach, all included reviews were appraised on their methodological rigor, specifically evaluating the extent to which each study addressed potential bias in its design, conduct, and analysis (see Additional file 6). All reviews clearly stated their objectives and mostly used adequate resources and appropriate inclusion criteria and search strategies. Directives for new research and methods used to combine studies were often well conducted, though some reviews lacked clarity or did not report details on the other components.

Study characteristics

Review characteristics are extracted in Tables 1 and 2, and summarized in Table 3. The included reviews were published between 2009 and 2025, and all were English. Most reviews were systematic reviews (8, 44%) [13, 36, 40, 43, 45, 47, 49, 51] and systematic reviews including a meta-analysis (7, 38%) [39, 41, 42, 44, 48, 50, 52] while the remaining three reviews were other types of reviews, such as a scoping review (1, 6%) [37], an integrative literature review (1, 6%) [46], and a realist synthesis (1, 6%) [49]. The top five countries or regions of the underlying studies included in the reviews originated from the United States (41, 18%), Australia (31, 14%), Europe (23, 10%), North-America (23, 10%) and Canada (20, 9%). Furthermore, the number of studies included in the reviews ranged from less than five studies to more than twenty (224 studies in total) and the total number of patients included varied between less than 500 to more than 20,000. All reviews that included transitions involving hospital and/or ED admission were included in this review, while those that did not involve hospital/ED admission were excluded. Furthermore, the types of care transitions examined also varied within different settings such as hospitals, (EDs), nursing homes, wards, residential care facilities, and home care.

Review findings: types of interventions, risk factors and outcomes addressed

Types of risks addressed

Three reviews (17% of all reviews) [36–38] specifically examined organizational risk factors related to transition-based outcomes (Table 1). The risk factors were diverse and therefore organized into categories according to their content (Table 4). Two risk categories that were identified by all three reviews (100%) included communication and coordination issues. Risk factors within the communication category consist of a lack of communication, miscommunication and a lack of timely discharge planning. Risks related to the coordination category comprised of a lack of timely coordination between hospital and community providers and poor coordination, lack of standardized processes across organizations, early hospital release, lack of post-discharge follow-up and health monitoring, and lack of information about post-discharge life. Second most frequently cited risk categories, included risks related to discharge documentation [37, 38], time and resource constraints [36, 38], and staff/organizational factors (two reviews, 67%) [36, 38]. Risks within these categories comprised inadequate, unclear, incomplete, outdated, unstandardized, duplicate or disorganized discharge documents, missing medication lists/unclear transfer data, different computer patient record systems, time constraints, limited availability of community resources, cost containment and a lack of staff continuity and dilution or discontinuity of social worker roles. Less frequently mentioned risks, such as inadequate patient education [38] and lack of family support [36], were reported by only one review (33%).

Types of interventions addressed

Seventeen reviews (94%) [13, 36, 37, 39–52] examined organizational interventions related to transition-based outcomes (Table 2). Only one review (6%) [38] did not explicitly focus on interventions. The interventions were varying and structured into categories based on their content and transitional care models (Table 5). The most frequently mentioned intervention category was discharge planning (twelve reviews, 67%) [13, 36, 37, 41, 42, 44–47, 49, 51, 52]. Most cited interventions within this category were individualized discharge planning, (comprehensive geriatric) assessment and designating a key coordinator (liaison, advance practice nurse, ED coordinator) for continuity of care, comprehensive transitional care programs, coordination of care and use of discharge checklist. The second most frequently mentioned category was medication management (ten reviews, 56%) [13, 40–43, 45, 47, 48, 50, 51] including medication reconciliation/review, pharmacist-led interventions and medication transfer summary/medication management. Another important category was patient and family education/involvement (nine reviews, 50.0%) [13, 36, 40, 41, 45–48, 51], including patient and family education on self-management, fall prevention and medication. Additionally, home-based interventions (eight reviews, 44%) [13, 39, 42, 44, 45, 48, 51, 52] were important, such as (interdisciplinary) home visits for education, safety checks and follow-up. Furthermore, standardized communication and information (seven reviews, 39%) [13, 37, 40, 43, 46–48] was frequently discussed followed by interdisciplinary collaboration (six reviews, 33%) [13, 36, 40, 42, 45, 46]. Interventions such as interdisciplinary teams, interdisciplinary ward rounds, transfer of information, provision of standardized, structured and patient-centred discharge documentation belong to these categories. Other categories included hospital therapy interventions (four reviews, 22%) [42, 47, 51, 52], post-discharge follow-up [42, 48, 51, 52] (four reviews, 22%), education of care providers (two reviews, 11%) [13, 41] and other interventions (four reviews, 22%) [41, 44, 47].

Types of outcomes addressed

All reviews that examined organizational interventions also reflected on different transitional safety outcomes. These outcomes varied and were categorized into different categories based on their content (Table 6). Fourteen reviews (77%) [13, 38–42, 44–47, 49–52] examined outcomes related to readmission and revisits. These reviews reflected on hospitalization, readmission (rate) and (emergency) (re)visits. Eleven reviews (61%) [38–40, 42, 43, 45–47, 50–52] studied adverse event related outcomes, such as falling events, mortality, functional status and mobility. Another ten reviews (56%) have reflected on health service use related outcomes, consisting of increased costs, length of stay (LOS), length of treatment (LOT), hospital service use, ED throughput time and long term care admission [13, 37, 40, 43, 46–48, 51]. Six reviews (33%) [13, 38, 40, 43, 45, 50] examined medication related outcomes, such as medication errors/discrepancy medication appropriateness Index (MAI), medication/drug related readmission, medication related outcomes and drug related problems. Other outcomes (ten reviews, 56%) included quality of care (six reviews, 33%) [45, 46, 48, 49, 51, 52], patient satisfaction (five reviews, 28%) [13, 45, 46, 51, 52], efficiency of communication, discharge planning and care transitions (three reviews, 19%) [36–38], life impact outcomes (two reviews, 11%) [51, 52], clinical outcomes (one review, 6%) [51] and continuity of care (one review, 6%) [38].

Influence of interventions and risk factors on outcomes addressed

The outcome measures varied, but also the effect of interventions on these outcomes varied greatly (see Table 7 and for more details see Additional file 7). First off, some reviews focused on broad outcomes, such as hospital admissions, adverse events, revisits and overall readmission rates, while others focused on more specific measures, such as fall incidents, functional mobility, readmission within a specific time frame (e.g., one day). Second, the effect of interventions varied, as indicated by the symbols used in Table 7. Some interventions showed increasing (↑), decreasing (↓), mixed (↓↑) or no (statistical significant) (X) effect. For instance, assigning a key coordinator has been associated with a reduction (↓) in readmission rates [13, 46]. Another example of a mixed effect ((↓↑) in combination with no significant effect (X)) is the effect of a nurse or geriatric assessment of the patient’s individual needs, on hospital admission and ED revisit [49] and discharge planning on readmission [13, 49, 51, 52] (also see Additional file 7). Third, several interventions affect multiple outcome measures across different transition time points, with these outcomes often being interrelated. An example is the influence of geriatric assessment [44, 47, 49, 51, 52] on multiple outcomes within different time moments. This intervention showed positive (no harmful) effects on readmission rate, but it also showed an increase in hospital admissions, mortality and ED revisits. Fourth, the reviews showed substantial variability in how they measured and reported the impacts of interventions on different outcomes. Because many reviews were unable to pool the data, no statistical analysis was conducted; instead, the data were synthesized using alternative methods. While some reviews found significant associations, others reported no significant impact (X). For example, one review found a decreasing effect of a comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) by an Advanced Nurse Practitioner (ANP) on hospital admission rate (p = 0.048) [44], while another review found mixed (↓↑) and no significant (x) effects on hospital admissions [49]. Reviews that demonstrated significant effects often relied on statistical measures such as p-values or relative risk (RR) [13, 36, 37, 39–45, 47–51], whereas others made qualitative claims without statistical validation [13, 37, 43, 46–48, 51, 52]. Finally, some reviews focused on the effects of a single intervention, assessing its impact on specific outcomes in isolation [13, 37, 39, 40, 42, 43, 46–49, 51, 52], while other reviews focused on the effect of a bundle of interventions [13, 37, 41, 44–47, 49–52]. For example, the effect of medication review as a single intervention was examined in one review [48] in contrast to a review which examined medication review in combination with other interventions [41].

Discussion

This systematic umbrella review provides a comprehensive synthesis of a wide range of organizational risk factors and interventions aimed at improving patient’s safety during care transitions including a hospital admission or an ED visit, among older adults. These interventions showed considerable variability on different safety outcomes.

The organizational risks, interventions and outcomes varied across different transitions in the care process and align with existing literature and models. First of all, organizational risk factors and interventions align with the tools/components of foundational evidence-based care transition (TCM, CTI, INTERACT and Project RED and BOOST) [19–24]. For instance, patient and family education [13, 36, 38, 40, 41, 45, 47, 48, 51] identification of patient risks, problems and needs [44, 45, 47, 49, 51, 52], tools for health professionals, collaboration and communication [13, 36–38, 40, 45, 46, 48], medications (reconciliation) [13, 37, 38, 40–43, 45, 47, 48, 50, 51], discharge planning [13, 36, 42, 45, 46, 51, 52], home visits and follow-up [13, 39, 42, 44, 45, 48, 51, 52], transition coach [48] and written discharge instructions [13, 37] overlap with the models. Additionally, the interventions identified in this umbrella also (partially) align with the seven key elements deemed essential for safe and seamless transitions by the National Transitions of Care Coalition (NTCC) [53]. These seven elements that are similar consist of medications management services & coordination and information transfer [13, 36–38, 40–43, 45–48, 50, 51], transition planning [13, 36, 42, 45, 46, 51, 52], patient and identified family caregiver engagement & education [13, 36, 38, 40, 41, 45, 47, 48, 51], follow-up care [13, 39, 42, 44, 45, 48, 51, 52], healthcare provider engagement and shared accountability across the healthcare continuum. Based on the diversity of the identified risks and interventions, as well as their alignment with existing models, it can be concluded that a multifaceted set of risks and interventions spans various types of care transitions. Additionally, considerable variation in outcome measures was observed, which is also consistent with care transition models [19, 21–23]. There consists overlap in outcome measures, such as reduced hospital readmissions [13, 38–42, 44–47, 49–52], ED (re)visits [40, 44, 47, 49–52], healthcare costs [13, 45, 51, 52], and improved patient outcomes (e.g., patient satisfaction, functional status, QOL) [13, 42, 45, 46, 48, 49, 51, 52]. The heterogeneity in outcome measures highlights the need to emphasize multiple outcome and patient safety measures across different care transitions. The variation in patient risk characteristics, transitions of care interventions, and patient outcomes demonstrates the complexity of patient safety across care transitions, and alignment with the complex interventions framework by the medical research Council on the National Institute for health research [54].

Effects of interventions varied, with specific interventions ranging from helpful to ineffective or even harmful for certain transitional outcomes [55]. For example, one review found a significant decreasing effect (p = 0.048) of a geriatric assessment on hospital admission rate [44], while another review also found an increasing effect on hospital admission [49]. Additionally, medication reconciliation/review showed both a decreasing and increasing effect on readmission rate [13, 41, 47, 51]. So, the effectiveness of an intervention is not universally assured, as it depends on the manner of implementation and contextual factors, such as variations in healthcare systems across regions and countries [56]. Hence, caution is required before applying an approach without first establishing whether the factors that enabled its success elsewhere are also present in the given setting. A framework can be useful in gaining a clear understanding of which interventions are effective in specific settings. For example, the Care Transitions Framework can serve as a valuable tool for health care providers and policy makers to explore how, why, and where care transition interventions succeed or fail to achieve the desired outcomes and determine whether the framework components can be adapted and expanded to other settings [57].

Furthermore, the effectiveness of interventions appeared to vary not only between contexts but also across transition time points and different types of outcomes measured. Several interventions affect multiple outcome measures across different transition time points, with these outcomes often being interrelated. An example is the influence of geriatric assessment [44, 47, 49, 51, 52] on multiple outcomes within different time moments. For example, geriatric assessment showed positive (no harmful) effects on readmission rate, but it also showed an increase in hospital admissions, mortality and ED revisits. In contrast, our analysis also identified multiple interventions that affect multiple outcomes positively (meaning that a strong body of evidence demonstrated a positive impact on outcomes, with only a few showing no significant effect, and none exhibiting any harm). Key interventions, such as information transfer, comprehensive transitional care programs and follow-up (phone calls/home visits), appeared to be effective across multiple outcomes and transition time points (e.g., readmission rate, ED revisit and diverse adverse events). So, it is important to recognize which interventions are effective at what specific time points of transitions and for particular outcome measures, and that effects of interventions are interrelated.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this systematic umbrella review is its comprehensive synthesis of the variety of organizational risks and interventions aimed at improving patient safety during care transitions for older adults. Since we used an AI-driven literature search strategy in addition to a direct identification of reviews we were able to identify and double check relevant data. However, we were not able to use AI for our update of the search, because the algorithm and model of ASReview changed in meantime, which could have led to missing relevant data.

However, despite its strengths, this review has limitations. The overall quality appraisal was moderate to good, but many of the included reviews either did not assess publication bias within the included studies [13, 36–38, 41, 49] or it was unclear whether they did [39, 40, 42–47, 50], so the results should be interpreted with caution [58].

Furthermore, the scope of the search strategy might have led to the exclusion of relevant reviews. Because the aim of our review was primarily on the narrowed focus of patient safety perspective, reviews that included broader “quality of care” perspectives were excluded. For example, potential relevant reviews by Allen et al. [59], Fønss Rasmussen et al. [7], Lee et al. [60] and Joo et al. [61] focused on transitional care interventions for older people from hospital to home but with a more general focus on quality of care. However, these reviews demonstrated some similarities in interventions and outcome measures [59–61].

Finally, our research strategy may have produced results that do not reflect the prevalence of different organizational risks and interventions. Discharge planning, (standardized) communication and information, discharge documentation, medication management, family involvement, education for staff, family/social support, and post-discharge follow-up and hospital coordination are not meant to reflect on the relative importance of these individual strategies. The discrepancy in prevalence may have been caused by the search strategy, giving the impression that certain risks and (effects of) interventions are less researched, even though their importance is increasingly recognized.

Conclusion

This systematic umbrella review has revealed a wide variability in organizational risk factors, and interventions influencing patient safety outcomes during care transitions which include a hospital admission or an emergency department visit, for older adults, with considerable variability in both implementation and outcome measures. Policymakers, healthcare organizations, and providers must be aware that interventions during care transitions often influence multiple, interrelated outcomes at various time points, underscoring the importance of context-specific, adaptable strategies to effectively improve patient safety outcomes.
