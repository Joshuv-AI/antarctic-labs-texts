Antithrombotic strategies in adult COVID-19 patients: a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis
Authors: Han-Bing Chen, Hui Chen, Jing-Yuan Xu, Rui-Xuan Yu, Nan Shi, Ying Chi, Yi-Yue Ge, Lun-Biao Cui, Shi Zhang, Jianfeng Xie, Haibo Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Since the outbreak in 2019, our battle against COVID-19 has lasted for 4 years. Despite the WHO’s declaration that the COVID-19 pandemic no longer constitutes a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, the epidemic has not ended completely. The continued presence of COVID-19 poses a significant threat to public health. Effectively reducing the mortality and decreasing the occurrence of COVID-19 complications are long-term challenges that we must confront.

Coagulopathy is a common complication of COVID-19,1 and its incidence gradually increases with the severity of the disease.2 Coagulopathy is associated to some extent with poor prognosis in patients and may lead to life-threatening events such as arterial thrombosis, venous thrombosis and even pulmonary embolism.3,5 Microvascular and macrovascular thrombosis formation is a significant characteristic manifestation of COVID-19, and it is also an important factor leading to respiratory failure and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).6 Extensive pulmonary microvascular thrombosis found in autopsies is considered an important cause of hypoxaemia in COVID-19 patients.7 8 Given the potential risks of coagulation disorders in hospitalised patients, including anticoagulants, antiplatelet (AP) agents and even thrombolytics, various antithrombotic strategies are often empirically used. The use of antithrombotic drugs improves the hypercoagulable state of patients and may improve oxygenation by reducing intrapulmonary shunting, but on the other hand, it may increase the risk of bleeding. Studies have found that the overall bleeding rate in COVID-19 patients is 4.8% (95% CI, 2.9 to 7.3), with a major bleeding rate of 2.3% (95% CI 1.0 to 4.2), and the incidence of venous thromboembolism in critically ill patients is 7.6% (95% CI 3.9 to 13.3), with a major bleeding rate of 5.6% (95% CI 2.4 to 10.7).9 Finding a balance between bleeding and thrombosis is a challenge faced by clinicians.

Up to now, multiple randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have extensively discussed various treatment methods for COVID-19-related coagulopathy.610,16 However, the optimal antithrombotic strategy has not yet been determined. Traditional pairwise meta-analyses have been conducted, but the results remain conflicting.17,19 For instance, a meta-analysis by Reis et al suggested that therapeutic-dose anticoagulation (TA) may reduce mortality in hospitalised COVID-19 patients compared with prophylactic dosing,17 whereas Giossi et al and Duo et al reported no significant difference in mortality between the two dosing regimens.18 19 These inconsistencies may arise due to heterogeneity in patient populations, timing of treatment initiation or inconsistent definitions of outcomes across studies. Moreover, some antithrombotic strategies have not been directly compared in clinical trials, and their impact on patient prognosis is unclear. Based on existing data, many aspects, such as drug dosage, timing of intervention, combination strategies, etc, seem to lack definitive conclusions. Compared with the early stages of the pandemic, we now have substantial high-quality evidence from evidence-based medicine, and it is necessary to summarise and sort out the antithrombotic strategies for hospitalised COVID-19 patients based on high-quality research evidence. Network meta-analysis (NMA) provides an alternative to traditional meta-analysis techniques, using both direct and indirect evidence to compare different strategies. This makes it possible to assess comparisons that were not previously evaluated positively and improves the overall accuracy of estimates.20 Currently, there is a lack of a macroscopic understanding of different strategies in the issue of antithrombotic strategies for COVID-19. Therefore, we conducted an NMA to systematically compare the benefits and risks of various antithrombotic strategies in adults with COVID-19.

Methods

Design

We conducted an NMA using a Bayesian framework to compare different antithrombotic strategies in adult COVID-19 patients. This NMA was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement21 and the PRISMA extension for NMA.22 The research was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), and the registration number is CRD42022355213.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Eligibility criteria

Type of study

We only included RCTs that compare different antithrombotic strategies in COVID-19 patients published in English without regional restrictions. Trials published as abstracts only will be excluded.

Type of participants

Eligible patients were adults (aged 18 years or older) with laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection. There were no restrictions on gender, race or nationality.

Type of intervention and comparison

Any antithrombotic strategy would be included. The interventions mainly included anticoagulants (eg, unfractionated heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin and direct oral anticoagulants, such as rivaroxaban, apixaban and dabigatran), AP agents (eg, aspirin, clopidogrel and ticagrelor), fibrinolytic agents (FAs; eg, alteplase and tenecteplase) and their different combinations (eg, AP plus anticoagulant and FA plus anticoagulant). The antithrombotic strategies were categorised based on the dose and type of drugs: prophylactic-dose anticoagulation (PA), TA, AP, TA plus AP (TA+AP), AP plus PA (AP+PA) and FAs plus PA (FA+PA). Eligible trials should include at least one active arm of antithrombotic therapy, and controls could be other types of antithrombotic strategies or standard of care (SoC). SoC was defined as placebo alone, no treatment or supportive care excluding anticoagulation. If a study labelled an intervention as SoC but all participants actually received standard-dose or PA, we reclassified this arm as PA for consistency in analysis. This reclassification was done to enhance the transitivity of the network by aligning treatment categories according to actual interventions rather than trial-reported labels.

Type of outcomes

The primary outcome was the all-cause mortality at 28 days. If 28-day mortality data were not available, we selected the closest follow-up time, up to 90 days. Secondary outcomes included major thrombotic events and major bleeding. The definitions of major thrombotic event and major bleeding were primarily based on the criteria used in each individual trial. For trials that did not provide a clear definition for these composite outcomes, we included reported individual events, such as venous thrombotic events, arterial thrombotic events, pulmonary embolism, ischaemic stroke, myocardial infarction and bleeding events (as applicable), in our analysis of the respective secondary outcome.

Search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library and ClinicalTrials.gov up to August 2023 and then updated our searches in November 2023 and February 2024. The search strategy combined terms for COVID-19 (eg, ‘SARS-CoV-2’ and ‘coronavirus disease 2019’), antithrombotic agents (including anticoagulants, AP drugs and FAs) and their synonyms. The details of the search strategy were shown in online supplemental appendix 1. In addition, we also manually searched the reference lists of eligible articles and related reviews for additional studies.

Study selection

All studies identified through electronic and manual searches were imported into EndNote. After removing duplicates, two reviewers independently screened and selected studies through the titles and abstracts of each study and identified relevant studies. After identifying preliminary eligible studies, further full-text screening was conducted to determine the final eligible studies. Any differences of opinion were resolved through discussion. If no consensus was reached, a third reviewer made the final decision.

Data extraction and management

Two independent reviewers extracted data using self-made standardised Excel (Microsoft Excel V.2019; Microsoft, USA) tables. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion. A third reviewer acted as the arbitrator when no consensus was reached. The data extracted from each study mainly included study characteristics (first author, country, published year and study design), baseline data (number of participants, age, sex, etc), the RoB data, intervention and control details and outcomes.

RoB assessment

Two independent reviewers assessed the RoB using the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool for RCTs. After an independent evaluation, reviewers compared their judgements for each domain. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer arbitrated. The finalised judgements for each domain were then synthesised into a single RoB assessment per study. The RoB was rated according to the following domains: (1) random sequence generation; (2) allocation concealment; (3) blinding of the participants and personnel; (4) blinding of the outcome assessment; (5) incomplete outcome data; (6) selective reporting and (7) other biases. We performed a RoB assessment for the primary outcome (28-day all-cause mortality). We judged the trials as ‘overall low RoB’ if all domains were at low risk, ‘overall high RoB’ if any domain was at high RoB and ‘unclear’ if at least one domain was unclear but no domain was at high RoB.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analysis

For all direct comparisons, when two or more trials were available, we performed a traditional pairwise meta-analysis using both the random-effects model and fixed-effects model. We calculated the pooled risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI for dichotomous outcomes and the standardised mean difference (SMD) with 95% CI for continuous outcomes.

Bayesian NMA

We conducted the NMA within a Bayesian framework using the Markov chain Monte Carlo method. Four chains with different initial values were run simultaneously with 1000 adaptations, 10 000 burn‐ins and 50 000 iterations to eliminate the influences of the initial value. Trace plots and density plots were used to assess the convergence of the simulations.23 RR with 95% credible interval (CrI) and SMD with 95% CrI were used as summary statistics for dichotomous and continuous outcomes, respectively, to quantify the effects of different antithrombotic strategies, and the statistics with 95% CrI were calculated based on non-informative priors (a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 10 000). We used both the fixed-effects and random-effects models for the NMA and chose the model with a lower deviance information criterion (DIC). We estimated the ranking probabilities for all antithrombotic strategies through the surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA)24 and showed the results with rankograms and cumulative ranking probability plots. SUCRA is used to rank different treatments based on their relative efficacy or safety. SUCRA values range from 0% to 100%, where 100% indicates the treatment is certainly the best, and 0% means it is the worst among all options. A higher SUCRA value reflects a greater likelihood that treatment ranks favourably compared with others. However, SUCRA rankings should be interpreted with caution, particularly when the 95% CrIs of treatment effects are wide and overlapping. A high SUCRA does not imply a statistically significant advantage; it only suggests a trend towards better performance based on the overall network evidence. Therefore, when SUCRA is high but CrIs overlap substantially, the ranking reflects a probabilistic tendency rather than a definitive conclusion. We emphasise that both SUCRA and CrIs should be considered together for a balanced interpretation. All the Bayesian NMAs were performed through the ‘BUGSnet’ package (V.1.1.2)25 of the R V.4.2.2 software.

Assessment of heterogeneity

To assess the heterogeneity in the network, we estimated the between-study variance (τ²) from the random-effects model. Prediction intervals were also calculated to represent the range within which the true effect of a future study is expected to lie, accounting for both uncertainty in the mean effect and heterogeneity. These intervals provide a more realistic reflection of expected variation than CrIs, which only capture uncertainty in the estimated mean. For completeness, we also performed pairwise meta-analyses and reported I² and Cochran’s Q statistics where applicable; however, these measures are limited in the context of NMA and should be interpreted with caution.

Assessment of transitivity and inconsistency

For each treatment node in the network, we calculated and reported the crude event rates for each outcome, which were used to assess the plausibility of the transitivity assumption (online supplemental appendix 6.2), and drew leverage plots for the consistent model and the inconsistent model separately to examine inconsistency at the global level. We then drew a posterior mean deviance comparison plot to compare the posterior mean deviance of each data point between consistency and inconsistency models to check whether there were potential problems with the transitivity assumption.

In addition to statistical assessments of inconsistency, we also considered the assumption of transitivity through a qualitative examination of clinical and methodological similarity across treatment comparisons. Important effect modifiers such as disease severity (eg, non-hospitalised, hospitalised non-critically ill and critically ill), baseline risk and SoC were carefully evaluated. While we included both hospitalised and non-hospitalised adults to maximise evidence synthesis, we recognised potential transitivity violations due to setting differences. To address this issue, we performed prespecified subgroup analyses based on hospitalisation status (non-hospitalised vs hospitalised) and further stratified hospitalised patients by disease severity (non-critically ill vs critically ill). This approach helped us assess the robustness of our results in more homogeneous populations and better account for clinical heterogeneity that may influence treatment effects.

Assessment of publication bias

For direct pairwise comparisons with at least 10 studies, publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and funnel plots26 (p<0.05 was regarded as significant evidence of publication bias). For the NMA, we qualitatively examined the asymmetry of the funnel plot to assess publication bias.

Quality of evidence

We used the CINeMA (confidence in NMA) framework27 to evaluate the quality of evidence, which considered six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence.

Results

We retrieved a total of 4789 articles through electronic and manual searches. To ensure the inclusion of the latest research, we updated our search two times and found 2759 articles remaining after removing duplicates. After further screening based on titles and abstracts, 87 studies were included for full-text screening. Ultimately, 35 trials (comprising 39 949 patients) met our inclusion criteria, including 31 published studies610,16 28 and 4 completed trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov from which results were extracted directly from the publicly posted outcome data on the registry platform (ie, NCT04391179, NCT04424901, NCT04504032 and NCT04505592),51,54 though not yet formally published in peer-reviewed journals at the time of our analysis. 11 studies were excluded due to population ineligibility: one study enrolled paediatric COVID-19 patients (aged<18 years), and 10 studies involved non-COVID-19 populations. The screening process is illustrated in online supplemental appendix 2, and the list of included studies and major exclusions is shown in online supplemental appendices 3 and 4.

Characteristics of included studies

Online supplemental appendix 5 summarises the basic characteristics of the included literature. All studies were published between 2020 and 2023, with sample sizes ranging from 13 to 14 892. The age of patients typically ranged from 50 to 70 years, with a body mass index (BMI) of approximately 30. Approximately 60% of the patients were male. Among the included population, about 25% had diabetes, 45% had hypertension, 15% had chronic cardiovascular diseases and 23% were current or former smokers (these summary statistics represent the approximate mean values reported in each included study). Detailed baseline data for individual studies are provided in online supplemental appendix 6.1. According to our search strategy, a total of seven antithrombotic strategies, including SoC, were identified, comprising four standalone antithrombotic regimens (SoC, PA, TA and AP) and three combination regimens (TA+AP, AP+PA and FA+PA).

RoB, heterogeneity and inconsistency

The results of the RoB assessment and reasons for judgement of each domain of the RoB are shown in online supplemental appendix 7. Since our primary outcome was an objective finding (mortality), we judged that the outcomes were not likely to be influenced by a lack of blinding. Therefore, we assessed the domain related to blinding in all trials as low risk. Among the 35 trials, 16 studies (45.7%) were overall assessed as low risk, 8 studies (22.9%) were unclear and 11 studies (31.4%) were high risk. The most common reasons for high-risk bias were early termination of trials, followed by allocation concealment and incomplete outcome data.

For heterogeneity assessment, we quantified heterogeneity using the between-study variance (τ²) for the entire network. For the primary outcome measure of 28-day all-cause mortality, τ² was estimated to be 0.04 with a 95% CrI of 0.00 to 0.15, indicating low to moderate heterogeneity across the network (online supplemental appendix 6.3). We fitted both the fixed-effects and random-effects models. The random-effects model provided a better fit, as evidenced by a lower DIC (DIC=120.85 vs 123.17 for the fixed-effects model), and was thus chosen for the final analysis (online supplemental appendix 6.4). In addition, the paired meta-analysis showed no significant heterogeneity for most direct comparisons (I²=0%), but moderate heterogeneity was found in the comparison of therapeutic and preventive doses of anticoagulation (I²=47%) (online supplemental appendix 8.4).

The posterior mean deviance comparison plot showed no evidence of significant inconsistency between the consistency and inconsistency models (online supplemental appendices 6.5 and 6.6). Trace plots showed adequate mixing and stationarity with no evident drifts or patterns across chains. Density plots demonstrated unimodal, symmetric distributions with substantial overlap between chains, indicating convergence was achieved for all model parameters (online supplemental appendix 6.7).

28-day all-cause mortality

A total of 35 RCTs, involving 39 949 patients in total, reported results on all-cause mortality for seven different types of antithrombotic strategies (figure 1). Visual inspection of the funnel plot (online supplemental appendix 8.1) revealed no substantial asymmetry, suggesting a low risk of publication bias. Compared with SoC, both PA (RR 0.71, 95% CrI 0.44 to 0.99, moderate certainty) and TA (RR 0.65, 95% CrI 0.38 to 0.94, moderate certainty) reduced the 28-day all-cause mortality (figure 2, online supplemental appendix 8.2). Among them, TA ranked the highest in reducing 28-day mortality, with a SUCRA of 91.44% (figure 3, online supplemental appendix 8.3), while other antithrombotic strategies did not significantly reduce mortality. CINeMA indicates that the overall quality of evidence for antithrombotic strategies on 28-day mortality is mainly very low to low (online supplemental appendix 9).

Major thrombotic events

Among the 32 RCTs involving 39 448 participants, 1528 patients reported major thrombotic events. Compared with SoC, all antithrombotic strategies except for the AP and FA+PA significantly reduced the incidence of major thrombotic events. Specifically, TA (RR 0.19, 95% CrI 0.09 to 0.31, SUCRA 87.42%), TA+AP (RR 0.27, 95% CrI 0.05 to 0.95, SUCRA 76.82%), PA (RR 0.33, 95% CrI 0.18 to 0.53, SUCRA 59.50%) and AP+PA (RR 0.52, 95% CrI 0.25 to 0.94, SUCRA 37.96%) were effective in reducing major thrombotic events (online supplemental appendix 10.1).

Major bleeding events

In the 32 RCTs involving a total of 35 373 participants, 424 patients reported major bleeding events. Compared with SoC, AP was associated with an increased risk of major bleeding events (RR 2.27, 95% CrI 1.01 to 5.07, SUCRA 12.12%). Other antithrombotic strategies did not show statistically significant differences in bleeding risk, as indicated by the wide and overlapping 95% CrIs. Although the s overlapped considerably, SUCRA values provided additional insights into the overall ranking of safety profiles. PA (SUCRA 90.20%) and TA (SUCRA 63.68%) had the highest probabilities of ranking among the safest strategies, suggesting a favourable trend. However, given the imprecision of the estimates and absence of statistical significance, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Specifically, TA+AP (RR 2.76, 95% CrI 0.14 to 12.79, SUCRA 33.55%), AP+PA (RR 1.21, 95% CrI 0.32 to 2.93, SUCRA 44.67%), FA+PA (RR 1.08, 95% CrI 0.22 to 3.16, SUCRA 56.30%), TA (RR 0.84, 95% CrI 0.19 to 2.01, SUCRA 63.68%) and PA (RR 0.56, 95% CrI 0.17 to 1.22, SUCRA 90.20%) did not significantly affect major bleeding events (online supplemental appendix 10.2).

To provide a more intuitive representation of major thrombotic and major bleeding risks, we plotted a two-dimensional graph using the RR and 95% CrI from the analysis of major thrombotic and major bleeding risks. From the graph, we found that to minimise both thrombotic events and bleeding risks simultaneously, TA or PA may be the preferable choice (figure 4).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity analyses, after removing studies with zero events, removing high-RoB studies or adopting three scepticism priors, showed results similar to our main analysis, in terms of reducing the 28-day all-cause mortality, TA and PA remained the top two ranked antithrombotic strategies (online supplemental appendix 11).

Because disease severity and hospitalisation status may act as effect modifiers that challenge the transitivity assumption, we performed subgroup analyses based on hospitalisation status (non-hospitalised vs hospitalised) and further stratified hospitalised patients by severity (critically ill vs non-critically ill). In the subgroup of non-hospitalised patients, PA significantly reduced the 28-day mortality of COVID-19 patients (RR 0.52, 95% CrI 0.26 to 0.90, SUCRA 62.92%). In the subgroup of hospitalised patients, various antithrombotic strategies did not show statistically significant differences in reducing the 28-day mortality (figure 5, online supplemental appendix 12.1). However, after further stratified analysis of hospitalised patients based on the severity of illness, it was found that for non-critically ill patients, TA was more favourable than PA in decreasing the 28-day mortality (fixed-effect model: RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.91; random-effect model: RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.48 to 1.05) (online supplemental appendix 12.2). Whereas for critically ill patients, all antithrombotic strategies did not show significant advantages (online supplemental appendix 12.3).

Discussion

Principal findings

To our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively compare different antithrombotic strategies in adult COVID-19 patients, providing a ranking order for their use in clinics in terms of individual efficacy and improvement in adverse events. This systematic review and NMA comprehensively evaluated the intervention effects regarding the different types of antithrombotic strategies on the 28-day all-cause mortality of adult COVID-19 patients. We found that compared with SoC, both the PA strategy and TA strategy effectively reduced the 28-day all-cause mortality, while the reduction in mortality with AP or fibrinolytic therapy was not significant. After conducting subgroup analysis on patients based on whether the patients were hospitalised, we found that for non-hospitalised patients, PA significantly reduced the 28-day mortality of COVID-19 patients. However, for hospitalised patients, various antithrombotic strategies did not show statistically significant differences in reducing the 28-day mortality. After further stratified analysis of hospitalised patients based on the severity of illness, it was found that for non-critically ill patients, TA was more favourable than PA in decreasing the 28-day mortality, but all antithrombotic strategies did not show significant advantages for critically ill patients. In terms of reducing the risk of thrombosis and bleeding, TA and PA can achieve a good balance, not only reducing thrombotic events but also not increasing the risk of bleeding.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Our systematic review has several strengths. We searched several well-known databases and retrieved data from clinical trial registries. Our findings represent the most comprehensive report of antithrombotic therapy for COVID-19 patients to date. We included only RCTs and excluded observational studies, which enhances the quality of evidence in our analysis. Different team members independently conducted duplicate assessments of literature eligibility, data extraction and RoB, ensuring strong reliability of the data results. While there have been numerous studies on antithrombotic therapy for COVID-19 patients, the lack of direct head-to-head comparisons among various antithrombotic strategies prompted us to integrate different strategies using NMA methodology for the first time. The results hold significant practical implications for the formulation of antithrombotic strategies for COVID-19 and the rapid selection of appropriate strategies by healthcare professionals.

Our study has several limitations. First, we excluded non-English studies, which may lead to potential publication bias. Second, the methods of employing the same antithrombotic strategy vary across different RCT studies, including differences in drug types, dosages, routes of administration, etc, which may lead to potential heterogeneity. Additionally, patient information was not confirmed at the individual participant level, and the severity of patients’ illness in some studies is so mixed that we cannot make specific subgroup divisions, leading to the data on the severity of illnesses not being used in the subgroup analysis. Therefore, when conducting subgroup analysis based on the severity of illness, we only selected literature with clear definitions, but this may result in the inability to objectively reflect the current RCT research results. Lastly, in our study, the inclusion of both non-hospitalised and hospitalised patients may introduce clinical heterogeneity, as the SoC, disease severity and likelihood of receiving TA differ substantially between these groups. Although we attempted to address this by conducting stratified analyses, we recognise that some inconsistency may still persist and that pooled estimates across heterogeneous populations should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, some antithrombotic strategies have fewer associated articles, and the results of comparisons with other antithrombotic strategies need to be interpreted with caution.

Comparisons with similar research

Previously, there has been no extensive comparison of different antithrombotic strategies in adult COVID-19 patients regarding prognosis and major adverse events through NMA. In clinical practice, there are numerous controversies regarding the selection of antithrombotic regimens for COVID-19 patients, with a lack of thorough and comprehensive examination and integration. We provide the most complete and up-to-date data to support the selection of antithrombotic regimens for COVID-19 patients, assisting clinicians in making decisions in clinical practice. Our analysis results suggest that the PA and TA strategies appear to be potentially beneficial to reduce mortality in COVID-19 patients, but with important variations depending on patient subgroups. Specifically, for non-hospitalised patients with mainly mild disease, different from the latest published traditional meta-analysis55 and our direct comparison results, our network results show that PA can significantly improve patient 28-day mortality compared with SoC. For non-critically ill patients who need to be hospitalised, the dose of anticoagulant drugs needs to be further increased to a therapeutic dose, and this is consistent with the results of some previously published meta-analyses comparing PA and TA directly.56 However, in hospitalised critically ill patients, any antithrombotic regimen no longer appears to play an important role in reducing 28-day mortality, possibly because the coagulation status may no longer be the primary cause of life-threatening illness. Overall, anticoagulation strategies, whether at prophylactic or therapeutic doses, will not increase the incidence of complications, such as thrombosis or bleeding, in patients. These findings undoubtedly simplify clinicians’ choice of anticoagulant drug dosage for COVID-19 patients, allowing clinicians to make quick judgements when facing COVID-19 patients.

A traditional meta-analysis conducted by Kollias et al,57 incorporating two randomised studies and five observational studies, suggested that the therapeutic-dose antithrombotic strategy appears relatively safe for hospitalised patients compared with prophylactic-dose and is associated with additional survival benefits. However, this study was conducted early, the number of included studies was limited and the severity of hospitalised patients was relatively mixed, which may have led to unsatisfactory overall quality.57 An exploratory analysis of TA strategy also focused on patients hospitalised with moderate or severe COVID-19, examining which patient characteristics were associated with the benefits or harms of therapeutic-dose heparin.58 The findings of this study suggest that therapeutic-dose heparin is more likely to be beneficial for patients with milder illness, while critically ill COVID-19 patients requiring organ support or intensive care unit (ICU) care do not benefit from therapeutic-dose heparin. These analytical research results support the conclusions drawn from our stratified analysis based on the severity of the condition. A traditional meta-analysis conducted by Pilia et al,59 comprising four RCTs, regarding adverse events, such as major thrombotic events and major bleeding events, indicated that therapeutic-dose heparin anticoagulation was associated with a lower incidence of major thrombotic events compared with the prophylactic-dose heparin. Moreover, it did not increase the risk of bleeding in non-critically ill patients.59 We endorse the aforementioned analyses regarding prophylactic-dose versus TA strategies and have further expanded on antithrombotic strategies, ranking them based on the SUCRA curve. Heparin and low molecular weight heparin not only possess anticoagulant properties but also exhibit anti-inflammatory effects and potential improvements in antiviral and endothelial function.60,63 Timely administration of adequate doses of anticoagulant drugs can maximise their efficacy, thereby improving patient outcomes.

Our finding that the PA group had a significantly lower mortality rate than the SoC group in non-hospitalised patients differs from the result of Reis et al.17 However, we note that the pooled relative risk (RR=0.70) in their study also indicated that PA may be more beneficial in improving mortality, although without reaching statistical significance (95% CI 0.29 to 1.67), likely due to the limited sample size (six trials). In contrast, our NMA incorporated data from 10 RCTs with direct comparisons and an additional six studies contributing indirect evidence. The inclusion of indirect comparisons via the network structure enhances the comprehensiveness and precision of treatment effect estimates. While the inclusion of indirect evidence introduces assumptions of transitivity, it also allows better integration of all available evidence. Taken together, these differences in methodology and scope may explain the more favourable and statistically significant estimate observed in our analysis. Nonetheless, we emphasise that these findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and more high-quality, targeted RCTs are needed in non-hospitalised populations.

In our NMA, TA was not associated with a statistically significant increase in major bleeding compared with SoC. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously for several reasons. First, the 95% CrI for this result was wide (0.19 to 2.01), encompassing values both below and above 1.0. This range includes the possibility of both reduced and increased bleeding risk, indicating substantial imprecision in the estimate. Second, the absolute incidence of major bleeding events was low across included trials, with some studies reporting zero events. This rarity substantially limits the statistical power to detect a difference. Our findings are in line with those of a recent meta-analysis by Reis et al,17 which similarly noted that ‘there were too few who experienced major bleeding (zero events) to determine whether TA made a difference compared with placebo.’ Thus, while our analysis suggests no significant increase in bleeding risk, this should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of safety. Further research with larger sample sizes and more events is warranted to confirm this finding.

Policy implications

This study provides high-quality evidence from evidence-based medicine regarding the application value of different types of antithrombotic strategies in adult COVID-19 patients. For non-hospitalised patients with mild disease, direct comparison results between any two antithrombotic strategies showed no statistical difference, which is consistent with the latest antithrombotic guidelines for COVID-19 patients recommending that PA will not improve overall health in non-hospitalised patients.64 However, our NMA results after integrating direct comparisons and indirect comparisons suggest that PA is beneficial in improving the primary outcomes of non-hospitalised patients, which is inconsistent with the guideline opinions.64 More RCTs may be needed to focus on non-hospitalised patients.

Through a comprehensive analysis of various antithrombotic regimens attempted in recent years, we found that anticoagulant therapy, especially TA, can effectively improve all-cause mortality in non-critically ill adult COVID-19 patients in the hospital, significantly prevent the occurrence of thrombotic events and entail a lower risk of bleeding. For critically ill patients, even more aggressive antithrombotic treatment regimens have not demonstrated significant benefits, which may be because coagulopathy is no longer the primary factor leading to death in these critically ill patients; addressing severe immune dysregulation within the body appears to be more crucial for these patients. Our study largely supports the current guidelines on antithrombotic therapy for COVID-19 patients65 and provides high-quality, evidence-based assistance for the selection of thromboprophylaxis and treatment strategies in the context of COVID-19.

Conclusions

Compared with SoC, both PA and TA reduced the 28-day all-cause mortality of adult COVID-19 patients. While our NMA indicates possible advantages of PA in non-hospitalised patients and TA in hospitalised non-critically ill patients, these recommendations should be interpreted cautiously, considering the variability across studies and potential limitations of indirect comparisons.
