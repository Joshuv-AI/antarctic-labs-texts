ADAM17 blockade restores natural killer cell antibody-dependent cytotoxicity against human immunodeficiency virus-1
Authors: Sho Sugawara, Stephanie Jost, Griffin Woolley, R. Keith Reeves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural killer (NK) cells are critical innate effector cells against human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) transmission and pathogenesis. Among the diverse NK cell functions, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) is the most critical for HIV-1 control. Nevertheless, multiple reports have demonstrated dysfunctional NK cell activity in people living with HIV-1 (PLWH), including impaired ADCC. As a putative mechanism, CD16 signaling activation in PLWH is perturbed via ADAM17-mediated CD16 downregulation, and ADAM17 blockade can reverse CD16 downregulation in PLWH NK cells. However, the link between elevated ADAM17 function and diminished HIV-1–specific NK cell ADCC has remained unclear. Thus, to fill this knowledge gap, we tested whether ADAM17 perturbation can restore HIV-1–specific NK cell ADCC. We demonstrated that inhibition of ADAM17 can restore HIV-1–specific ADCC by recovering surface CD16 expression. Furthermore, we observed that PLWH NK cells experience impaired ADCC associated with ADAM17 elevation. Altogether, we found that ADAM17 blockade can restore impaired NK cell ADCC against HIV-1, and this approach could be applied to further advance NK cell-based immunotherapeutics toward an HIV-1 cure. These findings establish ADAM17 as a tractable checkpoint to enhance Fc‑effector responses and support the integration of ADAM17 targeting into NK‑cell–based HIV cure strategies.

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells exert critical innate effector functions to limit human immunodeficiency virus-1 (HIV-1) and simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) transmission and pathogenesis [1–6]. Specifically, robust CD16 + NK cell antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) responses are associated with more successful protection following HIV-1 vaccination [1, 7–10]. Despite their significance, dysregulated NK cell responses, including perturbed cytotoxicity and ADCC, are frequently observed in HIV-1 infection [11–21]. Recently, our group demonstrated that in PLWH, NK cells show reduced surface CD16 expression and downstream signaling activation, a critical pathway for ADCC, via ADAM17 [22].

Notwithstanding this evidence of NK cell dysfunction in PLWH, the mechanistic link between ADAM17 activity and reduced ADCC remains unclear. Our recent efforts have revealed that CD16 cell-surface expression and signaling activation in NK cells from PLWH can be restored by disrupting ADAM17 function [22]. This indicates that dysfunctional NK cell activities could be reversed by targeting the ADAM17 pathway. However, whether ADAM17 activity causally limits HIV‑1–specific NK cell ADCC, and whether its perturbation can restore this function, has not been directly demonstrated. In this study, we show for the first time that ADAM17 blockade restores HIV‑1–specific NK cell ADCC, linking ADAM17‑dependent CD16 loss to functional impairment and providing a readily actionable axis for NK‑cell–based immunotherapy.

Results

Antigen-agnostic and HIV-1-specific NK cell ADCC was restored by ADAM17 blockade

To investigate the recovery of pathogen-agnostic NK cell ADCC by perturbing ADAM17 function [23], we first used CEM.NKR.CCR5 cell lines, which endogenously express abundant levels of CD4. First, we treated healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) with IL-18 to experimentally induce ADAM17-mediated CD16 downregulation on NK cells and inhibited ADAM17 using either a blocking antibody or the ADAM17 inhibitor TAPI-1 [22, 24, 25]. To maximize the viability of cultured NK cells, we also supplemented low levels of IL-15 [22]. Consistent with prior studies, IL-18 treatment significantly diminished CD16 expression on NK cells, whereas incubation with an anti-ADAM17 blocking antibody or TAPI-1 restored CD16 expression (Fig. 1A).

Next, we co-cultured NK cells with anti-CD4 antibody–coated CEM.NKR.CCR5 cells to test whether pathogen-agnostic NK cell ADCC is modulated by IL-18 and ADAM17 inhibition. Consistent with surface CD16 alterations, IL-18 treatment of NK cells significantly reduced pathogen-agnostic NK cell ADCC. Overall, inhibition of ADAM17 function restored ADCC potency (Fig. 1B) and the level of CD16 expression positively correlated with ADCC potency across donors (Fig. 1C; r = 0.49, p < 0.005), underscoring CD16 restoration as a key mediator. Interestingly, we also observed a group of individuals who restored ADCC (ADAM17-inhibition responders, or “responders”) that were distinct from others who did not recover ADCC (ADAM17-inhibition non-responders, or “non-responders”). Responder/non‑responder status was defined a priori by ADCC fold‑change > 1 vs. < 1. Between these groups, IL‑18–induced CD16 downregulation tended to be more pronounced in non-responders, and recovery of CD16 expression by ADAM17 perturbation was modestly lower in non-responders (Fig. 1D, E). This further confirms that the ADCC rescue is largely dependent on the magnitude of CD16 restoration and highlights donor‑intrinsic variability that could guide prospective enrichment strategies. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that an ADAM17 blockade approach can restore NK cell ADCC by restoring surface CD16 expression.

Next, we tested whether these effects extend to bona fide HIV‑1–specific ADCC by preparing HIV-1 BaL–infected CEM.NKR.CCR5 cell lines [23] as target cells. To illustrate the recovery of HIV-1–specific NK cell ADCC by blocking ADAM17 activity, we co-cultured NK cells with HIV-1–infected CEM.NKR.CCR5 cells coated with the anti–HIV-1 envelope (env) antibody A32 [26] following incubation with IL-18 and the ADAM17 inhibitor TAPI-1. Corroborating findings from pathogen-agnostic ADCC, IL-18 treatment reduced HIV-1–specific NK cell ADCC, whereas perturbation of ADAM17 activity restored HIV-1–specific NK cell ADCC (Fig. 1F).

PLWH NK cells exhibit ADAM17-associated ADCC perturbation

To validate our findings ex vivo/in vivo, we next tested whether NK cells from PLWH also exhibit ADAM17-dependent suppression of ADCC. We first compared the levels of CD16 and ADAM17 expression on NK cells between healthy donor controls and PLWH. Consistent with prior findings [22], NK cells from PLWH diminished surface CD16 expression (Fig. 2A), and correspondingly, the levels of ADAM17 significantly increased on NK cells from PLWH(Fig. 2B). Corroborating the reduction in CD16, NK cell ADCC was significantly abrogated in PLWH (Fig. 2C). Finally, we investigated whether ADAM17 perturbation could restore PLWH NK cell ADCC. Concomitant with previous studies [22], ADAM17 inhibition rescued CD16 downregulation on PLWH NK cells (Fig. 2D). However, ADAM17 inhibition alone was not sufficient to restore diminished ADCC (Fig. 2E).

Discussion

Here, we illustrated that ADAM17 inhibition restores HIV‑1–specific NK cell ADCC by rescuing surface CD16 expression, establishing a causal link between ADAM17 activity and functional NK cell impairment in HIV‑1 infection. Our initial observations revealed that disrupting ADAM17 activity reinstated CD16 expression, in agreement with earlier studies. We also demonstrated that ADAM17 inhibition recovered pathogen-agnostic and HIV-1-specific NK cell ADCC depending on the magnitude of CD16 restoration. we also confirmed that ADAM17-associated suppression of NK cell ADCC was also observed in PLWH. While ADAM17 perturbation was able to restore CD16 expression, we did not see significant recovery of NK cell ADCC in PLWH by solely inhibiting ADAM17 activity.

Unlike our prior systems‑level work that implicated IL‑18/ADAM17 in CD16 signaling attenuation, the present study provides direct functional evidence in an HIV‑1–specific ADCC assay, thereby closing a critical mechanistic loop. Whereas NK cells from PLWH are well-documented to become dysfunctional, prior studies have mainly characterized modulation of surface receptor profiles [11–15, 17–19]. Our recent report indicated ADAM17-mediated downregulation of CD16 signaling in PLWH NK cells as a potential mechanism [22]. However, its link with perturbed NK cell ADCC remains understudied. Data from this study showed that ADAM17 inhibition can restore HIV-1–specific ADCC in PLWH NK cells, suggesting that impaired NK cell function is triggered by ADAM17-dependent CD16 reduction.

Surprisingly, despite CD16 recovery, NK ADCC suppression was not adequately rescued by ADAM17. This insufficient recovery of NK cell ADCC responses in PLWH could be attributed to CD16 polymorphisms [42, 43], the effects of immune complexes [44], HIV-1 evasion of ADCC [45], or somewhat irrecoverable functions in PLWH. To maximize the effect of ADAM17 targeting, this approach may require complementary immunotherapeutic regimens such as BiKE/TriKE, elimination of immune complexes, and targeting of more conserved HIV-1 antigens.

The data from this study will also offer novel insights into advancing HIV-1 cure immunotherapeutic regimens. Several immunotherapeutic regimens have been developed using NK cells, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-NK cells and bispecific (BiKE) and trispecific killer engagers (TriKE), specifically for cancer therapies [27–30]. Multiple cancer therapies also target ADAM17 to optimize NK cell ADCC, as NK cells reduce their surface CD16 expression during the course of immunotherapeutic treatment [35–40]. These findings parallel ADAM17‑focused NK cell optimizations in oncology (e.g., ADAM17‑resistant CD16 variants, CRISPR‑mediated ADAM17 knock‑out) and suggest immediate combinability with Fc‑dependent HIV strategies—such as bNAb‑mediated clearance and engager platforms (BiKE/TriKE) [30, 34]. Beyond CD16 restoration, ADAM17 is also known to modulate the expression of other surface receptors, including CD62L and CD25 [25, 41]. Thus, ADAM17 perturbation strategies can also ameliorate other NK cell functions, including increased tissue trafficking and altered cytokine responses.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that HIV-1–specific ADCC can be restored by blocking ADAM17 activity, and that PLWH NK cells can restore CD16 expression by perturbing ADAM17 activity. Collectively, these results can open an unexplored avenue for further advancing NK cell immunotherapeutics against HIV and other viral infections. Specifically, these data position ADAM17 as a targetable checkpoint to potentiate NK cell ADCC against HIV‑1, supporting rapid translation alongside antibody‑based and engineered‑NK cure strategies.

Methods

Human samples

HIV-1-seronegative and viremic human blood was collected under Institutional Review Board (IRB)–approved protocols by Zen-Bio Inc. (Durham, NC), Duke University Hospital, and University of Alabama at Birmingham Hospital. PBMCs were isolated via Ficoll-Hypaque–based density gradient centrifugation. Additional vials of frozen healthy donor PBMCs were purchased from Stemcell Technologies (Vancouver, British Columbia, CA). All participants signed IRB-accredited informed consent forms before this study.

Flow cytometry

PBMCs were stained with the viability dye LIVE/DEAD Near Infrared (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA) for 30 min, followed by surface receptor staining with anti-CD3-BV786 (BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ; clone SP34.2), CD14-BUV737 (BD Pharmingen, clone M5E2), CD19-BUV395 (BD Pharmingen, clone SJ25C1), CD56-BV605 (BD Pharmingen, clone NCAM16.2), HLA-DR-BUV661 (BD Pharmingen, clone G46-6), and CD16-BUV496 (BD Pharmingen, clone 3G8), NKp46-PE-Cy5 (Beckman Coulter, clone BAB281), NKG2A-PE-Vio770 (Miltenyi Biotec), NKG2D-PE-Dazzle594 (Biolegend), CD2-PerCp-Cy5.5, and anti-ADAM17-Pacific Blue (conjugated in our lab using anti-human ADAM17 antibody (R&D systems, clone MAB9304) and the Pacific Blue conjugation kit (ThermoFisher) Cells were then fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde, and data were collected on an A5 Symphony (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). FlowJo v10.9.0 (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR) was used for data analysis. A single antibody lot per analyte and daily cytometer QC (calibration beads) [46] minimized batch effects.

HIV-1 infection

CEM.NKR.CCR5 cell lines (NIH HIV Reagent Program, Rockville, MD) [23] were spinoculated with p24 90 ng of HIV-1 BaL (NIH HIV Reagent Program, Rockville, MD) per 2 × 10⁵ cells at 1,200 × g for two hours and cultured in RPMI medium (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) with 10% fetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher Scientific) (R10) for six days. One day before the killing assay, a fraction of BaL-infected cells was stained with anti–HIV core antigen–FITC (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA; clone KC57), and the percentage of HIV⁺ cells was quantified by flow cytometry. We confirmed that > 99% of CEM.NKR.CCR5 cells express CD4, and following spinoculation, we observed that 30–60% of cells became BaL⁺ the day before the experiment. To minimize the background cell death, dead cells were removed prior to antibody-coating using the Dead Cell Removal kit (Miltenyi Biotec).

Annexin V-based NK cell ADCC assay

Human PBMCs were cultured in RPMI medium (Corning Inc., Corning, NY) with 10% fetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher Scientific) (R10), with and without 1 ng/mL recombinant human IL-15 (Miltenyi Biotec, Gaithersburg, MD), 10 ng/mL recombinant human IL-18 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), 1 µg/mL anti-TACE antibody (Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA; clone D1[A12]), and 2.5 µM ADAM17 inhibitor TAPI-1 (Selleckchem) [22, 25, 47] overnight. For PLWH samples, PBMCs were incubated with 1 µg/mL ADAM17 blocking antibody and 2.5 µM TAPI-1 overnight. To confirm the modulation of CD16 by cytokine treatment and ADAM17 perturbation, PBMCs were then stained with anti-CD3-BV786, CD14-BUV737, CD20-BUV395, CD56-BV605, HLA-DR-BUV661, and CD16-BUV496. Mean Fluorescent Intensity (MFI) for CD16 on NK cells was quantified, and the fold changes from untreated to IL-18-treated, and from IL-18-treated to IL-18- and TAPI-1-treated conditions were calculated. In parallel, NK cells were isolated from PBMCs following overnight incubation using the human NK cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec).

To prepare target cells, BaL-uninfected or BaL-infected cells were stained with 50 nM CFSE (ThermoFisher Scientific) for 20 min, and cells were incubated with anti-human CD4 antibody (Nonhuman Primate Reagent Resource; clone CD4R1) [48] or anti–HIV-1 env antibody A32 (kindly donated by the laboratory of Dr. Guido Ferrari at Duke University) [26] at room temperature for 15 min. NK cells were then co-cultured at an Effector: Target ratio of 10:1 for six hours. Following co-culture, the cells were stained with anti–Annexin V–APC (BD Biosciences), and the percentage of Annexin V⁺CFSE⁺ cells was quantified by flow cytometry. Percent ADCC was calculated using the following formula: %ADCC = (% Annexin V⁺ cells with antibody) − (% Annexin V⁺ cells without antibody). %ADCC < 0 is considered as 0% ADCC. The ADCC fold change was calculated as follows: ADCC fold change = (% ADCC IL-18 and TAPI-1-treated)/(% ADCC IL-18-treated). People with an ADCC fold change > 1 are defined as responders, and those with an ADCC fold change < 1 are subgrouped as non-responders. When (% ADCC IL-18-treated) is 0 and (% ADCC IL-18 and TAPI-1-treated) > 0, the individual is categorized as responders.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was evaluated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). P-values smaller than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Mann-Whitney U tests were applied to assess statistically significant differences for paired and unpaired analyses, respectively. For correlation analyses, Spearman correlation coefficient was calculated.
