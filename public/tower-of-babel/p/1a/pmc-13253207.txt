Effect of different feed particle size on digestive tract morphology, digesta viscosity, nutrient retention, and blood biochemical parameters in slow-growing broiler chickens
Authors: Jakub Novotný, Lukáš Čumplík, Lucie Horáková, Michal Řiháček, Dana Zálešáková, Ondřej Šťastník, Eva Mrkvicová, Vojtěch Kumbár, Leoš Pavlata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Digestive traits respond more to feed particle size than growth performance.•Coarse diets enhanced gizzard development in slow-growing broilers.•Intestinal morphometric responses were segment-specific and moderate.•Nutrient utilization differs by particle size and nutrient type.•Coarse particles improved nitrogen and ash retention, fine particles ether extract retention.Keywords: Particle size, Poultry nutrition, Morphometry, Ileal viscosity, Health monitoring, Nutrient retention, GMD

Introduction

The proportion of coarse particles in the diet shows a positive effect on the proper functioning of the gastrointestinal tract and its health (Dhakal et al., 2025; Gabriel et al., 2008). Supporting the development of a gizzard is one of the interests of nutrition, which improves digestion and the subsequent utilization of nutrients (Svihus, 2011; Yousefian Astaneh et al., 2023). The feed particle size also affects the viscosity of the digesta. Fine-ground diets increased digesta viscosity compared with medium- and coarse-ground diets (Dorado-Montenegro et al., 2024; Tejeda & Kim, 2021), which was associated with reduced feed intake (Yasar, 2003).

Feed particle size has also been found to affect the gizzard and other segments of the digestive tract, but the results are contradictory (Novotny et al., 2023). Amerah et al. (2007b) noted a decrease in the relative length of all parts of the digestive tract as wheat particle size increased. A decreased intestinal weight or length may result in improved feed efficiency due to reduced maintenance costs (Rueda et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2015). Despite extensive research on feed particle size, its isolated effects on intestinal morphology and systemic health indicators under prolonged feeding conditions remain insufficiently characterized. As far as we know, relatively few studies have been reported on the effect of feed particle size on intestinal morphology in broiler chickens. Zang et al. (2009) found out an increase in villus height and villus height to crypt depth ratio in the small intestinal mucosa of broilers fed pelleted diets compared with those given mash diets, with no effect on crypt depth. Higher villus height may increase the area of absorption and subsequently the transport of nutrients at the villus surface. According to the conclusions of Novotny et al. (2023), the use of coarse particles in non-pelleted diet for broiler chickens had a positive effect on the gizzard weight as well as on the villus height and crypt depth of the small intestine, which could have increased the surface area available for nutrient digestion.

The effects of feed particle size remain an actively investigated topic, with recent studies focusing on its interactions with nutrient composition, feed additives, and gut health. For example, Wolfrum et al. (2025) pay attention to particle size in connection with Ca concentration and phytase supplementation on InsP6 degradation in broilers. Veluri et al. (2024) addressed the topic of the interaction of wheat bran size and Symbiotic supplementation on gut health and performance parameters of broiler chickens. The effects on broiler chickens of including variously ground and whole grain corn in diets with a low protein content were investigated by Naeen et al. (2024); Setiana et al. (2025) used the particle size of palm kenrel meal for its better utilization.

Monitoring the health status of poultry is essential to ensure animal welfare, productivity, and the early detection of potential health issues within the flock. Necropsy, histomorphology, monitoring of blood biochemical parameters, as well as production performance parameters are some of the key methods used to assess the health status of poultry.

Monitoring blood biochemical parameters is important aspect of poultry health control. Blood samples are collected from birds and analyzed for various parameters such as albumin, globulin, total proteins, glucose levels, liver enzymes, lipid markers, and kidney function indicators. These parameters provide valuable information about metabolic changes, organ function, and the overall health condition of the birds (Bozakova et al., 2015; Dunets & Slivinska, 2020; Hussein et al., 2018; Rezende et al., 2017).

Gut health is one of the key indicators of an organism’s overall health. Dysfunction of the intestinal epithelial barrier results in increased intestinal permeability, referred to leaky gut syndrome (Hollander, 1999), which is associated with intestinal disorders such as inflammatory bowel diseases (Dal Pont et al., 2021). An imbalance in the qualitative and quantitative composition of the intestinal microbiota—partially caused by poor feed structure—contributes to barrier dysfunction and increased intestinal permeability (Aleman et al., 2023). The intestine also plays a role in maintaining immunity and repairing the gastrointestinal mucosa (Schoultz & Keita, 2020). Intestinal hyperpermeability resulting from leaky gut syndrome causes changes in tight junctions, allowing toxic substances to enter the bloodstream and leading to dysfunction in various organs and systems. Stimulation of the immune system, fiber degradation, and enhanced function and motility of the gastrointestinal tract facilitate nutrient absorption and pathogen inhibition (Luca et al., 2019; Mirsepasi-Lauridsen et al., 2018; Schoultz & Keita, 2020).

The aim of this study was to evaluate how different feed particle sizes in chicken diets affect nutrient retention, digestive tract development, performance, and the overall physiological health status of the animals. The effects of particle size were assessed not only at the level of nutrient utilization and growth performance, but also through targeted monitoring of poultry health. Health status and metabolic responses were evaluated using selected key indicators, including biochemical blood parameters related to liver function (e.g. AST, ALP, total protein, albumin), nitrogen metabolism and kidney function (uric acid, creatinine), and energy metabolism (glucose, triglycerides, cholesterol), as well as morphological characteristics of the digestive tract (gizzard weight, intestinal length, villus height, and a comprehensive histomorphological description of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum).

A slow-growing broiler hybrid was used as the experimental model due to its longer fattening period, lower metabolic intensity, and more gradual organ and gut development compared with fast-growing broilers, which allows diet-related physiological effects to manifest more clearly (Akyüz et al., 2022). At the same time, studies comparing slow- and fast-growing broiler hybrids Šťastník et al. (2022) have demonstrated that slow-growing birds exhibit higher relative weights of visceral and digestive organs, including the gizzard and liver, when expressed as a proportion of body weight gain, which is directly relevant for studies focusing on feed particle size and physical feed structure (Akyüz & Onbaşılar, 2023).

Moreover, this genotype better reflects conditions in parent flocks, where birds are kept for extended periods and are commonly fed unstructured diets similar to those applied in the present study. This integrated approach enables a more precise identification of health- and performance-related parameters that are most informative for monitoring broiler chickens fed diets differing in particle size.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

The animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care Committee of Mendel University in Brno and by the Ministry of Education, Youth and Sports (MSMT-17,862/2022–4) of the Czech Republic.

Animals and experimental conditions

Slow-growing broiler chickens of Hubbard JA57 were chosen as model animals due to the longer period of fattening and growth of these chickens. The use of the effect of a longer fattening period means, in particular, a longer-lasting influence of individual diets on model animals, when possible changes or differences between groups can be better manifested. In 5 replicates, 120 one-day-old male broiler chickens were randomly divided into 3 different experimental groups (in total 40 chickens per feeding group) with 8 individuals per cage. The microclimatic conditions and the light regime were controlled according to the Technological procedure for Husbandry Guidelines Premium Chickens. According to the guideline, broilers were fed with a starter diet until 21th day of age. Chickens were fed with experimental grower diets from the 21th day until the 35th day of age and the last period with the finisher diets lasted till 49th day of age. The chickens were fed ad libitum, with the daily addition of a new dose. The feed intake of each group was recorded on daily basis. The feed residues were used twice a week to evaluate their feed particle size (after 24 h of feeding). The body weight was regularly noticed. Two times in the third period of the experiment (the finisher diet phase), the excrements from 24 h interval from each cage were collected and frozen (−20 °C) till other analyses (wet sieve analysis and chromium oxide determination).

Experimental diets

The overall design of diets creation was set as in our previous experiment (Novotny et al., 2023) on fast-growing chickens for better replicability and comparability. In the experiment, three non-pelleted diets with different particle size were used. The first experimental Coarse group – CG was fed with the most of coarse particle size, the Medium group (MG) had a lower amount of coarse parts and the Fine group (FG) had fine feed particle size in the diet. A hammer mill (3 mm and 6 mm sieve) and roller mill for grinding wheat and corn were used to ensure the different feed particle size. In the starter diets, the coarseness of the particles was taken into account according to the size of the chicks so that they were able to ingest all particles from the 1st day of age without difficulty. The ingredients in these diets were used in the same amount to ensure the isoenergic and isonitrogenous diets. The indigestible indicator chromium trioxide (Cr2O3) was added to the diets (grower and finisher) for subsequent determination of nutrient retention. The compounds and chemical composition of the used diets are shown in Table 1, Table 2. The content of the analyzed nutrients was determined in accordance with Commission Regulation (EC) No. 152/2009.

The starter diet consisted of particles of different sizes according to the groups, however, the size of the particles was adapted to the age and especially the size of the chickens. The other two diets, grower and finisher, have already been designed so that the size of their particles corresponds to each other, i.e. so that the proportional representation of different size particles in the grower of the given group corresponds to the size of the particles in the finisher of the same group.

Sieve analysis

The particle size of the diets and the feed residues was evaluated by dry sieving using a separator Retch AS 200 Control. A representative sample of 100 g of each diet was passed for 10 min through the set of sieves with 3 mm, 2 mm, 1.5 mm, 1 mm and 0.3 mm mesh sizes. An amplitude was set to 1.8 mm/g. After the shaking process, the amount of particles retained on each sieve was determined by subtracting the weight of the sieve and the retained feed from the blank weight of the sieve. The sieves were cleaned between individual samples to prevent cross-contamination. Samples from each diet were analyzed 20 times to verify measurement repeatability and to ensure reliable output values. As the diets were homogenized, subsamples were not re-homogenized to avoid secondary particle separation. The GMD (geometric mean diameter) and GSD (geometric standard deviation) were calculated (Naeem et al., 2024a). Collected feed residues were analyzed using the same procedure; however, they were not treated as representative samples but were analyzed as absolute quantities to avoid distortion of results due to sample subdivision. The weight of feed residue samples ranged from 80 to approximately 400 g. Feed residues were collected eight times per replicate (twice a week from 4th week; n = 40). Particle size preference was evaluated by comparing the proportional distribution (%) of particles retained on individual sieves in the diets versus the feed residues. End of the experiment and sample collections

At the end of the trial, broilers were weighted, slaughtered and eviscerated. In selected chickens, individual digestive tract sections were weighed and measured. Histomorphometric measurements were performed in sections of the small intestine. In addition, the digesta was collected and the viscosity and density were measured. Furthermore, retention of nutrients was assessed. Blood was collected for evaluation of blood biochemical parameters. Last but not least, yield and performance parameters were evaluated.

Measurements of the digestive tract. From each group, 15 broilers were slaughtered (3 from each replicate). After evisceration, the entire digestive tract was divided into individual anatomical sections: crop, proventriculus, gizzard, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, ceca, and colon. After removing the contents and fat tissue, the lengths and weights of the individual segments were measured. In the case of the gizzard, additional measurements were taken using a caliper, including height, width, depth, and thickness of the main muscle. All data were normalized to the live body weight of the chickens and expressed per 1 kg of body weight. The detailed methodology for segmentation and measurement was based on the publication by Novotny et al. (2023).

Histomorphological measurements. From 10 broilers in each group, samples of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum (defined intestinal segments) measuring 1 × 1 × 1 cm were collected. The samples were fixed in buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and processed using standard histological techniques (hematoxylin-eosin staining) (Aescht et al., 2010; Alshamy et al., 2018). From all samples, villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured microscopically at 100 × magnification using Quick PHOTO CAMERA 3.2 software. For each intestinal section, five measurements of lengths of villi/depths of crypts were taken per individual; n = 50 per group and each segment. The VH:CD ratio was also calculated. Measurements of VH and CD were based on the methods used by Abdelqader and Al-Fataftah (2016); Okpe et al. (2016); Shokryazdan et al. (2017). The Villus Height to Crypt Depth (VH:CD) ratio is a comparison of VH to CD (Santos et al., 2015). The samples were also evaluated for the presence of intraepithelial lymphocytes and granulocytes. All this was supplemented with a description of the given tissues (i.e. assessment of the surface epithelium, crypt dilation, mucosal fibrous tissue, etc.). The occurrence of Coccidia sp. was quantified at 40 × magnification in 10 visual high-power fields.

Ileal digesta viscosity. In 10 animals from each group, fresh digesta content was collected from the distal part of the ileum and analyzed for dynamic viscosity according to the method of Yasar (1999), using an RST rheometer (Brookfield, USA) at a temperature of 40 °C and a constant shear rate of 50 s⁻¹. The measurement was performed in 10 replicates, with a sample volume of 1.2 mL.

Nutrient retention calculated using chromium oxide determination. Feed and excreta samples were lyophilized, homogenized, and analyzed for Cr₂O₃ content using titration after ashing and fusion with a diet of KClO₃ and Na₂CO₃. Biochemical blood analyses. Blood from 2 birds per replicate was collected into heparinized tubes and processed within 2 h (centrifugation for 10 min at 3000 rpm); plasma was then frozen at –30 °C. Standardized biochemical methods using commercial Erba Lachema kits (Czech Republic) were applied, and analyses were performed on an Ellipse automated biochemical analyzer (AMS, Italy). Enzyme activities of aspartate aminotranferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), lactate dehydrogenase (LD), and creatine kinase (CK) were measured using the Ellipse analyzer. In addition, concentrations of glucose (Glu), cholesterol (Chol), triacylglycerols (TG), total protein (TP), albumin (Alb), albumin/globulin (A/G), urea, uric acid (UA), creatinine (Creat), and bilirubin (TB) were determined. A detailed list of the kits used is provided in the publication by Zálešáková et al. (2025).

Statistical analyses

The data were processed by Microsoft Excel (USA) and TIBCO Statistica version 14.0 (USA). The experimental unit was the pen for body weight, weight gain, feed intake, and feed conversion ratio. The Shapiro-Wilk W test was used to assess normality of data distribution. The data set was well-modeled by a normal distribution. It was used a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For evaluated differences between groups was used the Sheffé´s test and P< 0.05 was a significance level determinate statistically significant difference.

Results

The results regarding feed particle size preferred by the chickens are presented first. Table 4 shows the change in the mass ratio of the representation of particles in the feed residues compared to the original diets. A shift in particle size distribution was observed in feed residues across all groups, characterized by a decrease in the proportion of coarser fractions and a simultaneous increase in particles ranging from 0.3 to 1.5 mm. In the CG group, the proportion of particles larger than 3 mm decreased by 2.06%, while the 2–3 mm fraction decreased by 4.73%. In the MG group, the decrease in these fractions was 1.05 and 4.91%, respectively. In the FG group, a statistically significant decrease was also observed in the 2–3 mm fraction, amounting to 0.49%.

At the same time, the proportion of medium-sized particles increased in all groups, particularly in the 0.3–1.0 mm fraction, which increased by 12.37% in the CG group, by 13.34% in the MG group, and by 11.7% in the FG group. An increase was also observed in the 1.0–1.5 mm fraction, specifically by 3.60% in the CG group, by 2.76% in the MG group, and by 0.76% in the FG group. All of these changes were statistically significant.

The proportion of the finest fraction (<0.3 mm) did not change significantly in the CG and MG groups, whereas in the FG group it decreased by 2.11%. Nevertheless, the geometric mean diameter (GMD) in the FG group remained unchanged, which is likely related to the fact that the decrease in the finest fraction was offset by a pronounced increase in the 0.3–1.0 mm fraction, together with a slight increase in the 1.0–1.5 mm fraction. In contrast, a significant decrease in GMD was observed in the CG and MG groups, corresponding to a more pronounced reduction in the coarsest fractions in the feed residues.

Segments of the gastrointestinal tract were weighted and measured (Table 5). There were observed changes in proventriculus weight, where the highest value was noted in the CG compared with the MG and FG group compared with the MG and FG groups (P< 0.01). The similar trend was found in some gizzard characteristics including gizzard weight (P= 0.01), gizzard depth (P= 0.01), and gizzard muscle height (P< 0.01), with the highest values generally observed in the CG group. In addition, ileum weight was significantly lower in the MG group compared with the CG group (P= 0.02), while the FG group showed intermediate values. This reduction may reflect an adaptive response to medium particle size, potentially associated with altered digesta retention time and reduced mechanical demands on the distal intestinal tract. It may also be related to the smaller depth of the villi (Table 6). However, the biological relevance of this isolated effect remains to be further clarified.

Table 6 compares the average values of intestinal villus lengths, crypt depths and their ratios. The length of the villi differed in the duodenum, such that the CG had longer villi than the FG. In contrast, the depth of the crypts differed significantly in the sections of the jejunum and ileum. The crypts of the CG were deeper than those of the MG. The ratios between the lengths of the villi and the depths of the crypts differed in the part of the ileum, such that a lower average value was found in the CG and a higher one in the MG.

Histomorphological evaluation of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum across all three groups revealed no surface epithelial damage. Intraepithelial lymphocytes were present in numbers up to 10 per high-power field (HPF), and lacteals were of normal diameter (<25% of villous width). The villous lamina propria contained lymphocytes and plasma cells occupying up to 25% of the HPF area. Crypt dilation was minimal, affecting up to 2% of crypts, and mucosal fibrous tissue remained within normal limits (up to 2 fibrocytes between crypts). Coccidia sp. were absent in all samples (0/10 HPFs). The only notable differences were in granulocyte counts: in the duodenum and jejunum, granulocytes reached up to 10/HPF in the CG and FG (with one FG sample exceeding 31/HPF), while the MG showed counts up to 30/HPF. In the ileum, granulocytes reached up to 30/HPF in the MG and FG, but only up to 20/HPF in the CG.

Discussion

Many studies indicate that poultry prefer coarser feed particles over finer ones (Abdollahi et al., 2012; Amerah et al., 2007a; Nir et al., 1994b) and the present results are consistent with this pattern. In our experiment, birds preferentially consumed particles larger than 1.5 mm in the MG and FG diets and larger than 2 mm in the CG diet. This selective intake was reflected in the particle-size distribution of the feed residues, in which the proportion of coarser fractions decreased, whereas the proportion of intermediate fractions increased. Because the diets were homogenized before feeding and the feed residues were collected and analyzed as whole leftovers rather than subdivided subsamples, the observed differences are likely to represent true selective picking during feeding rather than artefacts of sample handling. The overall evaluation based on GMD supports this interpretation. In the CG and MG groups, selective consumption of coarse particles was sufficient to reduce the GMD of the residues, whereas in the FG group the GMD remained unchanged. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the more homogeneous structure of the fine diet, in which the decrease in the finest fraction was offset by an increase in particles of intermediate size. In this respect, the present study refines previous findings by showing that the response to feed structure is not determined solely by mean GMD, but also by the internal distribution of particle-size fractions within the diet. Ege et al. (2019) used 2 types of diets in their experiment with GMD 1.096 vs. 0.707 mm. Amerah et al. (2007a) used in their experiment 2 different sizes of wheat (hammer mill screen size 3 or 7 mm) with particles with GMD values of 1.164 vs. 0.839 mm. Providing poultry with particles according to their preferences can also increase welfare quality to some extent, as the poultry will be occupied for a longer period of time with their natural feeding behaviour of searching for larger particles.

Compared with recent studies addressing particle size primarily in relation to gizzard development, nutrient digestibility, or interactions with dietary supplements (Ege et al., 2019; Naderinejad et al., 2016; Wolfrum et al., 2025), the present study provides a broader evaluation by combining particle preference, nutrient retention, digestive tract morphology, performance, and biochemical health indicators in a slow-growing broiler genotype. This approach is relevant because it suggests that responses to feed particle size are not uniform across all measured traits. Rather, the biological effect appears to depend on both the GMD range tested and the specific digestive or physiological endpoint evaluated. Under the present conditions, the most consistent responses were observed for particle preference, gizzard development, and selected measures of nutrient retention, whereas the effects on intestinal morphology, blood biochemical profile, and overall performance were more limited.

The development of the digestive tract, particularly that of the gizzard, can be influenced by feed particle size (Gabriel et al., 2008; Svihus, 2011). The digestive system, especially the gizzard, rapidly adapts in response to changes in dietary structure, which is important for subsequent digestion and associated metabolic processes. Previous studies have shown that inclusion of structural components such as oat hulls, wood shavings, sugarcane bagasse, or whole and coarse grain particles stimulates gizzard development (Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2013; Kheravii et al., 2017; Svihus, 2011). Nir et al. (1994a,b) also demonstrated a beneficial effect of coarse particles on gizzard development, reporting increases in gizzard weight of 26% and 41%. Our results support this general interpretation, although the magnitude of the response was more moderate. Gizzard weight was 11.5% higher in MG and 12.1% higher in CG than in FG. Similarly, Ege et al. (2019) found that increasing dietary GMD increased gizzard weight in laying hens, whereas Biggs and Parsons (2009) observed higher gizzard weight in broilers offered wheat grains from an early age. Wolfrum et al. (2025) likewise reported increased gizzard weight in broilers fed coarse particles and additionally indicated a reduced gastric pH, which may be beneficial by suppressing pathogenic bacteria and enhancing nutrient availability (Hamid et al., 2018). At the same time, the literature is not entirely consistent. Some studies have found no differences in gizzard development in response to variation in feed particle size (Frikha et al., 2011; Koçer et al., 2016), and similarly inconsistent results have been reported for other sections of the gastrointestinal tract. These discrepancies may reflect differences in cereal source, diet composition, feed form (mash vs. pelleted), genotype, bird age, duration of exposure, and the absolute GMD range applied. This is an important consideration when interpreting the present findings. Our study was conducted in slow-growing broilers fed mash diets, and the results should therefore be interpreted within this specific context rather than generalized to all poultry species, diet forms, or production stages. In our trial, ileum weight was higher in CG than in FG, whereas no clear differences were observed in the remaining segments of the digestive tract. This agrees with Naderinejad et al. (2016), who reported that maize particle size had little effect on proportional weight or length of gastrointestinal sections apart from the gizzard. Taken together, these observations suggest that the response of the digestive tract to feed particle size may be segment-specific, with the gizzard showing the most consistent adaptive response.

The intestinal mucosal surface is another trait potentially influenced by feed structure. Villus height, crypt depth, and their ratio are important indicators of intestinal function because they are associated with absorptive capacity and epithelial turnover (Wang et al., 2008). In the present study, villus height in the duodenum was higher in CG than in FG. In the jejunum and ileum, crypt depth was higher in CG than in MG, whereas the villus height to crypt depth ratio was higher in the jejunum of MG than in FG and in the ileum of MG than in CG. These findings indicate that the response of intestinal morphology to feed particle size was not uniform along the intestinal tract. Rather, the effects were moderate and segment-specific. Previous studies have shown that intestinal villi can adapt morphologically to characteristics of feed macro- and microstructure (Amerah et al., 2007b; Qaisrani et al., 2014), but relatively few studies have examined these responses specifically in relation to feed particle size. Most published work has compared mash and pelleted diets rather than differently ground mash diets (Amerah et al., 2007b; Naderinejad et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2009). Ege et al. (2019) reported higher and wider villi and a higher villus height to crypt depth ratio in laying hens fed pelleted diets, whereas other studies have shown more limited responses. The present results therefore add to current knowledge by indicating that, under mash-feeding conditions, particle-size effects on intestinal morphology may occur, but they are likely to be localized and less pronounced than the effects observed for the gizzard.

The present study also showed differences in nutrient retention. Retention of nitrogen and starch was higher in birds fed diets containing coarser particles, which may be associated with improved mechanical digestion in the gizzard and, possibly, with changes in the absorptive characteristics of the small intestine. In contrast, ether extract retention was higher in birds fed the finely ground mash diet. These results indicate that feed particle size does not affect all nutrients in the same way and that the direction of the response may depend on the nutrient considered. Pacheco (2013) reported higher ileal digestibility of crude protein and fat in three-week-old chickens fed coarsely ground soybean meal and maize, and Parsons et al. (2006) also found that increasing maize particle size affected nitrogen retention. Wolfrum et al. (2025) reported an approximately 2% increase in amino acid digestibility when broilers were fed coarse maize particles rather than finely ground maize. Several other studies have indicated beneficial effects of coarser particles on starch digestion and energy utilization (Amerah et al., 2008; Qaisrani et al., 2015; Svihus & Hetland, 2001; Svihus et al., 2004). Naeem et al. (2024b) likewise used whole-grain maize to improve the digestibility of a low-protein diet. In contrast, Mtei et al. (2019) found improved apparent ileal digestibility of fat, starch, and nitrogen in laying hens but not in broilers. The present results therefore support the view that nutrient utilization responses to particle size are context-dependent and should be interpreted with regard to diet composition, feed structure, and bird type.

Digesta viscosity is another important indicator in the evaluation of feed effects on digestion, as elevated viscosity may impair nutrient utilization and increase the risk of undesirable microbial colonization. In our trial, feed particle size did not affect digesta viscosity. This contrasts with Amerah et al. (2007b), who suggested that finer grinding and more intensive processing of feed during pelleting may increase digesta viscosity and thereby increase the risk of intestinal colonization by pathogenic bacteria (Abdollahi et al., 2013; Jiménez-Moreno et al., 2013). Yasar (2003) likewise reported a marked increase in intestinal viscosity in broilers fed fine diets, together with reduced feed intake. However, that experiment was based mainly on wheat, which contains considerably higher concentrations of non-starch polysaccharides, particularly arabinoxylans and β-glucans, than maize. This likely explains at least part of the discrepancy. Under the present dietary conditions, digesta viscosity was not a sensitive response variable for particle size, although nutrient retention still differed among treatments. This suggests that viscosity should be interpreted together with diet composition and not as a universal indicator of particle-size effects.

Interpretation of blood biochemical parameters as indicators of health and nutritional status remains challenging in poultry because reference intervals are limited and published values are often inconsistent (Zálešáková et al., 2025). In the present study, no significant differences were detected among treatments for any of the analyzed biochemical variables. Under the conditions of this experiment, these results suggest that the applied differences in feed particle size did not induce measurable systemic metabolic disturbance. At the same time, the absence of significant effects should not be interpreted as evidence that blood-based indicators are uninformative in poultry nutrition studies. Rather, it indicates that the biochemical profile used here may not have been sufficiently sensitive to detect subtle physiological responses to feed structure alone. For this reason, future studies may consider expanding the analytical panel, particularly in experiments involving stronger dietary challenges or treatments expected to affect inflammatory status. Inclusion of hematological variables, especially leukocyte-related indices, or protein fraction analysis could be useful in such contexts. These possibilities should, however, be regarded as a perspective for future research rather than as conclusions arising directly from the present data. Similarly, possible interactions between feed particle size and dietary supplements remain hypothetical in the context of the present study. Although some published data suggest that additives such as multienzymes or specific amino acids may alter serum biochemical traits (e.g. Kareen et al., 2024; Rezaeipour & Gazani, 2014), our experiment does not provide evidence that combining coarse particles with such additives improves the biochemical blood profile. This should therefore be considered a potential direction for future work rather than an inference from the current results.

Performance traits such as feed intake, feed conversion, final body weight, and carcass yield are key zootechnical indicators and also reflect the overall functional status of the birds. They should therefore be considered an integral part of evaluating dietary particle size. Earlier studies produced inconsistent results. Some reported that coarser particles increased feed intake but reduced feeding efficiency and growth (Davis et al., 1951), whereas later studies generally supported beneficial effects of medium and coarse grinding in mash diets (Hamilton & Proudfoot, 1995, 1995; Nir et al., 1994a; Reece et al., 1986). Reece et al. (1985) found improved feed conversion and weight gain in broilers fed coarser maize diets, and Hamilton and Proudfoot (1995) also reported improved growth in birds fed diets containing coarse or very coarse maize particles. Abd El-Wahab et al. (2020) likewise observed higher weight gain in chickens fed coarse diets. Nir et al. (1995) reported higher body weight and improved feed conversion in broilers fed coarse wheat- and sorghum-based diets, while Amerah et al. (2007a) showed that broilers fed finely ground wheat had reduced body weight gain and lower feed intake than birds fed diets with a higher proportion of medium and coarse particles.

In the present experiment, body weight was not affected by dietary treatment. Nir and Pitichi (2001) recommended that broiler diets should contain predominantly particles in the range of 600–900 µm, which is broadly consistent with this interpretation. No differences were detected among treatments in feed intake, feed conversion ratio, carcass yield, or the yield of major meat parts. These findings are in agreement with several studies reporting no effect of feed particle size on performance variables (Galobart & Moran, 2005; Lv et al., 2015; Salari et al., 2006). Overall, the present data suggest that, under the tested conditions, particle size influenced selected digestive and nutrient-utilization traits more clearly than final production performance.

The present results are partly consistent with those reported in our previous study examining the effect of feed particle size on broiler health (Novotny et al., 2023), in which coarser diets promoted gizzard development and improved selected intestinal morphometric characteristics. In line with these findings, the broilers in our study fed coarser diets showed higher gizzard weight and differences in selected intestinal traits, including villus height and crypt depth. Given the broadly comparable GMD ranges used in both experiments, these observations support the view that coarser feed structure may positively influence digestive tract development. However, the agreement between studies was not complete, particularly with respect to blood biochemical variables. Unlike the above-mentioned study, which reported alterations in selected biochemical indicators in birds receiving the fine diet, we did not detect any significant differences among groups in the measured biochemical parameters. This discrepancy may indicate that blood biochemical responses to feed particle size are less consistent than morphological adaptations of the digestive tract and may be influenced more strongly by the overall dietary composition and specific experimental conditions, or possibly reflect the faster metabolism of fast-growing broiler chickens.

Taken together, the present study refines current understanding of feed particle-size effects by showing that responses in slow-growing broilers are not uniform across all traits, but depend on both the GMD range applied and the biological endpoint evaluated. The clearest effects were observed for particle preference, gizzard development, and selected nutrient-retention parameters, whereas intestinal morphology, blood biochemical profile, and overall production performance were less responsive under the present experimental conditions.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrated that broiler chickens preferentially consumed coarser feed particles, as indicated by the lower geometric mean diameter (GMD) of particles in feed residues relative to the original diets. This preference was associated with beneficial effects on digestive tract morphology, particularly gizzard development, and with changes in selected intestinal morphometric traits linked to nutrient absorption and utilization. Coarser dietary structure also improved the retention of selected nutrients, especially crude protein and ash, whereas finer particles were associated with higher ether extract retention. Despite these differences in nutrient utilization, performance traits were not improved, but neither were they adversely affected. Likewise, no significant changes were detected in the measured blood biochemical variables. Taken together, the results suggest that digestive tract morphometry and nutrient retention coefficients are the most informative indicators for evaluating broiler responses to dietary particle structure. Further studies are needed to confirm these relationships across different feed compositions, broader particle-size ranges, and alternative broiler genotypes or production systems. Future work should also assess whether inclusion of additional health-related markers, particularly hematological and gut integrity parameters, may improve the evaluation of poultry health in relation to feed structure and support the development of more precise nutritional recommendations.
