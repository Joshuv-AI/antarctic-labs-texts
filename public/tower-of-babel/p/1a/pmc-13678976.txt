Gonad–soma inter-tissue communication alleviates ultraviolet-induced neurotoxicity in C. elegans
Authors: Wenjing Zhang, Liu Ding, Shanpeng Qiao, Jiyu Xu, Qiyi Yi, Binmei Liu, Po Bian, Ting Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organismal genome stability is constantly challenged by endogenous and exogenous genotoxins, and DNA damage response (DDR) is a classical cell-autonomous mechanism for counteracting such damage. It remains unclear how somatic cells with DDR deficiencies maintain genomic integrity under stress. This study investigated whether inter-tissue communication, particularly from the reproductive system, coordinates DNA damage repair in repair-deficient cells. Using Caenorhabditis elegans, in which certain upstream DDR signaling components are transcriptionally repressed in somatic cells, we examined UV-induced neurotoxicity through behavioral assays (head thrashes, body bends, foraging, and salt chemotaxis). These deficits were associated with preferential impairment of dopaminergic, glutamatergic, and serotonergic reporter readouts, along with heterogeneous transcriptional remodeling of neurotransmitter pathway and pan-neuronal synaptic genes. Deficiencies in upstream DDR signaling (ATM/ATR), whether systemic or germline-specific, significantly exacerbated UV-induced neurobehavioral and transcriptional deficits. In contrast, germline-specific loss of nucleotide excision repair (NER) machinery had no significant effect, highlighting a unique inter-tissue signaling role for upstream DDR. Furthermore, CPR-4 was identified as a key mediator of this inter gonad–soma signaling. Germline DDR activation regulated cpr-4 expression, and loss of CPR-4 abolished UV-induced transcriptional activation of the somatic NER pathway and heightened behavioral sensitivity. Our study revealed a novel germline DDR to somatic tissue signaling axis essential for organismal stress resilience and proposed a new regulatory model centered on gonad–soma signaling.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03095-5.

Introduction

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation, a form of high-energy, short-wavelength electromagnetic radiation, poses significant exposure risks in occupational, clinical (e.g., phototherapy), and environmental contexts, leading to various forms of radiation injury [1]. Due to its short penetration depth, UV-induced damage to the skin and ocular lens is well-documented; however growing evidence also implicates UV in causing nervous system dysfunction and degeneration, although the precise mechanisms remain unclear [2–4]. As a potent genotoxic agent, UV radiation induces covalent crosslinks between adjacent pyrimidine bases, forming cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs) and pyrimidine (6 − 4) pyrimidone photoproducts (6-4PPs). These lesions distort the DNA double helix, stall transcription and replication, and trigger the DDR, which can lead to mutagenesis and tissue-level toxicity [5–9].

To preserve genome stability, organisms rely on a sophisticated DDR network that detects lesions, transduces signals, and coordinates repair [10–12]. This network is centrally orchestrated by the kinases ATR and ATM, which function as primary signal integrators. ATR is typically activated by replication stress and single-stranded DNA while ATM responds to double-strand breaks [13, 14]. Beyond imposing cell-cycle checkpoints, a major function of these kinases is to build a broad phosphorylation network that routes the initial damage-sensing signal into the appropriate repair pathways, such as NER for UV-induced bulky lesions such as CPDs and 6-4PPs, thereby shaping the overall repair outcome [15–17]. Systems-level phosphoproteomic studies further support this view, showing that ATM/ATR substrates span multiple repair and chromatin modules, confirming their role as central coordinators of the DDR [18]. Given the critical role, chronic DNA damage and DDR dysregulation are recognized as common features of neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [19]. While the DDR has been extensively characterized, particularly in proliferating cells, it remains poorly understood how DNA damage is managed in terminally differentiated, non-dividing cells (e.g., mature neurons) when the integrity of the DDR pathway itself is compromised [20, 21].

The classical view of the DDR centers on its cell-autonomous functions, where damaged cells autonomously coordinate DNA repair and cell-cycle checkpoints, however, within multicellular organisms, the DDR also operates as a systemic signaling network that mediates communications across tissues [22, 23]. Beyond immediate repair, DDR activation can trigger the release of secreted factors and immune-like signals that reshape gene expression and stress phenotypes in distant, unaffected cells, a phenomenon well-characterized in the radiation-induced bystander effect (RIBE) [24–27]. This systemic signaling establishes a distinct layer of organism-wide adaptation. In C. elegans, for example, DNA damage in germ cells enhances whole-body tolerance to heat and oxidative stress and influences somatic lifespan, demonstrating that germline status can remotely modulate somatic resilience through long-range signals [24]. Furthermore, inter-tissue regulation of DDR has been documented, whereby DDR activation in one tissue influences DNA repair outcomes in another, reinforcing the role of inter-tissue communication in shaping organismal damage responses [26]. These findings raise a key possibility that even cells with limited intrinsic DDR capacity, such as mature neurons, may be functionally supported or modulated through inter-tissue signaling with repair-proficient cells.

To address this gap, we employed C. elegans as a model. In this system, post-mitotic somatic cells, particularly neurons, exhibit transcriptional repression of the key DDR kinases ATM and ATR, preventing detectable downstream DDR signaling while retaining DNA repair capacity [28]. Using an in vivo whole-body UV irradiation model, we quantified neurotoxicity through behavioral endpoints (head thrashes, body bends, foraging, and salt chemotaxis). Our analysis revealed that UV induces severe neurotoxicity under conditions of DNA checkpoint deficiency. This effect was markedly exacerbated not only by germline ablation but also by germline-specific perturbation of ATM/ATR-related genes (e.g. hus-1, atm-1). Further investigation identified the secreted cysteine protease-like factor CPR-4 as a critical mediator of germline-to-nervous-system communication. UV-triggered, ATM/ATR-dependent germline DDR drives cpr-4 expression and secretion, supporting nervous system homeostasis and behavioral stability through inter-tissue DDR signaling. Our findings underscore the essential role of DDR-dependent cross-talk between the reproductive system and the nervous system in modulating the response of DNA repair-compromised neurons to genotoxic stress.

Materials and methods

C. elegans maintenance and growth

All C. elegans strains were maintained on nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with E. coli OP50 at 20 °C, unless otherwise stated. Strains used in this study included: N2 (wild type); WS2277: hus-1(op241); VC381: atm-1(gk186); VC3236: chk-2(ok3037); CB5348: mrt-2(e2663); SP506: clk-2(mn159); TJ1: cep-1(gk138); cpr-4(tm3718); DCL569: mkcSi13 II; rde-1(mkc36) V; and JK1107: glp-1(q224) (germline-deficient at 25 °C). Fluorescent reporter strains included GR1366: mgIs42 [tph-1::GFP + rol-6(su1006)] (serotonergic reporter); EG1285: lin-15B&lin-15 A(n765), oxIs12 [unc-47p::GFP + lin-15(+)] X (GABAergic reporter); DA1240: lin-15B&lin-15 A(n765), adIs1240 [eat-4::sGFP + lin-15(+)] X (glutamatergic reporter); BZ555: egIs1 [dat-1p::GFP] IV (dopaminergic reporter); and LX929: vsIs48 [unc-17::GFP] (cholinergic reporter).

Worm synchronization

Synchronized populations were obtained by hypochlorite bleaching. Briefly, gravid adults grown on NGM/OP50 plates (20 °C, 3–4 d) were washed off with M9 buffer and pelleted by gravity settling. After three washes with M9 (until the supernatant was clear), the pellet was resuspended in 0.75 mL M9 and mixed with 200 µL sodium hypochlorite solution and 100 µL 5 M NaOH. Tubes were inverted until adult carcasses were dissolved (monitored by microscopy). Samples were centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 1 min, the supernatant was removed, and eggs were washed with 1 mL M9 for 2–3 cycles. Eggs were resuspended in 3 mL M9 and incubated at 20 °C for 12–24 h to obtain synchronized L1 larvae, which were then plated onto NGM/OP50 for subsequent experiments.

UV exposure

For UV exposure, worms were washed off plates with M9, washed three times and transferred onto sterile (unseeded) 35 mm NGM plates. Worms were evenly dispersed and exposed to a 254 nm UV-C lamp (Xuelaite, model ZW6S15W-Z211) that was pre-warmed for 30 min. UV-C irradiance was measured with a calibrated UV radiometer, and the lamp-to-sample distance was adjusted to maintain a stable irradiance of 50 µW/cm². After irradiation, worms were transferred to fresh plates and allowed to recover for the indicated time prior to downstream assays.

RNA interference (RNAi) by feeding

RNAi feeding was performed using E. coli HT115 (DE3) carrying L4440 empty vector or gene-specific RNAi constructs. RNAi plates (NGM) were supplemented with ampicillin (50 µg/mL), tetracycline (10 µg/mL), and IPTG (1 mM), and used 4–7 d after pouring. RNAi clones were streaked on LB agar with ampicillin (50 µg/mL) and tetracycline (10 µg/mL) and grown overnight. Single colonies were inoculated into LB containing the same antibiotics and cultured at 37 °C, 200 rpm for 12–14 h. Cultures were induced with IPTG (final 1 mM) for 4 h, seeded onto RNAi plates, air-dried, and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Synchronized L1 larvae were placed onto RNAi plates (P0), grown to adults, and allowed to lay eggs. F1 progeny were maintained on the same RNAi bacteria for all assays. RNAi targets included: L4440 (vector control), atl-1, hus-1, rad-1, mrt-2, mre-11, rad-50, csa-1, csb-1, xpa-1, xpf-1, and xpg-1. Germline-specific RNAi was conducted in DCL569, a germline-restricted RNAi strain, under the same feeding scheme.

Head thrash analysis

After UV exposure, worms were allowed to recover briefly at 20 °C before testing. A single worm was transferred using an eyelash pick to a sterile 35 mm NGM plate and allowed to acclimate for 60 s. Head thrashes were counted for 30 s. One head thrash was defined as a complete side-to-side swing of the head. For each condition 20 worms were scored per biological replicate, with three independent biological replicates.

Body bend analysis

After UV exposure (300 J/m²), worms were allowed to recover at 20 °C for 4 h. A single worm was transferred to a sterile 35 mm NGM plate, acclimated for 60 s, and body bends were counted for 30 s interval. One body bend was defined as a distinct lateral flexion that produced an arc- or S-shaped posture. For each condition, 20 worms were scored per experiment, with three independent biological replicates.

Foraging analysis

Foraging plates (90 mm NGM) were marked with two 1 cm circles (A and B) separated by 4 cm center-to-center. A received 20 µL OP50 and one drop of 60 µM levamisole. L4 worms were irradiated with UV (50 J/m²) on sterile 35 mm NGM plates, then recovered for 4 h at 20 °C on NGM/OP50. Worms were washed off with M9 and rinsed three times. A total of 50–100 worms were placed in Area B. Plates were incubated for 4 h at 20 °C, and worms in Area A were counted. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Salt chemotaxis analysis

NaCl-free 90 mm NGM plates were prepared. A central 1 cm circle was marked as the start (S). Two edge circles (1 cm) along one diameter were marked as controls (C), and two edge circles (1 cm) along the perpendicular diameter were marked as NaCl (N). At each N site, a 1 cm NGM agar plug containing 100 mM NaCl was placed; C sites remained empty. Plates were stored overnight at 4 °C to establish a gradient and warmed to room temperature for 30 min before use. A 60 µM levamisole drop was added at each C and N site. L4 worms were irradiated with UV (50 J/m²), recovered for 4 h at 20 °C, washed with M9, and transferred to the S region (50–100 worms per plate). Worms in the N and C regions were counted after 4 h at 20 °C. The salt chemotaxis index (CI) was calculated as: CI = (Nₙ − Nc) / (Nₙ + Nc), where Nₙ is the total number of worms at both NaCl (N) sites and Nc is the total number of worms at both control (C) sites. Worms outside the N and C scoring regions were excluded from the calculation. Three independent biological replicates were performed.

Fluorescence imaging and quantification

Synchronized worms were grown at 20 °C until the L4 stage (~ 43 h post-plating) and randomized to control or UV (300 J/m²) groups. UV-exposed worms were allowed to recover for 4 h at 20 °C, while controls were handled identically without irradiation. Worms that were actively moving and had normal pharyngeal pumping were washed three times with M9, mounted on slides, and anesthetized with levamisole. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical settings using an inverted fluorescence microscope (AX10, ZEISS, Baden-Wurttemberg, Germany). Mean fluorescence intensity per worm was quantified in ImageJ using consistent regions of interest and background subtraction. At least 30 worms were analyzed per replicate, with three independent biological replicates.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Worms were cultured at 20 °C for 43 h. The UV group was irradiated at 300 J/m² and allowed to recover for 4 h at 20 °C, while control worms were handled identically without irradiation. Following treatment, worms were collected, washed with M9 buffer, and total RNA was extracted using a commercial kit. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA in a 20 µL reaction using the Transcriptor First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Roche, Cat. No. 04897030001). The resulting cDNA was diluted 1:10 prior to qRT-PCR. qRT-PCR was performed using FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche, REF 06924204001) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system following the manufacturer’s instructions. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Target gene expression was normalized to an internal reference gene tba-1, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Experiments were independently repeated three times, and statistical significance between two groups was assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (p < 0.05).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed by BioMarker (Beijing, China). Total RNA was extracted from C. elegans samples using a commercial kit, and RNA quality and integrity were assessed prior to library construction. Poly(A)+ mRNA was then enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and subsequently fragmented. First-strand cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription, followed by second-strand synthesis and adaptor ligation. Strand-specific libraries were generated by uracil-N-glycosylase (UNG) enzyme–mediated digestion of the second cDNA strand. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (or an equivalent strand-specific library preparation kit) and sequenced on a high-throughput platform. Raw reads were subjected to quality control to remove adaptor sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were mapped to the reference genome for expression quantification, followed by differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes were further subjected to functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. For focused neuronal gene analyses, normalized expression values were extracted for two curated gene sets: neurotransmitter-system-related genes and pan-neuronal synaptic/neuropeptide-related genes. Gene annotations and expression values are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Conditioned-medium transfer assay

To functionally test whether secreted factors contribute to CPR-4-mediated protection, we performed a conditioned-medium transfer assay. Synchronized donor animals, including wild type, glp-1(q224), and cpr-4(tm3718), were exposed to 0–300 J/m² UV and then incubated in liquid medium at 20 °C. After recovery, donor animals were removed by gravity settling or brief centrifugation, and the supernatant was collected as donor-conditioned medium. Synchronized cpr-4(tm3718) recipient animals were exposed to 0–300 J/m² UV and then transferred into the indicated donor-conditioned medium or standard medium (SM) for recovery before head-thrash analysis. Head thrashes were counted for 30 s. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple-comparison test for the indicated comparisons.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently performed in triplicate or more. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all quantitative datasets met the assumption of a normal distribution. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation, and the correlation coefficient (r) and two-tailed p value were reported. Differences between groups were evaluated using a student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA for multiple-group comparisons, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when applicable. For behavioral measures involving both genotype and UV treatment as factors, two-way ANOVA (factors: genotype, UV treatment) was performed, followed by Sidak’s multiple comparison test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

UV exposure induced neural toxicity in C. elegans

Previous studies have shown that UV irradiation can cause substantial damage in C. elegans [29]. To evaluate its impact on the nervous system, we combined behavioral assays, neurotransmitter-related readouts, and transcriptomic profiling. Because the nervous system development is largely established at the L4 stage, with synaptic networks relatively stable yet remaining plastic [30], subsequent experiments were conducted in L4 larvae to reduce developmental confounding and enable direct comparisons across conditions.

L4 larvae were exposed to graded UV doses (254 nm; 0–600 J/m²). Pearson correlation analysis showed that head thrash counts decreased with increasing UV dose (r = -0.9841) and approached a plateau at 400–600 J/m², a range in which survival was also markedly affected (Supplementary Fig S1). Therefore, 300 J/m² was used for subsequent experiments unless otherwise specified (Fig. 1A). L2/L3 larvae similarly exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in head thrash counts (L2: r = -0.9797; L3: r = -0.8768; Pearson correlation) (Fig. 1B). To define an appropriate recovery period, we monitored head thrash counts from 0 to 6 h after UV exposure. The inhibitory effect gradually weakened, and recovery became evident at 5–6 h. Accordingly, subsequent analyses were performed at 4 h after UV exposure (Fig. 1C), allowing us to capture a stable phenotype before pronounced spontaneous recovery. At a UV dose of 300 J/m², body bend counts were significantly reduced compared with controls (Fig. 1D). We then assessed more complex integrative behaviors, including foraging and salt chemotaxis. Because 300 J/m² caused pronounced suppression in these assays and thereby limited the dynamic range for comparative analyses, a lower dose of 50 J/m² was used. This dose still induced significant behavioral deficits while preserving sensitivity for comparative analyses and minimizing toxicity. Compared with controls, worms exposed to 50 J/m² showed significantly reduced foraging performance (Fig. 1E) and an altered salt chemotaxis index (Fig. 1F), indicating that UV-induced neurotoxicity disrupts not only locomotion-related outputs but also more complex behavioral responses.

Following the establishment of robust UV-induced behavioral deficits, we investigated alterations in neurotransmitter-related neuronal populations using five fluorescent reporter strains: EG1285/GABAergic, LX929/cholinergic, DA1240/glutamatergic, BZ555/dopaminergic, and GR1366/serotonergic. Quantification of fluorescence intensity revealed a non-uniform pattern among the tested reporter strains. The GABAergic reporter EG1285 and the cholinergic reporter LX929 showed a non-significant decreasing trend in fluorescence intensity after UV exposure (Fig. 1G, H). In contrast, UV exposure significantly reduced fluorescence intensity in the dopaminergic reporter BZ555, the glutamatergic reporter DA1240, and the serotonergic reporter GR1366 (Fig. 1I–K). These fluorescence changes were accompanied by distinct structural alterations. In UV-exposed BZ555 animals (Fig. 1I), soma-like signals in the head were largely preserved, whereas longitudinal fiber tracts along the body axis became thinner, shorter, and discontinuous with beading-like morphology and focal bleb-like swellings evidenced in head neurites. In UV-exposed DA1240 animals (Fig. 1J), fluorescent puncta within the head nerve ring were reduced and unevenly distributed, with blurred boundaries and occasional fusion; neurites in the head and trunk displayed bleb-like or beading-like protrusions, and fiber trajectories in the tail region appeared weaker with reduced continuity. In UV-exposed GR1366 animals (Fig. 1K), already low baseline signals became more diffuse, and long-range fiber-like signals along the trunk were harder to track continuously. Overall, UV irradiation predominantly impaired the glutamatergic, dopaminergic, and serotonergic pathways, reflected by both decreased fluorescence and compromised neurite integrity, while exerting minimal effects on the GABAergic and cholinergic pathways.

Transcriptomic remodeling after UV exposure

To elucidate the molecular basis of UV-induced behavioral deficits and alterations in neurotransmitter pathways, we performed a comparative transcriptomic analysis of worms after UV exposure (Fig. 2A–C). The heatmap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed clear separation between control and UV-exposed samples (Fig. 2A). Using |log2FC| > 1 and an adjusted p value (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05 as thresholds, we identified 4,320 DEGs, including 1,128 upregulated and 3,192 downregulated genes (Fig. 2B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that these DEGs were mainly enriched in neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, calcium signaling, and AGE–RAGE signaling pathways (Fig. 2C) suggesting involvement of neuronal signaling and stress-response processes.

To further characterize the impact of UV on specific neuronal populations, we extracted genes belonging to five major neurotransmitter pathways—dopaminergic, serotonergic, glutamatergic, GABAergic, and cholinergic, based on WormBase annotations (Supplementary Fig. S3A; Supplementary Table S3). All five pathways exhibited substantial transcriptional downregulation, albeit to varying degrees. This graded response suggests that UV exposure triggers a broad but non-uniform transcriptional suppression across neurotransmitter systems. Additionally, we analyzed pan-neuronal synaptic and neuropeptide-related genes (Supplementary Fig. S3B; Supplementary Table S3). These genes displayed a mixed expression pattern, with some genes downregulated (e.g. ehs-1, snb-1, ric-19, unc-108, tbb-1) and others upregulated, indicating that UV exposure elicits a heterogeneous transcriptional response beyond neurotransmitter pathway-specific changes.

Guided by the transcriptomic results and the fluorescence reporter data (Fig. 1G–K), we performed qRT-PCR validation on the three pathways that showed clear and significant fluorescence alterations, dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic, while noting that GABAergic and cholinergic reporters showed only non-significant decreasing trends. After 300 J/m² UV irradiation, most examined genes, including cat-2, dat-1, dop-1, dop-4, tph-1, mod-1, ser-1, glna-1, eat-4, snf-5, and glr-1, were significantly downregulated, whereas bas-1 and cat-1 showed no significant change (Fig. 2D–F). These qRT-PCR results confirmed the transcriptional suppression of the three affected pathways, consistent with both the RNA-seq data and the fluorescence readouts.

Upstream DDR signaling modulates UV-induced neural impairment

To assess how DDR regulates UV-induced neurotoxicity, we examined behavioral inhibition rates in mutants of key DDR components. The DDR checkpoint is primarily coordinated by the ATM/ATR kinase network, which drives repair and stress-responsive transcription [31]. In the ATM branch, atm-1(gk186) showed significantly higher inhibition rates across all behavioral measures compared to wild type (Fig. 3A–D). Similarly, mutants in other ATM-pathway genes, mrt-2 and clk-2, exhibited elevated UV sensitivity, whereas chk-2 mutation did not produce a clear phenotype (Fig. 3E–H). The ATR checkpoint and the downstream effector CEP-1/p53 are equally critical. The ATR-checkpoint mutant hus-1(op241) displayed strongly enhanced behavioral inhibition (Fig. 3I–L). Furthermore, mutation of cep-1(gk138), the C. elegans p53 homolog and a known transcriptional effector of DDR [32], also led to significantly higher inhibition rates (Fig. 3M–P). Examination of the corresponding raw behavioral values (Supplementary Fig. S4) showed that baseline performance in these mutants was largely comparable to wild-type, with only minor differences. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant genotype-by-UV interaction for most behavioral measures (p < 0.01), except for the foraging index of chk-2 and the chemotaxis index of hus-1 (p > 0.05). These exceptions are consistent with the lack of a clear phenotype for chk-2 and higher variability of chemotaxix assays. Overall, the data confirmed that the enhanced inhibition reflects UV-specific hypersensitivity rather than baseline defects. RNAi knockdown of the relevant DDR components yielded consistent results (Supplementary Fig. S5). Collectively, these results indicated that both ATM and ATR were essential for maintaining neurobehavioral resilience after UV exposure, with particularly strong roles for mrt-2, clk-2, and hus-1. The downstream transcriptional effector CEP-1/p53 integrates these signals to modulate behavioral outcomes.

Germline ATM/ATR support neural homeostasis via inter-tissue signaling

In C. elegans, somatic cells retain DNA repair capacity despite transcriptional repression of apical DDR signaling proteins [28]. This prompted us to investigate whether DDR signaling within the gonad influences UV-induced neural damage through inter-tissue signaling. We first compared UV-induced behavioral inhibition rates in wild type, germline-deficient glp-1(q224) and the DDR mutants hus-1(op241) and atm-1(gk186), all grown at 25 °C (Fig. 4A). The germline-deficient glp-1(q224) mutant exhibited inhibition rates for head thrashes, body bends, foraging, and salt chemotaxis index that were significantly higher than wild type and comparable to the DDR mutants hus-1 and atm-1 (Fig. 4B–E). Corresponding raw data confirmed that baseline behaviors were comparable across genotypes, with only minor differences. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant genotype-by-UV interaction for most behavioral measures (p < 0.01), except for the chemotaxis index of atm-1 (p > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S6). This exception likely reflects the higher variability of chemotaxis assays. Nevertheless, the data confirmed that the enhanced inhibition reflected UV-specific hypersensitivity rather than baseline differences. These results indicated that disrupting germline DDR signaling alone is sufficient to increase UV-induced neural sensitivity, supporting a non-cell-autonomous role for germline DDR in maintaining neural homeostasis.

To directly test the contribution of germline-intrinsic DDR, germline-specific RNAi was performed using the validated DCL569 germline-restricted RNAi strain [33]. Knockdown of ATM-pathway genes (mrt-2, rad-50, mre-11, or clk-2) or ATR-pathway genes (atl-1, hus-1, or rad-1) in the germline consistently exacerbated UV-induced inhibition across all behavioral assays (Fig. 4F–M). Thus, intact DDR signaling within the germline contributed to maintaining neural function after UV exposure. Germline deficiency or germline-restricted disruption of DDR recapitulated the heightened neural vulnerability observed in systemic DDR mutants, reinforcing a non-cell-autonomous regulatory axis from the gonad to the nervous system.

Germline DDR maintains transcriptional stability of the neurotransmitter under UV stress

To determine whether DDR signaling stabilizes neurotransmitter-pathway transcription, we analyzed the expression of synthesis-, transport-, and receptor-related genes in the dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic pathways by qRT-PCR. In wild-type animals, UV exposure (300 J/m²) caused a general reduction in the expression of many neurotransmitter-pathway genes (Fig. 2D–F). This suppression was significantly amplified in systemic DDR mutants, atm-1(gk186) and hus-1(op241), across all three pathways, with synthesis and transport genes showing the most pronounced decreases (Fig. 5A–C). The germline-deficient mutant glp-1(q224) exhibited a similarly enhanced downregulation after UV exposure (Fig. 5D–F), indicating that the germline is necessary to mitigate transcriptional suppression. In DCL569 animals, germline-specific knockdown of rad-1 or mre-11 exacerbated the transcriptional suppression compared to the L4440 control, particularly in synthesis and transport modules (Fig. 5G–I). Notably, these effects were not uniform across all genes or genetic backgrounds. For example, dop-1 showed significantly enhanced UV-induced downregulation primarily in the germline-deficient glp-1 background, but not in atm-1, hus-1, rad-1 RNAi, or mre-11 RNAi backgrounds; dop-4, ser-1, and snf-5 showed similar patterns, with significant enhancement only in certain backgrounds; whereas glr-1 exhibited particularly strong downregulation in rad-1 and mre-11 RNAi backgrounds. These observations suggested that different neurotransmitter pathways and regulatory nodes exhibit differential vulnerability to UV stress depending on the specific DDR branch that is compromised. Examination of the corresponding raw expression data confirmed that these enhanced suppressions were not simply due to pre-existing baseline differences across genotypes (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Collectively, these results demonstrated that functional DDR, particularly within the germline, is essential to buffer the nervous system against UV-induced transcriptional repression of neurotransmitter pathways, although the extent of this protection varies across specific genes and DDR components. This established a direct mechanistic link between germline DDR status and the maintenance of somatic neurotransmitter homeostasis under genotoxic stress.

NER pathway regulates neurobehavior independent of germline signaling

Our preceding results established that upstream DDR signaling exhibited non-cell-autonomous inter-tissue regulation. We next asked whether the downstream NER pathway, which removes UV-induced photolesions, also regulated neural function via germline-mediated inter-tissue communication. We performed RNAi knockdown of core NER genes (csa-1, csb-1, xpa-1, xpf-1, and xpg-1) and quantified UV-induced behavioral inhibition. Systemic knockdown of these NER genes significantly increased inhibition rates of head thrashes, body bends, foraging, and salt chemotaxis index (Fig. 6A–D), indicating that NER activity was essential for organismal neurobehavioral resilience. Corresponding raw data confirmed that these increases were not due to baseline differences (Supplementary Fig. S8). In contrast, germline-specific knockdown of the same NER genes did not significantly alter any of these behavioral metrics (Fig. 6E–H). These results indicated that NER is required for neurobehavioral resilience at the organismal level, likely through its canonical repair function in multiple somatic tissues. Germline-specific NER knockdown produced no significant effect, suggesting that the NER pathway did not operate via a dedicated germline-derived signaling axis. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that NER activity in other non-germline tissues also contributes. Unlike the upstream DDR, NER did not appear to exert its effect via dedicated germline-derived inter-tissue signaling. This clear functional distinction underscored that the non-cell-autonomous inter-tissue regulation of neural integrity is a specific feature of the upstream DDR signaling network, rather than a general property of DNA repair pathways.

CPR-4 mediates germline-to-soma signaling and supports somatic NER responses

Having established that upstream germline DDR signaling (ATM/ATR), but not NER, regulated UV-induced neurobehavior through inter-tissue signaling, we sought the specific gonad-derived factors mediating this communication. We focused on CPR-4, a secreted cysteine protease (cathepsin B homolog), previously implicated in radiation-induced bystander effects in worms and as a downstream signal of the germline RAD9–RAD1–HUS1 (9-1-1) checkpoint clamp–ATR pathway [34]. The cpr-4(tm3718) mutant exhibited significantly enhanced UV-induced inhibition across all behavioral assays (head thrashes, body bends, foraging, and salt chemotaxis) (Fig. 7A–D). Corresponding raw data confirmed that baseline behaviors were comparable to wild-type, ruling out pre-existing defects (Supplementary Fig. S9).

To test whether CPR-4 acts as a secreted signal, we performed a conditioned-medium rescue experiment: medium collected from UV-exposed wild-type animals significantly alleviated the neurobehavioral deficits of cpr-4 mutants, whereas control medium had no effect (Supplementary Fig. S10). These results indicate that secreted CPR-4 is sufficient to protect against UV-induced neurotoxicity.

UV exposure strongly induced cpr-4 transcription in wild type animals, as determined by qRT-PCR (Fig. 7E) and RNA-seq (Supplementary Fig. S3D). This induction was abolished in germline-deficient glp-1(q224) mutant and attenuated in the hus-1(op241) and atm-1(gk186) mutants. Germline-specific knockdown of rad-1 (ATR) or mre-11 (ATM) similarly reduced, but did not eliminate, UV-induced cpr-4 upregulation (Fig. 7F), confirming its placement downstream of germline DDR. In a cpr-4(tm3718) mutant background, the transcriptional responses of core DDR genes to UV exposure were altered, hus-1 induction was abolished, while atm-1 induction was markedly enhanced (Fig. 7G, H), suggesting that CPR-4 may differentially modulate the transcriptional responses of hus-1 and atm-1 under UV stress, although the underlying mechanism remains to be fully defined. Notably, loss of either hus-1 or atm-1 resulted in similarly severe neurobehavioral deficits (Fig. 3), indicating that both ATR and ATM branches converge on common downstream mediators, including CPR-4 itself and the somatic NER pathway. UV-induced transcriptional upregulation of core NER genes (xpa-1, xpc-1, xpf-1, and xpg-1) observed in wild-type animals was severely blunted or reversed to downregulation in cpr-4(tm3718) mutant (Fig. 7I–L). These data support a protective axis in which UV-triggered germline DDR promotes cpr-4 transcription and a CPR-4–dependent secreted protective activity. CPR-4 then functions as a critical inter-tissue signal that promotes the transcriptional activation of the somatic NER repair pathway, and preserved nervous system function, forming a coherent “germline DDR–CPR-4–somatic repair/nervous protection” regulatory chain.

Discussion

This study elucidated a non-cell-autonomous axis that protected against UV-induced genotoxic stress. We demonstrated that UV-triggered DNA damage activated upstream DDR components in the germline, which in turn orchestrated neuronal protection in somatic tissues through cpr-4 induction and a CPR-4-dependent secreted protective activity. This germline-to-soma inter-tissue pathway was distinct from cell-autonomous DNA repair within neurons, highlighting the reproductive tissue as a critical hub for systemic stress resilience.

CPR-4 emerged as a key mediator of this inter-tissue communication. This cathepsin B-like cysteine protease has previously been implicated in radiation-induced bystander effects and in non-cell-autonomous signaling downstream of germline DDR. Our study extends the function of CPR-4 to the coordination of UV-induced neuroprotection. Loss of cpr-4 exacerbated UV-induced neurobehavioral inhibition and impaired the transcriptional induction of multiple somatic NER-related genes. A conditioned-medium transfer assay further revealed that medium from UV-exposed wild-type donors, but not from glp-1(q224) or cpr-4(tm3718) donors, partially rescued the UV-induced head-thrash defect in cpr-4(tm3718) recipients, supporting the presence of a germline- and CPR-4-dependent secreted protective activity after UV exposure. Mechanistically, CPR-4 may promote neurofunctional recovery by sustaining somatic repair responses, potentially through extracellular or membrane-associated substrates. Although direct biochemical measurement of CPR-4 protein secretion and its downstream targets remains to be established, the conditioned-medium data provide functional evidence for its secreted protective role. Evolutionary considerations further support this model. The mammalian homolog of CPR-4, cathepsin B (CTSB) have been linked to correlate with cognitive health and memory performance [35, 36], and can confer neuroprotection through mechanisms that include amyloid-β degradation [37]. These parallels raise the possibility that this protease family may play a conserved role in maintaining nervous system integrity. Importantly, although cpr-4 loss differentially affects hus-1 and atm-1 transcription, both genes are required for proper cpr-4 induction and downstream NER activation, suggesting that the two DDR branches converge on common protective outputs.

Integrating this signaling axis within the nervous system, we interpreted the UV-induced damage through the functional organization of neurotransmitter circuits. UV exposure triggered a broad transcriptional suppression across multiple neurotransmitter systems, as revealed by RNA-seq analysis of genes associated with dopaminergic, serotonergic, glutamatergic, GABAergic and cholinergic pathways (Supplementary Fig. S3A). However, the magnitude of this suppression varied across pathways, with dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic systems showing more pronounced transcriptional downregulation (Fig. 2D-F). This graded transcriptional response was partially reflected at the protein level by fluorescent reporter assays. Glutamatergic signaling, which mediates sensory input and integration in the nerve ring, showed marked suppression, as indicated by reduced fluorescence intensity and altered neurite morphology in the DA1240 reporter strain. This is consistent with diminished excitatory drive upstream of motor execution [38, 39]. Dopaminergic pathways, which modulate motor strategy and state transitions rather than directly driving muscle contraction [40], exhibited structural beading and fragmentation in the BZ555 reporter, suggesting compromised long-range modulatory output. Serotonergic signaling, crucial for behavioral vigor and internal state [41], was severely downregulated, evidenced by marked collapse of head signals and reduced structural resolvability in the GR1366 reporter. In contrast, cholinergic and GABAergic pathways, which constitute the core motor output layer directly driving muscle contraction and rhythmic coordination [42], showed only a labeling intensity and major tract trajectories in EG1285 and LX929 (Fig. 1G, H). This apparent discrepancy between the transcriptional downregulation of GABAergic/cholinergic genes and the preservation of their fluorescence signals may reflect a temporal lag between mRNA changes and protein-level responses, or differential post-transcriptional regulation of these pathways. Together, these data indicate that UV irradiation produces differential vulnerability among neurotransmitter systems: glutamatergic, dopaminergic, and serotonergic pathways are more severely impacted, whereas GABAergic and cholinergic systems remain relatively stable at the protein level. This differential response accounts for the selective behavioral phenotype—severe impairment of learning and memory, but only partial reduction in locomotion.

Our study provided a mechanistic framework for understanding how organisms compensate for inherent DDR limitations in post-mitotic tissues such as neurons. This aligns with the adaptive resource-allocation/disposable soma perspective, wherein systemic signals originating from the germline may be mobilized under stress to preferentially support maintenance of essential somatic functions [43]. This view is supported by evidence in C. elegans showing that reproductive-system status can reshape somatic maintenance programs by modulating lifespan and stress resistance, and can enhance organism-wide stress resilience following germline DNA damage, thereby positioning the germline as an initiator of cross-tissue defenses under threat [24, 44, 45]. The implication of this cross-tissue DDR signaling model likely extends beyond development and aging. In recent years, the non-cell-autonomous functions of the DDR, often mediated by paracrine factors, have been increasingly recognized as important modulators of tissue-microenvironment biology [46]. This has direct relevance for cancer therapy. Although DDR defects, such as alterations in TP53 or ATM are hallmarks of many tumors and should theoretically confer radiosensitivity, clinical radioresistance remains common [47]. Our model offered a plausible explanation: upon irradiation, DDR-proficient stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment (for example, fibroblasts, immune cells) may secrete protective bystander factors that enhance the survival and/or repair capacity of neighboring DDR-deficient tumor cells, thereby promoting radioresistance. Identifying and targeting such protective bystander signals, potentially including protease-family factors such as CTSB/CPR-4, may offer a strategy to overcome therapeutic resistance.

Conclusion

In summary, we delineated a pathway in which germline DDR activation induced CPR-4 and, via CPR-4, promoted neuroprotection in somatic tissues. This revealed a fundamental layer of organismal defense: genomic surveillance in one tissue can coordinate functional homeostasis in another via secreted signals. Together, our findings expanded the DDR paradigm from a purely cell-intrinsic repair system to a systemic coordinator of inter-tissue resilience, providing a framework for understanding neuronal adaptation to genotoxic stress, organism-wide stress regulation, and mechanisms of resistance in cancer therapy.
