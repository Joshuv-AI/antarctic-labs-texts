TRIM29 promotes pancreatic cancer MVI via IκBα K48-ubiquitination and NF-κB activation in CXCL5⁺ epithelial cells
Authors: Long Liu, Zhenzhen Gao, Qi Wang, Xiaohong Zhao, Fabiao Zhang, Yu Zhu, Wangyang Sun, Yuxi Huang, Zhenbo Wang, Sheng Yan, Shaowei Li, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the mechanisms underlying the interaction and influence between microvascular invasion (MVI), which contributes to metastasis and poor prognosis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), and tumor microenvironment.MethodsThis study employed an integrative multi-omics approach, combining spatial proteomics (multiplex immunofluorescence), transcriptome sequencing, and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to profile MVI⁺ and MVI⁻ PDAC samples. The functional role of key genes was validated through in vitro and in vivo assays (migration, invasion, xenograft models). Molecular mechanisms were dissected using Co-IP, ubiquitination assays, and ChIP-qPCR.ResultsThe epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process was significantly activated in MVI⁺ tumors, with EMT-positive cells located spatially closer to microvessels. scRNA-seq identified a distinct epithelial subpopulation characterized by CXCL5 that exhibited a strong EMT phenotype and was enriched in MVI⁺ samples. Mechanistically, the transcription factor ZBTB7B was found to directly promote TRIM29 transcription. TRIM29 directly binds to IκBα via its BB2 domain and catalyzes its K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This event relieves the inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to its activation and the subsequent induction of EMT, ultimately enhancing the invasive and metastatic capabilities of pancreatic cancer cells. Clinical correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between TRIM29 and IκBα protein levels, and high TRIM29, low IκBα, and high CXCL5⁺EMT levels were all associated with poor prognosis.ConclusionThis study reveals the critical role of the ZBTB7B-TRIM29-IκBα-NF-κB signaling axis in promoting EMT in CXCL5-marked pancreatic cancer cells surrounding MVI, establishing TRIM29 as a potential therapeutic target for inhibiting early metastasis of PDAC.Clinical relevanceThis study provides deep insights into the biology of MVI, a crucial prognostic indicator in pancreatic cancer. TRIM29 expression could serve as a potential biomarker for predicting tumor aggressiveness and patient outcome. More importantly, targeting TRIM29’s E3 ligase activity or its interaction with IκBα represents a promising novel therapeutic strategy to inhibit early metastasis and improve the resectability of pancreatic cancer.Graphical Abstract We found that the EMT process is significantly activated in MVI⁺ tumors and exhibits spatial gradient dependence. Mechanistically, increased ZBTB7B in MVI⁺ samples promotes TRIM29 transcription. TRIM29 directly binds IκBα via its BB2 domain, catalyzing its K48-linked ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation. This process releases inhibition of the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to pathway activation and EMT induction, ultimately enhancing the invasive and metastatic capabilities of pancreatic cancer cells. Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most aggressive malignancies among all tumor diseases, with a poor prognosis and a five-year survival rate of only 13%, making it one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. The majority of patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) lose the opportunity for surgery due to vascular invasion or distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis, resulting in only about 20% of patients being eligible for radical surgical resection ultimately [2]. Therefore, understanding the initial mechanisms of vascular invasion or metastasis in pancreatic cancer is crucial for improving the resectability rate of pancreatic cancer surgery and the overall prognosis [3]. Microvascular invasion (MVI) in pancreatic cancer is considered a sign of early invasion of tumor tissue, manifested as tumor cells invading and destroying the surrounding blood vessel walls, leading to the formation of cancer thrombi within the blood vessels [4]. The microvessels invaded by pancreatic cancer are a potential source of local and distant metastases within the pancreatic tissue and a key factor in the early recurrence and prognosis of pancreatic cancer [5, 6]. Furthermore, tumor cells that have invaded the vascular lumen can only be detected through histopathological examination, making the diagnosis rather challenging overall. In summary, MVI in pancreatic cancer is a complex biological process involving multiple factors, including the interaction between pancreatic cancer cells and the microenvironment [7, 8]. Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of MVI holds significant clinical importance for the diagnosis, treatment, and management of pancreatic cancer.

In recent years, high-throughput technologies have greatly enhanced our understanding of the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Spatial proteomics and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have emerged as powerful tools for characterizing the tumor microenvironment (TME) and dissecting intratumoral heterogeneity [9]. Currently, some pioneering spatial proteomics have been applied to study the cell infiltration and heterogeneity in the pancreatic cancer microenvironment. However, due to limitations such as a limited number of indicators and image resolution, spatial proteomics has shown limitations in providing a more in-depth and comprehensive description of the mechanisms in pancreatic cancer tissue [10]. Meanwhile, the application of scRNA-seq in the field of pancreatic cancer, through a more comprehensive and in-depth analysis of the transcriptome data of individual cells, has helped us understand the microenvironmental heterogeneity, metastatic and invasive capabilities, and drug resistance mechanisms of pancreatic cancer [11, 12]. However, due to the lack of spatial information on cell-cell interactions in tissues, this can lead to false positives in the results of cell-cell interaction analysis [13]. Therefore, integrating tumor spatial information with scRNA-seq represents a more optimal approach to elucidate the cellular interactions, heterogeneity, and regulatory mechanisms of pancreatic cancer cells [14]. However, at present, there remains a lack of in-depth characterization of the multicellular ecosystems and molecular mechanisms that distinguish pancreatic cancer patients with microvascular invasion (MVI+) from those without (MVI-) at the single-cell resolution level integrated with spatial information.

It is well known that EMT plays a key role in tumor malignant progression by promoting tumor cell invasion and diffusion to distant organs, thus leading to cancer metastasis [15]. During this process, tumor cells lose their polarized characteristics, acquire a mesenchymal phenotype, and initiate tumor cell migration and invasion [16, 17]. A specific feature of pancreatic cancer that has been identified is the presence of EMT, which significantly contributes to the dense tumor stroma and invasive potential [18]. For example, studies have shown that hypoxia can promote PDAC metastasis by inducing EMT [19]. FZD7 accelerates liver metastasis in pancreatic cancer by activating EMT associated with TGF-β/SMAD3 signaling [20]. However, currently, it has not been investigated whether EMT plays an important role in the microvascular invasion of pancreatic cancer.

Tripartite motif (TRIM) proteins are widely known as E3 ubiquitin ligases with conserved domains, including a RING domain, a B-box type domain, and a coiled-coil domain [21]. Different from most TRIM family proteins, TRIM29 contains a B1-B2-CC domain in addition to the RING domain [22]. Studies have shown that the expression of TRIM29 is significantly increased in gastric cancer [23], ovarian cancer [24], and colon cancer [25], and there is a significant association between the upregulation of TRIM29 expression and the poor prognosis of patients with malignancies. In pancreatic cancer, studies have shown that TRIM29 promotes gemcitabine resistance through the MEK/ERK pathway [26] and promotes the growth and progression of pancreatic cancer by stabilizing the Yes-associated protein 1 [27]. However, the impact of TRIM29 on EMT and microvascular invasion in pancreatic cancer has not been reported.

In brief, in this study, we first mapped the spatial microenvironment of MVI + and MVI- pancreatic cancer samples through multiplex immunofluorescence staining. Combined with transcriptome sequencing, we found that EMT is closely related to the microvascular invasion of pancreatic cancer. Further single-cell sequencing of pancreatic cancer samples identified a strong EMT phenotype in the CXCL5 + EPC population. In-depth mechanism analysis revealed that TRIM29 promotes NF-κB-dependent epithelial-mesenchymal transition by ubiquitinating and degrading the IκBα protein in CXCL5 + EPCs. We depicted the microenvironmental landscape of microvascular invasion in pancreatic cancer, elucidated the important role of TRIM29 in accelerating microvascular invasion by promoting EMT in pancreatic cancer, and provided a theoretical basis for clinically curbing the early metastasis of pancreatic cancer.

Materials and methods

Collection of patients and tissue specimens

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University, and all participants provided written informed consent. Information from 602 pancreatic cancer patients was used to evaluate the relationship between microvascular invasion and overall survival (OS) as well as progression-free survival (PFS). Twenty fresh-frozen pancreatic cancer tissue samples were used for transcriptome sequencing. For single-cell sequencing samples, the patient inclusion criteria included no obvious metastasis, single-stage samples. Eight independent surgically resected tumor specimens were collected from 8 patients with PDAC. A tissue microarray containing 80 pancreatic cancer patients (including cancerous and paracancerous tissues) was used for multiplex immunofluorescence staining (targeting CD8, CD68, CD11c, CD20, and α-SMA), as well as for evaluating the relationships between the protein expressions of CXCL5, TRIM29, and IκBα and OS time. Detailed information of all patients is provided in Table S1.

Preparation of single-cell suspensions and cDNA libraries

Collected samples were rapidly stored in sCelLive™ Tissue Preservation Solution (Singleron) after cryogenic separation, then washed with normal saline to remove blood and contaminants. The samples were manually cut into small pieces of approximately 1 mm on ice and incubated with digestive solution at 37 °C. The digestive solution contained an enzyme mixture consisting of 1 mg/mL collagenase type VIII (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. C2139), 1 mg/mL collagenase type IV (Gibco, Cat. No. 17104019), 2 mg/mL dispase II (Roche, Cat. No. 4942078001), 1 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, Cat. No. 10104159001), and neutral protease (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 4942078001). After digestion, the digested tissue pieces were passed through a 40 μm filter. The suspended cells were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, then incubated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer (Roche, Cat. No. 11814389001) on ice for 10 min. After two washes with 1× PBS (GIBCO, Cat. No. C10010500BT), the cell pellet was resuspended in sorting buffer (PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum [FBS]). The concentration of the single-cell suspension was measured using a Count Star instrument and adjusted to 800–1200 cells/µL. The single-cell suspension was loaded onto a Chromium Single Cell Controller (10x Genomics) to construct cDNA libraries using the Single Cell 3′ Library and Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (10x Genomics, Cat. No. 1000268) and Chromium Next GEM Chip G Single Cell Kit (10x Genomics, Cat. No. 1000120) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [28]. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten with 150 bp paired-end reads.

Specimen and cell culture

The human pancreatic cancer cell lines BxPC-3 (RRID: CVCL_0186) and PANC-1 (RRID: CVCL_0480) were purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) on June 2022. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Invitrogen, Camarillo, CA, USA) in an atmosphere of 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Overexpression vectors, negative controls, and short hairpin RNA (shRNA) plasmids (Table S2, The original mutation plasmid file can be found in Raw Data.) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The culture medium was replaced 12–16 h after transfection. When the cell confluency reached approximately 80%, antibiotics were added to select positive cells, and the selection was maintained until detection.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was then synthesized from the total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Kit (Takara). RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR Prime-Script RT-PCR Kit (Takara, Beijing, China) and the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, New York, USA). RT-qPCR primers were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and their sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control to normalize the qPCR results. Relative gene expression levels were measured using cycle threshold (CT) values in the ΔΔCT calculation. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated three times.

Western Blotting Analysis and Immunoprecipitation (IP)

Total protein was extracted from cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) containing phosphatase and protease inhibitors. The protein concentration of cell lysates was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Germany). The membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk, incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S4; GAPDH was used as a loading control), and then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies. For IP, cells were lysed using IP/Western Lysis Buffer (P0013, Beyotime Biotechnology). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was incubated with the specified antibody with gentle rotation at 4 °C overnight. Thirty microliters of A/G-Sepharose was added to each tube, and the tubes were incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. The precipitate was collected by gentle centrifugation and washed three times with pre-cooled PBS. Subsequent procedures were performed according to standard Western blotting protocols. The Ultra High Sensitivity ECL Kit (Glpbio, GK10008, China) was used to detect protein bands. Western blots were quantified using ImageJ 1.8.0 software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Multiplex Immunofluorescence (mIF) Staining

Multiplex staining of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues was performed using the Opal 6-Color IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, Cat. No. NEL861001KT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, FFPE sections were deparaffinized, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and then subjected to antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6) or Tris/EDTA (pH 9) via heat-induced epitope retrieval. Each section underwent several rounds of sequential staining; each round included blocking of endogenous peroxidase and non-specific proteins. The sections were then incubated with primary antibodies from different panels overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and tyramide signal amplification (TSA). After each TSA step, the slides were subjected to an additional 10-minute antigen retrieval in heated citrate buffer (pH 6) or Tris/EDTA (pH 9) to remove antibodies before the next staining round. After all sequential staining reactions, the sections were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to label cell nuclei. Single and/or co-expressed markers in mIF images were quantified using HALO software (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA).

In vivo tumorigenesis and metastasis assays

This study was conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University, and adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Institutes of Health (NIH, USA). Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice and C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center (SLAC) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment at the Laboratory Animal Center of Zhejiang University, with strictly controlled housing conditions: temperature maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity at 50 ± 5%, and a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. Sterilized food and sterile water were provided ad libitum.

Tumor model establishment: Mice were anesthetized via isoflurane inhalation. A midline abdominal incision was made to expose the pancreas, and human pancreatic cancer BxPC-3 cells in the logarithmic growth phase (1 × 10⁶ cells per mouse) were slowly injected into the pancreatic parenchyma. Four weeks after surgery, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and orthotopic pancreatic tumors as well as spleens were completely dissected.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) based on at least three independent experiments. Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for statistical comparison between two groups and multiple groups, respectively. Spearman’s rank correlation and Pearson’s correlation were used for correlation analysis, and the ggstatsplot package was used for visualization. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis of OS and PFS in pancreatic cancer patients was performed using the “Survival” and “Survminer” R packages.

Results

MVI of pancreatic cancer is significantly associated with EMT

To explore the prognostic correlation between tumor MVI (Representative H&E staining of MVI is shown in Figure S1A) and pancreatic cancer progression, we collected clinical data from 602 pancreatic cancer patients who underwent surgical treatment at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University (Table S1) and conducted long-term follow-up. The results indicated that MVI in pancreatic cancer was significantly correlated with high TNM stage and poor differentiation (Figure S1B). Compared with pancreatic cancer patients without MVI, those with MVI had a significantly poorer OS and PFS (Fig. 1A).

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on 80 patients to label infiltrating cells in the pancreatic cancer microenvironment (CD8, CD68, CD20, CD11c, α-SMA). The results revealed that among MVI-positive (MVI⁺) pancreatic cancer tissues, only the number of α-SMA⁺ cells showed a significant change compared with MVI-negative (MVI⁻) pancreatic cancer tissues (Fig. 1B & C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that an increased number of α-SMA⁺ cells was associated with poorer prognosis in pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 1D-E). Subsequently, using HALO software, we centered on α-SMA⁺ cells and statistically analyzed single-positive cells (CD8, CD68, CD20, and CD11c) within a 50 μm radius. Regrettably, no significant difference in the spatial interaction between α-SMA⁺ cells and other cells was observed between MVI⁻ and MVI⁺ pancreatic cancer tissues (Figs. 1F & S1C).

To further explore the underlying mechanism of MVI in pancreatic cancer, we selected 20 pancreatic cancer samples (10 MVI⁺ and 10 MVI⁻) for transcriptome sequencing and identified MVI-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using the criteria |log₂ fold change| > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (Fig. 1G). Functional enrichment analysis of these DEGs showed that the extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling pathway and EMT pathway were significantly enriched (Fig. 1H). It is well known that EMT of pancreatic cancer cells promotes the migration and invasion of tumor-initiating cells. We further quantified E-cadherin⁺ cells and E-cadherin⁺α-SMA⁺ double-positive cells in 80 PDAC patients, and found that in MVI⁺ pancreatic cancer tissues, the expression of E-cadherin⁺ cells was decreased (Figure S1D), while the proportion of double-positive cells was significantly increased (Fig. 1I). Although the expression level of E-cadherin showed no significant correlation with the prognosis of pancreatic cancer patients (either in TCGA dataset or our own data) (Figure S1E), the change in the number of EMT-positive cells was significantly associated with prognosis (Fig. 1J). This suggests that tumor cells in MVI⁺ pancreatic cancer tissues may undergo EMT, which further affects patient prognosis.

We then conducted a comprehensive EMT assessment of the 20 patients included in the sequencing dataset using multiplex immunofluorescence staining with a panel of markers (E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin, Snail) (Fig. 1K). The results showed that in MVI⁺ pancreatic cancer tissues, the expression of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin, Snail) in tumor cells was significantly increased—with N-cadherin showing the most prominent elevation—while the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin was decreased.

To clarify the spatial characteristics of the EMT status and MVI niche in pancreatic cancer, we performed co-staining of α-SMA (mesenchymal marker), E-cadherin (tumor epithelial cell marker), and CD31 (vascular marker) on the 20 patients from the sequencing dataset. Epithelial cells undergoing EMT are defined as dual-positive cells for E-cadherin and α-SMA (EMT-EPC). In pancreatic cancer tissues with microvascular invasion, EMT-EPC expression is enhanced and exhibits a spatially gradient-dependent pattern near microvascular regions represented by CD31 (Figs. 1L & S1F). These results confirm that when pancreatic cancer tissues develop MVI, the EMT process of tumor cells is activated, and the EMT status of tumor cells shows spatial gradient dependence with microvessels.

EMT of CXCL5-Expressing Epithelial Cells (CXCL5⁺EPCs) is activated in MVI-positive pancreatic cancer tissues

To deeply characterize the EMT status of tumor cells in MVI-positive (MVI⁺) pancreatic cancer tissues, we performed single-cell transcriptomic analysis on surgically resected samples from 8 pancreatic cancer patients. After quality control and removal of abnormal cells, a total of 54,833 cells were obtained, which were classified into 8 cell types (Figs. 2A & S2A-C). We then reclassified epithelial cell and fibroblast clusters to investigate the EMT heterogeneity of tumor cell. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensionality reduction analysis of the 8 samples revealed 7 epithelial cell clusters and 4 fibroblast clusters (Fig. 2B). The markers and proportions of each corresponding cell population in MVI⁺ and MVI-negative (MVI⁻) pancreatic cancer samples are shown in Figure S2D-G.

To explore the potential source of EMT signals in epithelial cells of pancreatic cancer, we evaluated EMT signal scores between MVI groups and across different epithelial cell populations based on the expression of EMT-related genes (Table S5) [29]. The results showed that the EMT score was significantly higher in the MVI group (Fig. 2C), and among all epithelial cell subsets, the CXCL5⁺ epithelial progenitor cell (CXCL5⁺EPC) subset had the highest EMT score (Figs. 2D & S2H).

To investigate the dynamic changes of cell transformation during pancreatic cancer EMT, we performed trajectory inference on epithelial cell and fibroblast populations. Through diffusion mapping and principal component analysis (PCA), we observed significant interaction or crosstalk between these EMT-related populations (Figs. 2E & S2I-J). Specifically, various epithelial cell subsets transitioned to fibroblast populations via CXCL5⁺EPCs (Figs. 2F & S2K). Pseudotime analysis of epithelial cells and fibroblasts showed that during this transition, the expression level of the epithelial cell marker KRT19 gradually decreased, while the expression of the EMT marker VIM (vimentin) gradually increased (Figure S2L). Collectively, these results indicate that CXCL5⁺EPCs represent a key cell population undergoing EMT.

To validate the newly identified CXCL5⁺EPC subset in PDAC tissues, we performed immunofluorescence (IF) staining and confirmed the presence of epithelial cells expressing the marker gene CXCL5 (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, we demonstrated that CXCL5 positively promotes the EMT process at the cellular level (Figure S3A-C). To further investigate the spatial distribution of the CXCL5⁺EPC population, we used multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) to stain tumor sections from 20 patients for E-cadherin, α-SMA, CXCL5, and CD31 (Figs. 2H & S3D). The median percentage of CXCL5⁺EPCs among total EPCs was 14.5% (10.4% in the MVI⁻ group vs. 16.8% in the MVI⁺ group), and the proportion of CXCL5⁺EMT cells among total EMT cells was 46.9% (24.2% in the MVI⁻ group vs. 56.5% in the MVI⁺ group) (Fig. 2I). Notably, the proportions of both CXCL5⁺EPCs and CXCL5⁺EMT cells were significantly increased in MVI⁺ pancreatic cancer patients. Additionally, we found that CXCL5⁺EPCs localized closer to microvascular regions (marked by CD31) in MVI⁺ pancreatic cancer tissues, and this localization exhibited spatial gradient dependence (Fig. 2J).

Finally, to confirm the clinical relevance of CXCL5⁺EPCs, we retrieved clinical data from our institutional cohort. The CXCL5⁺EPC signature and the CXCL5⁺E-cadherin⁺α-SMA⁺ signature were identified as adverse prognostic factors for pancreatic cancer patients (Fig. 2K), further validating the clinical significance of the CXCL5⁺EPC subset.

TRIM29 promotes the EMT process of pancreatic cancer cells

Given the impact of CXCL5⁺ epithelial cells (CXCL5⁺EPCs) on pancreatic cancer EMT and prognosis, we further investigated the characteristics of the CXCL5⁺EPC subset. CXCL5, PI3, ACE2, and TRIM29 were identified as marker genes of CXCL5⁺EPCs, as these four genes were specifically highly expressed in the CXCL5⁺EPC cluster (Figs. 3A, Figure S3E).

To explore the biological properties of CXCL5⁺ tumor cells, we performed functional enrichment analysis on the CXCL5⁺EPC population. The results showed that CXCL5⁺EPCs were primarily associated with processes such as ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation, protein modification, and activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 3B). Notably, TRIM29—one of the marker genes of the CXCL5⁺EPC cluster—is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation by conjugating polyubiquitin chains to target proteins. This led us to hypothesize that TRIM29 may initiate the EMT process in the CXCL5⁺EPC cluster.

We first established stably transfected pancreatic cancer cell lines, including Sh-NC (negative control shRNA), Sh-TRIM29 (TRIM29-knockdown shRNA), Vector (empty vector control), and TRIM29-overexpressing cells. The EMT status of cells in each group was then evaluated by detecting the expression of E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail via Western blotting. Compared with the control group (Sh-NC), knockdown of TRIM29 resulted in upregulation of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and downregulation of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail in pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 3C). Conversely, overexpression of TRIM29 led to the suppression of E-cadherin (epithelial marker) and increased expression of N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail (mesenchymal markers).

Subsequently, Transwell migration and invasion assays were used to assess the effect of TRIM29 on the migratory and invasive capabilities of pancreatic cancer cells. Compared with the Sh-NC group, inhibition of TRIM29 expression significantly suppressed the migration and invasion of pancreatic cancer cells; in contrast, TRIM29 overexpression promoted these abilities (Fig. 3D-E). Meanwhile, the wound healing assay revealed that reduced TRIM29 expression significantly impaired the wound closure ability of pancreatic cancer cells, whereas TRIM29 overexpression enhanced this capacity (Fig. 3F).

In in vivo experiments, we found that low TRIM29 expression significantly inhibited the proliferative capacity of orthotopically transplanted pancreatic tumors in mice, while TRIM29 overexpression promoted pancreatic cancer tumor proliferation and metastasis—this effect is observed in both C57BL/6 mice and nude mice (Fig. 3G-H). Collectively, these results confirm that TRIM29 in CXCL5⁺EPCs promotes the EMT process of pancreatic cancer cells, as well as enhances their migratory and invasive capabilities.

ZBTB7B promotes TRIM29 transcription and activates the NF-κB signaling pathway

The Single-Cell Regulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) tool was used to identify upstream transcription factors (regulons) that drive the functional properties of the CXCL5⁺EPC cluster. The results showed that GATA5, ZBTB7B, FOXA3, IRF3, and IRX5 primarily regulate the transcriptional program of the CXCL5⁺EPC cluster (Fig. 4A). Additionally, we retrieved transcription factors potentially involved in TRIM29 transcription from the JASPAR database. After intersecting these factors with the SCENIC-identified regulons via a Venn diagram, ZBTB7B was finally identified as the only transcription factor potentially responsible for TRIM29 transcription in pancreatic cancer cells (Figs. 4B&Table S6).

Coordinated expression of ZBTB7B and TRIM29 was validated at the RNA level (TCGA-PAAD cohort, Fig. 4C) and protein level (our institutional cohort, Fig. 4D-E) in pancreatic cancer samples, respectively. Furthermore, both ZBTB7B and TRIM29 were highly expressed in pancreatic cancer (Figure S4A-B) and associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 4F-I)—consistently observed in both the TCGA-PAAD dataset and our institutional dataset.

To further verify the regulatory effect of ZBTB7B on TRIM29, we analyzed the impact of ZBTB7B overexpression on TRIM29 expression in pancreatic cancer cells. The qPCR confirmed that ZBTB7B overexpression significantly upregulated TRIM29 expression; consistently, TRIM29 expression was suppressed following ZBTB7B knockdown in pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 4J). The ChIP-qPCR analysis further confirmed the binding of ZBTB7B to the TRIM29 promoter region (Fig. 4K, Table S7), demonstrating direct transcriptional regulation of TRIM29 by ZBTB7B in pancreatic cancer cells.

To explore the molecular mechanism by which TRIM29 promotes EMT in pancreatic cancer cells, we performed transcriptome sequencing on pancreatic cancer cells from the Sh-NC and Sh-TRIM29 groups. After identifying DEGs (Fig. 4L), functional enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of the NF-κB signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, and ubiquitination signaling pathway (Fig. 4M). Given that the NF-κB signaling pathway was also significantly enriched in the functional analysis of the CXCL5⁺EPC cluster from single-cell data, we hypothesized that TRIM29 promotes the EMT process of pancreatic cancer cells by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

To clarify how TRIM29 regulates NF-κB signaling activity, we used Western blotting to detect the total and phosphorylated levels of core proteins involved in canonical NF-κB signaling. In TRIM29-knockdown cell lines, the phosphorylation levels of IKKα/β (Ser176/180), IκBα (Ser32/36), and P65 (Ser536) were consistently decreased. However, the total protein levels of these targets remained unchanged except for IκBα, whose total protein level was increased following TRIM29 knockdown (Fig. 4N). Conversely, in TRIM29-overexpressing pancreatic cancer cells, we observed increased phosphorylation levels of IKKα/β (Ser176/180), IκBα (Ser32/36), and P65 (Ser536), along with a significant decrease in total IκBα protein levels (total protein levels of all other targets remained unchanged). Dual-luciferase reporter assays also indicated that TRIM29 could enhance the activity of the NF-κB pathway (Figure S4C).

To investigate the mechanism by which TRIM29 regulates the expression of IκBα, we first detected the mRNA level of IκBα. The results showed that neither knockdown nor overexpression of TRIM29 in pancreatic cancer cells significantly altered the mRNA level of IκBα (Fig. 4O). This finding suggests that TRIM29 may regulate the protein level of IκBα at the post-translational level. To determine whether TRIM29 affects IκBα through ubiquitination, we used MG132 to inhibit the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of proteins. The results demonstrated that compared with the control group (solvent without MG132), the protein level of IκBα was significantly increased after the addition of MG132. Meanwhile, MG132 could abrogate the negative regulation of IκBα by TRIM29 (Fig. 4P). Therefore, TRIM29 may regulate the protein level of IκBα by ubiquitinating it. To analyze the effect of TRIM29 on the degradation rate of IκBα protein, we treated pancreatic cancer cells with cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit protein synthesis. The results showed that 60 min after CHX treatment, the half-life of IκBα protein was extended to 8 h in Sh-TRIM29 PANC-1 cells compared with the Sh-NC group (Fig. 4Q); similar results were observed in BxPC-3 cells. Additionally, in TRIM29-overexpressing pancreatic cancer cells, following 12 h of CHX treatment, the IκBα protein level in the TRIM29-overexpressing group decreased to 0.1% in PANC-1 cells (vs. 10% in the Vector group), with comparable results in BxPC-3 cells.

TRIM29 Ubiquitinates IκBα at the Lys21/22 Sites

Based on the above results, we found that inhibition of TRIM29 expression accelerates the degradation of IκBα protein. Ubiquitin-proteasome pathway-mediated protein degradation is a common mechanism by which cells regulate intracellular protein levels and functions. Given that TRIM29 acts as an E3 ligase involved in regulating protein ubiquitination, we further investigated the relationship between TRIM29 and the ubiquitination of IκBα protein.

Our results showed that overexpression of TRIM29 promoted the ubiquitination of IκBα protein in pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 5A). In contrast, treatment with Sh-TRIM29 reduced the ubiquitination level of IκBα. Using mutant ubiquitin constructs, we observed that IκBα protein was polyubiquitinated in a K48-dependent manner (Fig. 5B). To further confirm the ubiquitination pattern of IκBα, we used Ub-K63R and Ub-K48R plasmids. Consistently, our data demonstrated that IκBα was polyubiquitinated by Ub-K63R but not by Ub-K48R (Fig. 5C).

IκBα contains 9 lysine residues. We predicted potential ubiquitination sites in its protein sequence using UbiNet2.0 (cuhk.edu.cn), and 5 of these lysine residues were identified as potential ubiquitination targets (Table S8). Among them, the Lys21 and Lys22 sites exhibited the highest prediction scores. Previous studies have reported that ubiquitination of IκBα at either Lys21 or Lys22 is crucial for its ubiquitination-dependent proteasomal degradation. We therefore generated Lys21R and Lys22R mutants of IκBα by replacing the lysine residues at Lys21 or Lys22 with arginine, respectively. IP assay results showed that the Lys21R and Lys22R mutations partially impaired TRIM29-regulated ubiquitination of IκBα. We further constructed the IκBαLys21/22R double mutant (harboring mutations at both Lys21 and Lys22). In this double mutant, TRIM29-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of IκBα were almost completely abrogated (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results indicate that the Lys21 and Lys22 sites in IκBα are indispensable for TRIM29-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of IκBα.

To examine whether TRIM29 directly binds to IκBα, we first verified their co-localization by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 5E). We then performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays using cell lysates prepared from HEK293 cells (Fig. 5F) and pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 5G) transiently transfected with Flag-tagged TRIM29 and His-tagged IκBα expression vectors. Co-IP with either anti-Flag or anti-His antibody detected both Flag-tagged TRIM29 and His-tagged IκBα, indicating that these two tagged proteins interact with each other in HEK293 cells. The endogenous physical interaction between TRIM29 and IκBα was further confirmed by co-IP assays using pancreatic cancer cell lysates (Fig. 5H).

To identify the functional domains mediating their interaction, we predicted the binding sites between TRIM29 and IκBα using the ITScorePP function of HDOCK software. Based on their structural composition, we generated a series of deletion mutants of TRIM29 and IκBα (Fig. 5I). Of note, the electrophoretic migration differences of these mutants were not simply determined by molecular weight, but by protein conformation, SDS-binding efficiency, post-translational modifications, and electrophoretic separation effects. The results showed that the BB2 domain of TRIM29 is essential for its binding to IκBα (Fig. 5J), while the 1–109 amino acid domain of IκBα is responsible for interacting with TRIM29 (Fig. 5K). These findings suggest that TRIM29 promotes the progression of human pancreatic cancer by facilitating proteasome-mediated degradation of IκBα—a negative regulator of the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Additionally, the level of TRIM29 in pancreatic cancer cells affected the phosphorylation levels of IKKα/β, IκBα, and P65, implying that TRIM29 may play a critical role in the phosphorylation and activation of these proteins. To test this, we performed co-IP assays using an anti-TRIM29 antibody to determine whether these proteins bind to TRIM29 in pancreatic cancer cell lines. The results indicate that TRIM29 and IKKγ exhibit mutual binding interactions, whereas no mutual binding interactions were observed with IKKα/β (Figure S4E). Further studies revealed that the level of K63-linked polyubiquitination of IKKγ is positively correlated with the protein level of TRIM29 (Figure S4F). Moreover, compared with the IKKγ-WT group, expression of the IKKγ-K63R markedly inhibited the phosphorylation levels of IKKα/β and IκBα, while concurrently leading to a marked increase in the protein expression level of IκBα (Figure S4G). Collectively, these findings clearly demonstrate that TRIM29 orchestrates the degradation of IκBα through a dual regulatory mechanism: it not only directly binds to IκBα and promotes its K48-linked ubiquitination modification to induce proteasomal degradation, but also interacts with IKKγ and enhances its K63-linked ubiquitination modification to activate the IKK complex, thereby initiating the canonical β-TrCP pathway to mediate IκBα degradation.

IκBα mediates TRIM29-induced EMT and malignant biological functions in pancreatic cancer

To investigate the effect of TRIM29 on the biological behaviors of pancreatic cancer cells via ubiquitination-mediated regulation of IκBα, we first analyzed the impact of TRIM29 itself on the NF-κB pathway and EMT process. Results showed that knockdown of TRIM29 or deletion of the BB2 domain of TRIM29 significantly increased IκBα expression, inhibited NF-κB activation (evidenced by decreased phosphorylation levels of IKKα/β (Ser176/180), IκBα (Ser32/36), and P65 (Ser536)), and suppressed the EMT process (characterized by increased E-cadherin expression and decreased expression of N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail) (Fig. 6A). In contrast, overexpression of TRIM29 exerted the opposite effects. These findings suggest that TRIM29 can inhibit IκBα to activate the NF-κB pathway and EMT process, with the BB2 domain being a critical functional region of TRIM29.

We further analyzed the effect of TRIM29-mediated ubiquitination of IκBα on the NF-κB pathway and EMT process. Overexpression of wild-type IκBα (IκBα-WT) significantly inhibited NF-κB activation (decreased P65 phosphorylation) and the EMT process (increased E-cadherin, decreased N-cadherin, Vimentin, and Snail). This inhibitory effect was reversed by overexpression of full-length TRIM29 (TRIM29-WT) but not by overexpression of TRIM29 lacking the BB2 domain (TRIM29-ΔBB2) (Fig. 6B). Additionally, compared with the IκBα-WT + TRIM29 group, the IκBα-Lys21/22R mutant + TRIM29 group showed significantly suppressed NF-κB pathway activation and EMT process. Collectively, these results indicate that TRIM29 activates the NF-κB pathway and EMT process by ubiquitinating IκBα at the Lys21/22 sites, and the BB2 domain plays an essential role in this process.

Transwell migration and Matrigel invasion assays demonstrated that overexpression of IκBα reduced the migration and invasion abilities of pancreatic cancer cells. Re-expression of TRIM29-WT restored these abilities, while TRIM29-ΔBB2 failed to do so (Fig. 6C-D). Notably, the migration and invasion abilities of the IκBα-Lys21/22R mutant + TRIM29 group were lower than those of the IκBα-WT + TRIM29 group. Scratch-wound healing assays revealed that overexpression of IκBα impaired the wound healing capacity of pancreatic cancer cells; this capacity was restored by TRIM29-WT re-expression but not by TRIM29-ΔBB2 (Fig. 6E). Similarly, the wound healing capacity of the IκBα-Lys21/22R mutant + TRIM29 group was lower than that of the IκBα-WT + TRIM29 group.

In vivo experiments, we found that overexpression of IκBα reduced the proliferation and tumorigenic capacity of orthotopic pancreatic tumors. Re-expression of TRIM29-WT restored tumor proliferation and tumorigenicity, whereas TRIM29-ΔBB2 did not (Fig. 6F). Furthermore, the IκBα-Lys21/22R mutant + TRIM29 group exhibited lower tumor proliferation and tumorigenic capacity compared with the IκBα-WT + TRIM29 group. Taken together, these results demonstrate that TRIM29—with the BB2 domain as its core functional region—promotes the EMT process, as well as the migration, invasion, and tumorigenic abilities of pancreatic cancer cells by ubiquitinating IκBα at the Lys21/22 sites.

The TRIM29-IκBα-EMT axis correlates with prognosis in patients with pancreatic cancer

To further explore the clinical relevance of CXCL5, TRIM29, IκBα, and EMT expression in human pancreatic cancer tissues, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining on tissue microarrays containing 80 pancreatic cancer samples, targeting CXCL5, TRIM29, IκBα, E-cadherin, and α-SMA (Figs. 7A & S5A).

In pancreatic cancer tissues, TRIM29 expression was significantly higher in patients with vascular invasion (Figure S5B-C) and showed a significant negative correlation with IκBα expression (Fig. 7B)—a result consistent with our Western blot findings. However, at the RNA level, data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-PAAD) cohort revealed no significant correlation between TRIM29 and IκBα (Fig. 7C). This suggests that TRIM29 regulates IκBα at the post-translational level rather than the transcriptional level, which aligns with our previous observation that TRIM29 induces IκBα ubiquitination. Additionally, our staining results confirmed the co-localization of TRIM29 and IκBα proteins in pancreatic cancer tissues at the cellular spatial level (Fig. 7D).

Compared with adjacent non-tumor tissues, IκBα protein expression was lower in cancer tissues, with the lowest expression observed in pancreatic cancer tissues with microvascular invasion (MVI+) (Fig. 7E). Moreover, high IκBα protein expression was associated with better OS in patients with pancreatic cancer (Fig. 7F-G).

We also analyzed the distribution of EMT and CXCL5-marked EMT in cancer tissues versus adjacent non-tumor tissues, as well as their associations with MVI, TRIM29, and IκBα in pancreatic cancer. Results showed that EMT—especially CXCL5-marked EMT—was more highly expressed in cancer tissues than in adjacent non-tumor tissues, with significantly higher expression in MVI+ pancreatic cancer tissues (Fig. 7H). Furthermore, EMT was more pronounced in the high-TRIM29 group and low-IκBα group (Fig. 7I); notably, CXCL5-marked EMT exhibited a stronger correlation with TRIM29 and IκBα than general EMT (Fig. 7J). This indicates that TRIM29 and IκBα primarily regulate the EMT process in the CXCL5-expressing subset of pancreatic cancer cells. More importantly, both EMT and CXCL5-marked EMT showed a strong correlation with the clinical prognosis of pancreatic cancer (Figs. 1J and 2K).

In summary, these results support the hypothesis that TRIM29 promotes EMT in pancreatic cancer by facilitating IκBα protein degradation, thereby enhancing the invasive capacity of pancreatic cancer cells and contributing to poor clinical prognosis.

Discussion

Identifying signs of early invasion in pancreatic cancer and exploring the initiating mechanisms underlying early invasion are crucial for improving surgical resectability and preventing distant metastasis. MVI is recognized as a key marker of early invasion and malignant phenotypes in pancreatic cancer, with a close correlation to patient prognosis [30]. In this study, we first characterized the cellular infiltration status of the microenvironment in MVI+ pancreatic cancer using spatial protein profiling and transcriptome analysis, and identified a strong association between EMT status and MVI. Indeed, numerous studies have reported the impact of EMT on the invasive capacity of pancreatic cancer and other tumors, suggesting that EMT endows tumor cells with early migratory and metastatic potential. However, the role of EMT in early microvascular invasion of pancreatic cancer remains underexplored. Our spatial protein analysis not only characterized the state of infiltrating cells in the microenvironment of MVI+ pancreatic cancer tissues and predicted intercellular communication but also, for the first time, revealed a spatial density-dependent relationship between EMT status and MVI invasion in pancreatic cancer through spatial analysis centered on CD31 + microvessels.

A key feature of malignant tumor tissues is their heterogeneity, which includes heterogeneity of tumor cells and heterogeneity in microenvironmental composition, which complicates tumor treatment [31]. To date, the heterogeneity of EMT status in pancreatic cancer cells and its impact on microvascular invasion remain uninvestigated. To further elucidate the EMT status of tumor cells in MVI+ pancreatic cancer at the single-cell level, we performed scRNA-seq on MVI + and MVI- pancreatic cancer tissues. Through EMT scoring and analysis of cellular developmental trajectories, we characterized distinct EMT states in pancreatic cancer cell clusters and identified the CXCL5 + EPC cluster, which exhibits the strongest EMT characteristics in pancreatic cancer tissues. These findings were validated using pancreatic cancer tissue microarrays.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in the CXCL5 + EPC cluster highlighted the critical role of ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation in this subset. It is well established that the specificity of the ubiquitin conjugation system arises from the direct binding of E3 ligases to their substrates. TRIM29, a ubiquitin E3 ligase and one of the markers of the CXCL5 + EPC cluster, attracted our attention. Previous studies have shown that TRIM29 promotes immune dysregulation and tumor drug resistance [23, 26, 32–33], but its role in regulating EMT in pancreatic cancer has not been reported. SCENIC analysis of single-cell samples combined with transcription factor database predictions, followed by ChIP assays, demonstrated that ZBTB7B is responsible for the transcription of TRIM29 in pancreatic cancer. Additionally, protein and RNA analyses of human pancreatic cancer samples confirmed a significant correlation between ZBTB7B and TRIM29. Subsequent transcriptome sequencing and Western blot analysis of EMT markers in pancreatic cancer cells with perturbed TRIM29 expression confirmed that TRIM29 promotes EMT progression in pancreatic cancer. In vitro Transwell migration, invasion, and scratch-wound healing assays, along with in vivo orthotopic xenograft, further demonstrated that TRIM29 enhances the migratory and invasive capacities of pancreatic cancer cells. The promotional effects of ZBTB7B and TRIM29 on the malignant progression of pancreatic cancer were also validated in human pancreatic cancer samples.

The NF-κB signaling pathway is involved in regulating a wide range of biological processes in tumors, including immune responses, cell proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance [34]. Targeting the NF-κB signaling pathway is emerging as a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer. To date, research on NF-κB as a potential therapeutic target has primarily focused on non-specific compounds and inhibitors of the NF-κB pathway [34, 35]. However, NF-κB inhibitors can trigger widespread adverse reactions and side effects [36]. Therefore, specific inhibition of upstream factors of various mediators in the NF-κB signaling pathway may be more suitable for tumor therapy. In this study, by integrating functional enrichment analysis results of CXCL5 + EPCs and transcriptome sequencing data of pancreatic cancer cells with TRIM29 knockdown, we found that TRIM29-mediated regulation of EMT in pancreatic cancer cells may depend on the NF-κB signaling pathway. Western blot analysis of key proteins in the NF-κB pathway in pancreatic cancer cells with perturbed TRIM29 expression further confirmed the regulatory role of TRIM29 in the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Mechanistically, TRIM29 interacts with IκBα in a manner dependent on the BB2 domain of TRIM29 and the 1-109aa domain of IκBα, leading to the formation of K48-linked ubiquitin conjugates of IκBα. As a key negative regulator of the NF-κB signaling pathway, ubiquitinated IκBα undergoes proteasome-mediated degradation, ultimately promoting the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. More specifically, TRIM29-mediated ubiquitination of IκBα primarily occurs at the Lys21/22 residues, which constitutes the core post-translational regulatory mode of IκBα by TRIM29. Beyond this direct post-translational regulation of IκBα, we further identified that TRIM29 exerts a synergistic regulatory effect on the upstream IKK complex, which clarifies the reduced p-IKKα/β levels upon TRIM29 knockdown observed in our study: TRIM29 directly binds to the IKKγ regulatory subunit and promotes its K63-linked polyubiquitination, which in turn enhances the phosphorylation and activation of IKKα/β. This upstream regulatory event acts synergistically with TRIM29-mediated K48-linked ubiquitination of IκBα, collectively amplifying the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway to drive EMT and tumor invasion. Notably, the BB2 domain of TRIM29 acts as a bifunctional domain that mediates both of the above regulatory processes: it is required for both IκBα binding and IKKγ interaction. While TRIM29 regulates IκBα predominantly at the post-translational level, transcriptional downregulation of NFKBIA represents an independent, complementary mechanism for IκBα protein reduction in PDAC. Low NFKBIA mRNA expression correlates with significantly shortened OS in TCGA-PAAD patients, which acts synergistically with TRIM29-mediated IκBα degradation to drive sustained NF-κB activation, more aggressive MVI phenotypes and worse clinical outcomes.

In summary, our study reveals the cellular ecosystem in MVI+ pancreatic cancer from multiple perspectives and identifies the strong EMT characteristics of the CXCL5 + EPC cluster. We further demonstrate that the E3 ligase TRIM29 activates the NF-κB signaling pathway by promoting K48-linked ubiquitination of IκBα, driving EMT progression and ultimately enhancing the migratory and invasive capacities of pancreatic cancer cells. Our findings deepen the understanding of early invasive phenotypes in pancreatic cancer and suggest that pharmacological inhibition of TRIM29 E3 ligase activity or blocking the interaction between TRIM29 and IκBα may represent promising strategies to curb early invasion and improve resectability in pancreatic cancer.
