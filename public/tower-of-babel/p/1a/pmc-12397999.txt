Physical Performance Tests in 8008 Competitive Youth Tennis Players—A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis of Normative Values
Authors: Sebastian Axman, Martin B Stausholm, Nicola Reiner Volk, Alexander Ferrauti, S P Magnusson, Christian Couppé
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNormative values for physical performance tests (PTESTs) offer insights into athletes' profiles on strengths and weaknesses to enhance performance and mitigate injury risks. However, such data for youth tennis players have not previously been compiled in a systematic review. We conducted a systematic review of 17 popular PTESTs covering speed, agility, strength, and power in healthy, competitive youth tennis players under the age of 8–18 years. A search for eligible studies indexed in the PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases was conducted on the 26th of March 2023. Forty‐five studies (8008 players) were included and meta‐analyzed. The analysis accounted for age, sex, and the use of dominant/nondominant sides. Many of the results were associated with a high level of statistical heterogeneity likely due to variations in test procedures. Data for players under the age of 8–10 years were generally lacking, and males were studied more than females. Higher age, male gender, and the use of the dominant side were generally associated with higher performance levels. In male players, the internal rotation of the dominant shoulder appeared to decrease with age, which may lead to injury. The review offers preliminary references for coaches, clinicians, and researchers to identify players' strengths and weaknesses, potentially aiding the enhancement of performance and the prevention of injuries.Keywords: competitive, normative values, physical performance tests, systematic review, tennis players, youth

Introduction

Modern competitive tennis has evolved from its traditional roots as a predominantly technically skill‐based sport, focused on the serve‐and‐volley technique, into a more dynamic and explosive discipline characterized by greater hitting power and speed, as well as longer baseline rallies featuring numerous changes of direction (Luna‐Villouta et al. 2021; Moya‐Ramon et al., 2020). Tennis has also seen increased participation from an early age, particularly under 12 years old, which underlines the importance of testing players' physical feats both for performance enhancement and for identifying factors associated with injury risk. Youth tennis presents an additional set of challenges, as players compete within an age span of 2 years. Naturally, this can lead to differences in biological, physiological, and cognitive aspects (Kramer et al. 2017). Baxter‐Jones found that 85% of British youth elite tennis players were born in the first half of the year (Baxter‐Jones 1995).

When children undergo puberty, particularly during their peak height velocity, which occurs at around the age of 12 years in girls and 14 years in boys, there is a rapid increase in body dimensions, which may temporarily impair neuromuscular capability and negatively affecting coordination (Lopez‐Valenciano et al. 2023). However, the increase in muscle strength and power associated with maturation more than compensates for these issues (Luna‐Villouta et al. 2021). A tennis match involves intermittent high‐intensity efforts over an extended period (several hours), underlining the importance of well‐developed aerobic fitness and endurance, as well as anaerobic fitness characteristics, such as speed, agility, strength, and power (Cooke et al. 2011; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2015; M. S. Kovacs 2007) (Figure 1). This development of physical performance aspects has been proposed as a prerequisite for success in tennis (Hernández‐Davó et al. 2021). Although youth players require age‐appropriate training, they still face similar movement demands as senior players during rallies, highlighting the importance for youth players to develop motor coordination alongside the fitness characteristics to support both current neuromuscular control and long‐term progression toward the adult elite field. Kovalchik and Reid found that speed and agility were greater, whereas strength and power were lower in junior players compared to senior professional players. The largest differences in physical demands between junior and senior male players at Grand Slams can be attributed to the best‐of‐five set format in professional men's matches, compared with the best‐of‐three format used in junior competitions (Kovalchik and Reid 2017).

Overview of physical components for tennis performance (M. S. Kovacs 2007).

Lambrich and Muehlbauer hypothesized that elite tennis players (Davis Cup, professional, and division I players) would surpass their sub‐elite counterparts (competitive, regional, and division II players) in terms of physical performance (Lambrich and Muehlbauer 2022). They confirmed the hypothesis through a systematic review and noted that the greatest differences were lower extremity muscle power, endurance, and agility (Lambrich and Muehlbauer 2022). Therefore, training programs for sub‐elite tennis players should focus more on increasing physical performance of the lower extremity.

Vertical jump tests, including bilateral and unilateral countermovement jumps, are especially recommended to assess explosive lower body power as these movements closely mirror the vertical demands of the sport (Moya‐Ramon et al., 2020; Hayes et al. 2021). Medicine ball throws engage the kinetic chain, transferring forces from the lower to the upper body, similar to tennis‐specific actions (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2017; Bogataj et al. 2020). The strong correlation between sprint time and vertical jump emphasizes the importance of lower body strength and power for explosive actions on the tennis court (Sato et al. 2017; Tubez et al. 2017; McMahon 2017). The 5‐0‐5 agility test resembles the typical movements in tennis, more specifically the movements of an acute end‐range stroke and midcourt recovery (Moya‐Ramon et al., 2020; Akşit and Nalcakan 2016). The spider run test is a commonly used agility test specifically designed for tennis, as it involves multiple quick runs and direction changes across the dimensions of the tennis court (M. S. Kovacs 2007; McMahon 2017; Barber et al. 2016). The T‐test provides a highly reliable measure of agility and lower body speed and may be used to differentiate competitive from amateur level athletes (M. S. Kovacs 2007). Handgrip strength, measured using a dynamometer, is a straightforward test to evaluate grip strength and detect asymmetries between the dominant and nondominant hands (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2022; Anicic et al. 2023). Handgrip strength, although more isolated in nature, is a relevant physical attribute in tennis and has been associated with national ranking among youth tennis players (Ulbricht et al. 2016).

Currently, there is no global overview or clear consensus on the testing procedures for physical performance tests (PTESTs) or their respective normative data for each age group and sex in competitive youth tennis players. Such overview can offer insights into players' profiles on strengths and weaknesses, potentially enhancing physical performance and mitigating injury risks. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review of popular and reliable PTESTs among competitive youth tennis players.

Methods

Study Eligibility Criteria

We solely included studies with competitive tennis players, defined as actively participating in matches, aged between U8 and U18 years with results of minimum one of the following 17 PTESTs: countermovement jump, countermovement jump unilateral, horizontal jump, grip strength, medicine ball overhead throw, medicine ball forehand throw, medicine ball backhand throw, ball throw, serve velocity, 5 m sprint, 10 m sprint, 20 m sprint, 5‐0‐5 agility test, spider run, T‐test, hexagon test, and shoulder flexibility. Studies with injured players were excluded. The literature search was restricted to English and Nordic languages.

Search Strategy

A search for eligible studies indexed in the PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Cochrane Library databases was conducted on the 26th of March 2023. The search strings contained the terms tennis, youth, and performance test along with their synonyms. Controlled vocabulary, incorporating MeSH terms for PubMed and Cochrane Library, was applied in the search. The search strings are included in the Supporting Information S1. The search was extended by screening the reference lists of the included studies. The selection of studies was handled in a two‐person procedure; one reviewer (S.A.) selected the studies and subsequently another reviewer (M.B.S.) checked the work for correctness, that is, the eligibility of the included studies and the studies excluded by full‐text evaluation. Any disagreements with the study selection were resolved by discussion.

Data Extraction and Analysis

The data collection was handled in a two‐person procedure. One reviewer (S.A.) entered all the data in Excel sheets and subsequently another reviewer (M.B.S.) carefully checked all the work for correctness. Any disagreements in the data extraction were resolved by discussion. Extracted data included the first author's name, year of publication, country, the competition level, sample size, age, and sex of participants, anthropometric measures, and PTESTs used. For each specific PTEST, the most common test protocol was described, as well as deviations from the procedures.

To decrease the risk of greater biological differences at 2‐year chronological age groups, players were categorized into age groups of only 1 year, for example, age 10.0–10.9 in U11, instead of the age gap of 2 years in which they typically compete, for example, age 10.0–11.9 in U12. The age‐ and sex‐specific results of the PTESTs, prior to any intervention, were meta‐analyzed separately. Standard errors for meta‐analysis were estimated from standard deviation and number of players using the formula: standard error = standard deviation/square root (n). All the meta‐analyses were conducted using the DerSimonian and Laird random effects model, weighing the results of the individual studies relatively even when statistical heterogeneity is present (Higgins 2011). The mean difference method was chosen to maintain the original measurement scales of the PTESTs (Higgins 2011). The meta‐analyses were performed by M.B.S. using Stata version 17.

Results

Study Selection

Figure 2 illustrates the study selection process. The database search and screening of reference lists yielded 1582 records. A total of 45 studies (8008 players) were included and meta‐analyzed. The reasons for exclusion during the full‐text evaluation were no sex‐specific data (22 studies), no relevant PTEST (14 studies), age of players (10 studies), no relevant outcome (3 studies), language (2 studies), amateur playing level (1 study), and duplicate player data (1 study) (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Flow chart illustrating the study selection process.

Study Characteristics

The number of players in the studies ranged from 12 to 2461 (Table 1). There were 18 different countries represented and two studies with international players (Figure 3). The most frequently represented countries were Spain, Turkey, and Germany with twelve, six, and four studies, respectively. A total of nine countries were represented by a single study: Australia, Belgium, Chile, Cyprus, Hungary, Israel, Japan, South Africa, and USA. Twenty‐one studies focused exclusively on male players, whereas five studies focused exclusively on female players. The ages of the players ranged from U8 to U18. The most popular age groups studied were U14, U13, and U16, with 15, 13, and 12 studies, respectively. Twenty‐six studies had a sample size of more than 50 players, 15 studies included > 100 players, and four studies included > 500 players. Twenty studies were published between 2019 and 2023, 19 studies were published between 2010 and 2018, five studies were published between 2000 and 2009, and one study was published in 1992.

Overview and Measures

Thirty‐nine studies included at least one PTEST of strength and power, 34 included at least one PTEST of speed and agility, and four studies included shoulder flexibility testing. The most frequently used PTESTs for strength and power were the countermovement jump, serve velocity, and grip strength, appearing in 29, 15, and 12 studies, respectively. The most frequently used PTESTs for speed and agility were the 5‐10‐20 m sprint, 5‐0‐5 agility test, and spider run, featured in thirty‐one, eight, and six studies, respectively. Only shoulder internal rotation (IR) and external rotation (ER) range of motion (ROM) were assessed in the flexibility category.

A natural age‐ and maturity‐related progression in PTEST scores is evident from the linear trendlines in Figures 4 and 5, which illustrate the most popular PTEST for strength and power, and speed and agility, respectively. In the countermovement jump test, no difference between male and female was detected until U14 and older, where the male players all performed better than the female players. In the 20 m sprint test, male players consistently achieved faster times than female players across the different age groups, from U8 (M: 4.31 s ± 0.37, n = 193; F: 4.42 s ± 0.23, n = 63) to U18 (M: 3.08 s ± 0.11, n = 81; F: 3.38 s ± 0.19, n = 39), with the greatest difference occurring at U16 with 14%. When examining shoulder ROM, it is evident that male players had a greater ER compared to IR across all age groups, particularly in U14, on both the dominant side (ER: 109° ± 8 to IR: 42° ± 6, n = 21) and the nondominant side (ER: 102° ± 9 to IR: 53° ± 6, n = 21). Furthermore, the dominant side had a greater ER than the nondominant side, whereas the nondominant side had a greater IR compared to the dominant side. As the players matured, their ER increased and their IR decreased disproportionately, reducing their total ROM on the dominant side from 162° to 151° in U9 to U14 for males and from 213° to 174° in U13 to U14 for females, respectively. Description of PTESTs

The most frequent individual PTESTs procedures are described below, with a closing remark on any apparent deviations. The German Tennis Federation protocols were the most commonly used testing protocols (Fett et al. 2020; Ulbricht et al. 2013).

Countermovement Jump

The countermovement jump is performed without an arm swing on a contact platform. The player performs two maximal countermovement jumps interspersed with 45 s of passive recovery. The highest height attained in the two jumps is noted (Ulbricht et al. 2013). We noted that in eight studies, the players performed the jump on an infrared light mat (Kramer et al. 2017; Hernández‐Davó et al. 2021; Girard and Millet 2009; Kramer et al. 2016, 2021, 2016; Pardos‐Mainer et al. 2017; Parpa et al. 2022), and in two studies, the players performed the jump to reach a yard stick (Hayes et al. 2021; Myburgh et al. 2016).

Horizontal Jump

For the horizontal jump, the player is standing at a line with their feet together, facing the jumping direction. They are instructed to perform a double leg jump with an arm swing in a straight line. The distance between the line and the player's heel is measured. Among two efforts, the best performance is recorded to the nearest cm (Fett et al. 2020). We noted that in four studies, the players jumped with feet together (Luna‐Villouta et al. 2021; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2016; Yildiz et al. 2019; Pardos‐Mainer et al. 2017), and in one study, the players jumped with feet at shoulder width (Moya‐Ramon et al., 2020).

Grip Strength

Maximal hand grip strength is measured with a hydraulic hand dynamometer. Dominant and nondominant hand grip strength tests are performed in a standing position, with the elbow extended, and the arm positioned with the dynamometer parallel to the players' side. The player is asked to perform a maximal voluntary contraction, squeezing the dynamometer as hard as possible, for 3 seconds. The maximum force, measured in kilograms, achieved among two trials for each side is recorded (Fett et al. 2020). We noted that in three studies, the test was conducted in a sitting position (Girard and Millet 2009; Chapelle et al. 2022; Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2017).

Serve Velocity

A radar gun is used to measure first‐serve speed. The radar is positioned on the center of the baseline, 4 m behind the server, aligned with the approximate height of ball contact and pointing down the center of the court. The right‐handed player serves to the left serve box (from the right) and the left‐handed player serves to the right serve box (from the left). After a brief warm‐up consisting of serves with increasing velocities, eight first serves down the “T” (center line) are performed. The peak velocity of eight serves is recorded (Fett et al. 2020). We noted that four studies performed 3 × 10 serves in either the advantage court or the deuce court (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2020, 2018, 2019; Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2017), two studies performed five serves in either the advantage court or the deuce court (Sogut et al. 2019; Ayala et al. 2016), two studies performed six serves in both the advantage court and the deuce court (Colomar et al. 2020; Terraza‐Rebollo and Baiget 2021), one study performed ten serves (Gelen et al. 2012), and one study performed three serves (Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2015). It is important to clarify that some studies reported the peak serve velocity (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2020; Meckel 2015; Colomar et al. 2020; Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2017), whereas other studies reported the average serve velocity (Söğüt et al. 2019; Kramer, Huijgen, et al. 2016; Ayala et al. 2016; Terraza‐Rebollo and Baiget 2021; Gelen et al. 2012).

Medicine Ball Throw

For the overhead medicine ball throws, the player stands at a line with the feet side by side and slightly apart and facing the direction to which the ball is to be thrown, holding a 2 kg medicine ball. The ball is brought back behind the head and then thrown vigorously forward as far as possible without crossing the line. For the forehand and backhand medicine ball throw, the player stands sideways to the starting line and simulates a forehand and backhand stroke tossing the ball as far as possible without crossing the line. For all medicine ball throws, the distance from the line to the point where the ball lands is measured. The best performance, among two efforts, is recorded to the nearest 5 cm (Ulbricht et al. 2013). Two studies using a medicine ball with a different weight (1 kg (Myburgh et al. 2016) and 3 kg (Sarabia et al. 2015)) and one study with an undisclosed weight (Kovačević 2013) were excluded from the analysis.

Ball Throw

The player holds a tennis ball, weighing 56.0–59.4 g as defined by the International Tennis Federation, in the dominant hand, assumes a serve stance, and throws the ball overarm as far as possible in a straight line while keeping both feet on the floor. The distance between the starting line and the point of impact is measured, with the best of two attempts recorded (Kramer et al. 2017). We noted that two studies used a ball weighing 80 g (Siener 2021; Siener et al. 2021), two studies used a ball weighing 200 g (Kramer et al. 2016, 2017), and one study used a ball weighing 103 g (Dobos and Tóth 2021).

5‐10‐20 m Sprint

Time during a 20‐m sprint in a straight line, with 5 and 10 m split times, is measured by means of single beam photocell gates placed 1 m above the ground level. Each sprint is initiated from an individually chosen standing position, 50 cm behind the photocell gate, which starts a digital timer. The player performs two maximal 20‐m sprints interspersed with three minutes of passive recovery. The fastest time achieved is retained (Ulbricht et al. 2013). We noted that 12 studies started right behind the starting line (Kramer et al. 2017; Akşit and Nalcakan 2016; Girard and Millet 2009; Kramer, Huijgen, et al. 2016; Sogut et al. 2019; Kramer et al. 2021; Meckel 2015; Kramer, Valente‐Dos‐Santos, et al. 2016; Munivrana et al. 2015; Chapelle et al. 2023; Koya et al. 2022; Tsoulfa 2016) and three studies started 30 cm behind the starting line (Myburgh et al. 2016, 2020; Siener et al. 2021).

Spider Run

Five tennis balls are placed at the intersections of the single sidelines with the baseline and the service line, as well as on the “T” of the court. The player starts at the center of the baseline and is instructed to run as fast as possible in a clockwise path to retrieve each ball, one at a time, returning each ball onto a 30–45 cm quadrangle‐shaped area drawn just behind the center part of the baseline, before moving on to the next. The test concludes when the last ball is placed in the quadrangle area. The fastest time of two attempts is recorded using photocell gates at the starting point, with a two‐minute rest between attempts (Dobos and Tóth 2021). We noted that two studies performed the test in a counterclockwise path (E. P. Roetert et al. 1992; Huggins 2017) and one study finished with a 3‐m sprint behind the baseline (Huggins 2017).

T‐Test

To set up this test, a cone is placed 10 m from the starting line and two additional cones are placed 5 m to either side of the first cone. Photocell gates are positioned at the start/finish line. The player is instructed to run as fast as possible to touch the first cone with their right hand, then side shuffle 5 m to the left to touch the far‐left cone with their left hand, side shuffle 10 m to the right to touch the far‐right cone with their right hand, side shuffle 5 m to the left back to the center cone, and finish by running backward through the gates at the start/finish line (Sogut et al. 2019). Two studies performed the test with distances of 10 yards (9.14 m) and 5 yards (4.57 m) instead of 10 and 5 m (Kilit and Arslan 2019; Kilit et al. 2019). We noted that one study started 50 cm behind the starting the line (Kilit et al. 2019) and one study finished with a forward sprint toward the start/finish (Hernández‐Davó et al. 2021).

Hexagon Test

The player stands facing forward in the center of a hexagon, each side measuring 60 cm with 120° angles. With feet together and hips facing the same forward direction throughout the test, the player is told to hop forward and backward over each of the six sides of the hexagon in a clockwise direction. A penalty of 0.5 s is added if the player touches the line, and 1.0 s is added if the player fails to follow the correct hopping sequence. The fastest time in two attempts with a two‐minute rest between is recorded (Dobos and Tóth 2021). We noted that two studies did not include penalties (Söğüt et al. 2019; E. P. Roetert et al. 1992).

5‐0‐5 Agility Test

The player stands with their preferred foot behind the line and is asked to accelerate as fast as possible until reaching a line placed at 5 m, performs a rapid 180° pivot using either the right or left foot, and then accelerates back to the starting line. Two trials are performed for each leg, with a 2‐min rest between attempts. The fastest time is noted using photocell gates (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2022). We noted that two studies started 50 cm behind the starting line (Kozinc and Šarabon 2021; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2023) and one study started 1 m behind the starting line (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2020).

Shoulder Flexibility Test

The player lies in a supine position with the scapula stabilized and the shoulder abducted to 90°. The examiner passively rotates the shoulder externally and internally to its maximum capacity, while a second examiner measures the ROM angle with a goniometer (Chandler et al. 1990). We noted that four studies used an inclinometer (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2020, 2019; Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2017; Cools et al. 2014) and that four studies had no mention of a second examiner (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2013; Gillet et al. 2018; Busra Cigercioglu et al. 2021; Chang et al. 2018).

Benchmarks

Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. provided benchmark values for U12, U14, U16, and U18 male and female competitive tennis players regarding the PTESTs: countermovement jump, grip strength, serve velocity, medicine ball overhead throw, and 10 and 20 m sprint. They divided these values into four different categories: needs improvement, average, good, and excellent (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2014). M. Kovacs et al. established reference values with the same four categories regarding grip strength, medicine ball overhead throw, spider run, hexagon test, and shoulder flexibility (P. Roetert 2007; M. Kovacs 2016). Akşit and Nalcakan produced percentile norms for U10 and U11 male and female competitive youth tennis players in ball throw and 5m sprint. At intervals of 10%, they displayed data from their PTESTs from the worst performers at the 10th percentile to the best performers at the 90th percentile (Akşit and Nalcakan 2016). E. P. Roetert et al. established percentile norms in an equal fashion for U12, U14, and U16 male and female competitive youth tennis players in the spider run test and hexagon test (E. P. Roetert et al. 1995). Ulbricht et al. provided a percentile table for U16 male competitive youth tennis players in anthropometric measures, countermovement jump, grip strength, serve velocity, medicine ball overhead throw, medicine ball forehand throw, and medicine ball backhand throw (Ulbricht 2015). These benchmark values are the foundation of what our review is highlighting.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first global summary of normative values for PTESTs among competitive youth tennis players. Our review included 17 popular PTESTs, covering strength and power, speed and agility, and flexibility. We noted a lack of normative data for U8–U10 players and that males were studied more often than females. As expected, higher age, male sex, and the use of the dominant side were generally associated with greater performance.

Many of the PTESTs were conducted with considerable variability across studies, emphasizing the need for a clear consensus or strict guideline for test procedures. Still, this review offers initial insights for coaches, clinicians, and researchers to identify players' strengths and weaknesses, potentially aiding in performance enhancement and injury prevention. For the 5‐0‐5 agility test, some studies used a start position 50 cm behind the line, whereas others used 1 m. For consistency, we recommend starting 50 cm behind the line. Similarly, for the shoulder flexibility test, either a goniometer or an inclinometer was used. We recommend choosing the device based on personal preferences. Generally, we recommend adhering to the test protocols established by the German Tennis Federation (Fett et al. 2020; Ulbricht et al. 2013) as these were the most commonly used procedures.

General Considerations

The variations in test procedures likely contributed to the statistical heterogeneity observed in our meta‐analysis. Therefore, we opted to use the random effects meta‐analysis model. Only in some of the included studies, the players were motivated verbally to exert maximal efforts (Akşit and Nalcakan 2016; Cigercioglu et al. 2021). In some of the studies, players performed the tests on a clay court (Luna‐Villouta et al. 2021), whereas in others, the tests were performed on a hard court (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2016) or a synthetic‐surface tennis court (Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2015). Familiarization with the PTESTs prior to testing was specified in some studies (Dobos and Tóth 2021; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2016; Pardos‐Mainer et al. 2017; Chapelle et al. 2023; Parpa et al. 2022; Colomar et al. 2020; Gallo‐Salazar et al. 2017; Terraza‐Rebollo and Baiget 2021; Madruga‐Parera et al. 2020), but not all (Moya‐Ramon et al., 2020; Akşit and Nalcakan 2016; Yildiz et al. 2019; Koya et al. 2022; Gelen et al. 2012; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2023; Novak et al. 2023). Some of the studies included a 3–5‐min warm‐up prior to the tests (Hayes et al. 2021; Kilit and Arslan 2019), whereas others implemented a 10–20‐min warm‐up (Dobos and Tóth 2021; Söğüt et al. 2019; Ayala et al. 2016; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2013; Chapelle et al. 2023). Some studies allowed two attempts (Moya‐Ramon et al., 2020; Siener 2021) and others three attempts (Dobos and Tóth 2021). The allocated resting time in between attempts also varied, some studies allowing 3–5 min (Akşit and Nalcakan 2016; Chapelle et al. 2023), whereas others did not define the duration or simply indicating that complete rest was assured (Siener 2021). Such factors should be considered in the interpretation of the normative values. It is important to note that normative values based on single studies as well as normative values based on pooled data from ≤ 5 studies with high statistical heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 75%) may lack external validity (Jackson and Turner 2017).

Strength and Power Tests

Strength and power tests were the most frequently performed assessments in the included studies. These tests exhibited the largest percentage increases in performance with age, particularly in males. For the countermovement jump results in females, the wide confidence interval in the U17 age category complicates interpretation, suggesting that the true performance value likely lies between those of U16 and U18. The same assumption is reasonable to make for the two widest confidence intervals in the analysis of horizontal jump in the male category (ages U12 and U16). In the analyses of grip strength in males aged U12 and U14–17, it should be noted that most of the results came from a single author group, which can explain why the levels of statistical heterogeneity are lower than what we observed for many of the other PTESTs (Ulbricht et al. 2016; Ulbricht 2015). As for both medicine ball throw and serve velocity, the results were generally based on single studies or associated with a high level of statistical heterogeneity. Finally, in the ball throw analysis, females in the U15 age category appeared to outperform those aged U17. However, these results were based on very few observations and are unlikely to represent the true trends. Similarly, the data for males in this test were sparse, limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Speed and Agility Tests

The analyses of 5‐10‐20 m sprint tests revealed substantial performance improvements with age among players aged U8–U11, but minimal gains beyond U11. This pattern was consistent in both genders. However, the data for players aged U8–U10 were sparse, limiting the robustness of conclusions for these age groups. Notably, the analysis showed that male and female players aged U17 were slower to complete the 5‐m sprint test compared to players of younger ages. This anomaly was attributed to a single outlier in the U17 groups, with completion times of 1.4 s for males and 1.5 s for females (Munivrana et al. 2015). The reason for this outlier remains unclear. The data for the remaining speed and agility tests were very sparse, limiting the generalizability of the findings.

Shoulder Flexibility

Normative data on shoulder ROM were limited, particularly for female players. However, a consistent trend was observed across individual studies indicating that in male players, only IR ROM in the dominant shoulder decreased with age, which is in line with previous studies (Kibler et al. 1996).

In overhead athletes, posterior shoulder stiffness is a common adaptation on the dominant side. This stiffness is thought to result from cumulative loads on the posterior shoulder capsule and rotator cuff during the deceleration phase of the throwing motion, maybe leading to microtrauma. This adaptation may contribute to subacromial pain syndrome and labral pathology (Cools et al. 2015).

Shoulder IR asymmetry of ≥ 18° between the dominant and nondominant sides has been associated with an increased risk of injury in overhead‐throwing athletes (Cools et al. 2015). Cigercioglu et al. assessed 21 male and 21 female Turkish tennis players aged 14 and found that 46% had a shoulder IR deficit of more than 13° (Cigercioglu et al. 2021), highlighting the importance of early detection of such asymmetries. Research by Gillet et al. (2017) on shoulder ROM adaptations in 67 tennis players aged 7–13 found that increased shoulder ER did not fully compensate for decreased IR, leading to a reduced total arc of motion on the dominant side compared to the nondominant side (Gillet et al. 2017). In contrast, Wilk et al. found that although deficits in IR or total rotational ROM were not directly linked to shoulder injury or surgery, a deficit in the external rotation ROM of less than 5° on the dominant side significantly increased the likelihood of shoulder injury by 2.2 times and surgery by 4.0 times in professional baseball pitchers (Wilk et al. 2015). Nonpathological IR ROM deficits may progress to pathological changes in the glenohumeral joint, necessitating early intervention (Rose and Noonan 2018).

To prevent changes in shoulder mobility, stretching, mobilization, and strengthening of the shoulder girdle are recommended, particularly early in an athlete's career. However, it remains unclear whether these physiological adaptations are necessary for performance or if they contribute to injury risk (Gillet et al. 2018; Rose and Noonan 2018).

In our review, normative data on shoulder ROM were generally limited, particularly for female players. However, a consistent trend was observed across individual studies indicating that in male players, only IR ROM in the dominant shoulder decreased with age.

Limitations and Future Research Directions

This review did not include tennis experience or training exposure due to the difficulty in accurately comparing data across varying intervals. Instead, the competitive level was used to ensure that only data from nonnovice players were included. Four studies did not specify the competitive status (Sanchis‐Moysi et al. 2011; Kozinc and Šarabon 2021; Behringer et al. 2013; Sannicandro et al. 2014) and 29 studies did not specify the injury status (Kramer et al. 2017; Ulbricht et al. 2015; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2017; Akşit and Nalcakan 2016; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2022; Ulbricht et al. 2016; Dobos and Tóth 2021; Girard and Millet 2009; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2014; Fett et al. 2020; Kramer, Huijgen, et al. 2016; Myburgh et al. 2016; Meckel 2015; Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2019; Sarabia et al. 2015; Kramer, Valente‐Dos‐Santos, et al. 2016; Munivrana et al. 2015; Filipčič 2004, 2005; Chapelle et al. 2023; Van Den Berg 2006; Myburgh et al. 2020; Kovačević 2013; Siener 2021; Siener et al. 2021; Tsoulfa 2016; Sannicandro et al. 2014; Ferrauti and Bastiaens 2007; Berdejo‐del‐Fresno et al. 2010) of participants, which led to potential inconsistencies. To address this, the corresponding authors of these studies were contacted. Of the 33 authors contacted, 13 responded. Two authors confirmed that their participants were at a competitive level (Kozinc and Šarabon 2021; Sannicandro et al. 2014), whereas one author clarified that they included amateur‐level players (Behringer et al. 2013). Additionally, 10 authors indicated that their participants were uninjured (Akşit and Nalcakan 2016; Dobos and Tóth 2021; Girard and Millet 2009; Chapelle et al. 2023; Van Den Berg 2006; Siener 2021; Siener et al. 2021; Tsoulfa 2016; Sannicandro et al. 2014; Berdejo‐del‐Fresno et al. 2010). Studies without a response from the corresponding author were included due to the likelihood of participants being uninjured and at a competitive level, based on the response from 13 authors regarding their studies. In cases where unilateral tests were conducted with scores separated by left and right sides, the higher score was assumed to be from the dominant side.

The relative reliability of the PTESTs has generally been found to be excellent (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] > 0.90) (Sato et al. 2017; McMahon 2017; Anicic et al. 2023; Huggins 2017; Scheuringer 2024; Benton et al. 2022; Dobos and Tóth 2021; Dardouri et al. 2023; Kolber and Hanney 2012), except for the medicine ball overhead, forehand, and backhand throws (test‐retest ICCs = 0.83–0.90) (Sell et al. 2015) and the hexagon test (test‐retest ICC = 0.88) (Hernández‐Davó et al. 2021). These findings support the overall consistency of the PTESTs, though some tests require further standardization.

Due to the lack of accessible, cost‐effective endurance tests that reflect the specific demands of tennis, this review excluded aerobic and anaerobic endurance tests, focusing instead on assessments that are easily implemented and globally applicable for competitive youth players. At the club level, where advanced methods such as VO2 max and blood lactate testing are often unavailable, tests like the Cooper test (Chapelle et al. 2022), Montréal track test (Léger and Boucher 1980), and Vam‐eval test (Chtara et al. 2005) may be practical, though they assess continuous running and do not reflect the intermittent nature of tennis (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2014; Léger and Boucher 1980; Chtara et al. 2005). Field tests, such as the multistage fitness test, beep test, Yo‐Yo intermittent recovery test, and 30–15 intermittent fitness test, are more relevant to tennis as they incorporate acceleration, deceleration, and direction changes (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2014; P. Roetert 2007). Additionally, tennis‐specific tests like the Girard test (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2014), Hit and Turn test (Fernandez‐Fernandez et al. 2014), and MK drill (M. Kovacs 2016) have been developed to better simulate the sport's unique physical demands. The Girard test, while effective, is complex and requires specialized equipment such as a ball machine, limiting its practicality. In contrast, the Hit and Turn test is simpler, aligns well with tennis workloads, and has existing normative values, making it a strong candidate for further research. The MK drill also holds potential but lacks sufficient clarity on age‐specific norms and has been infrequently studied. Planned and reactive agility test, tennis‐specific sprint test, and drop jumps were not included due to a lack of studies including these tests (Dobos and Tóth 2021; Girard and Millet 2009). Creating an individual profile is of great importance to better understand the individuals' strengths and weaknesses. Ulbricht et al. made an individual report every 6 months for each player as well as their coaching staff and parents, and this highlighted where they needed to put in more work in the following season to achieve greater values in each aspect of their physical capabilities (Ulbricht et al. 2013). A similar approach is recommended for clubs and players in the future.

Lastly, incorporating the assessment of biological age is essential to account for maturation stages, optimize training adaptations, and minimize injury risks in youth tennis players (Luna‐Villouta et al. 2021).

Conclusion

This systematic review has provided the most comprehensive summary of normative values for PTESTs among competitive youth tennis players, covering strength and power, speed and agility, and flexibility. Higher age, male sex, and the use of the dominant side were generally associated with greater performance. We noted that normative data for players U8–U10 were generally lacking and that males were studied more than females. In male players, the internal rotation of the dominant shoulder appeared to decrease with age, which may lead to injury. Our results should be interpreted with caution due to variations in test procedures across studies. Ultimately, the review offers preliminary insights for coaches, clinicians, and researchers to identify players' strengths and weaknesses, potentially aiding in performance enhancement and injury prevention. A consensus statement or strict guideline for the conduct of PTESTs is needed to improve the external validity of future research.
