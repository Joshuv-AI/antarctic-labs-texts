Variability of Circulating Tumor DNA Levels in Plasma Samples From Patients With Advanced Non–Small-Cell Lung Cancer in the Absence of Treatment
Authors: Aleksandra Markovets, Diana Merino Vega, Chris Abbosh, Katie Quinn, Kyle Chang, Sara Wienke, Ryan Hartmaier, J Carl Barrett, Darren Hodgson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEQuantifying changes in circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) during treatment can rapidly identify patients responding to therapy, but accurate interpretation requires an understanding of variability in ctDNA levels in the absence of treatment. This analysis of 360 patients with advanced epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–mutant non–small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) characterized ctDNA level changes in paired pretreatment samples and evaluated factors associated with variability.METHODSScreening and cycle 1, day 1 (C1D1) pretreatment samples from 132 previously untreated and 228 previously treated patients in the FLAURA and AURA3 trials, respectively, had ctDNA quantified using next-generation sequencing (Guardant360/GuardantOMNI NGS panel; mean variant allele frequency [VAF] or EGFR-mutant VAF) and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR, Biodesix) EGFR assays.RESULTSIn FLAURA and AURA3, ≥20% reductions in ctDNA levels (panel mean VAF) from screening to C1D1 occurred in 31/132 (23.5%) and 43/228 (18.9%) patients with paired pretreatment plasma samples, respectively, including ≥50% reductions in 12 (9.1%) and 23 (10.1%) patients, and 100% reductions (ctDNA clearance) in 0 and 5 (2.2%) patients. Background variability was not fully accounted for by intra-NGS or NGS-ddPCR technical variability or other potential sources of variability; larger changes were associated with low VAF and low cell-free DNA (cfDNA) input.CONCLUSIONIntrapatient ctDNA variability over time in EGFR-mutant advanced NSCLC includes changes that may confound interpretation of treatment activity, depending on the magnitude of change used to indicate molecular response. These findings demonstrate that evaluation of on-treatment changes must account for potential background variability, including technical variability because of factors such as cfDNA input level and tumor-related VAF, and that baseline samples should be obtained as close as possible to treatment initiation to minimize the impact of background variability.

INTRODUCTION

Evaluation of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA, the tumor-associated fraction of cell-free DNA [cfDNA] in blood) alleviates many challenges of tissue biopsies for comprehensively evaluating biomarkers for personalization of clinical management in patients with advanced cancer through its ease of sampling and less-invasive nature.1-6 An emerging use of ctDNA analysis is the rapid identification of tumor response to therapy through longitudinal quantification of changes in ctDNA levels, reflecting tumor burden dynamics,3,7-9 potentially facilitating earlier detection of efficacy signals in clinical trials and tailoring changes to patient treatment plans.10-12

The feasibility and reliability of ctDNA quantification during therapy have been demonstrated using assays on the basis of next-generation sequencing (NGS) and droplet digital polymerase chain reaction (ddPCR).3,13-16 Reductions in ctDNA levels during treatment have been associated with improved patient outcomes in various cancer types.2,6,9-11,16-23 However, the thresholds of ctDNA level reduction used in determining associations with clinical outcome have been variable,16,18,24,25 with cutoffs used including reductions of ≥20%,26 ≥30% (reflecting lesion size reduction threshold for partial response per RECIST version 1.1),27,28 ≥50%,16 and 100% (complete ctDNA clearance).11 Response to therapy per reductions in ctDNA at prespecified thresholds, also known as molecular response (MR), is therefore used in clinical research as it can demonstrate response to therapy earlier than radiographic scans, capture tumor burden changes in real time with more frequent collection time points, and have potential clinical significance.29

However, variability in ctDNA levels has been associated with factors other than treatment.30 Small studies reported biologic variability in cfDNA according to time of day when blood is collected, collection time relative to meal ingestion, levels of physical activity, and inflammatory stress.12,20,31-37 These variations, a combination of technical aspects of ctDNA quantification and patient-related variables, may introduce background noise and confound assessment of treatment efficacy. Without accounting for background variability in ctDNA quantification, accurate interpretation of ctDNA changes as a marker of therapeutic response, particularly when managing care in individual patients or estimating therapeutic effect in small cohorts as typically used in early drug development, is challenging.

We characterize intrapatient ctDNA level variability using paired pretreatment plasma samples in 360 patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–mutant (EGFRm) advanced non–small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) enrolled in the FLAURA5 and AURA337 phase III trials. EGFRm NSCLC is characterized by an often clonal and genomically stable mutation. Following changes in plasma EGFR enables accurate and tumor-informed ctDNA quantification that is invulnerable to confounding signals from clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), thereby providing a convenient benchmark.

METHODS

Study Designs and Patients

FLAURA (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02296125) and AURA3 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02151981) study details have been previously published.5,38 The randomized, double-blind FLAURA trial assessed osimertinib versus comparator EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs; erlotinib or gefitinib) in treatment-naïve patients with EGFRm-positive locally advanced or metastatic NSCLC. The randomized, open-label AURA3 trial assessed osimertinib versus platinum-based doublet chemotherapy as second-line therapy in patients with EGFR T790M-positive advanced NSCLC and disease progression after first-line EGFR-TKI therapy. In both trials, patients had confirmed EGFR-TKI–sensitizing mutations (eg, exon 19 deletion [ex19del], L858R) in tumor tissue. This tumor-informed approach enabled direct measurement and monitoring of target mutations in plasma.

FLAURA and AURA3 were conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice, and local regulatory requirements, and were approved by institutional review boards or independent ethics committees of participating study centers. All patients provided written informed consent.

Sample Processing

Per FLAURA and AURA3 study protocols,5,38 provision of plasma samples was mandatory for all patients who provided informed consent, including during the 28-day screening window and before treatment on cycle 1, day 1 (C1D1). Samples from patients who withdrew consent were excluded from this analysis. Sample collection and processing is described in Supplementary Methods. Samples collected during screening and before treatment on C1D1 (approximately 20 mL blood per time-point) were selected for analysis. cfDNA extraction and sequencing were performed at Guardant Health central laboratory (Redwood City, CA) as previously described.14 Plasma cfDNA samples (2-4 mL) were analyzed using the Guardant360 74-gene or GuardantOMNI 500-gene NGS assays. Genomic alterations were identified using Guardant Health's pipeline.14 GuardantOMNI is a superset of Guardant360 with equivalent variant detection performance, including equivalent sensitivity on the common panel space. Thus, only variants covered by Guardant360, as previously described,15 were included in this analysis.

Duplicate FLAURA C1D1 plasma samples were analyzed 1 year apart for technical reproducibility using GuardantOMNI and Guardant360. Duplicate plasma aliquots for AURA3 C1D1 plasma samples were analyzed for technical concordance by evaluating EGFRm levels (ex19del, L858R) in an orthogonal platform comparison using Guardant360 and an EGFR ddPCR assay (Biodesix, Boulder, CO; see the “Technical reproducibility analyses” section in Supplementary Methods).

ctDNA-Level Measurements

Levels of ctDNA in plasma samples were evaluated using the multigene NGS assay, applying Guardant Health's Guardant360 Response algorithm or AstraZeneca's internal method to discount the possibility that observed variability was intrinsic to a particular analytical pipeline (Supplementary Methods and Results). Assessment of ctDNA level with this multigene methodology was based on the mean variant allele frequency (VAF) of single-nucleotide variants, insertion-deletion mutations (indels), and fusion events with sufficient total molecule count and mutant molecule count, per Guardant Health's methodology. Samples with no qualified events were considered not quantifiable or ctDNA-low; that is, ctDNA-low classification was based on no somatic variant with sufficient mutant molecule count in both paired samples per Guardant360 Response algorithm. Assessment of ctDNA levels was also done using a single-gene (EGFR) method on the basis of the VAF of EGFR-TKI–sensitizing mutations (ex19del or L858R; EGFRm VAF) measured by the NGS or ddPCR assay. Nonquantifiable/ctDNA-low classification was based on no EGFR-TKI–sensitizing mutations with sufficient EGFR-mutant molecule count in both paired samples.

ctDNA Variability Analysis

Background variability in ctDNA level was evaluated by comparing ctDNA levels in paired samples acquired at screening and C1D1 and calculated as relative change in panel mean VAF or EGFRm VAF on multigene NGS assay as previously reported15,17,18 (see Supplementary Methods for calculations and categorization of ctDNA changes). Sample characteristics were evaluated as potential sources of variability against the continuous variable of percentage change in ctDNA level and compared between groups categorized by magnitude of change (Supplementary Methods).

Statistical Analyses

Associations of patient demographics and disease characteristics were evaluated using unpaired T-test to compare continuous variables or Fisher's exact test to compare categorical variables. All P values were corrected for multiple testing using Bonferroni methodology. The Pearson correlation coefficient and its significance level (P) were used to assess correlations between variables, including sample characteristics that were potential preanalytical sources of variability, and change in ctDNA level.

RESULTS

Median interval between collection of screening and C1D1 samples was 9 days (95% CI, 8 to 13; range, 0-55) overall, and 6 (95% CI, 5 to 7) and 17 (95% CI, 15 to 20) days in FLAURA and AURA3, respectively; per protocol, intervening therapies were not received (FLAURA)/restricted (AURA3; Fig 1). ctDNA Background Variability by Panel Mean VAF

Panel mean VAF levels in paired pretreatment screening and C1D1 samples showed bidirectional ctDNA variability in the absence of treatment (Figs 2A and 2B). Overall, 192/360 (53.3%) sample pairs exhibited ≥20% increases or ≥20% reductions in ctDNA levels, including 65/132 (49.2%) in FLAURA (Fig 2C; Data Supplement, Fig S1A) and 127/228 (55.7%) in AURA3 (Fig 2D; Data Supplement, Fig S1B). Among patients with ctDNA quantifiable at screening in FLAURA (n = 88; Fig 2C) and AURA3 (n = 168; Fig 2D), ≥20% reductions from screening to C1D1 occurred in 35.2% (n = 31) and 25.6% (n = 43), respectively, including ≥30% reductions in 28.4% (n = 25) and 22.6% (n = 38), ≥50% reductions in 13.6% (n = 12) and 13.7% (n = 23), and 100% reductions (ctDNA clearance) in 0% and 3.0% (n = 5); reductions according to panel mean VAF levels at screening are summarized in Figs 2C and 2D. Of five sample pairs in AURA3 that showed 100% ctDNA reductions, all had panel mean VAF of <5% at screening and three had C1D1 cfDNA input <15 ng. ctDNA increases of ≥20% appeared more frequent in samples with low panel mean VAF (≤1%) in both FLAURA (Data Supplement, Fig S1A) and AURA3 (Data Supplement, Fig S1B).

ctDNA Background Variability by EGFRm VAF

ctDNA levels were analyzed on the basis of change in EGFRm VAF only to ensure variability in the absence of treatment was not due to technical issues introduced by quantifying mean VAF with the NGS multigene panel assay. Findings were consistent with those obtained with the panel assay in both FLAURA (Fig 3A) and AURA3 (Fig 3B), with 177/360 (49.2%) sample pairs exhibiting ≥20% increases or ≥20% reductions in ctDNA levels by EGFRm VAF, including 61/132 (46.2%) in FLAURA (Fig 3C; Data Supplement, Fig S1C) and 116/228 (50.9%) in AURA3 (Fig 3D; Data Supplement, Fig S1D). Among patients with ctDNA quantifiable at screening in FLAURA (n = 80; Fig 3C) and AURA3 (n = 152; Fig 3D), ≥20% reductions from screening to C1D1 occurred in 36.3% (n = 29) and 28.3% (n = 43), respectively, including ≥30% reductions in 31.3% (n = 25) and 24.3% (n = 37), ≥50% reductions in 16.3% (n = 13) and 13.2% (n = 20), and 100% reductions (ctDNA clearance) in 0% and 2.6% (n = 4); reductions according to EGFRm VAF levels at screening are summarized in Figures 3C and 3D. Of four sample pairs from patients in AURA3 that showed ctDNA clearance, three and one had screening EGFRm VAF of >1 to ≤10% and >10%, respectively (Fig 3D), and three had C1D1 cfDNA input <15 ng. The frequency of ≥20% increases appeared higher in samples with EGFRm VAF ≤1% in both FLAURA (Data Supplement, Fig S1C) and AURA3 (Data Supplement, Fig S1D).

Potential Sources of Variability

Demographic and biologic characteristics were compared between patients with quantifiable or nonquantifiable ctDNA levels at screening, and between those with quantifiable ctDNA exhibiting ≥50% reductions or other magnitudes of change. After adjustment for multiplicity of testing, median age was statistically significantly higher in patients in FLAURA with undetected ctDNA compared with patients with quantifiable ctDNA (Table 1). No other significant between-group differences were observed in either the FLAURA (Table 1) or AURA3 (Table 2) cohorts.

Analysis of NGS technical reproducibility showed limited variability in duplicate C1D1 aliquots from patients in FLAURA evaluated approximately 1 year apart using two NGS assays (Data Supplement, Fig S3A), with only one pair of samples (panel mean VAF <1%) showing a ≥50% difference in panel mean VAF. In the orthogonal platform comparison, strong concordance in EGFRm VAF quantification was observed between the NGS and ddPCR assays, with greater variability at lower EGFRm VAF levels (Data Supplement, Fig S3B). Both analyses showed less variability than the variability in the absence of treatment observed between screening and C1D1.

Additional potential sources of variability or bias were evaluated across sample pairs with detectable ctDNA according to ctDNA variability, that is, percentage change in ctDNA level as a continuous variable (Fig 4). In FLAURA (Fig 4A), significant negative correlations were seen between panel mean VAF and cfDNA input at screening and percentage change in ctDNA level; in AURA3 (Fig 4B) a similar, stronger correlation was seen for mean VAF at screening. Similar correlations were seen in analyses on the basis of EGFRm VAF (Figs 4C and 4D).

DISCUSSION

Assessment of MR through observing reductions in ctDNA can indicate drug activity in studies across multiple cancers.2,6,9-11,16-23 However, it is important to understand whether these changes can be solely attributed to the drug, particularly when using findings to inform patient management and care or when interpreting findings in small numbers of patients in early-phase studies that may inform drug development decisions. In both circumstances, avoiding misinterpretation because of background variability is imperative. With our well-characterized data set on matched pretreatment samples from 360 patients with advanced NSCLC in a setting that enabled us to quantify background variability without the confounding impact of CHIP, we characterized the magnitude and potential sources of this variability. We observed bidirectional changes in ctDNA levels that appeared consistent with the transient nature of ctDNA,39,40 but, although ctDNA increases in patients with cancer not receiving treatment might be expected because of progressing disease or post–EGFR-TKI tumor-flare, we saw different levels of ctDNA reductions, including ctDNA clearance, in substantial proportions of cases in the FLAURA and AURA3 trials between pretreatment time points. Unless appropriately quantified and accounted for, such background variability may potentially confound interpretation of on-treatment changes and adversely affect decision making.

Overall, we observed ≥20% reductions in ctDNA level between screening and C1D1 samples (median interval, 9 days) in 23.5% of previously untreated patients in FLAURA (corroborating previous findings in a smaller data set26), including ≥50% reductions in 9.1%. In patients receiving second-line therapy in AURA3, 18.9% had ≥20% reductions in ctDNA levels, including ≥50% and 100% reductions in 10.1% and 2.2%, respectively. Our data thus suggest the need for caution when interpreting treatment effects using ctDNA reductions, especially with lower thresholds, as background variability may account for some of the observed reductions.

The magnitude of ctDNA reduction that can be confidently ascribed to a therapeutic intervention is affected by the range of variability occurring in the absence of therapy, and the clinical impact of the variability is proportional to the threshold used to define a meaningful treatment effect. Disambiguating between treatment effect and background variability will require a sufficiently stringent threshold to define MR and overcome confounding by background variability.

In the FLAURA and AURA3 studies of osimertinib,5,38 the stringent threshold of ctDNA clearance was used to define MR, and efficacy was substantial; in patients with baseline-quantifiable ctDNA, week 6 EGFRm ctDNA clearance rates were 82% and 65%, respectively.11 In the present analysis, in patients with ctDNA quantifiable at screening in FLAURA and AURA3, we observed ctDNA clearance by EGFRm VAF in the absence of treatment in 0% and 2.6%, respectively. Thus, in AURA3, the week 6 ctDNA clearance rate was approximately 25-times higher numerically than the background variability rate in the absence of treatment, suggesting that the crude likelihood of an observation of ctDNA clearance being not related to drug efficacy would be approximately 4%.

However, using a threshold of ≥50% reduction in ctDNA by EGFRm VAF, we observed an effect in the absence of treatment in 13.2% of patients with ctDNA quantifiable at screening in AURA3. Using the most conservative assumption, that the 6-week rate of ≥50% ctDNA reduction in AURA3 (data not available) were the same as that for ctDNA clearance, that is, 65%, this would be only approximately 5-times higher numerically than the background variability rate and would suggest a crude approximately 20% likelihood of an observation not being related to drug efficacy. In small dose-escalation cohorts in early-phase clinical evaluation, there would therefore be a relatively high probability of an observed ctDNA reduction not being related to drug efficacy, confounding interpretation.

Notably, cases in which we observed ctDNA clearance in the absence of treatment commonly had low VAF at screening and/or low C1D1 cfDNA input, potentially suggesting stochastic positives near the assay limit of detection (LOD). Although none of the sampling parameters evaluated consistently accounted for observed pretreatment background variability, our findings suggested that changes correlated with VAF and cfDNA input at screening, with larger ctDNA changes in AURA3 associated with lower VAF and cfDNA input. These findings and the cases of complete clearance suggest the need to consider absolute VAF in addition to percentage changes in analyses with low VAFs close to assay LOD, for which the noise-to-signal ratio will be higher. Moreover, in cases with low VAFs, more accurate methods for assessment of tumor content would help minimize this variability.41 Nevertheless, we saw substantial changes between screening and C1D1 in sample pairs with higher VAFs, highlighting the broad importance of background variability to interpretation of ctDNA findings. In this context, our findings suggest it is imperative for baseline samples to be taken at the start of therapy (ie, C1D1) to ensure any on-treatment samples are compared with a baseline that is as proximal to the start of treatment as possible.

We evaluated multiple other potential sources of variability and demonstrated high technical reproducibility for the NGS panel assay, as well as quantification agreement between the NGS and ddPCR assays. We observed strong correlations between analyses of ctDNA variability on the basis of NGS evaluation of panel mean VAF or EGFRm VAF and were thereby able to eliminate the risk that tracking a subclonal or CHIP variant may have confounded interpretation of our panel-based analyses, as observed previously.8,36,42-44

A comprehensive evaluation of background variability in ctDNA levels in diverse settings is warranted, plus determination of appropriate stringent thresholds and methodologies for removing additional sources of noise to ensure accurate identification of patients who are truly exhibiting a therapeutic response.41,45,46 Several collaborative efforts are focusing on standardization of practices that would help minimize technical, analytical, and preanalytical variability.16,47-53 Additional potentially relevant factors to consider include diurnal fluctuations in cfDNA levels,12,32 increases in cfDNA associated with elevated exercise duration and intensity20 and infection severity,31,33,34 lingering effects of previous therapies, clinical variables that could influence ctDNA levels,54 and time interval between samples.

In conclusion, as our understanding of the biologic mechanisms that govern tumor growth and development evolve in parallel with advanced, highly sensitive detection technologies, we need to continue refining how best to interpret ctDNA levels and dynamics, given the meaningful background ctDNA variability reported here. To our knowledge, our findings for the first time quantify the intuitive understanding that larger ctDNA changes can be more confidently ascribed to drug activity in an individual patient. Understanding the drivers of ctDNA variability is an important goal for informing the use of ctDNA reductions in decision making in drug development and clinical practice.
