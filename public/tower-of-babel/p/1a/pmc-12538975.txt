Evaluation of cartilage regeneration using a hybrid bilayer scaffold based on omentum: evidence of limited efficacy
Authors: Melih Oral, Emin Suha Dedeogullari, Selin Onen, Cansu Ozdemir, Bihter Muratoglu, Zeynep Caglar, Halil Murat Aydin, Duygu Uckan Cetinkaya, Petek Korkusuz, Egemen Turhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose The ideal treatment of osteochondral defects is controversial. Regenerative treatment methods aim to produce ideal scaffolds using different biomaterials. Decellularized extracellular matrix (ECM) containing scaffolds are good options recently. Promising results have been reported with decellularized human omentum xenografts in animal models for tissue regeneration. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and growth factors also form other parts of the tissue engineering triad for cartilage regeneration The objective of this study is to evaluate the regenerative effects of the decellularised omentum-based scaffold, MSCs and platelet-rich plasma (PRP) in the rat knee articular cartilage model. To our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the combined effect of a decellularized human omentum composite scaffold, mesenchymal stem cells, and PRP on osteochondral defect repair.Methods Osteochondral defects were bilaterally created in the femoral trochlea of 42 rats. The animals were randomly divided into five groups: negative control (C), scaffold only (O), scaffold combined with mesenchymal stem cells (OM), scaffold combined with PRP (OP) and scaffold combined with both MSCs and PRP (OMP). At 12 weeks postoperatively, cartilage repair was assessed by macroscopic and histomorphometric evaluation.Results The O group (p = 0.022) and the OMP group (p = 0.001) showed higher scores than the C group macroscopically. No statistically significant differences were observed between the other group comparisons (p > 0.05). According to the total O’Driscoll evaluation scores, none of the experimental groups showed a significant improvement compared to the C group. However, the OMP group had significantly higher total scores than the group O (p = 0.002). According to the ICRS scoring system, the OMP application showed significantly better surface continuity (p = 0.0314), healthier calcified cartilage formation (p = 0.0327), and higher cell viability (p = 0.0029) compared to group O. The OM group demonstrated improved calcified cartilage structure compared to the O group (p = 0.0429).Conclusions The omentum-based scaffold, along with MSCs and PRP orthobiologic interventions applied in a single-dose, 12-week protocol, demonstrated limited efficacy in osteochondral defect regeneration.Level of evidence Level NA-Animal studies.Keywords: Cartilage, Omentum decellularization, Mesenchymal stem cells, Platelet rich plasma

Introduction

Articular cartilage defects are frequently encountered in orthopedic practice and are primarily caused by trauma or degenerative changes [1, 2]. The ideal treatment of articular cartilage defects is controversial in that these defects possess a restricted ability to heal intrinsically due to their avascular, aneural, and alymphatic nature, along with a low cellularity [3]. In the treatment of osteochondral injuries, in addition to conservative approaches, surgical interventions are commonly categorized into palliative, reparative, and restorative procedures [4]. Current strategies include marrow stimulation techniques such as microfracture, osteochondral autograft or allograft transplantation, and autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI) which has progressively advanced from periosteal flap–based techniques to collagen- and matrix-induced approaches nevertheless, the optimal method of membrane fixation in ACI remains controversial [5]. The efficacy of these treatment strategies, which aim to regenerate native hyaline cartilage tissue, remains limited, and they are associated with several disadvantages. Consequently, cartilage repair represents a persistent challenge in orthopedic surgery, characterized by ongoing debate, continuous innovation, and active research efforts [6].

Tissue engineering approaches typically involve the use of biocompatible scaffolds in combination with stem cells and bioactive factors to promote cartilage regeneration. Despite extensive research utilizing both synthetic and natural scaffolds, the successful regeneration of native hyaline cartilage remains elusive [7, 8]. Modifications in the scaffold structure are of particular importance, as they can influence the differentiation of stem cells [9]. An ideal scaffold should closely mimic the natural extracellular matrix (ECM), providing a biomechanically and functionally compatible environment for tissue regeneration [10]. Decellularized ECM derived from allogeneic or xenogeneic tissues serves as a widely used scaffold source in various tissue engineering applications due to its high immunological tolerance [11].

Various biomaterials have been investigated for scaffold fabrication in osteochondral tissue engineering, including natural polymers (e.g., collagen, hyaluronan, alginate, chitosan), synthetic polymers (e.g., PLGA, PLLA, PCL), bioceramics (e.g., hydroxyapatite, tricalcium phosphate), and their composites [12–14]. With the recent advances in nanotechnology, nanostructured scaffolds produced using these materials have also begun to yield promising results [15, 16]. Natural polymers offer high biocompatibility and bioactivity but have limited mechanical strength, whereas synthetic polymers provide tunable structural and mechanical properties but lack sufficient bioactivity. Bioceramics, particularly hydroxyapatite, exhibit strong biocompatibility and osteoconductivity due to their similarity to natural bone [17]. To overcome the limitations of single-material systems, hybrid and multiphasic scaffolds have been developed, combining the advantages of different materials. While monophase scaffolds provide homogeneous structures, they fail to replicate the complex layered organization of the osteochondral unit, leading to suboptimal outcomes [18]. Therefore, biphasic and multiphasic scaffolds that mimic cartilage and subchondral bone layers with distinct but integrated architectures have gained increasing attention, showing superior regenerative potential in both in vivo and in vitro studies [19]. Recent advances in bioprinting, which employs bioinks composed of cells and biomaterials, have enabled the fabrication of 3D constructs resembling native tissues. Particularly, the integration of nanoscale biomaterials has emerged as a promising approach in recent years, enhancing stem cell differentiation and regenerative potential [20].

Omentum is a highly vascularized peritoneal fold, part of the peritoneum, enriched with adipocytes and various immune cells such as macrophages, lymphocytes, and mast cells, supported by a reticular fiber network [21]. Its potent angiogenic activity, driven by growth factors like VEGF and FGF, enables it to support tissue repair, particularly in hypoxic and inflamed environments [22]. Due to its adhesive properties and content of bioactive molecules, the omentum has been widely utilized in reconstructive surgery [23]. Previous studies have demonstrated its regenerative potential in diverse tissues, including neural, hepatic, and musculoskeletal systems [24, 25]. Notably, its ability to reservoir for chondrocytes and support bone healing has been shown in both experimental and preclinical models, highlighting its promise as a biological scaffold in tissue engineering applications [26, 27]. Natural extracellular matrix composite scaffolds are of particular interest because they most closely resemble native tissue, providing an appropriate environment for cellular adhesion and proliferation, while also being non-antigenic to the host tissue. For these reasons, tissue-engineered adipose substitutes have been developed with the aim of promoting regeneration. The omentum, with its abundant extracellular matrix, ease of availability, capacity to adhere to surrounding tissues, and richness in growth factors, has been successfully applied in the repair of defects in other organs, most commonly in reconstructive and cardiovascular surgery in the form of a decellularized scaffold. However, despite these advantages, the use of decellularized omentum scaffolds in orthopedic surgery has not yet been reported,

The combined use of synthetic and biological polymers in scaffold design has shown superior outcomes compared to the use of either component alone, by integrating the mechanical advantages of synthetic materials with the biocompatibility of biological ones [28–30]. Gelatin methacrylate (GelMA), a viscosity-enhancing hydrogel, has been utilized in combination with hydroxyapatite (HA) to form multilayered hybrid scaffolds that closely mimic the biomechanical properties of native cartilage and different tissues [31, 32]. Several studies have reported successful cartilage regeneration by mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) within GelMA-based hydrogels [33]. HA, known for its osteoinductive properties, also contributes positively to bone and cartilage regeneration [34]. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that the combination of hydroxyapatite and gelatin possesses osteoinductive and osteoconductive properties, supporting their potential in bone defect regeneration [35]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that the use of hyaluronic acid membranes in focal chondral defects also reduces failure rates and revision surgeries [36]. Moreover, composite scaffolds combining GelMA and HA have demonstrated enhanced osteogenic differentiation of MSCs compared to scaffolds containing HA alone [37]. Therefore, designing composite scaffolds with stratified layers composed of different biomaterials represents a promising approach for osteochondral repair, as it allows for the simultaneous support of both cartilage and bone regeneration.

MSCs have been extensively studied for their potential in cartilage regeneration due to their ability to differentiate into chondrocytes [38]. In osteochondral defect models, MSC based groups have demonstrated superior macroscopic and histological outcomes highlighting the regenerative advantage of cell-based tissue engineering. Also the effects of MSCs on different issues like tenogenic differentiation have also been demonstrated when combined with appropriate scaffolds [34, 39, 40].

In parallel, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has gained attention for its multifaceted biological effects on cartilage tissue, including anti-inflammatory activity, angiogenesis promotion, chondroprotection, and stimulation of cellular proliferation and differentiation. When used in combination with MSCs, PRP has been shown to reduce inflammatory responses and enhance chondrogenic differentiation, thereby offering a synergistic effect in cartilage repair strategies [41].

Despite ongoing advances, the search for effective and biologically compatible strategies to promote cartilage regeneration remains an active area of research. This study aims to evaluate the therapeutic potential of a novel composite scaffold composed of decellularized human omentum, HA, and GelMA in the treatment of osteochondral defects. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to assess the combined application of this composite scaffold with MSCs and PRP. The primary objectives are to examine the regenerative capacity of decellularized human omentum in cartilage repair and to explore its synergistic effects when used in combination with MSCs and PRP.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Hacettepe University Animal Experiments Ethics Committee (approval number: 2023/03–10) and the Hacettepe University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 2023/07–26). This study was financially supported by Hacettepe University Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit under project number THD-2023–20840.

Decellularization of human omentum and scaffold preperation

Omental tissue fragments were obtained from residual specimens collected during previous unrelated experimental studies, using surplus tissue from patients who underwent non-tumorous abdominal surgeries. The decellularization protocol for human omentum was adapted with modifications from the method developed by Shevach et al. [42]. The tissue was washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 24 h to remove blood and debris.

A freeze–thaw cycle was performed using a hypotonic buffer (0.01 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.0) to initiate cell lysis. This was followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series, and delipidation using isopropanol. For this purpose, samples were incubated in isopropanol at 70 rpm for 48 h using a Thermoshaker (Gerhardt, Germany). After delipidation, tissues were rehydrated with a reverse ethanol gradient and subsequently treated with 0.25% (w/v) trypsin at 37 °C. To determine the optimal concentration of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), various concentrations, 0.5% (w/v), 1% (w/v), and 2% (w/v), were tested by incubating the tissues at 70 rpm for 24 h. The tissues were washed in PBS for 48 h to remove residual detergents and then stored at – 80 °C until further use.

The efficiency of the decellularization protocol was assessed through DNA quantification. An effective decellularization process is typically defined by a residual DNA content of less than 50 ng per mg of dry tissue [43]. Measured DNA concentrations were 111.18 ± 4.57 ng, 56.32 ± 21.3 ng, 28.93 ± 17.61 ng, and 0 ng for the control and decellularized groups D1, D2, and D3 respectively. These results confirmed that D3 achieved complete cellular removal and was selected for hybrid scaffold fabrication. Decellularized omental tissue from group D3 was homogenized in PBS and ground into powder, then lyophilized and stored at – 80 °C.

For hybrid scaffold formation, GelMA prepolymer synthesis was first performed using a modified version of the protocol described by Gizem et al. [44]. A 10% (w/v) gelatin solution was prepared in PBS (pH 7.4) at 50 °C. Subsequently, 10% (v/v) methacrylic anhydride was added at a flow rate of 3 mL/min under continuous stirring, and the reaction was carried out in the dark for 3 h. To remove unreacted methacrylic anhydride, the resulting GelMA solution was dialyzed in distilled water at 40 °C with daily water changes. After dialysis, the GelMA prepolymer solution was frozen at – 80 °C, lyophilized for one week, and stored at – 80 °C until use.

The omentum–hydroxyapatite–GelMA (O-HA-G) hybrid scaffold was crosslinked using ultraviolet (UV) light in the presence of a photoinitiator. Initially, a 1% (w/v) solution of Irgacure 2959 (I2959) was prepared in PBS (pH 7.4) at 40 °C. This solution was then mixed with GelMA prepolymer to achieve a final concentration of 15% (w/v). The resulting homogeneous pre-gel solution was combined with powdered decellularized omentum and HA at a weight ratio of 1:2 (omentum:HA). This mixture was carefully layered in cylindrical molds (27.5 mm diameter × 3 mm height) to form a bilayered structure. The scaffold was photo-crosslinked using a UV crosslinking chamber (UVP, Germany) under a dose of 7200 µJ for 15 min (20 cycles with 15-s intervals). The resulting bilayer scaffold comprised a 2 mm-thick HA-rich bottom layer and a 1 mm-thick omentum-rich top layer, both embedded within the GelMA matrix.

Proliferation of mesenchymal stem cells

Bone marrow-derived MSCs were isolated from the femur and tibia of two rats after bone excision. Bone marrow was flushed with 10 mL DMF10 medium (DMEM-LG (Low-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, Gibco), 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum, Biological Industries), 1% L-glutamine (Serana), and 1% Pen/Strep (Penicillin/Streptomycin, Serana) and layered onto Ficoll/Lymphosep (Biowest) for mononuclear cells (MNCs) isolation. The buffy coat, containing the MNCs fraction, was collected using a Pasteur pipette, washed twice with 1X PBS and centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting cell pellet was resuspended in DMF10 medium. Cell counting was performed using Turk’s solution, 20 × 106 cells were seeded into T75 culture flasks containing DMF10 medium. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Upon reaching 70–80% confluence, cells were passaged using 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA (Gibco). All experiments were conducted using third-passage (P3) MSCs.

Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that over 99% of the cells expressed stromal surface markers anti-mouse/rat CD29 APC (Biolegend, #Cat:102,216, anti-rat CD90 PE (Biolegend, #Cat:202,524), and anti-rat CD44 FITC (Biolegend, #Cat:203,906), while showing negative expression for non-stromal marker anti-rat CD45 FITC (Biolegend, #Cat:202,205). For differentiation assays, P3 MSCs were seeded into culture plates at a density of 3000 cells/cm2 for osteogenic differentiation and 6000 cells/cm2 for adipogenic differentiation. Osteogenic differentiation medium consisted of 100 nM dexamethasone (DEX, Sigma-Aldrich, #50–02-2), 200 μM ascorbic acid (Sigma), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Applichem) in DMF10 medium. Adipogenic differentiation medium included 1 μM DEX, 0.2 mM indomethacin (Sigma), 0.1 mg/mL insulin, 1 mM IBMX (3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, Applichem), 1% L-glutamine, 10% FBS, and high-glucose DMEM. After 21 days of differentiation, osteogenic cultures were stained with Alizarin Red S (Applichem, #Cat: A2306) to assess calcium deposition, while adipogenic cultures were stained with Oil Red O (Sigma, #Cat:O-0625) to evaluate intracellular lipid accumulation.

For in vivo tracking, MSCs were magnetically labeled. Magnetic labeling was performed using 25 µg/mL iron oxide nanoparticles (Endorem; Guerbet, Villepinte, France) and 0.1875 µL/mL poly-L-lysine (PLL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were washed thoroughly after labeling. Cell viability was assessed via Trypan Blue exclusion assay and found to be approximately 87%. Cell numbers were determined using a hemocytometer, and a total of 16 × 106 cells were resuspended in 400 µL PBS for in vivo application.

To visualize intracellular iron, Prussian Blue staining (FacePath Patoloji, Cat: 8,681,065,133,678, Türkiye) was performed. After labeling, cells were fixed in 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde) for 30 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with distilled water. A Prussian Blue working solution was prepared by mixing 10 drops each of hydrochloric acid (Reagent A) and potassium ferrocyanide (Reagent B), and samples were incubated in this solution for 20 min with gentle agitation. After staining, cells were rinsed with distilled water and counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red (Reagent C) for 5 min. This was followed by four 1-min washes with distilled water and sequential ethanol washes (75 and 100%) to dehydrate the samples. Under microscopy, ferric iron appeared as bright blue deposits.

Preparation of the PRP

Intracardiac blood (10 mL) was collected from a single donor rat under sterile conditions. To prevent coagulation, 1 mL of sodium gluconate was added immediately after collection. The sample was first centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min to separate erythrocytes. The plasma layer was then transferred to a new tube and centrifuged again at 1200 rpm for 10 min to concentrate platelets. The resulting allogenic PRP was collected and used during the surgical procedure.

Animal model and surgical procedure

The study was carried out in an animal research laboratory using male inbred Wistar albino rats with an average weight of 350 g, following approval from the local ethics committee. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The rats were randomly divided into five groups, with eight animals in each group. In addition, two reserve animals were included in the study. During the experimental follow-up period, three animals died; therefore, the study was completed with a total of 39 rats. Prior to the surgical procedure, all animals were acclimatized to the laboratory environment for 10 days. During the study, the rats were housed in plastic cages under controlled environmental conditions (22 ± 2 °C) with a 12-h light and 12-h dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. None of the rats had been previously used in any experimental procedures. No additional procedure was performed in the control (C) group (n = 15). Only the decellularized omentum based scaffold was applied to O group (n = 14). OM group was treated with a scaffold combined with 1 × 106 MSCs (n = 15). OP received a scaffold along with PRP (n = 17). The scaffold, 1 × 106 MSCs and PRP were applied to OMP group (n = 17).

Prior to the surgical procedure, anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of a 50 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride and 5 mg/kg xylazine. Following anesthesia induction, a midline incision was made over the anterior aspect of the knee to expose the skin and subcutaneous tissue. Using a medial parapatellar approach, the fascia and joint capsule were incised together. The incision was extended proximally into the vastus medialis and distally to the medial side of the tibial tuberosity. With the knee in full extension, the patella was laterally dislocated to expose the trochlear surface. A 3 mm depth was marked using a ruler on a 2 mm drill. Subsequently, a single osteochondral defect (OCD) with a depth of 3 mm was created in the trochlear region using a 2 mm drill in all groups (Fig. 1). After 12-week follow-up, rats were euthanized and distal femurs were excised for macroscopic and histomorphometric evaluation.

Histomorphometric and macroscopic evaluation

The macroscopic evaluation of the observed morphological characteristics was performed using the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) macroscopic scoring system [45]. Scoring was independently repeated by two blinded observers.

Samples taken from rats, including the osteochondral defect area of the knee and surrounding tissue, were fixed in a 10% phosphate-buffered formaldehyde solution (pH 7.0) and then decalcified at room temperature in De Castro solution (chloral hydrate, nitric acid, and distilled water) in a controlled manner. The decalcified samples were washed with PBS, dehydrated by passing through graded alcohols in a tissue processor with a constant vacuum (Leica, Germany), cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections in 3–4 µm thickness were obtained from the paraffin blocks using a sliding microtome (Leica, Germany), and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin for general tissue healing assessment and with Safranin O for evaluating the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content of the ECM of the articular cartilage. The presence of iron-labeled MSCs in the defect area was verified using Prussian blue staining.

The healing of the induced articular cartilage defect in the control and experimental groups was assessed semi-quantitatively under a light microscope (Leica DMB6 B, Wetzlar, Germany) using the ICRS and O'Driscoll scoring methods [45, 46]. For this purpose, micrographs were transferred to a computer via a digital camera (Leica DFC7000 T, Wetzlar, Germany) attached to the microscope, and the defect area was scored by two blinded researchers based on the cellular morphology, staining of matrix, surface regularity, structural integrity, thickness, bonding to the adjacent cartilage, hyposellularity, chondrocyte clustering, freedom from degenerative changes, surface, matrix, cell distribution, cell viability, subchondral bone, and calcified cartilage parameters using an image analysis program (Leica, LASV3, Germany).

Statistical analysis

After determining whether the data followed a normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test, differences between multiple groups were analyzed using the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test. The Dunn test was used for pairwise comparisons. Spearman's test was performed for correlation analysis of macroscopic and microscopic data. Descriptive statistics were presented as minimum, maximum, and median values. The study was conducted at a 95% confidence interval, and statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS v23 software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Macroscopic evaluation

Macroscopic evaluation revealed heterogeneous outcomes among the experimental groups (Fig. 2). Based on total macroscopic scoring, the mean scores were calculated as follows: 6.36 in the group C, 9.03 in the group O, 8.63 in the OM, 8.64 in the OP, and 9.35 in the group OMP. As the data did not show a normal distribution, mean ranks were used for comparison. The mean rank values were 19.8 for the C group, 45.43 for the O group, 39.63 for the OM group, 41.15 for the OP group, and 50.24 for the OMP group. According to the ICRS macroscopic scoring scale, the scores obtained at week 12 were classified as “nearly normal” in the O, OM, OP, and OMP groups, whereas the C was classified as “abnormal.” Notably, only two specimens from the OMP group achieved the maximum score of 12 (Table 1). When the total macroscopic scores were compared among the five experimental groups, the O group (p = 0.022) and the OMP group (p = 0.001) demonstrated significantly higher scores compared to the control group. However, no statistically significant differences were observed among the remaining groups (p > 0.05).

The OMP significantly improved the structural characteristics of the osteochondral cartilage at the defect area in terms surface regularity when compared to both C (p = 0.0094), O only (p = 0.0006), and OM groups (p = 0.0002) (Fig. 4C). OMP ameliorated structural integrity (p = 0.0011) and adhesion to adjacent cartilage (p = 0.0333) compared to the scaffold-only application (Fig. 4F), but did not improve cartilage thickness (Fig. 4E) when compared to the scaffold-only application on week 12. Taken together, the structural characteristics did not benefit from scaffold, scaffold + PRP and scaffold + MSCs applications (Fig. 4).

Scoring for the freedom from degenerative changes in adjacent cartilage revealed an amelioration by OMP in terms of hypocellularity but not chondrocyte clustering when compared to C and other experiment groups (p = 0.0105 for control, p = 0.0001 for scaffold-only, p = 0.0002 for OM, p = 0.0284 for OP, Fig. 4G). However, no improvement was observed regarding chondrocyte clusters.

According to the total O’Driscoll histological scores, none of the experimental groups showed a significant improvement compared to the control group. Nonetheless, the OMP group achieved a significantly higher total score than the O group (p = 0.002). Furthermore, the higher total score observed in the OMP group compared to the OM group suggests a supportive role of PRP in enhancing cartilage regeneration (p = 0.003; Fig. 4J).

The OMP provided higher cartilage (p = 0.0314, Fig. 5A), healthier calcified cartilage formation (p = 0.0327, Fig. 5F), and higher cell viability (p = 0.0029) by histochemistry when compared to scaffold-only application but not the C. The OM application resulted in better calcified cartilage formation compared to the scaffold-only group (p = 0.0429). However, no differences were observed between the experimental groups and the C group. The OMP application demonstrated better cell distribution compared to the OM group (p = 0.0249). However, the cartilage cell distribution and the cartilage composition, consisting of a mixture of hyaline and fibrocartilage and the subchondral bone remodeling state remained the same in all control and experiment groups on week 12. When total ICRS scores were evaluated, no statistically significant difference was observed between the experimental and control groups (p = 0.24; Fig. 5G).

In conclusion, within the scope of this study, PRP, omentum–HA composite xenograft, and MSC-based orthobiologic interventions—applied as a single dose and evaluated at a single 12-week time point—demonstrated limited efficacy in osteochondral defect regeneration. No substantial regenerative benefit was observed from the HA and decellularized omentum matrix compared to the control.

Discussion

In the rat knee articular cartilage defect model, the hybrid bilayer scaffold composed of omentum, HA, and GelMA demonstrated limited effectiveness in promoting healing and cartilage regeneration. While decellularized ECM-based scaffolds have shown promising regenerative potential in non-cartilaginous tissues, this benefit could not be replicated in osteochondral tissue using the omentum-based composite scaffold. The hypothesis of this study was to provide an alternative scaffold material for osteochondral tissue engineering, thereby contributing to regenerative treatment options commonly used in clinical settings. However, findings revealed that the omentum-based composite structure failed to achieve the desired level of regenerative support.

While the omentum-based composite scaffold demonstrated limited regenerative capacity on its own, its combination with MSCs and growth factors—although not yielding fully satisfactory outcomes—showed potentially promising effects. This finding emphasizes once again the critical role of cellular components and bioactive factors in osteochondral tissue engineering strategies. Future use of omentum tissue in scaffold design may become a viable alternative with advancements in decellularization techniques and the integration of various biomaterials and scaffold architectures. Although our study did not demonstrate the suitability of omentum for clinical translation at the current stage, it provides a novel contribution to the literature as the first study investigating the combined use of decellularized omentum, MSCs, and PRP in cartilage defect repair.

In osteochondral tissue engineering, the fundamental components consist of cells, growth factors, and a suitable scaffold. In line with current bioengineering applications, our study utilized a decellularized omentum-based composite scaffold in combination with bone marrow MSCs and PRP as a source of growth factors. The documented success of omentum-based scaffolds in various fields of tissue engineering motivated our selection of this material for scaffold composition in the present study [47].

Omentum was selected as the scaffold material in this study due to its high ECM content. In the literature, there are multiple examples of omentum being used as a scaffold in applications outside of osteochondral defect repair. For instance, autologous decellularized omentum has been shown to provide suitable structural and mechanical support for cardiac cells [42]. The effects of decellularized porcine omentum on cell adhesion and proliferation have also been demonstrated in vitro [48]. Several studies have reported that omentum can support cell proliferation, modulate cellular functions, and maintain its architectural structure and vascular features even after decellularization [49]. Consistent with the findings of a previous study conducted by our senior author, the placenta-based scaffold demonstrated promising potential for hyaline cartilage regeneration [50]. However, in our study, while macroscopic healing of the defect area was observed, this was not corroborated by histomorphometric improvement. The inherently limited regenerative capacity of cartilage tissue compared to other tissues may have contributed to the scaffold’s lack of success in this context. Additionally, the failure to achieve the desired level of regeneration may be related to factors such as the decellularization protocol used for ECM-based scaffolds or possible limitations arising from the composite structure of the scaffold being overshadowed by other biomaterials. Supporting this, Farr et al. reported a 72% failure rate in a prospective clinical study involving 32 patients treated with decellularized osteochondral allografts for cartilage lesions, based on clinical, radiological, and histological outcomes [51].

In ECM-derived scaffolds, the removal of cellular and nuclear components is essential to reduce antigenicity and improve biocompatibility. Decellularization protocols typically combine chemical, enzymatic, and physical methods. Freeze–thaw cycles aid in disrupting cell membranes, while alcohol-based dehydration and delipidation enhance cell removal. The goal is to eliminate immunogenic cellular content while preserving key structural elements such as collagen fibers, GAGs, and adhesion proteins. In our study, we employed an optimized decellularization protocol described by Shevach et al. [42], and DNA quantification confirmed effective removal of nuclear material. Although successful decellularization was achieved, further evaluation of the impact of the protocol on scaffold architecture and mechanical integrity may help improve regenerative outcomes.

The combination of synthetic and natural polymers in scaffold design offers synergistic advantages over the use of each component alone [28]. GelMA, derived from collagen, is a biodegradable and biocompatible material that becomes more stable at body temperature upon methacrylation, making it suitable for tissue engineering applications [31]. GelMA has shown promising results in supporting MSC-mediated cartilage regeneration, particularly when combined with HA, a biomaterial known for its osteogenic potential. Composite scaffolds incorporating GelMA and HA have demonstrated improved osteointegration and osteogenic differentiation compared to HA alone[52, 53]. The positive effect of composite GelMA scaffolds containing nano-hydroxyapatite on osteochondral regeneration has also been demonstrated in a rabbit knee model of chondral defects [53]. Based on this evidence, our study utilized a bilayer scaffold composed of decellularized omentum, GelMA, and HA. While high GelMA concentrations increase compressive modulus, they may reduce porosity and swelling capacity, potentially impairing cell attachment. It was observed that GelMA-based scaffolds with a 5% concentration exhibited enhanced mesenchymal stem cell adhesion [54]. The limited regenerative success observed in our MSC groups may be due to reduced porosity and cell binding at higher GelMA concentrations. Further studies are needed to evaluate the diffusion capacity and porosity of such scaffolds.

Although an ideal design to replicate the osteochondral unit—comprising cartilage and subchondral bone—has not yet been established, layered composite scaffolds are increasingly favored over monolithic constructs. Previous in vivo and in vitro studies have shown that bilayer architectures improve regenerative outcomes by better mimicking the native tissue structure. In line with these findings, our study employed a bilayer scaffold with hydroxyapatite and GelMA in the lower layer and omentum combined with GelMA in the upper layer. This design likely contributed to the enhanced osteochondral regeneration observed in our experiments, supporting the notion that spatially tailored scaffolds can direct tissue-specific cellular responses and improve overall functional recovery [55–57].

Recent advances in nanotechnology have also highlighted the potential of green nanomaterials in tissue engineering applications. Green nanomaterials, derived from sustainable and eco-friendly sources such as plant extracts, natural polymers, or biogenic synthesis routes, offer significant advantages including reduced cytotoxicity, environmental safety, and inherent bioactivity [58]. Their incorporation into scaffold structures may improve biocompatibility, promote cellular adhesion, and enhance osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation. In the context of osteochondral regeneration, the integration of such green nanomaterials with composite scaffolds represents a promising approach, which could serve as a safe and effective alternative to conventional strategies while remaining open to further optimization and development [59, 60]. Moreover, combining green nanomaterials with omentum-based composite scaffolds may yield even more successful outcomes by leveraging the omentum’s extracellular matrix composition and bioactive factors; however, further experimental and clinical investigations are required to validate and optimize these strategies.

MSCs are widely studied in cartilage regeneration due to their multipotent differentiation capacity, particularly toward chondrogenic lineages. Several studies have demonstrated the positive effects of MSCs on osteochondral defect repair [61, 62]. While xenogeneic MSC transplantation carries a risk of immunogenicity, allogeneic MSCs are associated with reduced immune response and improved survival, which is why they were preferred in our study [63–65].

MSCs can be applied either directly into the defect site or seeded into scaffolds. In our model, 1 × 10⁶ bone marrow-derived MSCs were injected after scaffold implantation, a dose supported by previous studies showing enhanced [66]. Nishimori et al. demonstrated in a rat osteochondral defect model created using a drilling technique that the group receiving 1 × 106 mesenchymal stem cells showed improved outcomes compared to the control group, although the results were not fully optimal [61]. Although the MSC-containing groups in our study did not show significant improvement in extracellular matrix composition or cell morphology, they demonstrated better cartilage calcification. However, MSCs alone did not outperform the C or O groups in total histological scoring. When combined with PRP, MSC efficacy improved, suggesting a synergistic effect. These findings align with literature stating that the regenerative potential of MSCs can be limited by factors such as cell preparation methods, scaffold compatibility, and integration challenges, warranting further investigation [67].

Various cell labeling techniques have been described to evaluate the survival of transplanted MSCs within host tissues. Numerous animal studies have assessed MSC viability post-transplantation, reporting survival durations ranging from as long as 70 days to significant reductions by days 7 or 14 [68–70]. In our study, MSCs were labeled with iron particles; however, no iron-labeled MSCs were detected histologically after the 12-week follow-up period. This may be attributed to either the number of cells injected or the extended follow-up duration. Incorporating intermediate follow-up time points into the experimental protocol could provide more meaningful insights into cell survival dynamics.

PRP, through its growth factor content, promotes chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs and enhances matrix synthesis, contributing to tissue regeneration [71]. In this study, PRP was prepared using a double-centrifugation method to maximize growth factor release [72], and allogeneic PRP was preferred due to its demonstrated effects on cell proliferation, matrix production, and anti-inflammatory properties [73]. Although PRP combined with the scaffold did not show histomorphological superiority over the control or scaffold-only groups, its combination with MSCs resulted in modest improvements in cell morphology, GAG content, cartilage integrity, and integration with surrounding tissue. These findings support PRP’s potential additive effect, particularly when compared to the MSC-only group. Kon et al. reported that while hydroxyapatite-collagen scaffolds enhance osteochondral lesion repair, the addition of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) may disrupt the regenerative process, resulting in amorphous cartilage and poorly organized subchondral bone tissue [74].

In our study, outcomes were assessed using both histomorphological ICRS and O’Driscoll scoring systems [75]. A strong correlation was observed between the two histological scoring methods; however, when compared to macroscopic findings, only the O’Driscoll scores showed a mild correlation, while the ICRS scores did not correlate. These results highlight the limited reliability of macroscopic evaluation in osteochondral healing, reinforcing the importance of histological assessments for accurate interpretation of regenerative outcomes.

This study has several limitations. A single 12-week follow-up period was chosen without interim evaluations. The doses of administered MSCs and PRP were not optimized, and experimental groups assessing the isolated effects of PRP and MSCs were not included, as the primary objective was to evaluate their combined effect with the implanted scaffold. Additionally, the structural architecture of the implanted scaffold was not assessed by electron microscopy.

Conclusion

The application of a single-dose, 12-week protocol involving the decellularized omentum-based xenograft, MSCs, and PRP demonstrated limited efficacy in osteochondral defect regeneration. Macroscopically and with respect to specific histomorphometric parameters, the omentum was found to contribute to the healing process. Nevertheless, the study highlights the potential of omentum-based scaffolds as a biomaterial and suggests that further investigations combining this structure with improved architectural designs and advanced biomaterials may contribute to the development of future regenerative strategies for osteochondral repair. In this context, omentum-based composite scaffolds could represent a valuable alternative to current treatment modalities, although they remain open to further optimization and development.
