All-Cause and Disease-Specific Mortality Among U.S. Adults Living With Co-Existing Cancer and Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Reed Mszar, Leah M Ferrucci, Danish Iltaf Satti, Erica S Spatz, Tarang Parekh, Lauren A Baldassarre, Sarah C Hull, Brenda Cartmel, Melinda L Irwin, Suparna C Clasen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite increasing evidence on the bi-directional association between cancer and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), less is known about differences in mortality among individuals with co-existing diseases.ObjectiveWe examined all-cause and disease-specific mortality among U.S. adults living with cancer, ASCVD, and co-existing cancer and ASCVD.MethodsAmong adults aged ≥18 years in the National Health Interview Survey (2005-2018) linked to the National Death Index (through December 31, 2019), we estimated multivariable-adjusted HRs (aHRs) and 95% CIs using Cox proportional hazards models. Mortality outcomes were compared across individuals with cancer alone, ASCVD alone, and co-existing conditions, relative to those with neither condition.ResultsAmong 388,632 individuals, 1.3% had co-existing cancer and ASCVD, representing approximately 3.0 million U.S. adults annually. Over a mean follow-up of 7.2 years, co-existing disease was associated with the highest all-cause mortality (aHR, 2.45 [95% CI: 2.23-2.70]), exceeding ASCVD alone (aHR, 1.93 [1.82-2.04]) and cancer alone (aHR, 1.90 [1.77-2.04]). Co-existing disease was associated with elevated risks of both cancer mortality (aHR, 4.18 [3.48-5.03]) and cardiovascular mortality (aHR, 2.73 [2.30-3.24]). Among those living with co-existing diseases, older age (≥75 years) (aHR, 6.02 [1.97-18.37]), diabetes (aHR, 1.23 [1.03-1.46]), and current/former smoking status (aHR, 1.26 [1.07-1.49]) were associated with increased hazards of all-cause mortality.ConclusionsAmong community-dwelling U.S. adults living with cancer and/or ASCVD, co-existing disease was associated with substantially higher mortality. These findings highlight the elevated burden of mortality among individuals with co-existing cancer and ASCVD and underscore the importance of integrated cardio-oncology care.Key words: atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, cancer, cardio-oncology, mortality

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and cancer are two of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in the United States.1 Cancer survivors are known to have an increased risk of incident ASCVD compared to individuals without a history of cancer,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 which is partly attributable to shared risk factors and pathophysiologic mechanisms between both diseases.10, 11, 12, 13 In addition, certain cancer treatments have cardiotoxic properties that can further exacerbate cardiovascular risk among cancer survivors.14, 15, 16 While some recent observational studies have shown that individuals with prior ASCVD, including heart failure, have a higher risk of cancer mortality than those without ASCVD,17, 18, 19, 20, 21 the combination of limited follow-up periods, use of less contemporary cohorts and non-U.S. populations, and a focus on only specific cancer types (eg, leukemia/lymphoma, breast, prostate, and lung cancer) has limited the broader generalizability of these findings.

In recent years, there has also been increased attention on the bi-directional link between ASCVD and cancer,22 and a large proportion of the population is estimated to have co-existing diseases (eg, ∼16% based on data from the United Kingdom).23 With prior cohort studies using specific eligibility criteria (eg, those without prior ASCVD or existing cancer at baseline) in order to assess the relationship between ASCVD incidence and cancer mortality, or vice versa, and investigate mortality risks attributable to shared risk factors rather than existing ASCVD,24, 25, 26 minimal information exists pertaining to the secondary prevention of ASCVD in cancer survivorship populations and the population with co-existing chronic diseases.

As the cancer survivor population is projected to increase to 26 million survivors by 204027 and is at greater risk of developing and dying from other causes, primarily ASCVD, there is an urgent need to quantify the mortality risks for those with co-existing diseases in a contemporary U.S. population. To address this gap, using a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, we assessed all-cause, cardiovascular-specific, and cancer-specific mortality risks associated with combined and separate history of cancer and ASCVD. We also examined sociodemographic characteristics and shared risk factors associated with greater mortality risks in those individuals with co-existing diseases.

Methods

Study design and population

We used multiple years (2005-2018) of pooled data from the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), an annual weighted survey of the noninstitutionalized U.S. population that consists of interviews and a multistage sampling strategy to produce nationally representative estimates of a broad range of health conditions and behaviors.28 Individuals aged ≥18 years with complete, nonmissing information on cancer and ASCVD history and mortality outcomes were included in this analysis. All materials and data have been made available by the National Center for Health Statistics and can be accessed at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhis/index.htm. As all data are deidentified and publicly available, this study was exempt from ethics approval.

Cancer and ASCVD status

The NHIS methodology has been reported previously.29,30 Briefly, self-reported cancer status was assessed based on the following question: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professionals that you had cancer or a malignancy of any kind?” If the participant responded affirmatively to this prompt, they were then asked to specify the type of cancer. Individuals with nonmelanoma skin cancer were not considered as having a history of cancer in this analysis. In addition, individuals were asked to specify the age when they were first diagnosed with the specific type of cancer. Participants’ time since their cancer diagnosis was calculated by subtracting their age at diagnosis from their reported age at the time of survey administration. Individuals’ time since cancer diagnosis was categorized as <1 year, 1 to <5 years, 5 to <10 years, and ≥10 years. Similar to previous studies, we used a self-reported coronary or cerebrovascular disease diagnosis to classify individuals with ASCVD.31, 32, 33 Specifically, individuals were considered to have ASCVD if they reported “Yes” to any of the following questions: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professionals that you had … coronary heart disease? … angina? … heart attack/myocardial infarction?… or a stroke? (Supplemental Table 1).

Mortality outcomes

Using public-use-linked mortality files derived from the data linkage between the NHIS and death certificate records from the National Death Index (NDI), we examined the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular-specific, and cancer-specific mortality with mortality follow-up data through December 31, 2019.34 All-cause mortality was defined as death by any cause (leading underlying causes of death coded based on the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Injuries, and Causes of Death). Cardiovascular-specific mortality included both diseases of the heart and cerebrovascular diseases, while cancer-specific mortality was defined as death due to malignant neoplasms.

Covariates

We adjusted for factors associated with ASCVD and cancer in our multivariable analysis: age (18-44 years, 45-54 years, 55-64 years, 65-74 years, ≥75 years), sex (male, female), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic [NH] white, NH Black, NH Asian, Hispanic, American Indian/Alaska Native, Multiracial), level of completed education (≥some college, ≤high school/general education diploma), family income (based on a percentage of the federal poverty limit and categorized as high income [≥400%], middle income [200%–<400%], low income [125%–<200%], and lowest income [<125%]), insurance status (private, public [Medicare and/or Medicaid], private + public, uninsured), marital status (married, widowed, divorced/separated, never married), region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West), immigrant status (U.S.-born, non-U.S.-born), and usual source of care (yes/no).

Shared cancer and ASCVD risk factors, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, overweight, obesity, and smoking, were also included in our multivariable adjusted models. Hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes were defined using the question, “Have you ever been told by a doctor or health professional that you have … hypertension or high blood pressure? … high cholesterol? … or diabetes or sugar diabetes?" Given the variable collection of self-reported high cholesterol during the study period, we also used the following question to define hyperlipidemia status: “During the past 12 months, have you been told you had high cholesterol?” Obesity was defined as a body mass index of ≥30.0 kg/m2 (ascertained from self-reported height and weight), and smoking status was defined as current/former or never. Covariates were selected a priori based on prior literature and clinical relevance as potential confounders of the association between cardio-oncology status and mortality, including sociodemographic characteristics, healthcare access variables, and cardiometabolic risk factors.

Statistical analysis

Our final analytic sample included 388,632 individuals. We excluded 554,492 participants for missing complete cancer and ASCVD data and 8,921 participants ineligible for mortality follow-up (Supplemental Figure 1). Baseline sociodemographic characteristics and shared risk factors were compared across ASCVD and/or cancer subgroups using chi-squared tests or Fischer’s exact tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-tests for continuous variables. To obtain nationally representative estimates, given the complex and multistage sampling design of the NHIS, we utilized sample weights that represented the inverse probability of selection into the sample adult supplement. These weights were adjusted for nonresponse and further post-stratified using the U.S. Census Bureau's population control totals (http://www.ipums.org).28,35 Per NHIS guidance, all person weights were pooled and divided by the number of years studied (14 years) given that this analysis aggregated multiple years of cross-sectional data (2005-2018).

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to calculate adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% CIs for all-cause, cardiovascular-specific, and cancer-specific mortality. Follow-up time was defined as the time from survey completion to the time of death or the end of the 15-year study follow-up period (December 31, 2019). Time of death using the public-use NHIS-NDI files was ascertained using quarter and year of death information by assigning the midpoint of the reported quarter as the death date to calculate approximate survival time, consistent with prior studies using this dataset.36, 37, 38 Individuals who died during the same quarter/year as their NHIS household interview were assigned a small, nonzero survival time (ie, 0.01 years [∼3.65 days]) to maintain their presence in the risk set for a minimal period and prevent a biased underestimation of early mortality. Separate event indicators were created for each cause-specific mortality outcome. Cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate cardiovascular- and cancer-specific mortality, with deaths from nonindex causes treated as competing events and censored at the time of occurrence. This approach estimates cause-specific hazard ratios, reflecting the instantaneous risk of the mortality outcome among individuals who remained alive and free of competing causes of death. We assessed the proportional hazards assumption by testing interactions between the primary exposure and log-transformed follow-up time in survey-weighted Cox models. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used for descriptive visualization of all-cause and disease-specific mortality across disease groups. Log-rank tests were used to compare mortality distributions between the four disease groups. Crude mortality rates were calculated as the number of deaths divided by total person-years of follow-up and expressed per 1,000 person-years. Person-time was accrued from the NHIS interview date until death or censoring at the end of the data linkage (December 31, 2019), whichever occurred first. Estimates incorporated the NHIS complex survey design using sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units.

Potential interactions by age and sex were assessed using cross-product interaction terms, comparing full and reduced models via likelihood ratio tests to examine the association between ASCVD and/or cancer status and mortality outcomes. Focusing on the population with a history of both ASCVD and cancer, we also conducted exploratory analyses to evaluate the risk of all-cause mortality (given smaller sample sizes for the disease-specific mortality outcomes) by sociodemographic characteristics and shared risk factors. Among those with a history of cancer, we also investigated all-cause and disease-specific mortality rates, comparing those with and without ASCVD across the most prevalent cancer types, as well as by age at individuals’ initial cancer diagnosis and the time since their initial cancer diagnosis. For this exploratory analysis, we limited the sample to survivors’ first reported diagnosis and excluded subsequent self-reported diagnoses. Lastly, sensitivity analyses were conducted, both excluding cancer survivors with “unspecified cancer,” given the potential for misclassification, and restricting the cohort to individuals with at least 5 years of follow-up to minimize potential reverse causation from subacute conditions. All statistical analyses were survey-weighted and performed using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute), and statistical tests were two-sided with a statistical significance level of 0.05.

Results

Overall sample characteristics

Our total sample of 388,632 U.S. adults represented 225.2 million individuals annually, with a median age: 48.0 [IQR: 30.0] years, 50.8% female; and 66.9% NH White (Table 1). The mean follow-up time for the mortality outcomes was 7.2 (standard deviation [SD]: 3.9) years. We found that 86.7% of individuals did not have a history of either ASCVD or cancer, 6.9% (n = 30,663) had ASCVD alone, 5.0% (n = 21,658) had cancer alone, and 1.3% (n = 6,012) had co-existing diseases. In older individuals (aged ≥75 years), this translated to 4.9% having neither disease, 27% (n = 9,188) with ASCVD alone, 22.2% (n = 5,540) with cancer alone, and 43.8% (n = 2,739) lived with co-existing diseases. Overall, 20.8% of all cancer survivors in our sample (n = 27,670) had ASCVD.

Among the 6,012 adults (representing 3.0 million individuals annually) who reported a history of both ASCVD and cancer, the median age was 73.0 (IQR: 17.0) years, which was older than those with ASCVD (median: 67.0 [IQR: 20.0] years) or cancer (median: 65.0 [IQR: 21.0] years) alone and nearly 30 years older than those with neither disease (median: 44.0 [IQR: 28.0] years).

ASCVD- and cancer-specific characteristics

In the overall sample, the most common ASCVD subtype was coronary heart disease (4.6%) (Supplemental Table 2). Breast cancer was the most prevalent cancer type, present in 23.8% of those with a history of cancer alone and 17.7% among those with co-existing diseases, followed by prostate cancer (14.6% in adults with cancer alone and 22.7% among adults with co-existing diseases). Among cancer survivors, the mean age at initial diagnosis was 57.0 (SD: 17.8) years in those with ASCVD and 51.4 (SD: 17.7) years in those without ASCVD, with the mean time since initial diagnosis being 12.4 (SD: 13.5) years in those with ASCVD and 10.8 (SD: 11.6) years in those without ASCVD.

All-cause and disease-specific mortality

During the follow-up period, there were 37,023 deaths, of which 11,139 (29.3%) were due to cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, and 8,588 (24.1%) were due to cancer/malignant neoplasms (Supplemental Table 2). Among adults living with co-existing diseases, 33.7% died of cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease (compared to those with ASCVD alone [41.4%], cancer alone [18.5%], and 29.4% died of cancer (compared to those with ASCVD alone [13.6%], cancer alone [45.2%]).

After adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics and shared risk factors, individuals living with co-existing diseases had a higher hazard of all-cause mortality (aHR, 2.45 [95% CI: 2.23-2.70]) than individuals with neither disease (Table 2). Those with ASCVD alone (aHR, 1.93 [1.82-2.04]) or cancer alone (aHR, 1.90 [1.77-2.04]) also had a significantly higher adjusted hazard of all-cause mortality than those with neither disease (Central Illustration). Individuals with co-existing diseases had the highest mortality rate of 79.0 per 1,000 person-years, followed by those with ASCVD (48.7), cancer alone (34.8), and those with neither disease (8.6).

In terms of the disease-specific mortality outcomes, individuals with a history of both ASCVD and cancer had more than a four-fold higher adjusted hazard of cancer-specific mortality (aHR, 4.18 [3.48-5.03]) than those with neither disease (Figure 1B, Table 2). Those with ASCVD alone also had an increased hazard of cancer-specific mortality (aHR, 1.40 [1.21-1.63]). Adults with cancer alone had nearly a four-times increased hazard of cancer-specific mortality (aHR, 4.49 [3.94-5.11]), but no statistically significant increase in the hazard of cardiovascular-specific mortality (aHR, 1.00 [0.86-1.18]). Individuals with co-existing diseases (aHR, 2.73 [2.30-3.24]) and those with ASCVD alone (aHR, 2.66 [2.40-2.95]) had a similar increased adjusted hazard of cardiovascular-specific mortality (Figure 1C, Table 2) compared to those with neither disease. Individuals with co-existing diseases had the greatest cancer-specific mortality rate of 22.9 deaths per 1,000 person-years, followed by those with cancer alone (15.1), ASCVD alone (6.6), and neither disease (1.9) and cardiovascular-specific mortality at 26.3 deaths per 1,000 person-years, followed by those with ASCVD alone (20.2), cancer alone (6.9), and those with neither disease (2.3).

Stratified and subgroup analyses

Women (vs men) with co-existing diseases had a higher hazard of all-cause mortality (aHR, 2.70 [2.37-3.09] vs aHR, 2.28 [1.99-2.62]) but a lower hazard of cancer-specific mortality among those with cancer alone (aHR, 4.17 [3.52-4.93] vs aHR, 4.71 [3.87-5,73]) (Table 2). Across age groups, the co-occurrence of ASCVD and cancer was consistently associated with the highest all-cause mortality, with particularly pronounced hazards at younger ages (aHR, 4.10 [1.38-12.22] for ages 18 to 44 and aHR, 4.20 [2.55-6.93] for ages 45 to 54 years). For cause-specific outcomes, cancer was the main driver of cancer mortality, while ASCVD status primarily drove cardiovascular-specific mortality, but their co-occurrence still conferred substantially elevated hazards for both outcomes across most age strata (Supplemental Table 3).

Across cancer types, ASCVD was consistently associated with higher all-cause mortality, with particularly large effects in uterine (aHR, 2.78 [1.91-4.06]) and cervical cancer (aHR, 2.76 [1.45-5.26]), while cancer-specific mortality was generally not elevated in ASCVD, except for lymphoma (aHR, 1.74 [1.12-2.70]) and was lower in prostate cancer (aHR, 0.61 [0.39-0.94]). In contrast, ASCVD was strongly and consistently associated with increased cardiovascular mortality across nearly all cancer types, with pronounced risks in cervical (aHR, 8.98 [2.75-29.31]) and lung cancer (aHR, 7.41 [3.11-17.67]) (Supplemental Table 4).

ASCVD was associated with an increased hazard of all-cause mortality across most age groups and time since diagnosis ranges, with the strongest effects observed in middle-aged adults (aged 45-54 years: aHR, 1.88 [1.40-2.52]) and at 5 to 10 years after the diagnosis (aHR, 1.96 [1.58-2.44]). In contrast, ASCVD showed little consistent association with cancer-specific mortality but was strongly associated with cardiovascular-specific mortality across nearly all strata, with particularly elevated risks in those aged 45 to 54 years (aHR, 4.85 [2.71-8.69]) and 5 to 10 years after diagnosis (aHR, 4.68 [2.70-8.10]) (Supplemental Table 5).

Sensitivity analyses

When restricting our population to those living with co-existing diseases, we found that increasing age showed a strong gradient in all-cause mortality risk, with a markedly higher hazard among those ≥75 years (aHR, 6.02 [1.97-18.37]) compared with younger groups, while most sociodemographic factors, including sex, race/ethnicity, income, and region, were not significantly associated with all-cause mortality in this subgroup. Diabetes (aHR, 1.23 [1.03-1.46]) and smoking (aHR, 1.26 [1.07-1.49]) were associated with increased all-cause mortality, whereas obesity was associated with a lower mortality hazard (aHR, 0.76 [0.64-0.92]) (Figure 2).

Excluding participants with unspecified cancer (n = 1,779) did not meaningfully affect the results of the association between ASCVD and cancer status with the mortality outcomes (Supplemental Table 6). In addition, when using alternative reference groups, co-existing ASCVD and cancer remained associated with elevated all-cause mortality compared with ASCVD alone (aHR, 1.27 [1.15-1.41]) and cancer alone (aHR, 1.29 [1.16-1.44]), with a stronger relative effect in women than in men when cancer was the reference (Supplemental Table 7). After further adjustment for ASCVD status, cancer remained strongly associated with higher all-cause mortality (aHR, 1.64 [1.54-1.74]) and markedly higher cancer-specific mortality (aHR, 4.04 [3.60-4.53]), with similar effects in men and women when compared to those without cancer (Supplemental Table 8). Despite this attenuation in magnitude, the relative ordering of associations across exposure groups remained consistent (Supplemental Table 9).

Discussion

In this observational cohort study using linked NHIS and NDI data from nearly 390,000 adults in the U.S. from 2005 to 2018, representing mort than 225 million individuals annually, we found that those with a history of co-existing diseases had approximately a 2.5-fold higher adjusted hazard of all-cause mortality and a 4.2-fold and 2.7-fold increased hazard of cancer- and cardiovascular-specific mortality, respectively, when compared to those with neither disease. These findings highlight the importance of ongoing cardiovascular risk factor management and ASCVD monitoring in contemporary cohorts of cancer survivors. Interestingly, regarding the bi-directionality relationship between ASCVD and cancer, although disease-specific mortality did not substantially differ between those with co-existing diseases and either disease alone, individuals with co-existing diseases had a significantly higher hazard of all-cause mortality than those with either ASCVD or cancer alone (1.9-times). To our knowledge, this is one of the largest, nationally representative studies among U.S. adults to assess mortality outcomes over an average follow-up of 7 years and between distinct subpopulations with and without ASCVD and cancer.

Our findings on the increased mortality risks associated with the presence of ASCVD among cancer survivors add to the growing evidence base from prior observational studies. In particular, using data from the Kaiser Permanente Southern California–Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Registry (median follow-up: 4.4 years [range: 0-8 years]), Armenian et al. reported significantly worse overall survival among cancer survivors with cardiovascular disease (CVD) than among those without CVD at 5 years (75% and 87%, respectively).6 This compares to our finding that those with co-existing diseases had the highest risk of all-cause mortality, followed by individuals with ASCVD alone and then cancer survivors without ASCVD. In addition to using similar comparison groups that reflect real-world comorbidities in cardio-oncology, the current analysis builds on this landmark study by extending the generalizability of the results to adults in the general U.S. population and including a longer follow-up duration.

In addition, the observed proportion of individuals with co-existing diseases corresponds to prevalence estimates reported in previous studies. A population-based study conducted among those diagnosed with cancer in the United Kingdom from 2013 to 2018 found 16.2% of individuals had prior CAD, which was considered to be an underestimate given the focus on hospitalizations for case ascertainment.23 In our U.S.-based NHIS-NDI analysis, approximately 21% of cancer survivors reported a history of ASCVD, which included prior myocardial infarction, stroke, angina, or coronary heart disease. While this difference may be attributable to national differences in ASCVD risk factor burden and clinical criteria for defining existing ASCVD, the roughly comparable percentages underscore the large proportion of cancer survivors with existing ASCVD who is at high risk of future cardiovascular complications and adverse events. Furthermore, these findings further strengthen the case for integrating long-term cardiac surveillance and multidisciplinary cardio-oncology care in cancer survivorship programs, particularly given that nearly 188,000 deaths per year in the U.S. have been attributed to comorbid cancer and CVD.39 This is supported by the recent European Society of Cardiology guidelines on cardio-oncology40 that recommend annual assessment of cardiovascular risk factors (eg, blood pressure, lipid profile, hemoglobin A1c, electrocardiogram, natriuretic peptides) in cancer survivors (Class I Recommendation) and referral to specialized cardio-oncology care for survivors exposed to potentially cardiotoxic anticancer therapies.

Focusing on the higher-risk population with a history of both cancer and ASCVD (representing nearly 3 million U.S. adults annually), we found that specific subgroups carried an increased risk of all-cause mortality, even after adjusting for a breadth of sociodemographic characteristics and shared risk factors. Notably, we found that older individuals and those with a lower level of completed education, as well as individuals with diabetes and a current or former smoking status, had greater risks than the respective reference groups. These results can be interpreted in conjunction with key research on disparities and social determinants in cardio-oncology.41,42 Specifically, recent research has evaluated the association between composite social determinants of health scores and cardiovascular health among cancer survivors. Specifically, cancer survivors with higher levels of social deprivation were found to have an increased likelihood of suboptimal cardiovascular health based on the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8,30 as well as a higher risk of mortality.43 Moreover, individuals with comorbid cancer and ASCVD have been shown to experience a higher burden of financial hardship from medical bills and related consequences such as delayed/foregone care, food insecurity, and medication nonadherence compared to those with cancer or ASCVD alone.29 Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of early prevention and management of shared risk factors among cancer survivors, with a particular focus on ensuring equitable access to timely and affordable care to prevent exacerbating existing health disparities in higher-risk populations.

For the disease-specific mortality outcomes in the competing risk model, while we observed that individuals with ASCVD alone had an increased adjusted risk of cancer-specific mortality, those with cancer alone did not have a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular-specific mortality than those with neither ASCVD nor cancer. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence on the increased risk of cancer mortality observed among adults with existing ASCVD but is in contrast with some prior observational studies on cardiovascular mortality in cancer survivors.8,44,45 When interpreting these findings, it is important to note that NHIS does not capture heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or arrhythmias, which are key manifestations of cancer therapy-related cardiotoxicity. As a result, our ASCVD definition was limited to self-reported myocardial infarction, stroke, coronary heart disease, and angina. The absence of heart failure likely leads to an underestimation of the true cardiovascular burden among cancer survivors, particularly those exposed to potentially cardiotoxic therapies.46 Accordingly, the cancer survivorship population represented in NHIS may reflect a relatively lower-risk cardiovascular phenotype compared with studies using adjudicated outcomes or clinical diagnostic codes that focus on heart failure and related conditions.

Cardiovascular mortality risk also varies substantially by cancer type, with lower standardized mortality ratios having been previously reported for breast, prostate, and cervical cancer and melanoma,44,45 which together constituted a substantial proportion of the population of cancer survivors without ASCVD in this study. In this manner, the population of cancer survivors in NHIS was composed primarily of breast and prostate cancers, while cancers with poorer prognoses, such as lung cancer, were underrepresented relative to SEER incidence data.47 This reflects survivor and selection effects inherent to a community-dwelling survey and indicates that the cancer-alone group likely represents a comparatively favorable prognostic subset. As a result, observed differences may underestimate the true mortality burden associated with cancer diagnoses characterized by higher lethality. In addition, with the majority of the NHIS cancer survivorship population being more than 5 years from their initial cancer diagnosis, this may also contribute to a reduction in ASCVD-related risks, as prior studies have shown that excess ASCVD risk associated with cancer was greatest during the first year following a cancer diagnosis and tends to decline over time.4 Using the linked NHIS-NDI dataset to investigate cancer mortality risks in those with ASCVD adds to the relatively smaller, but growing, evidence base conducted in the general U.S. population,21 with most prior studies conducted in Japan19 and parts of Europe.17,18,20

Study Limitations

The results of this study should be evaluated in the context of several limitations. First, individuals’ history of prior ASCVD and cancer was ascertained via self-report and may be subject to misclassification bias. In the case of self-reported cancer diagnoses, previous research has found that individuals generally underreport rather than overreport their cancer history,48 and false-negative rates can vary widely by cancer type.49 Self-reported ASCVD may not fully reflect the underlying atherosclerotic disease burden given this approach captures clinically recognized events but may misclassify individuals with subclinical or undiagnosed disease and is subject to recall bias. In this study, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding those with unspecified cancer and observed similar findings for our primary outcome of all-cause and disease-specific mortality. Second, the absence of information on the timing of cancer diagnosis relative to ASCVD onset and cancer treatment history limits causal interpretation of these findings. Without temporal sequencing, we cannot distinguish ASCVD that preceded cancer from ASCVD that developed following cancer diagnosis or treatment, nor can we assess treatment-specific cardiotoxic effects. These factors may contribute to survivor bias and exposure misclassification and likely result in conservative estimates of cardiovascular risk among cancer survivors. Accordingly, results should be interpreted as reflecting mortality risk among adults living with co-existing cancer and ASCVD rather than risks attributable to specific treatment exposures or disease trajectories. Relatedly, this study is subject to survivor and selection bias due to the cross-sectional design of NHIS and its restriction to the noninstitutionalized population. Individuals who experienced severe ASCVD shortly after a cancer diagnosis and died prior to survey participation, as well as those residing in nursing homes or other institutions due to advanced morbidity, are not captured. Consequently, our estimates likely underestimate absolute mortality risk and may attenuate differences between disease groups. Importantly, comparisons between individuals with ASCVD alone and cancer alone should be interpreted as comparisons among community-dwelling survivors rather than at the time of disease onset. Early mortality following cancer diagnosis, particularly related to cardiotoxic therapies, would be expected to disproportionately affect individuals with both conditions, suggesting that observed relative risks may represent conservative estimates of the true burden associated with multimorbidity.

Third, information on cancer stage or grade, receipt of potentially cardiotoxic cancer therapies, and cardiovascular pharmacotherapy is not collected in NHIS. Consequently, our analyses could not adjust for treatment-related factors known to influence cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, including exposure to anthracyclines, chest radiation, targeted or immune-based therapies, or use of cardioprotective medications such as statins or antiplatelet agents. This limitation may contribute to residual confounding and likely results in conservative estimates of cardiovascular risk among cancer survivors. Future population-based cardio-oncology studies and national registries (for example, CARDIOTOX Registry, Global Cardio Oncology Registry)50,51 should incorporate detailed treatment and medication data to enable more refined cardiovascular risk stratification. Fourth, since only year and quarter of death were available in the public-use NHIS-NDI file, we estimated the date of death using the midpoint of the reported quarter. While this approach introduces uncertainty in the precise timing of death and potentially a misclassification of survival times, the impact is likely minimal due to the relatively long follow-up period and has been used in previous studies. Finally, multiple subgroup analyses were performed across demographic strata, increasing the potential for type I error due to multiple comparisons. These analyses were conducted to explore potential heterogeneity and assess consistency of associations rather than to test prespecified confirmatory hypotheses, and no formal adjustment for multiple testing was applied. Accordingly, subgroup-specific findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

Using nationally representative data in U.S. adults, those with a history of co-existing diseases had a significantly higher adjusted hazard of all-cause, cardiovascular-, and cancer-specific mortality than individuals with neither disease. While the risk of disease-specific mortality (ie, cardiovascular and cancer mortality) was comparable between individuals with co-existing disease and those with either disease alone (i.e., ASCVD or cancer alone), all-cause mortality was approximately 1.3-times higher in those with co-existing diseases than either disease alone. Long-term surveillance and management of shared risk factors in the setting of secondary ASCVD prevention are critical for individuals with a history of both cancer and ASCVD. Future research taking into account the temporality of cancer and ASCVD diagnoses, along with cancer treatment information and changes in shared risk factors over time, is needed.

COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: Cancer survivors with a history of ASCVD are at an increased risk of all-cause mortality compared to those with ASCVD or cancer alone and individuals with neither disease, findings that warrant continuous monitoring and management of shared risk factors throughout long-term cancer survivorship.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future studies are needed to evaluate how the temporality in cancer and ASCVD incidence influences adverse outcomes, along with the impact of cancer treatment-related cardiotoxicities on primary and secondary prevention populations for ASCVD.
