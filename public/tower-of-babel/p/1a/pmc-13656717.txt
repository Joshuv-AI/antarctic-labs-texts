The polycyclic mitochondrial genome of Angraecum sesquipedale mediated by intricate repetitive and diverse sequence compositions
Authors: Wentao Sheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAngraecum sesquipedale (DC.) Danser (commonly called Darwin's orchid) is an ornamental horticultural wild plant with ecological, economic, and evolutionary research value. While its chloroplast and nuclear genome sequence have been reported, the mitochondrial genome research remains absent. To analyze the genetic variation foundation of Darwin's orchid at the genomic level, this study utilizes genomic data of 45.38 Gigabase Illumina and 36.79 Gigabase PacBio HiFi sequencing in CNCB (PRJCA030186), to decipher the mitochondrial genome structure, composition, and sequence variation patterns of this plant.ResultsHerein, the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome length was 711,372 bp, composing of 14 circular DNAs and including 38 protein-coding, 30 tRNA, three ribosomal RNA genes, and two pseudo-genes. Its sub-mitochondrial genome length ranges among Circle 1 and Circle 14 were from 23,873 bp to 105,231 bp, and the GC content ranges from 39.22% to 44.41%, respectively. In total, 216 SSR, 103 tandem, and 1019 dispersed repeats are discriminated. RNA editing site prediction revealed 709 RNA editing events. Codon usage analysis indicated a preference for A/T (U) codon ending. Intergenomic DNA transfer was identified in all of the 14 chromosomes, with chloroplast DNA transfer fragments accounting for 11.03% of the mitochondrial genome. Comparative analysis showed that GC content is conserved, but their structure, size, and gene content of mitochondrial genome chromosomes vary significantly. Phylogenetic analysis showed that Hygrochilus parishii clustered with A. sesquipedale, indicating that the two were closely related. The relationship is agreement with the evolutionary history and taxonomic status of Darwin's Orchid. Compared to the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genomes and other reported Orchidaceae plants, the mitochondrial genome of Darwin's Orchid has lost three ribosomal protein-coding genes and sdh3.ConclusionThe mitochondrial genome characteristics and phylogenetic evolution relationship of Darwin's orchid were analyzed, providing a reference basis for enhancing germplasm identification, resource diversity development, utilization, and new variety breeding research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09366-0.Keywords: Angraecum sesquipedale, Mitochondrial genome, Phylogenetic evolution, Genome characteristics

Background

The Orchidaceae family is one of the most diverse groups of angiosperms, accounting for about 10% of the total number of angiosperms, containing 801 genera and approximately 28,237 species [1]. Orchid plants have highly diverse morphological and physiological characteristics in terms of flower phenotype [2]. The genus Angraecum (Comet orchid) is a representative group within the Vandeae tribe and Angraecinae subfamily of the orchid family, and is also the most numerous genus, containing 223 species [3]. This genus is mainly distributed on the African continent, Madagascar island, and its surrounding islands, with Madagascar island containing approximately 142 species [4]. Comet orchid plants have extremely high morphological diversity and unique floral fragrance, and most of their flowers have phenotypic characteristics adapted to the pollination of moths, including a flower spacing that can store nectar. For example, the long-distance comet orchid (Angraecum sesquipedale (DC.) Danser), commonly called Darwin's orchid, has a flower spacing of approximately 42 cm, which matches the length of the mouthparts of the pollinator Xanthopan praedicta in Madagascar [5, 6]. And some comet orchid plants can release special fragrances at night to attract nocturnal pollinators. For example, A. sesquipedale and A. compactum species have been identified as plants that release fragrance at night, making the genus Angraecum diverse in morphology-adaptation, ecology evolution and fragrance uniqueness [7]. As of 20th December 2025, the nuclear genomes of 42 species of Orchidaceae have been reported, including two species of Apostasioideae, 36 species of Epidendroideae, one species of Vanilloideae, and three species of Orchidoideae (https://www.plabipd.de/plant_genomes_pa.ep). Furthermore, over 1268 complete chloroplast genome DNAs of plants belonging to the family Orchidaceae were released in the CNCB database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/cgir/genome?term=Orchidaceae), whereas 25 mitochondrial DNAs (mtDNAs) have only been published for the genus Dendrobium (5), Apostasia (1), Gastrodia (13), Nervilia (1), Paphiopedilum (1), Hygrochilus (1), Epipogium (1), Bletilla (1), and Cymbidium (1) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/?term=Orchidaceae+complete+mitochondrial+genome). Up to now, the nuclear genome data (PRJCA030186) and chloroplast genome (NC_086704.1) of A. sesquipedale have been reported in the genus Angraecum [8, 9]. However, research on Angraecum plants has focused on the phylogenetic relationships of the Subtrib. Angraecinae [4, 9], interactions with pollinators [6, 10–12], analysis of the unique floral components of Angraecum [13], and identification of wild resources [14, 15].

Orchidaceae, as the pinnacle representative of adaptive radiation in angiosperms, has achieved a classic example of pollination ecology with over 28,000 members thanks to their exquisite flower organ design [16]. However, the mitochondrial genome research of this family is deeply mired in difficulties: Firstly, the high proportion of repetitive sequences (long terminal repeats retrotransposons > 30%) leads to highly fragmented assembly, and the average Contig N50 of the 25 published Orchidaceae mitochondrial genomes (as of December 20, 2025) is only 28.7 kb, which is less than 15% of the same index in the Poaceae family (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ nuccore). Secondly, Orchidaceae plants have a large population, diverse lifestyles, and unique floral characteristics, making their mitochondrial genome a giant dynamic database that constantly undergoes internal recombination and absorbs foreign DNA. Its complexity is reflected in the large and small changes in the genome size, diverse mitochondrial genome structure, rich DNA transfer, a large number of repetitive sequences, active homologous recombination, extreme intra- and inter-species variation, and loss of functional genes, far beyond our understanding of typical single circular plant mitochondrial genomes in angiosperm [17–23]. Thirdly, the tissue-specific energy metabolism regulatory network is completely blank, especially the lack of analysis of respiratory chain gene expression profiles for high-energy consuming structures such as flower organs (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/?term=Orchidaceae). In this context, A. sesquipedale became a key breakthrough due to its iconic evolutionary position. In 1862, Charles Robert Darwin predicted the existence of a matching long billed armyworm (Xanthopan morganii praedicta) based on its 30 cm long flower distance. This insect was furtherly confirmed by entomologists Rothschild and Jordan in 1903 [24], and 130 years later captured by ecologist Wasserthal in 1997, making the species a "living monument" to the theory of coevolution [8, 15]. However, this extreme adaptive phenotype harbors an energy crisis: the continuous synthesis of large amounts of polysaccharides and proteins is required for floral development, with an ATP consumption rate of 4.2 times that of leaf cells. The low light environment in the native habitat of Madagascar (canopy light transmittance < 15%) further limits photosynthetic capacity, forcing the mitochondrial respiratory chain associated genes to bear enormous selection pressure [25]. Thus, the core scientific question was raised: Has the Darwin's orchid mitochondrial genome resolved the energy deficit caused by its extreme reproductive strategy through structural recombination, horizontal gene transfer events, and molecular evolutionary innovation?

Up to now, no reports were released on the mitochondrial genome research of Darwin's orchid. Therefore, in order to analyze the genetic mechanism of the special flower spacing of Darwin's orchid at the mitochondrial genome level and explore the evolutionary relationship of A. sesquipedale, this study mainly uses second-generation and third-generation sequencing data to assemble the Darwin's orchid mitochondrial genome, analyze the size, gene composition, repeat sequences, genome collinearity, and systematic evolution of the Darwin's orchid mitochondrial genome, and lay a theoretical foundation for elaborating the rules of Darwin's orchid special traits and analyzing the phylogenetics of this species.

Results

Polycyclic chromosome structure

The A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome was assembled into 14 circular chromosomes, with sizes ranging from 23,873 bp to 105,231 bp, totaling 711,372 bp (Fig. 1 and S1-S2). The sequence numbers in NCBI were PX562769-PX562782. The average GC content of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome was 42.76%, with GC content among chromosomes varying between 39.22% and 44.54% (Table S1). For the assembled chromosomes, a sequencing average depth of 105 × for long-read fragments and 2150 × for short-read fragments was achieved, with consistent results between long-read and short-read assemblies (Figure S3 and S4).

Mitochondrial genome map of A. sesquipedale. This genome contains 14 circular chromosomes

This mitochondrial genome encodes 73 genes, comprising 38 protein coding genes (PCGs), 30 tRNA genes, three ribosomal RNA genes (rrn5, rrn18, and rrn26), and two pseudo-genes (Fig. 1, Table S1). The number of genes distributed on Chromosome 1 is the highest, with 11 PCGs and four tRNAs, while Chromosomes 6, 8, 11, and 14 have the fewest genes distributed. Among them, Chromosome 6 has one tRNA and one rRNA gene, Chromosome 8 has one PCG, Chromosome 11 has one tRNA gene, and Chromosome 14 has one PCG and one tRNA gene. In terms of sequence distribution, the GC content of protein functional genes is 43.81%, encoding a total of 32,973 bp, accounting for 4.64% of the complete genome length. The proportion of gene coding region length is low, and the gene distribution is sparse. Among the PCGs, nad5 has the longest length of 2015 bp, while rps14 has the shortest length of 303 bp. The tRNA gene has a GC content of 51.86%, encoding a total of 2200 bp, while the rRNA gene has a GC content of 52.82%, encoding a total of 5511 bp. The proportion of these sequences indicates that the mitochondrial genome is mainly made up of non-coding gene sequences. Nad1 and rps10 use ACG (ATG) as the starting codon, while atp9 and ccmFc use CGA (TGA) as the terminating codon (Table S2).

In terms of gene function, most genes in the mitochondrial genome are related to proteins and enzymes required for their own functions, such as 10 genes related to ribosome synthesis, five genes related to ATP synthase, as well as genes involved in encoding mature enzyme genes (matR) and regulating cytochrome synthesis (ccmB, ccmC, ccmFc, and ccmFn). Apart from biosynthesis, other genes are closely related to the regulation of electron transport chains, with a total of 15 genes involved. Among them, there are nine genes in complex system I (nad1-nad4, nad4L, nad5-nad7, and nad9), two genes in complex system II (sdh3, and sdh4), one gene in complex system III (cob), and three genes in complex system IV (cox1-cox3). In total, 13 genes in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome contain introns, with nad1, nad2, and nad5-nad7 having the highest number of introns at four, while ccmFc, rpl2, rps10, rps3, trnI-TAT, trnR-ACG, and trnT-TGT have one intron. Rps19, trnC-NTC, trnN-GTT, trnP-TGG, and trnY-GTA contain two copies, trnC-GCA has three copies, and trnM-CAT has six copies. Rps11 and sdh4 genes were lost due to pseudogenes, while sdh3 gene was lost (Table S3).

Repeat sequence

The number and distribution of simple sequence repeat (SSR), tandem repeat, and dispersed repeat in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome are displayed in Fig. 2 and Table S4-S6. In total, 216 SSRs were discriminated in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, with the highest number of single nucleotide repeats (65), followed by two-nucleotide repeats (58), four-nucleotide repeats (55), three-nucleotide repeats (30), five-nucleotide repeats (7), and six-nucleotide repeats (1). In single nucleotide SSRs, A/T has the highest number of base repeats (65) and no G/C type. Among the two-nucleotide repeats, AT/TA base repeats have the highest number (35) and the highest proportion (60.34%). Chromosome 1 has the highest distribution of 36 SSRs, while Chromosome 14 has the lowest distribution of six SSRs (Fig. 2A and Table S4).

Recombination frequency in A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome. A The distribution of SSR repeats; B The distribution of tandem repeats; C The distribution of dispersed repeats; D The distribution of repetitive sequences in the genome

Meanwhile, 103 tandem repeat sequences were detected in the repeated sequence results, with length sizes ranging from 9 to 70 bp and copy numbers between 1.9 and 4.4. The total repeat length of the concatenated sequence is mainly within 100, with the highest number of 22 repeats between 31–40 bp, followed by 20 repeats between 41–50 bp, and 10 repeats greater than 100 bp. The number of tandem repeats on Chromosome 1 is the highest, at 24, while on Chromosome 13, it is the lowest, at 2 (Fig. 2B and Table S5).

A total of 1019 long repeat sequences were identified in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, containing 461 forward repeats (F) and 558 palindromic repeats (P). In the long repeat sequence of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, the sequence number with 30–39 bp is the highest (228), followed by 40–49 bp (261), and 50–59 bp ranks third (178). However, the number of sequences with 100–199 bp (121), 60–69 bp (68), 70–79 bp (54), 80–89 bp (45), 90–99 bp (17), 200–999 bp (43), and > 1000 bp (4) is relatively small. The longest segment is the F sequence, with a length of 1919 bp, located on Chromosome 2. Other sequences larger than 1000 bp are of P type, namely 1293 bp P on Chromosome 13, 1085 bp P on Chromosome 7, and 1069 bp P on Chromosome 3. Long repeat is unevenly distributed on various sub-mitochondrial genomes, with Chromosome 2 having the highest distribution of 268, followed by Chromosome 1 with 249, and Chromosome 3 with 109. Most chromosomes are between 9 and 72, with Chromosome 13 having 9 (Fig. 2C and Table S6).

Condon preference

The codon preference analysis revealed that there are 29 codons with the relative synymous codon usage (RSCU) values > 1, among which 28 terminate with A/T(U) bases, demonstrating a stronger preference for A/T(U)-ending codons (Fig. 3 and Table S7). Among the 78 genes, there are 10,841 codons (excluding stop codons). The codon usage of leucine (Leu), and serine (Ser) is the most similar, with Leu being the most frequently used, contributing​ for 10.13% (1098 codons) of the total. Cysteine (Cys) has the least codon usage, with only 151 codons or about 1.39% of the total. The codons UUU (phenylalanine) and AUU (isoleucine) show the highest preference in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, while the codons AUG (methionine) and UGG (tryptophan) exhibit no preference (Fig. 3).

PCG polymorphism

The nucleotide diversity (Pi) of the genes including nad3, nad4L, nad6, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, cox2, matR, mttB, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFc, ccmFn, rps1, rps2-rps4, rps7, rps10, rps13, rps14, rps19, rpl2, rpl5, and rrn18 was high, with Pi values greater than 0.02 for all genes (Fig. 4). And the Pi value of rrn18 was the highest, at 0.06424, indicating that the above genes may have undergone relatively relaxed selection pressure. The genes with a high number of variations are rrn18, ccmFn, rrn26, rps3, matR, rpl2, rps4, atp1, and ccmC, with variations of 360, 226, 205, 199, 185, 161, 159, 113, and 111, respectively. The number of variations in 30 genes is greater than 10 but less than 100, and the number of variations in the rrn5 gene is the least of only 4 (Table S8).

Nucleotide diversity analysis of A. sesquipedale mitochondrial PCGs

RNA editing event forecast

The post-transcriptional modification characteristics were revealed in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome by predicting RNA editing events in PCGs. In total, 709 RNA editing sites were discriminated across 40 PCGs (Fig. 5 and Table S9), primarily involving C-to-T(U) transitions. The nad4 gene was the most prominent, with 61 editing events identified, followed by the nad5 gene (43 sites). Additionally, four RNA editing events were detected in each of the rps19 and rps7 genes. It was observed that editing events were focused on the first and second base locations of the start codon. Furthermore, these RNA editing events led to amino acid inter-changes, such as leucine to phenylalanine, threonine to isoleucine, serine to phenylalanine, histidine to tyrosine, arginine to cysteine, and arginine to tryptophan. For these editing events, 338 edits resulted in the conversion of hydrophilic to hydrophobic amino acids, 64 edits resulted in the conversion of hydrophobic to hydrophilic amino acids, 80 edits maintained hydrophilicity, 222 edits maintained hydrophobicity, and five edits lead in the conversion of hydrophilic to terminating amino acids (Table 1). It can be seen that many amino acid inter-changes caused by RNA editing introduce more hydrophobic amino acids into protein structures, thereby changing their hydrophilic properties, which may have a vital role in regulating its gene expression.

DNA horizontal transfer

The mitochondrial genome size of A. sesquipedale (711,372 bp) is approximately 4.69 times that of its chloroplast genome (NC_086704.1, 151,818 bp). Compared to the chloroplast genome, the mitochondrial gene distribution in A. sesquipedale is relatively sparse (Fig. 6). According to the investigation of similarity between organelle genomes, it was found that 110 fragments of chloroplast genes were identified as transferred to the mitochondrial genome. These fragments range are from 32 to 6122 bp, with a total 99,921 bp length and an average of 908 bp. The transferred sequences account for 11.03% of the complete mitochondrial genome length and 65.82% of its chloroplast genome (Table S10). These transferred fragments include 29 coding genes (psbM, petN, psbZ, psbN, psbH, petB, petD, atpI, psbT, ndhD, psaC, ndhE, petL, petG, psbL, psbF, psbE, psaJ, rpl20, rpl22, rpl23, rpl33, ycf3, rps14, rps4, rps15, rps2, rps12, and rps18), one rRNA gene (rrn5), and 20 tRNA genes (trnL-CAA, trnD-GUC, trnY-GUA, trnE-UUC, trnC-GCA, trnT-GGU, trnT-UGU, trnG-UCC, trnfM-CAU, trnR-ACG, trnN-GUU, trnR-ACG, trnP-UGG, trnW-CCA, trnF-GAA, trnQ-UUG, trnN-GUU, trnI-CAU, trnH-GUG, and trnM-CAU) (Table S10).

Schematic diagram of horizontal gene transfer between the A. sesquipedale chloroplast and mitochondrial genome

Among these transferred fragments, the longest sequence segment was 6122 bp, while the shortest was only 32 bp. In terms of length distribution, 37 fragments were longer than 1000 bp, 55 fragments ranging between 100 and 1000 bp, and 18 fragments shorter than 100 bp. For the 53 homologous fragments, the minimum E-value was zero, with Bit-score values ranging from 654 to 10,486, all fragments exceeding 460 bp. Moreover, Chromosomes 1, 4, 13, and 7 exhibited the highest number of transferred fragments with 17, 15, 14 and 13, whereas Chromosomes 8, 10, and 14 had the fewest, with only one transferred fragment each.

Systematic evolution

The phylogenetic tree built based on 35 conserved mitochondrial genes classified 41 representative plant species into 10 categories, as shown in Fig. 7. Utilizing two different software programs, RAxML and MrBayes, with the cruciferous plant Arabidopsis thaliana (Y08501.2) as the out-group, both methods yielded identical clustering results. The mitochondrial genome arrangement in the separation results corresponded to the families Orchidaceae, Asphodelaceae, Amarylidaceae, Asparagaceae, Poaceae, Juncaceae, Arecaceae, Hydrocharitaceae, Butomaceae, and Zosteraceae, with almost all nodes supported by bootstrap values exceeding 94%. And Orchidaceae and Asphodelaceae were positioned at closely related branching nodes, with A. sesquipedale and Hygrochilus parishii sharing the same branch, indicating their closest phylogenetic relationship. These species were more closely related to H. parishii, Cymbidium ensifolium, Bletilla striata, and Paphiopedilum micranthum, while being more distantly related to the genus Dendrobium and Gastrodia species. The analysis results grouped all species, including A. sesquipedale, into four clades-Alismatales, Arecales, Poales, and Asparagales-consistent with the APG IV system [26], demonstrating agreement between traditional and molecular taxonomy for this group.

Substitution rate in PCGs

To investigate the impact of selection pressure on the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome evolution, nine representative plants of the Orchidaceae family were slected, belonging to different genera (Dendrobium, Apostasia, Gastrodia, Nervilia, Hygrochilus, Epipogium, Bletilla, Cymbidium, and Paphiopedilum) that are closely related to A. sesquipedale in the same branch. The basis for selecting these species is to choose one representative from each genus of the sequenced species in the Orchidaceae family, ensuring that their sequencing assembly and annotation data are complete. The proportion of non-synonymous substitutions (Ka) to synonymous substitutions (Ks) was also evaluated among 37 shared PCGs. The change pattern of Ka/Ks values for these 37 shared PCGs is similar across ten species. All genes undergo purification selection, and the range of Ka/Ks ratio is from 0.128 for cox1 to 0.967 for nad1. Ka/Ks ratio values are all below 1, indicating that harmful mutations have been eliminated and proteins remain unchanged, reflecting the conservation and stability of PCGs in the orchids evolutionary process (Fig. 8 and Table S11).

Orchidaceae comparative mitochondrial genomics

The 25 orchid mitochondrial genome sizes ranged from 416 to 2131 kb, with Epipogium aphyllum having the smallest mitochondrial genome and Gastrodia javanica the largest (Fig. 9 and Table S12). The GC content of the mitochondrial genomes mostly fell between 42.76% and 45.28%, with H. parishii and Epipogium aphyllum exhibiting the highest GC content at 45.28% and 45.2%, respectively. A. sesquipedale had the lowest GC content at 42.76%. Dendrobium henanense, Dendrobium wilsonii, and Gastrodia peichatieniana exhibited a linear-circular hybrid mitochondrial genome structure, while Dendrobium brymerianum had a linear mitochondrial genome structure. All other reported species possessed circular mitochondrial genome structures. Notably, Dendrobium hancockii and A. shenzhenica contained one circular structure, whereas Gastrodia flexistyla had 71 circular structures (Table S13). These findings indicate that the GC content in orchid mitochondrial genomes remains stable with minimal fluctuations, but the genome sizes vary significantly. The structural composition of mitochondrial genomes in orchids demonstrates complexity and diversity.

Among the PCGs in the mitochondrial genomes of different species, 18 genes such as nad3, cob, and atp4 are the most conserved among the mitochondrial genome of 25 orchid species. However, the variability of five genes including rpl6, rpl10, rps8, sdh3, and sdh4 is not present in all species. These genes have a certain degree of variability, containing atp9, ccmB, ccmC, cox2, matR, mttB, nad1 (2, 4, 5), rps1-rps4, rps7 (10, 11, 13, 19), rpl2 and rpl16. In the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, the sdh3, rps8, rpl6, and rpl10 genes were lost, sdh4 and rps11 were pseudo-genization, and rps19 had two copoies.

Genomic comparison of ten typical Orchidaceae mitochondrial sequences

As shown in the point graph analysis (Fig. 11 and Table S14), the homology analysis of mitochondrial sequences indicates that the homogeneity between A. sesquipedale and H. parishii is the longest and most similar, demonstrating a close genetic relationship. Paired homology analysis shows that many homologous collinear blocks co-exist among individual mitochondrial genomes, although their arrangement order is different. This indicates that the ten orchids mitochondrial genomes are highly non-conserved in structure and exhibits extensive rearrangement events. The most homologous sequences have been found between the A. sesquipedale and H. parishii mitochondrial genomes. In addition, the number of homologous sequences between A. sesquipedale and H. parishii mitochondrial genomes is much higher than that between other pairs (Fig. 12). Utilizing the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome as a reference, collinear alignment was performed with nine other orchid species (Table S15). It’s shown that the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome had the highest similarity with the H. parishii mitochondrial genome, and the lowest similarity with the Paphiopedilum micranthum mitochondrial genome. This further indicates that A. sesquipedale is closely related to H. parishii, which has certain reference value for the evolutionary relationship between orchids and the construction of phylogenetic trees.

Discussion

Hybrid assembly of mitochondrial genome derived from public-published genomic data

The complex structure of mitochondrial genomes varies in different plants, which also poses certain difficulties for their sequencing and assembly [27]. Due to the shorter read lengths obtained by the first and second generation sequencing technologies, it is difficult to meet the assembly requirements for the complete structure of plant mitochondrial genomes. On the other hand, the development of third-generation sequencing technology can provide longer read length fragments, making it easier for de novo assembly of mitochondrial genomes [28]. Currently, 34,923 complete plant chloroplast genomes were assembled in CNCB (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/ cgir/) [29], and only 5272 published mitochondrial genome sequences were published on plant research (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore). It can be seen that the complete mitochondrial assembly of most new species is difficult to adopt the reference genome assembly strategy, and more choices are made for de novo assembly strategy [30]. To obtain more scientific assembly results, the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome study adopted the strategy of de novo assembly of genome sequencing data on one hand, and used the Orchidaceae mitochondrial genome as a reference sequence on the other hand, verifying that the combination of these two methods can successfully assemble the mitochondrial genome of published species genome sequencing data.

A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome characteristics

Comparison with animal mitochondrial genomes, plant mitochondrial genomes show that significant differences in sequence size, gene composition, functional gene arrangement order, and so on [31]. At present, it is known that the smaller mitochondrial genome in plants belongs to Rhopalocnemis phalloides, whose mitochondrial genome consists of only 21 small circular chromosomes with a total 130,713 bp length [32], while the Silene conoidea mitochondrial genome size is 11.3 Mb [33]. In genome size of Orchidaceae, the mitochondrial genome of Epilogium aphyllum is 416,775 bp, and that of Gastrodia Javanica is 2,131,312 bp (Table S13). The size of the A. sesquipedale's mitochondrial genome is 711,372 bp, which is moderate in size. This genome is distributed in 14 circular structures. Among the genome structure of Orchidaceae plants, Dendrobium Hancockii and A. shenzhenica are assembled into a single circular structure, while other reports have shown that the mitochondrial genome of this family is assembled into a combination of multiple-rings or the combination of circular and linear structures (Table S13), demonstrating the complexity of mitochondrial structure in this family.

The majority of sequences in the A. sesquipedale's mitochondrial genome are non-coding, with PCGs accounting for 4.63%. In Gastrodia elata, A. shenzhenica, C. ensifolium, Epipogium aphyllum, and P. micranthum, the proportion of PCGs is 2.45% [19], 4.21% [20], 6.03% [22], 8.03% [19], and 8.95% [23], respectively. The above data indicates that the proportion of PCGs in this family of plants fluctuates greatly, and a large number of sequences are composed of non-coding elements. Compared with other angiosperm mitochondrial genomes, the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome is conserved in gene quantity, encoding 38 out of 41 PCGs present in the common ancestor of angiosperms [34], except for sdh3, rpl6, and rpl10, which were lost from the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome (Table S3). Sdh3 and sdh4 encode succinate dehydrogenase, which are repeatedly lost or pseudogenetically altered [35]. But sdh4 remains in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome and undergoes pseudo-genizaton. In the Dendrobium mitochondrial genome, the phenomenon of ribosomal protein gene loss was observed [21], and eight ribosomal protein genes were lost in the C. ensifolium mitochondrial genome [22]. In the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, three ribosomal protein genes are lost, one gene is pseudogenic, and one gene has a double copy.

Abundant chloroplast-origin sequences transfer, RNA site editing, and RSCU value

DNA transfer includes the transfer between organelles, between organelles and the nucleus, and between the genome itself and different species. The chloroplast origin sequence occupies for 0.1% to 10.3% of the mitochondrial genome [36]. In addition, the sequences obtained from chloroplasts occupy less than 5% of the mitochondrial genomes in most angiosperms [37]. In contrast, all of 14 chromosomes in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome contain chloroplast derived fragments, covering 11.03% (99,921 bp) of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome (Table S10). The chloroplast derived sequences in Chromosome 12 (Chr12) even reached 17.76 kb (50.27% of Chr12), followed by 43.05% in Chromosome 13 and 24.21% in Chromosome 4. The chromosome length derived from the entire chloroplast account for between 0.38% and 50.27% of the entire sub-mitochondrial genome. The chloroplast fragments transferred on Chromosomes 8, 10, 14, and 9 are 1, 1, 1, and 3, with very small proportions (Table S1 and S10). Compared to most other reported mitochondrial genome fragments derived from chloroplasts, the chloroplast derived sequences in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome are more common and unequally widespread in both relative and absolute values. In total, 110 fragments were transferred, with fragment sizes ranging from 32 to 6122 bp. There are 18 fragments with less than 100 bp transferred, accounting for 16.36%, and 37 fragments with more than 1000 bp transferred, accounting for 33.64% (Fig. 6 and Table S10). These results suggest that there is abundant large fragment transfer in the mitochondrial genome of Darwin's orchid, which may be related to its complex multi-ring structure. Interestingly, these chloroplast transfer fragments were randomly distributed in every region of mitochondrial genome, not just repeat regions. The ancestors of orchids obtained a fungal mitochondrial genome region of approximately 270 bp through horizontal gene transfer, which contains three tRNA genes [23], but this exogenous sequence was not identified in Darwin's orchid.

RNA editing is a post-transcriptional modification that occurs in the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of higher plants, facilitating protein folding [38]. In terms of the RNA editing type and frequency, each organelle has high lineage specificity [39]. And a total of 709 RNA editing sites were estimated in the 38 PCGs of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genomes. However, the predicted number of RNA editing sites in different Orchidaceae species is very conservative, ranging from 416 sites in A. shenzhenica to 530 sites in C. ensifolium. And further research is needed to determine whether the abundance of RNA editing sites in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome is related to its complex multi-ring structure.

The codon usage bias analysis is crucial for studying the origin and genetic differentiation of species [40]. In the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, all PCGs except nad1 and rps10 use the typical ATG start codon. And the latter two utilize both ATG and ACG start codons simultaneously. This is different from the initial codon usage observed in A. shenzenica, where mttB use TTG, and nad1 uses ATT, which may be the result of RNA editing [41]. It was found that almost all NNA and NNT codons have RSCU ratios higher than 1.0, except for the values of Ala (GCA, 0.9392), Ile (AUA, 0.7773), Leu (CUA, 0.8321) and Ser (AGU, 0.9834), which are similar to the observations of C. ensifolium [22]. The codon usage in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome is usually biased towards the A or T(U) position of the third codon, showing a common pattern in terrestrial plant mitochondrial genes [42].

Collinearity and repeat sequence of A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome

Even though the gene composition of plant mitochondrial genomes is relatively conserved, there are still obvious differences in the position and arrangement order of these genes [43]. The plant mitochondrial genome length is relatively large, with fewer coding genes, and correspondingly, the proportion of intergenic regions is also higher. And this non-coding region sequence is mostly composed of exogenous migration fragments and repetitive sequence self-recombination duplication, so the evolutionary conservation of this region is also poor [44]. For example, the gene spacer region in the P. micranthum mitochondrial genome commonly contains repetitive sequences, which are related to the variation and recombination of the genome itself [23].

Repetitive sequences are the source of continuous rearrangements in the mitochondrial genome [45]. Direct repeat mediated recombination has been discriminated in A. chinensis [46] and Glycyrrhiza glabra [47]. Li et al. [23] reported a mitochondrial genome recombination mediated by direct repeat sequences (> 300 bp) in P. micranthum. The 447,368 bp main loop was fragmented into 26 chromosomes. According to traditional multi-party models, large repeats in the main loop induce genomic recombination, resulting in a set of subgenomic loops [48]. Similarly, there are abundant large fragment repeats in the A. sesquipedale repeat sequence, with dispersed interval repeat sequences accounting for as much as 76.15%, resulting in the formation of 14 circular chromosome structures.

Comparative study of mitochondrial genomes in Orchidaceae plants

To better understand the structure and organization of plant mitochondrial genomes, the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome was compared with the mitochondrial genome reported by Orchidaceae. The selected plants exhibit highly inconsistent structures and genome sizes, indicating that many gene losses, gene acquisition, and rearrangement events have occurred among mitochondrial genomes [49]. The Ka/Ks ratio can be utilized to determine whether a specific protein coding gene is under selection pressure [50]. The Ka/Ks ratio of all common genes in the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome is less than one, indicating the existence of purification selection, which is similar to the ratio found in the C. ensifolium mitochondrial genes [22]. However, there are reports that the Ka/Ks ratio of some mitochondrial genes is greater than one. For example, it was found that the Ka/Ks values (ccmB and nad4) were greater than one between those of Bupleurum chinense and other mitochondrial genomes closely related to it [51]. It’s indicated that these genes have undergone positive selection, which is crucial for further studying genome selection and evolution within their respective populations.

Genome size, structure, gene content, and GC content are important factors in evaluating the genome differences [43]. These features of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome were compared with those of 25 other orchid species (Table S9). Although there are significant differences in genome size, structure, and gene content, the GC content of the selected plants is relatively consistent. This indicates that GC content is highly conserved in Orchidaceae plants [44]. But the non-coding genes of higher plant mitochondrial genomes have rich genetic variation, and become valueable molecular markers for studying the species origin, evolution and population genomics [52].

Although plant mitochondria exhibit significant diversity in genome size, most mitochondrial PCGs are highly conserved, consisting mainly of 24 core conserved genes and 17 variant genes, and can be classified into complex I (nad), complex II (sdh), complex III (cob), complex IV (cox), complex V (ATP), cytochrome c biosynthesis genes (ccm), and transport RNA (tRNA) [35]. Comparative analysis shows that among the 11 PCG groups in Orchidaceae, only the cytochrome c biosynthesis gene is conserved, while the other genes exhibit varying degrees of differences. The preservation rate of PCGs in orchids is much lower than that in cereal crops [53]. However, compared to other orchids, A. shenzhenica have lost three NADH dehydrogenase genes (nad1, nad2, and nad4) [20]. It is worth noting that among the 25 selected orchids, the A. shenzhenica has a single circular mitochondrial genome structure; Others have multiple circular structures. As a Orchidaceae plant with multi-circular structures, the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome lost sdh3, rps8, rpl6, and rpl10, while sdh4 and rps13 were pseudogenetically modified, and rps19 has a double copy. The fundamental reasons for these differences may be due to different evolutionary strategies, which deserve further investigation.

As is well known, phylogenetic analysis is usually based on nuclear gene and genome data or chloroplast gene and genome information [54]. With the advancement of mitochondrial genome sequencing, significant differences in mitochondrial genome were observed, containing different size and structural abundances. The mitochondrial genome has become an important tool for taxonomy, systematics, evolution, and population genetics research [55]. And a phylogenetic analysis was conducted on the mitochondrial genomes of A. sesquipedale and 46 published plant mitochondrial genomes. Both construction methods produced the same clustering results, showing the consistency between traditional classification and molecular classification. Previous studies on the mitochondrial genomes of C. ensifolium and 24 selected plant species have also indicated the accuracy of constructing evolutionary trees based on mitochondrial genomes, highlighting the potential of utilizing mitochondrial genome information in plant phylogenetic research [22].

As an epiphytic plant, the magical Darwin's Orchid is famous for its story of coevolution,. Addressing the core scientific question: the extreme reproductive strategy of A. sesquipedale (long flower spacing, high energy demand for floral development) leads to an energy crisis, which is mitigated by the mitochondrial genome through multiple mechanisms: 1) conserved respiratory chain genes ensure basic energy supply; 2) RNA editing enhances the stability and functionality of energy metabolism-related proteins; 3) abundant chloroplast-derived fragments supplement energy metabolism-related genes; 4) the multi-circular structure may improve the efficiency of gene expression and recombination, facilitating rapid adaptation to energy demand. However, the specific regulatory mechanisms of these processes require further investigation at the transcriptomic and proteomic levels.

Conclusions

In this research, the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome was accurately assembled using a combination of Illumina short-read and PacBio long-read data (downloaded from CNCB-NGDC under BioProject PRJCA030186). Its mitochondrial genome exhibits a typical multi-chromosome structure (14 circular chromosomes) with a moderate size (711,372 bp), and retains a large number of horizontally transferred fragments derived from chloroplasts (11.03% of the genome). Key findings include: 1) abundant repeat sequences (216 SSRs, 103 tandem repeats, 1019 dispersed repeats) mediate genome rearrangement and multi-circular structure formation; 2) 709 RNA editing events (primarily C-to-T transitions) regulate protein hydrophilicity and may enhance energy metabolism-related protein stability; 3) A. sesquipedale is closely related to H. parishii, consistent with traditional taxonomic status; 4) the species has lost sdh3, rpl6, and rpl10 genes, with sdh4 and rps11 pseudogenized; 5) chloroplast-derived fragments and RNA editing may help mitigate the energy deficit caused by the extreme reproductive strategy. These findings fill the gap in mitochondrial genome research of A. sesquipedale and provide new insights into the diversity and evolution of Orchidaceae mitochondrial genomes. The results lay a foundation for studies on the adaptive evolution of Orchidaceae plants, especially the relationship between mitochondrial genome characteristics and extreme reproductive strategies. Further research should focus on functional verification of key genes and regulatory mechanisms of energy metabolism to deepen our understanding of orchid adaptive evolution.

Methods

Raw data acquisition

The raw data used to assemble the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome comes from the National Genomics Data Center (CNCB-NGDC, https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/). The A. sesquipedale whole genome data has been saved in BioProject under accession number PRJCA030186 [8].

Mitochondrial genome assembly and annotation

The original long read sequencing data was compared with the reference gene sequence data of plant mitochondrial core genes using Minimap2 comparison software (https://docs.nvidia.com/clara/parabricks/4.5.0/documentation/tooldocs/man_minimap2. html#). Similar fragment sequences with a length exceeding 50 bp were selected as candidate sequences. Selected orchid species candidate sequences with more aligned genes and higher alignment quality are chosen as seed sequences. Then, the original long read sequencing data is compared with the seed sequence, and the sequence with a minimum overlap of 1 kb and a similarity of at least 70% is added to the seed sequence. The process is iteratively repeated to obtain all long read sequencing data of the mitochondrial genome. Canu v2.3 assembly software (https://github.com/marbl/canu/releases) is made to calibrate long read sequencing data [56], while Bowtie v2.3.5.1 (https://www.encodeproject.org/software/ bowtie2/) is utilized to align short read sequencing data with the calibrated sequence. Unicycler v0.4.8 (https://github.com/rrwick/ Unicycler) was made to connect short read sequencing data and correct long read sequencing data to obtain the final multi-circular A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome.

The annotation of mitochondrial genome structure is carried out in several steps. Firstly, PCG and rRNA were aligned and manually adjusted with published plant mitochondrial genomes (Cymbidium ensifolium, OR754263.1; Dendrobium hancockii, LC771085.1; Gastrodia angusta, PP239747.1; Nervilia mackinnonii, PP239994.1) using BLAST (evalue: 1E-5, -word_size 21). TRNA annotation was conducted using tRNAscan-SE (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/), and an open reading box finder (http://www.ncbi.nlm.NIH.gov/gorf/gorf.html) was utilized to annotate the open reading frame. Sequence lengths less than 102 bp and sequences that overlap with known genes are excluded. The comparison results with a length greater than 300 bp were annotated with the Non-Redundant Protein Sequence Database library. In order to obtain more accurate annotations, the above results were manually corrected. Then OGDRAW v1.3.1 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) was utilized for drawing the genome map.

Discrimination of chloroplast-derived fragments and repeat sequences

The chloroplast gene search was conducted on the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome using BLASTn v.2.14.1 + [57] to identify chloroplast derived fragments. The results were displayed using TBtools-II v2.136 [58] with a 7-word size, an E-value cutoff of 1E-5, and a length > 100 bp. Scattered repeated sequence analysis was using the Reputer tool (https://bibiserv.cebitec. unibielefeld.de/reputer) [59]. The minimum repeat size was set to 30 bp, E value to less than 1 E-10, selecting all types of repeat sequence types, and storing the output results as a table for analysis. SSR analysis was conduced using MISA Online Tool (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben. de/misa/index.php). The single nucleotide repeat count was set to be greater than 10, the dinucleotide repeat count to be greater than 5, the trinucleotide repeat count to be greater than 4, and the tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide repeat counts to be greater than 3. And the minimum distance between two SSRs is set to 100 bp, and the output file format is selected as "Misa". Tandem Repeat Finder online tool is utilized for analyzing concatenated repeated sequences (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html) [60], using default parameters. The results were visualized utilizing Circos v0.69–6 (https://circos.ca/software/download/) [61].

Ka/Ks value and RNA eding-site predication

Nine selected orchid plants, including Dendrobium hancockii (LC771085.1), A. shenzhenica (NC077647.1), Gastrodia elata (PP239863.1-PP239927.1), Nervilia mackinnonii (PP239994.1- PP240051.1), Hygrochilus parishii (MH591775.1-MH591793.1), Epipogium aphyllum (PQ285825.1-PQ285847.1), Bletilla striata (PQ859845.1-PQ859864.1), C. ensifolium (OR754263.1-OR754281.1) and P. micranthum (OP465200.1-OP465225.1) were made to analyze Ka/Ks values. The shared PCGs were aligned using MAFFT v7 [62] and the value of Ka to Ks substitution was count using KaKs_Calculator v2.0 [63]. The PREPACT3 software (PREPACT3-Request 30,522) was made to predict potential RNA editing events of the A. sesquipedale PCGs, in order to explore modification mechnism [64].

Nucleotide diversity and RSCU

Based on 40 common genes (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFc, ccmFn, cob, cox1-cox3, matR, mttB, nad1-nad4, nad4L, nad5-nad7, nad9, rpl16, rpl2, rpl5, rps1, rps10, rps12-rps14, rps19, rps2-rps4, rps7, rrn18, rrn26 and rrn5) in the ten above-mentioned mitochondrial genomes, sequence alignment was conducted using MAFFT v7.526 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/ software/). The aligned sequence was imported into DnaSP v6.12.03 to count Pi value [65].

The base percentage of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome was calculated and the PhyloSuite software (https://pypi.org/project/PhyloSuite/) was used to parse the GenBank format file of the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome, extracting PCGs [66]. Mega 12.1 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) was utilized to analyze the codon usage of mitochondrial PCGs and calculate the RSCU value. The RSCU measures the ratio of observed frequency to predicted frequency of a specific codon encoding the same amino acid. A RSCU value greater than one demonstrates that the codon is preferred for use, and the larger the value, the higher the preference.

Comparative mitochondrial genomics and homologous fragment

Nucmer 4.0.0 beta2 [67] with maxmatch parameters was utilized to perform genome alignment among the A. sesquipedale mitochondrial genome and nine selected mitochondrial genomes from Orchidaceae, in order to generate point maps. BLASTN v2.10.1 + software was utilized to draw collinear plots of A. sesquipedale and selected plants, with word length set to 7 and E value set to 1E-5, and screen and compare fragments over 300 bp in length [68].

Phylogeny

To investigate the evolutionary relationship of A. sesquipedale in a broader context, a series of mitochondrial genomes were downloaded from different plants, including Orchidaceae, Asphodelaceae, Amarylidaceae, Asparagaceae, Poaceae, Juncaceae, Arecaceae, Hydrocharitaceae, Butomaceae, and Zosteraceae, from the NCBI database, while using A. thaliana as an out-group (Table S16). A total of 47 PCGs shared by all species were extracted using PhyloSuite (https://dongzhang0725.github.io/); multiple sequence alignment was conducted using MAFFT v7.526 and trimed using trimAI tool. The jModelTest v2.1.10 (https://github.com/ddarriba/jmodeltest2) was utilized to infer the optimal phylogenetic model for trimmed sequences and identified GTR + G + I as the best-fit model. Subsequently, the maximum likelihood (ML) tree was built using RAxML v8.2.12 (https://evomics.org/learning/phylogenetics/raxml/), with 1000 times repeats (GTRGAMMAI model). MrBayes v3.2.7a (https://github.com/NBISweden/MrBayes) was utilized to build a Bayesian inference tree, all parameter settings referring to the analysis of ML phylogenetic tree. We defined nst = 6 and rates = invgamma, ran 1,000,000 MCMC generations, and sampled trees every 100 generations. The initial 25% of sampled trees as burn-in was discarded and a final majority-rule consensus tree was constructed.
