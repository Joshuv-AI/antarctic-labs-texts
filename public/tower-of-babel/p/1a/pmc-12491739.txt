Yellow fever in children and adolescents amid the South American outbreak, 2024/2025
Authors: Alfonso J Rodriguez-Morales, Daniela Torres-Hernández, Maria Eugenia Guevara, Alicia Chang-Cojulun, Jose Brea-Del Castillo, Romina Rios-Blanco, Manuela Isbeth Mérida-Barrios, Melissa Palmieri, Maria L Avila-Agüero
Published: 2025

Yellow fever (YF) has long been recognized as a significant vector-borne disease in South America (transmitted by Haemagogus and Sabethes mosquitoes, in rural settings, and by Aedes in urban areas) [[1], [2], [3]], with cyclical outbreaks that have shaped public health policies for over a century [4]. However, the ongoing resurgence of YF in multiple South American countries during 2024–2025 represents the most significant epidemic wave in Colombia in more than two decades [5] and continues to affect other nations, notably Brazil and Peru [1,6]. Despite this, a crucial and vulnerable population—children and adolescents—remains underrepresented in both research and public discourse surrounding these outbreaks [7,8].

According to recent data from the Ministries of Health of Colombia, Brazil, and Peru (Table 1), a substantial proportion of YF cases and deaths are also occurring in individuals under 20 years of age. In Colombia, 13.4 % of cases and 4.0 % of deaths were reported among those under 20 years. Brazil shows a comparable impact, with 11.8 % of cases and 4.3 % of deaths in this age group, while Peru reports even higher proportions—26.1 % of cases and 26.9 % of deaths in children and adolescents (Table 1). These numbers are alarming, yet the scientific literature has paid little attention to them.

YF vaccination has been the most effective method of prevention since its introduction [9]. In Latin American endemic countries, YF vaccine has been offered for years as part of the immunization programmes in risk areas, however, as has been recently reported by the Pan American Health Organization (https://www.paho.org/sites/default/files/2025-05/2025-may-23-phe-risk-assessment-yellow-fever-enfinal.pdf), vaccination coverage rates are not optimal in most countries, leading to a significant number of unvaccinated children, adolescents, and adults at risk of infection and disease [8]. As observed in Colombia over the last two decades and in Brazil, YF vaccination coverage in children and adolescents was not optimal (Fig. 1). Notably, in Colombia, national coverage among adolescents was just 34 %, with 28 departments or districts (Fig. 1), and only one department with coverage of ≥95 %. Similarly, in Brazil, in 2023, vaccination coverage for children under one year of age with the first dose of yellow fever vaccination fell below 80 % in 25 states and the Federal District. Only Paraná reached 81 % of the target group, with 16 states reporting rates below 70 %. These results are reflected in municipalities, particularly in most of those in the North and part of the Northeast regions (Fig. 1) (https://iqc.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/anuario-vacina-interativo-2025.pdf).

Several factors may contribute to why children and adolescents are disproportionately affected during this epidemic. First, as explained, vaccination coverage in these age groups remains suboptimal (Fig. 1). While YF vaccination is part of routine immunization schedules in endemic areas, and for children traveling there [[10], [11], [12]], coverage gaps persist, particularly in regions where vaccination campaigns have been inconsistently implemented or interrupted due to social unrest, logistical challenges, or public health emergencies such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, some of the affected areas had been considered low-risk for YF transmission for decades, leading to a false sense of security and a reduction in vaccination vigilance [13,14].

Secondly, exposure patterns differ by age. Children and adolescents in rural areas may accompany parents during agricultural or forest-related activities where the sylvatic mosquito vectors (such as Haemagogus spp. and Sabethes spp.) are prevalent [15]. Additionally, schools, playgrounds, and community centers in forest-fringe regions often lack adequate vector control measures. These social and environmental determinants increase the risk for these younger populations [16].

Beyond ecological drivers, social factors also contribute to explaining why children and adolescents are increasingly affected during this epidemic. Chronic poverty, structural discrimination, and state neglect force many Indigenous families to rely on informal, high-risk work such as illegal mining, where exploitation is common. As it has been previously reported in Peru and Brazil in the last two years by news reports, children are used as ‘human shields’ by illegal miners (https://www.reuters.com/world/americas/indigenous-leader-peru-says-illegal-miners-use-children-human-shields-amazon-2025-02-17/) or recruited by organized criminal groups that have expanded their operations into the Amazon (https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2024/12/16/amazon-pcc-cartels-indigenous-mining/). These conditions push families into remote, forested areas, increasing their contact with infected mosquitoes and thereby raising the risk of YF. Although this is not the primary driver of low vaccine coverage, it is another contributing factor.

The clinical course of YF in children and adolescents also deserves further investigation [7]. Infancy is associated with increased severity and lethality of YF infection, likely due to age-related physiological vulnerabilities [17]. While severe hepatic and hemorrhagic manifestations are well-described in adults, pediatric-specific clinical patterns and prognostic factors are poorly characterized [18].

In addition, diagnostic challenges in pediatric YF should not be underestimated. Children often present with atypical or milder clinical manifestations, which complicates the differential diagnosis with other endemic arboviral infections, particularly dengue [19,20]. This overlap in early symptoms can delay clinical suspicion and, consequently, the timely collection of samples for molecular confirmation by RT-PCR, a test with a narrow diagnostic window. Once this window is missed, clinicians must rely on serologic methods, which are further complicated by the potential for cross-reactivity with antibodies from other flavivirus infections or vaccinations, thereby delaying definitive diagnosis. These delays not only hinder prompt case management but also have implications for surveillance accuracy and outbreak control [21,22].

Some studies from past outbreaks have suggested that children may have atypical or less recognized presentations, potentially delaying diagnosis and care. The relatively high case-fatality rates among pediatric patients in the current epidemic—particularly in Peru—emphasize the urgency for pediatric-specific clinical guidelines and management protocols [7,18].

Another underappreciated aspect is the risk to unvaccinated travelers. South America attracts thousands of tourists and eco-travelers each year, many of whom travel with their children and adolescents on excursions and adventure trips to areas experiencing active YF transmission [6,11,23]. In some cases, travel medicine consultations often fail to sufficiently emphasize YF vaccination, particularly when destinations are not traditionally considered high risk [23]. Cases over the last decade of imported YF in unvaccinated travelers returning to Europe and North America from Brazil underscore this global health concern [6].

In the context of the ongoing epidemics, public health responses should urgently prioritize comprehensive vaccination campaigns targeting children and adolescents, particularly in endemic and epidemic-prone areas [14]. Routine immunization programs should be reinforced, with catch-up campaigns implemented where gaps are identified. Special attention should be paid to indigenous communities, remote rural populations, and migrants, who often face significant barriers to accessing preventive healthcare [24].

The gaps documented above mirror the global picture and precisely what the World Health Organization-led “Eliminate Yellow Fever Epidemics” (EYE) Strategy 2017–2026 (https://www.who.int/initiatives/eye-strategy). EYE— a coalition of WHO, UNICEF and Gavi—sets three mutually-reinforcing objectives: (i) protect at-risk populations through introduction of YF vaccine into every routine immunization programme and risk-based preventive campaigns; (ii) prevent international spread by strengthening IHR (2005) measures and traveller vaccination checks; and (iii) contain outbreaks rapidly via early detection, a 6-million-dose global emergency stockpile and coordinated surge response. The partnership aims to protect approximately 1 billion people in Africa and the Americas by 2026 and has already added more than 226 million people who have been vaccinated by mid-2023 (https://www.who.int/initiatives/eye-strategy). Aligning national plans with EYE pillars would secure paediatric vaccine supply, strengthen adolescent catch-up campaigns, and embed age-disaggregated surveillance within the programme's monitoring framework.

Equally important is strengthening surveillance systems to provide public age-disaggregated data, enabling the timely identification of high-risk groups and geographical hotspots. As our analysis (Table 1) demonstrates, ministries of health report data for all age groups, but specific figures for children and adolescents are sometimes lacking or inconsistently reported. Improved data transparency and collaborative information exchange are crucial for guiding targeted interventions.

Clinical care capacity must also be strengthened. Healthcare providers in endemic regions should receive updated training on the diagnosis and management of YF in children and adolescents, including the new WHO Guidelines for Clinical Management of Arboviral Diseases (https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240111110) [25]. Many cases initially present with nonspecific febrile syndromes, often indistinguishable from dengue, leptospirosis, malaria, or other co-circulating infections in endemic areas [26,27]. Enhanced laboratory capacity, including access to PCR and serologic testing, can aid in prompt diagnosis and case confirmation.

Beyond endemic countries, the implications extend to travel medicine practitioners worldwide. Pre-travel consultations must include a careful assessment of YF vaccination status, and healthcare providers must maintain a high index of suspicion for YF in febrile children and adolescents returning from South America, especially those who visited rural or forested regions of Colombia, Brazil, or Peru [6]. In addition to rural areas, periurban areas, particularly in Colombia and Brazil, are currently considered at risk for YF.

Finally, the current epidemic is a stark reminder of the ongoing vulnerabilities in YF prevention and control. Despite the availability of an effective vaccine for over 80 years, YF continues to cause preventable morbidity and mortality [4]. The neglect of pediatric populations in both research and response strategies reflects a broader pattern of overlooking vulnerable groups in epidemic settings.

We call upon governments, international health agencies, and the scientific community to address this critical gap. Research on the epidemiology, clinical presentation, outcomes, and potential therapeutics for YF in children and adolescents is urgently needed. Public health interventions must ensure that these vulnerable populations are not left behind in vaccination campaigns, surveillance systems, clinical care efforts, and evidence generation. In contrast to adults, for whom clinical management guidelines for severe YF have recently been developed in some countries, such as Colombia [28], there remains a significant gap, with no equivalent guidelines available for children and adolescents.

As South America confronts its largest YF epidemic in decades, the health of its youngest citizens hangs in the balance. Timely, targeted, and inclusive actions are essential to prevent unnecessary suffering and save lives.
