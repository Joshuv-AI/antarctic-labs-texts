A Probable Case of Postpartum Sacroiliac Joint Tuberculosis: The Diagnostic Value of Imaging in a High‐Endemic Setting
Authors: H. Saad Nasir, Huda Raja, Abdullah Nadeem, Shuja Shaukat, Shabih Ayesha, Nahid Khalid Raufi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculous sacroiliitis is a rare form of osteoarticular tuberculosis and is particularly uncommon in the postpartum period. Its presentation is often nonspecific and can mimic more common postpartum musculoskeletal or infectious conditions, leading to delayed diagnosis, especially in high tuberculosis‐endemic regions. We report the case of a 24‐year‐old woman presenting 10 days postpartum with severe, debilitating right‐sided lower back pain and markedly elevated inflammatory markers. Initial evaluation revealed retained products of conception (RPOC), which were managed surgically and with broad‐spectrum antibiotics, resulting in biochemical improvement but no clinical relief. A comprehensive rheumatologic work‐up was negative. Magnetic resonance imaging of the sacroiliac joint demonstrated unilateral joint effusion, bone marrow edema, joint space widening, and adjacent soft tissue inflammation, features highly suggestive of infectious sacroiliitis. In the context of a tuberculosis‐endemic setting, lack of response to conventional antibiotics, negative blood cultures, and characteristic MRI findings, a diagnosis of presumed tuberculous sacroiliitis was made. The patient was started on empirical anti‐tuberculous therapy with a standard four‐drug regimen, followed by continuation therapy. She showed marked clinical improvement with resolution of pain and restoration of mobility. Follow‐up MRI at 7 months demonstrated significant regression of inflammatory changes, confirming an excellent treatment response and no evidence of ongoing infection. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for tuberculous sacroiliitis in postpartum women from tuberculosis‐endemic regions presenting with severe, refractory lower back pain. Early MRI plays a pivotal role in diagnosis when routine investigations are inconclusive. Prompt initiation of empirical anti‐tuberculous therapy, even in the absence of microbiological confirmation, can lead to excellent outcomes and prevent long‐term joint damage.

Background

Tuberculous sacroiliitis is a rare manifestation of extrapulmonary tuberculosis, often presenting with vague symptoms that mimic more common musculoskeletal conditions. In postpartum women, this condition is particularly uncommon and may be overlooked or misdiagnosed due to the physiological and mechanical changes associated with childbirth.

Introduction

Sacroiliitis, or inflammation of the sacroiliac joint, is an uncommon clinical condition with diverse etiologies, including autoimmune diseases, mechanical stress, and infection. Among infectious causes, tuberculous sacroiliitis is particularly rare and often underdiagnosed due to its subtle, nonspecific presentation [1]. While tuberculosis primarily affects the lungs, extrapulmonary involvement accounts for a significant number of cases in endemic regions like Pakistan. Osteoarticular tuberculosis constitutes approximately 3%–5% of all TB cases, while sacroiliac joint involvement is especially rare, representing about 10% of skeletal tuberculosis [2].

Postpartum sacroiliitis is reported even less frequently. Diagnosing a tubercular origin is challenging [3], as symptoms are often mistaken for common postpartum musculoskeletal discomfort. The physiological changes of pregnancy and delivery can mask early signs, contributing to diagnostic delay. When routine investigations are inconclusive, advanced imaging, particularly MRI, can be instrumental in detecting early joint inflammation and abscess formation [1]. This report highlights the diagnostic complexity of postpartum sacroiliitis and emphasizes the need to consider tuberculosis in endemic regions [4], as early treatment can prevent long‐term joint damage [5].

Case History and Examination

A 24‐year‐old female presented to the clinic 10 days postpartum with debilitating, non‐radiating lower back pain localized to the right sacroiliac region. The pain was severe enough to greatly limit her ambulation, requiring assistance from two people. She reported no urinary, fecal, or neurological symptoms.

Physical examination revealed normal vital signs and a negative straight leg raise test. However, the FABER test, compression test, and point tenderness were positive over the right sacroiliac joint, raising a strong suspicion of sacroiliitis.

Initial laboratory investigations showed significantly elevated inflammatory markers: C‐reactive protein (CRP) was 170 mg/L and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased. A pelvic ultrasound revealed retained products of conception (RPOCs).

To evaluate for underlying inflammatory or autoimmune causes of sacroiliitis, a targeted rheumatologic work‐up was performed. Laboratory investigations included antinuclear antibodies (ANA), rheumatoid factor (RF), HLA‐B27 typing, and complement levels (C3 and C4), all of which were within normal limits or negative. Acute phase reactants (ESR and CRP) were elevated initially but improved following the treatment of the presumed pelvic source, without corresponding improvement in pain or function. Anti‐neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were not obtained, as the patient demonstrated no clinical features suggestive of systemic vasculitis, such as constitutional symptoms, renal abnormalities, dermatologic lesions, or neuropathy. Clinically, the patient lacked extra‐articular manifestations associated with spondyloarthropathies, and the unilateral, destructive nature of the sacroiliac joint involvement with para‐osseous abscess formation on MRI was atypical for autoimmune inflammatory arthritis. Collectively, these findings made a primary rheumatologic etiology unlikely.

Regarding the imaging findings of the lumbar spine, degenerative changes were observed. The patient exhibited a loss of lumbar lordosis, likely due to muscular spasms. However, no fractures, dislocations, or bony erosions were identified. The bony spinal canal, pedicles, lamina, facet joints, and paravertebral soft tissues appeared normal. Additionally, there were no lytic, sclerotic, or expansile lesions detected.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis was broad. Given the elevated inflammatory markers, primary considerations were postpartum septic sacroiliitis or an infection secondary to RPOC.

Common mechanical postpartum back pain was considered but deemed unlikely due to the severity and debilitating nature of the pain. In the context of an endemic region, brucellosis was a strong consideration as a known cause of osteoarticular infections.

Inflammatory spondyloarthropathy was also part of the differential, though its acute postpartum onset and unilateral presentation made it less likely. Ultimately, despite its rarity, tuberculous sacroiliitis remained a critical possibility due to the high regional prevalence of tuberculosis and the patient's lack of response to conventional antibiotic therapy.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient was initially managed by the gynecology team with dilatation and curettage (D&C) for the RPOCs and a one‐week course of antibiotics (ciprofloxacin and metronidazole). While her CRP levels decreased and leukocyte count normalized, her severe pain persisted.

An MRI of the sacroiliac joint was subsequently performed, revealing features consistent with right‐sided sacroiliitis. These included joint effusion, multiple small adjacent abscesses, and marrow edema in the adjacent sacral and iliac bones (Figures 1 and 2). Blood cultures and serologic testing for Brucella were negative.

Given the lack of response to conventional antibiotics, the characteristic MRI findings, and the high endemicity of tuberculosis, a diagnosis of presumed tuberculous sacroiliitis was made. Empirical Anti‐Tuberculosis Therapy (ATT) was initiated. The patient was started on the standard intensive phase of anti‐tuberculosis therapy with a four‐drug regimen (Isoniazid, Rifampin, Pyrazinamide, and Ethambutol) for 2 months, followed by a continuation phase with two drugs (Isoniazid and Rifampin) for a total planned treatment duration of 12 months. Adjunctive therapy included nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), at analgesic doses for pain management and physiotherapy to improve mobility and strengthen the lumbopelvic musculature. She was monitored monthly for clinical response and drug‐related adverse effects.

The patient showed a remarkable clinical response, with significant pain reduction and improved mobility. A follow‐up contrast‐enhanced MRI performed 7 months later demonstrated marked resolution of the joint effusion, bone marrow edema, and soft tissue inflammation, confirming a positive response to treatment (Figure 3). The patient achieved full functional recovery.

Discussion

Sacroiliac joint infection is rare, and diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific symptoms and the difficulty of examining the joint [6, 7, 8]. Our case was complicated by the postpartum state and the presence of RPOCs, which initially directed management toward a gynecological source of infection.

The diagnosis of tuberculous sacroiliitis remains challenging. In the early stages with minimal destruction, diagnostic aspiration or closed needle biopsy is recommended to confirm the diagnosis and rule out pyogenic organisms [9]. However, in our setting, a biopsy was not feasible due to a lack of expertise. It is noteworthy that joint aspirates can be negative for tuberculosis in a significant proportion of cases (14%), even with concurrent pulmonary disease [9]. As previously reported by Ramlakan and Govender [8], open biopsy can have therapeutic benefits by draining pus. However, the excellent response to ATT alone in our patient underscores the effectiveness of medical management when the diagnosis is based on strong clinical and radiological evidence [8, 9].

While the presentation of tuberculous sacroiliitis is rare, it can be precipitated by disparate risk factors, as illustrated by comparing a case triggered by local trauma in a schoolteacher [10] with this case arising in the postpartum period. This demonstrates that both localized biomechanical stress and systemic physiological shifts can create a susceptible environment for the manifestation of skeletal tuberculosis. The pathophysiology can differ significantly; the postpartum case likely involved hematogenous spread from a reactivated latent focus, fueled by peri‐partum immunological shifts, whereas the schoolteacher's presentation was probably triggered by direct inoculation or local reactivation at a site of previous mechanical trauma. This highlights how a systemic physiological state (postpartum) and a localized biomechanical event (trauma) can serve as distinct risk pathways for the same rare condition. Pregnancy is characterized by a shift toward T‐helper 2 (Th2) immunity to maintain fetal tolerance, which can concurrently suppress the T‐helper 1 (Th1) responses critical for containing Mycobacterium tuberculosis . The postpartum period involves rapid immune reconstitution and a shift back toward Th1 dominance [11], a period of immunological flux that can destabilize a previously contained latent infection and facilitate reactivation. The initial presence of RPOC was a significant confounding factor, as it represents a well‐documented nidus for pelvic infection that can lead to secondary septic arthritis, including sacroiliitis, via hematogenous or direct contiguous spread [12]. This justified the initial management with evacuation and broad‐spectrum antibiotics targeting typical pelvic pathogens. However, the persistence of severe symptoms and characteristic imaging findings despite this treatment argued against a standard pyogenic infection originating from the RPOCs. The resolution of systemic inflammatory markers concurrent with the ongoing local joint pathology further steered the diagnosis away from a simple septic process and toward a more indolent, granulomatous infection like tuberculosis. Varkas et al. [13] consolidate evidence that mechanical stress (which would include the trauma of childbirth) can cause bone marrow edema in the sacroiliac joints. Crucially, it notes that these stress‐related changes are often bilateral and symmetrical and are not typically associated with the structural damage (erosions, sclerosis) or soft tissue involvement that are hallmarks of infectious or inflammatory pathology [13].

The diagnostic challenges illustrated in our case are well encapsulated in the contemporary clinico‐radiological review of sacroiliac joint tuberculosis by Jain et al. [14]. Their analysis reaffirms that tuberculous sacroiliitis typically presents with nonspecific, insidious symptoms, where MRI is the cornerstone for identifying characteristic features such as joint effusion, erosions, and crucially, para‐articular and iliopsoas abscesses. Our case extends the context by highlighting the postpartum period as a specific high‐risk state due to immunological flux, underscoring that even without pulmonary symptoms or pathological proof, tuberculosis must be a leading consideration in endemic regions to prevent long‐term morbidity. Another report highlights how tuberculous sacroiliitis can masquerade as common mechanical low back pain, leading to significant diagnostic delays [15]. Our case aligns with this spectrum, positioned as a ‘probable’ diagnosis based on a combination of high endemicity, characteristic MRI findings, and a definitive therapeutic response, in the absence of bacteriological proof. The decision to initiate empirical ATT, though made without pathological confirmation, was ethically justified in this context by the high pretest probability in an endemic region, the severe and refractory nature of the illness, the exclusion of common alternative diagnoses, and the significant risk of permanent joint damage with further delay.

Limitation

We acknowledge several important limitations in this report. Primarily, the diagnosis remains probable rather than definitive due to the lack of microbiological or histopathological confirmation from sacroiliac joint or abscess samples. Consequently, we cannot fully exclude alternative, rare causes of sacroiliitis. Furthermore, the absence of tissue biopsy precludes detailed histological analysis and drug susceptibility testing, which would have strengthened diagnostic certainty and guided optimal therapy. Despite these constraints, we justify the empirical treatment decision based on the following factors specific to our high‐endemic setting: (a) the classic clinical and radiological presentation; (b) the exclusion of other common causes like Brucella and pyogenic infections; (c) the failure of initial broad‐spectrum antibiotics; and (d) the definitive and sustained clinical‐radiological response to anti‐tuberculous therapy, which is considered a validating criterion in such diagnostic scenarios.
