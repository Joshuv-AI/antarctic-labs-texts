Conceptual replication and validation of the PACER survey in the multi-ethnic study of atherosclerosis (MESA)
Authors: Yvonne L Michael, Kari Moore, Reyhaneh A Yazdi, Jana A Hirsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Perceptions About Changes in Environments and Residents (PACER) survey was developed to measure individual experiences of health-related neighborhood gentrification. Initial validation in Philadelphia and Montreal supported its reliability and validity, but generalizability remained limited by geographic and demographic constraints. We conducted a multi-site replication study within the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), a diverse cohort of older adults across six U.S. cities.MethodsParticipants completed the 21-item PACER instrument during MESA Exam 7 (2022–2024). We used confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess internal consistency, factor stability across subgroups, and criterion-related validity. We assessed differences by sociodemographic characteristics.ResultsCFA largely supported the hypothesized four subscales: Feelings, Built Environment, Social Environment, and Affordability. The factor structure was stable by age and hours in neighborhood, but varied modestly by site. Subscale correlations and expected associations with perceived amount and pace of change supported criterion validity. Lower socioeconomic status and Black or Hispanic respondents reported more affordability concerns and perceived more neighborhood change.ConclusionsThis study affirms PACER's validity in a large, geographically and demographically diverse U.S. cohort. PACER provides a resident-centered tool for measuring the lived experience of neighborhood change and its health implications across different residential contexts.Keywords: Neighborhood change, Perceived change, Aging, Urban health, Measurement, Equity, Psychometric

Introduction

Gentrification and neighborhood transformation are uneven processes shaped by structural inequities, influencing residents' lived experiences and health (Bostic and Martin, 2003; Ding et al., 2016; Schulz and Northridge, 2004; Zuk et al., 2018). Rising housing costs and the threat of displacement can additionally undermine housing security and disrupt long-standing community connections, both of which are associated with chronic stress and adverse health outcomes. Additionally, changes in local businesses and shifts in access to services may create new health-related opportunities (Schnake-Mahl et al., 2020; Tulier et al., 2019).

Individuals may have different experiences of neighborhood change, related to their engagement with their surroundings, salient roles and identities, and personal histories. Age-related loss of physical and cognitive function may influence how older adults experience their neighborhood and perceive change (Carr, 2023). Individuals with marginalized identities may also interpret neighborhood change differently due to lifetime experiences of discrimination and everyday microaggressions. Prior research supports differences between groups identified by race/ethnicity in perceived neighborhood characteristics (Cagney et al., 2005). Time spent in a neighborhood and life course experiences further shape connection to place and perceptions of change, beyond objective measures, these lived experiences may be more influential in shaping behaviors and health outcomes (Hyun et al., 2024).

Perceptions About Changes in Environments and Residents (PACER) survey was developed to measure perceptions of gentrification-related neighborhood change experienced by individuals relevant to health and was initially validated in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, United States with strong construct validity and reliability (Hirsch et al., 2021). PACER also showed strong concurrent validity in Montreal, Canada, where residents in census-tract–defined gentrifying neighborhoods reported more change, faster change, and greater perceived gentrification than those in neighborhoods with less exposure (Youngbloom et al., 2023). Although PACER was developed with input from experts across more than 25 metropolitan areas, validation has been limited to single-city studies, which may not generalize to other contexts. These relatively dense East Coast cities have distinct historical and development trajectories, and the modest sample sizes (n = 209 and n = 505) limited evaluation across diverse individual characteristics.

To address these limitations, we conducted a replication study of PACER within the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA). This conceptual replication of prior validation work (Hirsch et al., 2021; Youngbloom et al., 2023) examines whether the structure and validity hold in a larger, older cohort across diverse U.S. contexts. Our aims were to 1) evaluate PACER's underlying factor structure in MESA, 2) assess concurrent and criterion-related validity, and 3) examine differences in PACER subscales by sociodemographic characteristics.

Material and methods

We applied the previously validated PACER instrument and analytic framework to a distinct cohort differing in age, geography, and sociocultural composition.

Study design and population

The MESA cohort was recruited in 2000–2002 from six Field Centers: Baltimore MD (Johns Hopkins University Field Center); Chicago, IL (Northwestern Field Center); Forsyth County, NC (Wake Forest University); Los Angeles, CA (UCLA Field Center); New York, NY (Columbia Field Center); and St. Paul, MN (University of Minnesota Field Center). MESA participants were 45–84 years of age and free of clinical cardiovascular disease at baseline. In March 6, 2022 through July 10, 2024, measures of neighborhood environment were assessed by administering validated survey instruments to MESA participants, including PACER (Hirsch et al., 2022). Out of 2276 MESA participants who attended the exam, 2261 (99%) completed the survey. Participants with complete data on the PACER survey were included in the analytic sample for this analysis (n = 2232) (See Supplemental Fig. 1).

This secondary analysis of existing data was conducted in accordance with the data use agreement governing MESA data access and approved by Drexel University Human Research Protection Program.

Measures

The MESA neighborhood questionnaire assessed a variety of characteristics hypothesized to be related to cognitive function in older adults. Based on an initial validation study, PACER's items assessed four subscale domains of perceived neighborhood change with high internal consistency: feelings (7 questions; sentiment toward change), built environment (5 questions; visible transformation through new businesses, replacement of long-standing establishments, higher-end grocery stores, new construction, and improved local resources), social environment (4 questions; social changes such as property flipping, arrival of new residents, shifts in neighborhood character, and increased tension or conflict), and affordability (3 questions; including housing affordability, confidence in finding comparable housing locally, and low concern about rising costs or displacement). Additionally, the survey includes questions of overall change in terms of amount and pace (2 questions) (Hirsch et al., 2021). To eliminate differences in answer structures (i.e. agree-disagree versus amount of change), we recoded all PACER questions following methods in previous work (See Supplemental Table 1) (Hirsch et al., 2021).

Variables of interest for describing differences by PACER include demographics (age, gender, race/ethnicity, field site), individual socio-economic indicators (highest education attained, family income), language of administration (English, Spanish, Chinese), and time in neighborhood (average hours per week in neighborhood).

Statistical analysis

Model fit within MESA cohort was assessed using standard fit indices, including the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and the Bentler-Bonett Normed Fit Index (NFI)(Sun, 2005). Fit statistics were evaluated overall and stratified by study site, age group categorized at the median (median age = 75), time in neighborhood (categorized into tertiles), and language of administration. Standardized factor loadings were examined to assess item performance across groups.

To investigate the sources of suboptimal model fit observed in the primary confirmatory factor analysis, we conducted post-hoc diagnostic analyses on the full-sample CFA model. We examined modification indices for factor loadings and error covariances using the MODIFICATION option in PROC CALIS. Modification indices (Lagrange multiplier index) estimate the improvement in model chi-square that would result from freeing each fixed parameter; values greater than 10 were considered indicative of meaningful local misfit. We also examined the standardized residual covariance matrix using the RESIDUAL option, which reflects the difference between observed and model-implied item covariances standardized for comparability; pairs with absolute standardized residuals greater than 2.0 were flagged as inadequately reproduced by the model.

We examined correlations between the PACER subscales and two theoretically relevant external variables: amount of neighborhood change and pace of change. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the strength and direction of association between each subscale and the change variables.

We calculated PACER subscale scores and examined their variation across key population characteristics. Group means and standard deviations were reported for each characteristic. Statistical differences between groups were assessed using generalized linear models (GLM), and associated p-values were used to determine significance. We used these analyses to evaluate construct relevance and the ability of the subscales to detect meaningful population-level differences.

All analyses were conducted using SAS software [9.4] (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Sample characteristics

The study sample was 77.3 (SD 7.5) years of age on average, primarily male (55%) and diverse in race/ethnicity, including 25% Black/African American, 23% Hispanic, 12% Chinese, and 40% White non-Hispanic. Over 40% are college graduates or higher and almost a quarter of the sample have a household income of 100,000 or more. The majority own more than one car (52%) and are not currently working (68%) (Table 1).

Factor structure and validity

Based on the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) results, the PACER instrument demonstrates moderate model fit in the overall MESA sample, with RMSEA of 0.07 (slightly above the conventional threshold of 0.06), and comparative fit indices (CFI = 0.75, NNFI = 0.70, NFI = 0.73) below the commonly accepted standard of 0.90 (Table 2).

When stratified by study site, age group, and time in neighborhood, similar patterns emerge: RMSEA values are consistently above the acceptable range (0.07–0.08), CFI and other fit indices remain below threshold (Supplement Table 1). Within this sample of older adults, loadings were similar for participants above and below the median age, providing no evidence of inconsistency in instrument performance across the age range represented in MESA. Minor variations in item loadings by hours spent in neighborhood were observed, but the patterns were broadly stable, indicating robustness of the factor structure across differing levels of neighborhood engagement. We observed greater variation by study site, with generally higher loadings in New York and Chicago and lower loadings in Baltimore (See Supplemental Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3). Due to the small sample sizes completing the survey in Spanish or Chinese, we were unable to conduct model fit analyses or estimate item factor loadings stratified by language of administration.

Across the full MESA sample, most items demonstrated moderate to strong loadings (generally ≥0.40) on their respective factors, supporting the construct validity of the PACER subscales: feelings, built environment, social environment, and affordability (Table 2). Loadings were highest for the built environment subscale and the lowest for the affordability subscale. To investigate sources of suboptimal overall model fit, we conducted post-hoc diagnostic analyses examining modification indices and standardized residual covariances.

Modification indices identified two items as the primary sources of misfit. The Personality item (neighborhood personality has changed) showed the largest modification indices in the table, indicating that model fit would improve if this item were permitted to load onto the affordability, feelings, and built environment subscales. The Housing item (cost of housing has increased) showed similarly large modification indices for cross-loading onto the social environment, built environment, and feelings subscales.

The standardized residual covariance matrix identified 20 item pairs with absolute values greater than 2.0 (Supplemental Table 3). The Housing item accounted for seven of these pairs, all with built and social environment items and all negative, indicating that greater perceived neighborhood change co-occurred with reports of increased housing costs in ways the model does not capture. The largest residuals overall were for Flipping–Housing (−5.59), Unsure–Pushed (+5.32), Buildings–Housing (−5.14), and Unsure–Personality (−5.08).

Spearman correlation coefficients among the subscales ranged from −0.10 to 0.46 (Table 2), indicating that the subscales capture related but distinct dimensions of neighborhood change. As expected, perceived changes in the built and social environment subscales were positively correlated, while affordability was positively associated with feelings about change and negatively associated with increased built and social environment change.

Correlations with two single-item measures assessing the amount and pace of neighborhood change provided additional evidence of criterion-related validity (Table 2). Greater perceived built and social environment change was associated with reporting more and faster overall neighborhood change. In contrast, affordability scores were negatively associated with both the amount and pace of change, indicating that more rapid or extensive change may be perceived as undermining affordability. Feelings about change were not correlated with either the amount or pace of change.

Subscale differences by sociodemographic characteristics

Respondents in this sample reported generally positive perceptions of neighborhood change, with a mean score of 0.44 on the feelings subscale. On average, participants perceived moderate levels of change in the built environment (mean = 0.24) but minimal change in the social environment (mean = 0.02). Respondents perceived affordability negatively, with a mean score of −0.11 (Table 2).

We observed expected differences in PACER subscales by sociodemographic characteristics (Table 3). Differences by age and gender were minimal, while significant differences emerged by race/ethnicity, education, and income. White non-Hispanic respondents reported slightly more positive feelings about neighborhood change than other groups. Black/African American and Hispanic respondents expressed greater concerns about affordability and perceived more built and social environment change than White non-Hispanic and Chinese respondents. Lower socioeconomic status (SES) respondents, including those with less than a high school education or low income, reported the greatest affordability concerns and the least positive feelings about change, with a consistent linear trend toward more favorable views as SES increased. The lowest SES group also perceived less physical and social transformation than the highest SES group. While perception did not vary by hours spent in neighborhood for affordability or feelings, participants perceived more built and social environment change with increasing time in neighborhood. We observed significant differences in all the PACER subscales by MESA site and language of administration.

Discussion

This conceptual replication addressed three aims. First, the hypothesized four-factor structure was broadly supported in MESA: most items loaded as expected on their intended subscales, and this pattern held across age groups, levels of neighborhood engagement, and study sites, extending prior validation work in Philadelphia and Montreal (Hirsch et al., 2021; Youngbloom et al., 2023) to a larger, older, and more geographically diverse cohort. Second, concurrent and criterion-related validity were supported by subscale correlations with perceived amount and pace of neighborhood change that aligned with theoretical expectations. Third, PACER subscales demonstrated meaningful differences by sociodemographic characteristics, hours spent in neighborhood, and geography.

While 17 of 19 items loaded at or above 0.40 on their intended factors, the overall model fit indices did not meet conventional thresholds (Sun, 2005). Post-hoc diagnostic analyses indicated that misfit was concentrated in several items. For housing costs, standardized residuals showed that perceived increases in housing costs were negatively associated with perceived change across the built and social environment subscales. This pattern aligns with prior work describing rising housing costs as a marker of gentrification-driven transformation rather than an independent dimension of residents' affordability concerns (Ding et al., 2016; Zuk et al., 2018). The perceived change in neighborhood personality item showed substantial cross-loading onto the affordability, feelings, and built environment subscales, suggesting that it may function as a broad indicator of perceived neighborhood change rather than a dimension specific to the social environment subscale. Additionally, lack of fit for housing and neighborhood personality may reflect the 2022–2024 data collection window, a period of pandemic-related shifts in neighborhood life, including housing market volatility, and other individual and contextual factors (Cornelissen and Hermann, 2025; Finlay et al., 2022; Newton and Vickery, 2022). Finally, the correlated residual between Pushed (afraid of being pushed or forced out) and Unsure (feel unsure I will stay) suggests that these items capture a shared dimension of displacement precarity, consistent with qualitative work describing the fear of displacement and uncertainty about remaining as a meaningful experience for older residents rather than two separable judgments (Versey, 2018, Versey, 2023; Versey et al., 2019).

MESA participants reported modest built environment change, little social change, slight declines in affordability, and overall positive feelings. Consistent with our prior work suggesting that greater time spent in one's home neighborhood increases exposure to, and the salience of, neighborhood conditions (Rosso et al., 2013), perceived built and social environment change increased with hours spent in the neighborhood. Variation in PACER subscale scores across MESA study sites may reflect known differences in the pace, scale, and character of gentrification across these urban contexts, consistent with prior research documenting that gentrification unfolds differently across cities (Delmelle, 2015). These geographic distinctions may influence how neighborhood change is experienced and perceived, underscoring the importance of considering context in interpreting PACER responses. PACER subscale scores varied along racial/ethnic and socioeconomic lines. This pattern is consistent with scholarship characterizing gentrification as a racialized socioeconomic process in which longstanding residents who are disproportionately people of color and lower-income are more likely to perceive neighborhood change as threatening to their housing security, social ties, and sense of belonging (Hwang and Sampson, 2014; Versey, 2018, Versey, 2023; Versey et al., 2019). The differential perceptions we observe likely reflect differences in structural vulnerability to displacement rather than differences in awareness of or exposure to neighborhood change per se.

Overall, this study supports PACER's utility for research on neighborhood change and gentrification. Often, change has been measured quantitatively using repeated cross-sectional assessments (Diez Roux et al., 2016; Tcymbal et al., 2020), or by emphasizing area-level quantitative measures from Geographic Information Systems (GIS) (Bhavsar et al., 2020; Ding et al., 2016). Recently, novel measurement tools have emerged to understand residents' experiences of change, including PACER (Binet et al., 2022; DeVylder et al., 2019; Dsouza et al., 2022; Hirsch et al., 2021). PACER's ability to capture resident perspectives on specific dimensions of change rather than relying solely on GIS-based administrative data makes it particularly valuable for studies aiming to understand the social and health consequences of gentrification in varied neighborhood contexts and among diverse individuals (Delmelle, 2015; Smith et al., 2020).

Several limitations should be noted. The CFA model fit indices did not meet conventional thresholds, with misfit concentrated in two items and one cross-subscale item pair. The overall factor structure, strong performance of most items, and support for criterion-related validity nonetheless indicate that PACER subscales remain useful for capturing residents' perceptions of neighborhood change (Hirsch et al., 2021). Misfit may reflect pandemic-related disruptions (Cornelissen and Hermann, 2025; Finlay et al., 2022; Newton and Vickery, 2022); replication during periods free of widespread disaster events would help clarify whether this reflects a stable feature of the instrument or a period-specific effect. Future work testing alternative item wordings or subscale placements for the misfitting items would help determine whether these changes improve model fit without compromising content coverage. Further, as MESA only administered PACER during one exam, cross-sectional data limit the ability to assess temporal stability. Finally, while MESA's diversity is a strength, the cohort consists of older adults and may not generalize to younger populations or to metropolitan areas with different development trajectories than the six included in the MESA sample. Perceptions of neighborhood change may also vary by cultural context and acculturation. Although PACER was administered in multiple languages to improve accessibility, differences in lived experience and interpretation may influence responses; future research should examine measurement performance of translated instrument across cultural contexts.

Conclusion

This replication provides support for the validity of PACER in capturing perceived neighborhood change beyond the settings of the original validation studies. PACER offers a valuable tool for investigating the complex pathways linking gentrification, neighborhood change, and health across varied social and physical contexts.
