Experimental study on the influence of non-penetrating crack spatial distribution on brittle fracture process
Authors: Jun Xu, Sen Luo, Xiaochun Xiao, Jiaxu Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo gain insights into the spatial distribution of non-penetrating cracks during the rock fracture process, a comprehensive uniaxial compression test is conducted on cubic gypsum specimens (100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm) containing two non-penetrating cracks. The two pre-formed cracks are rectangular, with dimensions of 25 mm length, 2 mm width, and depths of 80 mm and 35 mm on adjacent sides of the specimen. The depth of the 80 mm crack can be adjusted from 0° to 150° in increments of 30°, while the other is fixed at a 45° angle. The results show that the spatial distribution of non-penetrating cracks can significantly influence the strength of the specimen. Initially, the strength of the specimen exhibits an upward trend and subsequently declines as the pre-crack inclination angle of the main rupture plane increases, ultimately reaching its pinnacle at 90°. The total percentage of tensile cracks in specimens with different inclinations are found to be 57%, 57%, 63%, 77%, 68%, and 61%, respectively. This change aligns seamlessly with the fluctuation in specimen strength as influenced by the angle of inclination. Non-penetrating cracks can also induce spalling on the specimen surface and give rise to anti-wing cracks, thereby exacerbating the spalling on the specimen surface. The inclinations of non-penetrating cracks can inevitably exert a certain influence on the propagation of neighboring non-penetrating cracks. Additionally, the macro-scale shear fracture of the specimen often occurs on the side of the non-penetrating crack that is deeper. The curved tensile fracture surface formed by the extension of the non-penetrating crack bears resemblance to the non-penetrating region in its ability to somewhat restrain the propagation of new cracks. Even under uniaxial compression, the spalling surface of the specimen containing spatial non-penetrating cracks frequently exhibits fracture characteristics belonging to I-III mode fracture, while its interior may display characteristics belonging to I-II-III mode fracture. These findings hold significant implications for comprehending and elucidating the genuine fracture process and three-dimensional fracture theory of rocks.Keywords: Non-penetrating crack, Spatial distribution, Brittle fracture, Inclination angle, RockSubject terms: Civil engineering, Petrology

Introduction

In practical applications, cracks in rock engineering often exhibit non-penetrating characteristics1. The cracks in the rock mass are often overlooked due to their limited observability. However, when subjected to external factors, these cracks can easily trigger engineering disasters, posing a threat to equipment and even endangering the safety of workers. The susceptibility of non-penetrating cracks to external environmental influences in deep rock engineering renders certain engineering disasters more prevalent2, including rock wall spalling, local collapse, and even rockburst, as shown in Fig. 1.

Disasters in deep rock engineering. (a) Rock wall spalling at Micang mountain3, (b) Rock wall local collapse4, (c) Rockburst in Micang mountain3.

Consequently, the attention from scholars towards the engineering disasters caused by non-penetrating cracks has been steadily increasing. Using rock or rock-like specimens, scholars have made numerous valuable discoveries. Actually, it is challenging to obtain rock samples with consistent primary fractures in practical engineering applications. Scholars often resort to utilizing laboratory-prepared rock materials for specimen preparation. Among the commonly employed materials, resin and gypsum stand out due to their exceptional plasticity, ease of molding, and suitability for mass production. Wang et al.5, Dyskin et al.6,7, Germanovich et al.8 and Sahouryeh et al.9 conducted a series of uniaxial and multiaxial compression tests on resin materials with embedded 3D cracks. They reported that under uniaxial compression, the prefabricated 3D cracks exhibited a tendency to be wrapped by wing cracks. Within a specific range of pressure, the propagation of 3D cracks is found to be impeded; however, the presence of confining pressure results in a reduction or even elimination of this inhibition. Cui and Wong10 emphasized that the primary cause of this inhibition lies in the intermediate principal stress, to which wing cracks exhibit high sensitivity under multiaxial compression. Wang et al.11–14 also investigated the propagation of embedded 3D cracks using resin materials and correlated it with ultrasonic fracturing in engineering. Similar findings can also be seen in the investigations of Han et al.15,16, Xu et al.17, Zhou et al.18, Fu et al.19, Tang et al.20, Wong et al.21 and Guo et al.22. With pre-cracked gypsum specimens, Xu et al.1 highlighted that the propagation of non-penetrating and penetrating cracks exhibited minimal disparity compared to the surface of specimen, whereas the propagation surface of these cracks significantly deviated from the fracture surface. Yang et al.23 investigated the influence of 3D crack spacing on both propagation and interconnection in cement mortar, revealing that an increase in spacing leads to a larger load-bearing region between the initiation and peak stresses of cracks. Teng et al.24 fabricated a cube specimen with surface cracks using cement mortar and investigated its shear failure behavior. Similarly, Mei et al.25 fabricated cylindrical specimens containing two adjacent 3D cracks embedded in cement mortar and found that the creep failure of these neighboring cracks in rock mass exhibits a composite failure mode characterized by tension-shear. By considering the shape and inclination of pre-fabricated 3D cracks, Wang et al.26 investigated the influence of temperature, defect morphology, and ice filling on the mechanical properties of rocks. Xu et al.2 emphasized that non-penetrating cracks in rock mass are prone to induce localized stress concentration under cyclic disturbance, thereby leading to more severe failure of the rock mass. Furthermore, the utilization of 3D printing technology has provided a glimmer of hope for investigating intricate fracture networks within rock masses27,28; however, due to limitations in available printing materials and costs, the application of 3D printing technology remains restricted when studying non-penetrating fractures. Based on the laser-medium damage theory, Wang et al.29, Li et al.30 and Zhang et al.31 successfully developed a method for achieving 3D internal cracks of any number and size; however, this approach still exhibits a significant reliance on the material properties.

Although scholars have conducted valuable investigation and exploration using resin materials and rock-like substances, it is important to note that these materials are not actual rocks, which means the conclusions may have certain limitations. Based on the gabbro specimens containing 3D surface cracks, Guo et al.32 observed that during uniaxial compression, the fracture behavior of pre-existing cracks is predominantly characterized by anti-wing cracks, and new crack initiation does not occur at the end of these pre-existing cracks. By employing granite specimens containing 3D surface cracks, Yin et al.33 reported that during uniaxial compression, in addition to the occurrence of anti-wing cracks, petal cracks also manifest and significantly contribute to the initiation, propagation, and coalescence of new cracks. The findings hold significant implications for the investigation of engineering disasters resulting from the progressive development of non-penetrating cracks. In the investigations of Xu et al.34 and Wang et al.35, they also provide a comprehensive and systematic overview of non-penetrating cracks from diverse perspectives, highlighting the challenges encountered in current research along with potential solutions. It should be noted that due to the limitation of specimen size, many experiments can only be conducted in a laboratory setting. Scale effects have always been a critical issue in rock mechanics study, and mechanical properties such as peak strength, peak strain, and modulus of elasticity may changes as the scale transitions from the laboratory to engineering scale36,37. These challenges are currently difficult to effectively address, however, conducting laboratory-scale experiments remains essential. For example, laboratory-scale tests can provide us with fundamental data and initial understanding of rock mass collapse38 and rock bursting problems39, which can offer valuable reference points for understanding the behavior of large-scale rocks.

These studies hold significant theoretical and scientific implications in comprehending the evolutionary patterns of non-penetrating cracks in rocks. However, cracks in rocks are often randomly distributed and previous investigations often overlook the spatial distribution characteristics of non-penetrating cracks by placing prefabricated cracks on a single side of the specimen during preparation. Therefore, this study investigates the influence of spatial crack distribution on crack propagation, connection, and fracture processes under compression by considering their spatial arrangement. In this study, considering the propagation and coalescence of cracks, a test specimen is designed with two pre-fabricated cracks of varying depths and inclinations. The two pre-fabricated cracks are located on adjacent sides of the specimen, with the inclination of one crack able to be varied while the inclination of the other is fixed at 45°. The study findings contribute to a more profound comprehension of the evolution of non-penetrative cracks in rocks and offer novel insights into the mechanisms through which such cracks precipitate disasters.

Experimental study on the spatial distribution of non-penetrating cracks

Gypsum is a special kind of solid brittle material. Due to its numerous advantages, such as easy availability, the ability to be molded into various shapes, and similar physical and mechanical properties to ordinary rocks, many scholars40–44 have proposed the use of gypsum in the study of rocks, ceramics, and concrete. Therefore, gypsum is used to prepare the experimental specimens in this investigation.

Specimen design and preparation

The cracks encountered in actual engineering are often non-penetrating1,2 and rarely distributed in the same plane. Considering the distribution law of non-penetrating cracks, experimental specimens are designed for this study. The specific specimen size and the distribution of non-penetrating cracks are illustrated in Fig. 2a. It is important to note that crack 1 and crack 2 are the same size, with the only difference being their varying depths. Additionally, the inclination of crack 2 is set at a fixed angle of 45°, whereas the inclination of crack 1 can be adjusted.

Specimen size and specimen preparation process.

In the process of specimen preparation, the specimen is prepared according to the gypsum powder-water ratio of 1:0.32. The specific specimen preparation scheme is as follows: First, the gypsum powder is slowly poured into the water and stirred slowly in one direction. Then, the stirred slurry is allowed to stand for 5 min before being slowly poured into the mold to reduce the impact of bubbles on the specimen. Finally, the mold is installed and allowed to stand for 15 min before pulling out the latch and demolding, and after being removed from the mold, the specimens are subjected to a 12-hour thermal treatment at 90 °C in a thermostat before being utilized.

Experimental equipment and scheme

The experiment is conducted using displacement loading at a rate of 0.008 mm/s. During the experiment, the acoustic emission (AE) signal of the test specimen is monitored during the fracture process using a DS5-8A full-information AE signal analyzer. To minimize the impact of environmental noise on AE, the signal threshold value is set at 100db. Additionally, the entire process of specimen failure is documented using the NPX-GS6500UM high-speed industrial camera, as depicted in Fig. 3. Strength and damage behaviors of specimen containing non-penetrating cracks

Strength of the pre-cracked specimen

Defects in materials often exert a discernible influence on the strength of materials1. To investigate the load fluctuations of the specimen throughout the loading procedure, the stress–strain curves of pre-cracked specimens-A and the distribution of peak stress among all pre-cracked specimens are illustrated in Fig. 4.

The stress–strain curves and the peak stresses of all pre-cracked specimens. (a) Stress and strain curves of specimens A, (b) Stress and strain curves of specimens B, (c) Stress and strain curves of specimens C (d) Average value with peak stress.

As depicted in Fig. 4a, the rock-like specimen with non-penetrating cracks also demonstrates various characteristic fracture processes during uniaxial loading: compaction stage, elastic deformation stage, stable fracture stage, unstable fracture stage, and post-peak macroscopic failure stage. It can also be seen that the strength of the specimen is also influenced by the inclination (angle) of non-penetrating cracks. In order to further investigate the influence of non-penetrating crack inclination on specimen strength, a statistical analysis is conducted on the peak stresses of all specimens, as shown in Fig. 4d. The observed trend indicates that the strength of the specimen progressively increases as the inclination of the non-penetrating crack gradually rises, until it reaches 90 degrees. However, beyond this threshold, a decline in specimen strength is evident with further increase in inclination. These results exhibit a resemblance to those acquired from a uniaxial compression test conducted on a specimen containing two penetrating cracks, as shown in Fig. 5.

Peak load distribution of the specimen containing two penetrating cracks43.

Moreover, in comparison to the intact specimen, the presence of non-penetrating cracks exerts a detrimental influence on the strength of the specimen. This underscores the imperative need to avoid crack formation in practical engineering applications, particularly during geotechnical and civil construction processes.

Acoustic emission characteristics

During the loading process of the solid brittle specimen, the initiation and propagation of microcracks are accompanied by the accumulation and release of energy, which can be effectively monitored and reflected by the AE system45. In order to further investigate the impact of spatial non-penetrating cracks on the fracture process of the specimen, a random selection of experimental results from Group A is conducted. Figure 6 illustrates the AE energy and stress-time curves of pre-cracked specimens-A with varying crack inclination angles.

AE energy and stress-time curves of pre-cracked specimens-A. (a) Pre-cracked specimen A-0, (b) Pre-cracked specimen A-30, (c) Pre-cracked specimen A-60, (d) Pre-cracked specimen A-90, (e) Pre-cracked specimen A-120, (f) Pre-cracked specimen A-150.

From Fig. 6, the stress-time curve aligns closely with the standard stress–strain curve that is typical of rocks, demonstrating a high level of consistency. The curve can also be roughly divided into four stages: (1) Compaction stage: the axial stress increases gradually with the increase of axial strain, exhibiting a concave downward curve; (2) Elastic deformation stage: during this stage, the stress–strain relationship is approximately linear; (3) Unstable expansion stage: as microcracks in the pre-cracked specimen continue to propagate and accumulate damage, the slope modulus of the curve decreases with increasing strain. At this stage, the stress reaches its peak and slightly decreases; (4) Post-peak failure stage: the axial stress rapidly decreases with increasing axial strain as macroscopic cracks expand and connect, ultimately leading to specimen failure, as shown in Fig. 6a. In addition, it is evident that an increasing loading time leads to a potential release of acoustic emission energy throughout the entire loading process, particularly before and after the specimen’s peak, where frequent occurrences of acoustic emission energy release are observed.

It should be noted that the loading level in this study is defined as the ratio of arbitrary loading time to total loading time. Specifically, at the beginning of the loading process, the calibrated damage value is initialized to 0, indicating a loading level of 0%. Upon completion of the loading process, when the calibrated damage value reaches 1, it signifies a loading level of 100%.

Additionally, through the AE waveform with parameters, as presented in Fig. 7, average frequency (AF) is defined as the ratio of ringing counts to the duration of ringing, while rising angle (RA) is defined as the ratio of rise time to waveform amplitude. In the AE waveform, the characteristics of tensile cracks differ significantly from those of shear cracks. Tensile cracks exhibit a shorter wavelength, higher frequency, rapid rise time, and significant amplitude, reflecting the rapid accumulation and release of internal stresses in the material under tension. Conversely, shear cracks display distinct features including a longer wavelength, lower frequency, relatively longer rise time, and smaller amplitude, as shown in Fig. 7b and c. This disparity arises due to the relatively slow propagation speed of shear waves within the material leading to this waveform characteristic. During experimentation, a larger AF value indicates a tensile crack while a larger RA value signifies a shear crack. Therefore, distinguishing between different types of cracks can be achieved by examining the ratio of AF to RA46,47.

From Fig. 8, during the compaction stage at loading levels of 0–20%, the initial micro-cracks gradually close without significant damage or only minimal damage. In the loading range of 20–60%, internal cracks in the specimen enter a relatively stable growth stage, accompanied by a gradual increase in the damage value up to approximately 0.2. Subsequently, when subjected to loading levels between 60 and 100%, brittle failure occurs at some point leading to rapid expansion and penetration of cracks throughout the entire specimen. At this stage, there is a sudden increase in the damage value reaching around 0.85 during peak-post stage, followed by a plateau phase with significantly slower increment.

To further analyze the distribution characteristics of cracks in different loading time intervals, the mode of tensile and shear cracks can be identified by the ratio of AF/RA48. The proportion of tensile and shear cracks in each loading level interval is presented in Fig. 9.

Distribution of tensile and shear crack fractions at various loading levels. (a) Pre-cracked specimen A-0, (b) Pre-cracked specimen A-30, (c) Pre-cracked specimen A-60, (d) Pre-cracked specimen A-90, (e) Pre-cracked specimen A-120, (f) Pre-cracked specimen A-150.

According to Fig. 9, in the loading range of 0–40%, except for the specimen with a 30° inclination, the proportion of shear cracks in other specimens is below 2%. Conversely, the proportion of tensile cracks significantly exceeds that of shear cracks during this stage, indicating that tensile cracking predominantly occurs during early loading stages. As the load increases, the proportion of both types of cracks gradually escalates, and upon specimen failure, it yields a substantial quantity of tensile and shear fractures. After reaching the peak stage, there is a decrease in both the number of AE signals and cracks observed. Ultimately, for specimens A-0, A-30, A-60, A-90, A-120, and A-150, the total percentage of tensile cracks are found to be 57%, 57%, 63%, 77%, 68%, and 61%, respectively. It can be observed that the overall percentage of tensile cracks initially increases and then decreases as the inclination angle increases. The highest percentage is observed at an angle of 90°, which aligns with the trend shown in Fig. 5b depicting peak stress. Furthermore, shear cracks account for approximately 40% of failures, indicating a mixed failure mode involving both tension and shear.

Fracture characteristics of specimen containing spatial non-penetrating cracks

Analyzing crack propagation and fracture patterns on the surface and within the pre-cracked specimen is of paramount importance for comprehending the failure process of both the pre-cracked specimen and rock fracture mechanics. Therefore, in this section a comprehensive analysis and discourse on the macroscopic fracture morphology of the pre-cracked specimen will be undertaken.

Crack pattern on the pre-cracked specimen surface

To examine the crack propagation pattern on the pre-cracked specimen surface, the test results are meticulously organized and annotated, as shown in Figs. 10 and 11.

Schematic diagram of pre-existing surface crack propagation pattern.

Surface features of the pre-cracked specimen, where W is the wing crack, and A-W is the anti-wing crack.

In Fig. 10a, the newly generated cracks at the tip of the primary crack are classified as wing cracks, anti-wing cracks, and secondary cracks. Previous studies33,43 indicate that wing and anti-wing cracks typically initiate at or near the tip of the primary crack under uniaxial compression and extend along the loading direction. In contrast, while secondary cracks also generate from the tip of the primary crack, their extension direction is less certain. In Fig. 10b, three types of rock fracture are presented.

From Fig. 11, during the loading process, there is a certain degree of spalling observed on the surface of the pre-cracked specimen, particularly in the upper region where it makes contact with the pressure testing apparatus. The occurrence of spalling is closely associated with localized stress concentration on the surface of the specimen. In the investigation of Xu et al.38, they systematically examined the formation mechanism of spalling in rocks and posited that it could serve as a precursor to complete rupture of the specimen. It can be anticipated that the occurrence of localized wall surface spalling in deep rock engineering will induce a shift in stress transfer pathways, resulting in the emergence of new stress concentration phenomena, hastening the progression of rock damage, and ultimately precipitating catastrophic engineering failures, including rock bursts. Therefore, in practical engineering, it is imperative to allocate adequate attention to the localized failure resulting from non-penetrating cracks.

The fracture behavior of specimens featuring non-penetrating cracks in various spatial positions exhibits a higher level of complexity when compared to those with penetrating cracks38. According to scholarly studies, in uniaxial compression, wing (or tensile) cracks and secondary (or shear) cracks typically initiate at the tip of pre-existing cracks. In Fig. 11, at the non-penetrating crack tips, in addition to the wing and secondary cracks, anti-wing cracks are also observed, typically exhibiting tensile characteristics33. It can also be found that the observed experimental phenomena on the surface of these specimens containing non-penetrating cracks exhibit a high level of consistency with previous studies1,32,33. However, this result deviates from the experimental findings of specimens containing penetrating cracks, where the occurrence of anti-wing cracks is infrequent. The primary reason for this phenomenon may be the propensity of non-penetrating cracks to induce stress concentration at the pre-existing crack tip, thereby accumulating energy. Energy dissipation can occur through two mechanisms: intensified fracture at the pre-existing crack tip and initiation of new cracks at the same location.

Additionally, according to Fig. 11, despite the fixed inclination of 45° for the non-penetrating cracks on one side of the specimen, significant variations in the propagation path of tensile-type crack extension at the pre-existing crack tips are observed with respect to the inclination of the adjacent non-penetrating crack. This observation suggests a discernible influence between two spatially adjacent non-penetrating cracks.

Relevant features of fracture within the pre-cracked specimen

In order to analyze the macroscopic fracture characteristics of the pre-cracked specimens containing spatial non-penetrating cracks under uniaxial compression, the front surfaces of the pre-cracked specimens have been meticulously cleansed, as shown in Fig. 12.

Macro fracture features of the pre-cracked specimens.

Based on the observations from Fig. 12, it is evident that a majority of the pre-cracked specimens exhibit macro shear fracture, and the development of the shear fracture surface follows a distinct trajectory, originating from one of the diagonals of the specimen and extending directly towards both ends of the pre-existing crack. In its entirety, the symmetrical shear fracture surface exemplifies a characteristic mode of shear failure. Additionally, it can also be found that the macroscopic shear failure plane extends from the frontal surface of the specimen towards its interior, while not manifesting on the right side of the specimen. This phenomenon may exhibit a strong correlation with the depth of pre-existing cracks. The right surface of the specimen exhibits predominantly a spalling phenomenon. This observation suggests that the presence of non-penetrating cracks in the specimens leads to failure as they propagate from the surface towards the interior under uniaxial compression.

To further investigate the fracture features within the pre-cracked specimen, the tested specimens are collected and presented in Fig. 13.

Fracture features within the pre-cracked specimens.

According to Fig. 13, under uniaxial compression, the presence of two non-penetrating cracks results in significant mutual influence, as indicated by the blue and red circles in Fig. 13. The expansion of wing or anti-wing cracks results in the formation of a relatively flat depression within the blue marked area. The area marked by the red circle exhibits uneven fracture surfaces resulting from the combined influence of wing cracks and secondary cracks, leading to the generation of substantial debris during the fracturing process. Furthermore, the non-penetrating region exerts a discernible inhibitory role in the process of non-penetrating crack propagation, as indicated by the yellow circles in Fig. 13. This inhibitory effect not only influences the trajectory of crack propagation but also diminishes its velocity, thereby impacting both the overall mechanical properties and failure mode of the specimen. In fact, during the process of non-penetrating crack propagation, particularly under uniaxial compression, a wrapping phenomenon is observed in the propagation of wing cracks or anti-wing cracks, which effectively impedes the further advancement of newly formed cracks5,10,34.

Discussion on the fracture characteristics induced by spatial non-penetrating cracks

The results of uniaxial compression tests on specimens containing non-penetrating cracks with varying spatial distributions are systematically analyzed in Sections "Strength and damage behaviors of specimen containing non-penetrating cracks" and "Fracture characteristics of specimen containing spatial non-penetrating cracks". Based on the crack propagation patterns observed on the specimen surface, similarities can be observed in the propagation of both non-penetrating and penetrating cracks. Specifically, wing and secondary cracks tend to form at the tip of pre-existing cracks, leading to local spalling due to stress concentration38. However, it is noteworthy that non-penetrating cracks may also generate anti-wing cracks on the specimen surface33, as presented in Fig. 11. The fracture of rocks is widely acknowledged to be closely associated with the propagation and coalescence of cracks within them. In addition to generating wing and secondary cracks under compression, non-penetrating cracks also give rise to anti-wing cracks, thereby contributing to the complexity of rock fractures induced by such cracks. This observation is further supported by the internal fracture characteristics depicted in Figs. 12 and 13. Figure 14 shows the fracture surface features of the specimens containing non-penetrating and penetrating cracks with varying spatial distributions.

Fracture surface features of pre-cracked specimens, (a–d) are the fracture surface features of the specimens containing non-penetrating cracks, and (e–g) are the fracture surface features of the specimens containing penetrating cracks, which are modified from Xu et al.38.

In Fig. 14, it is obvious that the surface and internal fracture surface characteristics of specimens containing non-penetrating cracks and penetrating cracks exhibit distinct disparities. Notably, the fracture surface resulting from non-penetrating cracks displays conspicuous features associated with composite crack propagation, both on the specimen’s surface and within its interior. Despite the presence of an identical tensile crack surface in both specimens, distinct characteristics are observed. The specimen with penetrating cracks exhibits a comparatively smoother tensile crack surface (as shown in Fig. 14e–g), whereas the specimen with non-penetrating cracks displays lance-like patterns along its edges49, as shown in Fig. 14b and d. This shape of the fracture surface is primarily determined by the combined influence of mode-I and mode-III fractures. The aforementioned experimental phenomenon is also observable in the outcomes of experiments conducted on other brittle materials11,12. It can also be seen that under uniaxial compression, in the interior of the test specimen containing non-penetrating cracks, particularly in the region where wing cracks initiate at various locations, the fracture characteristics correspond to a I-II-III mixed mode fracture, as depicted in Fig. 14b and c. Additionally, based on the experimental findings, the specimens containing spatial non-penetrating cracks under uniaxial compression frequently undergo macroscopic shear fracture originating from the deeper side of the non-penetrating crack, as illustrated in Fig. 12. These results suggest that the comprehension and characterization of the spatial distribution of these non-penetrating cracks within rock masses in practical engineering applications can significantly enhance the efficacy of monitoring and safeguarding the stability of such geological formations.

It is worth noting that this study has some limitations. Firstly, the specimens used in the study consist of gypsum powder poured into fabricated molds with synthetic fractures (controlled fractures). Such materials may behave differently from actual rock masses. Secondly, the experimental testing is limited to uniaxial loadings. However, in deep rocks, true triaxial loadings (where σ1>σ2>σ3) are often more representative. Some studies have shown that intermediate and minor principal stresses significantly influence crack propagation in highly stressed rocks5. Additionally, the study of any potential anisotropic behavior of gypsum is crucial50 and should be taken into account in future research.

Conclusions

Non-penetrating cracks are widely distributed in (deep) rock engineering. To gain insights into the spatial distribution of non-penetrating cracks during the rock fracture process, a comprehensive uniaxial compression test is conducted on pre-cracked gypsum specimens with varying depths and inclinations, and some useful conclusions are obtained:

The spatial distribution of non-penetrating cracks can significantly influence the strength of the specimen. Initially, the strength of the specimen exhibits an upward trend and subsequently declines as the pre-crack inclination angle of the main rupture plane increases, ultimately reaching its pinnacle at 90°.
