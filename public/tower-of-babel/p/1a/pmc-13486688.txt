Visualizing dual-sites synergistic catalysis in non-iridium catalysts for acidic oxygen evolution reaction
Authors: Meihuan Liu, Qianqian Xu, Peiqiong Zhou, Jianling Liu, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding electron transfer at the solid/liquid electrochemical interface is critical for deciphering synergistic catalysis, yet elucidating how metal dopants mediate charge transfer between adjacent active centers remains challenging. Here, we incorporate a series of metal dopants (Cu, Mn, W) into RuO2 to investigate how dual-site (M–Ru) electron transfer affects catalytic activity and stability. Combining in situ characterization with theoretical simulations, we reveal that Mn doping intensifies Ru 4d–O 2p orbital coupling and establishes unidirectional electron transfer, which peroxidizes Ru under high voltage and accelerates performance degradation. In contrast, W doping modulates the Ru 4d- and O 2p-band centers to reduce orbital overlap, weakening Ru–O covalency and enabling bidirectional electron transfer. Functioning as a voltage-gated electron regulator, the W site dynamically switches roles: at low potentials it acts as an electron acceptor to activate Ru sites and promote water dissociation, while at high potentials it serves as an electron donor to suppress Ru peroxidation. This mechanism endows the catalyst with good stability, showing negligible activity decay during 300 hours of operation at 500 mA cm⁻².

Introduction

Electrified solid-liquid interfaces lie at the heart of diverse electrochemical processes, governing charge transfer, ion transport, and catalytic activity. These functions underpin critical applications in energy conversion and storage, biological systems, and geochemical reactions1–5. In particular, charge transfer at the solid-liquid interface has garnered widespread attention owing to its profound influence on the electronic structure, the adsorption and dissociation of reactive species, and ultimately the activity, selectivity, and stability of electrochemical reactions6–9. However, the dynamic nature of the electrolyte, encompassing its fluidity and dispersion, coupled with the adsorption behavior of reactive species, introduces considerable complexity and uncertainty at these interfaces10–12. Experimentally, directly tracking real-time electron transfer pathways at this interface remains a formidable challenge. This limitation impedes a comprehensive understanding of catalytic reaction mechanisms at the atomic and electronic levels, posing substantial obstacles to the rational design of advanced catalyst systems13,14. Indeed, electron transfer at an active site is often modulated by neighboring sites, and theoretical calculations reveal that elemental doping and strain can optimize the electronic states and d-band centers of active sites, thereby dictating catalytic activity3,15–17. Nonetheless, due to the paucity of direct experimental evidence, the detailed electron transfer pathways between multiple sites and their interplay with reaction intermediates remain elusive. Therefore, elucidating the charge‑transfer route between dual sites under operating electrocatalytic conditions, using in situ spectroscopy, is essential for advancing our mechanistic understanding of solid‑liquid interfaces and for designing more efficient electrocatalysts.

Due to its tunable active sites and characteristic multi-electron, proton-coupled reaction pathways, the electrocatalytic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) was selected as an ideal model system for investigating the correlation between dual-site electron transfer dynamics and catalytic mechanisms at the solid-liquid interface18,19. According to previous studies, the efficient acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which serves as the anode reaction in proton exchange membrane water electrolyzers (PEMWE), is crucial for the production of clean and high-energy-density hydrogen20–22. This process addresses critical challenges related to the energy crisis, energy storage, energy conversion, and environmental pollution. Currently, ruthenium (Ru, US $9523 kg−1) based catalysts are promising lower‑cost alternatives to iridium (Ir, US $60, 670 kg−1) dioxide due to their high activity23. Unfortunately, the onset of oxygen evolution is often accompanied by Ru dissolution, primarily driven by the formation of over-oxidized Ru species (e.g., RuO4) and the loss of lattice oxygen via the lattice-oxygen-mediated mechanism (LOM), resulting in a significant decline in catalytic stability24,25. Inspired by dual-site electron transfer synergies, we propose that introducing heteroatoms at sites adjacent to Ru within the RuO₂ lattice can modulate the electronic structure of Ru and suppress its over‑oxidation at high potentials. By incorporating a series of heteroatoms (Cu, W, Mn) into RuO2 and employing acidic OER as a model system, we establish a versatile platform to systematically examine how dual-site electron transfer pathways govern catalytic activity and reaction mechanisms. Consequently, elucidating the electron‑transfer kinetics between dopants and active sites at the solid–liquid interface provides essential theoretical insights for designing highly efficient and stable advanced electrocatalysts.

Herein, a series of metal-doped RuO2 catalysts was synthesized via an “ordered template growth” strategy (denoted as TG-M/RuO2, M = Cu, W, Mn) to construct diverse dual-metal-site configurations. Doping with transition metals of varying valence states induces distinct electron-transfer behaviors at M-Ru sites, modulates the Ru 4d–O 2p orbital coupling and Ru−O covalency, and alters the electronic structure and reaction pathways of Ru sites, ultimately enhancing catalytic activity. Through combined in situ X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (XAFS) and synchrotron-radiation infrared spectroscopy (SRIR), we uncover the dual-site electron transfer pathway at the solid-liquid interface, where the W site serves as an effective electron regulator owing to its valence-cycling capability under OER conditions. At low potentials, W accepts electrons from Ru, facilitating rapid water adsorption and dissociation to enhance activity. At high potentials, W donates electrons to Ru, preventing Ru over-oxidation and improving operational stability. Theoretical calculations reveal that W doping in TG-W/RuO2 can modulate Ru 4d- and O 2p-band centers to decrease the overlap of Ru 4 d and O 2p orbitals, triggering a sustained and stable adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) pathway, which contributes to both enhanced activity and stability. For comparison, Mn doping strengthens Ru 4d–O 2p orbital coupling, establishing a unidirectional electron transfer route across dual sites that triggers Ru peroxidation under high voltage and leads to subsequent performance degradation. As a result, TG-W/RuO2 delivers good stable operation with low degradation rate during a 300 h test at 500 mA cm−2 in the resultant PEMWE device. In addition, in situ spectroscopic analysis clarifies how heterogeneous dual-site electron-transfer pathways influence the co-catalytic reaction and its underlying mechanism.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of TG-M/RuO2

Typical metals including Cu, Mn, and W were chosen as dopants due to their variable oxidation states and electronic configurations, making them suitable models for studying electron-transfer behaviors at Ru–M dual sites. Diverse dual-metal-site configurations were successfully constructed by using an “ordered template growth” strategy with different metal doping (denoted as TG-M/RuO2, M = Cu, W, Mn). As illustrated in Fig. 1a, precursors containing the metal dopants were infiltrated into a three-dimensionally ordered mesoporous KIT-6 template via capillary force-driven impregnation, followed by ultrasonic-assisted dispersion to achieve atomic-level homogeneity within the interconnected pore architecture. The mixture was then reduced by ascorbic acid to form low-valent metal complexes, which are conducive to enhancing the lattice interaction between Ru and doped metals. Subsequently, precursors@KIT-6 underwent oxidation via air annealing to yield TG-M/RuO2@KIT-6. Finally, the KIT-6 template was removed by etching in hot NaOH solution, producing highly dispersed, small M-doped RuO2 nanoparticles, hereafter referred to as (template-growth) TG-M/RuO2 (M = Cu, Mn, W). The optimal annealing temperature (400 °C, Supplementary Fig. 1) and metal doping ratio (atomic ratio of 3.6%, Supplementary Fig. 2) were determined based on the best OER activity. For comparison, template-growth pure RuO2 (TG-RuO2) and commercial RuO2 were used as reference samples. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1b, c, Supplementary Figs. 5, 6) show TG-M/RuO2 nanoparticles synthesized via the KIT-6 templating strategy exhibit a homogeneous distribution with an average diameter of ~ 10 nm, in stark contrast to commercially RuO₂ (~ 60 nm, Supplementary Fig. 4), underscoring the critical role of the mesoporous framework in mitigating particle agglomeration through nanoconfinement effects. In addition, energy-dispersive spectroscopic (EDS) mapping in Fig. 1d (Supplementary Figs. 7, 8) reveals a uniform and periodic distribution of the dopant elements (W, Cu, or Mn), Ru, and O throughout the TG-M/RuO2. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 1e) confirm the retention of the RuO₂ rutile phase (PDF# 40-1290) upon transition metal doping26. The absence of diffraction peaks from secondary phases indicates isostructural substitution of Ru sites by of Ru sites by the dopant cations within the oxide lattice. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images (Fig. 1f–h) reveal atomic-scale dispersion of the dopants. Color-coded annotations demarcating isolated Cu (green), Mn (blue), and W (yellow) atoms embedded in the RuO₂ lattice, as evidenced by distinct Z-contrast variations23. In particular, a few scattered bright spots highlighted by yellow circles correspond to W atoms (Fig. 1h), while the well-aligned features exhibiting dimmer contrast are assigned to the Ru species. Line profiles analysis of the HAADF intensity for TG-W/RuO2 (Fig. 1i) demonstrates that atomically dispersed W replaces Ru sites in the RuO2 lattice. Similar substitution is observed for Cu and Mn dopants (Supplementary Figs. 9, 10). W L3-edge extended XAFS (EXAFS) analysis (Fig. 1j) reveals a W−O first coordination shell (1.36 Å) with no detectable W−W metallic bonding27. The second coordination sphere at 2.92 Å confirms W−O−Ru heterometallic bonding, clearly distinct from the W−W or W−O−W coordination found in WO3, confirming the atomic‑level incorporation of W into the RuO₂ matrix. Similarly, Cu and Mn K-edge EXAFS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 11) unambiguously verify atomic‑scale doping. Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful construction of atomically dispersed M–Ru dual sites in the RuO₂ catalysts.

Electronic properties of TG-M/RuO2

To probe the distinct electronic interactions within the dual-site architectures, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 12). It shows the high-resolution XPS Ru 3 d and C 1 s spectra28. Compared with TG-RuO2, the doping of low-valent Cu did not markedly alter the valence state of Ru. In contrast, introducing Mn caused the Ru 3 d peak to shift to a higher binding energy by 0.13 eV, suggesting strong electronic interaction between Mn and Ru sites. Notably, W doping also induces a positive binding energy shift (+ 0.07 eV) in Ru 3 d orbitals through electron withdrawal effects, though less pronounced than that for Mn. This attenuated shift originates from the limited electron-accepting capacity of Wx+ (5 < X < 6) species (Supplementary Fig. 13) in octahedral coordination, effectively suppressing Ru over-oxidation for enhanced activity and stability. Moreover, X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectroscopy was performed to investigate the metal doping influence. The Mn K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) in Fig. 2b reveals a shift toward lower energy relative to MnO2, indicative of electron transfer from Ru to Mn, which is consistent with the XPS results (Supplementary Fig. 14). Meanwhile, the W L3-edge XANES (Fig. 2c) confirms the formation of high-valent W in TG-W/RuO2. Conversely, Cu K-edge XANES and Cu 2p XPS (Supplementary Fig. 15) verify the low-valent state of Cu dopants. Moreover, the pre-edge of the Ru K-edge XANES (Fig. 2d) appears at higher energy for TG-Mn/RuO2 and TG-W/RuO2 compared to TG-RuO2. The local coordination environments of Ru centers was investigated through the corresponding Fourier-transform (FT) EXAFS spectra29. As shown in Fig. 2e, a dominant peak located in the range of 1.4-1.5 Å for TG-M/RuO2 can be assigned to Ru−O first-shell coordination. Notably, TG-W/RuO₂ exhibits an elongated Ru−O apparent bond length (1.50 Å) versus control samples (1.46 Å), indicative of diminished Ru−O covalency. In contrast, TG-Mn/RuO₂ shows a contracted Ru−O apparent bond length (1.44 Å), consistent with enhanced Ru−O covalency. The peak at ~ 3 Å in TG-M/RuO2 can be attributed to second-shell Ru−O−Ru/M coordination. A decrease in peak intensity is accompanied by an increase in the number of oxygen vacancies and disorderliness after Mn and W doping. The increased oxygen vacancy concentration primarily serves to relax the lattice and compensate charge for stabilizing the high-valent dopant. As seen from the Supplementary Figs. 16–18 and Table 1, the fitted curves show that the M−O bond length is 1.86 ~ 1.95 Å and coordination numbers are close to 5 ~ 6. Further insight into the Ru−O and Ru−O−Ru/M coordination shells emerges from Ru K-edge EXAFS wavelet transform (WT) analysis (Fig. 2f–i and Supplementary Fig. 19). Clearly, the two intensity maxima are observed for TG-W/RuO2, in which one shift positively and the other negatively compared to RuO2, reflecting increased Ru−O bonding and the presence of W − O−Ru backscattering. In compassion, no significant change in the Ru−O bond is observed for TG-Cu/RuO2 and TG-Mn/RuO2 (Supplementary Figs. 20, 21). Correspondingly, the W L3-edge WT analysis further demonstrates similar results (Supplementary Fig. 22). Taken together, these results reveal that the homogeneous incorporation of W atoms into the RuO₂ lattice weakens Ru−O covalency through structural modulation.

Visualization of the dual-sites electron transfer pathway

The oxidation states of metal ions and the electron transfer behavior at dual-active sites dictate the thermodynamics of surface reaction by controlling intermediate adsorption energetics and transition-state configurations, thereby derectly influencing the activity and stability of the electrocatalytic process. To probe potential-dependent charge-transfer dynamics within these dual sites during the OER, we performed in situ XANES measurements using a custom-designed electrochemical cell at selected potentials30,31. To minimize interference from vigorous gas-bubble formation at high current densities on the XAFS signal-to-noise ratio, the in situ XAFS measurements were conducted within a potential window of 1.2 − 1.5 V. The W L3-edge XANES in Fig. 3a show that the white-line intensity enhances at 1.2 V (versus the reversible hydrogen electrode) compared to the ex situ condition (electrode immersed in electrolyte without applying potential). As the potential rises from 1.20 to 1.35 and then to 1.50 V, the white-line intensity first decreases and subsequently recovers, indicating that the oxidation state of W initially declines and then increases. To quantitatively track W’s electronic structural evolution under electrocatalytic polarization, we employed differential XANES (Δμ) spectroscopy32. By subtracting in situ spectra from the W foil and WO3 reference, the Δμ profiles was revealed in Supplementary Fig. 23. It shows the valence state of W in TG-W/RuO2 decreases from 5.45 to 5.03 at 1.35 V, and then rises to 5.70 at 1.50 V, implying a cyclic electron transfer process at the W site (Fig. 3b). For comparison, we also analyzed the electron-transfer behavior of the Mn site in TG-Mn/RuO2, using Mn K-edge XANES (Fig. 3c). With increasing potential, the absorption edge gradually shifts to higher energy, indicating a continuous increase in the Mn oxidation state. To further investigate the electron-transfer paths at the dual sites, Ru K-edge XANES for TG-W/RuO2 was performed. As shown in Fig. 3e, f, the Ru K-edge absorption edge exhibits a positive shift toward higher energy with increasing applied potential, indicating a gradual rise in the Ru oxidation state during the OER. Notably, this shift for TG-W/RuO₂ is significantly smaller than that observed for TG-Mn/RuO₂, demonstrating effective stabilization of lower Ru oxidation states in Ru–W pairs. To quantify the change in the oxidation state of the Ru center, the average valence state was fitted based on Ru K-edge XANES by subtracting in situ spectra from the Ru foil and RuO2 reference (Supplementary Fig. 25). As shown in Fig. 3g, the Ru valence in TG-W/RuO₂ at the activation stage (1.35 V) of the OER is 4.74, higher than the value of 4.65 in TG-Mn/RuO₂ at the same potential. This suggests that a higher Ru oxidation state promotes the activation and protonation processes of water molecules at low overpotentials. Interestingly, when the potential is increased to 1.50 V, the Ru valence in TG-W/RuO₂ rises only slightly to + 4.82, which is markedly lower than the value of TG-Mn/RuO₂ (+ 5.43). This result demonstrates that electron transfer between the W-Ru pair inhibits the peroxidation of Ru at large overpotentials, thereby enhancing the stability of ruthenium dioxide under high currents. Figure 3h directly correlates the potential‑dependent valence states of W and Ru in the W–Ru pair, illustrating their dynamic electron-transfer behavior under OER conditions. In situ XANES analysis reveals that as the potential increases from ex situ conditions to 1.35 V, the W valence decreases from 5.45 to 5.03, while the Ru valence rises sharply from 4.21 to 4.74. This demonstrates that the W site acts as an electron receptor, withdrawing electrons from Ru and thereby activating Ru sites at low overpotentials. Remarkably, at 1.50 V, the W valence rebounds to 5.70 while the Ru valence increases only marginally (by + 0.08), indicating a reversal of electron flow from W back to Ru. This charge‑compensating back‑donation dynamically suppresses Ru over‑oxidation (the schematic diagram of electron transfer is shown in Fig. 3i), ultimately enabling both high catalytic activity and operational stability.

Visualization of the reaction pathway

To elucidate the catalytic mechanism and reaction pathway in detail, in situ synchrotron‑radiation (SRIR) spectroscopy was performed using a custom‑built three‑electrode electrochemical cell33,34 (Fig. 4a). As depicted in Fig. 4b, two distinct bands emerged at 1068 and 913 cm⁻¹ in TG-W/RuO2, which are assigned to the stretching vibrations of *O − OH and Ru−O intermediates. This assignment aligns with the characteristic wavenumber ranges for *O − OH (1000–1100 cm⁻¹) and Ru-bound oxygen species (900–950 cm⁻¹), respectively23,35. Meanwhile, the intensity of these IR bands increases with applied potential, indicating that higher potentials promote the rapid accumulation of surface intermediates. For comparison, in situ SRIR measurements under analogous OER conditions were conducted on TG-Mn/RuO2 (Fig. 4c). Upon applying voltage, two absorption bands appear at 1134 cm⁻¹ and 927 cm⁻¹, attributed to the formation of *O–O* and Ru–O species. This assignment is consistent with the characteristic O–O stretching vibrations (1100–1150 cm⁻¹) observed in oxygen-containing intermediates23,36. Given that *OOH intermediates are central to the AEM, whereas *O–O species dominate in the LOM, the exclusive detection of *OOH intermediates on TG-W/RuO2 during oxygen evolution suggests that dynamic electron transfer between W − Ru dual sites triggers a stable AEM reaction pathway. To further elucidate the divergent reaction pathways of TG-W/RuO2 and TG-Mn/RuO2, isotope-labeled operando differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) was employed (Fig. 4d). TG-W/RuO2 and TG-Mn/RuO2 were deposited on Au working electrodes and first subjected to linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in an 18O-labeled acidic electrolyte to achieve sufficient labeling, and then transferred to an 16O-labeled electrolyte for cyclic voltammetry (CV) testing. As shown in Fig. 4f, TG-Mn/RuO2 consistently generated 32O234,O2, and 36O2 during each CV cycle, indicating that two H218O molecules adsorb onto Ru sites and adjacent oxygen vacancies, with subsequent coupling probabilities to form 36O2. In contrast, TG-W/RuO2 (Fig. 4e) exclusively produced 32O2, and 34O2, confirming the absence of lattice oxygen participation. These results demonstrate that TG-W/RuO2 follows the AEM pathway during oxygen evolution. Above all, dynamic electron transfer across W–Ru dual sites activates Ru centers at low potentials, accelerating the protonation of oxygenated intermediates and thereby enhancing catalytic activity. To access to the origin of activity and stability observed in TG-M/RuO2, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed37. A slab model of M-doped (Mn, W) rutile RuO₂ (110) was constructed based on experimental XRD and TEM data (Supplementary Data 1). After evaluating multiple doping sites, the most stable configuration (Supplementary Fig. 26) reveals a clear preference: M doping resides more stably at the fully coordinated bridge Ru site (BRI) than at the coordinatively unsaturated Ru site (CUS). Bader charge analysis (Fig. 5a–c) reveals that electrons transfer from Ru to metal dopants (Mn and W) via the bridge O atom. Notably, Mn doping induces better electron transfer than W doping, resulting in a higher Ru valence state in TG-Mn/RuO₂ relative to TG-W/RuO₂. Consequently, the choice of dopant metal dictates distinct electronic structures, underscoring the importance of fine-tuned control in catalyst design. The d-band center (εd) and p-band center (εp) were derived from the partial density of states (PDOS) of Ru 4 d and O 2p orbitals in RuO₂ (Fig. 5d), TG-Mn/RuO₂ (Fig. 5e), and TG-W/RuO₂ (Fig. 5f)38. Upon Mn and W doping, both εd and εp shift downward relative to the Fermi level. Notably, the energy gap between εd and εp narrows to 0.369 eV in TG-Mn/RuO₂, compared to 0.577 eV in pristine RuO₂. This reduced gap reflects enhanced overlap between Ru 4 d and O 2p orbitals, strengthening Ru─O covalency in the Mn-doped catalyst. Subsequently, the two predominant OER mechanisms for Ru-based catalysts, the LOM and AEM, were investigated39. In the LOM pathway, surface-adsorbed O* species react with lattice oxygen to form critical *O − O* intermediates before O₂ release. Analysis reveals that the O* → *O − O* step constitutes the most energetically demanding transition. For TG-W/RuO₂, this step exhibits a reaction energy of 2.97 eV, which is 0.43 eV higher than that of pristine RuO₂ (Supplementary Fig. 27a), indicating that LOM kinetics are less favorable on the W‑doped catalyst. Conversely, the AEM pathway proceeds through the coupling of O* with H₂O to generate *OOH intermediates. Upon Mn doping, the energy barrier for the *O → *O − O* step drops to 0.20 eV, which is lower than that of the competing *O → *OOH step (0.64 eV, Supplementary Fig. 27b). This confirms that LOM dominates on TG-Mn/RuO₂, a consequence of the enhanced Ru 4d–O 2p orbital hybridization induced by Mn. For TG-W/RuO₂ (Fig. 5h), accelerated electron transfer and weakened Ru − O bonding collectively promote the AEM pathway, yielding a rate-determining step energy of 1.69 eV that establishes AEM as the kinetically favored mechanism over LOM during the OER (Supplementary Fig. 28). Above all, Mn doping intensifies Ru 4d–O 2p orbital coupling, establishing a unidirectional electron transfer pathway across dual centers. This leads to the dissolution of the Ru site and a decline in performance. Notably, the W site, endowed with valence cycling capability, serves as an effective electron regulator, which not only decreasing Ru − O covalence by diminishing d-p orbital overlap but also inhibiting the peroxidation of Ru, significantly enhancing both catalytic activity and stability.

Electrocatalytic properties in acidic electrolyte

The OER catalytic efficiency of transition-metal-doped RuO2 catalysts was assessed through a three-electrode configuration, using pure RuO2 as the reference. The LSV curves presented in the main text were obtained after applying 90% iR compensation, and uncompensated LSV data likewise confirm the optimal electrochemical activity of TG-W/RuO2 (Supplementary Fig. 29). As depicted in Fig. 6a, LSV analysis reveals a hierarchical activity trend: TG-W/RuO2 exhibits the best performance, requiring an overpotential of 194 mV to reach 10 mA cm−2, outperforming TG-Mn/RuO2 (218 mV), TG-Cu/RuO2 (227 mV), TG-RuO2 (254 mV), and commercial RuO2 (297 mV). This catalytic advantage persists under high-current operation, where TG-W/RuO2 maintains efficiency at 200 mA cm−2 (Fig. 6b). LSV measurements performed with catalysts loaded on a glassy carbon electrode further demonstrate that TG-W/RuO2 achieves 10 mA cm⁻² at the lowest overpotential (Supplementary Fig. 30). The kinetics of TG-W/RuO2 was further corroborated by its low Tafel slope of 51.6 mV dec⁻¹ (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 31), markedly lower than other catalysts (60 − 80 mV dec−1), confirming accelerated reaction kinetics in acidic OER conditions40. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 32) reveals that the W-modified composite exhibits the smallest interfacial charge‑transfer resistance among all tested materials, indicating good charge‑transfer efficiency. The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) and mass activity (MA) were determined to evaluate the intrinsic activity of TG-W/RuO2. ECSA quantification, derived from double-layer capacitance (Cdl) analysis, highlights the structural advantage41. Based on cyclic voltammetry scans at different sweep rates (Supplementary Figs. 33–37), TG-W/RuO2 delivers the maximum Cdl value (9.60 mF cm-2), exceeding those of RuO2 (4.44 mF cm−2) and the other metal-doped RuO2 catalysts (5.22 − 9.27 mF cm−2), indicating more accessible active sites due to dispersion of small nanoparticles. Importantly, when normalizing activity by ECSA (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 38), TG-W/RuO2 maintains good intrinsic activity. It exhibits a mass activity of 2312 A gRu−1, surpassing conventional RuO2 (316.1 A gRu−1) by a factor of 7.3 (Fig. 6e). Catalytic stability is a critical performance metric for acidic OER, especially for Ru-based catalysts. TG-W/RuO2 demonstrates good durability, sustaining stable operation at 100 mA cm⁻² for 100 h with negligible activity loss (Fig. 6f). In contrast, RuO2 and TG-Mn/RuO2 suffer rapid performance degradation, requiring voltages above 3.0 V within 20 h. This stability improvement originates from the dynamic electron-transfer behavior at W − Ru dual sites, which effectively suppresses Ru peroxidation while reinforcing the RuO2 lattice structure under high-current conditions. Moreover, Post-catalysis characterization confirms that the morphology and electronic structure of TG-W/RuO2 are well retained, as evidenced by TEM and XAFS results (Supplementary Figs. 39, 40). The W dopant exhibits a retention rate of 87.2%, significantly higher than that of Mn (58.3%) by ICP-OES analysis (Supplementary Fig. 41). The strong electronic coupling between W and Ru effectively suppresses the dissolution of W, thereby enhancing the overall catalyst stability. More importantly, the practical viability of TG-W/RuO2 was further validated through a PEMWE electrolyzer assembled with a Nafion 117 membrane. Current–voltage (I–V) curves (without iR compensation) in Supplementary Figs. 42, 43 clearly show that the TG-W/RuO2 electrolyzer reduces the cell voltage compared with commercial RuO₂ under identical conditions. In contrast with several prior reports, our experimental outcomes appear less competitive. A likely explanation lies in our limited access to both the technical know‑how and the instrumentation required for manufacturing commercial‑quality membrane electrodes. This, however, does not affect the evaluation of our catalyst’s intrinsic activity (as shown in our standard three-electrode set-up) or the following durability tests. Cross‑sectional SEM images further illustrate the membrane-electrode structure, detailing the anode catalyst layer (TG-W/RuO2), the cathode catalyst layer, and the proton exchange membrane. As depicted in Fig. 6g, the optimized configuration achieves a current density of 500 mA cm−2 at 1.78 V and maintains good operational stability for over 300 h at 60 °C under industrial simulation conditions with low-degradation-rate activity decay. In addition, the complementary ICP-OES analysis revealed less than 7.7% ruthenium dissolution during operation, confirming good catalyst durability. These results also indicate better performance of TG-W/RuO2 in PEMWE systems.

In summary, we have synthesized a series of metal-doped RuO2 catalysts to construct diverse dual-metal-site configurations. The incorporation of W enables dynamic electron redistribution at W–Ru dual sites, which simultaneously optimizes Ru − O bond covalency and modulatesd-p orbital coupling. This electronic modulation establishes favorable reaction coordinates, leading to enhanced oxygen evolution performance. In situ XAFS and SRIR investigations elucidate the interfacial electron-transfer dynamics, revealing W’s role as a voltage‑gated electron regulator: at low potentials, it accepts electrons from Ru to accelerate water adsorption and dissociation, while at high potentials, it donates electrons back to suppress Ru over‑oxidation. Theoretical calculations demonstrate that W doping in TG-W/RuO2 may downshift the Ru 4d-band and upshift the O 2p-band centers, reducing orbital overlap to establish a stabilized AEM mechanism. The optimized catalyst achieves good durability with sustained 300-hour operation at 500 mA cm−2 in PEMWE. Despite the fact that our TG‑W/RuO2 catalyst’s activity and stability have not yet reached the level typically required in practice, this dynamic electron coupling between the two metals provides a direction for improving the performance of Ru-based catalysts. Future development should focus on further improving high‑current operational stability through synergistic strategies such as multi‑element doping, interfacial engineering, and advanced cell design.

Methods

Chemicals

Ruthenium (III) chloride trihydrate (RuCl3·3H2O, ≥ 99.9%), Manganese (II) chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O, ≥ 99.9%), Copper (II) chloride pentahydrate (CuCl2·5H2O, ≥ 99.9%), Sodium tungstate (VI) dihydrate (Na2WO4·2H2O, ≥ 99.9%) and commercial RuO2 ( ≥ 99.9%) were purchased from Aladdin Co., Ltd. Kit-6 molecular sieve were purchased from Jiangsu Xianfeng nanomaterials Technology Co., Ltd. HClO4 (70.0–72.0%) and Nafion (5%) solution were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Deionized (DI) water (18.2 MΩ cm resistivity) was used in all experiments.

Synthesis of TG-M/RuO2

Typically, RuCl₃·3H₂O (100.0 mg) and one of either MnCl₂·4H₂O (15.0 mg), CuCl₂·5H₂O (15.0 mg), or Na₂WO₄·2H₂O (15.0 mg) were dispersed in deionized (DI) water (2 mL) and ultrasonicated for 30 min to ensure homogeneity. KIT-6 was then added to this solution. To facilitate complete infiltration of the metal ions into the KIT-6 pores, the mixture underwent sequential ultrasonication for 10 min followed by vacuum treatment. Subsequently, the material was vacuum-dried overnight at 75 °C, yielding a brown powder denoted as precursor@KIT-6. Next, this powder was gently reduced using a 1 M ascorbic acid solution, then thoroughly washed with DI water, and vacuum-dried again. The resulting solid was annealed in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 3 h under air, producing TG-M/RuO₂@KIT-6 (where M = Mn, Cu, or W). Finally, to remove the silica template, the material was etched with 2 M NaOH solution at 80 °C under continuous stirring for 2 h. After repeated washing with DI water and vacuum drying, the black TG-M/RuO₂ powder was obtained.

Synthesis of TG-RuO2

The general procedures of synthesis were similar to those of the synthesis of TG-M/RuO2, except without adding metal dopants.

Material characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired on a Philips X’Pert Pro Super diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) were performed using a JEM-2100F microscope operating at 200 kV. Spherical aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) utilized an FEI Titan Themis microscope (300 kV). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted on a Kratos Axis supra + spectrometer equipped with an Al Kα source (hν = 1486.6 eV), operating at 150 W with a 300 μm × 700 μm beam spot; all binding energies were referenced to the C 1 s peak at 284.5 eV. Ru, Cu and Mn K-edge and W L3-edge X-ray absorption spectra (XAS) were collected at the 1W1B station (Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility, BSRF) and BL14W1, as well as BL20U (Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility, SSRF), respectively. Elemental composition was determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES; Perkin-Elmer Optima 7300 DV).

In situ XAFS measurements

Electrochemical cells were employed to collect in situ XAFS spectra at the Cu K‑, Mn K‑, and W L₃‑edges, with measurements carried out at the 1W1B beamline of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) and BL20U station at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) in China. Ru K‑edge data were acquired separately at the BL14W1 station of SSRF. The storage rings of BSRF and SSRF were operated at 2.5 GeV and 3.5 GeV, respectively. Harmonic rejection from the bending‑magnet source was achieved by detuning the Si (111) monochromator (used for Cu, Mn, and W) by roughly 30–20% and the Si (311) monochromator (for Ru) by 15%. The working electrodes, composed of catalyst‑loaded carbon cloth and covered with Kapton® film, were polarized at potentials between 1.20 and 1.50 V (vs. RHE) to track changes in active sites and electronic interactions during OER. Standard EXAFS data reduction was performed with the ATHENA routine of the IFEFFIT package. For Cu and Mn, the k3-weighted EXAFS oscillations in the ranges 2.4–11.3 Å⁻¹ and 2.6–10.9 Å⁻¹ were Fourier‑transformed into R-space using a Hanning window (dk = 1.0 Å⁻¹) to resolve contributions from different shells. For W, the k2-weighted χ(k) over 2.1–10.6 Å⁻¹ was transformed similarly. The structural parameters such as coordination number (N), bond length (R), Debye-Waller factor (σ2), and energy shift (ΔE0) were refined by fitting the k2- and k3- weighted EXAFS functions in the R-interval of 1.0–2.1 Å. The curve fitting was done on the k2,3-weighted EXAFS function χ(k) data in the R-range of 1.0–2.1 Å, with all these parameters treated as freely adjustable variables.

In situ SRIR measurements

In situ synchrotron radiation infrared spectroscopy (SRIR) at BL01B (National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory, China) acquired reaction intermediates and pathways using a Bruker 66 V/s IR spectrometer featuring a KBr beam-splitter, liquid N₂-cooled MCT detector, and Hyperion 3000 microscope (16 × objective). Critically, microscale electrode-window spacing control minimized aqueous-phase signal interference. Spectra collection followed 15 min potential equilibration to prevent sample-desorption artifacts, with systematic OER measurements spanning 1.00–1.50 V vs. RHE after acquiring catalyst background references at immersed in the electrolyte.

Operando DEMS measurements

A gold (Au) film (Shanghai Linglu Instrument Equipment Co., Ltd). The catalyst ink was applied directly onto the Au film and dried. Electrochemical measurements employed a standard three-electrode cell configuration (Ag/AgCl and Pt wire were used as reference and counter electrodes, respectively). For isotope labeling experiments, the electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 0.1 M HClO₄ in H₂¹⁸O (1 mL). To label the catalyst surface with ¹⁸O, both TG-W/RuO₂ and TG-Mn/RuO₂ underwent linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) cycles within this H₂¹⁸O-based electrolyte. Following labeling, the catalysts were thoroughly rinsed with copious amounts of H₂¹⁶O. This washing step removed physically adsorbed H₂¹⁸O molecules while preserving ¹⁸O-containing species chemically bound to the catalyst surface. These isotope-labeled catalysts were then operated in a standard electrolyte consisting of 0.1 M HClO₄ in H₂¹⁶O. The gaseous oxygen evolution products (³²O₂, ³⁴O₂, and ³⁶O₂) were continuously monitored using mass spectrometry.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

All DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) code, which incorporates the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange‑correlation functional under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) framework and projector augmented wave (PAW) pseudopotentials. The calculations employed a high‑accuracy mode, with a plane‑wave basis set truncated at 500 eV. A 4 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack mesh was adopted for Brillouin‑zone integration. To avoid artificial interactions between periodic slabs, a 15 Å vacuum layer was inserted. Occupancy smearing was treated with the Methfessel–Paxton scheme of second order and a broadening parameter of 0.1 eV. Geometry optimization continued until the energy convergence per self‑consistent cycle reached 10⁻⁵eV and the maximum force on each atom was less than 0.03 eV Å⁻¹. However, one must recognize that these computational results stem from idealized structures and thus might not completely account for the complicated electrochemical surroundings, such as solvation, external voltage, and dynamic surface changes during reaction.

Electrochemical measurements

OER activity was evaluated in O₂-saturated 0.1 M HClO₄ using a standard three-electrode configuration (CHI760E workstation). An Ag/AgCl electrode (saturated KCl) and a carbon rod served as reference and counter electrodes, respectively. Catalyst ink, prepared by dispersing 5.0 mg of electrocatalyst in 1 mL H₂O: isopropanol (3:1 v/v) with 20 μL Nafion, was deposited onto carbon cloth of 1 × 1 cm2 (loading: 0.25 mg cm⁻²). A certain amount of HClO₄ was slowly added to deionized water to obtain 0.1 M HClO₄ (pH = 1 ± 0.1), the electrolyte should be stored away from light and used promptly. All final potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale: E(RHE) = E(Ag/AgCl) + 0.197 V + 0.0591 × pH. The Ag/AgCl reference electrode was corrected experimentally by using Pt/C as the working electrode potential in a H2-saturated solution. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) employed a 5 mV s⁻¹ scan rate with iR compensation. Electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl), derived from CVs scanned between 1.05–1.15 V vs. RHE at 5–30 mV s⁻¹ (5 mV increments), was used to calculate ECSA (ECSA = Cdl × A / Cs, Cs = 0.035 mF cm⁻²)42. Specific activity (JECSA) represents current density normalized to ECSA. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed at an applied potential of 1.55 V (vs. RHE) over a frequency range from 0.1 to 100 kHz. The mass activity of the TG-M/RuO2, TG-RuO2 and RuO2 catalysts was determined based on the Ru loading and current density (j) at typical overpotentials of 260 and 380 mV, using the equation: MA = (j × A)/mRu, where mRu is the calculated Ru mass loaded on the electrode, A is the geometric area, and j is the geometric current density. All the tests were carried out in 0.1 M HClO4 solution at room temperature (25 °C).

Electrochemical measurements in PEMWE tests

Before PEMWE testing, the as-received Nafion™ 117 membrane (N117, DuPont, thickness 183 μm, size 2 × 2 cm2) underwent a pretreatment procedure. This involved sequential cleaning in heated (60 °C) aqueous solutions of 3 wt% H₂O₂ and 0.5 M H₂SO₄ for 1 hour, followed by storage in distilled water. For the PEMWE measurements, TG-W/RuO₂ served as the anodic catalyst and platinum-coated titanium (Pt/Ti) as the cathodic catalyst. The anode was prepared by depositing the OER catalyst (TG-W/RuO₂, 1 mg cm⁻²) containing a 5 wt% PTFE binder onto one side of N117 via spraying. Subsequently, the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was fabricated by hot-pressing the catalyst-loaded membrane, Ti felt, and the Pt/Ti cathode under controlled pressure. The resulting MEA was then integrated into the PEMWE cell for performance assessment. Cell operation utilized a 0.1 M HClO₄ electrolyte flowing at 20 mL min⁻¹ and maintained at 60 °C. The stability of the cell employing the TG-W/RuO₂ anode catalyst was evaluated through chronopotentiometry at a constant current density of 500 mA cm⁻² for 300 hours.
