Early Diagnoses of Concern Appear Valid in Predicting Adverse Neurodevelopmental Outcomes in Mid‐Childhood
Authors: Leonora P Noe, Christina E Hoei‐Hansen, Gorm Greisen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine the relationship between ‘diagnoses of neurodevelopmental concern’ before the age of 2 years and definite adverse outcomes before age 10.MethodData from national registries were linked. Children born in Denmark from 1994 to 2017 were included. Eight diagnoses of neurodevelopmental concern recorded before age 2 were compared to four definite outcomes recorded before age 10. The association was also analysed in three subpopulations at risk of perinatal brain damage.ResultsA total of 1.5 million children were included. At least one diagnosis of neurodevelopmental concern was recorded in 34 392 (2.3%) of children before the age of 2 years, but numbers increased over the years. Definite outcomes were recorded in 35 300 (2.3%) children before age 10. Of these 7 757 (22.6%) had previously had an early diagnosis of neurodevelopmental concern OR (95% CI) = 12.0 (11.5; 12.5). In the three subpopulations, diagnoses of concern as well as the risk of a definite outcome were increased 3‐to‐4 fold.ConclusionEarly diagnosis of concern appeared valid indicators of adverse neurodevelopmental outcome. The use of diagnoses of concern increased gradually over the years and cannot serve as a simple monitor at the population level.Keywords: early warning, neurodevelopmental disorder, perinatal brain injury, register‐based cohort study, statistical process control

Introduction

The growth and development of the brain is fast during the second and third trimesters [1]. A strong association has been demonstrated between premature birth and low birth weight and the risk of neurodevelopmental disorders such as cerebral palsy (CP), epilepsy, cognitive impairments and school difficulties [2, 3, 4, 5]. Lower gestational age (GA) at birth has been associated with an increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders [6, 7, 8, 9]. Among children born at term, both white matter injury and grey matter injury are associated with disabilities [10]. In these children, the brain damage may be caused by perinatal complications such as hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy [10, 11].

The practices within perinatology are continuously evolving and have contributed to a decrease in mortality. Antenatal steroid and surfactants treatment introduced in the 1990's have contributed to increased survival of preterm children [7, 12, 13]. However, new practices may carry a risk of iatrogenic harm, as was evident from the implementation of postnatal steroids in the 1990's [12, 13, 14] or the use of prophylactic antibiotics for inhibition of preterm labor among women with intact membranes [15].

While the effects of new practices, in terms of clinical course and survival, are immediately apparent to clinicians, longer‐term effects are not. National registers of diagnoses given under routine clinical care can be used to assess how children who survive later fare. The drawback is that it takes years before this can be evaluated, as definite diagnoses such as CP come with a delay. Thus, the long‐term effects of routine implementation of new treatments cannot be reassessed as quickly as one might wish.

Some diagnoses are given to children at an early age. These diagnoses are typically based on less well‐defined symptoms and occur at an earlier stage in the development of neurological and cognitive functions, and are therefore associated with greater uncertainty. The aim of this study was to explore the use of early diagnoses in Denmark and to examine their relation to more definite adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes until mid‐childhood. The hypothesis was that the presence of one or more of the early diagnoses can serve as an ‘early warning indicator’. The goal was the construction of a statistical process control chart with two standard deviation control limits that could be used annually to verify that the ‘early warning indicator’ remained within limits.

Methods

Study Population

Register data for all children born in Denmark between the 1st of January 1994 and the 31st of December 2017 were screened. Children who had died or emigrated before the age of 2 years were excluded. The individuals were identified from the Danish Medical Birth Register [16]. Data on diagnoses was collected from the Danish National Patient Register [17] and The Psychiatric Central Research Register [18] and data from Danish Educational registries [19] were linked to individuals based on personalised identification number given all Danish citizens at birth. All individuals were followed up until the end of 2022. To ensure a five‐year follow‐up, the cohort was born the years 1994–2017. All registries were accessed through Statistics Denmark. This study was registered in the systems of the Capital Region of Denmark by number P‐2023‐209. Ethical approval from The Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics is not required for register studies in Denmark.

The Danish Educational registry is only available from 2011 and onwards, and variables cover a school year. Danish children begin school at the age of 6 years. Therefore, children born before 2004 do not have their first years in school covered by this registry. Since we also needed a follow‐up until 10 years of age for the ‘definite outcomes’, all calculations of OR were based on the birth years 2004–2012.

Conditions

The diagnoses of interest were divided into two groups: ‘Diagnoses of concern’ and ‘definite outcomes’. We use the term outcomes rather than the term diagnoses to reflect that our study included special educational needs (SEN) and death. This study investigated the association between diagnoses of neurodevelopmental concern and definite outcomes. Previous studies have found an association between perinatal brain damage and definite outcomes. Thus, an ‘early warning indicator’ was constructed as at least one diagnosis of concern or at least one definite outcome recorded before 2 years of age, all describing clinical conditions that are possibly due to perinatal brain injury. Therefore, we use the term ‘indicator’. From 1994 all diagnoses in Danish registries follow WHO International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD‐10).

The diagnoses of concern were: Delayed psychomotor milestones (ICD‐10: DR62 + DR620), suspicion of neurological disease (ICD‐10: DZ033), suspicion of epilepsy (ICD‐10: DZ033A), one record of epilepsy (ICD‐10: DG40*), suspicion of psychiatric or behavioural disorder (ICD‐10: DZ032), specific developmental disorder of motor skills (ICD‐10: DF82 + DF829), behavioural disorders including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) (ICD‐10: DF84* except DF842) and specific developmental disorders of speech, language, learning, or mixed developmental disorders or psychiatric developmental disorder (ICD‐10: DF80*, DF81*, DF83*, DF88* and DF89*). The definite outcomes were: CP (ICD‐10: DG80*), two or more records of epilepsy given on separate days (ICD‐10: DG40*), SEN (> 9 h a week of special educational support) during at least 1 year in primary school, mental retardation (MR) (ICD‐10: DF70*‐DF79*) and death.

Subpopulations

Three subpopulations at increased risk of perinatal brain damage were defined: (1) prematurity defined as children born before GA of 32 weeks [20], (2) perinatal asphyxia defined as term‐born children born with an Apgar score at 5 min below seven [21], and (3) term‐born children small for gestational age (SGA) [22]. The variable SGA was calculated as birth weight for gestational age below three standard deviations [23]. These populations were chosen as they carry a moderate risk of brain damage but do not have a standardised follow‐up in Denmark.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.4.1. The odds ratio (OR) of a definite outcome before 10 years was calculated comparing children with and without a diagnosis of concern. The OR was also calculated comparing the three subpopulations at risk of perinatal brain damage with their remaining subpopulations. The likelihood ratio of a positive test (LR+) was calculated for each diagnosis of concern before 2 years for each definitive outcome before 10 years.

The ‘early warning indicator’ was set to one if the child had at least one diagnosis of concern or a definite outcome recorded before the age of 2 years and set to zero if not. A P‐chart was constructed using the R package ‘qcc’. The P‐chart is a tool in statistical process control, and the purpose of constructing a P‐chart is to illustrate whether the process is in control or out of control (whether it stays within the control limits: Lower control limit (LCL) and upper control limits (UCL) [24]).

Results

The cohort included 1 506 564 children born in 1994–2017. At least one diagnosis of neurodevelopmental concern before the age of 2 years was recorded in 34 392 (22.8‰) children. The most frequent diagnoses were ‘delayed psychomotor milestones’, ‘suspicion of neurological disease’ and ‘one record of epilepsy’, occurring in 6704 (4.4‰), 22 412 (14.9‰) and 6344 (4.2‰) children, respectively (Table 1 and Figure 1). At least one definite outcome before 10 years was recorded in 35 300 (23.4‰) children. The frequency across the outcomes was distributed as follows: CP in 5027 (4.1‰), epilepsy in 11 062 (9.1‰), SEN in 17 589 (14.5‰), MR in 8166 (6.7‰) and death in 1130 (0.9‰) children. Among children with a diagnosis of concern before 2 years, 7757 (225.5‰) of children had a definite outcome before the age of 10 years.

The OR of having a definite outcome before the age of 10 years among children with at least one diagnosis of concern compared to those without was 12.0 (95% CI: 11.5; 12.5). The OR comparing the asphyxia, SGA, and preterm subpopulations with the remaining populations was similar for children who had at least one diagnosis of concern (range 2.8–4.6) and for children who had at least one definite outcome (range 3.4–4.4).

The likelihood ratio of a positive test (LR+) of the diagnoses of concern before age 2 years for each definite outcome before 10 years varied considerably (Table 2). The pattern indicated various elements. First the pattern suggested that the overall value of at least one diagnosis of concern was significant with the likelihood ratios ranging between 7.4 and 17.7. The pattern also suggested a certain ‘specificity’ in the association between diagnoses of concern and definite outcomes, i.e., delayed psychomotor milestones predicted CP (LR+ = 42.6) and one record of epilepsy predicted epilepsy (LR+ = 59.6). Furthermore, the pattern also suggested a certain ‘unspecificity’, i.e., delayed psychomotor milestones also predicted epilepsy (LR+ = 13.7), suspicion of epilepsy also predicted CP (LR+ = 13.4).

During the study period the frequency of definite outcomes before 2 years remained stable (rate approx. 5 per 1000 or 0.005), whereas the frequency of at least one diagnosis of concern before 2 years nearly doubled from approximately 0.027 in 2004 to approximately 0.049 in 2015 (Figure 2). The rate of change was so fast that the quality indicator fell below the lower control limit before 2002 and exceeded the upper control limit after 2009 and stayed above thereafter (Figure 3). In contrast, the frequency of definite outcomes before the age of 10 years was about 0.0175 before 2010 and rose to about 0.045 in 2012 after data on SEN became available and stayed stable (Figure 2). Discussion

In this study we found that diagnoses of neurodevelopmental concern were very frequently used in children below age 2 years. The diagnoses of concern were found to have a high predictive value of definite neurodevelopmental outcomes given before age 10 years. Definite outcomes were rare in children below 2 years. The use of diagnoses of concern was increased in the three subpopulations at risk of perinatal brain injury to the same degree as the increased risk of definite outcomes before 10 years of age. At a rate of 2–3000 per year, the statistical uncertainty of the early warning indicator could be acceptable for a monitoring purpose at the national level. Unfortunately, the use of diagnoses of concern increased gradually over the years, preventing the use of an early warning indicator in clinical practice.

Statistical process control is a discipline used to monitor and improve production systems. In one perspective, mother and child health services (from pregnancy check‐ups over fetal medicine, delivery services and neonatal medicine) can be seen as a production system. Mortality rates have been used as a quality indicator for at least a century. When a quality indicator is stable with fluctuations only due to random variations, values outside the control limits may be attributed to a non‐random factor influencing the process. The goal of this study was to produce a quality control chart to monitor the processes of routine clinical perinatal medicine in Denmark with a potential influence on the risk of brain injury. Unfortunately, the indicator increased over the years. Given that the yearly numbers of definite outcomes appeared to be stable, however, the increasing trend of the diagnoses of concern is more likely to indicate a change in diagnostic practice. This may have multiple contributors. Worth mentioning is the use of diagnoses in the allocation of social funds, for children under 2 years most likely allocation of special care in day care institutions or communal compensation for loss of income when children are cared for at home. It has been described how diagnoses work as documentation requirements in social services and how a comparable situation is observed within the educational system [25]. Furthermore, some describe ‘medicalization’ as part of societal development [26]. Thus, it is not possible to employ these ‘diagnoses of concern’ to monitoring of potential perinatal brain damage using a simple control chart, since the basic assumption for this is a stable process. Nevertheless, the diagnoses of concern appeared to be reasonable predictive of definite neurodevelopmental conditions as well as reasonably strongly associated with rather common neonatal characteristics that are risk factors of perinatal brain damage.

This study is based on some very broad assumptions. First, we assumed that what we called ‘definite outcomes’ in part represents the results of brain damage that happened during the perinatal period or the following dysmaturation [27]. Certainly, all those outcomes have other—and likely more important—contributing factors. Second, we assumed that perinatal brain damage, although there are many kinds, does have common elements and thus also to some degree does have common effects in the motor, cognitive and behavioural domains. The rationale behind the primary analysis was that—everything else equal—those common elements could be quantified with more statistical power by lumping diagnoses.

This study is mainly based on the ICD‐10 codes (although the variable SEN contributed a large part of the definite outcomes). ICD‐10 codes are recorded for all contacts with the hospitals in Denmark. But the completeness of recording of relevant diagnoses is probably variable for contacts due to unrelated problems, such as acute illness. Also, diagnostic practices are likely to vary more before 2 years of age partly due to less well‐developed phenotypes, partly due to wider normal ranges of function. The increase over the years suggests a gradual change in practice.

There was a considerable variation between the LR+ among the diagnoses of concern. Children with the diagnosis ‘suspicion of psychiatric or behavioral disorder’ had low LR+ for epilepsy, MR and death, and ASD had low LR+ for CP and epilepsy, and ‘specific development disorders of motor skills’ had low LR+ for MR and death. This suggests that already at this early age, symptoms of abnormal behaviour may be less indicative of perinatal brain damage. In contrast, a diagnosis of ‘delayed psychomotor milestone’, ‘suspicion of epilepsy’ and ‘one record of epilepsy’ before the age of 2 years appeared to be more valid indicators for definite outcomes before 10 years and thus—likely—of perinatal brain damage.

An important consideration in this context is whether there is a causal relationship between perinatal brain damage and the diagnoses of concern before 2 years of age. At this age it was estimated early enough to act as an early warning, while still encompassing enough developmental milestones to identify atypical patterns. However, the neurodevelopmental problems that led the doctor to use these diagnoses have numerous other potential causes. A minority will later be proven to suffer from severe genetic disorders. In many more, the most likely cause is the complex interplay between genes, the developing brain, and postnatal environment. In a few, the likely cause is a severe postnatal event such as infection, trauma, or hypoxic insult. Assuming that these causal factors change slowly at the population level, the effect of implementation of a new perinatal care praxis could potentially be detectable. It could be a change in the timing of induced birth or in ways of screening, prevention, or treatment of neonatal hypoglycemia. This study attempts to construct an early warning indicator for predicting definite outcomes. Given this focus, establishing causality is of subordinate importance.

The three subpopulations were chosen for their sufficient size and their moderately increased risk of perinatal brain damage and adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. There is a risk of bias due to proactive diagnosis of neurodevelopmental consequences during routine follow‐up. The very similar increased OR for diagnoses of concern and definite outcomes, however, supported the construct validity of the early warning indicator and strengthen the arguments for the use of diagnoses of concern as indicators of ‘a burden of perinatal brain injury’ and risk of definite adverse outcomes.

A strength of this study is the use of nationwide registries, thus being truly population‐based. The annual number of births in Denmark is 55.000, thus the results may be relevant for health regions of such size. The registries are well‐organised, and diagnoses are based on a relatively homogeneous standard of care across hospitals. As a detail, the risk of misclassification bias of epilepsy diagnosis was minimised by requiring two or more records given on separate days. As another detail, the prevalence of CP exceeds that of the dedicated national register and follow‐up program: this is likely due to misdiagnosis of other potentially relevant neuro‐motor problems in childhood. We have previously documented that a CP diagnosis in the national patient register only correlates to a neuropediatrician‐verified CP diagnosis in 66% of cases [28]. This may be explained by the fact that doctors attending children may not be aware of the strict criteria for a CP diagnosis, which for neuropediatricians in Denmark follow the guidance of the Surveillance of Cerebral Palsy in Europe. Furthermore, a CP diagnosis in Denmark is accompanied by the possibility of free physiotherapy from the municipality, which may tempt the clinician to use the diagnosis in similar disorders or in children with self‐limiting symptoms of motor function. The study was first planned as a cohort with a minimum of 5 years of follow‐up, thereby limiting to children born until 2017. All calculations on OR and likelihood ratios of a positive test for definite outcomes were made in a subpopulation consisting of birth cohorts from 2004 to 2012 to allow for the 10 year age span. This meant a reduced sub‐cohort size and a corresponding decrease in statistical power.

Conclusion

The record of at least one diagnosis of neurodevelopmental concern before the age of 2 years was associated with a 12‐fold increased odds of an adverse neurodevelopmental outcome before the age of 10 years at the population level. In three subpopulations at risk of perinatal brain damage, the diagnoses of concern were increased 3 to 4‐fold, very similar to the increased risk of abnormal outcomes before 10 years. Unfortunately, the use of diagnoses of concern increased gradually over the years, and therefore it is not possible to use the early warning indicator and a simple statistical control chart to monitor late effects of perinatal care at the national level.
