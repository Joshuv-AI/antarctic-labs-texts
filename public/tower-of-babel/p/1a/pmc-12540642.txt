Influence of oral health on smoking cessation intention in current smokers with insights from a nationally representative survey
Authors: Hye-Lim Hong, Sun-Young Han, Chun-Bae Kim, Nam-Hee Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examined the influence of oral health factors, including oral health status, behaviors, and service utilization, on smoking cessation intentions among current smokers. We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using data from the Korean Community Health Survey (2010–2023), which included 551,982 current smokers. The primary outcome was smoking cessation intention, and key predictors included self-rated oral health, chewing difficulty, periodontal symptoms, oral health behaviors (brushing after lunch, flossing), and service utilization (dental checkups, dental scaling, unmet dental care needs). Multiple logistic regression models were employed, adjusting for sociodemographic and smoking-related factors. Among smokers, 66.3% intended to quit. Our findings suggest that several oral health-related factors are modestly but significantly associated with smoking cessation intention among current smokers. Specifically, individuals with better self-rated oral health were slightly more likely to report an intention to quit smoking (OR 1.09; 95% CI 1.01–1.19). Similarly, chewing difficulty was positively associated with cessation intention (OR 1.14; 95% CI 1.05–1.25), possibly reflecting heightened perceived health risks. Positive oral hygiene behaviors, such as brushing after lunch (OR 1.14; 95% CI 1.07–1.22) and flossing (OR 1.22; 95% CI 1.11–1.34), were also associated with increased quit intention, suggesting a broader health-conscious attitude. Additionally, unmet dental care needs were associated with greater likelihood of quit intention (OR 1.17; 95% CI 1.08–1.27), potentially indicating increased awareness of healthcare access and oral disease burden. These findings suggest that both adverse oral health experiences and positive oral health behaviors are associated with increased smoking cessation intention among current smokers, highlighting the relevance of oral health as a multidimensional factor in motivating quit attempts.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a significant risk factor for a variety of systemic diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, and cerebrovascular conditions1. As the primary entry point for harmful substances such as carbon monoxide, nicotine, and tar, the oral cavity is especially vulnerable to smoking-induced damage2. Such damage occurs via multiple mechanisms, including nicotine-induced vasoconstriction that harms periodontal tissues and tar accumulation that causes tooth discoloration3,4. Consequently, smokers face a 2.4-fold higher risk of tooth loss and a 4.5-fold higher risk of edentulism compared to nonsmokers5. Moreover, chronic nicotine exposure contributes to oral inflammation and may exacerbate nicotine dependence through neural mechanisms associated with addiction6. Chronic oral inflammation may affect neuroplasticity in the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, thereby reinforcing addictive behaviors through modulation of reward-related neural pathways. Supporting this mechanism, recent studies7–9 have shown that nicotine-induced inflammation can disrupt dopamine-mediated reward processing in the brain, ultimately strengthening the cycle of nicotine addiction. The relationship between smoking and oral health is complex3–6. Although smoking clearly damages oral health, poor oral health may also influence smoking behavior in a paradoxical manner. According to the Health Belief Model, oral pain or a diminished sense of taste can reduce the pleasurable aspects of smoking, thereby undermining motivation to continue the habit10. Additionally, direct experiences of dental pain11, halitosis12, and tooth discoloration13 can increase perceived vulnerability, reinforcing the desire to quit. These mechanisms highlight not only the physiological consequences of smoking but also the role of perceived health threats and benefits in motivating smokers to cease the behavior14.

In Korea, a range of smoking cessation interventions—from public health centers to mobile services—have been implemented15. Nevertheless, in 2022, the national adult smoking prevalence remained at 17.7% (30% for men and 5% for women), falling short of targeted reduction goals (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency [KCDC], 2023). Research consistently emphasizes that the intention to quit is a key predictor of successful cessation16. For example, among individuals who clearly expressed an intention to quit when enrolling in a smoking cessation clinic, the 3-month cessation success rate was 25.0%, 2.7 times higher than the rate among those without such an intention (9.3%)17. Furthermore, participants who completed a 12-week structured program were 7.16 times more likely to successfully quit18. These findings demonstrate that quitting intention is more than a psychological state; it is a crucial predictor of actual behavioral change16–19.

Recent studies suggest that awareness of oral health issues may play a pivotal role in motivating smokers to quit10–13,20. In fact, integrating oral health care with smoking cessation counseling has proven more effective than usual care in promoting tobacco cessation21. Additionally, socioeconomic factors significantly influence the relationship between oral health and smoking cessation22. In lower-income populations, limited access to dental care, concerns about potential complications, and the financial burden of treatments may strongly motivate cessation, thereby amplifying the impact of poor oral health on the intention to quit23.

The influence of oral health on smoking cessation intention can be understood through two conceptual pathways. The positive pathway involves cases where poor oral health—such as masticatory discomfort or periodontal symptoms—increases perceived susceptibility to harm, thereby motivating individuals to consider quitting smoking. This mechanism aligns with health behavior theories, particularly the Health Belief Model (HBM), which posits that perceived threat enhances motivation to engage in preventive behavior24. In such instances, oral symptoms serve as internal cues that trigger health-protective intentions, including smoking cessation25. In contrast, the negative pathway refers to situations in which oral health conditions or behaviors fail to elicit quit intention. Individuals may underestimate their personal risk (“it won’t happen to me”) or hold fatalistic beliefs (“it’s already too late to change”), both of which can diminish perceived susceptibility and self-efficacy26. These psychological barriers—even in the presence of poor oral health—may result in inaction, an outcome consistent with findings from behavioral science literature27.

Therefore, this study utilized community health survey data to comprehensively examine how various dimensions of oral health—such as periodontal disease, tooth loss, oral hygiene behaviors, and dental service utilization—affect the intention to quit smoking among current smokers.

Specifically, we tested two hypotheses: (1) Poor oral health conditions (e.g., chewing difficulty, periodontal symptoms) increase smoking cessation intention by heightening awareness of health risks and smoking-related discomfort. and (2) individuals with better self-rated oral health exhibit higher smoking cessation intention because they are more likely to engage in health-conscious behaviors. These hypotheses allow us to examine both negative and positive pathways through which oral health influences smoking cessation motivation.

Methods

Study design

This study employs a secondary cross-sectional analysis using raw data from the Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS), which was conducted by the KCDC between 2010 and 2023 through computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI). The KCHS is a standardized survey designed to generate comparable regional health statistics to support the development and evaluation of community health plans. The survey targets adults aged 19 years and older, with an average of approximately 900 participants surveyed per local health center. Since 2010, trained interviewers have conducted one-on-one interviews using electronic questionnaires on tablet PCs. The survey covers a wide range of health-related factors, including smoking behavior, morbidity, and healthcare utilization, as well as household characteristics such as income.

Study subjects

The target population consisted of current smokers, defined according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as individuals who have smoked at least five packs (100 cigarettes) in their lifetime and currently smoke either daily or occasionally. Of the 3,209,747 adults who participated in the KCHS between 2010 and 2023, 229,269 respondents from the 2020 survey were excluded because that year’s survey did not include questions on smoking cessation intention. Additionally, non-current smokers (n = 2,427,982) and respondents who provided invalid answers to the smoking cessation question (e.g., "Refuse to answer" or "Don’t know," n = 1114) were excluded. The final study sample comprised 551,982 current smokers (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Flow diagram of sample selection.

Variable definitions

Dependent variable: intention to quit smoking

Following the guidelines of the International Tobacco Control Four Country Survey (ITC-4)28, respondents were asked, "Do you intend to quit smoking?" Based on their responses, we classified them according to the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) of behavior change16. Participants who answered “within the next month” were categorized as being in the preparation stage; those responding “within the next 6 months” were categorized as being in the contemplation stage; and those replying “someday but not within 6 months” were considered in a precontemplation with awareness stage. These three groups were collectively classified as having an "intention to quit." Responses indicating no intention to quit were categorized as "no intention to quit."

Independent variables: oral health factors

Statistical analysis

The Korean Community Health Survey (KCHS) employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to ensure representativeness of the national population. To account for this complex sampling structure, all analyses were conducted using complex sample procedures that incorporated sampling weights, stratification variables, and clustering information34. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distributions of smoking cessation intention, oral health factors, and confounding variables. Weighted frequencies (N) and percentages were calculated using complex sample analysis to obtain population-representative estimates. For group comparisons, independent t-tests were applied to continuous variables (e.g., age, age at smoking initiation), while chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the impact of oral health on smoking cessation intention. The dependent variable was dichotomized, with "intention to quit" coded as 1 and "no intention to quit" coded as 0. Five distinct models were employed: Model 1 examined unadjusted odds ratios; Model 2 adjusted for oral health status; Model 3 adjusted for oral health behavior; Model 4 adjusted for oral health service utilization; and Model 5, the most comprehensive, adjusted for all oral health components and smoking-related characteristics. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1, R Core Team, 2023), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Among current smokers (N = 551,982), 66.2% expressed an intention to quit smoking within one year. Regarding oral health, 63.0% reported good self-rated oral health, 69.7% indicated no chewing discomfort, and 66.1% reported normal gum health. Oral health behaviors were suboptimal, with 49.5% not brushing teeth after lunch and 81.7% not using dental floss or interdental brushes. Over 60% had not undergone dental checkups or scaling in the past year, and 22.6% reported unmet dental care needs. Demographically, the sample was predominantly male (91.9%), with the largest age group being those in their 40 s (25.6%). A higher household income (40.9%) and university-level education (48.4%) were common, and manual labor was the most prevalent occupation (33.3%). Approximately one-third (33.1%) reported past quit attempts. Regarding health behaviors, 79.1% consumed alcohol, and only 5.6% engaged in regular walking. Hypertension (16.0%) and diabetes (7.6%) were the most frequently reported health conditions. Discussion

This study examined the influence of oral health factors—including oral health status, behaviors, and service utilization—on smoking cessation intentions among current smokers. Overall, the findings align with previous research35 on the prevalence of smoking cessation intentions (with 66.2% of smokers expressing an intention to quit) and offer additional insights into how various aspects of oral health contribute to this intention. These results highlight a potential association between oral health and the intention to quit smoking.

Oral health status and behaviors

The analysis revealed that both self-rated oral health and chewing discomfort were significantly associated with smoking cessation intention. Specifically, smokers with good self-rated oral health had 1.09 times higher odds of intending to quit (p = 0.033), while those experiencing chewing discomfort had 1.14 times higher odds (p = 0.003). Notably, self-rated oral health did not reach statistical significance in the model adjusting only for oral health status (Model 2), but became significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 5), suggesting that its association may be more evident when considered alongside oral health behaviors and service utilization. These findings suggest that a positive self-perception of oral health and discomfort stemming from poor oral conditions can both serve as motivational factors for quitting smoking10. In essence, satisfaction with one’s oral health may reinforce health-promoting behaviors, whereas discomfort can increase awareness of the need for change36.

However, no significant relationship was found between periodontal symptoms (e.g., gum bleeding, excessive tartar) and smoking cessation intention (p > 0.05), suggesting that these symptoms may not directly influence the motivation to quit. Although prior research has linked smoking cessation to improved periodontal outcomes37,38, the lack of association in our study could be attributed to limitations in the measurement of periodontal symptoms. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the assessment of periodontal symptoms. While previous studies have often relied on clinical diagnoses to evaluate the severity of periodontal disease, our study utilized self-reported periodontal symptoms collected through survey responses. As a result, discrepancies between perceived and actual periodontal health (e.g., clinical attachment level) may have influenced our findings. To enhance assessment accuracy and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between periodontal health and smoking cessation intention, future research should incorporate objective indicators such as probing depth and clinical attachment loss.

Additionally, oral health behaviors were strongly linked to smoking cessation intention. Smokers who brushed after lunch were 1.14 times more likely to intend to quit (p < 0.001), and those who used dental floss or interdental brushes had 1.22 times higher odds (p < 0.001). These results imply that structured education and counseling on oral health behaviors may enhance quit motivation, highlighting a potential avenue for integrating such components into comprehensive cessation programs39.

Utilization of oral health services

Dental checkups and scaling initially demonstrated a positive association with smoking cessation intention, potentially reflecting the influence of overall oral health status. Individuals who regularly utilize dental services may be more health-conscious, which could contribute to their motivation to quit smoking. However, the association between scaling and quit intention, which was significant in the model focusing solely on oral health service utilization (Model 4), was attenuated and became non-significant in the fully adjusted model (Model 5). In contrast, recognizing unmet dental care needs emerged as a strong motivating factor for smoking cessation, possibly due to increased awareness of the adverse effects of smoking on oral health. These findings highlight the potential role of oral health services in smoking cessation efforts. Integrating preventive oral health services, such as routine dental checkups and scaling, into smoking cessation programs may enhance quit motivation by providing targeted health education and continuous monitoring40.

Smoking-related characteristics

The analysis further revealed that smoking cessation intention decreased with age, particularly among older smokers with longer smoking histories and greater cumulative exposure. In contrast, higher income and education levels were associated with increased cessation intention. These findings suggest that smokers with higher socioeconomic status may be more receptive to cessation interventions, whereas older smokers might require more intensive or tailored support. Additionally, factors such as previous quit attempts41, depressive symptoms42, and chronic conditions (e.g., asthma, diabetes)43–45 were linked to higher cessation intention. These associations underscore the importance of addressing both psychological and physical health factors in smoking cessation strategies, as supported by previous studies46.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its large, nationally representative sample of over 500,000 current smokers, which enhances the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, categorizing oral health into status, behaviors, and service utilization provided a more detailed analysis of its role in smoking cessation intention.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences, making it unclear whether oral health influences smoking cessation intention or if individuals with stronger cessation motivation maintain better oral health. Second, self-reported oral health measures may be subject to recall and reporting biases, as perceived oral health does not always reflect actual conditions. Third, the dataset lacked validated dietary items related to oral health, such as sugar intake frequency, which limited the inclusion of diet as a behavioral factor. Lastly, unmeasured factors such as nicotine dependency and prior exposure to smoking cessation interventions may have influenced the results.

Given these limitations, longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to clarify causal mechanisms and strengthen the evidence on the bidirectional relationship between smoking cessation efforts and oral health behaviors.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that both unfavorable and favorable aspects of oral health are meaningfully associated with smoking cessation intention among current smokers. Indicators of poor oral health—such as chewing discomfort and unmet dental care needs—were associated with a higher likelihood of intending to quit, potentially reflecting increased awareness of oral health problems and their perceived consequences. Conversely, individuals who engaged in positive oral hygiene practices, including brushing after lunch and flossing, and those with more favorable self-rated oral health also exhibited greater cessation intention, suggesting an alignment with broader health-oriented attitudes.

These results underscore the relevance of oral health as a multidimensional determinant of motivation to quit smoking. Despite this, oral health remains largely unaddressed in conventional cessation interventions. Integrating oral health education, screening, and counseling into tobacco control programs may enhance cessation motivation and contribute to more holistic public health strategies. Future studies should examine the mechanisms linking oral health and smoking behavior and assess the effectiveness of integrated approaches in promoting cessation and reducing health disparities.
