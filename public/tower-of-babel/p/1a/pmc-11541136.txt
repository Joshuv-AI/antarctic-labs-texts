Application of Intraoperative Individualized Cup Combination—Cup‐on‐Cup Technique in Severe Acetabular Defects
Authors: Weihua Li, Daobi Liu, Xuqiang Liu, Xiaofeng Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the application of the cup‐on‐cup technique in revision total hip arthroplasty (THA) and report clinical and radiographic outcomes from a series of case follow‐ups. Retrospective analysis of 10 patients who underwent acetabular prosthesis revision with cup‐on‐cup technique. According to the Paprosky classification of acetabular bone defects, there were 2 cases of type II C, 3 cases of type III A, and 5 cases of type III B. The average follow‐up was 54.8 ± 5.1 months, and the Harris score of the hip joint increased from 37.0 ± 9.9 preoperatively to 80.5 ± 3.1 postoperatively at the final follow‐up (p < 0.001). Comparing the surgical side's hip center of rotation (COR) to the contralateral side, the preoperative average upward displacement was 33.8 ± 15.0 mm, while the postoperative average upward displacement was 0.2 ± 3.3 mm (p < 0.001). Similarly, the preoperative average inward displacement was 9.1 ± 5.1 mm, while the postoperative average outward displacement was 1.8 ± 1.6 mm (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference (p = 0.71) between the average density values of the contralateral and surgical sides at the final follow‐up, which were 127.4 ± 13.7 and 125.0 ± 14.8, respectively. During the follow‐up period, all patients achieved satisfactory radiographic outcomes, and no prosthetic loosening was observed. The cup‐on‐cup technique can reconstruct acetabular bone defects and restore hip COR in revision THA, with favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes.Keywords: acetabular bone defect, cup‐on‐cup technique, revision total hip arthroplasty

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA), an excellent treatment for end‐stage hip disorders, has been hailed as one of the most successful surgical procedures for the treatment of hip disorders. 1 With the widespread implementation of THA and the advent of an aging population, the number of primary THA has been increasing annually. 2 Despite significant advances in implant materials and surgical techniques compared to just a few decades ago, the number of revision THA is expected to increase by 137% between 2005 and 2030 in the United States. 3

Among the reasons for revision THA, aseptic loosening occupies the top position, followed by periprosthetic infection. 4 Stem loosening is less common than acetabular cup, which is frequently accompanied by severe bone loss. 5 , 6 In revision THA, the degree of acetabular bone loss is crucial in shaping the surgical reconstruction plan, particularly in cases of substantial segmental loss. The recognition of anatomical structures, removal of the original prosthesis, reconstruction of bone defects, and ensuring stability on the acetabular side of the prosthesis present substantial challenges for surgeons.

There were various methods for acetabular reconstruction, as reported in the literature, including the use of a Jumbo cup, structural autograft or allograft bone grafting, impaction grafting, an anti‐protrusion cage, a cup‐cage, tantalum metal wedges, and custom implants based on three‐dimensional imaging. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 In the past decade, experienced surgeons utilized porous metal acetabular components in the revision of complex acetabular bone defects, achieving acceptable postoperative joint function and a lower rate of postoperative complications. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Regrettably, the actual extent of bone loss during surgery may significantly differ from preoperative radiographic assessments.The irregularity of the lateral acetabular bone defect made it challenging for a regularly shaped trabecular metal cushion to match it perfectly. Larger acetabular cups necessitate increased soft tissue exposure, thereby heightening the risk of damage to the superior gluteal nerve and abductors. 15

For the aforementioned reasons, we used the cup‐on‐cup technique to manage intricate acetabular bone defects. This technique utilized two porous acetabular cups amalgamated with bone cement to form a “hybrid cup,” effectively addressing the acetabular bone defect. Three‐point fixation of the “hybrid cup” was accomplished through additional drilling of pubic and ischial acetabular screws, thereby enhancing initial stability. However, there is limited literature exploring this technique for treating severe acetabular bone defects, and the long‐term prosthesis survival rates remain unclear. Therefore, it is necessary to provide a detailed description of this technique and report clinical and radiographic outcomes from a series of case follow‐ups.

The purpose of this study was to: (i) report the surgical procedure of the cup‐on‐cup technique for managing acetabular bone defects in revision THA; (ii) explore the indications and key surgical steps for applying this technique in complex acetabular bone defects; (iii) assess the potential benefits of this technique in facilitating early postoperative weight‐bearing; (iv) evaluate the mid‐ to long‐term clinical and radiographic outcomes of the technique in a case series; and (v) assess its overall effectiveness and safety in revision THA. The findings are expected to provide new therapeutic approaches and insights for managing severe acetabular bone defects in clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

General Information

The case data of acetabular revision using the cup‐on‐cup technique in the joint center of our hospital from February 2018 to June 2019 were retrospectively analyzed. All surgeries were performed by a single senior surgeon.This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (2022CDYFYYLK11‐015). Informed consent was obtained from participants before data collection, and all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Patients were included based on the following criteria: (i) acetabular bone defects classified as Paprosky type II or higher; (ii) who underwent revision THA using the cup‐on‐cup technique; and (iii) had a minimum follow‐up period of 4 years. The exclusion criteria included: (i) a history of periprosthetic fractures; (ii) revision total hip arthroplasty due to hip infection or tumors; (iii) refusal to participate; (iv) death or loss to follow‐up; and (v) incomplete hip radiographic data.

A total of 10 patients were included, comprising five males and five females, with an average age of 64.0 ± 11.6 years. The reasons for acetabular revision in all cases were aseptic loosening, with one case involving concomitant hip dislocation and another case associated with periprosthetic femoral fracture. According to the Paprosky classification, 16 there were two cases of acetabular lateral bone defects classified as type II C, three cases as type IIIA, and five cases as type IIIB. All patients had no significant segmental defects in the anterior or posterior walls of the acetabulum. All patients received clinical and radiological follow‐up, with an average follow‐up time of 54.8 ± 5.1 months (Table 1).

Surgical Method (Acetabular Side)

Reevaluation of Acetabular Bone Defects

The surgeries of all patients were performed by the same surgeon with senior professional titles in this joint center, using a standard lateral decubitus position and a posterior‐lateral approach. During the surgery, we excised the joint capsule and scar tissue, and thoroughly scraped away granulation tissue in the bone resorption area until a pinpoint bleeding bone bed was achieved, which was to ensure the osteogenic vitality of the bone bed. However, caution should be exercised to avoid excessive force, as it may lead to acetabular fractures or defects in the anterior and posterior walls. At this time, the lateral acetabular bone defect needed to be reevaluated, including the extent of upward and inward displacement of hip COR (Figure 1A). Subsequently, two acetabular cups were used for a preliminary simulation of the final acetabular cup position. For bone defects at the top of the acetabulum, appropriate‐sized acetabular rasps should be used for grinding and preserving bone as much as possible to facilitate the attachment of a porous‐coated acetabular cup. Localized cavitary bone defects can be addressed with allograft bone grafting. Then, the first porous‐coated biological acetabular cup was implanted and fixed with multiple acetabular screws.

(A) During surgery, the joint capsule, scar tissue, and granulation tissue were excised, followed by reassessment of the acetabular bone defect and hip center of rotation displacement. (B) Using the transverse acetabular ligament, obturator foramen, or C‐arm fluoroscopy for localization, the true anatomical acetabulum position was determined. To ensure secure prosthesis fixation, additional screw holes were created in the ischial and pubic directions of the acetabular cup using a tungsten steel drill. (C) The overlapping area of the cups was filled with bone cement. The porous acetabular cup was then implanted into the true anatomical acetabulum, with adjustments to the anteversion and abduction angles to ensure secure press‐fit fixation.

Anatomical Localization and Prosthesis Preparation

With reference to the position of the transverse acetabular ligament and the obturator foramen, or using intraoperative C‐arm fluoroscopy for anatomical localization, the anatomical acetabulum position was determined. Additional reaming with an appropriately sized acetabular reamer allowed the remaining bone of the anterior and posterior acetabulum walls to firmly hold the cup in the anatomical true acetabular position. Subsequently, used a high‐strength tungsten steel drill to create screw holes in the acetabular cup for fixation in the pubic and ischial directions (Figure 1B). This step required attention to three key points: Firstly, the ideal direction for drilling was oriented toward the denser bone areas of the pubis and ischium (typically located in the anterior and inferior aspects of the pubis and ischium). Mark the location before drilling to ensure excellent pull‐out force for the inserted acetabular screws. Secondly, it was crucial to avoid drilling too close to the edge of the acetabulum to prevent damage to the locking mechanism, leading to failure of the high cross‐linked polyethylene liner lock or ceramic liner rupture. Thirdly, repeatedly inspected the burial of the acetabular screw cap. Prominent screw caps can also cause difficulties in securing the polyethylene liner lock or result in ceramic liner fractures.

Implantation and Fixation of Acetabular Cup

Then, implant the second porous biological fixation acetabular cup into the true anatomical acetabular position. Adjust the anteversion and abduction angles and gently tap to achieve a secure press‐fit fixation (Figure 1C). After fixing the acetabular screws sequentially in the pubic and ischial directions, the overlapping portion of the two acetabular cups was filled with bone cement. For the non‐overlapping part in contact with the host bone, if there were small gapped bone defects, autogenous bone grafting was performed before placing the acetabular cup in the true acetabulum. This step also required attention to three points: First, avoid bone cement entering the inner surface of the acetabular cup, which may impact the locking mechanism of the liner. Second, throughout the solidification process of the bone cement, ensure that the anteversion and abduction angles of the acetabular cup in the true acetabulum remain within the appropriate range. Third, pay attention to protecting the posterior sciatic nerve to prevent thermal injury caused by the bone cement.

Perioperative Management

All patients were placed with incision drainage tubes, and antibiotic prophylaxis was administered for infection prevention within the first 24 h after surgery. Anticoagulant therapy begins on the first day after surgery, with subcutaneous injections of low‐molecular‐weight heparin during hospitalization and oral rivaroxaban upon discharge. The drainage tubes were removed within one day postoperatively, and anticoagulant therapy continued until 5 weeks postoperatively.

During the first 1–2 weeks postoperatively, patients performed quadriceps and knee flexion‐extension exercises while bedridden. After 3–4 weeks, patients began toe‐touch weight‐bearing exercises with the aid of a walker, except those with severe bone defects, who delayed this until 6 weeks postoperatively. Following this, patients gradually increased their weight‐bearing as tolerated, usually reaching full weight‐bearing within 2–3 months after surgery.

Efficacy Evaluation Indicators

Collect the patient's surgical information, including the duration of surgery, type and size of prosthesis used during the procedure. Postoperative outpatient follow‐ups were scheduled at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months, followed by annual visits thereafter. Follow‐up assessments included hip joint Harris scores, incision status, gait analysis, and hip joint x‐ray imaging. The radiographic evaluation included measuring the vertical and horizontal displacement of the hip joint COR relative to the contralateral side both preoperatively and postoperatively, and observing whether there were radiolucent lines, loosening, and signs of infection.

We utilized the method described by Daniel Hernandez‐Vaquero et al. to calculate the optical density values on both the surgical and contralateral sides of standard hip joint x‐rays, which was reliable and effective for quantifying bone mineral density (BMD). 17 , 18 The hip x‐rays of the patients during their final follow‐up were examined using ImageJ software. First, the images were processed with histogram equalization, using metal and air optical density values as cutoff values, ranging from 0 to 255. Next, regions of interest (ROIs) in the acetabulum (top, supra, and infra) were selected on both the contralateral and surgical sides, and the average optical density values were calculated for further analysis.

Statistical Analyses

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences 26 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA) and ImageJ software (version 1.48). General demographic characteristics were presented as Mean ± SD. Radiometric measurements were independently performed by three observers. A paired t‐test was used to assess the significance of differences between quantitative variables, with a p‐value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Result

Demographic Data

From February 2018 to June 2019, our center performed 10 cases of revision THA using the aforementioned method. All surgeries were performed by the same senior‐level physician at our center, the average surgical time was 153.5 ± 23.3 min. Ten patients underwent revision surgery due to aseptic loosening of the prosthetic components, with no clinical evidence of periprosthetic infection. In two cases, preoperative assessment and intraoperative confirmation revealed firm fixation of the femoral component, and no revision was performed on the femoral side. Clinical and Radiographic Outcomes

The Harris score improved from an average of 37.0 ± 9.9 preoperatively to 80.5 ± 3.1 at the final follow‐up (p < 0.001). Preoperatively, the average upward displacement of the hip COR compared to the contralateral side was 33.8 ± 15.0 mm, and postoperatively, it averaged 0.2 ± 3.3 mm (p < 0.001). The preoperative average inward displacement of the hip COR was 9.1 ± 5.1 mm, and postoperatively, it averaged an outward displacement of 1.8 ± 1.6 mm (p < 0.001). The postoperative average abduction angle at the true acetabulum was 43.7° ± 3.5°, and the anteversion angle was 21.5° ± 2.3°. There was no statistically significant difference (p = 0.71) between the values (density average of the three ROIs) of the contralateral side and the surgical side, which were 127.4 ± 13.7 and 125.0 ± 14.8, respectively (Table 3).

Post‐Revision Complications

Acetabular component osseointegration adopts the Morre criteria 19 : (i) no radiolucent line; (ii) superolateral buttress; (iii) medial stress‐shielding; (iv) radial trabeculae; (v) inferomedial buttress. At the final follow‐up, all patients achieved osseointegration of the acetabular prostheses with no radiographic signs of loosening (Figure 2A–D). In one case, a radiolucent line was observed postoperatively in Zone 1 of the acetabular cup. 20 The patient resumed normal weight‐bearing at 6 weeks postoperatively, and during the follow‐up, the radiolucent line showed no progression. By 6 months postoperatively, the radiolucent line had disappeared, and radiating trabeculae formation was visible (Figure 3A–D).

One patient experienced a Vancouver C‐type fracture around the femoral prosthesis during intraoperative reduction. The long‐stemmed femoral prosthesis was replaced with steel plate fixation, and bone healing was confirmed by imaging at 4 months postoperatively. At the last follow‐up, all patients showed no signs of periprosthetic infection or loosening.

Discussion

Generally, performing revision THA for severe acetabular bone defects (Paprosky II C–III B) in THA involves the following challenges: (i) accurately assessing the extent of bone loss preoperatively to formulate a comprehensive and adequate surgical plan; (ii) selecting bone defect grafting materials intraoperatively and integrating them with the acetabular cup, maximizing the preservation of bone mass while effectively filling bone defects; (iii) ensuring the initial stability of the acetabular cup in situations where achieving optimal press‐fit fixation is challenging. In this study, the cup‐on‐cup technique was used to manage complex acetabular bone defects in revision THA. This method effectively filled bone defects and restored the hip joint's center of rotation (COR). Additionally, the use of pubic and ischial screws enhanced the initial stability of the revision acetabular cup and achieved favorable clinical and radiographic results.

Assessment of Acetabular Bone Defects

Currently, preoperative assessment of acetabular bone defects is still based on radiological data. Walde TA et al. reported that computed tomography (CT) had a sensitivity of 74.7% for assessing bone defects, and x‐rays had a sensitivity of only 51.7%. 21 However, it is important to note that preoperative imaging assessments of bone defects are static. Due to the presence of metallic artifacts from previous acetabular prostheses, plain radiographs and even CT scans were insufficient to fully comprehend the true extent of periacetabular bone dissolution. Therefore, deviations from reality were inevitable regardless of the imaging method used.

According to the study by Gozzard et al., the accuracy of the preoperative assessment compared to the actual intraoperative extent for the Paprosky classification of acetabular bone defects was as follows: Paprosky Type I: 100%; Type II: 73%; and Type III: 50%. 22 Intraoperative procedures such as clearing periacetabular soft tissues, extracting the original acetabular prosthesis, and scraping away bone dissolution lead to an enlargement of the bone defect area. This significantly underestimates the actual extent of acetabular bone defects that need reconstruction during surgery. Additionally, partial bone ingrowth of the acetabular prosthesis during its extraction inevitably results in extra bone loss, potentially causing acetabular fractures. Therefore, it is evident that as the severity of acetabular bone defects increases, the accuracy of preoperative assessments shows a decreasing trend. Moreover, 3D‐printed metal wedges and acetabular cups may be difficult to completely match the bone defect area during surgery, which somewhat limits their effectiveness in filling and reconstructing the defect area.

Selection of Surgical Strategies for Acetabular Bone Defects

For mild acetabular defects, options such as allograft bone transplantation, small structural bone grafts, or porous metal augments can effectively restore hip joint function and anatomical offset. 23 , 24 However, options for acetabular reconstruction were still restricted in cases of severe bone loss (PaproskyIII A and III B). With the application of computer‐assisted and 3D printing technologies in orthopedics, individually customized triflange cups also seem to be a promising direction. Nevertheless, the substantial size of triflange cups requires larger incisions and more extensive soft tissue exposure during surgery, potentially posing catastrophic risks. Sershon RA et al. recently reported in their study that, following revision THA using a triflange cup, 46% of patients developed Trendelenburg gait, and 12% experienced hip dislocation. 15

In our cup‐on‐cup technique, two acetabular cups placed above and within the true acetabulum can be freely combined, forming a “hybrid cup” together with bone cement. In contrast to the 3D printing integrated “hybrid cup,” the positions of the two acetabular cups can be individually combined and integrated through bone cement, enabling diverse combinations to suit various types of acetabular bone defects. This technique is characterized by its flexibility, enabling adaptive decision‐making based on intraoperative conditions and ensuring satisfactory filling during the procedure. In comparison to customized triflange cups, this technique minimizes the need for excessive exposure and dislocation, effectively avoiding damage to the gluteus medius and reducing the risk of sciatic nerve injury. In several studies on the cup‐on‐cup technique, the overall survival rates after revision THA ranged from 80% to 89%, which were similar to the results of our study. 25 , 26 Previous studies had a follow‐up period of only about 2 years. In contrast, our mid‐ to long‐term follow‐up conclusions were more persuasive. Moreover, the technique also obtained satisfactory outcomes in filling and reconstructing bone defects, especially in restoring the COR of the hip joint. 27 , 28 Du et al. in using this technique for revision THA found that the average postoperative correction of the hip COR was 29.2 mm downward shift and 5.3 mm outward shift. 27 Similar to their findings, in our case series, the average postoperative COR shifted downward by 33.6 mm and outward by 10.9 mm.

However, for patients with pelvic discontinuity, the cup‐cage technique and custom‐made triflange acetabular component remained the primary method of reconstruction. 29 , 30 The triflange cage typically required sufficient exposure of the ilium and ischium to securely fix the cage to the host bone with screws, which may increase the risk of vascular and neural injury. 31 Additionally, in a study on complex acetabular side revision THA with pelvic discontinuity, intraoperative fixation with superior pubic ramus and ischial screws provided acetabular component stabilization compared to patients without such fixation, significantly increasing prosthesis survival rates. 32 In our study, for patients with severe acetabular defects, we used a porous acetabular cup in the true acetabulum and secured it with screws in the superior pubic ramus and ischium to increase the initial stability of the prosthesis. During follow‐up, we did not observe any vascular or neural injuries in patients. Unfortunately, our case series did not include patients with pelvic discontinuity, and there is no literature reporting on the efficacy of the cup‐on‐cup technique in this regard. It was unclear whether the cup‐on‐cup technique offered advantages in this area, and large‐scale clinical studies were needed to determine its efficacy.

Radiographic Evaluation of Bone Ingrowth Post‐Surgery

Typically, the thin fibrous layer at the bone‐implant interface of cementless prostheses may consist of newly formed bone tissue and fibrous tissue, appearing as a radiolucent zone less than 2 mm. When the femoral head center shifts laterally from the anatomical acetabulum, tension may develop at the bone‐implant interface, which could also contribute to the formation of the radiolucent zone. 33 In the typical case discussed in this study, the patient developed a radiolucent line on the acetabular side postoperatively, but it had disappeared by the 6‐month follow‐up. During the surgery for this patient, the acetabular side had severe defects that the lateral acetabular cup could not fully fill. We used a reamer to harvest cancellous bone from the anatomical true acetabulum to fill these irregular defects, and subsequently implanted the porousacetabular cup. Moreover, according to Bingham JS et al., radiolucent lines were present in 8% of patients with tritanium cups who received adjuvant screw fixation, compared to 45% in those without screw fixation. 34 HA‐coated porous titanium cups may enhance bone ingrowth more effectively than high‐porosity titanium cups. 35 , 36 Therefore, the early postoperative functional rehabilitation, secure fixation of the prosthesis with screws, and bone ingrowth facilitated by the prosthesis coating may explain the disappearance of radiolucent lines during follow‐up.

BMD is a reliable indicator for predicting prosthesis failure, and patients with osteoporosis and reduced bone mass are more prone to prosthesis‐related complications. However, due to equipment limitations, it was difficult to perform dual‐energy x‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scans on every patient during follow‐up. Therefore, we adopted a simple and practical method to evaluate bone reconstruction by calculating the mean optical density of ROIs in the acetabulum on both the surgical and contralateral sides. The results showed no significant difference in density values between the surgical and contralateral sides at the final follow‐up, suggesting that the prosthesis had achieved stable bone ingrowth with the host bone locally.

Early Weight‐Bearing Depends on Prosthesis Stability

Whether early weight‐bearing and rapid rehabilitation exercises could be performed after THA revision mainly depended on the stability of prosthesis fixation. In a study using customized cages to treat Paprosky type III bone defects, patients were allowed partial weight‐bearing with crutches within 6–12 weeks postoperatively. 37 Similarly, a study that used bone grafting to treat acetabular aseptic loosening only permitted patients to engage in partial weight‐bearing with crutches for 12 weeks postoperatively. 38 Hongxing Li et al.'s study allowed patients treated with allograft bone grafting for acetabular defects to only toe‐touch weight‐bearing for 6–8 weeks postoperatively. 39 In contrast, in our study, we allowed most patients to begin ambulation with a walker at 3–4 weeks postoperatively, and follow‐up showed that within 2–3 months postoperatively, most patients were able to achieve unsupported full weight‐bearing. In the presence of solid fixation, the cup‐on‐cup technique for revision THA may offer greater advantages in early weight‐bearing compared with other surgical approaches.

Advantages of Three‐Point Fixation

In primary THA, whether using acetabular screws or not, the initial stability of the acetabular cup is achieved through the press‐fit technique. When employing the cup‐on‐cup technique to address severe acetabular bone defects, obtaining press‐fit fixation for the acetabular cup in the anatomical true acetabular position is difficult. However, whether it is primary or revision TH, the stability of the acetabular cup depends on the three‐point fixation of the ilium above, pubic bone in front, and ischium in the back. Three‐point fixation allows for effective dispersion of stresses in all directions of hip joint motion, which has obvious advantages in improving the initial stability of prosthesis and joint function. 40 In previous cases of revision THA using the cup‐on‐cup technique, the acetabular cup at the anatomically true acetabulum was only fixed to the cup above with bone cement. 27 , 40 , 41 In the cases presented in this study, the oval‐shaped acetabular defect had a continuous pelvic structure. Therefore, three‐point fixation can still enhance the initial stability of the acetabular cup. In cases of severe acetabular bone defects (Paprosky II C‐III B), aseptic loosening typically resulted in the acetabular cup moving posteriorly and medially. Extensive bone dissolution in the pubic and ischial regions made three‐point fixation difficult to achieve. Therefore, we used high‐strength tungsten steel drills for additional acetabular cup drilling and placed acetabular screws in the healthy bone anteriorly and inferiorly of the pubic bone and sciatic bone to achieve firm fixation. In this study, there were no radiographic signs of acetabular cup loosening in all patients during the follow‐up period, the initial and long‐term stability of the acetabular cup was satisfactory.

Strengths and Limitations

Thus, we believe that the cup‐on‐cup technique is a novel and promising surgical approach to manage acetabular bone defects in revision THA. The personalized combination of two acetabular cups with bone cement can fill various irregular acetabular bone defects and effectively restore the hip joint's COR, which is more efficient and less costly. Moreover, the additional fixation of the porous acetabular cup with pubic and ischial screws significantly improves the prosthesis's initial stability.

However, there were inevitably some limitations in this study. Firstly, this was a retrospective cohort study rather than an ideal prospective randomized controlled trial. Secondly, all patients in this study were from the same hospital, and the sample size was relatively small. Thirdly, the follow‐up duration was short, with an average follow‐up time of only 54.8 months, which may necessitate a longer follow‐up period to observe the long‐term outcomes of this technique. Fourthly, despite employing alternative methods, limitations in diagnostic equipment prevented us from conducting hip DEXA to monitor changes in BMD during follow‐up. Lastly, for severe acetabular bone defects involving segmental deficiencies of the anterior and posterior walls, especially in patients with pelvic discontinuity, further research was needed to explore its efficacy.

Conclusion

For severe acetabular bone defects, intraoperatively combining two acetabular cups in any position to form a “hybrid cup” can effectively fill bone defects, restore the hip joint's COR, achieve three‐point fixation of the acetabular cup, and enhance the initial stability of the acetabular cup.
