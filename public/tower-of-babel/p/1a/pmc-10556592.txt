Growth mechanisms and anisotropic softness–dependent conductivity of orientation-controllable metal–organic framework nanofilms
Authors: Ming-Shui Yao, Ken-ichi Otake, Tomoyuki Koganezawa, Moe Ogasawara, Hitoshi Asakawa, Masahiko Tsujimoto, Zi-Qian Xue, Yan-Hong Li, Nathan C. Flanders, Ping Wang, Yi-Fan Gu, Tetsuo Honma, Shogo Kawaguchi, Yoshiki Kubota, Susumu Kitagawa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis work presents a significant contribution to the field of interface sciences by addressing the challenge of preparing and characterizing high-quality conductive metal–organic framework thin films. The study explores the growth mechanisms of electrically conductive Cu-HHTP (Cu=Cu2+ ion, HHTP = 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexahydrotriphenylene) nanofilms with reversal orientations. It uses operando synchrotron technology to reveal a unique conductivity modulation arising from the anisotropic softness of the surface-mounted skeleton. The study also addresses the existence of crystallinity at the early growth stage and whether the generally considered rigid Cu-HHTP can be soft. The findings offer a pathway for designing conductive crystalline porous materials based on lattice expansion and molecule packing behaviors and general methods for characterizing thin films.

In recent years, electrically conductive metal–organic frameworks (cMOFs) gradually emerged by unlocking their potential to conduct charges in porous crystals (1–3), breaking the inherent cognition of their insulating nature, which is almost unknown just 13 y ago (4, 5). During the following decade, extensive research has been conducted worldwide to explore the controlled preparation of high-quality cMOF thin films to realize the high performances required by modern electrical devices. However, it has been beyond our reach because of the unexplored interface chemistry, the study of chemical phenomena that occur at the interface of two or multiphases, including dynamic growth mechanisms, transport properties, and advanced characterizations (6–10). Specifically, existing challenges are as follows: 1) synthesis of the crystalline thin film down to one or few layers (1, 11, 12); 2) controlled orientation of the molecules that build up the thin films (12–18); and 3) advanced characterizations of such downsized surface-mounted cMOFs (19–21). Among them, controlled orientation turns out to be the decisive factor for the interface chemistry of cMOF thin films. Notably, the “edge-on” orientation control in Cu-HHTP films has been successfully achieved rather than commonly observed “face-on” orientation very recently (17). However, their work mainly focuses on the nonlinear properties, while challenges in understanding growth mechanisms of such reversal orientation control and obtaining edge-on film with thickness less than 100 nm and smooth surface remain.

To address such challenges, the essential scientific issues are controlling the molecules during the coordination reactions and revealing the growth mechanism and structural property evolution employing molecule-level (space resolution) and real-time (time resolution) nondestructive characterizations. Here, we report the successful synthesis of orientation-controllable cMOF nanofilms by manipulating organic molecules “stand-up” behaviors (Fig. 1). This implementation enables us to carry out research on revealing the possible growth mechanism and the relationships between surface-mounted network softness and charge transport behaviors (conductivity) in an anisotropic way (12, 22–24). Accordingly, the possible defect-repair model, crystallinity of cMOF nanofilms with thickness down to a few nanometers, and unique anisotropic softness–dependent conductivity property are observed by the molecule-level resolution frequency modulation atomic force microscopic (FM-AFM) imaging and operando [the Latin gerund, which means working or operating (25)] synchrotron technology (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). It also reveals surface-mounted anisotropic structural softness’s unexpected yet dominant role in the electrical conductivity.

Results and Discussion

Syntheses and Structures of cMOF Nanofilms with Face-On and Edge-On Orientations.

The synthesis of an orientation-controlled thin film of Cu-HHTP has been reported by others recently (17). In their work, the edge-on thin film was obtained by changing the types of solvents (deionized water) and reaction temperature (70 °C) through a layer-by-layer (LbL) liquid-phase epitaxial (LPE) dipping method. Differently, the reversal orientations, and crystalline domains of 2D π-conjugated [Cu3(HHTP)2] (Cu-HHTP; HHTP = 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexahydrotriphenylene) (16, 26), can be controlled by manipulating the molecule-level stand-up behaviors via a facile concentration controlled LbL LPE spraying method on various surfaces (27). Using the LbL spraying setup, the fast coordination reaction can be effectively disrupted and only occurred upon exposure to sprayed building units. The resulting thin films have controlled thickness increment down to 1 to 2 nm per cycle with smooth surface, which is well suited for in situ observation and characterization to reveal the unproven growth mechanisms.

As a typical example, the LbL growth of Cu-HHTP on -OH functionalized substrates was achieved by alternatively spraying the ethanolic solution of copper (II) acetate and organic ligands. Between each step, pure ethanol was used to rinse the substrate to remove unreacted reactants. As indicated in Fig. 1, the formation of the honeycomb-like porous structure is derived from the extended hexagonal 2D π-conjugation layers constructed by the [CuO4] planar units along the ab-plane (represented by the coordination of golden Cu2+ spheres with pink HHTP ligand triangles) and the AB slipped-parallel π-π stacking of 2D layers along the c-axis (the hexagonal P6mmm space group with the cell parameters of a = b =21.75 Å and c = 6.66 Å) (28).

Different from the expected edge-on of the 2D planes due to the hydrophilic -OH edge and hydrophobic triphenylene core, the experimentally observed c-orientation was in fact face-on of the 2D planes on the substrates (Figs. 1 and 2A (red curves) and Fig. 2B). With regard to Cu1.0 P0.1-20C and Cu0.1 P0.1-40C, the notation of CuαPβ-γC stands for the film prepared by the LbL-LPE spraying method using α mM Cu salt solution and β mM HHTP solution with γ cycles. To fabricate the edge-on orientation plane, an ultrahigh concentration of the ligand solution was used. This method was inspired by the “standing-up” behaviors of molecules with hydrophilic groups on the hydrophilic surface in the Langmuir−Blodgett technique (11, 12, 29). In practice, we used a 5 to 20 times higher concentration of HHTP solution (0.5 to 2.0 mM) compared with the fixed low concentration of Cu salt solution (0.1 mM) to continuously provide ultrahigh molecule density (~43.3 to 173.1 molecules nm−2 s−1, for calculation details, see SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The edge-on orientation, although not very clear, can be observed by using the solution with 0.5 mM of HHTP concentration (Cu0.1 P0.5-10C, SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). The further increment of the HHTP concentration successfully maintains the vertically standing-up status of ligands during the coordination with Cu sites and π-π stacking between neighboring molecules (Fig. 1B). Accordingly, we obtained the clear edge-on thin film with the c-axis parallel to the substrate (Cu0.1 P2.0-20C and Cu0.1 P1.0-20C, Fig. 2A (blue curves) and Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4). Such reversal orientation will lead to reversal peak intensity of either (h00)/(0k0) or (00l) for the hexagonal crystal structure of Cu-HHTP. Considering the good crystallinity of (200), its reversal intensity distribution well-confirm the reversal orientation of face-on and edge-on, including the azimuthal angle φ ranging from 0° (in plane) to 90° (out of plane) shown in grazing incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) images (30–32), the in-plane/out-of-plane GIWAXS profiles, as well as φ-dependent orientation distribution function (ODF) of the 200 facet (Fig. 2D).

GIWAXS of cMOF thin films with face-on and edge-on orientations (λ = 1.00 Å). (A) The in-plane/out-of-plane GIWAXS profiles, GIWAXS images obtained at grazing angle of (B) 0.15° for face-on and (C) 0.12° for edge-on thin film, and (D) φ-dependent ODF of the 200 facet.

The transmission electron microscopy images clearly show the crystalline domains of the thin film with distinct spacings of (100)/(010) with a visible channel size of ~1.8 nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The fluorescence X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS), a powerful and reliable technique that can detect atomic-level local structures and identify valence states of thin or dilute samples (33–35), clearly visualized the stronger signals of the edge-on Cu0.1P2.0-20C at k = 3~7 Å than those of the face-on Cu1.0P0.1-10C from the wavelet transform for the k2-weighted EXAFS signals (SI Appendix, Figs. S5–S8) (36). These results further justify the crystallinity and orientation difference of these cMOF nanofilms.

Cycle-Dependent Properties of cMOF Thin Films.

AFM results reveal that all cMOF nanofilms have small roughness (<10 nm) and controllable thickness of less than 100 nm (SI Appendix, Figs. S9–S17). For face-on nanofilm, by simply reducing the concentration of Cu salts from 1.0 mM (Cu1.0 P0.1) to 0.1 mM (Cu0.1 P0.1), the reduced growth rate from ~4 nm to ~2 nm per cycle was observed, which corresponds to ~6 layers of 2D planes. Together with the better orientation of Cu0.1 P0.1 nanofilm than Cu1.0 P0.1 (φ-dependent ODF, Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4), we can observe the good crystallinity along the ab-plane verified by the narrowed 200 peaks (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Figs. S18 and S19). The HHTP densities calculated from the thickness increment of Cu1.0 P0.1 and Cu0.1 P0.1 are 6.2 and 3.1 nm−2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2), respectively, which are comparable with the feeding rate (~5.6 molecules nm−2 s−1).

cMOF thin films with face-on and edge-on orientations. (A) The FWHM value of 200 and 002 PXRD peaks obtained by GIWAXS (insets are the schema of the crystalline domain size) and (B) the electrical conductivity of various cMOF thin films (Insets are the schema and the digital photo of the electrodes sputtered on the thin film).

In contrast, in the case of edge-on mode, the increase of HHTP concentration from 1.0 mM (Cu0.1 P1.0) to 2.0 mM (Cu0.1 P2.0) resulted in a decreased growth rate from ~5 nm to ~3 nm per cycle. By taking into consideration the roughness value, the average thickness increment might be the result of the coexistence of the completed hexagonal unit (~3.6 nm) and one ligand missing hexagonal unit (~2.7 nm, SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The compact stand-up packing of HHTP ligands significantly limited the growth along the ab-plane, but allowed for the free growth along the c-axis, thus resulting in the formation of the a/b oriented nanofilm. The calculated HHTP density of edge-on nanofilm is between 4.3 to 8.5 nm−2; which is more than one order of magnitude lower than the feeding rate (~86.5 to 173.1 molecules nm−2 s−1). The combination of a/b orientation and strong peaks of lattice along the a/b-axis also permit the clear XRD observation of crystalline Cu0.1 P2.0-5C nanofilm with a thickness of 13.4 ± 2.3 nm (SI Appendix, Figs. S16K and S20).

By sputtering parallel counter Os electrodes on the cMOF nanofilms, the electronic conductivity parallel to the substrate can be deduced from the linear I-V curves (SI Appendix, Figs. S21–S24). All samples show enhanced conductivity with increasing growth cycles (Fig. 3B). It can be attributed to better crystallinity after repeated LbL growth, which is consistent with fewer defects evidenced by the narrowed PXRD peak of 200 facet in Fig. 3A, and better connections observed from high-resolution AFM images in SI Appendix, Fig. S17. By repeating growing cycles, all films finally reach the maximum value (σmax) at ~1.0 S cm−1 and ~0.2 S cm−1 for face-on (charge carriers mainly transport along the ab-plane, in-plane) and edge-on films (charge carriers mainly transport along the c-axis, π-π stacking direction, out of plane), respectively. The in-plane σmax compares favorably with the highest conductivity reported for single crystalline microcrystal of Cu-HHTP (26) and Cu-HHTT (37). The five times higher value for face-on film compared with edge-on film indicates higher transport efficiency along the ab-plane of Cu-HHTP than that along the c-axis.

Surface-Mounted Nucleation and Nanoparticle Intergrowth: The Defect-Repair Model.

Based on the cycle-dependent crystallinity and conductivity, the possible growth mechanism concerning the defect-repair model was indicated when we assume that the individual particle is constructed by two parts (SI Appendix, Fig. S25), one of which is the highly crystalline part (defect-less) and the other of which is the amorphous/weakly crystalline part (defect-rich), similar to particles of organic polymers (38). The interruption of ultrafast coordination of Cu with HHTP using LbL feeding effectively avoids the formation of the dominant amorphous phase. First, the confined nucleation and subsequent formation of nanoparticles occur due to the surface-mounted effects. When their grain boundaries contacted each other, the intergrowth of nanoparticles also induced the formation of lattice dislocation between grains. With increasing LbL cycles, the defects of the bottom part of the particles will be fixed by the repeated feeding of building units, thus increasing crystallinity and conductivity.

Direct Imaging of the Lattice of cMOF Thin Films with Few Nanometers.

Although the possible defect-repair model was proposed for films with a relatively great thickness (>10 nm), whether it can be applied to the most original state of the thin film remains unknown. Benefiting from the rapid development of FM-AFM using an aqueous interface, the molecule-level resolution of flat samples was obtained under solution conditions (Fig. 4A) (39, 40). It is difficult to observe the lattice of the top surface of face-on films (the highly possible defect-repair model), while the defected honeycomb-like lattice consistent with the cMOF structure was observed at the bottom of the film after peeling off the upper-part grains by the tips (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S25A). As for edge-on thin films, ordered nanograins with lateral sizes of 10 to 30 nm were observed (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Figs. S25B–S28). The good crystallinity and robust ac plane enable the direct imaging of the 1D channel (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S29), despite the relatively large height drop of 4 nm. Both results confirm the coexistence of crystalline and defects even for the ultrathin film at the nucleation stage and early-stage particle growth.

Synthesis and Structure of MOF-on-MOF (MoM) Thin Films.

Motivated by the curiosity about the orientation control on complicated surfaces, we further investigated the growth of the cMOF layer on the porous and defected surfaces of the bottom MOF layer, that is, MoM thin film. Similar to pristine functional groups, the bottom MOF showed little effect on the original orientation of cMOFs but significantly affected the quality of the secondary cMOF thin films (SI Appendix, Figs. S30–S32). As shown in the illustration of SI Appendix, Fig. S30, typical images of upper cMOF film at 0.10° (the grazing angle) and dual-MOF films at 0.15° of face-on Cu-HHTP-40C-on-Cu-BTC (for details, see SI Appendix, Fig. S33) were observed, supporting the bilayer structure of the MoM films. Similarly, typical images of dual-MOF films at 0.15° of edge-on Cu-HHTP-20C-on-Cu-BTC were also observed (SI Appendix, Figs. S34 and S35). By further growing a third Cu-BTC layer on MoM films, the reverse order of iMOF and cMOF patterns can be seen (SI Appendix, Figs. S30 and S36). These observations indicate that iMOF-on-cMOF and multilayered MoM films can be prepared to produce complicated heterostructures and realize potential cascading functions.

Anisotropic Softness–Conductivity of Oriented cMOF Thin Films by Operando GIWAXS-Sensor Investigation.

With additional asymmetry and anisotropy, the downsizing of surface-mounted MOFs thin films has indicated the unique anisotropic framework softness by controlling the orientations (41–44). The integration of cMOFs with the responsively anisotropic softness enables both mass and charge transport along the interlayer or intralayer pathway (12, 23, 37), which provides the opportunity to give insights into the unexplored anisotropic softness–conductivity relationships of porous frameworks.

We envision that the nanoscale thickness and crystalline domain of the cMOF and cMOF-on-iMOF thin films enable fast mass and charge transport through the film. To clarify our hypothesis, we investigated the in situ study of the softness–conductivity relationships by the Operando GIWAXS-Sensor measurements (the characterization of the crystal structure under working conditions involving the simultaneous measurement of gas-responsive conductivity). The thin films with electrodes were placed inside a polymer-free sealed Al chamber with two Al foils as X-ray windows (Fig. 5A and SI Appendix, Fig. S37). The in situ synchrotron GIWAXS measurements were conducted in operando under the controlled gas atmosphere and by simultaneously applying a DC voltage while monitoring the resistance of the thin film. By using H2O vapor as the stimuli molecules, the anisotropic softness–conductivity of cMOF thin films with different orientations and crystalline domains were well observed from the in situ GIWAXS images, which is accompanied by the corresponding resistance changes. As for c-oriented face-on cMOF thin films, 3.8% lattice expansion of 002 was observed for Cu1.0-P0.1-20C upon exposure to the air with 70% relative humidity (70% RH air) at room temperature (RT, Fig. 5 B and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S38), while the value decreased to 2.5% for Cu0.1-P0.1-30C with large crystalline domains (SI Appendix, Fig. S39), which further decreased to a negligible value for edge-on Cu0.1-P2.0-20C (Fig. 5 C and E).

(A) Schematic illustration of the Operando GIWAXS-Sensor; the GIWAXS patterns, and the schema of the anisotropic softness of (B) face-on Cu1.0 P0.1-20C nanofilm and (C) edge-on Cu0.1 P2.0-20C nanofilm under RH air and dry air at RT; the in-plane/out-of-plane GIWAXS profiles of (D) face-on Cu1.0 P0.1-20C nanofilm upon reversible exposure to dry air and different RH air at RT, and (E) edge-on Cu0.1 P2.0-20C nanofilm under RH air (black) and dry air (red) at RT obtained at φ = 0° and 90°; (F) the anisotropic lattice expansion–dependent conductivity decrease of face-on and edge-on cMOF nanofilms (red for face-on Cu1.0 P0.1-20C, green for face-on Cu0.1 P0.1-30C, and blue for edge-on Cu0.1 P2.0-20C).

In contrast, 2.6% lattice expansion of 200/020 was observed for edge-on Cu0.1-P2.0-20C nanofilm, while the value of 0.9% was observed for face-on Cu1.0-P0.1-20C films and 0.0% for Cu0.1-P0.1-30C. The φ-dependent operando PXRD patterns shown in SI Appendix, Figs. S40 and S41 further reveal the gradually increased lattice softness of the c-axis and a/b-axis for face-on and edge-on cMOF nanofilms, respectively. All results mentioned above clearly indicate the anisotropic softness of Cu-HHTP nanofilm, that is, the limited expansion for lattices anchored to the substrate (parallel direction) and the free softness along the contact-less lattice (vertical direction). The Operando GIWAXS-Sensor enables the in situ collection of the conductivity change with the structural distortion along a different axis, which clearly shows the positive correlation between the lattice expansion and the conductivity decrease (Fig. 5F and SI Appendix, Figs. S42–S44).

In addition, the downsizing-induced softness toward water molecules adsorption was also observed for Cu-HHTP nanocrystals (SI Appendix, Fig. S45), accompanied by a smaller structural response compared with those of nanofilms (lattice expansion, 1.6% of 200/020, and 2.5% of 002). The change of the position of 100 peak was also observed, but it is rather difficult to accurately quantify the lattice expansion based on the weak and broad 100 peak under RH air at small degree. The enlarged and anisotropic lattice expansion caused by surface-mounted oriented nanofilms also enables the clear observation of adsorption–desorption route of guest molecules in the frameworks. As indicated by the in-plane/out-of-plane GIWAXS profiles, the guest molecules first adsorbed in the channels and induced slight lattice distortion along a/b-planes (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S38), which results in the sharply decreased intensity and slight shift of 100/200 peaks. Second, the enlarged lattice distortion with increasing adsorbed guest molecules finally resulted in the expansion on both the a/b- and c-axis (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Figs. S38–S41). Reversal behaviors occurred during the desorption process (Fig. 5D) and were further confirmed from the in situ synchrotron PXRD of Cu-HHTP nanocrystals (SI Appendix, Fig. S45B). Such results clearly reveal the anisotropic softness–controlled conductivity of its nanofilms and the key role of the lattice expansion in the conductivity change of the material. It also answers the reason for the contradictory observation of a large conductivity decrease and negligible charge transfer upon water molecules’ adsorption by FT-IR spectra (SI Appendix, Fig. S46). Since the decrease in conductivity induced by lattice expansion in this study does not exhibit a clear linear relationship with the extent of the expansion, further research is required to quantitatively assess the efficiency of charge transport in various directions.

As for cases of cMOF-on-iMOF heterostructured thin films, the operando results also confirm similar anisotropic softness–conductivity behaviors, though slightly weaker than bare cMOF nanofilm, depending on the face-on or edge-on orientation of the Cu-HHTP layer (SI Appendix, Figs. S47–S50). The bottom iMOF layers act as the substrates limiting the expansion of lattices anchored to iMOF and rendering flexible softness along with the contact-less lattice.

As an interesting topic, both theoretical and experimental studies have been performed to reveal the anisotropic mechanical properties of MOFs (45–50). After the successful measurement of the hardness and Young’s modulus of iMOF microcrystal (51) and oriented film (52) using nanoindentation techniques, the thermal-induced anisotropic expansion of the HKUST-1 thin film was successfully observed in 2016 (53). Then, in 2019, the anisotropic structures of rod-like NU-1000 iMOFs films were employed to modulated electrochemical conductivity in liquid electrolyte (54). Our work provides a typical example that by using intrinsic conductive MOFs and reversal orientation, the anisotropic softness can be directly used to modulate the electrical conductivity.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully synthesized cMOF smooth nanofilms with reversal orientations of face-on and edge-on form in a facile manner, explored the possible growth mechanism using combined techniques of FM-AFM lattice imaging and GIWAXS, and found the unique anisotropic softness–dependent conductivity behaviors of the surface-mounted oriented networks. The ultrahigh concentration of the organic ligand solution enabled their molecule-level stand-up packing during the LbL spraying-coordination process with metal ions, thus leading to the formation of the rare Cu-HHTP nanofilm with a/b orientation rather than commonly observed c orientation. Furthermore, the FM-AFM lattice imaging and GIWAXS patterns confirmed the crystallinity of the thin film even down to few nanometers. The anisotropic framework softness associated with electrical conductivity was revealed by the operando GIWAXS imaging on the cMOF nanofilm. In addition to redox interactions, the structural softness has been confirmed to modulate the electrical conductivity in an anisotropic way (up to two orders of magnitude). The anisotropic softness–conductivity behaviors of the cMOF nanofilm not only pave a way for the design of soft conductive crystalline porous materials from the perspective of anisotropic lattice expansion and molecule packing behaviors but also provide a general method to operando characterize the thin film applied in electrical devices such as sensors, batteries, supercapacitors, catalysis, etc.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of Face-On Cu-HHTP Thin Films.

Cu-HHTP thin films were grown on functionalized substrates using the LPE spray method modified from Woll’s work (27). Cu1.0 P0.1-xC thin films were fabricated using the following diluted ethanolic solutions: copper acetate (1.0 mM) and HHTP (2,3,6,7,10,11-hexahydrotriphenylene) (0.1 mM). Cu0.1 P0.1-xC thin films were fabricated using the following diluted ethanolic solutions: copper acetate (0.1 mM) and HHTP (0.1 mM). The spray time was 20 s for the copper acetate solution and 40 s for the HHTP solution, followed by a spray step with pure ethanol to remove residual reactants. Above steps were repeated to realize LbL assembly with the drying process by N2 in each step. By repeating this LbL protocol, face-on Cu1.0 P0.1-xC and Cu0.1 P0.1-xC with various thicknesses were successfully obtained.

Preparation of Edge-On Cu-HHTP Thin Films.

Similar procedures were applied to prepare edge-on thin films, except that the concentration of the ethanolic solutions was changed to copper acetate (0.1 mM) and HHTP (0.5 mM) for Cu0.1 P0.5-xC; copper acetate (0.1 mM) and HHTP (1.0 mM) for Cu0.1 P1.0-xC; and copper acetate (0.1 mM) and HHTP (2.0 mM) for Cu0.1 P2.0-xC; and the spray times were 13 s for the copper acetate solution and 26 s for the HHTP solution.

Preparation of Cu-BDC Thin Films.

Modified from Woll’s work (55). For details, see SI Appendix.

Preparation of Cu-BTC Thin Films.

Modified from Weckhuysen’s work (56). For details, see SI Appendix.

Preparation of Face-On/Edge-On Cu-HHTP Thin Films on iMOF Heterostructured Thin Films.

Similar procedures were applied to prepare face-on/edge-on Cu-HHTP thin films, of which the substrates were replaced by Cu-BDC or Cu-BTC thin films.

Preparation of iMOF-on-cMOF-on iMOF Heterostructured Thin Films.

Similar procedures were applied to prepare Cu-BTC-10C thin films, of which the substrates were replaced by face-on Cu1.0 P.01-40C-on-Cu-BTC thin films.

Preparation of Cu-HHTP Nanocrystals.

The preparation method is modified from our previous work (16). For details, see SI Appendix.

Characterizations.

The synchrotron GIWAXS patterns data were collected at beamline 46XU in SPring-8, Japan (λ = 1.00 Å). FM-AFM measurements were performed by a laboratory-built ultralow noise FM-AFM system combined with an ARC2 controller (Asylum Research, Oxford Instruments) and Nanonis OC4.5 controller (SPECS Surface Nano Analysis GmbH). A silicon cantilever (160AC-NG, OPUS, MikroMasch) with a nominal spring constant of 26 N/m was used. The freshly cleaved mica was used for the LbL growth of the cMOF thin film, and the as-prepared sample was transferred to the FM-AFM setup and imaged immediately. All FM-AFM imaging experiments were performed in ultrapure water at RT (25 °C). For details, see SI Appendix.
