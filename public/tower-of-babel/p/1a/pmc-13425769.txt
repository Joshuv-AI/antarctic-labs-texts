A successful individualized comprehensive therapy for carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae induced multifocal brain abscesses: a case report and literature review
Authors: Boyuan Huang, Minglu Yuan, Mengyang Wang, Gengsheng Mao, Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP)-induced brain abscess is an exceedingly rare disease. We presented the fourth case of CRKP-induced brain abscess but the first multifocal and most complicated one accompanied by intracranial Epstein-Barr virus detection positive. The patient was treated with a regime of intravenous ceftazidime/avibactam combined with intrathecal colistimethate sodium. To our knowledge, this represented the first reported application of such a regimen for a CRKP-induced brain abscess. When the abscess formed an intact envelope, the patient underwent craniotomy for resection. Postoperatively, the antibiotic regimen was continued for 2 weeks until the CSF examination became nearly normal. The patient remained asymptomatic over the next 12 months follow-up without recurrences. This case highlights the rarity of CRKP-induced brain abscess and provides a novel combination of antibiotic regimen, which also emphasizes the necessity for individualized and comprehensive therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Brain abscess is a severe intracranial infectious disease, with a distinct biofilm, a high rate of lethality and disability, which belongs to one of the most serious central nervous system infection (CNSI) [1]. It has a prevalence varying globally, accounting for 1–2% of intracranial diseases in developed countries, and approximately 2–8% in China and other developing countries [1–3]. The most common pathogen involved in brain abscess is gram-positive cocci (GPC), leaving only 20% of gram-negative bacilli (GNB) [4–5]. Among GNB, Klebsiella pneumoniae (KP), a virulent and invasive organism, has been increasingly recognized as a cause of brain abscess, although its overall incidence remains lower than that of classical GPC infections [5]. Notably, infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) pose a major therapeutic challenge because of limited antibiotic options, high recurrence rates, and mortality exceeding 50% [6, 7]. When faced with CRKP-induced brain abscess, the therapeutic strategy should be individualized and comprehensive.

Previous reports of CRKP-associated brain abscesses have described various antimicrobial regimens. However, treatment strategies for CRKP brain abscesses that sequentially use intrathecal (I.T) colistimethate sodium (CMS) and intravenous (IV) ceftazidime/avibactam (CAZ/AVI) are exceedingly rare. Here, we presented a rare and complex case of CRKP-induced multifocal brain abscesses accompanied by intracephalic Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) positive. The patient was successfully treated with an individualized and comprehensive therapy of intravenous administration, intrathecal administration and neurosurgical operation. To our knowledge, this case was among the few reported cases employing this specific sequence of CMS (I.T) and CAZ/AVI (IV) for multifocal CRKP-induced brain abscesses. This case highlights the rarity of such complicated intracranial infections, provides a novel antibiotic regimen for CRKP-induced brain abscess, and underscores the need for individualized and comprehensive treatment in intracerebral abscesses.

Case report

A 33-year-old man presented at our outpatient with intermittent low fever for one month and progressive disturbance of consciousness for one day. The patient had multiple visits to the Department of Respiratory in the past month and received intermittent courses of different antibiotics, but the clinical response was suboptimal. His highest temperature was 38.3℃, accompanied by nausea, vomiting and somnolence. The patient also experienced fatigue and dizziness, without headache, chest pain, epilepsy, aphasia, and dyskinesia, etc. The physical and neurological examinations showed that he had positive cervical resistance, with normal myodynamia and muscular tone. Laboratory examinations revealed a white blood cell count of 14.27 × 109/L [reference range: 3.5–9.5 × 109/L], neutrophil percentage of 87.3% [reference range: 40–75%], C-reactive protein of 23.68 mg/L [reference range: <10 mg/L], and procalcitonin of 1.89 ng/ml [reference range: < 0.5 ng/ml]. Head computed tomography (CT) scanning showed an oval lesion with central hypodensity, thin irregular wall, and perilesional edema measuring 27 × 15 × 12 mm in the right basal ganglia (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanning showed that the lesion appeared hypointense on T1-weighted, and hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with irregular and thin ring enhancement after contrast agent administration (Fig. 1). In addition, the lesion compressed inwards into the ventricle with evident edema around the adjacent brain parenchyma. Apart from this lesion, enhanced-MRI also identified other three focal lesions respectively located in left frontal lobe, posterior right superior frontal gyrus near to the midline, and pons. Based on these above evidences, the patient was diagnosed as multifocal brain abscesses with ventriculitis.

On day 1, the routine and biochemical examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) showed a positive PAN test with leukocyte 1920/mm3 (normal value range: 0–8/mm3), CSF protein 173 mg/dL (normal value range: 0-40 mg/dL), chloride 98.6mmol/L (normal value range: 120-132mmol/L), and glucose 1.64mmol/L (normal value range: 2.5-4.4mmol/L). Although intrathecal administration was not typically recommended as empirical therapy for CNSI, it may be considered as a rescue option in life-threatening circumstance. Given the patient’s critical condition and the limited CSF penetration of intravenous vancomycin, the patient was first treated empirically by vancomycin (1 g, q12h, IV) combined with meropenem (2 g, q8h, IV). Moreover, lumbar puncture drainage was also performed for vancomycin (20 mg, qd, I.T). On day 2, the result of metagenomic next generation sequencing (mNGS) showed that the pathogens were KP (relative abundance: 47.78%) and EBV (relative abundance: 1.99%). Since the infection was community-acquired, the likelihood of CRKP was considered low. The empirical antibiotic regimen was adjusted to meropenem (2 g, q8h, IV and 20 mg, q12h, I.T). Given the detection of EBV sequences in CSF and the severity of the patient’s intracranial infection, empirical antiviral therapy with acyclovir (500 mg, q8h, IV) was initiated. However, the initial CSF cultures obtained on day 5 failed to identify any pathogens.

During the next week, the patient’s symptoms exhibited no significant improvement. On day 9, he had epileptic seizure with a maximum temperature of 39.1℃. The CSF examination showed an elevated leukocyte (12080/mm3) and increased CSF protein (260 mg/dL). The antimicrobial susceptibility test from the CSF culture demonstrated that the pathogen was resistant to carbapenems (Table 1). Based on the combined findings of mNGS and CSF culture, a diagnosis of CRKP-induced multifocal brain abscesses was established. Then the regimen was adjusted to CAZ/AVI (2.5 g, q8h, IV) and CMS (125,000 U, qd, I.T) combined with acyclovir (500 mg, q8h, IV).

On day 14, the temperature of the patient had gradually become normal. The CSF examination showed that leukocyte decreased to 164/mm3 with CSF protein 91 mg/dL. The regimen lasted for two weeks until the 23rd day when leukocyte and CSF protein in CSF further decreased to 80/mm3 and 91 mg/dL, respectively. Enhanced MRI showed that the dominant lesion in the right basal ganglia had distinctly narrowed with more thickened and intact cyst wall compared with the last time. And the other three lesions had also become more focal with weaker enhancement after contrast agent administration (Fig. 2). All the above demonstrated that the dominant lesion had formed an intact envelope and the intracranial infection had been effectively controlled. Then the lumbar puncture drainage tube was removed and a craniotomy of brain abscess resection was performed.

During the surgery, we applied neuronavigation techniques to locate the dominant abscess and design surgical path. After a right frontal craniotomy, we employed intraoperative ultrasonic apparatus to explore the depth of the lesion in the surgical path planned by neuronavigation. Then a transfrontal cortical fistulation was performed through the designed path. Next under microscope, we carefully dissected and removed the dominant abscess from its adjacent brain parenchyma. Finally, the abscess was completely stripped out, leaving the cyst wall intactly without any pus flowing out (Fig. 3).

Postoperatively, the patient’s left muscle strength remained unchanged compared to preoperative level, without any disturbance of consciousness, epileptic seizure, or other neurological dysfunctions. CAZ/AVI (IV) and acyclovir (IV) combined with CMS (I.T) through surgical cavity drainage which was indwelled during the surgery were continued. On day 33, the surgical cavity drainage was removed and intrathecal CMS was still maintained through daily lumbar puncture because of the unnormal CSF leukocyte (92/mm3) and CSF protein (128 mg/dL). On day 38, the CSF examination showed a normal level of leukocyte (23/mm3) and slightly high level of CSF protein (48 mg/dL), while CSF culture and mNGS showed negative results. Enhanced MRI showed that the dominant lesion was completely excised, with the lesion in the pons exhibiting weaker enhancement than ever before, and the other two lesions nearly vanished (Fig. 4). Ultimately the patient was successfully cured and discharged from our hospital. He remained asymptomatic over the next 12 months follow-up and had no recurrences. Discussion

Brain abscess is a life-threatening focal intracranial infectious disease, with an annual prevalence of approximately 0.3–1.3 per 10,000 people. It occurs more frequently in male patients, especially those middle-aged and elderly patients [1]. The infectious routes of brain abscesses are mainly divided into four categories: direct infections secondary to craniocerebral trauma and surgery, direct infections from adjacent lesions, hematogenous infections, and cryptogenic infections [1]. According to the the process of pathological development, brain abscess can be staged into acute encephalitis stage, the suppurative stage and envelope formed stage [1]. Therapeutic management is stage-dependent. Pharmacologic intervention should be the preferred approach during the encephalitis stage. When the abscess is in the suppurative stage or envelope formed stage, surgical resection or puncture drainage becomes necessary [1].

Rapid identification of pathogenic microorganisms and appropriate antibiotic use are crucial in the treatment of brain abscesses. Traditional diagnostic approaches, such as gram staining and microbial cultures, have been regarded as the gold standard for pathogen identification, providing antimicrobial susceptibility data to guide antibiotic selection [8]. However, these methods are constrained by prolonged turnaround times, limited sensitivity and specificity. mNGS, which extracts all microbial DNA or RNA directly from clinical specimens, uses genomic strategies, offering higher pathogen detection rates, shorter turnaround time, and broader pathogen coverage [8, 9]. However, mNGS still exhibits certain limitations: it cannot provide antimicrobial susceptibility data, along with high costs and technical complexity, which restrict its application in resource-constrained institutions [8, 9]. In this case, enhanced MRI showed that the dominant lesion exhibited irregular and thin ring enhancement, which indicated that the abscess had not formed an intact envelope and was still progressing at the suppurative stage. Considering the abscess was multifocal with ventriculitis, we first adapted pharmacologic therapy to control the complicated intracranial infections according to the results of mNGS and bacterial culture. And then, when MRI showed the abscess had formed an intact envelope, we adapted surgical resection of the dominant lesion combined with pharmacologic therapy against other three lesions.

Currently, there are limited reports about CRKP-induced CNSI, so the optimal regimen for antimicrobial treatment is still unclear. Traditionally, only tigecycline, fosfomycin, aminoglycosides and polymyxins which have anti-CRKP activity are recommended [6]. Tigecycline, with a large apparent volume of distribution and disability to penetrate the blood brain barrier (BBB), were reported to be applied in some severe CNSI with overdoses [10]. However, the adverse effects and death rate were also elevated in a dose-dependent manner among patients [10]. Fosfomycin has a small molecular weight and can easily penetrate the BBB, with CSF/blood concentration reaching more than 50%. However, owing to its weak antibacterial capacity and poor post-antibiotic effect against GNB, it is seldom used as a single-agent therapeutic regimen for CRKP [11]. Aminoglycosides are presented in the CSF at less than 10% of the plasma concentration, which requires large doses of intravenous drip to achieve efficient bactericidal effect. However, considering aminoglycosides are excreted by kidney, a large-dose administration systemically is prone to cause serious renal damage [12]. Thus, current guidelines recommended that ventricular/intrathecal administration of aminoglycosides should be combined with other anti-CRKP medicine to optimize clinical cure rates while minimizing the incidence of adverse events [13]. Polymyxins have excellent antimicrobial activity against most carbapenem-resistant GNB, including CRKP, by increasing the permeability of the bacterial cell membrane and leads to the leakage of cellular contents [14]. Currently, the polymyxins applied in clinic include CMS, polymyxin B and polymyxin E, but only CMS is approved for ventricular/intrathecal administration in China. The clinical application of polymyxins via intrathecal or intraventricular routes represents a critical salvage option in specific cases and requires restrict pharmaceutical supervision [15, 16]. In this case, we employed intrathecal CMS via preoperative lumbar cisternal drainage and postoperative surgical cavity drainage. A 2-million-unit vial of CMS was dissolved in 100 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride solution, and a daily dose of 6.25 mL (equivalent to 125,000 U) was administered via slow intrathecal injection over at least 5 min. Following each injection, the drainage catheter was clamped for 2 h to ensure adequate drug distribution. Close monitoring for any neurological adverse reactions was conducted during this therapy. CSF routine and biochemical examinations were performed every two to three days to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and detect any potential side effects. The CMS I.T therapy lasted for 20 days, until the patient’s laboratory examinations basically normalized.

New generation of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations including CAZ/AVI, meropenem/vaborbactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, imipenem-cilastatin/relebactam are also recommended for the treatment of CRKP [17]. Among these agents, only CAZ/AVI has been approved for clinical application in China. AVI is a new type of β-lactamase inhibitor capable of long-lasting enzyme inhibitory effects and avoiding inducing β-lactamase production. In combination with CAZ, it can restore the activity of CAZ against carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales resistant due to the production of Ambler class A, class C (AmpC enzymes), and some class D carbapenemases [18]. However, its activity is limited against metallo-β-lactamase-producing strains, which significantly restricts its spectrum of efficacy. Some studies have shown that up to 80% of CRKP and 100% of non-enzymatic and serine enzyme-producing CRKP are susceptible to CAZ/AVI [19]. CAZ/AVI has been reported to penetrate the blood-brain barrier, thereby demonstrating potential for the treatment of CNSI [20]. However, up to now, only one case has reported the successful use of CAZ/AVI, in combination with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim and amikacin, for the management of a brain abscess caused by CRKP [21]. In this case, although molecular resistance mechanisms were lacking, given the relatively low resistance rate of CRKP to CAZ/AVI, we decided to administer CAZ/AVI for treatment.

It was reported that CMS can help CAZ/AVI to bind to the penicillin-binding proteins by removing the lipopolysaccharide in bacterial outer membrane, which can in turn inhibit the synthesis of bacterial cell wall [18]. Thus, the sequential application of CMS and CAZ/AVI is supposed to exert a synergistic antimicrobial effect. This case represented the first application of intrathecal CMS combined with intravenous CAZ/AVI for the treatment of multifocal brain abscesses caused by CRKP. Actually, this combination of CMS and CAZ/AVI powerfully controlled the patient´s intracranial multifocal infection and thus created an appropriate condition for the subsequent surgical treatment. Hitherto, there has been reported only three cases of brain abscesses in which CRKP was the causative agent and was successfully treated [6, 21, 22]. Although this report was the fourth case, it was the most complicated one because the abscess was multifocal. Furthermore, compared with the other three reported cases, the antimicrobial regimen in this case required fewer treatment adjustments, involved fewer combined antibiotics and a shorter overall treatment duration, yet achieved a favorable clinical outcome (Table 2).

It is worth noting that mNGS from CSF of the patient also identified EBV, which was known to occur in critically ill or immunocompromised patients [23]. The clinical significance of EBV detection in CSF remains uncertain in this case. EBV positivity may represent viral reactivation rather than true pathogenic involvement, particularly in the absence of EBV viral load testing, serologic evidence, or underlying immunodeficiency. Given the patient’s critical condition and the inability to completely exclude viral central nervous system involvement, empirical acyclovir therapy was initiated as a precautionary measure. Thus, the contribution of antiviral therapy to clinical improvement could not be determined.

Currently, surgical treatment is still an essential approach for brain abscess when it is in the suppurative or periosteal formation stage, which mainly includes surgical puncture drainage, and surgical resection [24, 25]. The advantage of puncture drainage is simpler to operate and less damaged to adjacent brain tissue compared to surgical resection. However, owing to disability of.

eliminating the abscess wall, puncture drainage may cost more curative time with a higher long-term recurrence rate as well as a risk of pus leakage [26]. The advantage of surgical resection is that it can thorough eliminate the abscess with a lower long-term recurrence rate, but more likely to cause neurological damage [27]. Since the cure rates between surgical resection and puncture drainage differ slightly, there is still a great deal of controversy about which surgical treatment to prioritize [24–28]. However, when faced with resistant pathogens induced abscess, multilocular abscesses, and post-traumatic abscesses, surgical resection should be preferably recommended for radical treatment. In our case, the dominant lesion was deep located in the region of the basal ganglia. Given that it had invaded the right ventricle, puncture drainage should have result in rupture of the abscess wall and entry of pus into the ventricle, and thus causing severe ventriculitis. Therefore, we chose surgical resection and eventually eliminated the abscess completely without any neurological damage.

Conclusion

Herein, we presented the first case of CRKP-induced multifocal brain abscesses which was successfully treated with aggressive antimicrobial regimen and rational surgical timing and approach. Based on our successful experience, sequential use of CMS (I.T) combined with CAZ/AVI (IV) may represent a potential therapeutic option in selected cases, particularly when conventional therapies are ineffective. In addition, a reasonable option of surgical timing and approach is also critical for the treatment of CRKP-induced brain abscess. Therefore, intracranial CRKP infections, especially brain abscess, need individualized and comprehensive treatment, which requires combined efforts from internists, clinical pharmacists, and surgeons.

However, the implementation of this regimen presents several challenges. A key limitation involves potential accessibility barriers to CMS, particularly in resource-limited settings or regions with underdeveloped technological infrastructure, which may compromise the generalizability of this method across diverse healthcare systems. Despite the favorable outcome observed in this case, intrathecal CMS therapy is generally considered a salvage therapy for severe and refractory CNSI. Given the potential risks associated with intrathecal CMS, this approach should be reserved for carefully selected patients and performed under strict clinical monitoring. Furthermore, although antimicrobial susceptibility testing confirmed CRKP in this case, genotypic analysis of resistance mechanisms was not performed. Therefore, the effectiveness of CAZ/AVI observed in this case should not be generalized to all CRKP infections, as treatment response may vary depending on the underlying resistance mechanisms. Moreover, although CAZ/AVI demonstrates therapeutic potential for CNSI, its use remains off-label, and current clinical evidence is restricted to isolated case reports. Rigorous large-scale, multicenter studies are still needed to further evaluate its efficacy and safety.
