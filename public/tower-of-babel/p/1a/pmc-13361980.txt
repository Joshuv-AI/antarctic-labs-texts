Regional abnormalities of cardiac sympathetic innervation in athletes with nonischaemic left ventricular scar and exercise-related ventricular arrhythmias
Authors: Alessandro Zorzi, Francesca Graziano, Domenico Corrado, Pietro Zucchetta, Diego Cecchin
Published: 2026

Nonischaemic left ventricular scar (NILVS) is a recognized feature of healed myocarditis and inherited cardiomyopathies; however, in a relevant proportion of patients its aetiology remains unexplained. In athletes, NILVS has been consistently associated with ventricular arrhythmias (VA) and exercise-related cardiac arrest, even in the absence of significant left ventricular (LV) dysfunction.1–5 Notably, VA typically occur during physical exertion and often respond favourably to beta-blocker therapy, suggesting a prominent adrenergic trigger.5–7 However, the pathophysiological mechanism underlying the adrenergic dependence of VA in athletes with NILVS, despite the presence of a fixed structural substrate, remains poorly understood. Clarifying this mechanism may provide clinically relevant insights for therapeutic management and arrhythmic risk stratification.

We sought to investigate whether athletes with NILVS and exercise-related VA show regional abnormalities of cardiac sympathetic innervation, potentially providing functional pathophysiological information beyond the structural characterization of scar offered by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) able to explain the correlation between sport activity and VA.

We included in this pilot observational study a series of five consecutive male competitive athletes, aged 30–50 years, engaged in endurance sports for more than 6 h per week, who were evaluated between 2024 and 2025 for asymptomatic ventricular arrhythmias (VA) detected during preparticipation cardiovascular screening. These patients were drawn from a multicentre registry of athletes with NILVS established in 2020, whose clinical characteristics and diagnostic work-up have been recently described.5 In brief, all athletes underwent comprehensive evaluation including personal and family history, cardiopulmonary exercise testing, 24-h Holter monitoring including a training session, transthoracic echocardiography, CMR, and genetic testing. From 2024 onwards, endurance athletes with NILVS and distinctly exercise-induced VA also underwent single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) with [123I]metaiodobenzylguanidine (MIBG) to assess myocardial sympathetic innervation. [123I]MIBG-SPECT was performed using a CZT gamma camera system (StarGuide, General Electric Healthcare, Haifa, Israel), with integrated computed tomography-based attenuation correction. Early (15 min) and delayed (4 h) images were acquired according to a standard clinical protocol. Regional uptake defects were visually defined as reduced uptake compared with remote myocardium, grades as mild, moderate or severe and compared with LGE distribution on corresponding CMR short-axis slices, while acknowledging the different spatial resolution of the two techniques.8 When present, beta-blocker therapy was discontinued at least 72 h before imaging, and no patient was receiving other medications known to interfere with MIBG uptake. MIBG-SPECT images were interpreted in consensus by two senior nuclear medicine physicians (PZ and DCe), aware of the clinical indication but not of the CMR images; topographic CMR–SPECT comparison was subsequently performed with a CMR-experienced author (FG). The study was approved by the local ethical committee and all patients provided their written informed consent.

All five athletes showed exercise-induced premature ventricular beats (PVBs), either isolated or occurring in couplets/triplets, with a right bundle branch block/superior axis morphology consistent with a lateral LV origin. On CMR, all athletes showed NILVS with a subepicardial-to-midmyocardial distribution, predominantly involving the lateral wall, with a mean extent of 12.5% of LV mass (range 9.8–17.7%), as assessed by the full width at half maximum method. Whole-exome sequencing did not identify pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. On [123I]MIBG-SPECT, performed 2–5 months after CMR, all patients demonstrated regional reduction in myocardial tracer uptake on both early and delayed images, involving the same LV territory as the scar detected by CMR (lateral and infero-lateral LV wall). In 3 of 5 cases (b, c, and e), reduced MIBG uptake and fibrosis were roughly concordant. However, CMR and SPECT findings were not completely overlapping in the remaining 2 cases. Despite the different spatial resolution of the two imaging techniques, in 1 patient (Figure 1D) the [123I]MIBG abnormality extended more apically than the scar detected by CMR, whereas in another (Figure 1A) fibrosis extended beyond the area of reduced tracer uptake (Figure 1). Global heart-to-mediastinum ratios were within the normal range, suggesting no global sympathetic denervation.

[123I]MIBG-SPECT allows in vivo assessment of postganglionic cardiac sympathetic innervation, providing information on both global and regional abnormalities.8 While globally reduced myocardial [123I]MIBG uptake has been associated with adverse prognosis in heart failure, regional abnormalities of cardiac sympathetic innervation have been described in both ischaemic and nonischaemic cardiac diseases, supporting a link between autonomic inhomogeneity and ventricular arrhythmias.8,9 In particular, a recent study demonstrated an association between regional [123I]MIBG uptake inhomogeneity and sustained VA in nonischaemic cardiomyopathy.10 However, the relationship between focal NILVS and regional sympathetic innervation abnormalities has not been previously investigated in athletes with exercise-induced ventricular arrhythmias and idiopathic NILVS.

In our case series, all athletes exhibited regional sympathetic innervation abnormalities, suggesting an association between localized structural myocardial alterations and autonomic remodelling even in the absence of overt cardiomyopathy. Regional [123I]MIBG uptake defects involved the same LV territory as the nonischaemic scar at CMR, supporting an association between myocardial scarring and sympathetic dysfunction. However, the spatial extent of reduced tracer uptake and fibrosis was not completely overlapping. In one patient, the [123I]MIBG abnormality extended beyond the scarred myocardium, whereas in another fibrosis exceeded the area of reduced tracer uptake.

Although the mechanism underlying scar formation in athletes and sympathetic dysfunction are not fully understood, it is noteworthy that both NILVS and sympathetic efferent fibres predominantly involve the subepicardial layers. Therefore, abnormalities of sympathetic innervation may reflect functional and/or structural damage to sympathetic nerve trunks travelling within the subepicardial ventricular wall, potentially affecting not only fibrotic tissue but also adjacent viable myocardium.9 This may contribute to the mismatch between CMR and SPECT, although its clinical significance remains uncertain.

Experimental and clinical studies have shown that heterogeneous sympathetic innervation may paradoxically increase arrhythmogenic vulnerability. Compared with innervated myocardium, denervated regions may develop adrenergic supersensitivity due to upregulation of β-adrenergic receptors, resulting in exaggerated shortening of the refractory period during catecholamine exposure and increased dispersion of ventricular refractoriness, which may act as a functional substrate for electrical instability during adrenergic stimulation.9

This exploratory study has several limitations including the very small sample size, absence of a control group, qualitative visual image interpretation, lack of formal blinded and interobserver analyses, nonsimultaneous CMR and SPECT acquisitions, and absence of outcome data linking MIBG abnormalities to subsequent arrhythmic events. Nevertheless, these observations support the study of sympathetic innervation imaging in athletes with NILVS and suggest that regional [123I]MIBG uptake abnormalities may complement CMR in characterizing the arrhythmogenic substrate in this setting. They also raise the possibility that sympathetic dysfunction may contribute to the exercise-related pattern of VA typical of athletes with NILVS. However, this preliminary observation needs to be confirmed by larger studies.
