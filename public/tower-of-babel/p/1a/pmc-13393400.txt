DeepSAT + SMART-Driven Identification of Diterpene Glycosides with α‑Glucosidase Inhibitory Activities from the Rhizomes of a Crop Plant, Isodon suzhouensis
Authors: Jin Chen, Duo-duo Wang, Zhaohui Guo, Shuo Li, Jia-yi Li, Fu-cai Ren, Xue-song Liu, Chuan-pu Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Five previously unreported diterpene glycosides, named wangzosides A–E (1–5), were targeted isolated using the DeepSAT + SMART technology from the rhizomes of Isodon suzhouensis, which was used as a herbal tea in China. Structural analysis of the materials was performed using the 2D/1D NMR and ECD calculations. During the separation and structural elucidation process, an interconversion relationship between 1 and 2 was identified and subsequently verified through experimental evidence. Based on the traditional uses of the plant, inhibition of α-glucosidase was investigated. With the exception of 2, all compounds displayed substantial inhibitory activity, with IC50 values lying within the range extending from 3.60 ± 0.75 to 32.12 ± 1.08 μM. Molecular docking studies suggested that these compounds interact with α-glucosidase to form stable complexes characterized by favorable binding free energies. Molecular dynamics simulations further verified that compounds 4 formed a stable complex with α-glucosidase, demonstrating favorable conformational stability, strong binding free energies, and good conformational stability. Collectively, this study reported five previously unreported diterpene glycosides from I. suzhouensis, with 4 emerging as potential scaffolds for the discovery of an antidiabetic drug.

Introduction

In China, it is common practice for people to consume herbal teas for health maintenance. The aromatic profile of herbal tea elicits is appealing to consumers, while its bioactive constituents, including anti-inflammatory, free radical scavenging, and antioxidant characteristics, exert beneficial effects on physiological homeostasis and health outcomes. Classified within the genus Isodon of the family Lamiaceae, Isodon suzhouensis has a documented distribution restricted solely to Suzhou City, Anhui Province, China, where it has been widely cultivated broadly there for many years. The leaves and rhizomes of this crop can be used as a Chinese herbal tea, known as “Wangzaozi” in Suzhou City, valued for its broad medicinal and edible applications. It can be prepared as a tea, incorporated into dishes, or used as a topical or oral anti-inflammatory and antidiabetic agent.

Natural product-derived oral hypoglycemic agents, exemplified by phlorizin derivatives, play a crucial role in preventing and treating diabetes. The high-polarity fraction of the I. suzhouensis extract has been found to exhibit certain moderate α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, consistent with the traditional uses of the genus Isodon. This suggests the potential to identify bioactive compounds of medicinal value from Isodon and its botanical counterparts. Plants of the genus Isodon constitute an important resource for medicinal compounds. Research on this genus has identified characteristic ent-kaurane-type and abietane-type diterpenoids, which have neuroprotective, antitumor, hepatoprotective, and anti-inflammatory characteristics. , Recent studies have revealed that plants of the genus Isodon contain a class of polymers composed of rare diterpenoids and small molecular acids, as well as diterpenoid dimers. , Our earlier work focused on the aerial parts of this plant, resulting in the identification of a suite of ent-kaurane diterpenoids. Subsequent activity evaluations confirmed the potential anti-inflammatory properties of these compounds. In contrast, the root system remains unexplored with respect to its phytochemical constituents. The most characteristic constituents of the high-polarity extract fraction of the rhizomes of I. suzhouensis were examined via DeepSAT and SMART technologies (Figure SI in the Supporting Information), which are efficient approaches for obtaining chemical features attributed to molecular structure directly from HSQC spectra. Analysis revealed that diterpene glycosides represent the most characteristic constituents within the rhizome phytochemical profile, thus establishing them as a central research target.

As a result, five previously unreported diterpene glycosides were successfully isolated from the rhizomes of I. suzhouensis (Figure ). Their structures were fully established by combining quantum-chemical calculations and spectroscopic methods. This study offers a detailed account of the structural determination, isolation, and biological activity assessment of these diterpenes from the rhizomes of I. suzhouensis.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

NMR profiles were obtained by utilizing an Avance III 500 spectrometer (1H: 500 MHz, 13C: 125 MHz, Bruker), with TMS as a reference. HR-ESI-MS spectra were obtained utilizing an Ultimate 3000 UPLC-Q Exactive Plus system from Dionex. Silica gel was procured from Qingdao Haiyang Chemical Co., Ltd., while RP-C18 (40–63 μm, Fuji, Japan) was utilized for column chromatography. Semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was conducted on two systems: a Thermo N3000 series device, coupled to a Hypersil GOLD DIM RP-C18 column (10 × 250 mm, 4.6 μm) from Thermo Scientific, and an LC-20AR series device from Shimadzu fitted with an SPD-20A detector and a Shimpak RP-C18 column.

Plant Material

The rhizomes of I. suzhouensis used in this study were obtained from the Yongqiao District. The plant material was authenticated by Z. B. Han and was cultivated at Suzhou Lyuyuan Traditional Chinese Medicine Technology Co., Ltd. A voucher sample (no. 20220921) was submitted to the School of Pharmacy, Anhui Medical University.

Acid Hydrolysis and Sugar Analysis

Each of compounds 1 and 3–5 (approximately 2 mg) was mixed within 2 M HCl (5 mL) and heated in a water bath at a temperature of 90 °C for 2 h. The cooled mixture underwent CHCl3 extraction, followed by reduced-pressure concentration of the aqueous layer to approximately 1 mL. Glucose was detected by paper chromatography using the BAW solvent system (v/v/v of n-butanol–H2O–acetic acid = 4:5:1) as the eluent, with reference to an authentic glucose standard.

The remaining aqueous fraction was dried under reduced pressure, reconstituted in 10 mL of 1 N HCl, and then heated again at 90 °C for 2 h. Following cooling to ambient temperature, the resultant mixture was successively retrieved with CH2Cl2 and EtOAc. The resulting aqueous phase, containing the hydrolyzed sugars, was derivatized to determine the absolute configurations of the sugars following established procedures. The derivatives were evaluated via a Thermo N3000 series HPLC system coupled to a Thermo Scientific Hypersil column (10 × 250 mm, 4.6 μm) maintained at a temperature equal to 30 °C. The mobile phase comprised 25% acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid, with elution at 1.0 mL/min and detection at 254 nm. Comparison of retention times with standards (t R 9.23 min for the derivative) verified the presence of d-glucose.

ECD Calculation Methods

Conformational analysis was conducted based on the OPLS3 molecular mechanics force field within Maestro 10.2 using the MacroModel panel, considering an energy window of 3.0 kcal/mol. Optimization of the resulting conformers was undertaken using Gaussian 09 (level B3LYP/6-311G­[d,p]). For each optimized conformer, the 90 lowest-energy electronic transitions were assessed using time-dependent density functional theory. A Gaussian function was utilized to obtain electronic circular dichroism (ECD) profiles for the individual conformers using Boltzmann weighting to determine the overall theoretical ECD plot. The ECD profiles were then analyzed via SpecDis software and compared with the experimental results.

Inhibitory Effect of α-Glycosidase Activity

Compounds 1 and 3–5 were analyzed for their α-glucosidase inhibitory activities. Briefly, phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 6.8, 175 μL/well) was placed in 96-well microplates, with inclusion of 25 μL of α-glucosidase solution (0.4 U/mL, Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.) and 25 μL of the test compound. Control wells received 25 μL of DMSO instead of the test sample, and background wells contained an additional 25 μL of phosphate buffer in place of the enzyme solution. Following preincubation for a duration equal to 10 min at a temperature of 37 °C, 25 μL of 5 mM p-nitrophenyl-α-d-glucopyranoside (p-NPG) was added to all wells. The reaction mixtures were incubated for 30 min, followed by halting of the reaction with 25 μL of 0.1 M Na2CO3. Absorbances at 405 nm were read using a microplate reader. Acarbose functioned as the positive control. All tests were conducted independently 3×.

Molecular Docking Analysis

The three-dimensional structures were saved as .mol2 files. Protein structures were acquired from the Protein Data Bank, selecting crystal structures with higher resolution for molecular docking. Structures were preprocessed using PyMOL 2.6 to remove phosphate groups and H2O molecules. The resulting structure was saved in the PDB format. The small-molecule ligand and protein were prepared via AutoDock 1.5.6. Hydrogen atoms were attached to the protein, while the ligand was hydrogenated, and its rotatable bonds were assigned. Docking box coordinates were defined. Molecular docking was conducted via AutoDock Vina to analyze the ligand–protein interactions. The optimal docking conformation for molecular simulations was chosen based on a comparison of the docking scores. The docking data were finally analyzed via PyMOL 2.6 and Discovery Studio 2019, generating both 3D analysis plots and 2D interaction diagrams illustrating the interactions between the key residues and compounds.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) was conducted by using GROMACS 2022. The force field (FF) parameters were determined using pdb 2gmx. The topology file for the ligand based on the GAFF2 FF was generated via sobtop_1.0 (dev3.1), while atomic charges of the ligand were obtained using the RESP approach to ensure that the charge distribution conformed to physicochemical characteristics. The AMBER14SB FF was utilized for the receptor proteins. The system was solvated during the simulation through the TIP3P water model inside a cubic water box with an edge length of 1 nm to guarantee electrical neutrality and adequate solvation. To ensure system neutrality, Cl– and Na+ ions were introduced using gmx genion in GROMACS at 0.15 M NaCl. Long-range electrostatic forces were visualized via the particle mesh Ewald approach, with cutoff distances equal to 1 nm. Optimization of the PME settings and FF parameters followed the GROMACS guidelines. Bond length restrictions were imposed through an LINCS algorithm.

Energy minimization was undertaken using 3000 and 2000 steps, respectively, of steepest descent and conjugate gradient optimization. It was completed in 3 stages. To begin with, the solute was constrained while the energy of the H2O molecules was minimized. Counterions were then constrained and energy-minimized, followed by minimization of the overall system without any constraints. The pressure was kept at 1 bar during the production run utilizing the Parrinello–Rahman barostat, while the temperature was kept at a value equal to 310 K via the Nosé–Hoover thermostat. The simulation was executed under an NPT (isothermal–isobaric) ensemble for 100 ns with an integration time step of 2 fs.

The following GROMACS tools were employed during the simulation: gmx rmsf, gmx rmsd, gmx gyrate, and gmx sasa, which were utilized to determine the root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), respectively. The complex binding free energy was estimated using the MM-PBSA approach by utilizing the g-mmpbsa software in GROMACS. These analyses were carried out to assess the structural changes, solvent effects, and system stability.

Results and Discussion

Wangzoside A (1) was a colorless gum with molecular formula (MF) C35H48O11, assigned from the HRESIMS ion signal [M – H]−at an m/z value of 643.3121 (calculated as 643.3112). According to the 1H NMR profile (refer to Table ), 1 contained five methyl signals at δH 1.40 (s), 0.99 (s), 0.94 (s), 0.73 (d, J = 8.0 Hz), and 0.63 (d, J = 8.0 Hz), a pair of olefinic proton signals δH 7.52 (d, J = 15.0 Hz) and 6.29 (d, J = 15.0 Hz), and a set of para-substituted benzene ring proton signals δH 7.44 (d, J = 7.5 Hz) and 6.82 (d, J = 7.5 Hz). The 13C NMR displayed 35 carbon resonances (Table ), including one ketone carbon signal, one ester carbonyl carbon signal, and 10 benzene ring or olefinic carbons. Careful evaluation of 1H–1H COSY and 1H, 13C NMR profiles led to the conclusion that 1 contained a glucose moiety, as evidenced by the 7 proton signals at δH 3.18–4.80 and 6 carbon signals at δC 103.0, 75.8, 76.8, 72.1, 78.2, and 62.9, respectively. Furthermore, analysis of the 13C and 1H spectroscopic results combined with the HSQC and HMBC spectral analysis (Figure ) established the 2″E-p-coumaroyl group, which was constructed based on spin–spin coupling and HMBC correlations from C-9″ and C-4″, H-3′’ to C-1″, and from H-6″ to C-4″ and C-7″, from the 1H–1H COSY spectra. Upon exclusion of the glucose-derived resonances, the 13C NMR profile of 1 revealed 20 carbon peaks, indicative of a diterpene skeleton. Key features included a ketone carbonyl (δC 203.7), an olefinic bond (δC 164.1, 131.0), two oxygenated methine carbons (δC 81.8, 66.9), and one quaternary carbon (δC 73.3). Compound 1 was therefore identified as an abietane-type diterpenoid glycoside.

The planar structure of 1 was built via HMBC analysis (Figure ). Correlations from H3-20 to C-1, C-10, and C-9, together with H-5 to C-10 and H-11 to C-8, and from H-15, H-14, and H-16 to C-13 demonstrated hydroxyl groups at C-11, C-1, and C-13, respectively. Associations from H-20 to C-9, H-5 to C-7, and H-6 to C-8 established the α,β-unsaturated-ketone moiety at C-7-C-8-C-9. Furthermore, H-1 to C-1′ and H-1′ to C-1 showed attachment of the glucose at C-1, while the correlation from H-2′ to C-1″ verified the placement of the coumaroyl group. The relative configuration (RC) of the aglycone of 1 was determined from NOESY spectra and spin–spin coupling. The β-configuration of the glucose moiety and the trans-oriented C-2’’/3′’ double bond was identified based on the J VIC value of H-1 and H-2’’/H-3′’. Moreover, the NOESY correspondences from H-1 to H-5, H-5 to H3-18, H3-19, and H3-20 indicated β-orientation of the hydroxyl at C-1. The NOESY correlations from H-15 to H-11 and H-11 to H3-20 revealed an 11-α hydroxyl group (Figure ). The ECD spectrum was obtained employing the TDDFT approach at the B3LYP/6-311G­(d,p) level, thereby establishing the AC of the stereogenic centers (Figure ). Accordingly, the AC of aglycone of 1 was found to be 1R, 5S, 10S, 11R, and 13R. These confirmed the presented structure of 1.

Wangzoside B (2) was also isolated as a colorless gum. The molecular formula C35H48O11 was also deduced from the HRESIMS ion peak [M – H]− 643.3122 (calculated as 643.3112). A pure sample of compound 2 was initially obtained during the separation process, and its purity was verified through 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectroscopies (Table ) and HPLC analysis. Based on the signals found in the one-dimensional NMR spectrum of this compound, it was concluded that its structure is highly similar to that of Compound 1. Owing to constraints in NMR testing conditions, the two-dimensional NMR analysis of this compound was conducted nearly 1 month later. At that time, it was observed that the compound had transformed into a mixture of 1 and 2. Due to the limited quantity of the compound, repurification of compound 2 was not possible. Consequently, the spectral data of the mixture were analyzed, resulting in determination of the structure. During storage, the α,β-unsaturated ketone moiety between C-8 and C-11 together with the 7-OH group in 2 was transformed, resulting in the formation of an α,β-unsaturated ketone between C-7 and C-9 and an 11-OH group in compound 1. To further elucidate the interconversion between these two compounds, compound 1 was dissolved and stored in a clear glass vial without protection from light. Trace amounts of compound 2 were detected after 1 day. Because the NOESY spectrum of 2 did not allow for RC establishment of the relative configuration of the newly formed 7-OH group, the structural elucidation of this compound is presented in Figure .

Wangzoside C (3) was separated as a colorless gum, and its molecular formula was found to be C26H42O9, as shown by the quasi-molecular ion signal at a m/z value equal to 533.2521 [M + Cl]− (calculated as 533.2512) in its negative-ion HRESIMS spectrum. The 13C and 1H NMR profiles (Table ) closely resembled those of 1 as a diterpene glycoside, apart from the lack of additional coumaroyl group signals. Further examination of HMBC, HSQC, and 1H–1H COSY profiles established the planar structure of 3. Specifically, HMBC correspondences from H-1 to C-20 and from H3-20 to C-1 suggested attachment of the glucose at C-1. Correlations from H-15 to C-12, H3-16/H3-17 to C-13, and H-12 to C-9 verified the existence of dihydroxyl groups at C-13 and C-12. The relative configuration of 3 was determined through the NOESY correspondences, specifically those from H3-20 to H3-19 and H-1 to H-5, H-5 to H3-18, which revealed the β-oriented 1-OH. Meanwhile, the NOESY correlations from H-12 to H-15 and from H-12 to H-20 revealed α-oriented 12-OH and 13-OH (Figure ). The absolute configuration was established by a DFT-based ECD plot. The absolute configuration was found to be 1R, 5S, 10S, 12S, and 13S, as indicated by comparisons of the computed and experimental ECD profiles (Figure ).

Wangzoside E (5), also isolated as a colorless gum, had a molecular formula of C26H42O8 according to the HRESIMS ion peak [M-H]− at an m/z value equal to 481.2809 (calculated as 481.2795). The 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopic results (Table ) indicated that 5 was a diterpene glycoside. The 1H NMR spectra revealed two olefinic proton signals δH 5.85 (d, J = 6.5 Hz) and 5.40 (d, J = 5.5 Hz); three singlet methyl signals δH 1.05, 0.93, and 0.91; and 2 doublet methyl signals δH 0.95 (d, J = 7.5 Hz) and 0.92 (d, J = 7.5 Hz). The 13C NMR spectrum demonstrated 26 carbon signals, including four olefinic carbon resonances, three oxygenated carbons, and a glucose moiety. Further investigation using two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (2D NMR) techniques revealed that the glycosylation site and the type of sugar moiety are identical to those of compound 3. The only structural difference is in the functional groups on the aglycone. A comprehensive assessment of the HMBC profile indicated that the HMBC correlation of H3-20 to C-8, H-5 to C-7, H-6 to C-7, and H-14 to C-7 and C-8 established a hydroxyl group at C-14 and a double bond at C-7/C-8. Additionally, the HMBC correspondences from H-11 to C-13 demonstrated a hydroxyl at C-13. The relative configuration of 5 was determined from the NOESY spectral analysis (Figure ), with correspondences of H-1 to H3-20, H3-20 to H3-19, and H3-18 to H-5, indicating a 1β–OH. Meanwhile, the H-14/H-15 correlation indicated that the 13-OH and 14-OH groups are cofacial. The glycosidic moieties of all compounds were characterized via acid hydrolysis, thin-layer chromatography (TLC), and HPLC analysis, confirming the consistent presence of β-d-glucose as the sole sugar component.

Given that this plant is traditionally consumed as a herbal tea owing to its hypoglycemic properties, the isolated diterpene glycosides were subjected to screening for α-glucosidase inhibitory activity. With the exception of compound 2, which was excluded due to insufficient quantity and structural instability, all remaining compounds were assessed for their biological activity. The screening results indicated that all evaluated compounds possessed potential inhibitory properties ( Table ). Compound 4 displayed the highest inhibitory activity among all the compounds, with an IC50 of 3.60 ± 0.75 μM.

Interactions between all tested compounds and α-glucosidase (PDB ID: 3A4A) were evaluated using molecular docking. The cocrystallized ligand from 3A4A pdb was utilized to analyze the active pocket and key binding residues; the docking grid was validated by redocking with an RMSD of 0.717 Å, confirming the reliability of the protocol. As illustrated in Figure , all compounds were securely docked near the allosteric site of α-glucosidase, indicating binding energies all below −8.0 kcal/mol. Specifically, 1 displayed the strongest binding affinity with a binding free energy equal to −10.1 kcal/mol, forming H-bonds with SER240, GLN279, GLU411, TYR158, and ASN415 as well as hydrophobic interactions with ARG315, PHE178, PHE159, and VAL216; 3 exhibited a binding energy equal to −9.0 kcal/mol, with H-bonds to ASP307, GLN279, and PRO312 and hydrophobic interactions with ARG315, HIS280, LYS156, and TYR158; 4 had a binding energy equal to −8.9 kcal/mol, forming H-bonds with ARG315, TYR158, and GLU411 and hydrophobic interactions with PHE314, LYS156, and HIS280; 5 demonstrated a binding energy equal to −8.6 kcal/mol, with H-bonds to GLN279, TYR158, and GLU411 and hydrophobic interactions with HIS280, ARG315, PHE314, and LYS156; acarbose demonstrated a binding energy equal to −7.8 kcal/mol, developing H-bonds with SER311, PRO311, ASP307, SER240, LYS156, TYR158, and HIS280 and a carbon–hydrogen bond with PHE314. All test ligands showed stronger binding affinity than acarbose and stabilized the complex, primarily via hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding within the active pocket of 3A4A.

The aforementioned molecular docking analysis revealed significant interactions between both the aglycone and sugar moieties with the enzyme molecule. Therefore, it is suggested that both the aglycone and the sugar group of this class of compounds contribute importantly to biological activity.

Furthermore, MD simulations were carried out to elucidate the interactions between 4 and α-glucosidase further. The value of RMSD was utilized to evaluate the conformational stability. As illustrated in Figure A, the complex system achieved equilibrium following 85 ns, with overall fluctuations of approximately 2.3 Å, indicating that the small molecule binds with high stability. The value of Rg, indicative of the compactness of a protein, exhibited only minor fluctuations throughout the simulation (Figure B), indicating that the protein structure remained well-packed during dynamic adjustments. The value of SASA, which is an indicator of protein surface exposure, showed slight variations (Figure C), demonstrating that ligand binding moderately influences the local microenvironment. Finally, the value of RMSF, which provides a measure of individual residue flexibility, remained mostly below 3 Å (Figure D), reflecting limited conformational freedom and high overall structural stability.

After that, the binding free energy between 4 and α-glucosidase was determined via the MM/PBSA approach based on the equilibrated binding conformation (Figure E). The complex system displayed a binding free energy equal to −4.46 kcal/mol, consistent with favorable affinity for the protein. Furthermore, the per-residue energy contribution was analyzed to identify key amino acids involved in ligand binding. The results indicated that residues ARG263, HIS295, ASN259, ALA292, GLU271, LYS13, and TRP15 exhibited high contribution values (Figure F), suggesting their potential importance in the catalytic mechanism. The Gibbs FEL plots (see Figure G,H) indicated densely concentrated blue regions, exhibiting the energetically preferred and highly stable binding interactions between 4 and α-glucosidase. In summary, the complex system displayed low RMSD values, further confirming favorable binding between 4 and the target protein.

Conclusion

In this work, a DeepSAT + SMART-driven technology was utilized to target-mine the diterpene glycosides from the rhizomes of I. suzhouensis, which were cultivated in Suzhou city. Five previously unreported diterpene glycosides were isolated and elucidated. Their structures were examined via detailed chemical and spectroscopic analyses. The bioassay tests showed that 4 had significant inhibitory activity against the α-glucosidase enzyme, which was further confirmed by molecular docking and MD simulations.

Diterpenoids are well established as characteristic metabolites of Isodon plants and have been extensively studied. Yet, diterpene glycosides constitute a rare subgroup within this genus, making them a compelling target for further investigation. Collectively, these results further elucidate the diversity of Isodon metabolites and provide valuable insights for developing novel lead compounds for treating diabetes, while further research on their bioavailability and absorption is warranted.
