Intrathecal antibody synthesis – Reiber method revisited
Authors: John J Halperin, Dane Granger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCSF immunoglobulin (IgG) often informs diagnosis in CNS inflammatory disease– qualitatively (oligoclonal bands; OCBs), or quantitatively (CSF: serum IgG: albumin, e.g. IgG index). Most US laboratories estimate excess CSF IgG using linear models, most in Europe, Reiber’s hyperbolic model - the latter often also incorporated into CSF pathogen-specific immunoreactivity calculations. We compared Reiber hyperbolic and linear approaches.MethodsRequired data were available for 1,872 samples, Lyme-specific data for 1,365. We compared linear and Reiber’s hyperbolic models to determine which better describes normal CSF: serum IgG/albumin, assessed correlations with OCBs using ROC analysis and compared Lyme-specific antibody indices (AI) by both methods.ResultsOn linear regression (CSF: serum albumin vs. CSF: serum IgG) in 890 normals, r 0.98; 0.86 on polynomial. In 982 abnormals r was 0.95 by both linear and polynomial regression. Linear regression slope was 74% greater in abnormals than normals. We tested the linearity of the Reiber hyperbolic model and found it to be essentially linear in the clinically relevant range.Dichotomizing the full dataset by OCBs, IgG index ROC AUC was 0.81, Reiber 0.74 (p<0.001). Linear models consistently demonstrated stronger associations with OCBs than the Reiber method. IgG index and IgG synthesis cutoffs from ROC analyses differed substantially from, and were more robust than, customary ones.On Lyme AI analysis, Reiber modification was potentially applicable in just 14.7% of samples, possibly changing the diagnostic conclusion in just 0.1%.ConclusionsLinear models more accurately reflect intra-CNS inflammation, including a novel linear regression model. Notably, we found that, despite its equation’s complexity, the Reiber hyperbolic model is also linear in the clinically relevant range. In CNS inflammation, local antibody production and altered BBB/BCB permeability both contribute to excess CSF IgG. ROC-derived cutoffs for IgG index and IgG synthesis more accurately reflect CSF immunoreactivity. Reiber QLimit substitution in the Lyme-specific AI denominator did not improve accuracy.

Background

Measurement of antibody synthesized within the central nervous system (CNS) – intrathecal antibody (ITAb) – has long played a role in the diagnosis of CNS inflammatory disorders [1]. Since normally minimal albumin or IgG are present in the CNS, any that is present – in the uninflamed state – must have crossed the blood brain and blood CSF barriers (BBB, BCB), a process dictated by their relative molecular weights, sizes, and lipid solubility. Several methods (Table 1) are used to assess increased CSF IgG – ranging from the oldest and simplest, IgG index (CSF: serum IgG: albumin) [1] to Tourtellote’s IgG synthesis rate [2] to the complex Reiber methodology [3], widely used in Europe – the latter based on a hyperbolic model of the relationship between albumin-concentration-normalized CSF and serum IgG concentrations.

With development of more sensitive immunodiagnostics, two approaches have come to largely supplant these older quantitative immunoglobulin-based methods in the diagnosis of MS [4]. Qualitative detection of oligoclonal bands (OCB) present in CSF but not serum provides greater sensitivity and specificity than quantitative immunoglobulin methods, replacing them in the recently updated MS diagnostic criteria [4]. Similarly, detection of kappa free light chains (kFLC) in CSF has been shown to provide sensitivity and specificity superior to total immunoglobulin measurement, with accuracy comparable to OCB detection [5–7].

Despite this, CSF immunoglobulin quantitation continues to be used diagnostically. Although no longer a diagnostic criterion for MS it can be informative in patients with CNS inflammation – being particularly diagnostically important in several CNS infections in which organism detection is problematic. However, it can also be informative in other neuro-inflammatory disorders precisely because it does not overlap entirely with OCB detection, sometimes providing evidence of CNS inflammation when other measures do not.

Several different approaches have been used to quantify intrathecal production of antibodies –with debate regarding their relative accuracy. Notably, the same debate is currently ongoing regarding assessment of CSF kFLC – whether to consider this as the ratio of CSF: serum kFLCs, a CSF: serum index normalized to QAlbumin [8], or some other method. Notably, the model of a QAlbumin-normalized kFLC index – analogous to the IgG index – is recommended in the recent update to the McDonald criteria [4].

Whether used to determine pathogen-specific or primarily immune mediated CSF antibody increases, assessing ITAb accurately is clearly essential. Available approaches differ along 3 dimensions. Some (IgG index) use a ratio measure, others a difference (IgG synthesis, the Reiber measure). Most use a linear model; Reiber, like the less used Auer model [9], is non-linear. Each incorporates constants derived from analysis of normative data gathered in different ways at different times.

The role of ITAb measurement in diagnosing CNS infections derives from the very reasonable assumption that different antibodies of the same class cross the BBB and BCB barriers similarly such that, absent immune stimulation within the CNS, the fraction of CSF antibodies specific to a given organism should be comparable to that in blood. Many CNS infections elicit migration of antigen-specific immune cells into CSF, particularly antibody-producing B cells, which then proliferate, producing pathogen-specific antibodies. Comparing the fraction of specific to total antibodies in CSF and serum – the antibody index (AI) - has proven useful in differentiating among intrathecal antibodies passively entering from blood, those non-specifically entering due to broadly increased BBB/BCB permeability, those stimulated non-specifically and those arising specifically in response to a local infection – the last being particularly relevant in the diagnosis of Lyme neuroborreliosis [10].

Of the methods used to determine AI (Table 1), the conceptually simplest measures the CSF: serum ratio of specific antibody –by direct ELISA or comparable technique – after diluting CSF and serum to the same final IgG concentration [11] , or by capture ELISAs [12, 13]. Other laboratories use serial dilution of samples followed by ELISAs, then compare the CSF: serum ratio of specific antibody to that of total antibody of that class, deriving a CSF: serum specific AI. In LNB, ratios above 1.3 (using the first technique, see Methods) or 1.5 (more broadly) are interpreted as indicative of disproportionate intrathecal synthesis of that specific antibody, in appropriate circumstances presumed to provide evidence of the presence of the responsible pathogen in the CNS – past or present. The Reiber method [14] similarly compares the ratios in the 2 fluids but additionally, when CSF IgG exceeds the upper limit predicted by the hyperbolic equation, replaces the AI denominator - the measured CSF: serum total IgG ratio - with the lower, model-predicted value, QLimit – based on the reasonable assumption that if the increased ITAb is attributable to the specific infection, using the higher unadjusted number would inappropriately lower the AI, decreasing the estimate of specific antibody synthesis.

Given that considerable debate surrounds the choice of approach - with strong opinions [15] but little comparative data – and since we have access to large numbers of samples tested for LNB ITAb, and our laboratories use 2 different approaches, we chose to collaborate and compare the different methods.

Our goals were to test 3 inter-related questions – 2 related to CSF immunoglobulin quantitation generally, one to determination of pathogen-specific antibody:

All studies were performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Demographics

Cohort 1

From 1987 to 2005, with IRB approval and patient consent, 1 author (JH), prospectively collected CSF and serum samples, and laboratory and clinical data, on all consenting patients in his practice in whom lumbar punctures were clinically indicated - a group skewed towards suspected neuro-inflammatory or neuro-infectious disorders. CSF and serum IgG and albumin, oligoclonal bands (OCB) and Lyme AI were measured in all [16].

Cohort 2

Anonymized CSF and serum data were collected for all patients undergoing clinically indicated lumbar punctures at the Mayo Clinic Health System between April 2024 and April 2025 and in whom CSF and serum IgG and albumin were assessed. CSF cell count and oligoclonal bands were available in a subset; data included in each analysis are indicated in those analyses. Available data included demographics but not clinical diagnoses.

Cohort 3

All patients undergoing CSF exam at Atlantic Health between August 2018 and September 2024 were identified from the electronic health record (Epic®). Anonymized laboratory data already obtained for clinical purposes were abstracted on all having measured CSF and serum IgG and albumin. Available data included demographics but neither Lyme-specific data nor clinical diagnoses.

Sample selection

All samples having the required data and meeting normal criteria (Tables 1 and 2) [3] were included in the initial analysis. A second analysis was then performed using all abnormal samples having the necessary data.

Techniques

Standard commercial laboratory techniques were used to measure CSF and serum immunoglobulin, albumin and OCBs. Pathogen-specific AI [(CSF: serum specific antibody)/(CSF: serum total IgG)] was determined using 2 different methodologies.

In Cohort 1, performed at the SUSB laboratory, using previously published methods, CSF and serum IgG were measured, both fluids diluted to the same final sample IgG concentration and standard ELISAs for anti-B burgdorferi-immunoreactivity performed simultaneously on both [16]. ELISAs were performed in a range where the ratio of optical density (OD) to IgG concentration was linear. Since diluted samples had the same IgG concentrations, the OD ratio was used as the AI. Samples were considered positive if either the serum or CSF ELISA OD exceeded the positive cut-off (3 standard deviations above the mean of normals), AND AI > 1.3, with AI values between 0.9 and 1.3 considered borderline, based on run-to-run assay variability [16].In Cohort 2, testing was performed at the Mayo central laboratory, using Reiber methodology. Paired serum and CSF samples were tested by a semi-quantitative (6-point calibration curve in relative units) anti-Borrelia IgG ELISA at initial dilutions of CSF 1:2 and serum 1:404, with additional serial dilutions performed to yield concentration values for both specimen sources in the same region of the analytical measuring curve. For each sample, the measured anti-borrelia IgG concentration was multiplied by the dilution factor. These values were then combined with measured CSF and serum IgG concentrations to calculate the ratio of CSF to serum concentrations of specific antibody (IgGspecific,CSF/IgGspecific, serum), termed Qspecific. This ratio was then compared to the corresponding one for total antibody, (IgGtotal,CSF/IgGtotal, serum), termed QIgG. The quotient Qspecific/QIgG constitutes the antibody index, AI, with AI > 1.5 considered elevated. Importantly the Reiber methodology uses a hyperbolic relationship (Table 1) between QIgG and QAlbumin (the albumin ratio, AlbuminCSF/Albuminserum), to calculate an upper limit of expected QIgG for any given QAlbumin, termed QLimit or QLimit, Reiber. When the measured QIgG exceeds this QLimit, QLimit replaces QIgG in the AI calculation’s denominator.

Statistics

Analyses were performed with StatPlus: mac Pro 8.0.4. (AnalyticSoft Inc). Continuous variables are generally described as means with 99% confidence intervals (CI)(exceptions noted in Tables). The relationship between QIgG and QAlbumin was tested using both linear and polynomial regressions. Receiver operator characteristic (ROC) calculations were performed dichotomizing data by presence or absence of OCBs.

Analyses

For each cohort, linear regressions, with 99% CIs, were performed on samples judged normal (criteria in Table 1), with dependent variable QIgG, independent QAlbumin. When the confidence interval for the intercept was found to include 0 in all 3 cohorts, analyses were repeated forcing the intercept to 0. When results in the 3 cohorts were found to be comparable in both the original analyses and with intercept forced to 0, data were pooled and linear and polynomial regressions performed on pooled data and compared. ROC curves were then constructed for both IgG index and (QIgG -QLimit) dichotomized by the presence or absence of OCBs.

When the IgG index was found to better correlate with OCBs than the Reiber method, an alternative linear model was tested to determine the extent to which the difference was related to a linear vs. non-linear approach. QLinear was calculated as QAlbumin x the upper 99%CI limit of the linear regression slope in normals (0.488) (Tables 1 and 3). (QIgG- QLinear) was then determined for all normal samples – an approach combining Reiber’s difference calculation with a linear equation. Data were then dichotomized by the presence or absence of OCBs. To further test the role of linear vs. nonlinear approaches, the same analysis was then repeated with a third linear model, Tourtelotte’s IgG synthesis calculation [2].

To test whether the different conclusions with the Reiber approach were due to its hyperbolic equation or the different incorporated constants, its non-linearity was tested by plotting both (0.93 x sqrt([Qalbumin]2+6 × 10− 6)-1.7 × 10− 3) and the comparable IgG index value (as 0.7 x QAlbumin) against hypothetical continuous values of QAlbumin from 0 to 0.160 (upper limit of normal QAlbumin being 0.008).

This analysis was then extended to determine the ranges over which Reiber equations for QIgG, QIgA and QIgM. vary from linearity. For each immunoglobulin class, IgX, QIgX was plotted against QAlbumin. As the non-linearity arises because of the addition of a quite small, immunoglobulin-class-specific constant to QAlbumin2 within the square root calculation, the point at which this effect becomes meaningful was estimated. For each, the mean slope of QIgX vs. QAlbumin and standard deviation were determined. For each IgX, the point at which the local slope differed from the (mean-2SD) was identified.

We then tested the Reiber approach to determine pathogen-specific AI, in which QLimit is first used to determine if QIgG (ratio of CSF: serum total IgG) is increased. Whenever QIgG exceeds QLimit, QLimit is substituted for QIgG in the AI denominator. To assess the relationship between B burgdorferi-specific AIs and overall ITAb, and the effect of substituting QLimit for QIgG in the AI denominator, we reviewed data on all patients in cohorts 1 and 2 for whom CSF and serum IgG, albumin and Lyme ELISA data were available. After identifying the subset in which QIgG > QLimit, AIs were recalculated in these, with and without Qlimit substitution. Since original AIs in Cohort 1 were calculated as Qspecific/QIgG (without QLimit substitution) these were multiplied by QIgG/QLimit. In the subset of Cohort 2 samples in which the original AI was calculated as Qspecific/QLimit the reverse was done, multiplying by QLimit/ QIgG. We then identified those in both cohorts where this substitution changed the conclusion that specific intrathecal synthesis of anti- B burgdorferi-antibody was or was not present.

Results

As detailed in Table 2, in each cohort, patients with complete data closely resembled the overall group demographically. In each cohort, there was a modest female predominance. Cohort 1 was the youngest, Cohort 2 the oldest. Cohort 1 had the greatest number of normal results.

On linear regression among normal samples (Table 3), QIgG and QAlbumin were strongly correlated, with statistically indistinguishable coefficients among the 3 cohorts. In each cohort, the CI of the intercept (a) included 0. Repeating the regression forcing the intercept to 0 increased r to 0.99 in each of the 3 cohorts. When data from the 3 groups were pooled, r was identical with polynomial and linear regressions except that when the intercept was forced to 0 on linear regression, r increased from 0.86 to 0.98, vs. 0.86 on polynomial analysis. Notably when the analysis was repeated among samples judged abnormal (Table 3) data again were strongly linearly correlated (r 0.95) but the slope (b) of the relationship between QIgG and QAlbumin was 74% greater among abnormal than normal samples – and the intercept in abnormals, now slightly negative, no longer included 0.

Given the results of linear and polynomial regressions, we assessed where divergence between the models might occur. Using the Reiber equation and a derived IgG index equation (QIgG=0.7 x QAlbumin) we calculated both Reiber and IgG index-derived values for hypothetical values of QAlbumin from 0 to 0.160 (normal < 0.008; our highest observed 0.130) (Fig. 1). When the graph of the Reiber-derived results appeared to be as linear as the other, and the correlation coefficient r comparing the 2 models was approximately 1.0, we expanded the scale of the Reiber equation-derived graphs to more closely inspect results at low QAlbumin, where deviation from linearity would be expected (Fig. 2). The statistical analysis –consistent with visual inspection of the graphs - indicates the QIgG:QAlbumin relationship is essentially linear by Reiber analysis for QAlbumin > = 0.005 – i.e. most normal and all above-normal values. For QIgA:QAlbumin (Fig. 2), the relationship is essentially linear with QAlbumin > = 0.007, just below the upper limit of normal – i.e. this appears linear for some normals and all samples with elevated QAlbumin. For QIgM this only occurs at QAlbumin 0.013 – i.e. calculated results would be non-linear both for normals and some abnormals. Notably the equations also suggest that for very low QAlbumin – less than the small constants included in the equations – the predicted QIgX would be entirely driven by the constant, largely independent of QAlbumin., suggesting the presence of baseline intrathecal antibody production in the absence of inflammation.

Finally, we assessed the effect of QLimit substitution in the calculation of the B burgdorferi AI (Table 5). QIgG exceeded QLimit in only 14.7% of samples overall. Since substitution decreases the AI denominator and therefore can only increase the calculated AI, substitution is irrelevant in patients in whom AI is already positive (2.7%). In the 48 remaining Cohort 2 patients where substitution was performed it changed 2 results from borderline to positive, although in both this seemed to be the result of low serum albumin with a resultant increased QAlbumin, with a normal IgG index in both, suggesting limited ITAb overall. Overall, the Reiber approach changed the conclusion – and that questionably - in just 2 of 1,365 samples.

Discussion

Quantitation of intra-CNS antibody, often important in diagnosis of neuro-inflammatory disease, is typically performed using one of several decades-old techniques [2, 3, 17, 18]. All are anchored both in theory and in normative data obtained prior to modern neuroimaging, from nominally normal CSF. Many of these “normal” samples were obtained at myelography or in patients with non-specific neurobehavioral changes – populations quite different from those undergoing lumbar punctures today. Spirited debates over relative strengths and weaknesses of these competing techniques notwithstanding, there is little comparative data to guide decisions about which method is most informative in complex neuro-immunologic disorders [7, 19, 20].

To address this, we first considered the ascertainment bias inherent in using clinically-obtained CSF results. Data are only available when treating physicians choose to obtain CSF and order relevant tests – fortunately, precisely the population where insights are most needed. To assess the impact of this bias, data were obtained from 3 large, independent databases, using data from every available sample in which the requisite datapoints were available. The remarkably congruous findings support the conclusion that they reflect underlying biologic principles in relevant patients, permitting pooling data to compare methods currently in use – in particular the simple IgG index and complex Reiber formulation.

The Reiber methodology, based on both empiric data and quite reasonable hypotheses, postulates a hyperbolic relationship between QIgG and QAlbumin. Unlike IgG index, it incorporates the difference between observed and predicted CSF QIgG rather than the ratio QIgG/QAlbumin. Several of our observations are inconsistent with the Reiber hyperbolic model. First, the correlation coefficient of 0.98 for a linear relationship in normals is considerably greater than 0.86 for a non-linear model. Second, a hyperbolic relationship would, by definition, predict values greater than those from a linear model at both low and high QAlbumin. In our 890 normal samples, using the same criteria of normality as Reiber, the linear regression equation intercepts 0 at low QAlbumin, inconsistent with a hyperbolic model. Third, the observed superiority of our novel model using QLinear, which like the Reiber method assesses the difference between observed and predicted values, but incorporates a simple linear relationship between QIgG and QAlbumin, suggests a non-linear model is problematic. Fourth, on ROC analysis, association with CSF OCBs, considered the best CSF marker of intra-CNS immune stimulation [21], was significantly better using the simpler IgG index and QLinear than the Reiber methodology.

Fifth, our findings are entirely consistent with those in a recent “big data” analysis [22], which concluded that, at least for IgG, there is “no difference in model performance measures between linear and non-linear models”. This led us to question the extent to which the Reiber approach actually is non-linear. Calculating values derived using the Reiber equation showed that, for IgG, it is essentially linear over the clinically relevant range, with non-linearity only in the rarely occurring, lowest values of QAlbumin. If, then, all commonly used models are essentially linear, it appears differences among the models are less conceptual but more related to the derived constants incorporated into the equations – constants reflective of the patient populations from which original normative data were obtained and likely quite different from patients undergoing CSF analysis today.

Having shown that simple, linear models apply in normal CSF, the greater challenge is to determine the best model in the presence of CNS inflammation. To address this, we used both regression and ROC analyses. For the latter we dichotomized samples by the presence or absence of OCBs – an approach driven by the absence of an alternative ‘gold standard’ but confounded by the known discordance between these quantitative and qualitative methods, with OCBs known to be more sensitive and specific.

The rationale for this choice began with the assumption that the lower sensitivity of quantitative methods could reflect conceptual, technical or specific-model-based issues. We believe our finding that all commonly used models find a robust linear relationship between QIgG and QAlbumin is reassuring that the problem is not conceptual. This is reinforced by recent studies showing the kFLC index – analogous to the IgG index - has sensitivity and specificity entirely comparable to OCBs [6]. Since kFLCs and immunoglobulins are produced by the same plasma cells in the same circumstances, the logical inference is that CSF kFLC and immunoglobulin concentrations should approximately parallel each other, with the observed lesser sensitivity of immunoglobulin quantitation likely attributable largely to “signal to noise” and/or model-based differences. More specifically, there undoubtedly are both patients with OCBs but an insufficient total increase in intrathecal IgG production to increase quantitative measures and others with substantial polyclonal increases in IgG but not OCBs. While there are methods to calculate a more specific antibody fraction [23] these are complex and generally only performed in research laboratories. The remaining third consideration then - and one that can be addressed – is the accuracy of the mathematical models used to identify quantitative increases in CSF immunoglobulin - precisely the question we chose to address.

Among the 982 abnormal samples, linear correlation was excellent, with the same r (0.95) for both linear and polynomial regressions, suggesting no advantage of a mathematically more complex model. Interestingly, the slope of the linear regression in abnormals was 74% greater than in normals, an increase presumably reflecting the presence of locally synthesized immunoglobulin. However, this remarkably strong linear correlation (r 0.95) between QIgG and QAlbumin in abnormal samples is challenging to explain. Presumably the autonomously produced specific IgG in the CNS would result in some uncoupling of these 2 quantities – with there being no obvious mechanism by which increased synthesis of relevant antibodies would induce a concomitant, if lower, increase in CSF albumin. Further, the amount of locally synthesized specific antibody is typically a fraction of total excess IgG, leaving undetermined the question of how much of the excess specific antibody is locally synthesized vs. excess that crosses from serum due to inflammation-related changes in BBB/BCB permeability. We suggest that the strong correlation and linearity of the relationship requires that a substantial part of this excess be due to altered barrier permeability related to intra-CNS inflammation, as is known to occur in many inflammatory states [24] allowing an increase in CSF albumin parallelling that of CSF IgG, but to a lesser degree than in normal CSF. It is interesting to compare our analysis to that performed by Reiber decades ago. One of the underlying observations is actually much the same. When moving from normal to abnormal samples, the slope of the QAlbumin vs. QIgG curve increases substantially. The Reiber approach appears to have considered this as part of a continuous process and therefore described the aggregate data with a single complex equation. Our observations suggest an altered underlying process in the presence of CNS inflammation, with induced changes in the mechanism of BBB/BCB permeability, consistent with current understanding [24].

Our observations on the relationships among the different quantitative measures of CNS inflammation and the presence of oligoclonal bands may be more broadly informative. First, we found that IgG index, our novel QLinear, and IgG synthesis all have considerably greater sensitivity than Reiber’s QLimit – if IgG index and IgG synthesis cutoffs are optimized for Youden J rather than for specificity. Notably, despite the greater specificity of QLimit, the combined measure of sensitivity and specificity, Youden J, favors the cutoff-adjusted IgG index or, absent any cutoff changes, QLinear. We then turned to the application of the Reiber approach to quantitation of pathogen-specific IgG in CSF – in which a calculated upper limit of serum-derived IgG, QLimit, is substituted in the AI denominator whenever QIgG> QLimit. The rationale for this was that whether CNS inflammation is present or not, the upper limit of IgG entering CSF passively from serum should mirror albumin’s, assuming the mechanisms of entry are the same. This substitution would then remove all antibody in excess of that from serum from the denominator in the AI calculation. However, even in the presence of a CNS pathogen, not all excess CSF IgG is pathogen-specific - with the proportion locally synthesized and specific varying widely [14, 25] – although this is not routinely quantified. Conceptually, though, subtracting the entire excess from the denominator, disregarding the part of the excess that is not pathogen-specific, will increase the AI in a manner that does not precisely reflect the limited contribution of specific antibody to that excess.

Both in MS and in CNS infections [14, 23, 26] there can be antibody increases – and low positive AIs – against irrelevant pathogens - a source of considerable confusion in the past [25, 27], for example previously thought to suggest a role of measles in MS’ pathogenesis. At one point this led the Reiber group to conclude [22] that actual quantitation of specific antibody, determining an antibody fraction, would be more accurate than an AI – an approach analogous to the capture assays still used in Denmark [13, 28] and for a time in the US [12] – without substitution of QLimit for QIgG. – an approach reinforced by Ruprecht and colleagues [25].

In the face of these concerns, our analysis of Lyme AI data from 1,365 patients found that the complex Reiber methodology conceptually could have provided a conclusion different from the simple approach in only 2 individuals (0.1% of our samples)- in both of whom it changed a borderline result to a positive – although in both, there were important confounding factors.

Limitations and open questions

Our data raise several important questions. First, the use of OCBs to validate quantitative immunoglobulin measurements is controversial. We hope our arguments at least in part address that. Second, the variations in the arithmetic constants incorporated into the various models raise questions about the need to periodically update normal values when the population being tested changes in substantive ways. More specifically, for IgG index and IgG synthesis – 2 long-used measures – our Youden J-based analysis suggests that different cutoff values would provide greater overall accuracy.

Further, our analysis suggests that the Reiber method’s substitution of QLimit for QIgG in the calculation of the antibody index in Lyme neuroborreliosis does not improve accuracy and we raise questions about its conceptual validity. That notwithstanding the method is used in diagnosis of other CNS infections. The extent to which our observations are relevant to these other infections remains to be determined. Finally, it would be of interest to update these observations incorporating quantitative data on kFLCs to validate the inferred relationship between them and total CSF immunoglobulins, and to test the observed relationships in patients with neuro-inflammatory disease.
