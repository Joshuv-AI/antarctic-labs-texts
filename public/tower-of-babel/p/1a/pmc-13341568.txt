Feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of mindfulness-based stress reduction program on patients with nocturnal hypertension: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shuqi Wang, Sze Nok Ng, Regina Wing Shan Sit, Chapman Cheuk Tung Ying, Shuk Yun Leung, Jinghao Han, Yue Kwan Choi, Kam Fai Chow, Wai Ho Chung, Benjamin Hon Kei Yip, Richard J. McManus, Samuel Yeung Shan Wong, Eric Kam Pui Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNocturnal blood pressure (BP) during sleep is a stronger predictor of cardiovascular events than daytime BP. This pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) aimed to assess feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) in managing nocturnal hypertension.MethodsEligible patients were randomized to either a generic MBSR program or usual care (1:1). Primary outcomes included recruitment rate, dropout rate, adherence to ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) and home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM), and MBSR participation. Secondary outcomes encompassed BP measurements from ABPM, mood symptoms, sleep quality, mindfulness levels, and safety at 8 weeks, with acceptability evaluated through interviews.ResultsAll eligible patients were recruited (76/113), with 13 participants recruited monthly and a retention rate of 100%. The 83.3% of MBSR participants attended at least six classes, and they engaged in home practices on 77% of days during the 8-week program. All participants completed ABPM at baseline and 8 weeks; however, only 55.3% reported adequate nocturnal HBPM readings. While MBSR significantly reduced nocturnal systolic BP (121.9 vs. 128.6 mmHg, P < 0.001), no significant differences were observed on other outcomes and no adverse events were detected. Patients noted benefits from the MBSR course, although HBPM and ABPM disrupted sleep for a minority.ConclusionThis is the first RCT to investigate the effect of mindfulness on nocturnal BP in a Chinese population. We confirm the feasibility of a definitive RCT regarding patient recruitment and retention, acceptability of the intervention (i.e., MBSR), and data collection (e.g., repeated ABPM). Although MBSR may reduce nocturnal systolic BP by 7 mmHg, this needs to be confirmed in a definitive RCT.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06510907

BACKGROUND

Hypertension (HT) is the most common chronic condition, affecting 30% of the adult population worldwide and is the leading cause of cardiovascular diseases and chronic kidney disease. HT therefore contributes significantly to morbidity and mortality [1]. Traditionally, the diagnosis and treatment of HT focused solely on daytime blood pressure (BP) taken while patients were awake [2]. However, nocturnal BP measured during patients’ sleep, which can be reliably assessed using 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) and specialized home blood pressure machines (HBPM), is consistently more predictive to cardiovascular outcomes and mortality than daytime BP [34]. Furthermore, non-dipping, as defined by a drop of BP of < 10% during sleep, is also an independent predictor of cardiovascular events and death [4]. Nocturnal HT is common, affecting approximately 60% of patients with HT, but is frequently under-detected and under-treated due to the limited use of ABPM and nocturnal HBPM in clinical practice [567]. Moreover, there is no standardized treatment for nocturnal HT, as evidenced by the lack of relevant recommendations in clinical practice guidelines [8]. Although it is common practice to adjust or add medications in the evening, strength of evidence supporting this approach is very weak [9]. Also, around 40% of HT patients have poor anti-HT medication adherence, due to concerns about side effects and the lifelong nature of treatment [10]. Clinically, achieving blood pressure targets often requires a combination of non-pharmacological and pharmacological treatments [8].

Mindfulness meditation training, which aims to enhance the ‘awareness that arises from paying attention on purpose, in the present moment non-judgmentally in the service of self-understanding, wisdom, and compassion,’ can be a potential novel non-pharmacological treatment for HT and nocturnal HT. In healthcare settings, mindfulness meditations are mostly taught in the format of a well-structured, group-based mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) program, which consists of at least 8 weekly sessions and daily meditation practices. MBSR can reduce BP in numerous ways. First, MBSR has been shown to reduce psychological stress and mood problems that are associated with elevated BP, nocturnal HT and cardiovascular diseases [1112]. At the physiological level, MBSR may reduce levels of inflammatory markers and sympathetic activities, thereby reducing BP [1314]. At the behavioural level, by cultivating self-awareness and being aware and open to lifestyle options, MBSR may enhance patients’ self-care abilities and adherence to HT treatment [15]. MBSR may be especially effective for nocturnal HT because nocturnal HT is associated with elevated sympathetic drive during sleep and poor sleep, both of which could be relieved by MBSR [1617]. Although other psychotherapies (e.g., cognitive behaviour therapy) may also reduce BP, MBSR is taught in standardized and group-based formats, which could lower the cost and be easily implemented in healthcare systems [18].

Our systematic review and meta-analysis showed that an 8-week MBSR can reduce office (therefore only daytime) systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) at post-intervention (8-week) by 6.64 and 2.47 mmHg, respectively, and that MBSR can also reduce stress and mood symptoms in patients with HT [18]. However, only a few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) used ABPM/HBPM and the effect of MBSR on out-of-office BP, including nocturnal BP, were conflicting and inconclusive (nocturnal SBP reduction: 1.37 mmHg; 95% confidence interval [CI], −4.3 to 7.05 mmHg and nocturnal DBP reduction: 0.22 mmHg; 95% CI, −6.55 to 6.98) [18]. Therefore, it remains unclear whether MBSR truly reduces BP or merely mitigates the white coat effect as MBSR participants may have reduced emotional reactivity during HT consultations. Despite our comprehensive searches, only 2 RCTs including a total of 129 participants reported the effect of MBSR on nocturnal BP; the results were conflicting with 1 RCT reported a significant reduction of asleep SBP of 6mmHg (n = 42) [1920]. This conflicting result can be due to, first, inadequate sample sizes of the RCTs. Second, the primary objective of these two RCTs was not to investigate treatment effects on nocturnal HT, thereby likely included HT patients without nocturnal HT [1920]. Furthermore, important covariates such as sleep quality and the presence of obstructive sleep apnoea (both of which affect nocturnal BP) were not controlled for [1920]. Our meta-analysis further highlighted the existing RCTs were unclear about their randomization process and under-reported safety data of MBSR [18]. To date, no published RCT has examined the effectiveness of MBSR in treating nocturnal HT.

Although we hypothesize that MBSR on top of usual care (intervention group), when compared with usual care (control group), can reduce nocturnal BP in patients with grade I daytime HT and nocturnal HT at 8-weeks (immediate post-intervention), a pilot RCT is essential for guiding the design of a future definitive RCT and enhancing its scientific rigor and value [21]. In line with recommendations in existing literature, the pilot study should assess the feasibility and acceptability of the intervention, evaluate the design and procedures, and help determine effect sizes for sample-size calculations [21]. Therefore, the primary outcome of this pilot RCT will include the rate of recruitment, rate of retention, acceptability of the RCT procedures and the intervention, adherence to MBSR and its “homework” and repeated nocturnal BP assessments (including ABPM and HBPM) in both groups. Assessment of feasibility of intervention and RCT procedures are important because, although MBSR has been utilized in RCTs involving Chinese patients with mood or psychiatric disorders, its acceptability among asymptomatic Chinese patients with only HT has not yet been examined. For secondary outcomes, we hypothesize that MBSR can reduce nocturnal, daytime and 24-hr SBP/DBP and that MBSR will also improve stress, mood symptoms, mindfulness level and self-care behaviours, and is safe in patients with HT. Nocturnal SBP/DBP, which will be the primary outcome of the future definitive RCT, has been assessed by the validated ABPM and HBPM methods according to international guidance [2].

METHODS

This was a 2-arm RCT involving a total of 76 patients with nocturnal HT and stage I daytime HT. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio into 2 groups: 1) the intervention group, which received an 8-week MBSR program in addition to standard care, and 2) the control group, which received only standard care. Due to the nature of the intervention, investigators and patients could not be blinded after group allocation; however, the statistician remains blinded to group allocation.

This RCT was registered at clinical trial registry (NCT06510907) on July 19, 2024 and was approved by the Joint CUHK-NTEC Clinical Research Ethics Committee (approval No. 2022.593). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All the procedures in this clinical trial were conducted as per the ethical principles of the Helsinki declaration of World Medical Association.

Participants

In total, 76 patients with HT were recruited by newspaper advertisements and from the publicly-funded general outpatient clinic in the Prince of Wales Hospital, where the primary care academic team is located. Patients with HT were recruited by posters, leaflets, case doctors, nurses (during annual check-ups in the Risk Assessment and Management Program [RAMP]), or by the research team when patients come to the clinic for follow-up. Hong Kong has a population of approximately 7 million, with about one-third of adults diagnosed with HT. Utilizing newspaper advertisements enabled recruitment from across the entire territory.

All patients received a 24-hour ABPM to confirm eligibility. Patients were included if they had 1) nocturnal HT (night-time SBP during sleep ≥ 120 mmHg) and stage I HT (daytime SBP = 135–159 mmHg), as detected by 24-hour ABPM [22]; 2) the sleep duration was defined by patients’ diary and no change of dose and type of anti-HT medication(s) in the prior 2 months.

Patients were not eligible if they met one of the following exclusion criteria, including 1) patients with atrial fibrillation (these patients have greater BP variability); 2) daytime office SBP ≥ 180 mmHg or DBP ≥ 120 mmHg (regardless of nocturnal BP) because these patients need urgent BP treatment and drug modification was not allowed during the RCT; 3) patients with known obstructive sleep apnoea because the night-time BP of these patients is predominately affected by the condition; 4) history of dementia or psychotic illnesses as modification of MBSR was required for these patients; 5) patients with end-stage malignancies; 6) nocturnal worker, because they have a reverse BP pattern to other participants; 7) patients receiving ≥ 3 BP medications representing resistant and possible secondary HT; 8) previous participation in structured mindfulness program(s) of ≥ 8 weeks or regular daily meditation practices; 9) reported sleep time of < 4 hours per day because these patients were unlikely to provide the minimum valid 7 BP readings during sleep; 10) patients who did not agree to keep the same dose of anti-HT medications during the trial period because drug changes would affect BP levels; and 11) patients receiving anti-coagulants because ABPM can induce significant bruises (due to repeated cuff inflations) in these patients.

Sample size

We use CIs approach to estimate sample size required to establish feasibility of this study, which is the primary objective. Using a 95% CI for the retention rate of this study, a margin of error of 0.05, a lower bound of this CI of 0.70 and an expected completion rate of 75%, which is a conservative assumption since the retention rate of studies involving MBSR and patients with HT was around 85%, the required sample for the pilot study would be at least 76 patients [18]. This calculation is based on a common formula for obtaining a 95% CI for a single proportion. The lower bound of CI 0.70 is based on the priori defined criterion for success, i.e., the main trial is “feasible” if the retention rate is ≥ 70%. This sample size was large enough to provide adequate data to calculate sample size for the main trial, as it is recommended that at least 70 patients will be needed in 2-arm pilot trials (each arm with 35 participants) for that purpose [23].

Intervention-MBSR

Mindfulness training was delivered through a generic 8-week MBSR. The MBSR consisted of weekly 2-hour classes, in which the techniques to various meditations (e.g., body scan, mindful awareness of breathing, body, sounds, thoughts, emotions, and mindful movements) were taught and discussed. Participants were asked to meditate for ≥ 40 minutes every day during the 8-week program as home practices. Besides cultivating attention control and a non-judgemental stance to experience, psychoeducation about psychological stress and stress response were discussed during the MBSR classes. The MBSR teacher discussed any difficulties encountered during home practice in every class to enhance adherence.

Each MBSR class had up to 25 participants. The 8-week program was taught by a qualified MBSR teacher who 1) finished the foundation training course for MBSR teachers, 2) had more than 2 years of experience conducting mindfulness training, 3) had personally engaged in mindfulness practice for more than 5 years, and 4) had attended a yearly mindfulness retreat in each of the last 5 years according to the UK Good Teacher Practice Guidelines.

Control group - usual care

Since all participants had sub-optimal daytime BP control (grade I HT), they were given an information sheet about lifestyle treatment for HT and were advised to adhere to these lifestyle changes at recruitment (to participants in both groups). There was no additional intervention provided to the control group. The case doctors and the patients in the control group were informed not to modify drug treatment before the ABPM at the 8-week endpoint. In Hong Kong, patients have unlimited access to publicly funded primary care clinics and emergency departments for any health problem. This is not limited to participants in the trial.

Primary outcome - feasibility and acceptability

The primary outcomes were the rate of recruitment (number of participants recruited per month), and the drop-out rate. In intervention arm, adherence to MBSR was assessed by the number of classes attended by the participants (out of a total of 8 classes) and adherence to home practice was assessed by patients’ diaries (number of days with any practice done). Furthermore, acceptability to repeated BP measurements were assessed by the percentage of patients who can repeat 2 ABPMs and who can measure nocturnal HBPM weekly (number of weeks with adequate [≥ 6 readings/week] nocturnal HBPM readings). The criteria to define feasibility is pre-determined and discussed in the Statistics section.

Acceptability was further assessed by randomly interviewing 20 patients (out of 36 patients) in the MBSR group by a trained research assistant to explore their experience concerning the intervention (MBSR) and trial design (repeated ABPM/HBPM, questionnaires, and/or blood/urine collection). The interview guide used for these assessments was developed by the research team (Supplementary Table 1).

Secondary outcomes

Blood pressure outcomes

These included 1) BP outcomes (ABPM/HBPM/office BP), 2) psychological outcomes (mood/stress symptoms, sleep quality, and mindfulness level), 3) behavioural changes (lifestyle/medication adherence), and 4) safety. Except for HBPM readings (refer to below), all outcomes were collected at baseline and at 8-week study endpoint.

24-Hour ABPM was conducted by using the validated Watch BP O3 AFIB ambulatory (Microlife, Widnau, Switzerland; https://www.stridebp.org/bp-monitors). ABPM was conducted in accordance with international recommendations, which included measurements taken on regular working days, using the patient’s non-dominant arm, and recording readings every 30 minutes. Actual sleep time was self-reported through a sleep diary.

Validated devices, the WatchBP home N (Microlife) and NightView HEM-9601T-E3 (Omron, Kyoto, Japan), were used to record weekly nocturnal HBPM throughout the 8-week period in both groups. The minimum number of night HBPM readings was 3 readings per night (2 a.m., 3 a.m., and 4 a.m.) for at least 2 nights [24]. This BP measuring algorithm was used in a nation-wide cohort in Japan and was found acceptable by Japanese patients [3]. Cut-off of elevated BP (≥ 135/85mmHg for daytime and ≥ 120/70mmHg for night-time) and non-dipping (a drop of SBP < 10% during sleep) will be defined according to international guidelines [222].

Automated office BP was measured by Watch BP office (model name) by Microlife (https://www.stridebp.org/bp-monitors).

Psychological outcomes

Mood/stress symptoms, sleep quality, and level of mindfulness, were measured by the validated 1) Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale-21 Questionnaire (DASS-21) [25], 2) Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [26], and 3) Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) [27], respectively. All these instruments were validated in Chinese populations and had excellent internal consistencies (from 0.8–0.83).

Behaviour outcomes

Adherence to non-pharmacological and pharmacological treatments were measured by the validated Treatment Adherence Questionnaire for Patients with Hypertension (TAQPH). The questionnaire consists of 6 domains that assess adherence to medication (9 items), diet (9 items), stimulation (e.g., alcohol/smoking, 3 items), weight control (2 items), exercise (2 items), and stress relief (3 items). Each item is rated by a 4-item Likert scale. Scores from each statement can be summated to provide scores for the domain sub-scales and the overall scale. A higher score signifies higher adherence to HT treatment. TAQPH has good internal reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 [28].

Safety

Any side effect was self-reported by participants in both groups. Data on hospitalization, serious adverse events, and death during the trial period were retrieved using the computerized system of the Hospital Authority.

Other data collection at baseline

Demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, education level, marital status, work status, comorbidities (such as diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, cardiovascular diseases [e.g., stroke, ischemic heart disease, arrhythmia, heart failure], and chronic kidney disease) were collected at baseline. Test results, including serum creatinine level, lipid levels (low-density lipoprotein, high-density lipoprotein, triglyceride, and total cholesterol), fasting glucose, and presence of microalbuminuria, were traced from the computerized medical system of the Hospital Authority. If these test results were > 3 months old, baseline blood/urine tests were repeated. Furthermore, body mass index (BMI) and details of HT medications (type, dosage, and timing of medication (morning/noon/evening) were collected at baseline and the 8-week endpoint (8 weeks).

Randomization

Age could moderate the effect of MBSR [18]. Furthermore, MBSR is effective in relieving mood disorders, which can influence BP levels. Therefore, stratified block randomization was employed to balance age (≥ 60 years or younger) and history of mood disorders (presence of depressive/anxiety disorders or not) between the 2 groups. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician using “Random Allocation Software,” printed, and sealed in light-opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes, which were only opened after a participant’s consent form was signed.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of covariance included BP values on ABPM (daytime/night-time/24-hour), BP values from nocturnal HBPM (from 8th week), BP values on automated office BP, medication/lifestyle adherence, DASS-21 scores, PSQI scores, FFMQ scores, and BMI at 8 weeks are the dependent outcomes and the baseline values and treatment group as covariates. The primary analysis was conducted using the intention-to-treat principle, while a sensitivity analysis was performed using the per-protocol principle, which included participants attending at least 80% of the MBSR classes. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. In the intention-to-treat analysis, the missing values at study endpoint (8-week) were replaced with last observed value (i.e., baseline data).

Data from the interviews were analysed using thematic analysis. All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and the transcripts were reviewed repeatedly to ensure familiarization with the content. Coding was then conducted, and related codes were organized into overarching themes. To enhance reliability, the coding was independently cross-checked by 2 researchers (S. Wang and C.C.T. Ying).

RESULTS

Participants characteristics

Recruitment and follow-up occurred between November 1, 2023 and December 31, 2024. Most participants were female (60.5%), retired (53.9%), had an education level of at least secondary school (98.6%), and were married (75.0%). The mean (± standard deviation) age was 63.1 ± 7.8 years. The mean nighttime BP, daytime BP and 24-hour BP were 129.4 ± 7.9/75.0 ± 7.6 mmHg, 143.4 ± 6.1/85.0 ± 7.8 mmHg, and 139.4 ± 5.9/81.9 ± 7.6 mmHg, respectively. Most participants had some sleep disturbance (71.1%), but only a minority had significant depressive (n = 1) and anxiety symptoms (23.7%) on DASS. Half of the patients (50%) have received anti-HT medications (Table 1). The pilot study completed as planned.

Primary outcome

Of the 113 patients assessed for eligibility, we recruited all 76 participants within six months, achieving a recruitment rate of approximately 13 participants per month. Most participants were recruited by newspaper advertisement (89%). One participant in the intervention group withdrew from MBSR but provided outcome measurements at 8-week, resulting in a retention rate of 100% (Fig. 1). Out of the 36 participants assigned to the MBSR group, the mean number of classes attended was 7 (out of 8), with 83.3% attending ≥ 6 classes. Among the 31 participants who submitted the home practice diary, they reported engaging in home practice on 77% of the days during the 8-week MBSR program.

All participants completed ABPM at baseline and at the 8-week. Although most participants (90.8%) provided nocturnal HBPM readings for ≥ 6 out of 8 weeks, only 55.3% reported adequate HBPM readings as recommended by the guidelines (i.e., ≥ 6 readings per week).

In patient interviews, 20 patients were interviewed and data saturation was reached. participants reported benefits from the MBSR course, such as improved stress management and emotional regulation. However, they faced difficulties with home practice due to distractions and time constraints. Although participants recognized the importance of HBPM and ABPM in monitoring and managing HT, some experienced discomfort while wearing these devices during sleep, which reduced both sleep quality and duration and thereby affected the accuracy of the readings. Most participants found the frequency of follow-up required by this RCT to be acceptable, although a few reported that it impacted their work (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

The current pilot RCT confirmed the feasibility and acceptability of conducting a definitive RCT to examine the effects of MBSR in treating nocturnal HT. Patients demonstrated a high level of acceptability toward MBSR and its home practice, as well as a high retention rate at 8 weeks. The recruitment rate was deemed adequate for the main RCT, which is expected to take 1 to 2.5 years to complete based on various sample size calculations (Table 4) that assume different minimal clinically meaningful BP differences and statistical power. Additionally, we found that patients had limited adherence to the frequent nocturnal HBPM (≥ 2 times per week for a total of 8 weeks). The recruitment rate in our pilot RCT was constrained by the availability of MBSR classes because we arranged only two consecutive 8-week MBSR classes, both held on Saturday afternoons. In the future, if the main RCT can offer concurrent MBSR classes at various times throughout the week, including weekday evenings, it will likely reduce scheduling conflicts with participants’ work and family commitments, potentially improving the recruitment rate.

The exploratory analyses demonstrated that MBSR reduced nocturnal SBP on ABPM by 6.7 mmHg (P < 0.001) and on HBPM by 6.4 mmHg (P = 0.01), but did not significantly lower daytime or 24-hour BP. Although the magnitude of nocturnal SBP reduction is potentially clinically relevant, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary given the pilot nature of the study. Only nocturnal BP was significantly reduced, whereas daytime and 24-hour averages were not. This may reflect insufficient statistical power for the latter comparisons, particularly if the variability in daytime and 24-hour BP was less favorable than that of nocturnal BP. Closer examination of our results revealed that all 24-hour and daytime SBP/DBP values, as well as the percentage of non-dippers, were lower in the MBSR group at the 8-week endpoint. These consistent directional trends potentially suggest a more generalized BP-lowering effect that this study was underpowered to confirm.

Alternatively, MBSR may preferentially affect nocturnal BP by enhancing sleep duration and quality, and by reducing sympathetic activation during sleep [1617]. In fact, Ponte Márquez et al. [20] also found that MBSR significantly reduced nocturnal SBP (n = 42, 108.73 ± 2.70 [MBSR] vs. 114.00 ± 2.99 [educational group]) and 24-hour SBP, but not daytime BP, in patients with high-normal and grade I HT. The only another published relevant RCT by Blom et al. [19] found no significant reduction in 24-hour (n = 101), daytime and nighttime BP measured by ABPM with MBSR compared to a waitlist control group in patients with mild daytime HT (e.g., nocturnal SBP: 123 ± 10.8/72 ± 6.9 [immediate MBSR] vs. 121 ± 11.4/70 ± 7.8 [waitlist control]). The reasons for these discrepancies are unclear, but they may relate to varying proportion of patients with nocturnal HT and differences in adherence to MBSR and its home practice. Furthermore, as noted in Table 4, all these RCTs including the current pilot study are underpowered, making them susceptible to both type I and type II errors. Since pilot RCTs are not intended to draw definitive conclusions about an intervention, future definitive RCTs should assess the effect of MBSR on 24-hour, daytime, nighttime and office BP.

In the current pilot RCT, although MBSR may reduce BP—particularly nocturnal SBP—the mechanisms underlying this potential BP reduction remain unclear. We collected data on sleep quality (PSQI), psychological symptoms (DASS-21), mindfulness (FFMQ), and treatment adherence (TAQPH); however, none of these outcomes showed significant changes in the present study. Noting that the exact mechanisms remain under investigation, several factors may account for this. First, as discussed above, the small sample size inherent to a pilot RCT increases the likelihood of type II error, limiting our ability to detect significant changes in potential mediating variables. Second, some of the questionnaires used may be relatively insensitive to subtle changes when compared with objective measures. For example, one study found that sleep quality assessed using overnight polysomnography showed poor agreement with PSQI scores [29]. Future studies may therefore consider incorporating objective monitoring. Furthermore, it is possible that MBSR have achieved direct effects on BP or by meditating pathways that were not measured in the current study. Finally, our participants had baseline scores within the normal range for several measures—for instance, mean depression, anxiety, and stress scores on the DASS-21 were all in the “normal” range—making further improvement unlikely and potentially obscuring any detectable mechanistic pathways.

Currently, there is no well-proven treatment specifically for nocturnal HT. Although BP findings from a pilot RCT should be treated as preliminary and not definitive, a 6.7 mmHg reduction in nocturnal SBP is clinically significant, as a 5 mmHg decrease in daytime SBP is associated with a 10% reduction in the incidence of cardiovascular events and nocturnal SBP is more predictive to cardiovascular events than daytime SBP [3430]. Given MBSR’s health benefits for stress and mood, along with its favorable safety profile in HT patients in existing RCTs, clinicians may consider consider recommending it as a supplementary intervention it to patients with nocturnal HT, noting that its effectiveness is not yet well established [18]. The latest 2023 European Society of Hypertension guideline suggests that mindfulness meditation can be considered for patients with HT (not specific to nocturnal HT), albeit with a lower level of evidence (grade C) [8]. Similarly, 2020 International Society of Hypertension guideline recommended stress reduction in HT patients and mindfulness be introduced “into the daily routine” [31]. However, mindfulness meditation is not regarded as a mainstream treatment and was not mentioned in the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association HT guideline or the recently published nocturnal HT guideline by the Chinese Hypertension League [3233].

Our results confirm the feasibility of and provided important information for the main RCT, which will definitively answer whether MBSR can become a standard treatment for nocturnal HT. However, adherence to nocturnal HBPM was only fair: although most participants (90.8%) provided at least some nocturnal HBPM readings for ≥ 6 of the 8 weeks, only 55.3% achieved adequate HBPM every week (i.e., ≥ 6 readings per week), which may limit the reliability of these data. Future RCTs may therefore retain nocturnal HBPM but reduce the required measurement frequency to improve adherence, as very frequent measurements can be burdensome. Nevertheless, nocturnal HBPM remains valuable because it can capture the trajectory of BP changes throughout the MBSR program, potentially enabling researchers to determine when BP reductions occur. Multiple home readings also support the use of linear mixed-models, which provide more reliable estimates than relying on only 2 or 3 ABPM measurements. Importantly, accumulating evidence shows that nocturnal HBPM provides similar nocturnal BP values to ABPM [34]. Moreover, nocturnal HBPM has been shown to be predictive of cardiovascular events [335]. Finally, HBPM tends to be better tolerated than ABPM—which can disturb sleep due to cuff inflations—meaning that participants who struggle with ABPM may still be able to provide useful nocturnal home readings [36]. Recently, MBSR has been adapted into the Mindfulness-Based Blood Pressure Reduction (MB-BP) program, specifically targeting HT [15]. While MBSR focuses solely on reducing stress, MB-BP may be more effective due to its inclusion of specific components that address HT, such as improving medication compliance and using mindfulness to promote other lifestyle changes [15]. A recent RCT (n = 201) showed that MB-BP reduced office SBP by 4.5 mmHg at 6 months compared to usual care, although its effect on out-of-office and nocturnal HT has yet to be examined. Currently, the mechanisms by which mindfulness meditation reduces BP are under-investigated and warrant exploration in RCTs. For example, although MBSR was hypothesized to reduce BP by decreasing sympathetic activation, the latest meta-analysis failed to demonstrate significant improvements in these parameters across various mindfulness-based programs, citing a “scarcity of studies” [3738]. Furthermore, long-term data on MBSR’s effects on BP is lacking, which is important since patients may become less adherent to regular mindfulness practices over time [18].

No similar RCT has been previously carried out in the Chinese HT population, which may respond differently to MBSR, because Chinese people have a distinct HT phenotype (e.g., higher salt sensitivity and a greater prevalence of nocturnal HT) [17]. The current RCT demonstrated good randomization with balanced baseline characteristics between both groups, and most patients had excellent adherence to the intervention. ABPM, the primary outcome for the future trial, was collected in 100% of the patients. Our sensitivity analysis also showed similar results with our main analysis.

However, due to the limited sample size (as a limitation shared by pilot trials), we were unable to draw definitive conclusions about MBSR’s effect on nocturnal HT. Recruitment was mostly conducted through newspapers, and most participants were literate and had high educational levels. Similarly, since MBSR classes required weekly in-person attendance, most participants were retired. Thus, the external validity of the results for patients with different demographics (e.g., working-age individuals, populations with lower educational attainment) remains unknown. Future trials can adopt more diverse recruitment strategies to enhance external validity, but these participation patterns may reflect real-life situations when MBSR is implemented in routine clinical practice. Originally, we planned to recruit mostly from primary care clinics, but the referral rate was low due to a lack of manpower in busy clinics and newspaper recruitment was considered necessary. Although a reduction of approximately 7 mmHg in nocturnal SBP may be clinically relevant, this pilot study was not designed to evaluate the long-term sustainability of the intervention, adherence to MBSR beyond the study period, or its long-term impact on clinical outcomes. We also did not collect data on patients’ antihypertensive medication-taking time, which may influence nocturnal BP readings, as lower-quality evidence with low certainty suggests that evening dosing may improve dipping status and reduce nocturnal BP [9]. However, all participants were explicitly instructed not to change their antihypertensive regimen during the trial, and all patients had type I HT and confirmed nocturnal HT at baseline on ABPM. These aspects will need to be addressed in future adequately powered, longer-term RCTs. In addition, due to the nature of the mindfulness-based intervention, participant blinding was not feasible, and bias from expectancy effects or behavior modification cannot be fully excluded. Lastly, although baseline characteristics were generally comparable between groups, minor imbalances were observed, as may occur in small pilot studies despite randomization. While outcome analyses were adjusted for baseline values to mitigate this issue, findings from secondary analyses should therefore be interpreted as exploratory.

CONCLUSIONS

This is the first RCT to investigate the effect of mindfulness on nocturnal BP in a Chinese population. The primary finding confirmed the feasibility of a definitive RCT regarding patient recruitment and retention, acceptability of the intervention (i.e. MBSR), and data collection (e.g. repeated ABPM). As secondary, exploratory analyses, a reduction of approximately 7 mmHg in nocturnal SBP was observed in the intervention group. While this finding can be clinically meaningful, it should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and requires confirmation in a fully powered definitive trial.
