Volumetric measurement to evaluate treatment response to induction chemotherapy on MRI outperformed RECIST guideline in outcome prediction in advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma
Authors: TSA Kwong, HS Leung, FKF Mo, YM Tsang, L Lan, LM Wong, TY So, EP Hui, BBY Ma, AD King, QYH Ai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTreatment response evaluated by tumour size change is an important indicator for outcome prediction. Advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (adNPC) grows irregularly, and so the unidimensional measurement may not be accurately applied to adNPC for outcome prediction. This study aimed to evaluate values of unidimensional and volumetric measurements for treatment response to induction chemotherapy (IC) for outcome prediction in adNPC and compared the values with that of RECIST 1.1 guideline.Materials and methodsPre-treatment and post-IC magnetic resonance images (MRIs) from 124 patients with stage III-IVA NPC were retrospectively reviewed. Sums of the maximum unidimensional diameters (D) and volumes of the targeted tumours (primary tumour and two largest metastatic lymph nodes) on the pre- (Dpre and Vpre) and post-IC MRIs (Dpost-IC and Vpost-IC) and percentage changes in D (Δ D%) and V (ΔV%) between two scans were calculated and correlated with disease-free survival (DFS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRRFS), and distant metastases-free survival (DMFS) using Cox regression analysis. Area under the curves (AUCs) of independent measurements and RECIST groups (RECIST response and non-response groups) for predicting disease recurrence, locoregional recurrence, and distant metastases, respectively, were calculated and compared using the DeLong test.ResultsUnivariable analysis showed correlations between high Dpost-IC with poor DFS and DMFS (P < 0.05), but not with LRRFS (P = 0.07); high Vpost-IC and low ΔV% (less decrease in volume on post-IC) with poor DFS, LRRFS, and DMFS (P < 0.05); and no correlations between Dpre, ΔD%, and Vpre and the outcomes (P > 0.05). Multivariable analysis showed that ΔV% was the only independent measurement for outcomes (P < 0.05). Compared with RECIST groups, ΔV% of 47.9% (median value) showed a higher AUC for disease recurrence (0.682 versus 0.526, P < 0.01) and for locoregional recurrence (0.782 versus 0.585, P < 0.01), but not for distant metastases (0.593 versus 0.518, P = 0.26).ConclusionsVolumetric measurement to evaluate treatment response to IC outperformed unidimensional measurement and RECIST guideline in outcome prediction in adNPC.Key words: volumetric measurement, RECIST, outcome, head and neck cancer, MRI, nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is one of the most common cancers worldwide.1 Due to the complex anatomy of the head and neck region, advanced head and neck cancers (adHNCs) that spread extensively to the adjacent structures is primarily treated with intensity modulated radiotherapy with/without chemotherapy, a non-surgical approach that can accurately irradiate the cancer deep while avoiding major surgery, which creates morbidity from critical functional deficits.2, 3, 4 However, disease recurrence, which is the main cause of death, is commonly seen in patients with adHNC after the treatment.5, 6, 7 In order to improve the outcomes in these patients, it is desirable to identify patients at risk of disease recurrence, particularly before or during the early course of treatment which would allow for the timely adjustment of the management strategy, i.e. additional personalised treatment, as well as to plan for close post-treatment surveillance for detection of residual or recurrent disease.

Tumour response to treatment can be a potential predictor for the identification of patients at risk of disease recurrence as tumours with poor treatment response are likely resistant to treatment, thus being at risk of recurrence afterwards. Treatment response in cancer is often evaluated according to RECIST, which assesses tumour shrinkage between two time points with unidimensional measurements on the cross-sectional imaging.8 Previous studies have shown that RECIST response group had better long-term outcomes compared with RECIST non-response groups in patients with liver, brain, breast, and lung cancer.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 However, RECIST may not be applicable to the evaluation of treatment response in HNC because HNC can be irregular in shape, particularly at advanced stages, so unidimensional measurement may not be representative of the actual tumour size.14 Furthermore, the unidimensional measurement of an irregular-shaped tumour could show significant interobserver variability15 (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933), which potentially increases the interobserver variability in the evaluation of treatment response. Compared with unidimensional measurement, volumetric measurement can objectively assess the sizes of irregularly shaped tumours, and is less influenced by interobserver variability.15,16 Therefore, volumetric measurement could be the optimal measurement for the size evaluation of the HNCs, although it has the drawback of being more time-consuming.

Advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (adNPC) is one of the HNCs in which the predictive value of treatment response for long-term outcomes can be easily studied because the management protocol is standardised. In addition to concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), induction chemotherapy (IC) is now also given to patients with adNPC before they start CCRT to eliminate potential microscopic metastases and shrink the primary tumour, which lowers the risk of recurrence and permits reductions in extent of the radiotherapy (RT) fields.17, 18, 19 In patients treated with IC + CCRT, pre- and post-IC imaging examinations are therefore mandatory for the assessment of treatment response and adjustment of the RT fields. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has the advantage over computed tomography in depicting soft tissue and differentiating metastatic nodes from adjacent tissues and primary tumours. MRI is therefore now routinely carried out for the assessment of NPC in many institutions. However, only few studies have investigated the role of treatment response to IC for the prediction of long-term outcome in adNPC20,21 and are even less focused on the comparative accuracies of using different methods to measure size including volumetric measurements.21

In this study, we applied the unidimensional and volumetric measurements to the pre- and post-IC MRIs of patients with adNPC treated with IC. We aimed to investigate the role of the tumour response to IC evaluated by volumetric measurement for the prediction of long-term outcomes in patients with adNPC by comparing it with that by unidimensional measurement and by using RECIST guidelines to group response based on the unidimensional measurement.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by the local institutional ethics review board, and the requirement for written consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All study procedures complied with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients who fulfilled the following inclusion criteria were included in the study: (i) ethnic Chinese adult patients with new biopsy-proven NPC; (ii) patients who had pre-treatment staging head and neck MRI from 2008 to 2023 showing stage III or stage IVA NPC in Prince of Wales Hospital, Hong Kong S.A.R.; (iii) patients treated with full-dose IC for two to three cycles followed by CCRT; and (iv) patients who had a head and neck MRI at the end of IC (post-IC MRI). Patients were excluded from the analysis if (i) patient was lost to follow-up; (ii) MRI images were severely degraded by motion artefacts or other artefacts; or (iii) patient had a history of other HNCs.

Image acquisition

All MRI was carried out using a 3.0-Testla whole-body system (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) which contains a body coil for excitation and a Philips 32-channel SENSE Head/Spine phased array coil for reception. The MRI protocol included at least (i) axial non-contrast-enhanced fat-suppressed T2-weighted images (non-CE FS-T2WI), (ii) axial non-CE T1-weighted images (non-CE T1WI), and (iii) axial contrast-enhanced with or without FS T1WI.

Evaluation of treatment response to IC

In keeping with RECIST 1.1, which requires a total of five lesions (a maximum of two lesions per organ) to be identified as target lesions at baseline,9 this study selected the NPC primary tumour and two largest metastatic nodes (with a short-axis diameter of >15 mm) on the pre-treatment MRIs as target lesions for assessments.

Unidimensional measurement

The maximum diameters of target lesions (longest diameter for the primary NPC and the short-axis diameter for metastatic nodes) on the pre- and post-IC axial non-CE FS T2WIs were measured by a researcher with 10 years of experience in head and neck MRIs (Figure 1). The sum of maximum diameters on pre- and post-IC MRIs (Dpre and Dpost-IC) were calculated. The percentage change in the unidimensional measurements of target lesions between pre- and post-IC MRIs (ΔD%) was calculated by ΔD% = (Dpre − Dpost-IC) ÷ Dpre × 100%, with positive values indicating decrease in size after IC.

Volumetric measurement

Areas of the target lesions on the pre- and post-IC axial non-CE FS T2WIs shown on each of the slices were manually contoured using the open-source software ITK-SNAP (version 3.4.0) by the same researcher who had carried out unidimensional measurements (Figure 1) and the volumes of the target lesions selected on the pre- and post-IC MRIs (Vpre and Vpost-IC) were calculated by multiplying the number of voxels in each of the contours with voxel sizes. The percentage change of target lesion volume between pre- and post-IC MRIs were calculated by ΔV% = (Vpre −Vpost-IC) ÷ Vpre × 100%, with positive values indicating decrease in size after IC.

RECIST 1.1 evaluation

Treatment response to IC was also evaluated according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria,8 which mainly referred to changes in the sum of maximum diameters of target lesions and non-targeted lesions, by the same researcher who had carried out unidimensional measurements. Treatment response to IC was categorised into (i) complete response (CR), defined as disappearance of target lesions and targeted lymph node metastases reduced to <10 mm in short-axis diameter; (ii) partial response (PR), defined as at least 30% reduction in the sum of target lesion diameters; (3) progressive disease, defined as at least 20% increase in the sum of target lesion diameters; or (iv) stable disease (SD), defined as insufficient increase or reduction in the sum of target lesion diameters. Patients were further subgrouped into RECIST response group (CR or PR) or RECIST non-response group (SD or PR) for the analysis.

Treatment

All patients were treated with IC followed by CCRT. The IC was administered intravenously once every 3 weeks for two or three cycles, with one of the following IC regimens: (i) 1000 mg/m2 of body surface area (BSA) of gemcitabine on days 1 and 8 along with 80 mg/m2 of BSA of cisplatin on day 1; (ii) 175 mg/m2 of BSA of paclitaxel and 75 mg/m2 of BSA of cisplatin on day 1; (iii) 75 mg/m2 of BSA of docetaxel and 75 mg/m2 of BSA of cisplatin on day 1; or (iv) 70 mg/m2 of BSA of paclitaxel on days 1, 8, and 15 and carboplatin area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) 5-6 mg/ml.min on day 1. The CCRT was given by administering 40 mg/m2 of BSA of cisplatin weekly or carboplatin target AUC of 2 mg/ml.min weekly for up to seven cycles intravenously, concurrently with 66-74 Gy of radiation to the primary tumour and enlarged lymph nodes, and 50-60 Gy of radiation to regions at risk of microscopic spread given in 33-35 fractions.

Patient follow-up and endpoints

All patients underwent regular follow-up after treatment once every 3 months for the first 12 months, then once every 6 months for the next 24 months, and then once yearly afterwards until diagnosis of recurrence or death. The disease-free survival (DFS), locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRRFS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and overall survival (OS) of patients were calculated from the date of the start of treatment to the date of any disease recurrence, locoregional recurrence, distant metastasis, and death, or the latest follow-up recorded, respectively. Follow-up ended upon documentation of death or date of censor (31 December 2023). For patients with no reported endpoint events, their survival was censored upon the date of censor.

Statistical analysis

The unidimensional and volumetric measurements (Dpre, Dpost-IC, ΔD%, Vpre, Vpost-IC, and ΔV%) were correlated with DFS, LRRFS, DMFS, and OS using univariable Cox regression. Significant measurements, together with age, sex, T- (T1-2 versus T3-4) and N- (N0-1 versus N2-3) categories, overall stage (stage III versus stage IVA), and treatment details were then added into multivariable Cox regression to identify the independent variables for clinical outcomes. The predictive performance of the independent variables (using median value thresholds for continuous variables) for disease recurrence, locoregional recurrence, and distant metastases were evaluated and the AUCs were calculated and compared with that of RECIST response groups using the DeLong test. The survival rates of each group were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier curve analysis, and the log-rank test was used to compare the differences in survival rates. Differences in ΔD% and ΔV% between T1-2 and T3-4 were also evaluated by using the Mann–Whitney U test. Furthermore, Dpre, Dpost-IC, Vpre, and Vpost-IC of 30 randomly selected patients were measured by a radiologist with 3 years of experience in head and neck radiology for the assessment of interobserver agreements, and the intraclass coefficient (ICC) was calculated. The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 27) (IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for statistical analysis. A two-sided P value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Unidimensional and volumetric measurements for the prediction of outcomes

The 3-year DFS, LRRFS, DMFS, and OS rates were 76.6%, 91.1%, 83.9%, and 91.1%, respectively. The correlations of unidimensional and volumetric measurements (Dpre, Dpost-IC, ΔD%, Vpre, Vpost-IC, and ΔV%) with clinical outcomes in univariable Cox regression are shown in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933. For the unidimensional measurements, high Dpost-IC correlated with poor DFS, DMFS, and OS (P < 0.01-0.01), but not with LRRFS (P = 0.07) (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933), and Dpre and ΔD% did not correlate with any outcomes (P = 0.06-0.35), except that high Dpre significantly correlated with poor OS (P = 0.04) (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933). For the volumetric measurements, high Vpost-IC and low ΔV% (less decrease in volume on post-IC) correlated with poor DFS, LRRFS, DMFS, and OS (P < 0.01-0.03) (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933); and Vpre did not correlate with any outcomes (P = 0.14-0.35) (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933).

Table 2 showed results from the multivariable Cox regression analysis for the correlation of significant measurements, together with age, sex, T- and N-categories, overall stage, and treatment details with outcomes. Results showed that the ΔV% independently predicted DFS, LRRFS, DMFS, and OS, with a greater ΔV% predicting better outcomes (P < 0.01-0.02); the N-category independently predicted DFS and DMFS, with a higher N-category predicting worse outcomes (P < 0.01-0.03) (Table 2); cycles of IC independently predicted DMFS (P = 0.04); and cycles of concurrent chemotherapy independently predicted DMFS and DFS, with more cycles predicting better outcomes (P < 0.01-0.03). Other factors were not significant predictors for any of the outcomes (P = 0.08-0.99) (Table 2).

In order to examine whether or not ΔV% being the independent variable for outcomes resulted from the overfit of the confounding variables in the multivariable analysis, we then carried out the multivariable analysis by only fitting the ΔV%, together with T- and N- categories, which are two confounding variables closely related to long-term outcomes. Results showed that ΔV% was an independent predictor for DFS, LRRFS, DMFS, and OS (P < 0.01-0.01) (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103933).

Performances of RECIST groups and ΔV% for the prediction of outcomes

Table 3 shows the performances of RECIST groups (response versus non-response groups) and ΔV% using the median value as the threshold (ΔV% of 47.9%) for the prediction of disease recurrence, locoregional recurrence, and distant metastases. Compared with RECIST groups, the predictive performances of ΔV% showed higher AUCs for disease recurrence (AUC: 0.682 versus 0.526, P < 0.01) and for locoregional recurrence (AUC: 0.782 versus 0.585, P < 0.01), but not for distant metastases (0.593 versus 0.518, P = 0.26). Compared with the non-response group, the ΔV% of ≤47.9% showed a higher performance for the prediction of disease recurrence (sensitivity from 52.9% to 76.5%; specificity from 52.2% to 60.0%, accuracy from 52.4% to 64.5%, PPV from 29.5% to 41.9%, and NPV from 76.4% to 87.1%) and for locoregional recurrence (sensitivity from 64.3% to 100%; specificity from 52.7% to 56.4%, accuracy from 54.0% to 61.3%, PPV from 14.8% to 22.6%, and NPV from 92.1% to 100%) (Table 3). Discussion

This study evaluated the value of treatment response to IC by using volumetric and unidimensional measurements for the prediction of long-term survival outcomes in patients with adNPC. Results showed that smaller tumour size on the post-IC MRI assessed by unidimensional (Dpost-IC) and volumetric measurements (Vpost-IC), and a greater shrinkage in size obtained by volumetric measurement (ΔV%) predicted good outcomes. When these significant measurements were added to the multivariable analysis, together with other confounding factors (age, gender, T- and N-categories, overall stage, and treatment details), ΔV% outperformed being the only measurement that predicted outcomes. Compared with the RECIST groups (response versus non-response), the median ΔV% (ΔV% of 47.9%) showed better AUCs for the prediction of disease recurrence (0.682 versus 0.526) and locoregional recurrence (0.782 versus 0.585), but not distant metastases (0.593 versus 0.518). The results showed that tumour response to the IC evaluated by using volumetric measurement predicted long-term outcomes in patients with adNPC, and the ΔV% was superior to that of RECIST response groups for the prediction of outcomes. Targeted lesions may shrink greatly after the IC and it is always difficult to accurately measure unidimensional sizes of the tiny tumours on any imaging modalities. It is therefore expected that the variability in the measurements on the post-IC MRI may be even more obvious. In our study, we indeed found that the interobserver agreements for unidimensional and volumetric measurements on the post-IC MRI were lower compared with that on pre-treatment MRI. However, on the post-IC MRI, the volumetric measurement remained excellent for the interobserver agreement (ICC of 0.90), while unidimensional measurement had much worse interobserver agreement (ICC of 0.64). These results suggested that volumetric measurement is more reliable and repeatable than unidimensional measurement for the assessment of NPC after treatment. While the process of manual volumetric measurement is laborious and time-consuming, with the development of artificial intelligence-based tumour segmentation algorithms22, 23, 24 for NPC on MRI, volumetric-based assessment is going to be a more reliable tool than the unidimensional measurement for the evaluation of tumour size and should be considered to be brought back to the evaluation of the treatment response in patients with adNPC.

Previous studies suggested various thresholds ranging from a ΔV% of 30%-65% for the prediction of long-term outcomes in renal cell cancer, liver cancer, pancreatic cancer, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and lung adenocarcinoma.25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31 In HNC, only one NPC study investigated the role of the change in volumetric size for the prediction of outcomes and reported an optimal value of 25.15% for the prediction of outcomes.21 In our study, we simply used the median value of ΔV% as threshold and showed that compared to the RECIST non-response group, the use of ΔV% of ≤47.9% improved the sensitivity for the prediction of patients with disease recurrence and locoregional recurrence. However, both the RECIST response group and ΔV% may not be clinically valuable for the identification of patients with disease recurrence and locoregional recurrence due to low PPV. Nevertheless, patients with a ΔV% of >47.9% showed a 3-year LRRFS of 100%, indicating that it may be valuable for the selection of patients with a good outcome who can undergo de-escalation of chemotherapy and/or post-treatment surveillance.

It is interesting to note that both ΔV% and N-category were the only independent predictors of DMFS. This indicated that not only change in size of the metastatic nodes, but also the N-category of pre-treatment laterality and sites of the metastatic nodes should be taken into account for the prediction of distant metastases in NPC. We also evaluated the predictive values of the absolute tumour size on the pre- and post-IC MRIs measured by unidimensional and volumetric measurements. In keeping with previous studies,32,33 the pre-treatment tumour size did not predict DFS, LRRFS, and DMFS in NPC. Interestingly, the large tumour size measured by both measurements on the post-IC MRIs correlated with outcomes, indicating again that the tumour size that represents late response or resistance to IC may be more valuable for the prediction of outcomes. However, none of the measurements representing absolute tumour size can be strong as independent predictors for the outcomes. In terms of treatment, our results showed that more cycles of chemotherapy may be helpful to decrease the risk of disease recurrence, particularly at distant sites.

In this study, we also compared the differences in ΔD% and ΔV% between early and advanced T-categories. Results showed that advanced primary tumours (T3-4) shrunk less than early primary tumours (T1-2), indicating that advanced primary tumours are more resistant to IC than early-stage primary tumours. However, change in tumour size is not just the surrogate for the advanced T-category. It can be used to complement T-category for the prediction of outcomes because our results showed that ΔV% was an independent predictor of post-treatment outcomes (i.e. primary NPC can be staged as T3 or T4 on pre-MRIs indicating the extensive invasion of the primary tumour, but greater shrinkage in tumours on post-IC further indicates that the tumour is sensitive to treatment).

This study has limitations. Firstly, this study only included the size of the targeted lesions for the unidimensional and volumetric measurements, while the non-targeted lesions were taken into account for the evaluation of RECIST response groups. Secondly, other changes in morphological features of targeted lesions, such as treatment-induced tumour necrosis between pre- and post-IC MRIs, were not taken into account for the prediction of outcomes. Thirdly, additional imaging modalities, such as positron emission tomography (PET) were not used for the assessment of treatment response, which may result in the overlook of the small new lesions during treatment. However, as PET is expensive and could increase radiation exposure, it is not routinely carried out for the assessment of treatment response, and so assessment of treatment response still mainly relies on cross-sectional imaging modalities. Fourthly, although the median follow-up time of patients without relapse in this study was long (45.9 months), it is possible that few patients may still relapse with a longer follow-up time. Fifthly, we did not include the radiotherapy details which may have an impact on the long-term outcomes in the analysis due to the irretrievable dosimetry data. Furthermore, the majority of patients in this study were treated with platinum-based regimens, and the predictive values of the ΔV% for outcomes shown in our study need to be further investigated in patients who were treated with other regimens. Finally, dynamic plasma Epstein–Barr virus DNA levels and PET biomarkers have shown the predictive values for outcomes in NPC,34,35 but this part of the data was not included in this study because not all patients had this test routinely at different phases of treatment.

Conclusion

Results found that tumour response to IC evaluated by using volumetric measurement is superior to that using unidimensional measurement for the prediction of long-term outcomes in patients with adNPC. A great percentage of decrease in the tumour volume between pre- and post-IC MRIs (ΔV%) is an independent predictor of good outcomes and the ΔV% achieved better performances for the prediction of disease recurrence and locoregional recurrence compared with RECIST response groups (response versus non-response groups). The use of ΔV% of about >50% may be valuable for identifying patients with a good long-term outcome for locoregional disease, who may benefit from the de-escalation of chemotherapy and post-treatment surveillance, but further studies are needed to validate our findings.
