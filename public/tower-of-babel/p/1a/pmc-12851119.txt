Reducing regulated medical waste: A roadmap for process improvement
Authors: Clarissa A. Niino, Raanan Meyer, Catherine John, Kelly Wright
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Up to 70 % of healthcare waste is disposed of as Regulated Medical Waste (RMW).•Disposal of Regulated Medical Waste requires additional handling and processing.•We found a process change effective in reducing RMW from the operating room.•Reducing RMW saved thousands of dollars and decreased our carbon footprint.

Introduction

Health care has an enormous impact on the environment, making up 4.4. % of global net emissions [1]. The United States is one of the top three emitters in the world, and together with China and the European Union makes up 56 % of the total health care climate footprint [1]. The majority of these emissions created by the health care system come from the health care supply chain— the production, transport, use, and disposal of the product and packaging at its end of life [1].

The Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and OSHA differentiate “Regulated Medical Waste” (RMW) from municipal solid or general waste as waste having the sufficient potential to cause infection during handling and disposal [[2], [3], [4]]. However, precise definitions are often made by geographic location, regulations, or different facilities, leading to myriad of practices and cultures [5].

Disposal of RMW requires additional handling and processing, which is performed most commonly through steam sterilization or incineration [2,6,7]. As incineration is known to produce harmful greenhouse gases, it is largely being phased out in the United States [8]. Additionally, RMW must be disposed of in selected waste facilities and often has to travel further than general waste. This processing and handling is at least an order of magnitude more expensive to dispose of per pound than general waste [9]. General waste, on the other hand, does not require any additional handling, making it both less expensive and less impactful on the environment [7]. Waste generated by the healthcare system has also been shown to directly affect communities with disadvantaged populations, as it is often disposed of in landfills in areas with lower average income and higher proportions of minority residents [10].

Previous studies have found that a third of hospital generated RMW comes from the operating room (OR) and a considerable portion of this consists of general waste, including packaging and wrapping materials for instrument sterilization [7]. The recommended standard percentage of RMW in health care facilities based on national benchmarks is 15 % or less of overall waste; however, studies have shown that up to 70 % of healthcare waste is disposed of as RMW [11,12]. Thus, a reduction in RMW is beneficial from an economic, environmental, and ethical standpoint.

Our goal was to reduce the amount of RMW produced in the OR through the identification and buy-in of key leaders, staff education on proper waste sorting, and a process change to one red RMW bag per room. We also aimed to create a roadmap of our process to help make implementation of this process change reproducible across not only different areas of the hospital, but also multiple institutions.

Methods and materials

This was a quality improvement project implemented at a single high volume, urban hospital, starting with one floor of ORs. All cases and surgical specialties that took place on this floor were included. This study was deemed IRB exempt. The objective of our study was to reduce the amount of RMW in the OR through Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) Cycles. This system involves four different stages. The “plan” stage identifies the improvement to be made, while the “do” stage enacts this change and tests it. The “study” stage examines the success of the change, and the “act” stage identifies modifications and next steps for the next cycle [13]. Use of this model to reduce waste has been previously described by Wyssusek et al. [14]. We used this framework to implement three PDSA cycles over the course of our study. Our primary outcome was change in proportion of RMW to general OR waste following a month long study intervention or “test of change.” Our test of change consisted of a specialized training known as an educational in-service and a process change to the number of red bags placed in the OR. While we included three PDSA cycles in the study, only Cycle 3 included the test of change and this is where our primary outcome is from. Waste totals were evaluated monthly for a total of fourteen months following the intervention and compared to waste totals in the nine months prior to the test of change. The cost of waste disposal during these time periods was evaluated as a secondary outcome.

A leadership team comprising 8 members was created to oversee this process. Members of the team included physicians, nurses, safety officers, and environmental services leadership. Specifically, key stakeholders included front line clinical staff of a surgeon and OR nurse champion, the OR nurse manager, OR nurse educator, environmental services (EVS) manager and vice president (VP), the VP of linen who oversees waste contracts, and the hospital safety officer. The team worked together, meeting bi-weekly to create goals and assign owners to oversee each of the action items. Statistical analysis

We compared the periods prior to and after the test of change. For categorical variables we used Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. For continuous variables we used the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as n ( %) and continuous variables as median (interquartile range). A 2-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Software Package for Statistics and Simulation (IBM SPSS version 27, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), and R (R Core Team 2021; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna Austria).

Results

PDSA cycle implementation roadmap

This study was developed in three PDSA cycles. Cycle 1 began with investigating the reason behind why all OR waste was being treated as RMW. Frontline clinical staff had noted that all bags in the OR were being set up as red bags and brought this to the attention of EVS leadership. It was found that, due to a citation seven years prior, EVS had been instructed to treat all OR waste as RMW. The official hospital policy was reviewed and found to specify that each room should have only one red bag for RMW. This step involved understanding the why: learning the background for the current process.

Cycle 2 involved working with EVS to demonstrate the discrepancy with hospital policy. The hospital policies were reviewed with EVS leadership. In this process, it was discovered that they thought a different procedure was occurring that followed the policy. EVS leadership were brought into the OR to observe flow and current processes. With new waste management contracts being due, EVS agreed to move forward with a test of change to one RMW bag in each OR. This step involved bringing key stakeholders into the frontline clinical work to show them the current processes were not working.

In Cycle 3, OR staff and EVS agreed on a test of change process and start date. The test of change was implemented in April of 2022 starting with one floor of the hospital, affecting a total of 13 ORs that perform a significant amount of surgical volume covering a variety of specialties and procedure types. The decision to focus on only one floor of ORs was made in order to trial the test of change in a smaller environment prior to expanding to all ORs. Both EVS and OR staff underwent an in-service before the test of change. The “Red2Red” in-service taught OR and EVS staff what items should go into the RMW bags (Fig. 2). EVS staff received additional instruction from their leadership on setting up rooms with one red bag per case. RMW was defined as tissue, blood, or items saturated in blood, according to the state department of public health guidelines. All other items were considered general waste, including urine, feces, saliva, and secretions. These were directed to go into the clear waste bags. Quality control measures including regular assessments of waste were implemented to ensure RMW was not being disposed of as general waste. While cycle 3 involved the test of change intervention, cycles 1 and 2 were actually the longest processes in the change, primarily due to challenges identifying and gathering stakeholders from across health system leadership and frontline workers. Understanding the why and gathering stakeholders in leadership and frontline workers takes the longest, as clinical staff may not understand which administrative leaders are motivated and empowered to make a change.

Discussion

Our test of change resulted in a significant reduction in waste designated as RMW from the OR. This subsequently led to a decrease in both the cost of RMW disposal and the total cost of waste disposal. The amount of general waste and cost of its disposal were found to increase in this time period due to the re-allocation of true general waste items from RMW to appropriate general waste receptacles. Following this test of change, the proportion of RMW generated by the hospital decreased to nearly the recommended amounts of 15 % or lower in a short amount of time.

Previous studies have implemented systems changes within their hospitals to reduce the amount of RMW. A study by Garcia implemented a 9- step process for more accurate waste sorting throughout a healthcare system [5]. This began with a survey and assessment, followed by involvement of administration and creation of a task force and staff education with monitoring of progress and quarterly evaluation of data [5]. Over the study time period the percent RMW and the cost for waste disposal both significantly decreased [5]. While waste from specific areas of the hospital was not examined, the process they outline is similar to our own roadmap and emphasizes the importance of a set process, support from leadership, and education of staff on all levels. OR personnel often do not receive training on proper waste segregation; however, educational programs have been found to improve waste management, underscoring the importance of staff education as part of this process [15,16]. A review of quality improvement studies to decrease the carbon footprint of the OR found a variety of initiatives that had both a positive environmental impact and also contributed to cost savings [17]. One of these studies sought to decrease the amount of improper sorting of RMW in the OR, leading to a monthly 30 % reduction in RMW [18]. Another waste segregation initiative focusing on the anesthesia workspace found staff education decreased the amount of waste disposed of as RMW and lead to an annual cost savings [19]. Similar initiatives at the Carolinas Medical Center and Harborview Medical Center in Seattle, Washington found a reduction in RMW by 75 % and 59–61 %, respectively [9,20]. However, these studies do not provide a guide for implementation of these processes and procedures (Table 2). We found that putting together a process change in the OR can have significant downstream effects. Having support from leadership, EVS, OR staff, and providers is an essential aspect, as these process changes cannot work without the support of each of these groups. Starting with a seemingly small project for improving waste triage can lead to further interest in sustainability initiatives from the institutional level, especially after demonstration of significant financial savings. This initiative was subsequently rolled out to other areas of the OR and hospital. New waste contracts meant replacing disposable sharps containers with reusable containers and reconfiguring custom OR packs to reduce items that were opened in cases but weren’t used. An executive sustainability committee was formed, leading to projects in anesthesia, nutrition, and linen, with benchmarking in multiple different aspects of sustainability. Ultimately, we hope that this roadmap and action plan for decreasing RMW will help to see this project implemented at hospitals across the country.

Our study is limited to one floor of our ORs; thus, the potential percent reduction in medical waste and fiscal impact are both likely greater than described. The single institution setting of this study may limit the generalizability of our results, given inter-institution variations in OR culture and RMW process steps, but the systems change roadmap outlined may be tailored across settings.

Conclusion

Our process change involved limiting RMW receptables to minimize the convenience of RMW overuse and an educational intervention to improve staff understanding of waste stewardship. These changes were ultimately beneficial on multiple levels: reducing our carbon footprint, decreasing costs, and paving the way for other earth friendly initiatives. We hope to encourage other hospitals to join in the effort to make our ORs more environmentally sustainable.
