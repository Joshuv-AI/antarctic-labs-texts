Explaining barriers and challenges to child safety during driving from parents’ perspectives: a qualitative content analysis
Authors: Saeed Fallah-Aliabadi, Somayyeh Sadeghi, Ahad Heydari, Mahnaz Sanaeefar, Mehdi Raadabadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraffic crushes can lead to acute and long-term consequences on children’s physical health, mental well-being, and overall quality of life. This study aimed to identify parents’ perspectives on traffic safety barriers and the causes of child road traffic collisions in Yazd, Iran.Materials and methodsThis qualitative study was conducted in 2023 with 24 participants, specifically, parents, selected through purposeful and snowball sampling. Data collection was performed using open and semi-structured interviews. The data analysis employed thematic analysis with an inductive approach.ResultsThe results show five main categories and 20 subcategories. The main categories include “Parent-related factors,” “Child-related factors,” “Executive/Managerial factors,” “Cultural factors,” and “Economic/Infrastructure Factors”. The “Parent-related factors” category includes seven \subcategories: “Behavioral factors”, “Risky behaviors”, “Personal /Mental Factors”, “Cognitive Factors”, “Safety Equipment”, “Strategies and Supporting Factors for Child Safety” and “Education”. The “Child-related factors” category consists of four subcategories: “Behavioral factors”, “Risky behaviors”, “Cognitive factors” and “Education”. The third main category, “Executive/Managerial factors,” includes three subcategories: “Prevention”, “Compliance with traffic laws”, and “Policymaking”. The fourth main category, “Cultural factors,” includes three subcategories: “Cultivating Road Safety”, “Child rearing/Communication with child” and “Safety education and awareness raising”. The final main category, “Economic/Infrastructure factors,” consists of three subcategories: “Financial Capability”, “Vehicle Safety and Quality”, and “Road Infrastructure”.ConclusionImplementing appropriate and specific traffic collision mitigation and prevention interventions at the family, school, and community levels can play a significant role in reducing both the incidence of traffic crush and the severity of injuries sustained by children.Keywords: Child, Parents, Traffic safety, Crashes, Traffic

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report, around 1.3 million people lose their lives annually due to road traffic crashes. Additionally, between 20 and 50 million others suffer non-fatal injuries, many of whom become disabled due to their injuries. Road traffic crashes impose considerable economic costs on individuals, their families, and governments. These costs arise from medical care and also from loss of productivity for those who have died or become disabled and family members who must take time off work or school to care for the injured. Road traffic crush cost most countries 3% of their gross domestic product (GDP) [1]. Mortality risk is three times higher in low-income countries compared to high-income countries [2].

In the Middle East, Iran is one of the countries with the highest road traffic fatalities [3]. According to the report by the Iranian Legal Medicine Organization (ILMO), in 2019, 17,183 people in Iran lost their lives due to road crashes, representing a 1.2% increase compared to 2018 [3]. A study on road injury incidence and mortality rates among children aged 0–14 in Iran (2010–2019) indicated bicycle-related crashes as the leading cause of injuries. The maximum Mean motor vehicle injury rate declined from 9.94 (8.34–11.57) per 100,000 in 2010 to 3.86 (3.03–4.94) in 2019 [4].

Considerable evidence indicates that young children are the most vulnerable age group [7]. Unintentional and accidental injuries in children and adolescents remain a recognized global public health problem at all ages, with a persistently high rate of disability, mortality and healthcare costs [8]. One of the most prevalent forms of unintentional injury encompasses various types of traffic crushes, such as those involving children as pedestrians and cyclists, for which significant global efforts have been undertaken to implement preventive measures [9].

Despite increased efforts to enhance child safety in recent years, parental awareness of child traffic safety remains low [14]. A lack of parental knowledge and awareness contributes to the neglect of preventive measures, heightened vulnerability, and inadequate management of high-risk traffic situations involving children [12]. The rate of road traffic injuries among children with illiterate parents is approximately seven times higher than that of those with university-educated caregivers [15]. Conversely, the imitation of parental behavior—particularly risky driving behaviors—by children increases the vulnerability of this population group [13].

Significant measures have been implemented to substantially decrease the incidence of traffic crushes and fatalities in Iran. However, unfortunately, the incidence of road crashes and fatalities in Iran remains alarmingly high, with children recognized as one of the most vulnerable demographics in traffic-related incidents. Cultural and social factors, along with Iran’s diverse climatic conditions, significantly influence parental driving behaviors and attitudes toward child safety. Addressing these challenges is essential for developing effective strategies to enhance child traffic safety. Policymakers and urban planners can leverage findings from similar studies to design and implement educational and preventive programs at the community level, aimed at raising public awareness regarding children’s traffic safety issues. Current research predominantly emphasizes technical and infrastructural aspects of traffic safety, often neglecting parental perspectives. This knowledge gap can hinder a comprehensive understanding of parents’ needs and concerns regarding child safety. Therefore, Investigating the challenges and barriers to child traffic safety from parents’ perspectives can provide valuable insights into existing deficiencies. Therefore, conducting a qualitative study that investigates parents’ experiences and concerns is essential for enriching the existing literature. The present study aims to explore the obstacles and challenges to children’s safety while driving from the perspective of parents through a qualitative content analysis conducted in Yazd, Iran.

Materials and methods

Given the complexity of understanding phenomena such as attitude and perspective and the importance of extracting data in categories and subcategories, researchers concluded that qualitative content analysis can better extract and categorize parental perspectives. This method is more applicable when we want to use a conceptual framework model to describe a phenomenon and structurally classify the extracted data [16]. The method allows us to conceptually examine the phenomenon of interest by categorizing the structured data [17].

Participants and data collection

Data collection was conducted through 24 face-to-face in-depth interviews with participants from January to May 2023. The participants were parents who had encountered challenges related to child safety while driving. A purposive and snowball sampling method was employed to identify the participants. Based on data obtained from the Yazd Traffic Department, drivers who were stopped and fined by the police for non-compliance with child safety regulations were identified and included in the study. Crashes involving physical injuries or fatalities among children were excluded to safeguard the psychological and emotional well-being of the affected families by avoiding the recall of distressing memories.

Data collection commenced with three unstructured interviews to develop an interview guide, followed by 21 semi-structured interviews. The study’s purpose, participant involvement, and interview recording procedures were thoroughly explained to participants, and informed consent was obtained before the commencement of interviews. All participants voluntarily took part in the study without receiving any financial or non-financial incentives. Data were gathered through open-ended questions that aligned with the research objectives. Interview content was recorded using an Airiver D15 32GB voice recorder, and subsequently transcribed into a Word document. In instances requiring clarification of participants’ responses, follow-up telephone calls were made. The interviews were conducted by a member of the research team, a Ph.D student specializing in disaster and emergency health. It was tried to select a quiet location for the interviews, and to keep this location as close as possible to the participants’ workplace or office. To enhance the interviewer’s skills and improve the quality of the interviews, the first two interviews were reviewed by a supervisor (AH), who provided the necessary training to address any technical deficiencies for subsequent interviews. The duration of each interview ranged from 30 to 45 min. The main discussion focus in the interviews was on parents’ experiences, perspectives, and reasons for not using child car seats while driving. The interviews continued until no new codes emerged and data saturation was ensured. To clarify this point further, data saturation was reached when additional interviews produced repetitive data without new themes or categories. In qualitative studies, sample size adequacy is generally determined by achieving data saturation, which was observed in this study.

The semi-structured questions used in the study included:

Please share your experiences of any crashes or near-miss involving children during driving.

Please share your experiences of any crashes or near-miss involving children during driving.

Data analysis

A thematic analysis approach based on Graneheim and Lundman was used to analyze the interview data in this study [18]. To enable an accurate understanding of concepts and prevent superficial and mechanical coding, the coding and conceptual categorization processes were manually carried out using paper and pen. After identifying Meaning Units in the text, condensed Meaning Units were extracted through memoing. Participants’ experiences were then categorized into concepts under the close supervision of the qualitative research supervisor and in consultation with other researchers. In the next step, the initial codes were extracted and subcategorized based on their similarities and differences. Since the original model defined one or more subcategories for each main category, some initial codes were placed under the existing subcategories.

During the preliminary coding, participants’ quotes were used and dense codes and meaning units were identified. The codes were then categorized into classes and subclasses based on their similarities and differences.

Study rigor

In qualitative research, ensuring rigor means ensuring credibility, Dependability, Transferability, and Confirmability. To ensure the rigor of this study, four mentioned criteria were considered [15, 18, 19].

Credibility

In the present study, the researchers ensured the validity of the research by continuous interaction from the initial design stage to the collection, analysis, and writing of qualitative data. Participant validation was employed to verify the accuracy and robustness of the data. This method involved sharing the findings with study participants, allowing them to review and confirm the results, thereby enhancing the reliability and credibility of the research.

Dependability

In addition to using the research group’s views, the results of data analysis were made available to other qualitative researchers to obtain their complementary and critical opinions and ensure consistency with the research group’s views.

Transferability

In this qualitative study, transferability was enhanced through a comprehensive description of the research context and the careful selection of participants who were most relevant to the research topic. By including individuals with similar experiences related to child traffic safety, the findings can be applied to other groups facing comparable challenges in different geographical or cultural settings. The simultaneous collection and analysis of data enabled researchers to adapt their questions and methods based on emerging insights, ensuring the study remained relevant and grounded in real-world experiences. Furthermore, maintaining coherence between research questions and methods strengthened the validity of the findings, increasing the likelihood that similar approaches would yield comparable results in various contexts. Comparing the results with other studies (in the discussion section) further validated transferability by highlighting consistencies and differences with broader literature. By integrating these strategies, the study was designed to ensure its findings could be meaningfully applied beyond the immediate research environment to other relevant contexts.

Confirmability

The researcher’s interest in the phenomenon under study, long-term engagement with it, verbatim transcription of conversations, and efforts to obtain complementary and critical views from participants, research team members, and other qualitative researchers ensured confirmability in this study. The researcher identified participants with motivation and experience relevant to the research topic to achieve authenticity.

Ethical considerations

The timing of telephone interviews was coordinated with participants. At the start of each interview, the researcher explained the research purpose and then obtained verbal consent to record the interview. Detailed explanations of the research aims were provided and participants were assured of confidentiality. Participants were also allowed to ask questions and informed they could withdraw at any time. The principles of maintaining confidentiality of information and data, as well as ethical considerations for disseminating findings, were observed without recording identifiable information such as names or identification codes. This study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shahid Sadoughi University of Medical Sciences, Yazd (IR.SSU.SRH.REC.1403.003). Prior to the interview sessions, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Parent-related factors

In this study, five main categories related to parents were identified: Behavioral Factors, Risky Behaviors, Personal/Mental Factors, Cognitive Factors, and Safety Equipment.

Behavioral factors

Behavioral factors refer to actions or inactions by parents that directly or indirectly impact child safety. These include lack of parental supervision and attention while driving, parental negligence in teaching or protection, and protecting the child with bare hands. Some parents fail to create excitement for the child, leading to unsafe behaviors, while others prioritize their own comfort, such as preferring the front seat for the child. Accepting the child’s unreasonable demands and making excuses to avoid proper action are also common behavioral issues. Additionally, some parents seek to blame others for crashes rather than taking responsibility for their own actions.

Risky behaviors

Risky behaviors are potentially dangerous acts that parents perform voluntarily or involuntarily without awareness. Some parents endanger their children’s lives through negligence and irresponsible acts inside vehicles. Furthermore, some unbelievable parental behaviors in vehicles can be extremely hazardous when interacting with children. Parents transporting children have a greater risk of distraction and crashes than those without children passengers. In this regard, parents say:

“When the mother is driving, to keep the child close and see him/her up close, and focus on driving while being reassured that if something happens the child is right next to her, for this reason, she places the child in the front seat” (Parent 19).

“Sometimes when it’s just me and my toddler in the car with no one else to hold the child, I’m forced to place him on my lap while driving, which I know is dangerous, and in an crashes, the child would be between me and the steering wheel and severely injured” (Parent 4).

Personal/mental factors

Individual behavior while driving can result from personality traits. Studies show some people have risk-seeking personalities [20] which causes them to exhibit dangerous acts while driving. Parents must recognize they are the primary role models for their children’s safe driving behavior. Therefore, parents must be responsible to ensure children’s safety and prevent traffic injuries, but some parents neglect these issues. In this regard, parents say:

“When I saw a child inside a vehicle, for example leaning out the window or with their hands out the window in a risky position, I try to cautiously warn the father or mother about potential hazards for their child inside the vehicle. Generally I don’t get a response after warning, and some completely ignore what I say, meaning they simply don’t care even though I feel parents know what they’re doing is wrong, they just don’t like being told” (Parent 13).

Cognitive factors

The ability to recognize and anticipate hazards in traffic crashes is recognized as one of the key factors in safe driving. Parents can reduce injuries from traffic crashes by evaluating risks and observing safety issues while driving. Some parents lack necessary caution while driving due to thinking such incidents will not happen to them, and take risks. In this regard, parents say:

“Some parents don’t believe and say nothing has ever happened to me so far and nothing will happen, and that God protects and guards our child, so they don’t take traffic-related hazards and driving injuries seriously for their children” (Parent 24).

“Some parents know certain behaviors of theirs or their children can be very dangerous, like sticking their head or even half their body out the sunroof, which has increased lately - with even minor braking or at high speeds the child could fall out or get chest injuries, or even have an object enter their eye, but parents don’t pay attention to these issues” (Parent 12).

Safety equipment

Vehicle safety equipment refers to all optional accessories chosen by the vehicle owner to increase safety and comfort for the driver and vehicle. Currently, safety equipment is the most important part of a vehicle, significantly reducing crashes and financial/physical injuries. The worst driving crashes generally occur in vehicles lacking standard vehicle safety systems. In this regard, parents say:

“When you use a child seat, vehicle space decreases. If I want to bring a family like my parents, there’s no space for a child seat in the back where three people can sit. With a child seat only two people can sit, and the seat is very hard to move and large” (Parent 2).

“While buckling up my child I kept them occupied with something in the car, using props like hanging toys on the back seat or clothes rack, always leaving a doll next to them on the car seat, or storybooks or something novel so my child wouldn’t get tired or fussy in the car, being entertained while driving” (Parent 12).

Strategies and supporting factors for child safety

Physical Safety Features: Parents mentioned concerns and measures related to preventing children from rolling down windows or opening car doors.

“I’m always worried that my child won’t be able to roll down the windows or open the doors, because sometimes there could be risks, and I never want to go anywhere without considering safety.” (Parent 9).

Behavioral Management and Comfort Strategies: Parents described practical strategies to keep children calm and engaged during trips, such as using hanging toys or other distractions.

“I usually keep my child busy in the car, using things like hanging toys on the back seat to keep him busy.” (Parent 6).

Education

The Education category in child safety encompasses a variety of subcategories aimed at educating parents and the community through diverse methods. These subcategories include using books and media to teach safety principles, instructing parents during licensing processes, raising awareness through police presence and media, and providing specialized programs for school bus drivers and other target groups. Additionally, leveraging modern technologies such as social media, educational animations, and digital programs helps parents easily access safety information, ensuring a broader and more effective dissemination of child safety knowledge.

In this regard, parents say:

“When a couple wants a child, during pregnancy the mother is educated on care/pregnancy care. Public education for mothers should occur, and health clinics providing pregnancy/newborn care and growth monitoring should supply booklets/handouts covering in-vehicle child safety tips and education” (Parent 7).

Child-related factors

Behavioral factors

Parents should be the first to follow rules. It is difficult for children to comply and they need parental help and support. Children don’t understand that not following safety regulations can have harmful consequences for themselves or others. Therefore, parents need to model safe behaviors and take correct actions to prevent risky behaviors. Family members’ characteristics, especially parents, can influence a child’s behavior. In this regard, parents say:

“In early childhood, we spoke to our child that the front seat is for older people, and young children should sit in the back. Now my child does not insist on sitting in the front and happily sits in the back of the car” (Parent 3).

“If a father sees his child crying in the back seat insisting to sit in the front or stand in front of the dashboard to look outside, he must calm the child by talking and explaining, without giving in to their illogical demands, even refusing to drive until the child agrees to sit in the back” (Parent 18).

Risky behavior

In the Risky Behavior category, various subcategories encompass unsafe actions exhibited by children in vehicles. These subcategories include the child’s desire to put their head or hands out of the window, the urge to stand or move inside the car, attempts to open doors or windows, and even taking the steering wheel and trying to control it while driving. These behaviors pose significant safety risks, highlighting the need for parental supervision, proper restraints, and continuous education on vehicle safety. In this regard, parents say:

“My child was around one year and three months. I had placed them unbuckled on the front seat beside me while driving. Then I saw the child had fully opened the vehicle window, and if I hadn’t noticed on time an crash like them falling out was possible, as I had no such experience and it was the first time I had taken the child” (Parent 1).

Cognitive factors

Children have varying cognitive abilities at different ages. For example, younger children may not understand the dangers of unbuckling seat belts or standing in a moving vehicle. They may have trouble judging distances and speeds or get distracted by external stimuli like passing cars or roadside attractions, diverting their attention from safe behavior inside the car. Fear or anxiety related to driving can cause children to become nervous or agitated when getting in the car. In this regard, parents say:

“When a child is seated in the back of the car, due to the distance between the car and surrounding vehicles like bicycles and motorcycles on the roadside or vehicles approaching from the opposite direction, they cannot differentiate them. Also, due to their small stature, other drivers do not easily notice children exiting the car and can cause danger and crashes” (Parent 17).

“Because the children in the back were unbuckled, and due to the front vehicle’s crash and my sudden braking, the children were thrown around inside the car - one child’s head hit the car window and another hit the front seat. The children got scared and cried, and I had to calm them down (Parent 2)”.

Education

The first and most essential step in prevention should be linked to “education”. Based on statistics of registered child crashes in vehicles, educating and raising awareness in young children and parents is vitally important. Thus, safety education should be emphasized as the most important education in early childhood so children can live safely throughout life with this education. In this regard, parents say:

“I educated my child at home, and part was through school/preschool. I remember when my daughter was 3 in preschool, a theater group came demonstrating all rules through performance, like not sitting in front as a child or sticking hands/head out windows” (Parent 12).

Executive/management factors

Executive/management factors are organized into three sub-categories of prevention, compliance with laws, and policymaking..

Prevention

Preventing traffic injuries is a challenge that significantly impacts the health, welfare, and economic growth of societies. Preventive measures to reduce child injuries on roads and necessary planning to protect children are crucial. Preventive behavior occurs when parents feel vulnerable to a hazardous topic and take the potential harm seriously. Governments must prioritize allocating resources to safety programs and budget for education and prevention. They should also allocate funds in annual budgets to subsidize purchasing protective equipment and provide financial aid to parents. Some parents noted subsidizing families, affordable pricing of child safety gear by governments, and allocating subsidies or grants for child safety in households. In this regard, parents say:

“The Ministry of Health and Medical Education can help with education on preventing and reducing child injury risks. Instead of spending on treatment costs from injuries, the ministry should have more effective activities at prevention and education levels” (Parent 17).

Compliance with traffic laws

One of the most important factors is respect for and compliance with laws. The law applies equally to all without exception, and implementing its provisions is undoubtedly necessary. Laws and regulations must be enforced to prevent unsafe driving behaviors. Violating driving laws, failure to establish lawful behavioral patterns among citizens, lack of traffic monitoring and control, and weak enforcement can increase harm from traffic crashes. One action parents must take during their children’s upbringing is properly teaching traffic laws and compliance. Compliance with laws was another issue most parents emphasized. They deemed enforcing driving laws and using safety devices in vehicles essential to reduce risks. Some parents proposed financial penalties for violating safety laws and mandatory child seat use in all private vehicles. In this regard, parents say:

“The manner and level of oversight by the Department of Traffic regarding child safety points is quite pale like the enforcement carried out for seatbelt use in adults and the mature is not applied to children. If this monitoring was established, it would be effective in ensuring child safety inside vehicles…the traffic police could fully monitor roads via cameras, fine offending drivers, stop vehicles, and thereby prevent other risky behaviors” (Parent 20).

Policymaking

Governments must take action at different societal levels to successfully implement, monitor, and sustain safety interventions and regulations. They should also empower communities to make streets safer for children and their guardians. The Ministry of Health and Medical Education uniquely impacts countries and can work with organizations like transportation, environment, and other relevant ministries to effectively reduce road injuries and damage. In this regard, parents say:

“When a traffic crash occurs, various organizations are responsible including police (110), traffic guidance (107), and emergency services (115) as well as hospital emergencies and hospitals themselves. The government must provide the necessary infrastructure for coordination between these organizations to properly perform their duties. Cultural agencies like media and schools are also important. Currently in Iran, different bodies accuse each other of shortcomings regarding crash, such as police blaming car manufacturers for unsafe vehicles, or claims injured patients are not treated sufficiently” (Parent 18).

Cultural factors

Cultural factors were one of the main categories obtained in this research, divided into secondary categories of society, child rearing/communication with children, and safety culture.

Cultivating road safety

Ensuring safety in society depends on all strata. Ensuring safety for children and youth is more sensitive and important as they are most at risk and typically not responsible for incidents. Safety culture on roads refers to collective attitudes, values, and behaviors that prioritize safety while driving. This culture promotes a preventive approach to identify and reduce potential hazards. A strong safety culture is needed to reduce crash, injuries, and road fatalities. It is essential for protecting drivers, passengers, and other road users against associated driving risks. In this regard, parents say:

“Urban cultures differ between cities and provinces, as do rule compliance methods. For example, differences in urban versus apartment living cultures or even lifestyles since I’ve lived in several cities” (Parent 12).

Child rearing/Communication with the child

Appropriate communication skills by parents positively impact all stages of a child’s life. With intimate parental behavior, establishing communication becomes more effective. Positive, effective parental communication indicates respect for the child. Parents should be intimately communicating throughout their child’s life, making the child feel supported and increasing self-confidence. In this regard, parents say:

“Today’s children differ greatly from past generations. You can’t force or angrily talk to a child - friendlier advice works better. Children decide themselves and are emotional at young ages, momentarily letting go of parents’ hands to run in streets - parents must be more sensitive and focused on children, predicting behavior” (Parent 17).

Safety education and awareness raising

Cultural shaping in society should be purposeful, continuous, and sustainable and should start from the early years of the formation of children’s personalities in the family because cultural shaping at lower ages has a greater impact compared to higher ages. Teaching traffic culture from early childhood does not mean that other age groups do not need it. Cultural shaping and increasing public awareness can be strengthened and complemented through official institutions such as education, media, the health system, police force, peer groups, and other related organizations. Therefore, expanding safety culture at the community level such as using child seats and observing safety precautions for children in cars, etc. is completely necessary. In this regard, parents say:

“When a new device is introduced to the market and you want to use it properly, its proper use also needs to be taught, such as using sunroofs in cars where parents open the sunroof just for the enjoyment of children without considering possible crash, and children stand in the middle of the car in a way that their heads stick out of the vehicle” (Parent 13).

Economic/Infrastructure factors

Some subsidiary topics were revealed under these overt titles, including financial capability, vehicle and road safety, and quality.

Financial capability

The cost of purchasing a child seat can affect parents’ access to child seats. Some parents cited the high cost of safety equipment and financial difficulties as the reason for not providing child seats and safety devices in vehicles. Some parents suggested providing free or low-cost child safety seats at the time of vehicle purchase. In this regard, parents say:

“Parents may not provide safety equipment for children for various reasons like ignorance, poverty, low income, unemployment or high prices of special child safety devices in cars” (Parent 20).

Vehicle safety and quality

A vehicle is typically a complex assembly of various parts working together, and the performance of each of these parts affects the vehicle’s functionality and safety. Some parents pointed out that initial safety features and new modifications should be added to vehicle designs to better protect children, and more protection should be provided for children. Issues such as multi-functional safety devices - smart and advanced vehicles - child door locks - installing child seat belts in vehicles - using advanced and smart technology. In this regard, parents say:

“Child locks are installed in vehicles in a way that when you activate the child lock, the door cannot be opened from inside but can be opened from outside. I always use the child lock but am still worried the door may open at any moment” (Parent 11).

“In any case, we pay a cost for the vehicle when purchasing it. Safety equipment should be available inside vehicles from the beginning, and the cost of safety equipment is determined as part of the initial costs by companies, people generally do not have a problem paying the vehicle safety costs along with other costs at the time of purchasing the vehicle from the factory” (Parent 4).

Road infrastructure

Interventions to make roads safe are essential to reduce significant harm. Children are often at greater risk of harm due to their small physique. To protect and reduce vehicle injuries for children, roads need to have adequate safety infrastructure, for example by developing safe infrastructure and observing standards in all areas, presence of warning signs before speed bumps, etc. Weak safety infrastructure exposes children to greater risks of injuries from traffic crashes on roads. In this regard, parents say:

“The speed bumps constructed on routes and roads may not have lighting beside them or warning/caution signs, which always made me fearful that going over them could cause children to be flung forward during braking due to their small physique” (Parent 11).

“A few years ago when I was working in a village, one of the villagers had a van. When returning from his farmland, he had placed two of his children in the back of the vehicle next to the agricultural produce. Due to the vehicle’s high speed and the narrow, curvy road, he was unable to control it, and the vehicle swerved left. Unfortunately, the children were thrown out and died on the spot” (Parent 24).

Discussion

The present study aimed to explain the barriers and challenges of non-compliance with child safety during driving from the perspective of parents. Five main categories were identified through interview analysis including factors related to parents, factors related to the child, executive/management factors, cultural factors, and economic/infrastructure factors, along with 18 sub-categories.

When child safety is not observed during driving, multiple barriers and challenges come with it. These barriers can expose children to a greater risk of physical harm from crashes such as collisions or sudden stops, which can lead to severe consequences like bone fractures, head injuries, or even death, and have long-term emotional and psychological impacts on them. Children may develop anxiety, fear, or post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Injuries from traffic crashes can significantly impact children’s physical, mental, and overall quality of life and well-being.

One of the most important factors in reducing accidental deaths in developed countries is focusing on education [21]. Educating children is the most cost-effective and sustainable approach [22]. In this study, education was considered important by all participants for preventing child traffic crashes. Childhood is a very important period for behavioral development [23]. Children’s traffic behavior is not stable; regular training and education are needed [24]. Considering the mutual influence between parents and children and the impact of their behaviors on each other, parents must be taught safe traffic behaviors and apply them in daily life; educated parents can indirectly teach children through proper behavior [25].

Another key challenge examined in this study was regarding parental use of child car seats, seat belts, and compliance with traffic laws and regulations as well as their implementation. Child safety in vehicles during driving must be prioritized for parents, caregivers, and drivers to ensure children’s overall well-being.

The aim of the Alhaqbani et al. study in Saudi Arabia was to evaluate parents’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding child safety in car seats. Among participants, 47.3% used vehicle safety measures for children. Also, a quarter of participants (24.8%) had the wrong answer when asked if holding a newborn infant in their lap is safer than placing them in infant safety seats, while (5.8%) did not know what is safe (26). Less than 50% of parents use appropriate restraints, studies have shown child restraints reduce the risk of fatal crashes by approximately 70% for infants and 54–80% for young children [27]. In the Hendaus, M. A. et al. study, only a small portion of parents (47%) always used seats, though most believed it protected children in car crashes (82%). We believe this is due to a lack of education on proper/consistent use of seats and seat belts [28].

Some participants pointed out that the financial costs of child seats could impact access to seats, while others showed that there are no excuses (if a family can afford to buy a vehicle, they must prioritize child seats in vehicles) [29].

Some participants, considering the benefits of airbags or air cushions in vehicles, expressed concerns about serious injuries and endangering the lives of children sitting on special seats when airbags open. Exposure to airbags increases the risk of minor injuries such as facial abrasions and chest injuries, as well as moderate to severe injuries, especially head injuries and upper limb fractures [30].

Most participants in this study cited weak enforcement of traffic laws and regulations as the main reason for unsafe road conditions. Many road users disregard traffic rules and regulations, increasing the risk of road traffic collisions (RTCs). For example, drivers often do not brake at yellow lights or stop at red lights, while pedestrians often cross streets. It was also noted that overall awareness of traffic laws is generally low [31]. Driver and passenger behaviors were one of the main safety concerns expressed by parents regarding their children on the roads. Parents felt roads could be improved through construction and enforcement measures. Traffic infrastructure, response to poor/damaged roads, weak management, encroachment of walkways, poor lighting, and improper/unsafe positioning of median breaks between opposing traffic lanes play an important role in collision prevention [31].

Several organizations are involved in child safety and traffic crash prevention related to children, with some overlapping roles and responsibilities. Some organizations do not properly carry out prevention and safety promotion duties, possibly due to a lack of adequate financial resources, weak community infrastructure, inadequate social engagement, etc. It is suggested to designate a single entity for traffic safety and incident prevention oversight, managing involved organizations. Key entities playing roles include the Ministries of Roads & Transportation, Industries, Health, Education & Medical Training, Police Traffic Office, Culture & Islamic Guidance, Education, Medical Organization, Insurance, Interior, Countrywide Emergency Services, Law Enforcement, Media and Judiciary [32].

It is suggested that safety and traffic programs, laws, and regulations be regularly revised and updated based on review data and feedback to ensure ongoing effectiveness.

An important strength of this present study worth noting is that participants were selected from all strata of society (low, high, and varied income levels, ethnic minorities), and children of different ages and genders. Few quantitative studies have addressed barriers and challenges to child safety compliance during driving using qualitative methodologies.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, it was conducted in a single city with a relatively small sample of parents recruited through purposive and snowball sampling, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other regions, cultural contexts, or population groups. Second, the qualitative, self-reported nature of data collection raises the possibility of recall bias and social desirability bias, as participants might have underreported unsafe practices or emphasized more socially acceptable views. Third, the study focused exclusively on parents’ perspectives, thereby missing potentially important insights from children themselves, educators, traffic authorities, and policymakers, which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. Finally, the qualitative design did not allow for assessing the frequency or relative impact of the identified factors. To address these limitations, future research should aim for larger and more diverse samples, include multiple stakeholder perspectives, and consider mixed-methods or longitudinal approaches to explore the dynamics and effectiveness of targeted interventions.

Conclusion

Based on the above findings, it can be concluded that general and specific education, empowering traffic police, a strong monitoring system, appropriate legislation and precise law enforcement, adequate funding allocation, government support, organizational collaboration, road design improvements, appropriate vehicular technologies for prevention and reduction of traffic crashes damages and ensuring child safety seem necessary.

The findings of this research draw attention to engaging families, schools, teachers, other institutions, and social establishments for traffic safety education and developing a safety culture that can effectively impact drivers’ risk comprehension, risky behavior reduction, and attitude regarding child traffic security.
