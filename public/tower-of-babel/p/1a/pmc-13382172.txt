Beyond valve replacement: integrating structural intervention and optimal medical therapy for better long-term outcomes
Authors: Mariagrazia Piscione, Marco Zimarino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractUnlabelled Image

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has transformed the management of severe aortic stenosis, offering a safe and effective treatment improving both survival and quality of life [1], [2]. However, procedural success does not necessarily translate into optimal long-term outcomes [3]. Despite continuous advances in device technology and refinement in procedural techniques, many patients remain at considerable risk of death and heart failure (HF) hospitalization after TAVI [3]. Even though the valvular defect has been corrected, the patient's cardiovascular vulnerability often persists.

In this scenario, the landscape of HF management has been profoundly reshaped by the introduction of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. Initially developed as glucose-lowering agents, these drugs have demonstrated consistent and robust benefits across the entire spectrum of HF, regardless of left ventricular ejection fraction, significantly reducing cardiovascular mortality and worsening HF events [4], [5]. Given the high prevalence of HF and the systemic nature of cardiovascular disease in the post-TAVI population, the potential extension of this evidence-based therapy to this specific subset of patients appears both logical and highly desirable.

In this issue of Int J Cardiol Heart & Vasculature, Spadafora et al. address an increasingly relevant clinical question through a systematic review and exploratory meta-analysis evaluating the impact of SGLT2 inhibitors after TAVI [6]. Their analysis suggests a favourable association with lower mortality and HF hospitalization while maintaining an appropriately cautious interpretation of the available data [6]. This balanced approach enhances the credibility of their work and highlights the need for dedicated randomised studies to establish causality.

However, the most important implication of this work extends beyond the pharmacological effect of SGLT2 inhibitors themselves [6]. Rather, it challenges a long-standing conceptual separation between structural heart interventions and contemporary HF management.

Over the past decade, structural cardiology and HF medicine have progressed along successful but largely independent paths. On one side, innovations in transcatheter devices, procedural planning, and multimodality imaging have transformed the safety and efficacy of TAVI [7]. On the other, landmark pharmacological advances have changed the prognosis of patients with HF [8]. Yet, despite sharing the same patients, these two disciplines have too often remained divided, with limited integration between procedural intervention and long-term disease-modifying therapy. Today, such separation is no longer tenable.

Patients undergoing TAVI are typically elderly, frail, and burdened by multiple cardiovascular comorbidities [9]. In this context, TAVI should no longer be viewed as the endpoint of treatment but rather as the beginning of a comprehensive cardiovascular care pathway. Successful intervention restores forward flow; optimal medical therapy (OMT) must subsequently preserve that benefit by targeting the persistent mechanisms responsible for adverse remodelling, congestion, neurohormonal activation, and recurrent hospitalization [8].

The emergence of SGLT2 inhibitors fits naturally within this evolving paradigm. They have consistently demonstrated cardiovascular and renal protection across a broad spectrum of patients, irrespective of diabetic status [8]. Their favourable safety profile makes them particularly attractive candidates for post-TAVI management [8]. While dedicated evidence in this population remains limited, the biological rationale is compelling, and the current meta-analysis provides encouraging preliminary support.

Nevertheless, pharmacological optimization after TAVI represents only one side of the equation. The future of post-TAVI care must be rooted in the principles of precision medicine, which requires a shift from the empiric correction of laboratory values to a more nuanced, individualised approach. Precision medicine is not simply about introducing new therapies; it is equally about abandoning conventional interventions that fail to improve outcomes or, worse, prove harmful. An instructive example of this transition is provided by the TRITAVI study, which challenged a deeply rooted clinical practice by demonstrating that routine preprocedural blood transfusion in mildly anaemic patients was not only ineffective and potentially deleterious [10]. Conversely, precision medicine also demands that we abandon nihilistic attitudes toward complex patients who were previously excluded from treatment. A compelling case in point is the management of patients with concurrent cardiac amyloidosis and severe aortic stenosis. Emerging evidence suggests that the presence of amyloidosis should not preclude therapeutic intervention; rather, it identifies a unique clinical phenotype that warrants a personalized, multidisciplinary approach, where TAVI serves as a critical component of a comprehensive disease-modifying strategy [11]. Ultimately, selecting the right patient, optimizing volume status, and correcting reversible conditions challenge a deeply rooted clinical practice and highlight the importance of individualised, evidence-based decision making rather than empiric correction of laboratory abnormalities.

This concept should now extend throughout the entire TAVI journey. Rather than considering procedural success as the primary endpoint, clinicians should adopt a longitudinal vision including preprocedural careful planning, meticulous intervention, and structured postprocedural medical management. Such integration requires closer collaboration between interventional cardiologists, HF specialists, imaging experts, geriatricians, and primary care physicians.

Future randomised trials evaluating SGLT2 inhibitors after TAVI will undoubtedly provide more definitive evidence. However, regardless of their final results, they already point toward a broader transformation in the way we care for patients with structural heart disease. The future will not be defined solely by better valves or more sophisticated devices, but by the ability to integrate procedural excellence with comprehensive disease-modifying therapy.

Ultimately, the greatest innovation may not lie in the next generation of transcatheter valves, but in abandoning a procedure-centred vision in favour of a patient-centred continuum of care. Structural intervention opens the door; optimal medical therapy determines how far the patient ultimately walks through it.
