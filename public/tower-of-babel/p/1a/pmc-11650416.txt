Lesion monitoring parameters as predictors of atrial arrhythmia recurrence after catheter ablation in persistent AF: A DECAAF II sub‐analysis
Authors: Mario Mekhael, Han Feng, Hadi Younes, Nour Chouman, Ala Assaf, Chanho Lim, Chao Huang, Eoin Donnellan, Swati Rao, Nassir Marrouche, Omar Kreidieh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe formation of transmural lesions is necessary for the ablation of persistent atrial fibrillation (prAF). Ablation index (AI) and generator impedance drop (ID) predict lesion size but their correlation with long‐term outcomes in prAF is not known. Furthermore, we proposed a new parameter, efficacy ratio (ER) calculated as ID/AI, to gain indirect insight into the role of factors affecting ID but not considered by AI.MethodsWe included ablations performed during the DECAAF II trial if they had uploaded lesion‐by‐lesion summary data and were performed with radiofrequency catheters on the CARTO system. Average patient‐level parameters were calculated from all generated Vizitags.ResultsA total of 427 ablations met inclusion criteria and 166 utilized AI. Analyzed as continuous variables, ID and ER predicted long‐term arrhythmia‐free survival but not AI. The ideal cut‐off for ID was ID ≥ 10.4 ohms and had a C‐index of 0.55. It predicted reduced risk of arrhythmia: hazard ratio 0.56 [0.36–0.88], p = .013 (arrhythmia‐free survival of 67% vs. 52%). Similarly, an ER of 1.7 ohms/100AI had a C‐index of 0.58 and predicted reduced arrhythmia recurrence: HR 0.39 [0.22–0.69], p = .001. ER < 1.7 ohms/100AI was related to just 32% arrhythmia‐free survival. ER improved prognostication as compared to ID alone and identified a subset of low ID patients with even worse outcomes.ConclusionAverage ID was predictive of improved outcomes following ablation of prAF. The ratio of ID/AI (ER) was postulated as a measure to summarize the overall impact of factors not considered in the AI formula and provided improved prognostication.

INTRODUCTION

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) remains the cornerstone of ablative treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF). 1 , 2 Despite advancements in technologies and lesion prediction algorithms, the durability of PVI remains imperfect and AF recurrences are frequently associated with pulmonary vein reconnections. 3 Another suspected source for AF recurrences is the presence of extra‐pulmonary vein substrate which is postulated to play an oversized role in the persistent AF (PrAF) population. Recently several attempts to improve outcomes of radiofrequency‐based ablation of PrAF have focused on targeting extra‐pulmonary vein substrate with varying results. Less attention has been focused on the potential role of improving the quality of lesion formation and PVI during radiofrequency ablation.

Several ablation parameters have been studied as surrogates for lesion formation. Impedance drop (ID) is consistently correlated with lesion size and occurs during radiofrequency‐based ablation due to a reduction in resistivity of heated myocardium. More recently, prediction algorithms such as ablation index (AI) use biophysical parameters that contribute to lesion formation to estimate lesion size. 4 , 5 , 6 While proven to be accurate, AI considers only the effects of lesion duration, power, and contact force while ignoring other contributors such as catheter‐tissue orientation, convective cooling, stability, baseline circuit impedance, presence of scar, adipocyte infiltration, and local tissue vulnerability. We, therefore, propose that the ratio of ID to AI which we will term efficacy ratio (ER) may be used as a readily calculatable parameter to assess the overall impact of those “other variables” not measured in AI (ER = ID/AI). Patients in whom those other variables have an overall favorable impact on tissue heating and ID would have a high ER (high ID/AI ratio). Conversely, wherever those “other” factors contribute negatively, one would expect a low ER (less ID per AI contribution).

Despite the wide‐spread availability of such biophysical parameters that allow for appraisal of lesion formation, their use has not been studied in detail in PrAF. We sought to test two hypotheses: First, that the quality of lesion formation as measured from widely available biophysical parameters remains an important predictor of outcomes in PrAF. Second, we propose specifically that a calculated ratio of ID/AI will provide new prognostic information in this population as compared to ID or AI alone.

METHODS

Study design and population

The DECAAF II trial design and methods have been previously described. 7 Patients were included if they had PrAF and were undergoing a first‐time AF ablation. Major exclusion criteria were a contraindication to gadolinium and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and previous AF ablation or valvular cardiac surgery. From July 2016 through January 2020, patients were recruited and randomly assigned to undergo ablation targeting left atrial fibrosis as detected on delayed‐enhancement MRI in addition to PVI, or PVI only. Treatment assignment was masked from patients, and the duration of follow‐up was 12–18 months after randomization. For our sub‐analysis, we included only ablations performed using radiofrequency ablation via the CARTO system (Biosense Webster). We further excluded any ablation procedures where cryo‐catheters were used. Summary data about lesion‐by‐lesion characteristics were provided for each case. This study was approved by the Tulane University Biomedical Institutional Review Board.

Biophysical parameters

Summary data regarding the maximum power, lesion duration, average contact force, average stability, baseline impedance, and ID were available for review. AI was also available from operators who utilized the Surpoint module. The AI is calculated from a proprietary formula that combines contact force, radiofrequency time, and radiofrequency power in a non‐linear fashion.

We defined ER as the ratio of ID over AI (ID/AI). Since ID reflects the tissue level response to ablation from heating, and AI predicts such responses based on certain biophysical parameters, we postulated that the ratio of those parameters provides insight in the role of factors not considered in AI. The lower the ratio, the more difficult it is to achieve tissue heating or ID for a given contribution of AI. Conversely, a higher ratio reflects a greater degree of tissue heating for a given contribution from contact force/power/duration. Since the ratio may be artificially lowered in very long ablation applications because of a prolonged impedance plateau during the terminal phase of ablation delivery, we also performed a sub‐analysis that excluded long lesions of AI > 500 from the calculation of ER.

Primary outcome

The primary end point of the study was the first confirmed recurrence of AA (including AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia) lasting for at least 30 s after the 90‐day blanking period, demonstrated by at least two consecutive 1‐lead smartphone electrocardiogram (ECG) device tracings, one positive reading on a clinical 12‐lead ECG tracing, ambulatory monitor, or if the patient underwent repeat ablation. The daily smartphone ECGs were intended as the primary method for assessing AA recurrence, but clinical and ambulatory ECGs served as back‐up methods for detecting recurrence in patients who failed to reliably transmit smartphone ECG readings. A core laboratory at the University of Washington adjudicated the ECG findings.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Patients underwent Late Gadolinium Enhancement Magnetic Resonance Imaging (LGE‐MRI) within 30 days before ablation using the Merisight delayed‐enhancement MRI protocol (MARREK Inc.). Left atrial volume (LAV) was calculated for each patient using LGE‐MRI.

Statistical analysis

Baseline demographics and patient characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviation. For each subject, we calculated the mean of relevant biophysical parameters from all Visitags generated during the patient's ablation procedure. Baseline demographics, risk factors, and imaging predictors of mean ID and mean ER were assessed. The predictive value of the primary outcome for each biophysical parameter was first tested using Cox regression. The corresponding p‐value and Harrell's C‐index were calculated to reflect the diagnostic accuracy for each test. An ideal cutoff value for each predictor was deduced using grid searching based on the maximum hazard ratio (HR). We also assessed the effect of ER on the primary outcome in patients with high and low AI using Kaplan‐Meier curves and the log‐rank tests.

A multi‐variable Cox regression analysis was then performed to assess the effect of AI, ID, and ER on the primary outcome after adjusting for different covariates: Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), antiarrhythmic medications (before the ablation), congestive heart failure, hypertension, stroke, smoking, LAV, and LA fibrosis. The relationship between predictors such as ID, AI, and ER with post‐ablation scar was also studied using Spearman correlation.

Results were further validated through KM curves under the whole population and then stratified by subgroups with respect to different treatment arms (PVI‐only and Fibrosis‐guided ablation plus PVI groups). Concordance and discordance between biophysical parameters were examined. All the analyses were performed through R version 4.3.0.

The majority of excluded patients had ablations with other mapping systems (320 subjects), had cryo‐ablation despite using the CARTO system (34 subjects), and a minority did not have recoverable case date (34 subjects). Average ID was a significant predictor of arrhythmia free survival. A cutoff of 10.4 ohms produced a C‐index of 0.55 (p = .004) and predicted a lower HR of primary outcome occurrence (0.56 [0.36–0.88], p = .013). The subgroup of patients with average ID ≥ 10.4 constituted only 20% of the population but this subgroup had excellent arrhythmia free survival: 67% versus 52% among patients with ID ≥ 10.4 versus ID < 10.4, respectively (65% vs. 48% in the PVI only subgroup, and 69% vs. 55% in the PVI + imaging guided ablation) (Figure 2).

When considered as a continuous variable, AI did not predict arrhythmia free survival, however, we considered the possibility that a threshold effect exists that may be seen during categorization. An optimal cutoff mean AI > 395 produced significant prediction of arrhythmia free survival with C‐index of 0.55 (p = .05) and predicted a lower HR of primary outcome occurrence of 0.62 [0.39–0.99].

The mean ER which combines both ID and AI were the most predictive both in continuous and categorized fashion. Our ideal cutoff value of 1.73 ohms/100AI produced a C‐index of 0.58 (p < .001) and a HR of 0.39 [0.22–0.69] for arrhythmic recurrence, p = .001. The findings were similar across treatment groups (PVI only or PVI + MRI guided ablation) (Figure 3). Patients who had an ER of less than 1.73 ohms/100AI were noted to have particularly poor arrhythmia free survival: Survival rates being 63% versus 32% among patients with ER ≥ 1.73 ohm/100AI versus ER < 1.73 ohm/100AI (67% vs. 22% in the PVI only subgroup, and 59% vs. 42% in the PVI + fibrosis‐guided ablation). The predictivity of ER was maintained when excluding long lesions of AI > 500 or AI > 550 (Figure S1).

Discordance between parameters

ID and AI

In the 166 patients with AI data, 98 patients had concordant categorization (33 patients had an AI ≥ 395 and an ID ≥ 10.4 ohms while 65 patients had low AI and ID). The discordant group included 53 patients with AI ≥ 395 but an ID < 10.4 ohms, and only 15 had an ID ≥ 10.4 ohms and AI < 395. The group of patients with ID < 10.4 ohms despite AI ≥ 395 exhibited the worst outcomes, suggesting that the presence of low ID despite “adequate” energy titration was an especially poor prognosticator. Taken as a reference, all other groups had improved arrhythmia free survival including the group of low ID and low AI: AI < 395, ID ≥ 10.4 ohms (0.31 [0.11–0.88], p = .03), group with AI ≥ 395, ID ≥ 10.4 ohms (0.37 [0.18–0.76], p = .01), and patients with an AI < 395, ID < 10.4 ohms (0.48 [0.29–0.82], p = .01).

ID and ER

In the 166 patients with ER data, 48 patients had an ER ≥ 1.73 ohms/100AI and an ID ≥ 10.4 ohms, 81 patients had an ID ≥ 10.4 ohms but an ER < 1.73 ohms/100AI, and only 37 patients had low ER and ID. When taking patients with low ER and ID as a reference, both groups with ER ≥ 1.73 ohms/100AI, ID ≥ 10.4 ohms (0.33 [0.17–0.63], p < .001) and patients with an ER ≥ 1.73 ohms/AI, ID < 10.4 ohms (0.50 [0.30–0.84], p = .01) did significantly better (Figure 4).

Relationship to ablation‐induced scar

Only ID was significantly correlated with ablation‐induced scar on the 3‐month MRI (r = .11, p = .031). In contrast, AI (r = .10, p = .24) and ER (r = .11, p = .17) were not predictive with a smaller sample size.

DISCUSSION

In our study, we report several important findings: Predictors of lesion formation quality including ID, ER, and categorized AI were related to long‐term arrhythmia free survival following ablation of PerAF. This suggests that optimizing lesion formation is still an important target for improving outcomes in this patient subgroup. AI was an imperfect but statistically significant predictor of ID. There existed a resistant subgroup of patients for whom adequate energy titration as measured by AI did not produce high ID and this subgroup exhibited the worst arrhythmia free survival. The ratio of ID/AI which we named ER provided insight into this phenomenon and improved prognostic information when compared to either ID or AI alone. Additionally, the effect of ER on the primary outcome persisted even after excluding lesions with high AI where a prolonged impedance plateau may artificially impact the calculation. The relationship between these factors and arrhythmic recurrence may be mediated by differences in lesion formation since ID was significantly associated with post‐ablation scar formation on cardiac MRI.

The theoretical importance of suggested biophysical parameters

Several biophysical parameters have been proposed to monitor lesion delivery. The AI accurately predicts lesion size based on estimating the effects of contact force, application duration, and power. 5 , 6 However, a limitation of this parameter is that it does not consider other relevant parameters such as tissue sensitivity, catheter orientation, convective cooling etc. 8 ID is conversely a response parameter that provides an accurate reflection of lesion size and may relate to tissue level heating during ablation. One caveat is that a similar ID may often be reached with high or low inputs of power, application duration, contact force etc. It can be hypothesized that achieving the same ID with lower inputs may reflect a more “ablatable” substrate with potentially different prognosis. The proposed ratio (ER = ID/AI) includes both the ID and the AI (reflecting the inputs of CF, Power, Lesion duration). This can provide information regarding such tissue level sensitivity and the contribution of other factors not measured in AI including catheter orientation. The higher the AI needed to achieve a specific ID, the more difficult it is to create a lesion. Conversely, for a fixed AI, larger IDs reflect better tissue sensitivity and optimized ablation technique. In clinical practice, ablations, where ID is not ideal may have occurred either because the electrophysiologist did not provide enough input of duration‐power‐contact force as measured by AI, because the technical approach and catheter orientation are not ideal, or because the underlying substrate is relatively resistant despite adequate inputs of the relevant parameters. Differentiating amongst those subgroups using ER may help clarify differing prognosis based on the etiology of low ID (Low energy input vs other factors).

The prognostic significance of lesion evaluation parameters in PrAF

It is notable that examination of biophysical parameters indicative of lesion formation identified subsets of patients with excellent or especially poor arrhythmia free survival. An average ID of 10.4 ohms or more predicted an excellent 67% arrhythmia free survival—a number closer to that of paroxysmal AF cohorts than prAF, especially given the frequency of rhythm monitoring in the DECAAF II trial. Conversely, patients with low ER potentially reflecting low tissue sensitivity or poor technique had a dismal arrhythmia free survival of 32%. While several recent studies have focused on extra‐pulmonary vein ablation to improve outcomes of radiofrequency‐based ablation for PrAF 9 , 10 with mixed results, the prognostic significance of lesion monitoring parameters brings back to focus the importance of optimizing lesion creation in this population. Interestingly, it is not entirely clear that up‐titration of parameters such as contact force, power, and application duration may be adequate for all patients. For example, we saw in our study a group of patients with a higher contribution of these parameters as measured by AI but still low ID and/or ER, and an especially poor arrhythmia free survival. We must consider in this case two hypotheses: First, we can consider whether these patients may be relatively resistant to radiofrequency‐based ablation but may be more amenable to ablation with other modalities. Second, we can consider that the ratio of ID to AI may also correlate with factors that impact prognosis separately from lesion formation. For example, lesion formation can be compromised and less predictable when ablating underlying fibrotic tissue, 11 , 12 and heavy fibrosis is often concomitantly associated with the presence of extra‐pulmonary vein substrate.

Lessons for impedance‐guided ablation

Previous studies have shown feasibility for impedance guided ablation to ensure adequate contact force 13 and lesion durability. 14 The current study adds that such impedance monitoring remains important even when contact force is measured and outperforms most other factors in predicting ablation success. Furthermore, impedance reduction continues to have the greatest importance even in a PrAF population where poor outcomes are often attributed to extra‐pulmonary vein substrate instead of inadequate lesion formation. Indeed, even amongst prAF patients, achieving adequate IDs > 10.4 ohms ensured excellent ablation success that mirrored that of paroxysmal cohorts. None‐the‐less, we also documented that the ability to achieve ID is variable based on patient differences in ER. We observed some prAF patients who have limited impedance reductions in response to high titration of power‐force‐duration. In such cases, it is prudent to attempt to alter technique and re‐orient the catheter to optimize ablation. In cases were the ER remains <1.73 ohms/100AI despite such attempts, it may even be prudent to consider alternative non‐radiofrequency‐based strategies given the predictably poor arrhythmia‐free survival in this study. Furthermore, future studies must try to improve our understanding of the factors that impact lesion formation in response to a set amount of applied energy, for example: obesity which creates resistance to current delivery, atrial scar/fatty infiltration which creates potential thermal insulation, atrial wall thickness, and modifiable procedural factors.

Limitations

Our study presents several limitations. First, the module to calculate AI was used in a small number of ablation cases and, therefore, the sample size for AI and ER analysis is smaller than the general cohort. We included only ablations performed as part of the CARTO system to avoid any systematic errors from differences in impedance reduction related to the differing technologies that would have occurred if we had considered multiple systems. We did not take into account the regionality of the lesions. Different walls within the LA have varying thicknesses and compositions as well as different commonly used AI targets which might affect outcomes. Operators were not blinded to ID or AI, and this may affect their titration of these parameters. While we proposed a new ratio to examine tissue “ablataility,” ER, this will still need validation in larger prospective cohorts. Last, our findings cannot be extended to paroxysmal AF.

CONCLUSION

Biophysical parameters including ID were significant predictors of AA recurrence in patients with PrAF undergoing CA. We propose the use of a new prognostic ratio, ER = ID/AI to assess the tissue‐heating in response to a given contribution of power‐force‐duration. The ER performed superiorly to ID and AI in predicting recurrence particularly when there is discrepancy between both parameters. Future trials are needed to prospectively assess AA recurrence rates based on those parameters and consider potential interventions such as specific ID targets or use of non‐radiofrequency‐based ablation for patients with a low ratio ER (ID/AI). The clinical and technical determinants of ER need to be better elucidated.
