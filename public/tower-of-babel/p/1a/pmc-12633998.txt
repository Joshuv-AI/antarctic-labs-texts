The mediating role of parental involvement in the relationship between adolescent mental health and academic performance: Effects of parental marital status and postdivorce living arrangements
Authors: Yaning Meng, Bo Zhang, Hong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:This study examines the interrelationships between adolescent mental health, academic performance, and parental involvement, focusing on the influence of parental marital status and postdivorce living arrangements.Methods:A large-scale open online dataset was analyzed to assess correlations and mediating effects among these variables.Results:Significant negative correlations were found between mental health problems and both academic performance and parental involvement (father and mother communication). Adolescents with divorced parents reported poorer mental health and lower parental involvement than those with married parents, though no significant differences emerged in academic performance. Students living with mothers showed higher academic achievement in Chinese and English, but not in mathematics. Parental involvement, particularly mother-child communication, partially mediated the relationship between mental health and academic performance.Conclusion:The findings highlight the importance of strong parent-child communication, especially postdivorce, and provide evidence for targeted interventions supporting adolescents from diverse family structures.

Introduction

Mental health refers to an individual’s capacity to adapt to a developing environment, maintain a well-rounded personality.[1] According to the “2022 Survey Report on the Mental Health Status of Adolescents” released by the Institute of Psychology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, among 30,000 adolescents surveyed, 14.8% exhibited varying degrees of depression risk.[2] This concerning trend is accompanied by an increasing incidence of emotional disorders, including anxiety, self-harm, and suicidal problems.[34] So the mental health of middle school students has garnered significant attention in recent years.

The mental health problem always complies with academic problems in adolescents, because students with mental health problems often demonstrate deficits in stress management during the learning process, leading to decreased academic performance.[56] Negative emotions such as anxiety and depression can also interfere with attention and cognitive processes, diminishing learning efficiency and academic achievement.[78] Mental health can also be influenced by marital status. Research indicates that children from divorced families often exhibit heightened anxiety, depression, and behavioral problems.[910] Postdivorce family conflicts and economic pressures can adversely affect children’s psychological well-being,[1112] which in turn may impair their academic functioning. Additionally, parental marital status influences academic performance directly, particularly when divorce results in reduced parental availability and involvement in children’s educational activities. However, there remains limited research examining how specific postdivorce living arrangements—particularly which parent (father or mother) the child primarily resides with, impact mental health and academic performance.

Parental involvement, defined as the active participation of parents or guardians in children’s education and development,[1314] potentially serves as a mediating variable in the relationship between mental health and academic performance.[15] Research indicates that active parental involvement can significantly improve students’ academic outcomes and enhance mental health,[161718] also reducing depression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents.

This study aims to investigate three critical aspects: (1) the relationship between students’ mental health and academic performance, (2) the impact of parents’ marital status and postdivorce living arrangements on mental health and academic performance, and (3) the mediating role of parental involvement in the relationship between mental health and academic performance. Through this investigation, we seek to provide evidence-based intervention strategies and guardianship arrangement recommendations for schools and parents, ultimately contributing to improved outcomes for adolescents from diverse family structures.

Method

Participants

This study utilized data from the China Education Panel Survey (CEPS) conducted by the Chinese Survey and Data Center of Renmin University of China. (http://www.cnsda.org/index.php?r=projects/view&id=61662993). We use the data collected during the 2013–2014 academic year, which employed a stratified sampling approach that randomly selected 28 county-level units, 112 schools, and 438 classes. From an initial 10,750 participants, we excluded invalid and incomplete responses pertaining to students’ gender, academic performance (Chinese, Mathematics, English, and weighted scores), parental marital status, single-parent family living arrangements, parent-child communication, and mental health scores. This yielded 8,060 valid data points for analysis. The final sample included 3,921 male students (48.60%), 576 students from divorced families (7.10%), 273 students living with their fathers after divorce (3.40%), and 213 students living with their mothers (2.60%) (see Table 1 for demographic results).

Research tools

Mental health

The CEPS employed a 10-item scale (items c2501–c2510) to evaluate students’ mental health status. Students responded to questions such as “How often have you felt depressed in the past seven days?” on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Always). The cumulative score reflects students’ mental health status, with higher scores indicating poorer mental well-being. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.912).

Parent-child communication

Parental involvement was assessed through separate measures of father-child and mother-child communication. Four items (a2101a–a2104a) evaluated father involvement, while four parallel items (a2101b–a2104b) measured mother involvement. A representative item was “How often do your parents discuss things that happen at school with you?” Responses were recorded on a 3-point scale (1 = Never, 2 = Occasionally, 3 = Often). Higher cumulative scores indicate greater parental involvement. Both scales demonstrated strong reliability (Cronbach’s α =0.821 and 0.824 for father-child and mother-child communication, respectively).

Academic performance

Students’ academic performance was measured using midterm examination raw scores in Chinese (w2chn), Mathematics (w2mat), and English (w2eng) from the fall semester of 2014.

Results

Descriptive statistics of variables

The collected data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS program version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The reliability of the scales is presented as an internal consistency coefficient (Cronbach’s α).

Correlation analysis

To examine the relationships among study variables, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted on the total scores for each dimension [Table 2]. Results revealed systematic patterns of associations among mental health, academic performance metrics, and parental involvement. Mental health demonstrated significant negative correlations with all other variables, including weighted academic performance (r = −0.088, P < 0.01), subject-specific performance measures (Chinese: r = −0.044; Mathematics: r = −0.087; English: r = −0.093; all P < 0.01), and parental involvement dimensions (total parental involvement: r = −0.158; father-child communication: r = −0.148; mother-child communication: r = −0.133; all P < 0.01). These findings suggest that higher mental health problems are associated with lower academic achievement and parental engagement. The academic performance variables showed significant positive correlations with parental involvement measures (total parental involvement: r = 0.203, father-child communication: r = 0.139, mother-child communication: r = 0.218, all P < 0.01). These consistent positive associations indicate the interaction between academic achievement and family engagement factors.

Parental marital status and living arrangements in single-parent families influence each variable

Independent samples t-tests revealed significant differences between children of divorced and nondivorced parents across multiple variables [Table 3]. Students with divorced parents reported poorer mental health scores compared to those with married parents (t = 4.141, P < 0.001). Similarly, parental involvement was significantly lower in divorced families, with marked differences in total parental involvement (t = −9.494, P < 0.001), father-child communication (t = −8.851, P < 0.001), and mother-child communication (t = −6.736, P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in academic performance measures, including weighted academic scores, Chinese, Mathematics, and English (all P > 0.05). Since parental marital status affects mental health, parent-child communication, and parental involvement but does not affect academic performance, we further explored whether living with one parent in single-parent families also affects these dimensions.

Mediating effect of parental involvement

To investigate the mediating role of parental involvement in the relationship between mental health and academic performance, a mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 4) with 5,000 bootstrap samples and 95% confidence intervals. In the mediation model [Figure 1], mental health was designated as the independent variable, academic performance as the dependent variable, and parental involvement as the mediating variable due to parents’ gender-specific variations in academic performance and parental involvement. Results revealed that mental health significantly negative influenced both father-child communication (β = −0.041, P < 0.001) and mother-child communication (β = −0.038, P < 0.001). Regarding pathways to academic performance, father-child communication demonstrated no significant effect (P > 0.05), whereas mother-child communication exhibited a significant positive influence (β =2.081, P < 0.001). The direct effect of mental health on academic performance remained significant (β = −0.1744, P < 0.001).

Mediation analysis

As presented in Table 5, the total effect coefficient was −0.2602 (P < 0.001), indicating a significant negative influence of mental health on academic performance. The direct effect accounted for 67.03% of the total effect, while the indirect effect constituted 32.97%. Bootstrap estimates yielded standard errors of 0.0326 for the direct effect and 0.0090 for the indirect effect, with 95% confidence intervals of [−0.2383, −0.1105] and [−0.1040, −0.0687], respectively. Neither confidence interval contained zero, confirming the statistical significance of both effects.

Discussion

The present study utilized a large-scale online dataset to investigate the complex relationships between mental health, academic performance, and parental involvement among adolescents, with particular attention to the impact of parental marital status and living arrangements in single-parent families. We found significant negative correlations between mental health problems and both academic performance and parental involvement (i.e. father and mother communication). Students with divorced parents reported poorer mental health scores and lower levels of parental involvement compared to those with married parents; however, no significant differences were observed in academic performance between groups. Students living with mothers demonstrated significantly higher academic achievement in weighted academic performance than those living with fathers, especially in Chinese and English subjects, but no significant differences were observed in mental health outcomes or mathematics performance. Parental involvement functions as a partial mediator in the relationship between mental health and academic performance, particularly good mother-child communication, which subsequently contributes to better academic outcomes.

The significant negative correlation we found between mental health problems and academic performance aligns with established research,[1920] confirming that psychological distress may impair cognitive functioning and learning capacity. This relationship underscores the importance of addressing mental health concerns to improve academic outcomes among adolescents.[56] Our results also revealed negative correlations between mental health problems and parental involvement (both father and mother communication), suggesting that quality parent-child interactions may serve as protective factors against psychological distress. This finding supports previous research indicating that parental engagement can buffer against the development of mental health problems in adolescents.[2122]

The observation that students with divorced parents reported poorer mental health scores compared to those with nondivorced parents aligns with prior research documenting the psychological impact of family dissolution.[1123] This finding highlights the emotional vulnerability that can accompany changes in family structure and suggests the need for targeted mental health interventions for adolescents from divorced families. Interestingly, despite differences in mental health outcomes, we found no significant difference in academic performance between students from married versus divorced families. This unexpected finding challenges assumptions about the direct impact of divorce on academic achievement and suggests that other factors (such as parents’ communication, minimizing conflict) may help compensate for the potential academic disadvantages associated with parental divorce.[24]

Most interesting, our finding that students living with mothers demonstrated significantly higher academic achievement, particularly in language subjects (Chinese and English), contributes novel insights to the limited literature on postdivorce living arrangements. The possible explanations are that mothers may place greater emphasis on verbal and language skills development or provide more support for language-related homework[2526]; communication patterns typically associated with mother-child relationships may foster linguistic development more effectively.[27]

Perhaps most significantly, our results identify parental involvement, especially mother-child communication as a partial mediator in the relationship between mental health and academic performance. This finding extends previous research and provides a mechanistic explanation for how mental health influences academic outcomes indicating that good parent-child communication may buffer against the negative academic outcomes of mental health challenges.[282930] This mediation effect highlights the potential for parental involvement interventions (especially mother-child communication) as a strategic approach to mitigating the academic outcomes of mental health problems.[3132]

This study advances our understanding of how family structure influences adolescent development by highlighting the complex interplay between mental health, academic performance, and parental involvement. The identification of maternal custody as advantageous for language-based academic subjects and the mediating role of parental involvement in the mental health-academic performance relationship provides valuable insights for developing effective interventions. By addressing these interconnected factors, educational and family support systems can better promote positive outcomes for adolescents from diverse family structures.

Limitations and future aspects

Several important limitations of the present study should be considered. First, as the study is based on the cross-sectional data, causal relationships between the variables cannot be established. Future research should employ longitudinal studies to better understand the temporal relationships among mental health levels, parental involvement, and academic performance. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Future research could incorporate multiple data sources, such as teacher reports and school records, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of academic performance and parental involvement and investigate the mediating effects of these factors. Third, as the study is conducted within the cultural context of China, the relationships among parental involvement, mental health, and academic performance may differ across cultural settings, and the sample from a specific region in China may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future research should consider diverse cultural contexts to explore whether similar patterns exist in different regions or countries and to enhance the generalizability and applicability of the findings.
