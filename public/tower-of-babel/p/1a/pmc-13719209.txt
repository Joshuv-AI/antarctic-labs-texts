Hepatocellular Carcinoma Associated with Hepatic Sarcoidosis and Budd-Chiari Syndrome: A Case Report and Review of the Literature
Authors: Yuta Minami, Takanobu Iwadare, Yuma Kamijo, Masahiro Kurozumi, Yoshinori Sato, Mai Iwaya, Takefumi Kimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatic sarcoidosis may progress to cirrhosis, whereas Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS) is characterized by a hepatic venous outflow obstruction and it is associated with the risk of liver injury. The coexistence of hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS is extremely rare, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) has not been previously reported in this setting. We describe the case of a 71-year-old woman with cutaneous sarcoidosis who presented with HCC and was diagnosed with BCS and hepatic granulomas with advanced fibrosis. This case suggests that these two conditions may act additively or synergistically to create a pro-carcinogenic hepatic environment. Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, hepatic sarcoidosis, Budd-Chiari syndrome

Introduction

Hepatic sarcoidosis is a manifestation of systemic sarcoidosis that manifests as noncaseating epithelioid granulomas in the liver (1). Although often asymptomatic, hepatic sarcoidosis can progress to hepatic fibrosis, a well-established risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) in chronic liver disease. However, direct evidence linking hepatic sarcoidosis with hepatocarcinogenesis remains limited.

Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS) is a rare disorder caused by a hepatic venous outflow obstruction, leading to hepatic congestion, fibrosis, and portal hypertension. Together with an increased hepatic arterial flow, these factors promote hepatocarcinogenesis (2).

The coexistence of hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS is extremely rare, with only a few such cases found in the literature, and no reports describing HCC development in such patients to date. We herein describe the first case of HCC in a combined setting of hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS, and discuss the potential synergistic mechanisms of hepatocarcinogenesis.

Case Report

A 71-year-old woman was diagnosed with cutaneous sarcoidosis at the age of X-4 years after presenting with a right lower leg ulcer and she was subsequently followed up at a local clinic. She had a history of a mildly abnormal liver function during routine health checkups since her late twenties, with no further evaluation. At the time of the diagnosis of cutaneous sarcoidosis, her liver biochemistry results showed slight abnormalities for aspartate aminotransferase (38 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (36 U/L), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (129 U/L), and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) (122 U/L). The patient's medical history included cutaneous sarcoidosis and the surgical repair of a right tibial fracture. She reported no history of alcohol consumption or viral hepatitis and had smoked five cigarettes per day since 20 years of age. Although she had no family history of liver disease, her father had gastric cancer and her sister had gastric and biliary tract cancer. On presentation, her height and weight were 158.5 cm and weight was 55.45 kg (body mass index 22.07 kg/m2), with no recent weight loss. Physical examination revealed pitting edema in both the lower extremities and mild dilatation of the abdominal wall veins in the absence of scleral icterus and asterixis. A blood examination revealed mild hypoalbuminemia (3.5 g/dL) without thrombocytopenia, a prolonged prothrombin time, or hyperbilirubinemia, which corresponded to Child-Pugh class A (6 points). Her serum fibrosis markers were elevated for hyaluronic acid (108.0 ng/mL), mac-2 binding protein glycosylation isomer (3.7 COI), autotaxin (1.433 mg/L), and type IV collagen 7S (7.1 ng/mL). The viral markers for hepatitis B and hepatitis C viruses were negative. Anti-inflammatory M2 antibody was negative, whereas antinuclear antibody was positive at a titer of 1:160. Tumor markers showed elevated alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) (43.7 ng/mL), AFP-L3 fraction (23.3%), and protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II) (186 mAU/mL), alongside normal carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (14.3 U/mL) and carcinoembryonic (3.5 ng/mL). The serum sarcoidosis-related markers were serially measured from X-4 years and showed a decreasing trend for both angiotensin-converting enzyme (25.4-20.7 U/L) and lysozyme (12.6-11.6 μg/mL) (Table 1). Abdominal ultrasonography (US) detected a 17 mm hypoechoic mass in segment 7 (S7) adjacent to the right hepatic vein in the background of chronic hepatitis (Fig. 1). Sonazoid contrast-enhanced US demonstrated hyperenhancement in the early vascular phase, washout in the late vascular phase, and a defect in the Kupffer phase, all of which were consistent with HCC. Transient elastography (FibroScanⓇ, Echosens, Paris, France) revealed a liver stiffness measurement of 19.7 kPa and a controlled attenuation parameter of 185 dB/m, suggestive of advanced hepatic fibrosis and absence of significant hepatic steatosis. Dynamic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated an irregular liver surface and blunting of the edge that indicated cirrhosis, along with a 17×16 mm hypervascular lesion in S7 showing early arterial enhancement and washout in the portal phase (Fig. 2). The mass compressed the right hepatic vein. Enlarged lymph nodes in the bilateral supraclavicular, hilar, and cardiophrenic regions were noted, consistent with sarcoidosis. Gadolinium-ethoxybenzyl-diethylene-triamine-pentaacetic acid-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging revealed a 21×21 mm lesion in S7 with mild hyperintensity on T2-weighted and diffusion-weighted imaging, diffusion restriction on apparent diffusion coefficient mapping, and partial hypointensity in the hepatobiliary phase as signs of HCC (Fig. 3). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed cardiac function without wall thinning or thickening, which was suggestive of cardiac sarcoidosis.

Surgical resection was initially considered as an appropriate treatment; however, due to right hepatic vein compression, further preoperative evaluation was deemed necessary. Dynamic contrast-enhanced CT confirmed stenosis at the confluence of the right, middle, and left hepatic veins with the inferior vena cava (IVC) (Fig. 4A). The right hepatic vein was hypoplastic and drained into the posterior inferior hepatic vein through a venovenous (V-V) shunt.(Fig. 4B, C). The middle and left hepatic veins were also hypoplastic and drained into the IVC via a V-V shunt to the caudate lobe (Fig. 4D, E). Collectively, these findings confirmed the diagnosis of BCS. The additional assessment of prothrombotic conditions revealed no abnormalities, such as anti-cardiolipin β2-glycoprotein I antibody, protein S activity, protein C activity, lupus anticoagulant, Clotting Screen Test, Silica Clotting Time (SCT) confirmation test, and SCT ratio were all within normal ranges, indicating no identifiable prothrombotic disorder as the underlying cause of BCS. Considering the high operative risk in the presence of BCS, percutaneous radiofrequency ablation (RFA) of the tumor was performed along with biopsy of the background liver. Histopathological examination revealed non-caseating epithelioid granulomas with Langerhans-type giant cells accompanied by infiltration of lymphocytes and eosinophils within the granulomas, portal tracts, and hepatic parenchyma (Fig. 5A, B). Advanced fibrosis was confirmed by Azan staining (Fig. 5C). These findings were consistent with hepatic sarcoidosis and concordant with cutaneous sarcoidosis pathology at year X-4 (Fig. 5D-F). At the 1-month follow-up, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated that the HCC had encompassed the RFA zone. Steroid therapy is currently planned for the management of hepatic sarcoidosis in this case.

Discussion

The reported case represents an extremely rare occurrence of HCC development in both hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS. Hepatic involvement in sarcoidosis is relatively common (1), often asymptomatic, and it typically presents with elevated ALP and GGT levels (3). However, hepatic sarcoidosis can progress to cirrhosis and portal hypertension as well (1). Cohort data have shown that clinically significant portal hypertension develops in approximately one-third of patients with hepatic sarcoidosis, 14-15% of patients experience cirrhosis, and portal hypertension may occur even without cirrhosis (4,5). Although chronic inflammation and fibrosis may theoretically contribute to hepatocarcinogenesis, direct evidence linking hepatic sarcoidosis to the carcinogenic risk remains limited. A review of previously reported cases identified only seven patients with HCC associated with sarcoidosis, highlighting the rarity of this association (Table 2) (6-12).

In BCS, chronic hepatic congestion and fibrosis caused by hepatic venous outflow obstruction, together with an increased hepatic arterial flow, are considered significant contributors to hepatocarcinogenesis. The reported HCC prevalence in BCS varies across studies, ranging from 10.6% to 17.6% (4,13,14). A systematic review of 16 studies demonstrated a pooled HCC prevalence of 17.6% in patients with BCS, with marked heterogeneity according to the venous phenotype (26.5% in IVC obstruction vs. 4.2% in isolated hepatic vein obstruction) and geography (2.0-46.2% in Asia vs. 40.0-51.6% in Africa) (4). These data highlight that carcinogenic risk is not uniform across BCS subtypes and appears to be the highest in IVC-predominant diseases (13). A European cohort also demonstrated IVC obstruction as an independent correlate of HCC (14), with a contemporary series reporting a nontrivial HCC rate of 10.6% (13).

The coexistence of hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS is exceedingly rare. Approximately 6% of primary BCS cases are attributed to systemic diseases, including sarcoidosis, connective tissue disease, celiac disease, Behçet's disease, mastocytosis, inflammatory bowel disease, human immunodeficiency virus infection, and myeloma (2). A PubMed search using the terms “sarcoidosis” AND (“Budd-Chiari” OR “Budd Chiari”) AND (“liver” OR “hepatic”) identified nine cases in the literature (Table 3) (15-23), none of which were complicated by HCC.

BCS is a rare vascular manifestation of hepatic sarcoidosis (2,24). A hepatic venous outflow obstruction due to BCS induces hypoxia, leading to stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) (25). This activation promotes glycolytic reprogramming, lactate accumulation, and angiogenic signaling, thereby creating an immunosuppressive and profibrotic microenvironment (26-28). In contrast, hepatic sarcoidosis is characterized by a CD4-positive T-cell-predominant Th1 immune response, resulting in sustained production of inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), which maintain chronic intrahepatic inflammatory signaling through pathways including NF-κB and STAT1 (29). Notably, inflammatory stimuli mediated by TNF-α and IFN-γ have been reported to act cooperatively with HIF-1α signaling under hypoxic conditions, further enhancing the expression of fibrosis-related factors, such as transforming growth factor-β and platelet-derived growth factor, and promoting the activation of hepatic stellate cells (30-32). In addition, granuloma formation and fibrosis-associated remodeling of the sinusoidal architecture may induce localized hypoperfusion and oxidative stress, thereby facilitating further stabilization of HIF-1α and establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of hypoxia and inflammation (33). Taken together, hypoxia related to BCS and chronic inflammation driven by hepatic sarcoidosis may mutually reinforce each other through HIF-1α-dependent pathways and inflammatory cytokines, leading to progressive fibrosis and the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, ultimately contributing to the stepwise development of a pro-carcinogenic milieu.

Previous reports have shown that HCC associated with BCS tends to occur at a relatively young age, with a mean age of approximately 47 years at diagnosis (34). Most reported cases involve BCS with IVC obstruction or an underlying thrombotic predisposition, and are characterized by a rapid progression of hemodynamic disturbance and hepatic fibrosis. In contrast, our patient developed HCC at the age of 71 years, which is atypical for BCS-associated HCC. In the present case, no apparent thrombotic predisposition was observed. Instead, hepatic vein stenosis secondary to mechanical compression by hepatic sarcoidosis, together with well-developed collateral circulation, resulted in chronic and compensatory hemodynamic alterations, suggesting that the short-term carcinogenic effect attributable to BCS alone is limited. Furthermore, long-standing chronic granulomatous inflammation due to hepatic sarcoidosis superimposed on BCS-related hypoxic stress and hemodynamic abnormalities may have led to the gradual accumulation of hepatic fibrosis and microenvironmental changes, ultimately resulting in the development of HCC at an advanced age.

In the present case, in addition to BCS and hepatic sarcoidosis, a history of smoking was identified as a general carcinogenic risk factor (35,36). Although it is difficult to quantify the extent to which smoking contributed to the development of HCC in this patient, it should be considered as a potential confounding factor. In contrast, the patient had no history of alcohol consumption, hepatitis B or C virus infection, or other major established risk factors for HCC such as significant hepatic steatosis or metabolic liver disease.

In patients with BCS, an elevated intrahepatic venous pressure and collateral circulation due to hepatic venous outflow obstruction are associated with an increased risk of bleeding during percutaneous hepatic procedures (37). Therefore, RFA and a liver biopsy should be performed with careful consideration of the indications. In the present case, several hemodynamic features likely contributed to the safe performance of RFA and a background liver biopsy. Specifically, the hepatic veins were not completely occluded, but instead showed hypoplasia and stenosis, and well-developed collateral pathways via V-V shunts were observed. These features may have mitigated the marked elevation in hepatic venous pressure (38). In addition, the patient was classified as Child-Pugh class A, with a relatively preserved coagulation function and platelet counts, which likely contributed to the reduced bleeding risk. Furthermore, during the procedure, the potential for hemorrhagic complications was carefully considered, and RFA was performed under conditions in which prompt hemostatic intervention via endovascular treatment was readily available, if needed.

In the short-term follow-up after RFA, contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated that the tumor was completely encompassed within the ablation zone with no evidence of local recurrence or newly developed intrahepatic lesions. For post-RFA surveillance of HCC, periodic imaging (CT or magnetic resonance imaging) was performed every 3-6 months, along with serum AFP measurements at each visit (39). Systemic corticosteroid therapy was initiated for hepatic sarcoidosis because of chronic inflammation, hepatic fibrosis, and associated liver dysfunction. Oral prednisolone was started at a dose of 25 mg, resulting in an improvement and normalization of the liver function tests, and the dose is currently tapered (40). Given the possibility that BCS represents a secondary condition related to hepatic sarcoidosis, changes in the hepatic hemodynamics and liver function are being carefully monitored while prioritizing corticosteroid treatment for sarcoidosis.

This case report is associated with several limitations. First, this was a single-case observation; therefore, the proposed synergistic effect of hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS on hepatocarcinogenesis cannot establish a causal relationship. The mechanistic discussion remains hypothesis-generating and is based on existing experimental and tumor biological evidence, rather than any direct validation in this patient. Second, although the clinical and imaging findings were compatible with BCS, genetic testing for myeloproliferative neoplasm-associated mutations, including JAK2, was not performed. Therefore, the presence of an underlying thrombotic predisposition cannot be completely excluded, which represents a limitation of the etiological assessment of BCS in this case.

The key features of this case were as follows: (1) advanced background liver fibrosis and portal hypertension caused by the combined effects of hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS; (2) imaging and tumor marker findings (AFP, AFP-L3, and PIVKA-II) that were diagnostic for HCC; and (3) histological confirmation of hepatic sarcoidosis with noncaseating epithelioid granulomas and marked fibrosis in a liver biopsy. This case highlights the possibility that chronic inflammatory and hypoxic stresses, when sustained over time in hepatic sarcoidosis and BCS, may cooperatively drive hepatocarcinogenesis. Recognition of this potential interaction may be clinically relevant when assessing the long-term cancer risk in patients with complex hepatic comorbidities.
