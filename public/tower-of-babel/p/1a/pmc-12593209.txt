Bilateral testicular atrophy and hypogonadism induced by paratesticular leiomyosarcoma and untreated cryptorchidism
Authors: Daniel A. Arreola-Ramírez, Alec Anceno, Fernando Fernández Varela-Gómez, Nancy B. Guzmán-Martínez, Alejandro Haddad-Servín, Yaromin Muñoz-López, César A. Silva-Mendoza, Pedro A. Alvarado-Bahena, Ricardo Cervantes-Zorrilla, José H. Chávez-Fraga, Ricardo E. Domínguez-Castillo, Andrey Ramírez-González
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Paratesticular leiomyosarcomas are rare low-grade malignancies from smooth muscle, presenting as painless scrotal masses in middle-aged men. This case describes a 48-year-old with a left-sided tumor and contralateral cryptorchidism, leading to bilateral testicular compromise and hypogonadism. He presented with asymptomatic left scrotal enlargement; examination revealed a 7-cm left mass and atrophic right inguinal testis. Labs showed normal tumor markers but primary hypogonadism. Imaging confirmed no metastasis. Bilateral orchiectomy yielded grade 1 pT1 leiomyosarcoma (positive for smooth muscle actin, desmin, vimentin) on the left and atrophy on the right. At 2-year follow-up, he remains disease-free on testosterone replacement.

Introduction

Paratesticular tumors are uncommon, accounting for approximately 7–10 % of intrascrotal masses, with the majority being benign, such as lipomas.1 Malignant variants represent about 30 % and are rare, comprising 1–2 % of genitourinary malignancies, with soft tissue sarcomas predominant.1 Among these, leiomyosarcoma is a common subtype in adults, alongside rhabdomyosarcoma and liposarcoma, with an overall soft tissue leiomyosarcoma incidence of ∼0.13 per 100,000, though paratesticular-specific data are limited to institutional series due to rarity.1

These tumors typically affect men in the fifth to seventh decades, presenting as painless, firm, slow-growing unilateral scrotal or inguinal masses, often well-circumscribed and mobile, mimicking benign conditions like hernias or cysts.2,3 Possible origins include the epididymis, spermatic cord, or dartos layer.4 Pathologically, they arise from smooth muscle in the spermatic cord, epididymis, or dartos, showing spindle cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm, nuclear pleomorphism, and variable mitoses; immunohistochemistry confirms diagnosis with positivity for desmin, smooth muscle actin, and vimentin.2 Most are low-grade (FNCLCC grade 1), with low mitotic rates and favorable prognosis after excision, though high-grade cases risk recurrence or metastasis.2

Evaluation begins with high-resolution ultrasonography to distinguish intra-from extratesticular lesions and assess vascularity, with MRI or CT for further characterization and staging for nodal or distant disease.5 Tumor markers (AFP, β-hCG, LDH) support staging for germ cell tumors but are often normal in sarcomas; hormonal profiles assess associated endocrine issues.6 Standard treatment for localized low-grade cases is radical orchiectomy with high cord ligation and negative margins; adjuvant therapy is not routine due to radioresistance and lack of evidence.1,4 No association with cryptorchidism has been reported in the literature, as cryptorchidism primarily increases risk for intratesticular tumors rather than paratesticular ones.4 This report presents a rare case of paratesticular leiomyosarcoma with bilateral testicular involvement leading to orchiectomy and hypogonadism management.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old male patient presented with a 2-year history of painless enlargement of the left testicular volume, without associated decrease in size or other symptoms. His medical history included phimosis at age 8, treated with circumcision, and absence of the right testicle in the scrotal sac since childhood, with no prior medical intervention. He denied trauma, infections, or family history of malignancies. Physical examination revealed bilateral gynecomastia and gynecoid adipose tissue distribution. Genitals were consistent with age and gender. The left testicle was lobulated, indurated (firm like stone), non-mobile, non-tender, and approximately 7 cm in size. The right testicle was absent from the scrotal sac but palpable in the right inguinal region, decreased in size (approximately 2 cm), and non-tender (Fig. 1). The remainder of the examination was normal. Laboratory studies showed a normal complete blood count, glucose 103 mg/dL, creatinine 1.1 mg/dL, normal serum electrolytes, coagulation times, and urinalysis. Testicular tumor markers were within normal limits: lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 223 IU/L, beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) 1.068 mIU/mL, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) 2.7 ng/mL. Hormonal profiling documented low total testosterone (0.5 ng/mL), elevated luteinizing hormone (LH) (12.3 IU/L), and elevated follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) (14.5 IU/L). A GnRH stimulation test showed exaggerated LH and FSH responses (peak LH 45 IU/L, peak FSH 52 IU/L), confirming primary hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. Testicular Doppler ultrasound demonstrated a diminished left testicle due to extrinsic compression from a heterogeneous paratesticular lesion dependent on the left epididymis, with increased internal vascularity on Doppler mode. The right testicle in the inguinal canal was diminished, heterogeneous, with normal vascularity (Fig. 2). Thoracoabdominal computed tomography with intravenous contrast showed no distant lesions. Given the bilateral pathological findings—right testicular hypotrophy with cryptorchidism and left paratesticular tumor with testicular atrophy—and biochemical evidence of primary hypogonadism, bilateral radical orchiectomy with inguinal approach was performed (Fig. 3). The procedure was uneventful, with 10 mL blood loss and 2-h duration, including bilateral high spermatic cord ligation. Histopathology revealed a right testicle (2.5 × 2 × 1.8 cm) with atrophic changes in the parenchyma and epididymis, without tumor. The left testicle (3.4 × 2.5 × 1.4 cm) showed parenchymal and epididymal atrophy, with an adjacent tumor (3.1 × 3.0 × 2.5 cm) diagnosed as paratesticular leiomyosarcoma, histological grade 1 (FNCLCC), negative surgical margins, no lymphovascular invasion, pT1. IHC was positive for smooth muscle actin (+++), desmin (+++), and vimentin (++) (Fig. 4). Final diagnosis: Left paratesticular leiomyosarcoma stage IA (pT1N0M0 G1). At 2-year follow-up, the patient is clinically well, with no evidence of recurrence on imaging. The patient received intramuscular testosterone undecanoate (TU) replacement therapy, initiated with a single dose of 1000 mg (4 mL vial), followed by a second 1000-mg injection 6 weeks later, and subsequent injections every 10–14 weeks. Discussion

Paratesticular leiomyosarcomas are typically unilateral, presenting challenges in preoperative diagnosis due to nonspecific imaging and normal tumor markers, as seen in this case where ultrasound revealed a heterogeneous, hypervascular epididymal lesion with extrinsic testicular compression.2 However, the bilateral involvement here—right-sided cryptorchidism with hypotrophy and left-sided tumor-induced atrophy—is atypical, as literature reviews of adult series report predominantly isolated tumors without contralateral pathology.1,3 This uniqueness stems from the concurrent untreated cryptorchidism, which exposed the right testis to elevated temperatures, impairing germ cell maturation and leading to hypotrophy, with 50 % of such testes lacking germ cells by puberty.7, 8, 9 No prior cases of paratesticular leiomyosarcoma associated with cryptorchidism have been reported, as cryptorchidism is a known risk factor for intratesticular tumors but not paratesticular ones; this association here appears coincidental, with the tumor arising in the eutopic (scrotal) left testis and cryptorchidism affecting the contralateral right side.4 Untreated cryptorchidism also heightens malignancy risk (RR 2.4–8.8), particularly for seminoma, due to persistent gonocytes; though no tumor was found on the right, the hypotrophy justified removal to prevent potential transformation.6,10,11 On the left, tumor compression induced atrophy and mechanically disrupted seminiferous tubules and vascularity, causing Leydig/Sertoli cell loss, reduced testosterone, and impaired spermatogenesis, culminating in primary hypergonadotropic hypogonadism with elevated LH/FSH and exaggerated GnRH response.12, 13, 14 This mirrors reports of paratesticular masses leading to endocrine failure via mass effect, with severity tied to tumor duration and size (3 cm here).15 The bilateral atrophy and hypogonadism, evidenced by gynecomastia and low testosterone (0.5 ng/mL), precluded testis-sparing options, requiring radical orchiectomy for oncologic control and symptom relief, while underscoring the need for endocrine monitoring in such cases.16 This aligns with indications for bilateral procedures in synchronous malignancies or non-viable contralateral tissue, prioritizing resection over preservation when fertility is not a concern (patient aged 48).16 The treatment was according to the recommendations found in the literature: inguinal radical orchiectomy with high cord ligation achieving negative margins, optimal for low-grade pT1 G1 disease without lymphovascular invasion.1,4 Due to the rarity of spermatic cord/paratesticular leiomyosarcoma, there is no consensus on treatment, but alternatives include radical orchiectomy with high spermatic cord excision and resection of surrounding soft tissues to ensure negative margins; lymph node dissection is recommended only if nodes are enlarged. Adjuvant radiotherapy may be considered to reduce local recurrence risk, while chemotherapy is limited to metastatic disease.3 In this case, bilateral radical orchiectomy was justified by the non-functional status of both testes (preoperative low testosterone and atrophy), eliminating the need for preservation and addressing the hypogonadism directly. Adjuvant therapy was omitted due to radioresistance and lack of benefit in localized low-grade cases.17,18 Prognosis is favorable: in different case series, grade 1 tumors show ∼29 % recurrence but 100 % disease-specific survival with complete excision, consistent with no recurrence at 2 years here.2 Overall disease-specific survival rates are approximately 77 %.19 Kamitani et al.'s retrospective analysis of 217 cases—the first and largest study focused on paratesticular leiomyosarcoma—identified subcutaneous location and high-grade tumors as predictors of poorer prognosis (higher distant metastasis and mortality risk), with positive surgical margins associated with increased local recurrence and metastasis.4 For subcutaneous types, high inguinal orchiectomy is beneficial for achieving negative margins and local control. Follow-up lacks formal guidelines but typically involves regular clinical exams, imaging (CT/MRI every 6–12 months initially, then annually), and tumor marker/hormonal monitoring to detect recurrence early, as emphasized in series like Kamitani.4 Post-orchiectomy, testosterone undecanoate supplementation normalized levels, improving quality of life, libido, and energy, as supported by trials showing benefits in symptomatic hypogonadism without consistent effects on erectile function or cognition.20 The dosing scheme—initial 1000 mg TU, second at 6 weeks, then every 10–14 weeks with pre-injection monitoring—aligns with recommendations for maintaining physiologic levels while minimizing fluctuations.20 Safety includes risks like polycythemia but no increased cardiovascular or prostate events; infertility is irrelevant post-bilateral procedure.20 This case highlights the need for early cryptorchidism correction to mitigate atrophy and malignancy risks, and routine endocrine evaluation in scrotal masses to address hypogonadism promptly.20 Limitations include reliance on institutional data for rare tumors; future studies could explore biomarkers for bilateral involvement.

Conclusions

The coexistence of paratesticular leiomyosarcoma and untreated cryptorchidism exemplifies a rare scenario where mechanical compression and thermal dysregulation converge to produce bilateral atrophy and primary hypogonadism, necessitating comprehensive surgical resection over conservative approaches. This outcome reinforces the efficacy of radical orchiectomy for low-grade, localized disease, achieving negative margins and no recurrence, while highlighting the role of hormonal supplementation in post-procedure management. Broader implications emphasize evaluation of endocrine profiles in scrotal pathologies and the preventive value of early cryptorchidism correction to avert long-term complications.
