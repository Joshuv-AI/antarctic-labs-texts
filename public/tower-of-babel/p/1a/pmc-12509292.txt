Estimating the financial burden of asthma among young adults in Sweden
Authors: Alma Holm Rovsing, Richard Ssegonja, Christer Janson, Anders Løkke, Rikke Ibsen, Ole Hilberg, Vibeke Backer, Charlotte Suppli Ulrik, Ulla Møller Weinreich, Kjell Erik Julius Håkansson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe financial burden of asthma in young adults remains sparsely investigated. Our study aims to estimate the Swedish national financial burden of asthma in adults aged 18–45 using nationwide databases.MethodsThe financial burden of asthma in Sweden was estimated using the Swedish burden of disease, cost of medical care, and sick leave utilization data from 2022, together with healthcare consumption estimates from a nationwide burden of asthma study (data from 2014 to 2018). Costs were calculated as total costs and excess costs compared to matched controls.ResultsThe total annual financial burden for individuals with asthma was €3,572 per patient, divided into primary care (€929), secondary care (€1,137), medicine (€605), and sick leave transfers (€902). When compared to an age-, sex-, cohabitation-status-, and residence-matched control group, an annual excess cost of €2,299 (2,288–2,310) per patient with asthma was found. When stratified according to disease severity, patients at GINA 2020 Step 2 incurred the lowest annual cost at €2,916 (2,960–3,092), whereas GINA 2020 Step 5 represented the highest annual cost at €6,697 (6,559–6,793). Pooling the total mean costs for all young Swedish adults with ICS-treated asthma (n = 88,898) revealed an excess annual financial burden of €204,375,739, of which 39% (€ 80,150,137) were sick leave transfers.ConclusionAsthma in Sweden is associated with a substantial financial burden from both direct costs and asthma-related sick leave compared to matched controls, and the pooled excess burden of ICS-treated asthma in young adults exceeded €200,000,000 annually.KEYWORDS: Costs, Scandinavia, chronic disease, original research, Airway disease, Europe, economics, Direct costs, Indirect costs

Introduction

Asthma, a chronic respiratory disease with estimated 330 million patients worldwide, is one of the costliest diseases among young adults in Europe [1]. The disease has an impact on both personal lives and society owing to increased healthcare utilization, high medical costs, absenteeism, and reduced productivity in the workplace (presenteeism) [2,3].

When treated according to the international GINA guidelines [4], a stepwise approach to asthma therapy, most patients can achieve disease control with inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) treatment in combination with long-acting beta-2-agonists, a cost-effective therapy with limited side effects when used at low to moderate doses [5]. Although effective treatments are available, poor asthma control and acute exacerbations remain common [6,7], driven by low adherence to ICS treatment, over-reliance on short-acting bronchodilators (SABA), or a general lack of knowledge of the risks associated with poorly controlled asthma [8].

In Europe, the economic burden of asthma has been estimated to reach €72 billion [1] and previous research suggests that a substantial part of the economic burden of asthma can be prevented by improving asthma control [9]. With an estimated asthma prevalence of 10.9–12.2%, a high proportion of patients with poor disease control, and a low prevalence of ICS-treated asthma, Sweden has a relatively high asthma burden [10–12]. Data from 2018 shows a prevalence of 2.3% patients with asthma on permanent ICS treatment [13]. There is a paucity of research on the societal financial burden of asthma in Sweden, yet previous studies have estimated costs ranging from €1,800 to €6,500 per patient, depending on severity [14,15]. Nationwide analyses of health-related financial burden can be challenging in Sweden due to the design of its national registries. However, the Nordic welfare model with universal healthcare, similar structures for social security and similar distributions of demographics among inhabitants allow for cross-border analyses of the burden of disease and societal impact [16–18].

In the present study, we aimed to estimate the societal financial burden of asthma in Sweden using Swedish healthcare and welfare reimbursement data, as well as Nordic healthcare utilization and burden-of-disease analyses.

Methods

The present study used healthcare utilization source data from the Danish REASSESS cohort (Uncontrolled and possible severe asthma in Denmark) to extrapolate the financial burden of asthma to a Swedish national context [19]. This method was used due to the lack of Swedish national registries suitable for nationwide, individual-level analyses and a high degree of similarities between the Swedish and Danish healthcare and welfare systems [16]. In terms of diagnosis and treatment of asthma, Sweden and Denmark both follow the GINA-guidelines [4], and patients in the two countries show a high degree of demographic and treatment similarities in the previous research [17,18].

Primary care utilization

The REASSESS cohort was used for primary care utilization. The cohort consists of all Danish residents aged 18–45, restricted to minimize the inclusion of COPD, who were on active ICS treatment during 2014–2016 (n = 63,130), identified using the National Prescription Database [8]. Severity, active treatment (defined as redeeming two or more ICS-containing prescriptions during a 12-month period), and treatment intensity were based on ICS dose and additional controller use during the follow-up period of 2 years, according to the GINA 2020 guidelines [4]. To determine asthma severity, GINA-guidelines 2020 were used, going from GINA step 1 (mild/intermittent asthma, medicine only taken as needed) to GINA step 5 (possible severe asthma, treated with at least a high dose ICS/LABA). Socioeconomic and healthcare utilization data were linked at the individual level using the central person registry. Annual healthcare utilization use was modelled using age, sex, and comorbidity-adjusted negative binomial regression. A full description of the group definitions, statistical method, and healthcare utilization in the Danish source data is available in Håkansson et al. [8].

Study approval was granted by the Capital Region of Copenhagen’s Data Safety Board (Ref. p-2019–142) and the Capital Region of Copenhagen’s Scientific Ethics Committee (ref. H-19042597). Data are available upon application to data sources, as required by Danish law.

Secondary care and medication data

Utilization rates for secondary care and medication from the REASSESS Cohort were used. Utilization was investigated for excess utilization compared to age, sex, municipality of residence, and civil status, and matched controls using a gamma-distributed generalized linear model regression model adjusted for comorbidity and education. Given the skewed nature of utilization data, a gamma distribution was fitted. Inclusion criteria for the source data used were identical to those described above for primary care. Utilization was stratified by the GINA step, as described above, and tracked for 2 years during individual-level follow-up periods between 2014 and 2018, subsequently annualized. Utilization was divided into secondary- (emergency, inpatient, and outpatient-) care and medication. For a full description of the source of secondary care and medication utilization used, see [8].

Healthcare cost analyses

In the present study, direct costs were estimated based on healthcare resource utilization from the two REASSESS cohort studies using unit costs from the publicly available Southern Swedish pricelist for healthcare utilization, 2022 [19]. Primary care costs included only visits to the general practice, due to differences in the primary care specialist sector utilization rate and costs between the countries. Cost for medications (both asthma- and non-asthma-related medication) was estimated based on prescribed drug redemption data, utilizing the lowest prices per anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) code in the publicly available price database published by the Swedish Dental and Pharmaceutical Agency [20]. Summarized source data is found in Supplementary Table S1. For analyses on the total financial burden of asthma in Swedish young adults, an estimated ICS-treated asthma prevalence of 2.3% was used [13].

Welfare utilization and cost analyses

Welfare transfers were based on aggregated sick leave data for the year 2022 from the Swedish Insurance Agency [21] due to asthma (ICD-10 codes J45 + J46) for the population aged 18–44 years using prespecified age-cutoffs from the Swedish Insurance Agency. The proportion of asthma patients who received sick leave was estimated based on publicly available statistics on asthmatic patients (ICD-10 codes J45+J46) in the year 2022 from The National Board of Health and Welfare [22]. It was estimated by dividing the number of patients with sick leave benefits due to asthma during the year 2022 by the total number of asthma patients in the same year. The numbers used are available in Supplementary Table S2.

A graphical overview of the study methods and source data can be found in Figure 1. All analyses were performed using either R (4.3.0) or Microsoft Excel (16.87). Exchange rates used were €1 = 7.45 DKK (fixed rate exchange policy) and €1 = 11.363 SEK (end-of-year 2021 exchange rate according to the Swedish National Bank). Data are presented as mean (95% confidence intervals (CI)), unless otherwise stated.

Results

In this nationwide study of the societal financial burden of ICS-treated asthma in young adults in Sweden, we found that the total financial burden of asthma was €317,542,470 (95% CI 313,897,666 – 321,187,275) annually. The excess per-patient cost of asthma compared to matched controls was estimated to be € 2,299 (2,288–2,310), distributed between direct costs (€1,397 (1,388–1,407)) and sick leave transfers (€902 (900–903)) (Figure 2(A)).

Direct healthcare costs

In individuals with asthma, we saw increases across all direct costs (emergency-, inpatient-, outpatient-, and primary care and medication costs) compared to matched controls. The total annual direct cost was €2,670 (2,631 – 2,710) for individuals with ICS-treated asthma and €1,273 (1,243–1,303) for controls (Table 1).

Primary care-related costs were the largest driver of direct healthcare costs at €929 (914–944) annually, compared to €482 (472–493) in the controls. Secondary care-related costs were also increased across all subcategories (emergency, inpatient, and outpatient care), with outpatient care being the cost driver at €712 (701–722) annually (control group = € 417 (409–425)). Medication costs were increased in individuals with ICS-treated asthma at €605 (600–610), of which €325 (322–327) were asthma-related (Figure 2(B)). While direct costs were stratified by asthma-relations, only 17% of the direct costs were due to asthma-related visits (Figure 2(C)); however, data limitations prevent stratification of primary care costs according to asthma relations.

Asthma-related sick leave transfers

Approximately 11.4% of the patients with a hospital diagnosis of asthma or status asthmaticus were granted sick leave (Supplementary Table S1), and the corresponding per-patient estimate of asthma-related sick leave transfers was €902 (€900–903) (Table 1), accounting for the average duration of sick leave and average sick leave transfer in euros during the study period (Supplementary Table S2).

Impact of disease severity

When stratifying all direct costs according to asthma severity based on the GINA 2020 steps, the lowest financial burden of disease was seen for GINA Step 2 at €2,916 (2,960–3,092) annually per patient, of which €2,582 (2,627–2,757) were direct- and €334 (333–335) were sick leave-related costs. The highest costs were seen in GINA Step 5 at €6,697 (6,559–6,793), of which €4,768 (4,633–4,861) were direct- and €1,929 (1,962–1,932) were sick leave-related costs (Figure 3(A)).

Similarly, when stratified according to mild-to-moderate disease (GINA Step 1–3) and possible severe asthma (GINA Step 4–5), differences in annual financial burden were observed. Mild-to-moderate asthma was associated with an annual cost of €3,586 (3,523–3,649), compared to possible severe asthma at €5,879 (5,777–5,954) (Figure 3(B)).

The total financial burden of asthma

Pooling the mean costs of ICS-treated asthma with a prevalence of 2.3% for the entire population of young Swedish adults resulted in an annual total financial burden of €317,542,470 (313,897,666–321,187,275), an excess annual burden of €204,375,739 (203,397,864–205,353,613) compared to matched controls. Of the total excess burden, 39% (€ 80,150,137) was due to sick leave transfers (Table 2). Lower or higher estimates of asthma prevalence affect the financial burden.

Discussion

In this registry-based study investigating the financial burden of ICS-treated asthma in Sweden, we found that young adults aged 18–45 with asthma carry a per-patient annual total cost of €3,572. Compared to matched controls, the annual excess cost was €2,299, resulting in an annual excess financial burden of €204,375,739 attributable to ICS-treated asthma in young adults in Sweden.

The direct costs of asthma

There are significant differences between the present findings and those of a previous study on the cost of asthma in Sweden by Jansson et al. from 2007 [14]. Jansson et al. found an annual direct cost per-patient of €790 after adjusting for inflation between 2007 and 2023, compared with €2,670 in our present study. The significant increases can be attributed to differences in populations, as Jansson et al. included intermittent asthma, whereas the present source population was defined as actively ICS-treated asthma [8]; in Jansson et al.’s study called persistent asthma. Indeed, when keeping with Jansson et al.’s findings on persistent asthma, the annual direct costs were closer to the €1,331 per patient in our present study. However, as the previous study was based on patient recall and not databases of healthcare utilization, underestimation due to recall bias cannot be excluded as a reason for the differences in costs.

A more recent study on severe asthma costs in Sweden from 2020 by Jansson et al. [15], published results that were similar to our findings. Jansson et al. estimated the mean annual direct costs to be €2,400 per patient, compared to the €2,670 in the present study. Despite the focus on patients with severe disease and a broader range of patient ages (18–75 years of age), costs were similar to our findings. Of note, indirect costs were much higher (€4,100/patient/year compared to the present €902/patient/year), arguably due to the inclusion of older individuals with more severe disease.

The major cost driver in asthma direct costs in previous studies has often been medication costs [2,8,14,23] in contrast to the present study where primary care was the largest single cost driver. A reason for this difference might be due to the fact that this study includes all healthcare visits and not exclusively asthma-related visits, or the assumption in the present analysis that patients always redeemed the cheapest option as detailed Swedish prescription data was unavailable on an individual level.

When stratified by severity, whether by GINA steps or mild-to-moderate versus possibly severe asthma, expected increases in direct costs were observed, with the exception of GINA Step 2. In contrast to GINA Step 1, GINA Step 2 would be expected to adhere to at least one low-dose ICS treatment daily, as compared to the more sporadic treatment in Step 1. As the largest efficacy of ICS treatment is seen with low-to-moderate daily doses [4], the findings suggest that patients who are well-controlled on GINA Step 2 fare better than those with very low ICS exposure or those requiring moderate-to-high doses of ICS. Additionally, the GINA steps serve as a marker of disease or symptom activity, as seen with the increasing financial burden by increasing the GINA step in the present study.

In a Scandinavian context, a few cost of illness studies have been performed. A 2017 Finnish nationwide registry-based study estimated an annual per-patient financial burden of € 545 (€ 675 after adjusting for inflation) in direct costs. The marked difference may be attributed to the 1987–2013 Finnish asthma program aimed at reducing the individual and societal burdens of asthma, but also the fact that the Finnish study did only include asthma-related costs, whereas our study included all healthcare costs generated by individuals with asthma, and individuals without asthma [24].

In the international literature, the direct costs attributable to asthma vary widely, with the US inflation-adjusted estimate between 1999 and 2002 reaching €4,350 per patient and the overall EU inflation-adjusted estimate 2002–2007 of €806 [2,23], highlighting the challenges when comparing the burden of disease analyses across countries and healthcare systems. However, it should be noted that Scandinavian countries, in general, have a high cost of living, resulting in higher wages compared to other European countries, causing stark differences in estimated costs. The present study is strengthened by cost comparison toward matched controls without asthma, thereby estimating the excess cost that asthma provides to Swedish society.

Our study investigates the financial burden of individuals with asthma aged 18–45, and since morbidity and co-morbidities increase with increasing age, as we see in, for example, COPD patients [25], the results in this study probably underestimate the total societal burden of asthma. The financial burden of asthma in older adults remains understudied.

Welfare transfer burden of asthma

The present study found significant increases of €902 annually per patient in terms of sick leave due to asthma. Indirect costs, however, typically comprise additional items such as foregone income, productivity losses (presenteeism), costs related to disability leave, unemployment, or even premature death [26]. The total indirect and welfare transfer due to sick leave associated with asthma is difficult to infer, as national databases in Sweden and Denmark only cover long-term sick leave, whereas official recommendations for clinicians cite a five-day sick notice [27]. In Danish national databases, a €36 excess societal expenditure due to sick leave was found for patients with asthma [8], suggesting that most of the welfare transfers attributable to sick leave are incurred by the employer and thus not reported in public records. While short-term absenteeism due to asthma is unavailable in the present analysis, there is ample evidence showing increased short-term absenteeism in asthma, especially in poorly controlled asthma [14,28–31]. Overall, the €902 estimated in the present analysis constitutes an underestimation of the actual indirect financial burden of asthma, as made evident by both previous Swedish studies at €2,500 (persistent asthma [14]) and €4,100 (severe asthma [15]), as well as €2,540 (persistent asthma, Denmark [8]) in annual indirect costs per patient. However, it should be noted that the welfare transfers estimated in the present study are not necessarily considered to be true costs but rather transfers between different parts of social security and welfare systems [32].

While short-term sick leave for asthma seems to primarily burden the employer financially, additional sources of asthma-related burden for the employer can be found in presenteeism, working while unwell with reduced productivity [33]. Several studies imply that asthma, and especially severe and/or uncontrolled asthma, is associated with significant indirect costs owing to workplace impairment, sick leave, and early retirement [29–31]. However, even in patients with severe asthma, studies have suggested that improving asthma control results in less work impairment. Van der Valk et al. [34] recently demonstrated that 12 months of anti-IL5 treatment for severe eosinophilic asthma reduced workplace impairment by approximately 30%, and earlier studies show a distinct relationship between asthma control and workplace impairment [28,30,31,35]. Taken together, these studies suggest that, by optimizing asthma treatment for patients, it may be possible to reduce the indirect financial burden of asthma.

With the advent of biological treatments and ICS/formoterol as both maintenance and reliever treatment, more strategies for achieving disease control in previously treatment-refractory and/or difficult-to-treat patients are available. However, the cost-effectiveness of newer strategies, particularly biologics, has yet to be fully elucidated in models considering the full direct- and indirect costs of disease [36–39].

Limitations

The present study was based on healthcare resource utilization rate data from nationwide Danish studies. While the organization of healthcare systems and healthcare expenditure per capita are highly similar, the results need to be interpreted with this assumption in mind. As mentioned above, underestimation of the actual costs is inevitable due to data gaps in both Swedish and Danish source data, such as the first 14 days of sick leave in Danish data and lack of individual-level prescription data in Swedish databases. The analyses are performed on 18–45-year-olds, and as previous Swedish data show, costs increase with age [14], hence we possibly underestimate the true total financial burden of asthma. Finally, the cohort is based on individuals who consistently redeem inhaled corticosteroids, limiting generalizability to those with persistent rather than mild intermittent asthma.

Conclusion

Asthma in Sweden is associated with a substantial financial burden from both direct costs and asthma-related sick leave compared to matched controls. Costs increased significantly with disease severity. On a national basis, the pooled excess mean cost of ICS-treated asthma in young adults exceeded €200,000,000 annually.
