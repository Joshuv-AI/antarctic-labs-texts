Post recurrence survival in early versus late period and its prognostic factors in rectal cancer patients
Authors: Fatemeh Shahabi, Ali Mehri, Abbas Abdollahi, Seyed Hossein Hosseini Hoshyar, Abolfazl Ghahramani, Mahdie Ghiyasi Noei, Ala Orafaie, Majid Ansari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo identify factors associated with post-recurrence survival (PRS), we examined our institutional recurrence patterns following definitive resection for rectal cancer. We reviewed all patients with rectal cancer diagnosed at three hospitals in the east of Iran from 2011 to 2020. The optimal cut-off value was determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to determine early recurrence. The effect of recurrence time was evaluated on PRS. 326 eligible patients with a mean ± SD age of 56 ± 12.8 years were included in this study. In a median (IQR: Inter-quartile range) follow-up time of 76 (62.2) months, 106 (32.5%) patients experienced at least any recurrence (locoregional or distant metastasis) following primary resection. The median (IQR) time from initial surgery to recurrence was 29.5 (31.2) months. Based on ROC analysis, early recurrence was specified at ≤ 29 months. However, for the patients who experienced only locoregional recurrence, 33 months was the cut-off to define early recurrence. Recurrence time and recurrence management were both significant variables on PRS. Moreover, TNM staging was significantly associated with early recurrence (P = 0.003). In this research, recurrence time, recurrence management and TNM staging were found to be correlated with PRS.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Early recurrence, Late recurrence, Post-recurrence survivalSubject terms: Gastroenterology, Medical research, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer globally and the fourth most common cause of cancer-related death1. In recent years, developments in early diagnosis, personalized therapy, and broader knowledge of cancer biology have led to an increase in patient survival2. Regular therapy for CRC depends on the tumor stage and usually consists of tumor resection, which may be combined with other treatment modalities, such as chemotherapy or radiation. However, patients with recurrence may need additional therapies3. In rectal cancer patients, recurrence following resection occurs in one in three cases, particularly within the first several years4,5. Given that neoadjuvant therapy for higher stages of rectal cancer has been standardized and because of improvement in surgical techniques, recurrence has declined over time but still is a major obstacle in the long-term survival of patients, and distant metastases in liver or lung continue to represent a significant proportion of recurrences6–8. Recurrence rates for CRC have been reported to be up to 40% depending on the stage9. Unfortunately, despite aggressive and multimodal treatments, most recurrent patients have a low probability of being cured10. In advanced rectal cancer, post-recurrence survival (PRS) is typically related to the characteristics of the tumor and patient at the time of diagnosis, including the tumor’s site, probability of surgical resection, performance status, and disease burden11,12.

Few studies on the association of recurrence time with PRS have been reported. For CRC, different cut-offs have been used to determine early and late recurrence most of them have been chosen arbitrarily and vary from 1 to 5 years13–15. In this study, we aimed to determine the optimal cut-off point of recurrence time after definitive resection through the Youden index based on the ROC curve. Moreover, we investigated whether the time between resection and recurrence could be relevant in predicting PRS considering other factors such as recurrence management in rectal cancer patients.

Methods

This cohort study was conducted on all 373 patients diagnosed with rectal cancer who underwent laparoscopic surgery at Razavi, Omid, and Ghaem hospitals in Mashhad, Iran from 2011 to 2020. The studied patients underwent surgery in three surgical procedures including the natural orifices specimen extraction (NOSE), transabdominal specimen extraction (TASE), and abdominoperineal resection (APR). TASE surgeries were predominantly performed for cases of high and middle rectal cancer, while for patients with low rectal cancer, the choice was between APR or NOSE surgery. NOSE surgery was typically reserved for patients eligible for sphincter-saving procedures, whereas APR was opted for in other cases. All patients involved in the study scored II or III in the ASA indexing score. The ASA score is a classification system used to evaluate a patient’s physical status before surgery which ranges from 1 to 6. Higher scores indicate a greater risk of complications16. Neoadjuvant chemoradiation therapy was administered for 97.5% of these patients, and at least a month after chemoradiation, the surgery was performed. The chemotherapy treatment for our patients included Capecitabine 1g/m2 BID, along with a total of 28 sessions of radiation therapy, with a total radiation dose of 5040 rad. 77.3% of the patients have received adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery. Studied patients were followed up based on standard rectal cancer surveillance till the fifth year after curative surgery16. After this period, phone calls have been made to patients annually. The last follow-up of these patients is for 2023. Demographic and clinical characteristics of age at diagnosis, sex, surgical procedure techniques, tumor location (from the anal verge, three parts are defined as follows: the lower rectum, 0 to 5 cm; the middle rectum, 6 to 10 cm; and the upper rectum, 11 to 15 cm)16, recurrence status (None/Yes), recurrence site (Only locoregional recurrence (LR)/only distant metastasis (DM) or DM + LR), circumferential resection margins (CRM) status, distal resection margins (DRM) status, and postoperative TNM stage of the patients were examined in the study. Since all of our cases were rectal cancer, pelvic recurrence was defined as locoregional recurrence. Recurrence diagnosis involves a combination of methods including physical examination, tumor marker testing, imaging studies (such as CT or PET scans), and confirmation through biopsy. Recurrence management was defined at three different levels. The first level involves no procedure. Patients in this group either had such aggressive tumors that there was insufficient time for a treatment procedure, or the multidisciplinary team decided against any procedure based on factors such as age, extent of tumor involvement, and patient performance. The second level involves salvage surgery combined with oncologic treatment. At this level, patients receive systemic oncology treatment in addition to local surgery or metastasectomy. The third level involves only oncologic treatment, where patients receive solely systemic oncology therapy. This variable had 12.3% missing values; instead of removing these, missing values were multiple imputed. Demographic and clinical characteristics as only predictors were included in this imputation. In addition, the recurrence management was considered both as predictors and as variables to be imputed.

In addition, complications of intra-operative bleeding and postoperative obstruction, anastomosis-fail, and pelvic collection/abscess were reported. A part of the data of this study was collected from the colorectal cancer registry (No: 4001728), Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative normal variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and non-normal variables were described as median (IQR). Categorical characteristics were also expressed in frequency (percentage). After checking relevant assumptions, the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was performed to compare the categorical variables and to compare continuous variables, after checking the normality, independent two-sample t-test or Maan-Whitney test (which one applicable) was used. Recurrence date to death date or last follow-up date was considered as post-recurrence survival time and surgical procedure date to death date or last follow-up date was mentioned as overall survival (OS). In addition, the time from the surgery date to the recurrence date was defined as recurrence time. The ROC curve analysis was used to assess the classifying role of recurrence time in death outcomes. The optimal cut-off point of recurrence time after definitive resection was defined through the Youden index. The Kaplan–Meier survival curves were provided to demonstrate OS and PRS time. Statistical differences in survival curves between groups were investigated through Log-rank test. To evaluate the effect of the variable on PRS, Cox regression analysis was administered. To observe the recurrence time variable (time from surgery to recurrence) effect on PRS, Cox regression analysis was fitted. The SPSS version 26.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) and MedCalc Statistical Software version 22.005 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2023) were rendered for analysis. The significance level was considered 0.05.

This research was conducted on all eligible patients with rectal cancer who underwent laparoscopic curative surgery at three hospitals in Mashhad, Iran from 2011 to 2020. Considering the hazard ratio of death in early recurrence rather than late recurrence as the outcome of interest (Hazard ratio:1.76, Table 3), significance level = 0.05, total recurrent sample size = 106, and allocation ratio = 1, 80% of power was obtained. This power analysis was performed through Stata//MP17statistical software, Stata Corp (2021) College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Mashhad University of Medical Sciences Ethical Committee (IR.MUMS.IRH.REC.1402.051) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Mashhad University of Medical Sciences Ethical Committee has waived the informed consent due to the study’s retrospective nature.

Results

326 eligible patients with a mean ± SD age of 56 ± 12.8 years were included in this study. The diagram of patients’ recruitment in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. In a median (IQR) follow-up time of 76 (62.2) months, 106 (32.5%) patients experienced at least any type of recurrence (locoregional or distant metastasis) following primary resection. All patients’ demographic and clinical baseline characteristics were reported in Table 1. It was clear that the TNM staging was highly correlated to recurrence status (P < 0.001). Moreover, the recurrence rate was not independent of the surgery technique (P = 0.018). The recurrence rate was significantly lower in the TASE patients (22.1%) especially compared to APR patients (43.8%). Figure 2 demonstrated that OS curves were highly significantly different between recurrence status groups (None/only LR/only DM or DM + LR) (P < 0.001). In contrast, OS curves were not statistically different between the “only LR” with “only DM or DM + LR” groups of patients (P = 0.597). Recurrence patterns are classified into six groups: locoregional, liver-only, lung-only, multiple sites, other sites, and distant metastasis (unknown site). Our results showed no statistically significant difference between early and late recurrence groups in categories of recurrence pattern variable (P = 0.623). Other sites included 3 brain and 2 bone metastases in the early recurrence group, and 2 brain, 1 bone, and 2 inguinal lymph node metastases in the late recurrence group.

Among the 106 individuals who developed recurrence, the median (IQR) time from initial surgery to recurrence was 29.5 (31.2) months. To investigate the role of recurrence time in death prediction, the optimal cut-off value was determined by ROC analysis. The cut-off time of 29 months as an early recurrence was associated with death (P < 0.001). The occurrence of recurrence before 29th months after initial resection was more correlated to death rather than after this cut-off point time. We ascertained early recurrence as the time to recurrence ≤ 29 months and the occurrence of recurrence after this cut-off point as late recurrence. However, the optimal cut-off points of equal to or less than 33 months after initial surgery were determined for patients with experience of only locoregional recurrence (P = 0.002) (Fig. 3). Furthermore, the PRS curve was statistically different between recurrence time groups (log-rank test, P = 0.010) (estimated median PRS, early recurrence 7.0 months vs. late recurrence 18 months) (Fig. 4).

A higher percentage of pCR patients experienced a late recurrence (early vs. late: 7.7% vs. 27.5%) and in contrast, a greatly higher percentage of the stage III cases faced an early recurrence (early vs. late: 61.5% vs. 29.4%). To evaluate the effective factors on PRS, the univariate Cox regression analysis considering α = 0.2 was used for detecting the candidate variables for multivariable analysis. In this step, recurrence time and recurrence management variables were candidates for entering to multivariable model. In the multivariable Cox regression model, recurrence time and recurrence management were associated with the PRS time of our patients. Post-recurrence mortality risk in patients who have experienced recurrence before the 29th month after surgery (early recurrence) was significantly higher than patients who have experienced recurrence after aforementioned time (late recurrence) in the presence of recurrence management variable in the model (Hazard ratio, 1.61 [95% CI 1.01, 2.55]; P = 0.044)). Moreover, salvage surgery accompanied by oncologic treatment showed a significance of 67% risk reduction in post-recurrence mortality rather than recurrent patients with no management in the presence of recurrence time variable in the multiple model (Hazard ratio, 0.33 [95% CI 0.15, 0.76]; P = 0.010)) (Table 3). However, the study demonstrated that the method of managing recurrences was not related to the type of recurrence (P = 0.313).

Discussion

Recurrence is a common clinical outcome among patients with rectal cancer after curative resection4,5. Data on recurrence time, as well as a widely acknowledged and evidence-based definition of early recurrence following CRC resection, are conflicting3,7. Regarding the published literature on CRC, different cut-offs have been used to determine early and late recurrence most of them have been chosen arbitrarily and vary from 1 to 5 years13–15,17–19. To illustrate, In 2014, Lan et al. used 3 years as the cut-off to define early and late recurrence for CRC patients20. This group also utilized a 24-month cut-off for early recurrence in their subsequent study published in 202121. In 2021, Wiesmueller et al. evaluated the optimal cut-off value using a minimum P-value approach for the first time. They demonstrated that, based on the overall prognosis, 16 months served as the optimal cut-off value to differentiate between early and late recurrences22. However, in terms of rectal cancer, data on postoperative cut-off points that help differentiate between early and late recurrence is relatively scarce. In 2020, in a large Chinese cohort of patients with locally advanced rectal cancer, a cut-off of 24 months after primary surgery was recommended. This cut-off was defined by considering a minimum P-value of evaluation of Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS) between early recurrence and late recurrence cases based on the length of overall survival. The mentioned study stated that the early recurrence was associated with worse PRS (P = 0.028)23. Following the Chinese study, Affi Koprowski et al. used 24 months as a cut-off point and evaluated the recurrence timing effect on PRS. Their results were not consistent with the Chinese cohort. They reported that early recurrence was not associated with worse PRS compared to late recurrence (P = 0.68) 7. The current study aimed to determine the optimal cut-off point of recurrence time after definitive resection through the Youden index based on the ROC curve. We established 29 months as the recurrence-free interval to define early versus late recurrence relative to PRS. Specifically, rectal cancer patients who developed early recurrence within 29 months had markedly worse PRS. Moreover, the optimal cut-off points equal to or less than 33 months after initial surgery were determined for the patients who experienced only locoregional recurrence.

The multiple analyses of the same outcome at different times are one example of multiplicity issues. Multiplicity refers to the potential inflation of the type I error rate as a result of multiple testing24. Hence, defining a cut-off based on the minimum P-value in the aforementioned Chinese cohort23 inflates the type I error. To overcome this problem, we determined the optimal cut-off point of recurrence time after definitive resection through the Youden index based on the ROC curve. We established 29 months as the recurrence-free interval. As a consequence, the PRS curve was markedly worse in rectal cancer patients who developed early recurrence within 29 months rather than late recurrence (P = 0.010). In the multivariable Cox regression analysis,the time to recurrence (P=0.044) and recurrence management (P=0.010) variables had significant effect on post-recurrence hazard of death .

Few studies have been conducted on the association between recurrence time or other features that may affect PRS in rectal cancer and those few studies are also conflicting. Most of this literature has investigated heterogeneous colorectal cancer populations. Unfortunately, the data for rectal cancer is lacking7. In 2008, O’Connell et al. investigated survival following recurrences in colorectal cancer patients from the ACCENT database. They found that TTR was an important prognostic factor, and recurrence interval was an independent predictor for PRS in patients with recurrent colon cancer25. On the contrary, some studies reported that early recurrence had no impact on the PRS of colorectal cancer patients10,26. In the case of rectal cancer as well, Affi Koprowski et al. reported that early recurrence (< 24 months) was not associated with PRS in patients with resected rectal cancer7. However, in the present study, postoperative recurrence up to 29 months was linked to poor PRS (P=0.044).

It is clinically important to identify patients most at risk for early recurrence in terms of postoperative screening and more intensive surveillance for the occurrence of recurrence27. Several kinds of literature have stated that factors including age at diagnosis, sex, the TNM stage, positive resection margins, and unfavorable histology are associated with the early recurrence of CRC10,18,23,28–30. To this point, we investigated the impacts of several features on the timing of rectal cancer recurrence after curative treatment. We observed that TNM staging was significantly associated with the recurrence time since a higher percentage of pCR patients experienced a late recurrence and in contrast, a greatly higher percentage of the stage III cases faced an early recurrence. Although it is instinctive to expect that those who have more aggressive tumors tend to recur earlier, data reported by Affi Koprowski et al. did not support this7. However, similar to us (Table 2), they observed that there was no significant correlation between the other parameters and recurrence time7.

Although it wasn’t statistically significant, the present study revealed a higher recurrence rate in male patients compared to female patients, possibly due to surgical techniques. Male patients, especially those with narrow pelvises and bulky mesorectum, undergoing laparoscopic total mesorectal excision (TME) may experience challenges due to the limited maneuverability of rigid instruments, potentially damaging the mesorectal fascia. Additionally, laparoscopic TME in male patients is associated with an increased risk of positive CRM, which can impact survival by raising the rate of local recurrences. In contrast, robotic tools may address these challenges, enhancing specimen quality and reducing local recurrence rates31,32. More recently, the same studies demonstrated comparable pathological and oncological outcomes in female patients undergoing laparoscopic and robotic TME, likely due to the wider anatomy of the female pelvis, which diminishes the advantage of robotic tools over laparoscopy33,34.

Similar to our results, early recurrence led to a worse prognosis for patients 28–31 in the studies conducted on other gastrointestinal cancers. Therefore, implementing more stringent surveillance of disease after tumor resection in high-risk patients may help to detect early recurrence and use of secondary curative treatments.

Rectal cancer that recurs can be a serious condition, a challenge for oncologists and surgeons35, and a life-threatening situation for the patient. A well-planned multidisciplinary approach is needed to enable curative treatment for tumor involvement in multiple pelvic organs. According to studies from specialized tertiary centers, 60% of locally recurrent rectal cancer patients can be surgically resected with clear margins, and up to 62% have been reported to survive 5 years after curative surgery36–38. However, it is a complex and demanding situation to manage locally recurrent rectal cancer. Fibrosis and altered anatomy could have occurred in the small pelvis due to previous radiation and surgery and it’s common for the reoccurring tumor to involve multiple organs and structures39. In rectal cancer patients who developed metastasis, compared to those who did not receive any resection, those who had synchronous metastasectomy, pulmonary, and hepatic metastasectomy experienced greater survival benefits40–42. Palliative chemotherapy can also be a treatment option that can shrink tumors and ease cancer-related symptoms when a cure is not feasible. In the present study, the role of salvage surgery along with oncologic treatment was significant in post-recurrence survival rather than in patients who did not receive any treatment procedure.

The study is limited by its retrospective nature. The strength of our data is the accurate and long-term follow-up of the patients. However, this study suggests defining a more precise cut-off, using a larger sample size which may improve the area under curve in ROC analysis.

Conclusion

Using a multi-center cohort of rectal cancer patients, we identified 29 months as the cut-off value to differentiate early versus late recurrence. Based on our results, the recurrence time and recurrence management were found to be prognostic factors for post-recurrence survival in rectal cancer patients. Indeed, early recurrence (≤ 29 months) was significantly associated with a poor prognosis. In addition, the TNM stage was significantly associated with the recurrence time. These data suggest that surveillance following rectal cancer resection remains beneficial for patients, with an appropriate consideration based on their TNM stage. Furthermore, the recurrence management method is a significantly effective determinant of post-recurrence survival.
