Health Care Contact Days Experienced by Decedents With Advanced GI Cancer
Authors: Vishal R. Patel, Vidhyalakshmi Ramesh, Alexander K. Tsai, Ramy Sedhom, Anders D. Westanmo, Anne H. Blaes, Rachel I. Vogel, Helen M. Parsons, Timothy P. Hanna, Ishani Ganguli, Stacie B. Dusetzina, Gabrielle B. Rocque, Christopher M. Booth, Arjun Gupta
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Patients with stage IV GI cancer spent 1 in 4 days alive with health care contact!

INTRODUCTION

Persons with cancer frequently visit health care facilities.1,2 Frequent health care contact can consume patients' lives, subtracts from time spent on social activities and occupational pursuits, and negatively affect overall quality of life for patients and their care partners.2-6 Time burdens are especially pertinent in the context of advanced GI cancer, given the often incurable nature of disease and limited survival,6 coupled with the complexity of management. For example, for several GI cancers (eg, colorectal, pancreatic, gastroesophageal, etc), the fluorouracil (5-FU) chemotherapy backbone is delivered over 46-hour infusions, often requiring multiple visits. Furthermore, although well-intentioned, increasing multidisciplinary management paradigms, including a growing focus on supportive care services and management of noncancer comorbidities, also result in more health care contact.7-9

These data serve as an urgent call for the oncology community to recognize patient time toxicity, benchmark appropriate care, improve care delivery efficiency, and better support patients especially during periods of high burden.

We previously developed a patient-centered measure of health care–related time losses, termed time toxic days, which we define as days that a patient physically leaves their home to access health care.5 We have demonstrated how the marginal survival benefit of a drug can be eroded when accounting for the days spent pursuing, receiving, and recovering from the drug.10 Although we initially envisioned time toxicity to frame modest survival benefits associated with treatments in the context of clinical trial reporting, health care–related time burdens are increasingly recognized outside the context of clinical trials. For example, the percentage of days with health care contact varies by cancer type: pancreatic cancer (10%-20%), advanced endometrial cancer (15%), and acute myeloid leukemia (40%).1,2,11-13

When evaluating time burdens in real-world data, it is difficult to establish whether specific health care contact (on a given day) is net beneficial and goal-concordant or not.14 Additionally, a specific health care contact day could very well represent necessary care. Thus, in the retrospective setting, we use the previously established more neutral term: health care contact days or contact days, when referring to days with healthcare contact outside the home (v time toxic days).3 Previous studies in the cancer literature have quantified time burdens related to receiving cancer-directed treatment1,11 but have not accounted for broader health care exposure. There is additionally a critical need to understand if and how contact days differ across certain groups such as racial and ethnic minorities, who experience structural racism and fragmented care,14,15 and how contact days evolve over a patient's disease course. Information on the sources, associations, and trajectories of contact days may provide insights into patterns of care delivery and ultimately guide improvement efforts.

To address these gaps in the literature, we measured the frequency of health care contact days for patients with advanced GI cancer and determined (1) the sources of contact days, (2) clinical and demographic factors associated with contact days, and (3) trajectories of contact days over the course of illness.

METHODS

Setting and Case Selection

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using the Fairview Tumor Registry. The registry represents patients within a single, large health care network, M Health Fairview, which is a collaboration between the University of Minnesota and Masonic Cancer Center, University of Minnesota Physicians, and Fairview Health Services. M Health Fairview delivers cancer care through six sites accredited through the Integrated Network Cancer Program as part of the Commission on Cancer. The M Health Fairview community—defined as the combined zip codes of all the sites' communities—includes 121 zip codes, serving a total geographic population of 2.4 million people.

Using the registry, we identified adults (18 years or older at diagnosis) with stage IV pancreatic (International Classification of Diseases-10 code C25*), colorectal (C18*, C19*, and C20*), esophagogastric (C16*), or hepatocellular cancer (C22*) diagnosed between January 1, 2011, and December 31, 2019. We included only decedents to have complete trajectories from diagnosis to death for all individuals. We chose this study period to ensure adequate follow-up and sample size and to enable analysis of temporal trends in health care contact over multiple years while avoiding disruptions in patterns of care delivery after 2019 because of the COVID-19 pandemic. A total of 1,010 potentially eligible patients were identified; 809 were included as we excluded patients who did not receive primary oncologic care at Fairview during the study period (n = 201). All patients in the registry agreed to participate in research. The University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board reviewed this study protocol and determined that it did not constitute human research.

Clinical and Demographic Variables

We extracted the following sociodemographic characteristics for each patient from the electronic medical record and tumor registry: age at diagnosis, sex, marital status, and insurance type. We also extracted the following clinical characteristics for each patient: primary cancer site, ever receipt of systemic cancer-directed therapy (yes/no), ever hospitalization after diagnosis (yes/no), and overall survival (OS). We calculated OS as the time from histologic diagnosis (or in some cases of hepatocellular cancer, radiologic diagnosis) to death.

Primary Outcome

We identified health care contact days by reviewing encounters in the electronic medical record, including ambulatory (including clinician visits, laboratory, scans, infusions, treatments, etc), emergency room, in-patient, and facility-based visits. As described previously, we classified all days with encounter-based health care contact outside the home for any length of time as contact days, regardless of whether they were oncology-related or not. We considered days without health care contact as home days. Thus, the OS for each patient was the sum of contact days and home days. If a patient had bloodwork on Monday, a scan on Tuesday, and an infusion on Wednesday, it counted as 3 contact days. If the bloodwork, scan, and infusion were all on the same date, it counted as 1 contact day. We categorized contact days as outpatient days or inpatient days (including days hospitalized or admitted to a facility such as skilled nursing or rehabilitation). We further categorized outpatient days by the reason for visit (clinician visit, laboratory, scan, and treatment). If a patient had multiple ambulatory visits on the same date (eg, laboratory, scan, clinician visit), we considered it a single contact day, but the day was counted in each of the subgroups (not mutually exclusive). If a patient had both outpatient and inpatient contact on the same date, we considered it as a single contact day, but it was included in both (not mutually exclusive). The contact days variable did not include encounters that did not result in the patient leaving their residence and traveling to a health care facility—for example, we excluded triage phone encounters, virtual visits, telephone visits, clinician multidisciplinary conferences/tumor boards, missed/canceled appointments, and home-based care (eg, home nursing, home infusion pump disconnect, home physical therapy, etc).

We calculated the percentage of contact days over the entire disease course (contact days divided by OS) and over specific, fixed periods (eg, every 30 days from diagnosis to death). For patients not surviving the entire time of the last period (surviving 8 months and 17 days), the percentage of contact in the last period was the contact days in that period divided by 17.

Statistical Analyses

We conducted standard descriptive statistics to provide an overview of the clinical and demographic characteristics. We reported the primary outcome, contact days, both as median days with IQR and the percent of days alive. We visualized the percentage of contact days and home days over each period following diagnosis. To facilitate visualization of trajectories of percentage of contact days over time across patients with differential survival durations, we standardized the time from cancer diagnosis to death for each patient (Data Supplement, Fig S1, online only) and fit a cubic smoothing spline to the standardized observations. We visualized both a standardized trajectory for all patients considered together, and since we hypothesized that trajectories could differ on the basis of survival duration, we created separate trajectories for patients in different survival cohorts (0-12 months, 12-24 months, 24-36 months, etc).

We modeled the percentage of contact days using beta regression, appropriate for fractional outcomes (range, 0%-100%). The beta model included the following covariates: age, sex, race, insurance type, site of primary cancer, receipt of systemic therapy, and hospitalization after diagnosis. We used OS (in days) as a scaling factor in the model to account for differential survival. We repeated this analysis for the first and last months of survival and for each primary site of cancer. We reported model estimates as adjusted odds ratios (ORs). We used MATLAB 2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA) and Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) for analyses.

RESULTS

We included 809 patients diagnosed with advanced GI cancer from 2011 to 2019. The median (IQR) age was 65 (56-73) years, 53.6% were male, and 84.1% were of White race (Table 1). Colorectal (39.7%) and pancreatic (37.2%) cancers were the most commonly occurring primary sites of cancer. Over a third of patients (34.0%) in the included cohort did not receive systemic cancer-directed treatment and received supportive care only.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we found that patients with advanced GI cancer spend approximately one fourth of their days alive after diagnosis (median survival, 6 months) with in-person health care contact. A majority (84%) of these days involve attending outpatient appointments. Patients spent more than 1 in 3 days with health care contact in the initial and last periods of the disease course. Furthermore, Black individuals experienced the highest levels of contact days. These findings serve as an urgent call for the oncology community to understand sources of and variation in contact days, benchmark appropriate care, and deliver more efficient and patient-centered care.

By accounting for all sources of health care contact, we provide a more comprehensive picture of contact days faced by patients than other studies, which note a 10%-20% contact day rate related to receipt of cancer-directed care.1,2,11 Of concern, our findings are likely still underestimates since patients receiving home-based care (eg, home 5-FU pump disconnect, home nursing, home physical therapy, etc) were not captured by the current metric that focused on care in health care facilities. We also did not include the time spent in other home-based care, such as telephone visits, virtual appointments, or of activities, such as picking up medications at private pharmacies, not captured in the electronic medical record.16,17 The primary source of contact days was frequent outpatient visits—these may in fact be more burdensome to patients and care partners since they impose daily travel and logistic burdens, in addition to psychological reminders to patients about their sickness.18,19 The median survival of the cohort, approximately 6 months, is similar to the other real-world studies and lower than what is reported in contemporary clinical trials.1,20-22 This reflects a population of decedents, from 2011 to 2019 when fewer treatment options were available, and one third of patients were not eligible for or opted not to receive cancer-directed treatment. It is critical to clarify that measures summarizing contact days are not intended as a criticism of cancer care broadly or of receiving cancer-directed treatments. There are many effective cancer-directed treatments for patients with advanced GI cancer with survival and quality-of-life benefit, and pursuit of these treatments is advised. In fact, in this study, patients who received cancer-directed treatment experienced a lower proportion of contact days compared with patients not receiving cancer-directed treatment. This reflects the fact that patients who did not receive cancer-directed treatment were likely sicker and provides an opportunity to improve the delivery of supportive care alone. In clinical trials of patients with advanced GI cancer, patients receiving best supportive care had a median survival of a few months, with an only 5% contact-day rate.10,23

There are additional important takeaways from this study. First, patients experienced a high rate of contact days—39%—in the first month after diagnosis. This represents health care utilization at or soon after diagnosis (eg, care for a cancer-related GI bleed or biliary obstruction), combined with the initial, intensive, multidisciplinary paradigm of modern oncology care (eg, medical oncology appointment, staging studies, portacath placement, etc). These data support previous work which found that older adults with stage I lung cancer treated with surgery or radiation therapy experience 1 in 3 days with health care contact in the first 60 days.24 These data indicate potential opportunities to improve care coordination and provide psychosocial and logistic support in the immediate period after diagnosis. Second, after the initial intensive phase, monthly contact days declined to a more moderate 15%-20%, before increasing again before death. The pattern of health care contact over the course of illness, depicted as a U-shaped curve, is a novel finding. Previous work in US older adults has described a similar U-shaped trajectory of the costs of cancer care over phases (initial, continuing, terminal phases) of care across tumor types, across different lengths of survival (<2 years to >8 years).25-27 Future work in other cancer types (eg, advanced breast and prostate cancer) can evaluate if the U-shaped curve has humps in the middle phase, associated with cancer progression and switching lines of treatments, and the predominant source of contact days (outpatient v inpatient) for the different peaks.

When interpreting patient-related factors associated with contact days, it is critical to note that contact days, in essence, represent health care use, including health care access. Structural racism and other barriers to care may limit health care access, resulting in fewer contact days and perceived good care.14 Some health care is of course necessary, but too much is bad, and there is a need to understand and benchmark appropriateness. The current cohort only included insured patients—since in our health system, initially uninsured patients receive emergency insurance and support to be able to receive longitudinal cancer care—and thus, barriers to accessing care are less likely, although still possible. We found that patients with Medicare or Medicaid insurance had slightly lower contact days than those with private insurance. In this study, Black individuals experienced significantly higher odds (almost 2.5 fold) of contact days than White individuals did. Previous work has noted that Black patients spend more time in travel and administrative tasks when attending clinic appointments14,28 and that Black individuals experience and spend more on high-acuity care (emergency department and inpatient) than ambulatory care.29 Black patients with cancer are also more likely to die in health care facilities.30 Thus, we specifically examined contact days at the end of life, where again, Black patients had higher odds of contact days. Higher contact days for Black individuals might represent more uncoordinated care and social determinants of health (eg, limited ability to pay for private help at home etc) that limit what can be done at home, necessitating contact with the health care system (eg, hospitalization). Additional mixed methods work is needed to explore how clinical factors such as comorbidities, social determinants of health, and care preferences affect contact days. Race differences were also starker in specific cancers, such as liver cancer. Patients with liver cancer often have comorbid cirrhosis and higher baseline health care needs. Disparities in access to proactive, quality ambulatory care, puts patients at risk of decompensation and higher contact days. Similarly, the finding that older adults had higher contact days might represent their needs, and the perception about their ease of scheduling since they may not be in the workforce and may not have childcare responsibilities. That said, many older adults report trouble getting to places like the doctor's office,19 and every effort should be made to minimize unnecessary contact days.

This study has limitations. First, our data capture patients from a single US health care network, which is uniquely positioned as a tertiary care center and safety net and includes patients from a large community health care network. We were unable to capture encounters that took place outside of our health care network, including facility-based hospice days. This is likely to underestimate health care encounter days. Some sociodemographic characteristics (eg, race, sex, etc) may not be completely accurate because of retrospective data, and we did not evaluate how travel distances or the site of care within the network or the type of systemic therapy (including if on a clinical trial) affected contact days. Although the results are not generalizable to all patients with advanced GI cancer in the United States, to our knowledge, the data represent the most comprehensive evaluation to date of the time-related health care burdens for such patients. Second, health care delivery underwent changes in and since 2020, including greater adoption of virtual appointments. We limited the study period before 2020 to avoid confounding shocks related to the COVID-19 pandemic. We are conducting qualitative work to explore how patients and care partners perceive home-based care and if and how the contact days measure should evolve. Future work should evaluate time burdens in a more contemporary era. Third, this study only included decedents during the study period, which by definition excludes long-term survivors who may have different patterns of health care contact. However, we observed similar levels, sources, and trajectories of contact days even in patients living 6 years. Fourth, we did not attempt to classify the quality of contact days—whether they were essential or goal concordant or not.31 We also did not attempt to parse out whether the care delivered on a contact day was cancer imposed, treatment imposed, or both. Prospective work can help understand how much a patient's health care exposure can be reduced. Fifth, given that the data source only comprised decedents, any interpretation of temporal trends is limited by the fact that the composition of shorter- and longer-term survivors in each with different treatment patters differs across years. Finally, we lacked information about prediagnosis comorbidities, which may be strongly associated with contact days.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that decedents with advanced GI cancer spend 1 in 4 days alive with health care contact. This is concerning by itself and even more so in the context of a median survival of approximately 6 months. An even greater frequency of days (1 in 3) is spent with health care contact immediately after diagnosis and before death, with a more moderate middle phase. This U-shaped curve of health care contact over the course of illness is novel. Contact days are increasing over time as cancer care becomes more complex, and certain populations, such as older adults and Black individuals, experience the highest rate of contact days. These findings highlight the need to benchmark appropriate care, understand sources of variation, and deliver more time efficient care, while providing adequate supports to this vulnerable population and their care partners.
