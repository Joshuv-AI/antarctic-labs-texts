Artificial intelligence-assisted UV–Vis spectrophotometric method for simultaneous quantification of naringin and naringenin in Thymus canoviridis Jalas
Authors: Fatma Demirkaya Miloğlu, Hira Beril Küçük, Burak Bayrak, Abdul Kayum Iddrisu, İbrahim Yücel Özbek, Leyla Güven, Yücel Kadıoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to develop an artificial intelligence (AI)-based UV–Vis spectrophotometric method for simultaneously quantifying naringin and naringenin in Thymus canoviridis methanol extract. Conventional spectrophotometric techniques often face challenges in analyzing mixtures of compounds with overlapping spectra, making accurate quantification difficult. This limitation calls for advanced modelling approaches that incorporate machine learning to enhance prediction accuracy while aligning with green chemistry principles.MethodsBinary mixtures of naringin and naringenin were prepared at concentrations ranging from 5 to 40 μg/mL. UV–Vis spectra were collected over the 240–450 nm range. The trained machine learning models included Support Vector Regression (SVR), Linear Regression, Lasso Regression, Ridge Regression, and Elastic Net. These models were used to predict the concentrations of both flavonoids from their spectral data. Performance was evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and the coefficient of determination (R2).ResultsThe Ridge Regression model showed superior predictive performance, achieving MAEs of 0.882 for naringin and 0.378 for naringenin, with R2 values of 0.9912 and 0.9984, respectively. The model was successfully applied to the Thymus canoviridis extract, enabling precise quantification of both compounds without chemical separation. The method’s green chemistry profile was assessed using the AGREE and ComplexGAPI tools. AGREE scored it 0.72, reflecting strong green chemistry compliance, while ComplexGAPI revealed an overall environmentally favorable profile with some moderate environmental burdens related to solvent use.ConclusionThis study presents a green UV–Vis spectrophotometric method enhanced by AI for the simultaneous quantification of naringin and naringenin in plant extracts. By leveraging AI for spectral differentiation, the method achieves high accuracy and reliability without requiring chemical separation. This approach represents a significant advancement in plant metabolite analysis, facilitating high-throughput screening in phytochemical studies. The method's minimal solvent use, low waste generation, and energy efficiency make it a sustainable alternative to traditional techniques, suited for environmentally conscious phytochemical analysis.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Naringenin, Naringin, Thymus canoviridis, UV–Vis spectrophotometry

Background

Flavonoids, found in plants either as free compounds or as glycosides, represent an important class of phenolic compounds. Recognized for their yellow color, these benzo-γ-pyrone (chromone) derivatives typically possess a phenyl ring attached to the 2nd and 3rd positions of the chromone ring. The presence of various substituents, hydroxyl groups and methyl ethers on the ring results in a diverse range of flavonoid compounds. Over 6000 different flavonoids have been identified in plants, contributing significantly to our understanding of the chemical complexity within plants [1]. Flavonoids, which exhibit a wide range of biological activities in plants, animals, bacteria and humans, act as UV filters, allelopathic compounds, phytoalexins and antimicrobial defences in plants. These compounds contribute to the pigments of fruits, medicinal plants, vegetables and herbs and are recognized for their beneficial effects on human and animal health [2, 3]. Flavonoids are classified into structural subclasses based on modifications to the central C-ring, including flavonols, flavones, flavan-3-ols, flavanones, isoflavones, and anthocyanidins [3]. Naringin (Fig. 1A), chemically identified as naringenin-7-O-neohesperidoside, belongs to the flavanone glycoside subclass. On the other hand, naringenin (Fig. 1B) itself is classified as a flavanone. The flavanone structure, characterised by a 15-carbon skeleton and a ketone group, is highly reactive. This structural reactivity makes flavanones susceptible to various chemical transformations. Notable reactions include hydroxylation, glycosylation and O-methylation [3]. Similar to other flavonoids, naringin exhibits a wide range of pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anticancer, antimutagenic, antioxidant effects, as well as benefits in bone regeneration, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, and nervous system health [1, 4–7]. Its aglycone, naringenin, also contributes to its anti-ulcer properties. Notably recognized for its ability to reduce LDL cholesterol, naringin may also enhance drug absorption by modulating intestinal enzymes. It also has a significant effect on lipid and alcohol metabolism in the liver, increasing the organ's antioxidant activity. Naringin extends its protective effects to reduce radiation-induced damage in the body [1, 4–7]. In particular, they share the ability to reduce LDL cholesterol and potentially increase drug absorption by affecting intestinal enzymes. In addition, both compounds significantly affect lipid and alcohol metabolism in the liver, thereby increasing antioxidant activity. In addition, naringenin crosses the blood–brain barrier, exerts neuroprotective effects and sensitizing hepatocytes to insulin, reducing insulin resistance and extending its broad biological effects [8–11]. Extensive research over the past two decades supports the development of naringin as a novel drug for cough relief and sputum reduction, with approval from the China Food and Drug Administration for clinical trials [12]. Both naringin and naringenin, besides sharing similar pharmacological properties, can create a synergistic effect when used together. This is especially relevant in the preparation of a formulation for conditions such as arteriosclerosis, high cholesterol, and osteoporosis [8–11]. Various methods have been utilized for the determination of naringin and naringenin, including high-performance liquid chromatography-ultraviolet detection (HPLC–UV) [13] and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) [4, 12], ultra-fast liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time-of-flight tandem mass spectrometry system (UFLC-Q-TOF-MS/MS) [14] in biological matrices In plant samples, methods such as HPLC [15, 16] and LC-MS/MS [17, 18] have been developed for the determination of naringin and naringenin with other phenolic compounds. However, a specific study focusing on the simultaneous determination of naringin and naringenin in the presence of other phenolic compounds in plant samples using a spectrophotometric method has not yet been conducted.

In both the quality control of pharmaceuticals and the analysis of plant extracts, spectrophotometric methods are widely used because of their simplicity and cost-effectiveness [19, 20]. These methods typically rely on active compounds with chromophoric groups that absorb UV–Vis light, yielding additive absorbance values in line with the Lambert–Beer law. However, overlapping peaks in UV–Vis spectra pose significant challenges to accurate compound and impurity determination [19, 21]. To address these challenges, artificial intelligence (AI) and chemometric approaches have emerged as viable solutions. These approaches aim to develop calibration models that reduce chemical consumption and contribute to green chemistry. Common chemometric methods include classical least squares (CLS), principal component regression (PCR) and partial least squares (PLS) [22]. However, CLS can struggle with spectral overlap and high-dimensional data, while PCR and PLS often exhibit limitations in complex datasets, such as higher mean absolute errors (MAEs) and limited generalization capacity [23]. To address these shortcomings, machine learning algorithms such as Support Vector Regression (SVR), Ridge Regression, and Elastic Net can be employed to achieve more accurate results without increasing model complexity [24]. This enables the development of environmentally friendly spectrophotometric methods that minimize the use of chemical substances. This study highlights the application of AI and machine learning algorithms, including SVR, linear regression, ridge regression, lasso regression, and elastic net, to enhance prediction accuracy and overcome these challenges without increasing model complexity, with a specific focus on the simultaneous estimation of naringin and naringenin in complex plant matrices.

Material and method

Instrument

The UV–Visanalyses were performed using a Thermo Scientific Multiscan GO 51119300 model spectrophotometer. Measurements were performed in absorbance mode over the wavelength range of 200–500 nm, suitable for monitoring the absorption characteristics of analytes dissolved in methanol. A fast scan speed and a 1-nm bandwidth were used to ensure adequate spectral resolution and reproducibility. Throughout the analysis, the microplate temperature was maintained at 23 °C to minimize potential variability in absorbance measurements.

Materials and solutions

Standards: Naringin and naringenin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Methanol (Merck, Germany) and double-deionized water (Milli-Q water) were used as analytical-grade chemical reagents. Stock solutions of both analytes were prepared by dissolving them in methanol at a concentration of 1.0 mg/mL for both naringin and naringenin.

Plant: Thymus canoviridis, an endemic plant, was collected on 20 June 2022 from an altitude of 2000 m (coordinates 40° 5′ 2′′ N, 41° 26′ 2′′ E) and dried in the dark for this study. The plant was identified by Prof. Dr. Yusuf Kaya (Atatürk University, Faculty of Science, Department of Biology). A voucher specimen (AUEF 1393 and AUEF 1389) was deposited at the Biodiversity Application and Research Centre, Atatürk University, Erzurum, Türkiye. The collection of plant material adhered to national regulations in Türkiye governing the collection of non-protected plant species for scientific research. For plant extraction, 50 g of Thymus canoviridis Jalas was finely ground using a grinder and extracted by boiling with a mixture of 1 L of methanol. The extraction process was carried out overnight at room temperature with stirring using a magnetic stirrer. After filtration, the filtrate was evaporated using a rotary evaporator at 120 rpm. The yield of Thymus canoviridis extract obtained was found to be 14.30% [18].

Construction of the training set for data preparation

Working solutions of naringin and naringenin were prepared by diluting their respective stock solutions in methanol to achieve concentrations ranging from 5 to 40 μg/mL for each compound. A calibration set comprising 25 combinations was systematically designed using a full factorial design. These combinations represented various concentration combinations of naringin and naringenin. The UV-Vis absorption profiles of these different combinations were carefully documented across the wavelength spectrum from 240 to 450 nm at 1 nm intervals. The measurements were repeated six times.

Prior to the application of AI methods, the datasets underwent comprehensive pre-processing. Missing and outlier data were identified and appropriately handled, while inconsistent or incorrectly stored entries were corrected. The data were subjected to dimension reduction and normalisation processes to make them suitable for analysis.

Singular value decomposition (SVD)

One of the preprocessing steps performed on the data is dimensionality reduction. The most used methods in data analysis for dimensionality reduction are Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and SVD. PCA was not preferred in this study due to its disadvantages such as being limited to basic patterns in the data, creating new variables that optimize only a certain variance, thus losing original features, and relying on assumptions about the distribution of the data. Instead of PCA, another dimensionality reduction approach, SVD, was preferred, which minimizes information loss by using the most important singular values in the dataset.

Artificial intelligence methods

Implemented AI and Machine Learning methods in Python for data analysis and prediction. The Python programming language, version 3.8.15, was chosen for training and testing due to its dynamic and interpretable features. The source code editor selected was Visual Studio Code (VSCode), a free and open-source software from Microsoft. Training and testing were conducted using VSCode on a MacBook Pro, equipped with the Apple M1 Pro chip, 16 GB RAM, and a 14-core GPU. Five different machine learning models were employed for training, and the optimum results were obtained by varying the hyperparameters to create different combinations.

As a result of the pre-processing processes of the train and test datasets, the data was separated to obtain separate outputs for the active ingredients of naringin and naringenin. During the training, five different machine learning models were selected for both Y values ​​and the combinations that gave the optimum result were performed by changing the hyperparameter.

Results and discussion

Optimization of spectrophotometric method

This study focused on optimizing spectrophotometric methods for the analysis of naringin and naringenin. Previous studies have demonstrated that while naringin and naringenin are insoluble in most organic solvents, they exhibit good solubility in water, ethanol, and methanol [30, 31]. Consequently, methanol was chosen as the solvent for the dissolution of both compounds [30], ensuring consistency with the methanol plant extracts and maintaining spectral stability by minimizing solvent-induced wavelength shifts that could adversely affect quantitative accuracy, while also taking overall green chemistry considerations into account. After establishing the optimal spectroscopic conditions, individual spectra of naringin and naringenin were recorded within the wavelength range of 240–450 nm in the methanol solution (Fig. 2A and B). The maximum absorbances for standard naringin and naringenin were observed at 283 nm and 288 nm, respectively. Both compounds exhibited a linear response over the measured wavelength range.

UV-Vis Absorption Spectra; A Naringin (5–40 µg/mL); B Naringenin (5–40 µg/mL); C Spectral Overlay of Naringin and Naringenin (25 Concentration Combinations)

Analysis of the absorption spectra of naringin and naringenin revealed significant spectral overlap between the two compounds, complicating their direct quantification (Fig. 2C). Among various analytical techniques, traditional c absorption spectrophotometry is extensively employed in pharmaceutical analysis and quality control due to its user-friendly nature, convenience, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness [32]. However, traditional spectrophotometric methods frequently encounter challenges when conducting simultaneous analyses of multiple active compounds in botanical matrices. This limitation is primarily due to spectral interferences from coexisting analytes, which hinder accurate determination of their respective concentrations [22, 33].

Spectral data processing

In this study, a calibration set was established by pooling multiple samples with varying concentrations of the active compounds naringin and naringenin, ranging from 5 to 40 µg/mL for each analyte. This calibration set effectively captured spectral variations across the entire concentration range, contributing to a more robust and reliable predictive framework. The calibration set consisted of absorbance values measured at 211 distinct points within the wavelength range of 240–450 nm, with intervals of 1 nm (Δλ = 1 nm). Experiments were conducted within this wavelength range during the selection of model hyperparameters. Each absorbance measurement was paired with its corresponding compound concentration, thereby facilitating the establishment of a mathematical relationship between absorbance and concentration throughout the modelling process.

Following the preprocessing of the training and testing datasets, the data were stratified to create distinct outputs for naringin and naringenin. During the training phase, five different machine learning models (SVR, Ridge Regression, Lasso Regression, Elastic Net, and Linear Regression) were applied to both analytes, with performance evaluated across various hyperparameter configurations. The goal was to identify the most accurate and generalizable model for quantifying naringin and naringenin concentrations based on spectral data.

Regression Model Performances for naringin and naringenin (MAE, and R2)

Support vector regression

The SVR model, applied with a linear kernel, was tested with regularization parameters that quantify C = 0.75, C = 1, and C = 1.5, and a range of epsilon values (error margin), ε = 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0. For naringin, the best performance was achieved with SVR (C = 1.5, ε = 0.5), yielding an MAE of 1.6066 and R2 of 0.9720. While this performance is strong, it is somewhat limited by the relatively high MAE compared to other models, such as Ridge and Lasso. For naringenin, SVR (C = 1.5, ε = 0.01) exhibited robust performance with an MAE of 0.4686 and an R2 of 0.9976. Although SVR effectively captures non-linear relationships within the data, it is more sensitive to parameter adjustments, leading to variability in error rates as epsilon values increase. Thus, SVR demonstrates high predictive capability, particularly for naringenin, but is surpassed by models with lower MAE and more stable performance across different parameters.

Ridge regression

The Ridge Regression model was evaluated across a range of regularization parameters = 0.001, 0.005, 0.009, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 1, 4.3, and 5.0. For naringin, the best performance was achieved with Ridge ( = 0.005), resulting in an MAE of 0.882 and an R2 of 0.9911, indicating minimal prediction error and a high level of accuracy. This performance was notably stronger than SVR, reflecting Ridge’s ability to capture linear relationships effectively while minimizing overfitting through regularization. For naringenin, the optimal configuration was also Ridge ( = 0.005), which produced an MAE of 0.3784 and an R2 of 0.9984. This high R2 value indicates that Ridge Regression with this parameter captures nearly all the variance in naringenin concentrations, with a very low prediction error. Compared to other regularization values, Ridge with = 0.005 provided the best balance of error minimization and model simplicity. Unlike SVR, which displayed sensitivity to changes in ε values, Ridge Regression was more stable across different values, showing only slight variation in performance. This stability makes Ridge Regression a highly reliable choice for both naringin and naringenin, offering strong predictive capability with less sensitivity to parameter adjustments. Thus, Ridge Regression, particularly with = 0.005, demonstrates both accuracy and robustness, making it an optimal model for these analytes.

Lasso regression

Lasso Regression was evaluated with regularization parameters = 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1. For both naringin and naringenin, the best performance was achieved with Lasso ( = 0.0001), resulting in an MAE of 1.0474 and R2 of 0.9883 for naringin, and an MAE of 0.4412 and R2 of 0.9978 for naringenin. This parameter setting minimized error while maintaining high accuracy, though with slightly higher MAE compared to Ridge Regression. Lasso’s ability to reduce some coefficients to zero added model simplicity, but it showed less stability across values compared to Ridge. Overall, =0.0001 was the optimal choice for balancing accuracy and model simplicity in Lasso Regression.

Elastic net regression

Elastic Net was tested with various combinations of regularization parameters, including α = 0.0005, 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, and 0.1, along with L1 ratios of 0.1 and 0.01. The best performance for both naringin and naringenin was achieved with Elastic Net (α = 0.0001, L1 ratio = 0.1), yielding an MAE of 1.0686 and R2 of 0.9873 for naringin, and an MAE of 0.4704 and R2 of 0.9976 for naringenin. This parameter setting provided a balance of Ridge and Lasso regularization, effectively minimizing errors while maintaining high predictive accuracy. However, Elastic Net showed more variability across α and L1 ratio settings than Ridge, indicating a greater sensitivity to parameter selection. Overall, α = 0.0001 with L1 ratio = 0.1 was the optimal configuration for balancing accuracy and generalizability in Elastic Net Regression. Thus, Elastic Net provides a balanced regularization approach but lacks the consistent low-error performance seen in Ridge Regression.

Linear regression

Linear Regression was used as a baseline model to provide a straightforward comparison with more complex models. For naringin, it achieved an MAE of 1.6863 and an R2 of 0.9688, indicating a reasonable fit but with higher error rates than other models. For Naringenin, Linear Regression produced an MAE of 0.7064 and an R2 of 0.9948, showing good predictive capability but lacking the precision of regularized models. While Linear Regression is simple and effective, it does not offer the fine-tuning available in models like Ridge and Lasso, leading to slightly higher residual errors.

In summary, Ridge Regression proved to be the most stable and accurate model, with consistently low MAE and high R2 values, making it the optimal choice for both naringin and naringenin concentration predictions. SVR, while effective for non-linear relationships, showed sensitivity to parameter changes, leading to variability in error rates. Lasso Regression provided model simplicity by reducing some coefficients to zero but had slightly higher MAE values than Ridge, particularly for Naringin. Elastic Net aimed to balance Ridge and Lasso’s strengths but was more sensitive to adjustments in α and L1 ratios, resulting in less consistent performance. Linear Regression, used as a baseline, lacked the fine-tuning capabilities of regularized models, resulting in higher residual errors.

So, the predictive performance of Ridge Regression ( = 0.005) for both naringin and naringenin was evaluated using calibration curves. Calibration plots for each compound demonstrated a strong correlation between predicted and actual concentrations, indicating the model’s high predictive accuracy and robustness in estimating concentrations across the analysed range. For naringenin, the calibration curve generated with Ridge Regression resulted in the equation y = 0.9982x + 0.0306 and an R2 value of 0.9984 (Fig. 3A). This high R2 value signifies that Ridge Regression explains approximately 99.84% of the variance in naringenin data, underscoring the precision of the model in capturing the true concentration levels. The slope close to 1 and the very low intercept indicate minimal bias, highlighting the model’s accuracy and consistency. The near-perfect linear correlation seen in the calibration plot for naringenin confirms the model’s suitability for precise quantification. Similarly, for naringin, the calibration curve provided the equation y = 0.9891x + 0.2253 with an R2 value of 0.9912 (Fig. 3B). While the R2 value for naringin is slightly lower than that of naringenin, it still indicates that over 99.1% of the data’s variance is explained by the model. The slope close to 1 and the low intercept confirm that the model accurately captures naringin concentrations, though with a minor deviation that reflects a strong, if slightly less precise, prediction compared to naringenin. The high R2 values in both calibration plots validate the accuracy and robustness of Ridge Regression for quantifying these compounds. The strong linear relationships observed between predicted and actual values suggest that the model is highly reliable for concentration estimation, effectively capturing the subtle variations in concentration for both analytes.

Applicability of AI-assisted UV–Vis spectrophotometric method to plant extracts

To evaluate the applicability of the developed Artificial Intelligence-Assisted UV–Vis Spectrophotometric Method for the quantification of phytochemicals, analyses of naringin and naringenin in Thymus canoviridis extracts were conducted. The extraction of these compounds was performed by diluting the original methanol extract, as outlined in “materials and solutions section”. Specifically, 1 g of the methanol extract was diluted in ratios of 1/2 and 1/5 to ensure representative concentrations of the active compounds.

The absorbance spectra of the prepared solutions were recorded within a wavelength range of 240–450 nm, utilizing a Δλ of 1.0 nm with UV–Vis spectroscopy. The associated spectra are illustrated in Fig. 4. As quantified via Ridge Regression ( = 0.005), the quantification results showed that for naringin, concentrations were 14.626 ± 0.078 µg/g extract in the 1/5 dilution and 33.881 ± 0.145 µg/g extract in the 1/2 dilution, indicating a higher concentration in less diluted extracts. Similarly, for naringenin, the concentrations were 30.963 ± 0.203 µg/g extract in the 1/5 dilution and 12.900 ± 0.346 µg/g extract in the 1/2 dilution, suggesting a higher yield in more diluted extracts.

In our previous study, we employed the UPLC-MS/MS method to qualitatively and quantitatively assess phenolic compounds in methanol extract of Thymus canoviridis sourced from Turkey. Notably, the methanol extract contained significant amounts of naringin (68.57 µg/g extract) and naringenin (63.14 µg/g extract), alongside other phenolic compounds such as apigenin, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, fumaric acid, gallic acid, luteolin, quinic acid, rosmarinic acid, and taxifolin [18]. The consistency of the quantification results obtained from the AI-assisted UV–Vis method with those from the UPLC-MS/MS analysis underscores the accuracy and reliability of the former for phenolic compound quantification (Table 2). This concordance between methodologies enhances the validity of the AI-assisted approach, facilitating rapid and simultaneous quantification of naringin and naringenin. Consequently, this method demonstrates significant potential for broader applications in plant metabolite profiling and phytochemical analysis.

Green assessment profile: AGREE and ComplexGAPI evaluations

In this study, the green chemistry credentials of the AI-assisted UV–Vis spectrophotometric method developed for the quantification of naringin and naringenin were assessed using the AGREE (Analytical GREENness Metric Approach) [34] and ComplexGAPI (Green Analytical Procedure Index) [35] tools. The assessment encompassed both the analytical measurement and the plant extraction step involved in sample preparation. These evaluations demonstrate the environmental advantages of the method, particularly the ability to achieve spectral resolution through artificial intelligence without the need for chromatographic separation.

The AGREE analysis yielded a score of 0.72, indicating strong compliance with green chemistry principles (Fig. 5A). The AGREE circular graph primarily shows green segments, indicating minimized environmental impact through reduced solvent use, low energy consumption, and minimal safety risks. The method's limited use of methanol further enhances its eco-friendly profile. This high AGREE score supports the method as a sustainable alternative, underlining its compliance with green analytical chemistry standards.

Green chemistry evaluation of the AI-assisted UV–Vis spectrophotometric method using AGREE (A) and ComplexGAPI (B)

The ComplexGAPI evaluation further confirmed the proposed method's greenness (Fig. 5B). The pictogram is largely characterized by green areas, indicating a low environmental burden. Red zones are associated with the use of methanol, a moderately toxic organic solvent, during extraction. Additionally, the plant extract was performed during sample preparation, the corresponding zone was represented in yellow to reflect a simple additional treatment. Despite the inclusion of a conventional extraction step, the overall ComplexGAPI profile underscores the method's environmental favorability.

The AI-assisted UV–Vis spectrophotometric method enables simultaneous, precise quantification of naringin and naringenin without chromatographic separation, distinguishing it from traditional methods such as HPLC and LC–MS. These conventional chromatographic methods often involve higher volumes of organic solvents, complex sample preparation steps, and substantial energy demands. In contrast, this study’s approach leverages AI-driven spectral separation, resulting in significant reductions in chemical consumption, waste generation, and energy use. This positions the method as a greener analytical tool that aligns with sustainable chemistry objectives.

Conclusion

This study introduces a pioneering Artificial Intelligence-assisted UV–Vis spectrophotometric method for the simultaneous quantification of naringin and naringenin in Thymus canoviridis extract. By employing AI models, particularly Ridge Regression, the method effectively differentiates overlapping spectral signals without requiring chemical separation, thereby addressing a major limitation of conventional UV–Vis spectrophotometry.

The integration of artificial intelligence enables direct, simultaneous quantification from UV–Vis spectra, eliminating the need for labor-intensive, time-consuming, and costly chromatographic separation. The strong agreement between the proposed method and UPLC–MS/MS results confirms the accuracy and reliability of the approach.

In addition to its analytical performance, the method was also evaluated from a green analytical chemistry perspective. Reduced solvent use, minimal waste generation, and the elimination of chemical separation steps contribute to a more sustainable and environmentally considerate analytical workflow, particularly for the routine analysis of complex botanical matrices. Overall, this AI-enhanced spectrophotometric technique provides an efficient, reliable, and environmentally mindful analytical strategy for phytochemical research. Its applicability to complex plant samples highlights its potential for broader use in natural product analysis and plant sciences.
