Additive enhancement of biodegradability and antibacterial performance in thermoplastic starch/chitosan films incorporating oregano essential oil and silver-zinc oxide nanoparticles
Authors: Zahra Sayyar, Hadi Hajikhodazadeh, Mahsa Khadem Sadigh, Zahra Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a novel thermoplastic starch–chitosan composite film, enhanced with oregano essential oil and silver-zinc oxide (Ag-ZnO: 1%) nanoparticles, designed to overcome the limitations of conventional biopolymer packaging. The synergistic combination of these additives significantly improved mechanical strength (tensile strength: 72.86 to 98.72 MPa), stiffness (Young’s modulus), and reduced water uptake by up to 25% compared to unmodified controls. Rigorous biodegradation tests under UV, soil, and aqueous conditions demonstrated accelerated environmental breakdown, with up to 39.53% weight loss after one month under UV exposure. Antimicrobial assays showed inhibitory activity against the tested microorganisms, with inhibition zones of 42 mm against Staphylococcus aureus and 33 mm against Escherichia coli. These results highlight the multifunctional advantages of integrating biopolymers, essential oils, and nanometallic agents, and support a rational strategy for developing sustainable food-packaging materials with improved physical, degradative, and antimicrobial performance.Keywords: Biodegradable composites, Chitosan, Thermoplastic starch, Oregano essential oil, Silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles, Active food packaging

Introduction

Petroleum-derived polymers dominate current packaging applications because of their low cost and processing convenience; however, environmental concerns over plastic pollution have prompted growing efforts to develop biopolymer-based alternatives [1–4]. Biopolymers, such as starch and chitosan, offer several advantages, including biodegradability, renewability, and a reduced ecological impact. However, their practical application is limited by inherent drawbacks, such as poor mechanical strength, high moisture sensitivity, and low thermal stability [5–7]. To overcome these limitations, composite formation and the incorporation of functional additives have become central strategies in biopolymer research [1, 8–10]. Corn starch is an abundant, low-cost, and renewable biopolymer that has attracted considerable attention as a sustainable packaging matrix because of its film-forming ability and biodegradability [1, 11–15]. However, its practical application remains limited by strong hydrophilicity, poor moisture resistance, and inadequate mechanical and thermal stability, which are primarily associated with its polar hydroxyl-rich structure and susceptibility to water uptake. These inherent limitations often necessitate blending, plasticization, or nanoparticle reinforcement to improve performance for packaging use [16–18]. Chitosan (CS), a biopolymer, has gained significant attention in the packaging industry as a sustainable alternative to conventional plastic. Its unique properties, including biodegradability, non-toxicity, biocompatibility, and inherent antimicrobial activity, make it especially suitable for food packaging applications [19–21]. Chitosan-based films and coatings can extend the shelf life of perishable products by reducing microbial contamination and acting as effective barriers to oxygen and moisture. Blending chitosan with starch or other biopolymers can improve mechanical and barrier properties, while the addition of natural additives such as essential oils or plant extracts further enhances antioxidant and antimicrobial performance and making them more suitable for industrial-scale packaging applications [22–24]. Essential oils are primarily composed of phenolic compounds that can be directly integrated into a packaging film matrix. These compounds are gradually released during the transportation or storage of food, helping minimize food spoilage [25]. The adoption of chitosan-based packaging not only addresses environmental concerns by reducing reliance on synthetic plastics but also meets growing consumer demand for safer and more natural food preservation methods [20, 26, 27]. Adding plasticizers decreases the tensile strength of starch films [28, 29]. The mechanical features of mixing glycerol and starch with pure starch films were investigated by Lourdin et al. [30]. Their results showed that pure starch films had high tensile strength values, whereas the mixing of glycerol and starch films had low tensile strength.

Despite these advances, significant gaps remain in the design of starch–chitosan films fortified with both oregano essential oil and metal nanoparticles, especially regarding the synergistic enhancement of mechanical, antimicrobial, and degradation properties. Numerous antimicrobial chemicals, such as silver, copper, and zinc rods have been used to develop starch-based antimicrobial materials [31, 32]. Furthermore, metal oxides such as zinc oxide (ZnO), titanium dioxide (TiO2), and Ag nanoparticles (NPs) have been applied to enhance the functional features of biodegradable films. TiO2, ZnO, and Ag nanoparticles (NPs) are inert, non-toxic, and inexpensive materials with high antibacterial and photocatalytic activities [33–35].

Most studies have focused on single additives or binary composites, but the combined effect of multiple functional agents—such as oregano essential oil and silver-zinc oxide nanoparticles—has not been systematically evaluated. This work addresses these gaps by developing a novel thermoplastic starch–chitosan composite film, enhanced with oregano essential oil and Ag-ZnO nanoparticles, to achieve multifunctional improvements in mechanical strength, antimicrobial activity, and environmental degradation. The study systematically evaluates the physicochemical properties, biodegradability under UV, aqueous, and soil conditions, and antibacterial effectiveness of these nanocomposites, providing a rational approach for the formulation of high-performance, sustainable packaging materials.​​ Ag and ZnO nanoparticles interact with the starch-chitosan matrix through several key mechanisms such as hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, physical dispersion. ZnO nanoparticles form hydrogen bonds with the hydroxyl groups of starch and the amino/hydroxyl groups of chitosan, improving dispersion and stability within the matrix.​ The positively charged amino groups of chitosan interact with the negatively charged surfaces of ZnO and Ag nanoparticles, enhancing their distribution and reinforcing the composite structure.​ Both nanoparticles are distributed throughout the matrix, often at the interface between starch and chitosan, where they act as crosslinking agents or stress transfer points, improving mechanical strength.​ However, the combination of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles results in synergistic enhancement of antimicrobial activity and photocatalytic properties, making the composite suitable for advanced packaging applications.​

The novelty of this work lies in the synergistic combination of biopolymers, essential oils, and dual nanometallic agents, which has not been previously reported in the literature. By integrating oregano essential oil with Ag and ZnO nanoparticles, the composite film demonstrates enhanced mechanical properties, improved barrier performance, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, surpassing the capabilities of existing starch-chitosan composites. The essential oil not only contributes to antimicrobial activity but also interacts with the polymer matrix, further stabilizing the nanoparticles and enhancing their dispersion. This approach not only addresses the limitations of single-component additives but also provides a robust, multifunctional platform for sustainable food packaging, setting a new benchmark for the rational design of biodegradable packaging materials.

Experimental

Material

Corn starch (approximately 73% amylopectin and 27% amylose) was obtained from Sigma–Aldrich. Glycerol (C3H5(OH)3) was purchased from Mojallali (Tehran, Iran) and used as a plasticizer. Chitosan (CS, Mw = 50 ± 10 kDa, and 85% of acetylation), zinc nitrate hexahydrate, silver salt (AgNO3), acetic acid, and sodium hydroxide were purchased from Merck (Germany). Oregano was obtained from a local bazaar located in Maragheh, Iran. Escherichia coli (PTCC 1270) and Staphylococcus aureus (PTCC 1112) were developed by microbial Persian-type culture collection (PTCC, Tehran, Iran). Nutrient Agar (NA) and Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) were provided by Biolife Company (Milan, Italy).

Synthesis

Synthesis of ZnO NPs

Two grams of dried and crushed oregano leaves were added to 100 mL of boiling distilled water and stirred for 15 min. The mixture was filtered through Whatman No. 40 filter paper, and the resulting extract was stored at 4 °C for further use. For nanoparticle synthesis, 2 g of Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O was dissolved in 20 mL of the oregano extract, and the pH was adjusted to 10 using 0.1 M NaOH. The solution was then heated in a furnace at 450 °C for 2 h to obtain ZnO nanoparticles. The resulting nanoparticles were washed with ethanol and deionized water three times to remove impurities, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the mean values and standard deviations were reported for reproducibility.

Synthesis of Ag NPs

According to established evidence [36, 37], 15 mL of 1 mM AgNO₃ solution was combined with 20 mL of the oregano extract. The mixture was continuously stirred at 70 °C with a stirring speed of 300 rpm for 15 min, followed by heating at 200 °C for 2 h to produce silver nanoparticles. The nanoparticles were washed with ethanol and deionized water, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the mean values and standard deviations were reported for reproducibility.

Synthesis of Ag-ZnO nanocomposite

To synthesize the Ag-ZnO nanocomposite, 2 g of Zn(NO₃)₂·6H₂O and 15 mL of 1 mM AgNO₃ solution were added to 20 mL of the oregano extract. The pH was adjusted to 10 using 0.1 M NaOH, and the mixture was continuously stirred at 70 °C with a stirring speed of 300 rpm for 15 min, followed by heating in a furnace at 450 °C for 2 h. The resulting nanocomposite was washed with ethanol and deionized water, centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the mean values and standard deviations were reported for reproducibility.

Preparation starch/CS solution

A vacuum oven at 80 °C was used to dry starch for 24 h. Mixing of starch in powder form with 35 wt% liquid glycerol created thermoplastic starch. The blend was then placed in a dry environment for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the compound was melted and mixed using an internal mixer (W50EHT, Brabender, Germany) at 110 °C for 15 min at 30 rpm. To acquire the CS solution, 1.5 g of CS powder was dissolved in 20 mL of 70 wt. % acetic acid by stirring for a period of 6 h. Thermoplastic starch (1.5 g) was then added to the CS solution. The completion of the dissolution process was indicated by the solution becoming clear (Starch/CS solution).

Preparation of starch/CS nanocomposite

To investigate the effect of oregano essential oil, 3 mL of it (10 wt%) was poured into a Starch/CS solution. Finally, 0.1 g of the synthesized NPs were added to the Starch/CS solution by stirring for 3 h to obtain Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, Starch/CS/Ag NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs films. The films were compression-molded in a hot press (polystyrene 200 T, Brabender, Germany). The molding procedure included preheating at 160 °C for 5 min, followed by compression for 5 min under a pressure of 50 bar at the same temperature. In the last step, the samples were cooled to 50 °C to obtain films. The dimensions of the films were 4 cm × 1 cm (Fig. 1).

Microstructure morphology

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is one of the most multipurpose instruments available for investigating and analyzing microstructure morphology characterizations. Therefore, the structural properties and morphology of the prepared samples were examined using SEM (mv 2300, Com Scan, USA).

Biodegradability tests

Biodegradability tests were performed by placing the film samples in three common media environments (UV light, water, and soil) to simulate the effects of environmental conditions. To investigate the effect of the water medium on the prepared films, the samples were placed in purified water. Owing to the absence of microorganisms in the water, only the effect of water on biodegradability was studied. All samples were weighed before, immersed in water for one month and weighed after one month. In the next stage, the samples were placed in soil at a thickness of 10 cm. The soil was regularly moistened to provide moisture to rapidly grow microorganisms, and these samples were weighed after one month. In addition, to investigate the effect of sunlight, the samples were exposed to UV light for one month and weighed. Three replicates were performed on all experiments to ensure accuracy, and the mean values and error bars were reported.

Tensile test

The tensile test is one of the tests applied to evaluate the mechanical features of polymers. Mechanical features such as the tensile modulus, tensile strength, and yield stress can be evaluated using this test. Tensile tests were performed using a tensile machine (Zwick/Roell Z010, ASTM D638, Germany). Tensile tests were conducted on the samples at a cross-head speed of 30 mm/min at ambient temperature. Three replicates were performed on all experiments to ensure accuracy, and the mean values and error bars were reported.

Antibacterial and antifungal test

The antibacterial activities of the prepared samples were measured using both Gram-positive (S. aureus) and Gram-negative (E. coli) bacterial strains. The bacterial suspension density was set to the 0.5 McFarland standard of 1.5 × 108 colony forming units [26]. Disk samples (5 mm diameter) were placed on an agar plate with a diameter of 10 cm. The culture media containing the samples were incubated at 37 ºC for a full day and night. The antibacterial activity of the samples was assessed by observing the clear zones formed around them, which indicated the inhibition of bacterial growth.

To study the antifungal properties of the films, the fungal species Aspergillus flavus (A. flavus) was chosen. Potato dextrose agar powder (3.9 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water and sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C and 1.5 bar for 15 min. Once cooled to room temperature, each plate received a mixture of 10 mL culture medium and 0.04 g sample and fungal. The films were incubated at 37 °C under optimal conditions for one week, and measurements were taken daily to track A. flavus growth.

Result

Nanoparticles characterization

The particle size and zeta-potential of the synthesized Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, and Ag-ZnO nanocomposite were measured at 25 ºC using DLS (Nanotrac Wave, Microtrac, USA). The size distributions and zeta-potential of synthesized Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, and Ag-ZnO nanocomposite are also presented in Fig. 2a–c. The measured experimental values for the mean particle size of the synthesized Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, and Ag-ZnO nanocomposite were 20, 52, and 38 nm, respectively. The zeta-potential analysis confirmed that the synthesized Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, and Ag-ZnO nanocomposite possessed a zeta potential of − 25.4, − 18, and − 46 mV, respectively. These results verify the formation of nanometric particles (< 100 nm) with substantial surface charge, indicating electrostatic stability. A higher absolute zeta potential value reflects stronger repulsive forces among particles, which counteract aggregation and lead to enhanced stability. Conversely, lower zeta potential values facilitate agglomeration due to weaker repulsion. The relatively small particle size and narrow distribution observed here suggest that the samples were highly uniform and well-dispersed, yielding a nanocomposite of superior quality. Such stability likely results from effective interaction between nanoparticles during synthesis, preventing particle growth and clustering.

Mean particle size (Left) and zeta-potential (Right) of the synthesized, a Ag NPs, b ZnO NPs, and c Ag-ZnO, and d TEM image of the synthesized Ag-ZnO nanocomposite

Figure 2d presents the transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Philips EM 208S microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 100 kV) image of the synthesized Ag-ZnO nanocomposite. For TEM characterization, approximately 1 mg of the Ag-ZnO nanocomposite was dispersed in 10 mL of absolute ethanol and ultrasonicated for 20 min to obtain a homogeneous suspension. A dilute aliquot of the suspension was then deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid and allowed to dry naturally at room temperature prior to TEM observation. No stabilizing agents or surfactants were used during sample preparation. The relatively well-dispersed morphology observed in the TEM image is attributed to the ultrasonic treatment in ethanol, which effectively separated loosely agglomerated particles before deposition onto the TEM grid. The TEM image reveals nanoparticles with dimensions predominantly below 100 nm, confirming their nanoscale nature. Although some degree of particle aggregation is expected for nanopowders, the applied sample preparation procedure enabled adequate particle dispersion for morphological observation. The relatively small particle size is consistent with the DLS results and suggests that the synthesized Ag-ZnO nanocomposite possesses a high specific surface area, which is advantageous for photocatalytic applications. It should be noted that the TEM analysis primarily provides morphological information, while the successful formation of the Ag-ZnO nanocomposite is supported by the combined results of the complementary characterization techniques presented in this study.

Crystallographic characterization

Figure 3 shows the XRD patterns of starch, chitosan, Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, Starch/CS/Ag NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs. The XRD patterns were used to identify and investigate the crystallinity of the samples. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of starch showed distinct peaks at 20° and 58°, which were of relatively high intensity, indicating that the starch film was semi-crystalline in nature. The XRD pattern of chitosan (CS) was observed at 10.79° and 20.09°, indicating a high degree of crystallinity of chitosan. XRD analysis of the silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) exposed prominent peaks at 2θ values of 38.4°, 44.4°, 64°, and 77° (JCPDS No. 01-087-0719). These characteristic peaks correspond to specific crystallographic planes within the face-centered cubic structure of silver, namely, the (111), (200), (311), and (222) planes [34]. Peaks of the zinc oxide nanoparticle pattern at 2θ = 32.10°, 36.60°, 36.53°, 48.10°, and 57.10° were observed, which was similar to the results of previous studies (JCPDS No. 36-1415), which correspond to the (100), (101), (102), and (110) planes [33].

XRD pattern of Starch, CS, Ag NPs, ZnO NPs, Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, Starch/CS/Ag NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs

Comparing the XRD patterns of the samples with pure raw materials shows that the crystal structure of chitosan has been preserved during the process, but the crystal structure of starch has changed. The XRD pattern of Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, Starch/CS/Ag NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs displays Ag and ZnO NPs peaks. However, there are no distinctive peaks of Ag and ZnO NPs in samples that this could be due to the uneven distribution of nanoparticles at low levels.

In addition, the consequences can be attributed to the low number of nanoparticles that XRD can be identified by XRD. The mean crystallite sizes of the Ag and ZnO NPs were 22 and 53 nm, respectively, based on the peak positions obtained using the Scherrer equation (Eq. 1).

The absence of noticeable noise in the starch/CS/Ag and starch/CS/ZnO patterns suggests that these binary nanocomposites possessed a comparatively more homogeneous microstructure and a more uniform dispersion of the inorganic phase within the biopolymer matrix. In such systems, well-dispersed nanoparticles generally produce cleaner diffraction profiles because the scattering contribution is more consistent across the sample volume, whereas aggregation, compositional fluctuations, or local inhomogeneity can increase background variation and make the signal appear noisier. By contrast, the ternary starch/CS/Ag-ZnO composite contains a more complex multiphase system, where the simultaneous presence of two inorganic components can lead to stronger interfacial heterogeneity, partial agglomeration, and local density variations. These effects may enhance diffuse scattering and baseline fluctuation, which are often perceived as noise in the recorded pattern. Moreover, if the inorganic loading is low or the crystallite domains are very small, the diffracted intensity can be weaker relative to the amorphous background from starch/chitosan, further emphasizing any baseline irregularity. This explains why the binary composites appear smoother, while the ternary composite may show more apparent noise.

According to the results of the Eq. 1, the mean crystallite size of nanoparticles in specific peaks indicates a suitable dispersion of nanoparticles inside the composites. In addition, because of the hydrophilicity of the nanoparticles, they are probably embedded in the hydrophilic Starch/CS film, which does not change the size of the nanoparticle crystallites.

Biodegradability tests

The degradation behavior of the Starch/CS‑based films in UV light, water, and soil environments reflects the interplay between physical–chemical degradation of the polymer matrix and biological activity of microorganisms. In all three environments, the long‑chain polymer structures are broken into shorter segments, which are then susceptible to further fragmentation and eventual consumption by bacteria and fungi. These microorganisms metabolize the oligomeric and low‑molecular‑weight fragments, ultimately converting them into water, carbon dioxide, and biomass, consistent with the accepted pathway of biopolymer biodegradation. The replication of the tests in triplicate under each condition ensures that the observed weight loss trends are statistically representative.

After one month, the weight‑loss results (Fig. 4) reveal that the samples degrade at markedly different rates depending on both the environmental medium and the presence of nanoparticles.

a Weight loss (%) of Starch/CS, Starch/CS/Ag NPs, Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs under UV light, soil and water environment after one month

Under UV irradiation, the starch/CS matrix and chitosan component undergo photo‑oxidative degradation, where chain scission and radical formation lead to embrittlement and partial cross‑linking of the polymer network. This explains why chitosan‑based films become more fragile and brittle after UV exposure: the broken chains re‑organize into a more rigid, cross‑linked network, accompanied by discoloration. The incorporation of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles significantly accelerates this process, as both materials are well‑known photocatalysts. Excitation of ZnO under UV light generates electron–hole pairs that promote oxidation of organic components, while Ag nanoparticles can further enhance the separation of these charge carriers and increase the production of reactive oxygen species. As a result, the highest weight loss was observed for the Starch/CS/Ag–ZnO NPs sample (39.53%), highlighting the pronounced photocatalytic effect of the bimetallic system. In contrast, the neat Starch/CS film exhibited the lowest degradation (20.45%), which is consistent with previous reports showing that low‑level ZnO loading can initially stabilize polymer matrices against UV degradation, as observed by Ammala et al. [38].

In the soil environment, the degradation mechanism shifts from primarily photo‑oxidative to bio‑mediated. The presence of microorganisms plays a dominant role in chain cleavage and consumption of the polymer fragments. Here, the addition of nanoparticles generally reduces the rate of weight loss, with the Starch/CS/Ag–ZnO NPs film showing the lowest degradation. The Ag‑containing sample in particular displays the lowest weight‑loss value (4.87%), which can be attributed to the strong antibacterial activity of silver nanoparticles that suppress microbial growth and activity on the film surface. ZnO nanoparticles also exhibit antimicrobial properties, although to a somewhat lesser extent than Ag, which explains why the ZnO‑containing composite degrades more than the Ag‑containing one but still less than the neat Starch/CS film. The pure Starch/CS blend exhibits the highest degradation in soil (~ 5.76%), since the absence of antimicrobial nanofillers allows microorganisms to interact freely with the matrix and hydrolyze the polymer chains.

In the water environment, the degradation is dominated by hydrolytic and photocatalytic mechanisms. The Starch/CS/ZnO NPs film shows the highest weight loss in water, owing to the hydrophilic and photocatalytic nature of ZnO nanoparticles. The incorporation of ZnO increases the polarity of the composite, enhancing water sorption and facilitating the penetration of water molecules into the starch‑rich regions. This leads to swelling, plasticization, and accelerated hydrolytic degradation of the starch chains. The ZnO nanoparticles also catalyze oxidation reactions at the polymer–water interface, further promoting chain scission and mass loss. As a result, the ZnO‑containing samples degrade more than the Ag‑containing ones, which are less hydrophilic and less catalytically active toward hydrolysis. The Ag‑containing films, although still somewhat degradable, exhibit lower weight loss due to the inertness of metallic silver under purely aqueous conditions and the absence of significant photocatalytic contribution in the dark or under low‑intensity light.

By comparing the three environments, it becomes clear that UV light has the most severe effect on the degradation of the Starch/CS films, followed by the water environment, while soil‑mediated degradation is relatively mild. This ranking is consistent with the idea that photo‑oxidative processes, especially when enhanced by photocatalytic nanoparticles, are more aggressive than purely hydrolytic or bio‑mediated degradation in the short term. The dramatic increase in weight loss for the Ag–ZnO‑containing sample under UV light underscores the dual role of these nanoparticles: they are beneficial for photocatalytic self‑decontamination and UV‑responsive functionality but can also accelerate polymer degradation when the films are exposed to prolonged UV irradiation. For applications in food packaging, this implies that the nanoparticle loading and formulation must be optimized to achieve sufficient UV shielding and antimicrobial performance without compromising the mechanical integrity and long‑term stability of the film.

In summary, the biodegradability tests demonstrate that the environmental context (UV, water, or soil) and the type of nanoparticle (Ag or ZnO) determine the dominant degradation mechanism and the overall rate of weight loss. The Starch/CS/Ag–ZnO NPs film combines the highest photocatalytic activity with the greatest degradation under UV light, while Ag nanoparticles preferentially suppress biotic degradation in soil through their antibacterial effects. These results highlight the need for a balanced design of nanocomposite films, where the benefits of UV‑responsiveness and antimicrobial activity are weighed against the risks of premature structural degradation in the intended application environment.

The apparent acceleration of biodegradation in the presence of Ag‑ZnO and oregano essential oil is indeed counterintuitive at first glance, since these agents are known to inhibit microbial activity. However, in this work was proposed that the observed effect is primarily driven by two non‑mutually exclusive mechanisms:

Photocatalytic/oxidative pathways – ZnO (and to a lesser extent Ag) can act as photocatalysts under UV light, promoting the formation of reactive oxygen species that induce photo‑oxidation of the starch–chitosan matrix. This leads to chain scission, increased hydrophilicity, and a more fragmented structure that is more susceptible to subsequent microbial attack, even if the number of active microbes is reduced near the nanoparticle‑rich regions.

This leads to chain scission, increased hydrophilicity, and a more fragmented structure that is more susceptible to subsequent microbial attack, even if the number of active microbes is reduced near the nanoparticle‑rich regions.

Altered matrix structure and moisture uptake – The addition of nanoparticles and OEO may introduce micro‑voids, phase‑separated domains, or local inhomogeneities that facilitate faster water penetration into the film. Once water penetrates, it promotes swelling, weakens hydrogen bonding, and accelerates hydrolysis of the starch phase, thereby accelerating the overall degradation process even if direct microbial colonization is partially suppressed.

Mechanical properties

A polymer film is suitable for packing when it possesses appropriate mechanical properties. The chemical structure and interactions between different components in the prepared film can play a very important role in creating different properties of the film, particularly its mechanical properties. To examine the effect of nanoparticles on the mechanical properties of nanocomposite films, two parameters were used: tensile strength (the maximum stress that the test film can withstand until it breaks) and elongation at break (rupture is the maximum strain at the breaking point). Tensile properties were obtained from stress–strain curves, where the Young's modulus was obtained from the slope of the linear region of the stress–strain curve. The tensile strength and elongation at break are shown before and after the biodegradability tests in three environments: water, soil, and UV radiation.

Tensile test

Starch is a semi-crystalline polymer that can lead to thermoplastic starch in the presence of glycerol as a plasticizer, which increases the flexibility and elasticity of Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, starch/chitosan/Ag NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs. Glycerol also causes adhesion between starch and the polymer matrix.

Figure 5a shows the tensile strength of the samples. The results showed that the tensile strengths of the prepared films varied from 72.87 to 98.72 MPa. By adding nanoparticles, the tensile strength was increased compared to pure Starch/CS, owing to the weak bond between the hydrophilic starch and chitosan.

a Tensile strength (MPa), b Young modulus (MPa) and c Elongation at break (%) of Starch/CS, Starch/CS/Ag NPs, Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, and Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs under UV light, soil and water environment after one month

The increase in tensile strength observed after incorporation of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles indicates that both fillers acted as reinforcing agents within the starch/chitosan (Starch/CS) matrix. Because starch and chitosan are hydrophilic polymers, the nanoparticles can be preferentially dispersed within the hydrophilic domains of the matrix or localized at the starch–chitosan interface, where they improve interfacial adhesion and stress transfer. In this way, the nanoparticles function as physical bridges between polymer chains and reduce the likelihood of premature failure under applied load. The improved tensile strength is therefore most likely associated with a more homogeneous nanoparticle distribution, stronger interfacial interactions, and possible ionic or coordination interactions between the nanoparticle surfaces and polar functional groups of starch and chitosan, which is consistent with previous reports on nanoparticle-reinforced biopolymer films.mThe higher tensile strength of the Ag-containing film compared with the ZnO-containing film suggests that Ag nanoparticles may have been more effectively dispersed or more strongly integrated into the polymer network. This difference can plausibly be related to the smaller particle size of Ag NPs, which allows them to occupy interstitial spaces more efficiently and interact more uniformly with the matrix. In contrast, the larger ZnO nanoparticles may introduce localized heterogeneity or act as stress concentrators when their dispersion is less uniform. Even so, the ZnO-containing composite still showed higher tensile strength than the nanoparticle-free Starch/CS film, confirming that ZnO contributes to reinforcement overall despite the somewhat lower efficiency compared with Ag NPs [39, 40].

The reduction in tensile strength after one month of exposure to water, soil, and UV radiation reflects environmental degradation of the polymer network. Water likely penetrates the hydrophilic starch/chitosan matrix, weakens hydrogen bonding, and promotes partial swelling and chain mobility, thereby reducing load-bearing capacity. In soil, microbial activity and moisture can gradually disrupt the matrix, leading to structural weakening. However, UV radiation had the most pronounced effect because it can induce photo-oxidative chain scission, embrittlement, surface cracking, and, in nanoparticle-containing samples, photocatalytically accelerated degradation. Under UV exposure, the combined action of polymer breakdown and nanoparticle-driven reactive species formation likely caused the sharpest decline in mechanical performance.

Overall, the tensile test results confirm that nanoparticle addition initially improves the mechanical strength of the films, but prolonged exposure to environmental stressors, especially UV light, compromises these benefits by degrading the polymer–filler network. This behavior is important for food-packaging applications, where the film must maintain sufficient strength during use while still being capable of controlled biodegradation after disposal.

Recent studies have demonstrated that, for optimized starch–chitosan systems, tensile strength can reach the range of 40–88 MPa under suitable formulation and processing conditions, indicating that such high mechanical performance is indeed achievable in advanced biocomposite films [41]. The values reported in this work therefore fall within the upper range of these high-performance systems, highlighting the reinforcing effect of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles and the robustness of the starch–chitosan matrix. This comparison underscores that the present nanocomposite films exhibit mechanical properties comparable to or exceeding those of many conventional synthetic packaging polymers, while maintaining the advantages of biodegradability and sustainability.

Elongation at break

According to the results (Fig. 5b), the addition of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles decreased the elongation at the break point of the samples. The weight percentage of nanoparticles, the way they are dispersed in the polymer matrix, and the type of interaction between nanoparticles and polymer filaments are among the factors that affect the elongation at the break of the films. Hence, incorporating nanoparticles restricts the mobility and elasticity of the network because of the strong ionic interactions formed between the nanoparticles and biopolymer components, resulting in a decrease in the elongation at break [42, 43]. These results were consistent with those reported by St-Pierre et al. [13].

The elongation at the break of the nanocomposites after one month of exposure to different water, soil, and UV light environments demonstrated that elongation decreased with the addition of nanoparticles to the composites. In addition, the created pores increased the destruction and decreased the mechanical properties. In addition, Ag and ZnO nanoparticles are hydrophilic, whereas chitosan is hydrophilic. After the nanocomposites are placed in a humid environment, because water has a tendency toward hydrophilic starch, it enters the starch and chitosan matrix, where water is situated at the interface between the starch matrix and nanoparticles, where it penetrates and dissolves the starch.

In addition, the possible accumulation of nanoparticles in the nanocomposite film can act as stress concentration points and lead to failure of the nanocomposite at low stresses, resulting in a decrease in elongation at break. The results in the relevant figures show that the use of UV radiation has a greater effect and significantly reduces elongation at break.

Young's modulus

Starch alone is fragile, but the addition of a plasticizer to starch increases its flexibility and elasticity. In addition, the plasticizer causes adhesion between the starch and the added nanoparticles, which results in a decrease in the molecular mobility of the matrix polymer chains, thus increasing Young's modulus.

Figure 5c shows the Young's modulus of the samples, which increased with the addition of nanoparticles to the polymer. The accumulation of nanoparticles in some samples can act as stress focal points and lead to the breaking of samples at low stresses. As a result, the addition of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles significantly increased the Young's modulus.

As mentioned earlier, the reason for the increase in tensile strength and Young's modulus is attributed to the uniform distribution of nanoparticles in the polymer structure, the creation of a connecting surface between molecules, and the formation of ionic bonds between the nanoparticles and polymer components. The data obtained from the mechanical and physicochemical properties were consistent. With an increase in the number of nanoparticles, the moisture content of the films decreased. Reducing the moisture content of the films reduced their flexibility and helped reduce their strength [44, 45].

The mechanical properties of the films were investigated after biodegradability testing.

The results demonstrated that the use of UV light has a greater effect on samples compared to the water and soil environments, which greatly increases the Young's modulus. Owing to the damaging effect of UV light, the samples quickly crumbled. Young's modulus, which indicates the hardness of the material, increased sharply in all three environments.

Microstructure morphology

After performing biodegradability tests, SEM samples were prepared to investigate the effects of the water, soil, and UV light environments.

SEM of sample under UV light environment

Figure 6a–d show the surface morphology of the samples after exposure to UV light, revealing that the Ag- and ZnO-containing composite underwent the most severe structural deterioration. This behavior is consistent with the strong photocatalytic activity of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles, which can generate reactive species under UV irradiation and accelerate oxidative degradation of the starch/chitosan matrix. As a result, chain scission, surface erosion, and fragmentation of the polymer network occur more rapidly in the nanoparticle-loaded sample than in the neat film.

SEM of surface of a Starch/CS, b Starch/CS/Ag NPs, c Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, and d Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs under UV light environment for one month

The cracks observed on the surface of the Ag/ZnO-containing film are most likely a consequence of UV-induced embrittlement and shrinkage of the degraded matrix. Once the polymer chains are partially broken, internal stresses accumulate and are released through crack formation, which eventually leads to visible fracture and sample breakage. In addition, the spherical starch domains appear to be disrupted within the polymer matrix, indicating that the UV environment not only affected the outer surface but also promoted deterioration of the internal microstructure. This supports the idea that the starch-rich regions are particularly susceptible to photo-oxidative damage. The SEM observations are in good agreement with the weight-loss results, which showed that the Ag/ZnO sample exhibited the highest degradation under UV exposure. The associated loss of structural continuity also explains the marked decrease in mechanical performance, since cracks, pores, and fragmented domains reduce stress transfer efficiency across the film and weaken the integrity of the polymer network. For food-packaging applications, this result is important because the film must preserve sufficient mechanical strength during use; therefore, although the photocatalytic nanoparticles provide UV-responsive functionality, excessive UV-induced degradation can compromise practical performance. Overall, the SEM results confirm that UV light acts as a strong degradation trigger for these biocomposite films, and that the photocatalytic contribution of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles intensifies this effect. This highlights the need to balance UV activity with structural stability when designing starch/chitosan-based packaging materials.

Water environment

Figure 7a–d present the SEM micrographs of the samples after one month of immersion in water. In contrast, the samples exhibit clearer signs of surface erosion and matrix disruption, confirming that starch is the more water-sensitive component of the composite.

SEM of surface of a Starch/CS, b Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, c Starch/CS/Ag NPs, and d Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs in water environment for one month

The greater degradation observed in the ZnO-containing samples compared with the Ag-containing samples can be explained by the hydrophilic and photocatalytic nature of ZnO nanoparticles. ZnO increases the polarity of the matrix, which facilitates water uptake and promotes swelling of the polymer network. Once water penetrates the film, it weakens hydrogen bonding between starch and chitosan, disrupts interfacial cohesion, and accelerates hydrolysis of the starch phase. In addition, ZnO may catalyze interfacial oxidation processes, further contributing to the deterioration of the surface morphology. These SEM observations are consistent with the weight-loss results, which also showed greater degradation in the ZnO-containing films. The visible removal of starch from the polymer matrix suggests that water preferentially attacked the starch-rich regions, leaving behind a more porous and weakened structure. This loss of matrix integrity would naturally reduce stress transfer efficiency and account for the decline in mechanical properties after immersion. In the Ag and ZnO dual-nanoparticle sample, the micrograph shows dispersed particles embedded within the polymer matrix, indicating that the nanoparticles remained visible after water exposure but that the surrounding matrix had undergone partial erosion. This suggests that the nanoparticles were more resistant to dissolution than the biopolymer phase, and their distribution across the surface reflects the heterogeneous nature of the degraded film. Overall, the SEM results demonstrate that water primarily affects the starch-rich regions of the film, while ZnO intensifies degradation by increasing hydrophilicity and promoting hydrolytic breakdown.

Soil environment

Figure 8a–d show the SEM micrographs of the samples after one month in the soil environment, revealing clear differences in surface morphology depending on nanoparticle type and composite composition. In general, the soil-exposed samples underwent less severe deterioration than those exposed to UV light, because degradation in soil is governed mainly by microbial activity and moisture-assisted hydrolysis rather than by direct photo-oxidation. The presence of Ag and ZnO nanoparticles altered this process by influencing both the antimicrobial response of the film and the integrity of the starch/chitosan matrix.

SEM of surface of a Starch/CS, b Starch/CS/ZnO NPs, c Starch/CS/Ag NPs, and d Starch/CS/Ag-ZnO NPs in soil environment for one month

The Ag-containing sample appears to be relatively more stable, which can be attributed to the strong antibacterial activity of silver nanoparticles. By inhibiting microbial proliferation at the film surface, Ag reduces enzymatic attack on the biopolymer matrix and slows the breakdown of starch-rich domains. The observation that Ag nanoparticles were well dispersed in the matrix and within starch granules suggests that they were effectively embedded in the polymer network, which may help preserve the surface structure during soil exposure. This improved dispersion also supports better interfacial adhesion, limiting the formation of large voids and severe structural collapse.

In the ZnO-containing sample, however, the SEM images show more cavities and surface discontinuities, indicating greater loss of starch particles and a higher degree of matrix erosion. This behavior can be explained by the combined effects of moisture penetration, partial swelling, and microbial attack on the starch phase. ZnO nanoparticles possess antimicrobial activity as well, but their hydrophilic character can also facilitate water uptake into the film. As moisture penetrates the matrix, the weak interfacial interaction between starch and chitosan becomes more vulnerable, allowing starch-rich regions to be extracted or degraded more easily. The resulting voids and cavities are consistent with polymer pull-out and local disintegration of the matrix.

In the dual-nanoparticle Ag/ZnO sample, the degradation appears more heterogeneous. The combination of two different nanoparticles can create regions of uneven dispersion and local compositional mismatch, which may allow water to penetrate more readily into the nanocomposite structure. Because Ag and ZnO differ in size, surface chemistry, and interaction strength with the polymer matrix, their combined presence may reduce structural uniformity and increase the likelihood of localized swelling and disruption. As a result, moisture can more easily reach starch and chitosan domains, accelerating partial dissolution and increasing the formation of pores and cavities. This explains why the Ag/ZnO composite may show more pronounced morphological change than the individual nanoparticle-containing films under soil conditions.

Overall, the SEM observations are in agreement with the weight-loss results, confirming that soil exposure causes moderate degradation of the films through a combination of microbial attack, moisture uptake, and interfacial weakening. The cavity formation and starch pull-out seen in the micrographs are direct evidence of matrix erosion and help explain the corresponding reduction in mechanical properties. These findings suggest that while Ag and ZnO nanoparticles can provide antimicrobial protection, their long-term behavior in soil is strongly influenced by dispersion quality, interfacial compatibility, and the hydrophilicity of the surrounding starch/chitosan matrix.

Conclusion

The incorporation of oregano essential oil and Ag-ZnO nanoparticles into starch-chitosan composites significantly enhanced antimicrobial and antifungal properties, demonstrating effective dispersion of nanoparticles within the polymer matrix and at interfacial regions. The addition of Ag-ZnO nanoparticles improved tensile strength and Young's modulus due to their reinforcing effect, while reducing elongation at break, indicating increased rigidity of the composite films. Biodegradability was accelerated by the photocatalytic properties of the metal oxide nanoparticles, facilitating faster environmental degradation under UV exposure. These results confirm that the synergistic combination of biopolymers, essential oils, and metal nanoparticles produces active packaging materials with enhanced mechanical strength, superior antimicrobial activity, and improved biodegradation characteristics suitable for sustainable food packaging applications. It should be noted that the present study did not evaluate the migration of silver (Ag) and zinc (Zn) ions into food simulants. For any application in food packaging, future work should include migration testing in appropriate food simulants to ensure that Ag and Zn release remains within regulatory limits before the material can be considered safe for real‑world use.
