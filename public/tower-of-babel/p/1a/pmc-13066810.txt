Transferability of aboveground biomass estimation using Sentinel-1/2 and GEDI data in subtropical forests of complex terrain, China
Authors: Guoqing Wang, Wenquan Dong, Huaiqing Zhang, Yongjie Ji, Wangfei Zhang, Han Zhao, Mengjin Wang, Yuqiao Ma, Ci Song, Ying Jiang, Chenfu Bai, Jing Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAccurate forest aboveground biomass (AGB) estimation across heterogeneous subtropical regions is essential for carbon accounting and climate change mitigation. We developed XGBoost and random forest models using GEDI L4A Lidar samples and multi-source remote sensing features (Sentinel-1/2, topography) to predict AGB in Xijiang Forest Farm (Guangdong) and transferred them to Simao District (Yunnan). XGBoost demonstrated superior performance and transferability, with parameter fine-tuning effectively adapting the source-domain model to the target region (R2 = 0.48) using only 20% of target samples, while full retraining achieved R2 = 0.52. SHAP analysis identified spectral indices (SIPI and SAVI) and SAR backscatter (VH) as key predictors. Monte Carlo uncertainty decomposition revealed GEDI measurement error accounts for 36%–39% of total predictive uncertainty. This transfer learning framework enables cost-effective AGB mapping in data-limited regions, supporting regional carbon monitoring and forest management.

Introduction

Forest aboveground biomass (AGB) accounts for more than 80% of global terrestrial vegetation biomass and serves as a crucial metric for evaluating carbon storage potential and ecosystem services.1,2 Accurate AGB estimation is critical for understanding forest ecosystem structure and function, assessing carbon cycle processes, and formulating effective strategies for forest resource management and climate change mitigation.3 Remote sensing is indispensable for achieving efficient, accurate, and spatially continuous forest AGB assessment, as traditional ground-based surveys are constrained by limited spatial coverage, high labor intensity, and substantial costs over large areas. However, developing large-scale, generalizable, and robust forest AGB models depends critically on the availability of global, high-precision, and openly accessible datasets.

The Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI), a space-borne full-waveform LiDAR system, collects high-resolution data on forest vertical structure at a global scale.4,5 GEDI L4A forest AGB products are derived by establishing regression relationships between relative height (RH) metrics from GEDI L2A waveform data and ground sample plots.6 GEDI L4A data have demonstrated high accuracy in estimating forest AGB, particularly in subtropical regions with complex terrain.7,8 Nevertheless, considerable uncertainty exists regarding the accuracy of GEDI L4A products in China, primarily due to insufficient regional validation and the inherent limitations of single-source remote sensing variables in fully capturing three-dimensional forest structure and stand heterogeneity.9,10 To enhance the scientific rigor and reliability of regional AGB estimation, we can incorporate the European Space Agency’s Climate Change Initiative (CCI) global AGB product as an independent reference dataset for model comparison and validation, while using GEDI L4A as the primary benchmark.11

Despite extensive research on forest AGB estimation using multi-source remote sensing data fusion and machine learning methods, the cross-regional transferability of machine learning models remains a major challenge for large-scale forest AGB mapping.12 Machine learning models with high accuracy developed in specific regions are often difficult to transfer directly to areas with distinct ecological conditions, as model generalization is highly susceptible to ecological heterogeneity, including variations in forest type, species composition, topographic relief, and climatic conditions.13 Investigating model transferability across different ecological zones is scientifically significant for revealing the ecological mechanisms underlying model degradation under environmental heterogeneity, particularly how terrain complexity and forest structural variations affect feature response stability, and for establishing a theoretical framework for cross-regional AGB prediction that can guide data-scarce regions in forest carbon monitoring.14,15 Such investigations enable quantitative assessment of model generalization boundaries and provide mechanistic insights into the interactions between remote sensing signals and ecological gradients. Therefore, generalization capability is critical for model construction under different transfer learning frameworks. With rapid advances in machine learning algorithms, data-driven forest AGB estimation models have significantly improved generalization capability through algorithmic structure optimization.16,17 Recent studies indicate that introducing transfer learning frameworks—such as domain adaptation or parameter fine-tuning—can effectively mitigate model degradation caused by inter-regional ecological heterogeneity, thereby adapting to AGB estimation needs across different forest types.18,19,20

In recent years, several researchers have explored transfer learning approaches for forest AGB estimation modeling. For example, studies by Huang et al., Stojanova et al., and Xu et al. have achieved varying degrees of accuracy improvement across different ecological regions through model adaptation, parameter adjustment, or domain adaptation strategies. These efforts demonstrate the potential of transfer learning to mitigate the impact of ecological heterogeneity on model performance and enhance regional generalizability of AGB estimation models. However, most existing studies have focused primarily on individual method applicability, while systematic comparisons and assessments of different transfer learning frameworks—particularly in subtropical regions with complex terrain—remain limited.21 Therefore, comprehensive comparative analyses of multiple transfer learning strategies using multi-source remote sensing data in typical subtropical forests are urgently needed to promote broader application of AGB models across larger spatial scales and diverse ecological conditions.

Although previous studies have made substantial progress by integrating various remote sensing data sources and machine learning algorithms for forest AGB estimation transferability, significant challenges remain in addressing pronounced ecological differences and strong topographic heterogeneity across subtropical regions of China under different transfer learning frameworks.12,22 Moreover, while current research predominantly emphasizes prediction accuracy improvements, quantitative uncertainty assessment remains underexplored.23 Forest AGB estimation uncertainty stems from multiple sources, including remote sensing measurement errors—such as GEDI’s systematic geolocation error of approximately 10 m and atmospheric correction biases—and algorithmic prediction errors.24 In cross-regional transfer learning scenarios, covariate shifts can amplify these uncertainties; yet, systematic uncertainty decomposition is rarely conducted, obscuring the relative importance of data quality enhancement versus algorithmic refinement. Consequently, establishing a rigorous uncertainty propagation and decomposition framework across multiple spatial scales to partition GEDI measurement uncertainty from model prediction uncertainty is essential for evaluating transfer learning reliability and prioritizing targeted interventions in forest AGB estimation.25

This study focuses on two representative subtropical forest regions with complex terrain in southern China: the Xijiang region in Guangdong Province and the Simao region in Yunnan Province. We use the GEDI L4A product as the target dependent variable and integrate multi-source remote sensing variables—including Sentinel-1 backscattering, Sentinel-2 spectral features, and topographic factors (digital elevation model [DEM])—to construct representative machine learning models, including random forest (RF) and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). We assess three transfer strategies—direct transfer, fine-tuned adaptation, and retraining—for cross-regional application and analyze key factors influencing model transferability across regions. We aim to investigate: (1) the accuracy and stability of GEDI L4A products in subtropical regions of China; (2) the role and influence of complex terrain on subtropical forest AGB estimation using multi-source remote sensing data; and (3) the transferability of machine learning algorithms under different strategies and the contribution of key features to model transfer. Building on these investigations, we establish an uncertainty propagation and decomposition framework to systematically quantify the respective contributions of GEDI measurement errors and model prediction errors, providing quantitative evidence for enhancing cross-regional transfer model reliability. This research aims to provide both theoretical foundations and practical approaches for cross-regional forest AGB estimation using multimodal remote sensing data, thereby promoting broader application in forest carbon stock monitoring, ecosystem assessment, and scientific forest management.

Research region and information sources

Overview of the field of study

China spans five major climatic zones—tropical, subtropical, warm temperate, temperate, and cold temperate—within the East Asian monsoon region. This broad climatic gradient, together with complex topography, supports a wide range of forest types, including tropical rainforests, coniferous forests, mixed conifer-broadleaf forests, deciduous broadleaf forests, and evergreen broadleaf forests. Among these, the subtropical zone is particularly extensive and heterogeneous, exhibiting strong topographic relief, pronounced climatic gradients, and highly diverse vegetation and ecosystem patterns. Data acquisition and preprocessing

Acquisition and preprocessing of GEDI data

In this study, GEDI L4A, a footprint-based AGB product, was used as the primary sample data for model training, validation, and cross-regional transferability analysis. GEDI is the first spaceborne Lidar mission deployed on the International Space Station, specifically designed to capture high-resolution three-dimensional information on global forest vertical structure. By emitting laser pulses, GEDI provides key forest structural parameters, including canopy height, ground elevation, and canopy cover, and the essential support for forest ecosystem monitoring and global climate change research.4 GEDI L4A data were obtained from the NASA Earth data Archive (https://search.earthdata.nasa.gov/search). The L4A product estimates forest structural parameters by processing GEDI footprint-level laser echo data and integrating global field plot measurements through regression modeling. With high spatial resolution (25 m × 25 m) and strong regional representativeness, it enables the detection of subtle variations in AGB at the stand scale,4,28 making it particularly suitable for carbon stock assessments across diverse forest types.

To ensure the accuracy of forest AGB estimates, this study conducted rigorous quality filtering of GEDI laser footprint points, based on criteria: quality flag (agbd_quality_flag = 1) for high-quality data, degradation flag (degrade_flag < 1) to ensure data integrity, and sensitivity (sensitivity > 0.95) to guarantee data reliability.29,30 After data filtering, 163 valid footprint points were obtained from Xijiang region in Guangdong Province (January 2023 to March 2023), and 2,392 valid footprint points were obtained from Simao region, Puer City, Yunnan Province (April 2020 to April 2021). To quantitatively assess the measurement uncertainty of GEDI data, this study leveraged the upper (AGBD_PI_UP) and lower (AGBD_PI_LO) bounds of the prediction intervals provided in the GEDI L4A product. Assuming that GEDI measurement errors follow a normal distribution, the approximate standard deviation (AGBD_std) was calculated based on the 95% confidence interval using the following formula:(Equation 1)AGBD_std=AGBD_PI_UP−AGBD_PI_LO3.92

The divisor 3.92 represents twice the critical value of the standard normal distribution at the 97.5th percentile (i.e., 2 × 1.96 = 3.92), which corresponds to the width of the 95% confidence interval in units of standard deviation. This approximation assumes that the prediction intervals provided by GEDI accurately reflect the 95% confidence bounds of the biomass estimates.31

To facilitate comparison across different biomass levels, the relative standard deviation (AGBD_rel_std) was calculated as the coefficient of variation:(Equation 2)AGBD_rel_std=AGBD_stdAGBDwhere AGBD denotes the point estimate of AGB for each footprint. The relative standard deviation provides a normalized measure of uncertainty that is independent of biomass magnitude, enabling more robust comparison of data quality across varying forest conditions.

These uncertainty metrics serve dual purposes in the subsequent analysis (Figures 3A and 3B). First, they provide a quantitative foundation for assessing the reliability of GEDI observations and identifying regional variations in data quality. Second, they are incorporated into the machine learning models either as additional predictor variables or as inverse-variance weights during model training, thereby allowing the models to adaptively account for heterogeneous data quality and potentially improve prediction accuracy by down-weighting less reliable observations.32Figure 3Boxplot distributions of relative standard deviation (AGB_rel_std) and absolute standard deviation (AGB_std) of GEDI L4A AGB products in the two study areas(A) Boxplot and probability density distribution of AGB_rel_std and AGB_std derived from GEDI L4A products for Xijiang Forest Farm and Simao District before filtering.(B) Boxplot and probability density distribution of AGB_rel_std and AGB_std derived from GEDI L4A products for Xijiang Forest Farm and Simao District after filtering. CCI AGB data acquisition and preprocessing

The European Space Agency Climate Change Initiative (ESA CCI) AGB Product v6.0 (2022) is currently one of the most widely used global datasets for forest AGB derived from remote sensing. This product integrates multi-source synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data from Sentinel-1 C-band SAR, Envisat ASAR, and ALOS-1/2 L-band SAR, employs the BIOMASAR-C and BIOMASAR-L algorithms for forest biomass inversion, and combines GEDI and ICESat-2 Lidar data for regression calibration, effectively enhancing the accuracy and consistency of AGB estimates for various forest types worldwide. CCI AGB v6.0 covers the entire globe with a spatial resolution of 100 m, providing multi-temporal AGB distribution products for the years 2007, 2010, 2015 to 2022, and includes uncertainty (standard deviation) information for each pixel. The relevant data can be obtained from the ESA website (https://climate.esa.int/en/projects/biomass/data/), supporting Geo TIFF and Net CDF formats. In this study, the CCI AGB v6.0 (2022) product was processed through reprojection, clipping, and resampling, and used as an independent reference dataset for regional comparison and consistency analysis with the forest AGB results predicted by GEDI L4A, thereby enhancing the reliability and scientific validity of local AGB estimation results.33

Sentinel-1 data acquisition and preprocessing

Sentinel-1 C-band dual-polarization (VV and VH) SAR data were used as remote sensing feature variables for model construction. All data were level-1 ground range detected (GRD) products provided by the European Space Agency (https://dataspace.copernicus.eu/explore-data) and accessed via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. The data processing strictly followed the official Sentinel-1 GRD workflow,34,35 including orbit correction, radiometric calibration, thermal and edge noise removal, and terrain correction (orthorectification), resulting in σ° backscatter coefficient (dB) products at 10 m spatial resolution.

This study utilized interferometric wide swath (IW) mode imagery, selecting VV and VH polarization images. For the Xijiang region, imagery acquired in January 2024 was used; for Simao region, imagery from June 2020 was used. In both cases, a single scene was selected per study area. To ensure spatial alignment with the GEDI L4A AGB product (25 m resolution), all Sentinel-1 imagery and auxiliary features were resampled to 25 m.

Sentinel-2 data acquisition and preprocessing

Multispectral features data were derived from the European Space Agency’s Sentinel-2 Level-2A (L2A) products, which are publicly available and processed through the GEE platform. L2A products provide atmospherically corrected and orthorectified surface reflectance, with spatial resolutions of 10, 20, and 60 m in the WGS 1984 coordinate system. To ensure data quality, images with less than 20% overall cloud cover were selected, and cloud masking was performed using the QA60 band. For the Xijiang region, imagery from January 2024 was used; for Simao region, imagery from June 2020 was selected, with one high-quality image obtained per region.

All multispectral bands and derived features (including vegetation indices, texture features, and tasseled cap transformation components) were resampled to 25 m resolution using bicubic interpolation to ensure consistency with the GEDI L4A laser footprint data.36,37

SRTM DEM data acquisition and preprocessing

The Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTMGL1_003) DEM with a spatial resolution of approximately 30 m was used to derive terrain variables in this study. DEM data were obtained via the GEE platform and clipped to the boundaries of each study area. To ensure spatial alignment and resolution consistency among the DEM, remote sensing imagery, and GEDI L4A products, all raster data were reprojected to the WGS 84/UTM coordinate system and resampled to 25 m resolution before features stacking.38

Topographic variables—including elevation, slope, aspect, and hillshade were extracted from the resampled DEM and incorporated as auxiliary variables in regression model training to improve AGB estimation accuracy. Throughout the data processing, SRTMGL1 DEM data were synchronized with the corresponding Sentinel-1/2 imagery to maintain spatial consistency.

Research methods

In this study, the AGB provided by the GEDI L4A product was used as the target variable for the model, while remote sensing features extracted from multi-source remote sensing data, including Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, and DEM, were used as input variables for the machine learning model in the Xijiang region. The model was trained and validated using a 5-fold cross-validation method, with GEDI L4A sample data split into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio. During the model construction process, a Monte Carlo simulation method (with 50 iterations) was employed to quantify uncertainty, combined with the prediction intervals (AGBD_PI_UP and AGBD_PI_LO) from the GEDI L4A product. Grid search was used to optimize hyperparameters and obtain optimal model configurations, which were further validated using ground survey plot data Figure 5.Figure 5Technology roadmap

Technology roadmap

After identifying the model exhibiting the highest accuracy and robustness, we established a generalized modeling framework under different strategies, including direct transfer, fine-tuning, and retraining, to estimate AGB in typical subtropical forests of the Simao region with complex terrain. In the target region, all available GEDI L4A samples were first randomly partitioned into two disjoint subsets: 20% of the samples were reserved as an independent test set shared by all three transfer strategies, and the remaining 80% were used for model adaptation. For the fine-tuning strategy, this 80% subset was further split into training and validation sets at an 8:2 ratio (i.e., 64% and 16% of all target samples), and the source-domain model was updated using only the target-region training subset. For the retraining strategy, a new model was trained from scratch on the same 80% pool, again with an internal 8:2 split into training and validation. The choice of a 20% hold-out fraction follows the commonly used 80/20 split in machine learning and provides a compromise between maintaining a sufficiently large, independent test set for a stable evaluation of transfer performance and retaining enough samples for effective adaptation in the target region.

Remote sensing data features information

Remote sensing features selection

High-dimensional and highly redundant remote sensing features can easily lead to model overfitting and noise amplification, thereby weakening the generalization ability and accuracy of forest AGB estimation models. Therefore, careful selection of key features is crucial for improving model performance.

We adopted a two-stage feature selection strategy combining Pearson correlation analysis and recursive feature elimination (RFE).41,42 First, Pearson correlation analysis is used to preliminarily filter variables that show at least a moderate linear correlation with AGB (e.g., |R| > 0.2), while weakly correlated variables are excluded. Then, the retained variables are passed to RFE, which integrates model training with recursive assessment of feature importance to further optimize the feature subset. Together, these two methods create an effective synergy between preliminary screening and in-depth optimization, enhancing the scientific rigor of the feature selection process and improving model accuracy, thereby providing a solid foundation for high-precision AGB estimation.43,44,45

Pearson correlation coefficient

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to conduct a preliminary analysis of the linear correlation between multi-source remote sensing features and AGB.46 The Pearson correlation coefficient is a statistical measure of the strength of the linear relationship between two variables, calculated as follows:(Equation 3)R=∑i=1n(Xi−X¯)(Yi−Y¯)∑i=1n(Xi−X¯)2∑i=1n(Yi−Y¯)2where R is the Pearson correlation coefficient, ranging from [−1,1]; Xi and Yi represent the observed values of variables X and Y for the i the sample; and are the mean values of variables X and Y, respectively; n is the number of samples.

Recursive features elimination

RFE is a features selection method based on model weights. The basic idea is as follows: at first, train the model using all features, evaluate the importance of each features, and recursively eliminate the least important features until the predefined number of features is reached.47 Through this process, the feature variables that contribute most to AGB estimation are retained. Additionally, this study employed the SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) method for quantitative analysis of feature importance, further explaining the contributions of different features to the prediction results of the AGB estimation model. The SHAP method calculates the marginal contribution of each feature to the prediction results, providing a more detailed and transparent explanation for the feature selection process.48

Forest AGB estimation model construction

RF regression model

RF is a model based on the idea of ensemble learning. It constructs multiple decision trees through bootstrap sampling, with each tree randomly selecting some features when splitting nodes, thereby reducing model variance and avoiding overfitting. In regression tasks, RF aggregates the prediction values from all trees and takes the average as the final result, making it widely used in forest AGB estimation.49

XGBoost regression model

XGBoost is an efficient gradient boosting tree (GBDT) model that iteratively optimizes the prediction error of each tree to improve overall prediction accuracy. Building on traditional GBDT, XGBoost incorporates optimization strategies such as regularization, pruning, and parallel computing, offering higher computational efficiency and generalization performance. It is widely applied in complex regression tasks such as AGB estimation.50 The objective function of XGBoost consists of two parts: the loss function and the regularization term. The specific expression is:(Equation 4)L(θ)=∑i=1Nl(yi,yiˆ)+∑K=1KΩ(fk)l(yiyiˆ) is the loss function, yi is the true value, and yiˆ is the predicted value. Ω(fk) is the regularization term, which controls model complexity and prevents overfitting.

Transfer framework

This study was conducted with the Xijiang Forest Farm in Guangdong Province as the source domain and Simao District, Pu’er City, Yunnan Province as the target domain. It aims to evaluate the transferability of models across geographical regions within a typical subtropical forest zone. After being trained in the source domain, the models were applied to the target domain using three strategies: direct transfer, fine-tuning, and full retraining, to systematically evaluate their generalization capabilities. During the direct transfer stage, both RF and XGBoost models were evaluated for their performance in the target region. The model with superior generalization ability (XGBoost, in this case) was selected for subsequent fine-tuning and full retraining, ensuring the model selection was data-driven and scientifically justified.51

To comprehensively evaluate the generalization ability of AGB estimation models based on remote sensing features across these different regions, a multi-strategy transfer learning experimental system was constructed. The specific procedure is as follows.

Transfer experiment workflow

According to mainstream practices in transfer learning for remote sensing, this study adopts three classical transfer strategies—direct transfer, fine-tuning, and full retraining—to construct the experimental framework. Given the limited GEDI L4A sample sizes (163 footprints in Xijiang and 2,392 in Simao), we did not introduce more sophisticated domain-adaptation frameworks, but instead focused on these three strategies with XGBoost to provide a clear and interpretable baseline for cross-regional AGB estimation.52,53

Direct transfer

Here, the best source-trained model (RF or XGBoost) is directly applied to the target region without any parameter update to assess zero-shot generalization under regional heterogeneity. This strategy implicitly assumes that the feature distributions and the underlying feature AGB relationship are sufficiently similar between the source and target regions so that the learned mapping f(x) can still be reasonably applied in the target domain. To characterize the source-target discrepancy, we additionally compute the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) between the feature distributions of the two regions.54(Equation 5)MMD2(Ds,Dt)=‖1ns∑i=1nsϕ(xi(s))−1nt∑j=1ntϕ(xj(t))‖H2where Ds and Dt denote the data distributions of the source and target domains, ϕ is the feature mapping, and H is the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS).

Fine-tuning

Building upon direct transfer, we assume that the model trained on the source region has already learned the main features of the target region. Fine-tuning is achieved by incrementally updating the model on the target region’s data. Mathematically, fine-tuning minimizes the loss function on the target region’s data while applying regularization to prevent overfitting to the target data samples.55 A common approach is to apply L2 regularization to constrain the excessive changes in the model’s weights. Specifically, 20% of labeled samples from the target region are randomly selected for incremental training or fine-tuning of the pretrained model, which helps enhance the model’s adaptability to the target region. The remaining 80% of the target region’s samples are reserved as an independent test set.56(Equation 6)minθ1nt∑i=1ntl(yi(t),f(xi(t);θ))+λ‖θ−θs‖22where f(·θ) denotes the prediction function (e.g., XGBoost or RF) parameterized by θ, l(·) is the loss function (e.g., squared error for regression), nt is the number of labeled target samples, θs are the fixed parameters learned from the source region, and λ controls the strength of regularization (i.e., the trade-off between fitting target data and staying close to the source model).

Retraining

Full retraining relies entirely on the target region’s data for end-to-end training. This strategy assumes that the target region has sufficient samples and can provide enough information to allow the model to learn from scratch and adapt to the feature distribution of the target region. Mathematically, the model is optimized by minimizing the empirical risk function on the target region’s data.57,58 The model is retrained from scratch using all available target region samples, while maintaining consistency in feature engineering and modeling pipeline as in the source region, with independent hyperparameter optimization.59(Equation 7)minθ1nt∑i=1ntl(yi(t),f(xi(t);θ))+Ω(θ)where Ω(θ) is the regularization term used to control the complexity of the model and prevent overfitting.

Transferability feasibility criterion

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test is a nonparametric method commonly used to determine whether there is a significant difference between the distributions of two independent samples. This test is based on the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and calculates the maximum absolute distance (D statistic) between the empirical CDFs (ECDFs) of the two samples, defined as:(Equation 8)D=sup|F1(x)−F2(x)|where F1(x) and F2(x) represent the ECDFs of the two samples, respectively.

In this study, the K-S test was used to assess the distributional consistency of GEDI L4A-derived AGB between the source region (Xijiang) and the target region (Simao). The test result (p > 0.05) indicated no significant difference between the two distributions, providing a theoretical basis for subsequent transfer learning experiments.60,61

Uncertainty analysis and Monte Carlo simulation

To quantify the uncertainty in model predictions, this study employs the Monte Carlo simulation method. Multiple training subsets were constructed using bootstrap resampling, and random perturbations were introduced for each sample based on the prediction intervals provided by the GEDI data (AGBD_PI_UP and AGBD_PI_LO). Specifically, in each iteration, the AGB value of the training sample is perturbed by a normal disturbance (Equation 1), thereby propagating observational uncertainty into the model training process. By conducting 50 independent iterations, a prediction distribution for each test sample is obtained, from which the mean (used as the final prediction), standard deviation (representing overall uncertainty), and the 95% confidence interval are derived.62 Based on variance decomposition principles, the sources of uncertainty are quantified. The total predictive variance (σ2total) is assumed to consist of two independent components: the GEDI measurement variance (σ2GEDI) and the model prediction variance (σ2model), i.e.,(Equation 9)σtotal2=σGEDI2+σmodel2where σ2_GEDI is calculated from the GEDI prediction interval. The total uncertainty (σ_total) is represented by the standard deviation of the 50 Monte Carlo simulation results. The model prediction uncertainty is then obtained through residual decomposition.(Equation 10)σmodel=σtotal2+σGEDI2

This method follows the uncertainty propagation theory,63,64 assuming that GEDI measurement errors and model prediction errors are independent. Through this analysis, the relative contributions of GEDI data quality and model generalization ability to prediction reliability under different modeling strategies can be quantitatively assessed.65

Model validation metrics

To quantify the estimation performance of the model in the source area and the target area, three metrics are used: R2, RMSE, and MAE. The closer the R2 value is to 1, the better the model fits; the smaller the RMSE and MAE, the higher the prediction accuracy. In this study, the GEDI L4A forest AGB data, filtered by quality, was used as the target variable, and 20 selected remote sensing features were used as independent variables to construct two machine learning models, RF and XGBoost, with the Xijiang Forest Farm as the study area. Model adaptability optimization and transfer results

Based on the performance of the two models described above, we selected the XGBoost model for subsequent optimization experiments. To mitigate the performance degradation observed when directly transferring the XGBoost model to the Puer region, we systematically tuned the learning rate (learning_rate) parameter to enhance model adaptability. Discussion

Applicability and limitations of GEDI L4A products in forest AGB estimation in complex terrain areas

This study area, comprising the Xijiang region and Simao region, is a typical subtropical complex terrain region. GEDI L4A AGB was used as the target variable for modeling by integrating multi-spectral remote sensing features (EVI and NDVI) with terrain factors (slope, aspect), achieving a maximum estimation accuracy of R2 = 0.68. The results of the related study demonstrate the modeling capability of the GEDI L4A AGB product in typical climatic regions and complex terrain in China. Dubayah et al. (2020) reported that the GEDI L4A product achieves AGB estimation accuracy (R2) of 0.5–0.7 in complex terrain areas with high forest cover, with particularly outstanding performance in subtropical evergreen broadleaf forests. Duncanson et al. (2022)further emphasized its superior performance in humid subtropical forests.6 The above scholars’ studies are more consistent with our study. The high estimation accuracy of this model is primarily attributed to the continuous representation of canopy information provided by Sentinel imagery and the effective characterization of spatial heterogeneity by terrain features. Additionally, the integration of multi-source data compensates for the limitations of single-source data in complex terrain. As pointed out by Hao Tang et al. (2021) in the GEDI L2B Algorithm Theoretical Basis Document (ATBD),67 the spatial heterogeneity caused by complex terrain and diverse canopy structure can systematically interfere with the integrity and stability of Lidar signals, thereby significantly affecting the accuracy of forest AGB estimation through remote sensing Dubayah et al.,4 Duncanson et al. (2022), and Hancock et al. (2019) further confirm that terrain undulations and canopy heterogeneity distort LiDAR waveform signals and discontinuities in features spatial distribution, which are the primary causes of increased local prediction errors in AGB models.6,11 The empirical results of this study in typical complex terrain areas such as the Xijiang and Simao regions indicate that, although the overall model achieves relatively good AGB estimation performance, in patches with steep slope changes or highly heterogeneous canopy structures, the stability of remote sensing features decreases, prediction residuals significantly increase, and local systematic or random biases exist. This phenomenon is highly consistent with previous studies, indicating that complex terrain and canopy heterogeneity are key challenges for improving the accuracy of forest AGB estimation via remote sensing. Additionally, multiple studies have shown that different remote sensing products exhibit significant differences in AGB estimation capability in complex terrain and high canopy heterogeneity regions. Hunka et al. (2023) noted that LiDAR products (such as GEDI L4A) exhibit significantly superior capability in capturing forest vertical structure and spatial variability in complex terrain and high biomass regions of tropical areas compared to optical remote sensing products (such as CCI AGB), and achieve higher consistency with ground survey data.11

Duncanson et al. (2022) and Dong, W. et al. (2023) emphasized that, although optical remote sensing data have advantages for large-scale monitoring and historical time-series analysis, they often exhibit significant errors in spatial patterns and absolute values in areas with rugged terrain and complex canopy structures, making it difficult to accurately characterize the spatial heterogeneity of forest AGB.6,13 The results of this study further confirm the above views. In the Xijiang region, the AGB predicted by the XGBoost model was highly consistent with the measured data (R2 = 0.66, RMSE and MAE = 30.22 and 28.75 Mg/ha,respectively), significantly outperforming the RF model. When compared with the CCI AGB product at the pixel level, the model results also demonstrated higher spatial consistency (R2 = 0.57, RMSE and MAE = 34.24 and 31.72 Mg/ha, respectively). After model transfer and optimization in the Simao region, the model also demonstrated high accuracy and good adaptability to CCI AGB, further proving the advantages of GEDI L4A and machine learning methods for AGB estimation in complex terrain areas and their regional generalization potential.

In summary, this study not only validated the advantages of LiDAR remote sensing products in complex terrain areas but also revealed that machine learning models based on GEDI L4A can more effectively capture the spatial variability and absolute levels of forest biomass compared to optical remote sensing products, significantly improving the accuracy and reliability of AGB estimation. In the future, efforts should be made to further promote the integrated application of multi-source data, such as LiDAR and optical remote sensing, to address the limitations of single data sources and improve the stability and adaptability of AGB inversion in complex environments.

Comparison of machine learning model robustness and transferability

We used RF and XGBoost as representative machine learning algorithms to estimate subtropical forest AGB and constructed a transferability framework based on the XGBoost model. However, although machine learning models for forest AGB estimation typically perform well within a single ecological region, they often experience substantial performance degradation when applied across different ecological zones, highlighting model robustness and generalization as core challenges in remote sensing-based AGB estimation.68,69 While traditional views often attribute this transfer failure primarily to feature distribution differences between source and target areas (distribution shift), recent studies suggest that a lack of systematic modeling of forest structural characteristics and spectral response mechanisms may be a more fundamental bottleneck.70 Consistent with this view, our ECDF-based K-S comparisons of GEDI-derived and field-measured AGB (Figure 13) showed no significant differences in AGB distributions between Xijiang and Simao, suggesting that the observed transfer degradation cannot simply be attributed to differences in AGB magnitude but is more likely linked to changes in forest structure and feature-AGB relationships. This study validates the rationality of this perspective. When the XGBoost model trained in the Xijiang region was directly transferred to the Simao region, model performance decreased significantly (R2 = 0.22, RMSE = 46.25 Mg/ha, MAE = 44.24 Mg/ha). This degradation magnitude aligns with the cross-regional error characteristics reported by Duncanson et al. (2022), confirming the prevalence of transfer failure. Beyond superficial feature distribution differences, ecological heterogeneity between the two regions in terms of forest stand composition, canopy structure, and terrain features led to spatial inconsistencies in the intrinsic relationship between remote sensing features and AGB, thereby inducing significant “relational uncertainty.” To improve model performance, we employed a parameter fine-tuning strategy, which increased the model R2 to 0.48 (RMSE = 36.21 Mg/ha, MAE = 34.56 Mg/ha), representing an approximately 20% error reduction. This improvement is consistent with the 15%–30% performance gain observed in fine-tuning experiments in temperate coniferous forests by Zhang, J.l. (2023),23 indicating that fine-tuning exhibits certain adaptability in structurally homogeneous regions. However, in the highly heterogeneous landscape studied here, the local prediction error of the fine-tuned model still fluctuated by over 20%, suggesting this method struggles to fundamentally overcome the intrinsic uncertainty arising from differences in ecological mechanisms.

Further expansion of the target area samples and model retraining improved R2 to 0.52 (RMSE = 31.21 Mg/ha, MAE = 29.56 Mg/ha), yet the accuracy improvement remained limited. This phenomenon implies that beyond the model’s own learning capability, other constraining factors exist. Particularly noteworthy is that inherent errors in the remote sensing data source itself may constitute a fundamental constraint on model accuracy. Through Monte Carlo simulation, we quantified the contribution of GEDI measurement uncertainty in the cross-domain transfer scenario to be approximately 25.99 Mg/ha, accounting for 39.4% of the total prediction uncertainty. This finding corroborates the conclusion of Roy et al. (2021) that GEDI geolocation errors can significantly impact AGB estimation in complex forest areas, and also supports the view of Wang, Chang et al. (2025) regarding the necessity to clearly distinguish between algorithm-induced errors and data source errors.71,72

Overall, the challenges in cross-regional AGB estimation stem not only from model uncertainties caused by feature distribution differences and ecological mechanism variations but are also significantly influenced by the inherent errors of the remote sensing data itself. However, it should be noted that our Monte Carlo experiment propagated only GEDI-related uncertainties, whereas predictor-side errors (e.g., Sentinel-1/2 processing, DEM derivatives, and temporal mismatches) were not fully modeled; thus, the reported GEDI share is conditional and likely conservative. Future research should concurrently focus on mechanism-guided model development and the enhancement of data quality, and further establish a full-chain uncertainty propagation scheme to effectively advance the capabilities of forest carbon stock estimation from regional to global scales, the challenges in cross-regional AGB estimation stem not only from model uncertainties caused by feature distribution differences and ecological mechanism variations but are also significantly influenced by the inherent errors of the remote sensing data itself.73 Future research should concurrently focus on mechanism-guided model development and the enhancement of data quality to effectively advance the capabilities of forest carbon stock estimation from regional to global scales.

Correlation and transferability of remote sensing characteristics of forest AGB in subtropical regions

This section focuses on the response variations of remote sensing features during regional transfer, and employs the SHAP importance analysis to assess their correlation and transferability. Forest AGB estimation models often suffer from degraded generalization due to feature response inconsistency across ecological domains. Understanding the mechanisms of feature transferability across ecological gradients is crucial for advancing the theoretical foundation of remote sensing-based AGB estimation. By quantifying how terrain heterogeneity and forest structural complexity modulate feature importance during model transfer, this study contributes to the broader scientific understanding of model generalization in heterogeneous landscapes.74,75 Furthermore, identifying stable feature sets that maintain predictive power across regions provides practical guidance for developing robust, scalable AGB monitoring systems applicable to diverse subtropical forest ecosystems.27,76 Tuia et al. (2016) noted that terrain heterogeneity and canopy structure complexity are the core mechanisms leading to remote sensing features drift and reduced model generalization capability.74 In this study, we systematically validated this phenomenon using the SHAP importance dynamic analysis system. Before transfer, spectral features such as SAVI, VARI, and VH from the source region (Xijiang) contributed significantly, but after transfer to the target region (Simao), the importance of these features generally decreased by over 50%, while the importance of terrain-structure features such as SIPI, elevation, and aspect significantly increased, reflecting a terrain-dominated transfer adaptability trend. These findings corroborate those of Zhang et al. (2023), who observed that model features weights vary significantly with terrain and structural heterogeneity, and that single spectral features alone are insufficient to support robust estimations in complex mountainous forest ecosystems.75 Additionally, Li et al. (2022) further pointed out that under the dominance of terrain and structural heterogeneity, texture features and terrain features maintain greater stability and transferability across different regions.76 The findings of our study not only validate the theoretical perspectives of the aforementioned literature but also provide data support and mechanism explanations for enhancing the model’s cross-regional generalization capability. Previous studies have shown that in complex mountainous forest areas, traditional spectral features often undergo features drift during remote sensing model transfer due to heterogeneity in lighting, terrain, and canopy structure, leading to a decline in model generalization capability. Further emphasized that the geometric effects of terrain directionality and canopy scattering play a dominant role in model transfer in complex terrain areas, and that a single spectral index is insufficient to adapt to variable ecological conditions. Li et al. (2022) also found that texture features and terrain features exhibit higher stability and cross-regional adaptability in complex region transfer modeling.62 The SHAP importance analysis in this study further validates the above viewpoints.77 In the complex mountainous forest area of Simao, terrain-structural features such as SIPI and slope aspect are far more important than spectral indices like NDVI, confirming the terrain- and structure-dominated transfer adaptation mechanism. Additionally, we observed that some high-contribution spectral features from the source area become ineffective in the target area, necessitating dynamic reconfiguration of feature combinations to adapt to environmental heterogeneity. This process-based evidence aligns closely with the aforementioned theoretical framework. Furthermore, texture features such as B5_Contrast and B6_Ent maintain stable importance before and after transfer and exhibit increased SHAP values in the target area, further demonstrating the robustness of texture features in complex topography and canopy structure heterogeneous environments.

This study addresses cross-regional remote sensing-based estimation of forest AGB in subtropical forests with complex terrain. Using GEDI LiDAR as the reference and integrating multi-source remote sensing predictors, we designed and evaluated three transfer frameworks—direct transfer, fine-tuning, and retraining—across the Xijiang (source) and Simao (target) regions. Limitations of the study

Several limitations motivate future work. First, temporal mismatch between GEDI acquisitions and field inventories may introduce additional uncertainty. Second, transferability is constrained by the representativeness of GEDI footprints in the target domain and residual domain shift not fully captured by the current feature set. Third, the Monte Carlo framework assumes approximately normal errors and independence among uncertainty components; future work should test more flexible error models and consider spatial autocorrelation. We recommend: (1) expanding multi-temporal feature design to reduce sensitivity to acquisition-date and seasonal effects; (2) integrating stronger structural constraints and improved terrain correction to better handle complex topography; and (3) adopting interpretable domain-adaptation strategies that explicitly align feature distributions between regions beyond fine-tuning.

Method details

Study area

The study was conducted in two representative subtropical forest regions in China to evaluate cross-region transferability of remote-sensing-based AGB models under strong gradients in climate, topography, and vegetation composition. The Xijiang region is located in the southwestern part of Zhaoqing City, Guangdong Province (22°18′–23°15′N, 112°04′–112°50′E), characterized by a warm and humid climate (mean annual temperature ∼22 °C; annual precipitation ∼1,500–2,000 mm), an elevation range of ∼100–800 m, and mixed subtropical forests (plantations and natural/secondary stands) with typical canopy heights of ∼15–25 m dominated by communities such as Pinus massoniana, Schima superba, and Castanopsis spp.26,27 The Simao region is located in the eastern part of Pu’er City, Yunnan Province (22°02′–23°05′N, 100°31′–101°34′E), featuring a subtropical monsoon climate with a distinct rainy season (annual precipitation ∼1,200–2,800 mm, mainly May–October) and an elevation range of ∼500–2,000 m, where major forest types include subtropical evergreen broad-leaved forests, tropical seasonal rainforests, and plantation/economic forests with representative species such as Quercus glaucoides, Dalbergia fusca, and Pinus kesiya. All geospatial datasets were processed in a consistent WGS84/UTM projection to facilitate spatial alignment across sensors and regions.39,40

Overall workflow and modeling grid

This study used GEDI L4A footprint-level aboveground biomass density (AGBD) as the response variable. Multi-source remote sensing predictors derived from Sentinel-1 SAR, Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery, and DEM terrain variables were used to build machine-learning models in the Xijiang region and to evaluate cross-region transferability to the Simao region. All geospatial layers were processed in a consistent WGS84/UTM projection and aligned to a 25 m modeling grid prior to feature stacking and sample extraction.

GEDI L4A data filtering and uncertainty metrics

GEDI L4A footprint AGBD samples were used for model training, validation, and transfer experiments. Footprints were filtered using strict quality criteria (agbd_quality_flag = 1, degrade_flag <1, and sensitivity >0.95). After filtering, 163 valid footprints were retained for the Xijiang region (January 2023–March 2023) and 2,392 valid footprints for the Simao region (April 2020–April 2021). To quantify GEDI observational uncertainty, the upper and lower bounds of the GEDI prediction interval (AGBD_PI_UP and AGBD_PI_LO) were used to derive uncertainty metrics (AGBD_std and AGBD_rel_std) based on the assumption of normally distributed errors and a 95% interval. These uncertainty metrics were used to characterize data quality and support subsequent uncertainty propagation analysis.4,28

Sentinel-1 SAR data and preprocessing

Sentinel-1 C-band dual-polarization (VV and VH) Level-1 GRD data were accessed via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) collection COPERNICUS/S1_GRD. Preprocessing followed the standard Sentinel-1 GRD workflow, including orbit correction, radiometric calibration, thermal noise removal, border/edge noise removal, and terrain correction (orthorectification), producing σ° backscatter (dB) layers at 10 m spatial resolution. IW mode imagery with VV and VH polarizations was used.31,32 For the Xijiang region, imagery acquired in January 2024 was used; for the Simao region, imagery acquired in June 2020 was used. In each region, one representative high-quality scene was selected. Sentinel-1 backscatter layers were resampled from 10 m to the 25 m modeling grid using bilinear interpolation before feature stacking.34,35

Sentinel-2 multispectral data and feature generation

Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance data were accessed from the GEE collection COPERNICUS/S2_SR. Scenes with <20% cloud cover were selected, and cloud masking was performed using the QA60 band. For the Xijiang region, imagery acquired in January 2024 was used; for the Simao region, imagery acquired in June 2020 was used, with one high-quality image selected per region. To standardize predictor generation, all Sentinel-2 spectral bands (B2–B12) were first harmonized to a 20 m processing grid. Vegetation indices and tasseled cap transformation components were computed on this 20 m grid. Texture features were computed for each Sentinel-2 band (B2–B12) using a 5 × 5 moving window based on the built-in GEE GLCM texture implementation, generating a suite of GLCM-based metrics (ASM, CONTRAST, CORR, DENT, DISS, DVAR, ENT, IDM, IMCORR1, IMCORR2, INERTIA, MAXCORR, PROM, SENT, SHADE, and VAR). Finally, all Sentinel-2-derived predictors were resampled from 20 m to the 25 m modeling grid using bicubic interpolation to match the GEDI footprint support.36,37

DEM and terrain variables

Topographic predictors were derived from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission DEM accessed via the GEE collection USGS/SRTMGL1_003 (∼30 m). The DEM was clipped to each study area, reprojected to WGS84/UTM, and resampled to the 25 m modeling grid using bilinear interpolation. Terrain variables including elevation, slope, aspect, and hillshade were derived from the resampled DEM and used as auxiliary predictors for AGB modeling.38

Feature stacking and GEDI footprint extraction

All predictors (Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2-derived features, and DEM terrain variables) were stacked on the common 25 m modeling grid. For each GEDI footprint, predictor values were extracted using a center-pixel sampling strategy, i.e., using the value of the 25 m pixel whose center contains the footprint location, ensuring consistent spatial support between predictors and the GEDI response variable.46,47,48

Field plot validation and AGB calculation

AGB was quantified using an integrated field–remote sensing framework combining GEDI L4A footprint AGB and independent field plot measurements. Field surveys established 102 plots in Xijiang (January 2024; 20 m × 20 m and 25 m × 25 m) and 96 plots in Simao (December 2020; 20 m × 20 m). Plot-level AGB was computed using species-specific allometric equations based on diameter at breast height (DBH) and tree height (H), with parameters from the Guangdong Province Forest Resources Survey Constant Table (and Chen Yongfu et al. for Melaleuca quinquenervia) for Xijiang and from Xu Hui et al. for Simao (Tables 1 and 2), and then aggregated within plots and converted to t/ha using the measured plot area. For field validation, plot AGB was compared against co-located model predictions extracted at the plot center on the 25 m modeling grid using the same masking rules for missing predictors.39,40

Quantification and statistical analysis

Software and implementation

All preprocessing and feature generation were implemented in Google Earth Engine (GEE). Model training, feature selection, uncertainty analysis, and statistical evaluations were implemented in Python using standard scientific computing and machine-learning libraries (NumPy, pandas, scikit-learn, XGBoost, and SHAP). Unless otherwise stated, the same preprocessing and modeling pipeline was applied consistently in both study regions.

Model training, feature selection, and transfer strategies

In the Xijiang region, GEDI samples were split into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio, and model performance was assessed using 5-fold cross-validation. Feature selection employed a two-stage strategy: (i) Pearson correlation analysis (|R| > 0.2), and (ii) Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE). Grid search was used to optimize hyperparameters. SHAP analysis interpreted predictor contributions. To evaluate transferability, target-region (Simao) GEDI samples were split into a 20% independent test set and an 80% adaptation pool. Three strategies were tested:

Direct transfer

Applying the source-trained model without updates.

Fine-tuning

Updating the source model using the adaptation pool (split 8:2 for training/validation).

Retraining

Training a new model from scratch on the adaptation pool.

Uncertainty propagation via Monte Carlo simulation

Predictive uncertainty was quantified using a Monte Carlo simulation framework with 50 iterations. In each iteration, GEDI observational uncertainty (derived from AGBD_PI_UP and AGBD_PI_LO) was propagated by perturbing training AGB values. The ensemble mean and standard deviation represented the final estimate and predictive uncertainty, respectively. Variance decomposition separated GEDI measurement variance from model prediction variance.

Statistical tests

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test was used to assess whether GEDI-derived AGB distributions differed significantly between the source and target regions. Statistical significance was evaluated at α = 0.05 (i.e., p > 0.05 indicates no significant difference). Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) was computed to quantify feature distribution discrepancies. For CCI consistency analysis, 25 m predicted AGB was aggregated to 100 m using mean aggregation, and agreement was quantified using correlation and error statistics.
