Impact of fecal microbiota transplantation on lipid parameters in patients with metabolic syndrome: a meta-analysis
Authors: Leyi Wang, Zhihao Liu, Fangfang Fan, Long Zhang, Jianping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic syndrome (MetS) is a global health challenge, with impaired lipid metabolism as a key feature. While fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) shows promise as a MetS therapy, existing meta-analyses have reported conflicting results and focused mainly on glycemic parameters, leaving its impact on lipid metabolism largely unexplored. This meta-analysis systematically assessed the influence of allogenic FMT on lipid parameters in patients with MetS, unraveling its potential as an innovative therapeutic modality for this population.MethodsA meta-analysis was performed to explore the impact of allogenic FMT on lipid parameters (triglycerides, total cholesterol [TCHO], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C] and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]) in patients with MetS. Terms regarding FMT and MetS were searched in PubMed/Medline, EMBASE, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Scopus from the inception of the databases until 31 August 2023.ResultsNine randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included and subgroup analyses according to follow-up durations were performed. Background

The first description of metabolic syndrome (MetS) traces back to nearly one hundred years ago [1], and in recent decades, the prevalence of MetS has increased significantly [2]. MetS has been highly associated with cardiometabolic complications and increased cardiovascular risk [3], and impaired lipid metabolism has been a crucial diagnostic criteria for MetS.

As the association between gut microbiota dysbiosis and the development of obesity and related metabolic disorders have been put forward in recent years, treatments targeting gut microbiota, including probiotics, prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) have been brought up to alter metabolic disorders [4]. Of these, FMT has been reported by only a small number of studies on its efficacy and safety. However, with growing interest in gut microbiota, studies exploring the application of FMT have recently been emerging.

Fecal microbiota transplantation involves the transfer of stool from a donor to a recipient with the aim of directly normalizing the gut microbiota composition in the recipient and thereby alleviating diseases [5]. FMT has been reported to have therapeutic effects on recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI), auto-immune diseases (e.g., ulcerative colitis, type 1 diabetes mellitus) [6], neurological diseases [7], and graft versus host disease (GvHD) [8], but has only been strongly recommended by clinical guidelines on the treatment of multiple rCDI refractory to antibiotic therapy [9, 10]. Recent studies propose that FMT may have therapeutic effects on MetS, but original studies or systematic reviews focus mainly on glycemic parameters, with conflicting results obtained regarding lipid parameters, leaving the impact of FMT on serum lipid parameters in patients with MetS unclear.

This systematic review explores the impact of allogenic FMT on serum lipid parameters in patients with MetS, unraveling its potential as an innovative therapeutic modality for this population.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

A meta-analysis was performed to explore the impact of FMT on lipid parameters in patients with MetS. The search was conducted in PubMed/Medline, EMBASE, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Scopus using the keywords ‘fecal microbiota transplantation’ and ‘metabolic syndrome’ in MeSH terms with no restrictions on language or study type. Publications from the inception of the databases until 31 August 2023 were screened, and bibliographies of review articles and meta-analyses, trial registries were also checked to identify possible additional studies as well as ongoing trials. The detailed search strategy is outlined in Supplementary Table S1 [see Additional file]. The protocol of this study is registered in the PROSPERO international database (ID CRD42023389890), and this study complies with the Cochrane Collaboration published guidelines and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [11].

The inclusion criteria involved: (1) randomized controlled trial (RCT); (2) included patients with metabolic disorders and have been diagnosed with MetS or obesity; (3) patients received FMT as a treatment modality. The exclusion criteria included: (1) publications not clearly reporting serum lipid parameters before and after FMT; (2) RCTs still ongoing; (3) meeting abstracts or literature with no full text available; (4) not published in English. Two investigators (L.W. and Z.L.) independently went through the process of searching, study selection and data extraction, strictly following the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions or adjudicated by a third investigator (J.L.). Characteristics of the studies were tabulated (Table 1), and eventual agreement was reached through thorough evaluation and discussion with team members with expertise in clinical medicine, statistics, and epidemiology.

Data analysis

Data were independently extracted from eligible articles by two investigators (L.W. and Z.L.) and then checked by one experienced supervisor (J.L.). Endnote 20.6 software was used for deduplicating and categorizing the publications. The following clinical information was extracted from each study and tabulated: the first author, year of publication, study population, the kind of disease of included participants, number of patients (experimental and control group), mean age, intervention regarding experimental and control group, lengths of follow-up, route of fecal microbiota administration, fecal donor information, number of FMT performed for each patient and adverse events reported.

Two investigators (L.W. and F.F.) evaluated the risk of bias of included RCTs independently and disagreements were resolved through discussion. The ROB 1.0 tool was adopted to evaluate the selection bias, performance bias, detection bias, attrition bias, reporting bias, and other biases of included studies.

Analysis of changes in triglycerides (TG) and cholesterol (including total cholesterol [TCHO], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]) levels after FMT were conducted, and subgroup analyses were performed by categorizing extracted data into allogenic and autologous FMT groups, as well as into different follow-up times (2 weeks, 4 to 6 weeks, 12 weeks or ≥ 24 weeks).

Pooled meta-analysis was conducted using the RevMan (Review Manager) software [21], and mean difference (MD) was pooled using a fixed effects model with 95% confidence interval (CI). The inverse variance method was used, and the presence of heterogeneity was assessed using Q statistic (Chi-square test), meanwhile the I2 statistic was used to assess the degree of heterogeneity among trials. All P values were from two-sided tests and results were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Funnel plots were employed to assess the potential publication bias.

Effect of FMT on serum TG level

Data concerning serum TG was extracted and pooled. As shown in Fig. 2a, we observed a significant decrease in TG level with an overall mean difference (MD) of −0.15 (95% CI: −0.29, −0.01), suggesting an effective alteration of TG level with allogenic FMT. The result remained significant in the subgroup with a follow-up of 4 to 6 weeks (MD −0.16 [95%CI: −0.32, −0.01]). Effect of FMT on cholesterol levels

Data reporting changes in cholesterol parameters (TCHO, HDL-C and LDL-C) were pooled and analyzed respectively, and the results were shown in Fig. 2 b-d. All 9 studies were included in the analyses of cholesterol parameters. No significant alteration was observed regarding TCHO level (MD −0.10 [95% CI −0.27, 0.07]) and LDL-C level (MD −0.02 [95% CI −0.17, 0.13]) after allogenic FMT compared with control group, and the results remained consistent regardless of follow-up lengths, as revealed in subgroup analyses. Nevertheless, an overall significantly increased HDL-C level was observed (MD 0.07 [95% CI 0.02, 0.12]), and the result remained significant in the subgroup with a follow-up of 4 to 6 weeks (MD 0.08 [95%CI: 0.02, 0.15]). Funnel plots [see Supplementary Fig. 2 (b)-(d) in Additional file] indicated acceptable levels of publication bias (with no significant asymmetry). This suggests that the likelihood of missing studies due to selective reporting is minimal, supporting the robustness of our findings.

Safety of FMT in patients with MetS

Adverse events (AEs) are documented in Table 1 and common moderate AEs included diarrhea, bloating and abdominal pain. No severe AEs related to FMT were reported, suggesting the safety of FMT acceptable after years of development.

Discussion

In this meta-analysis, we observed a reduction in TG level and an elevation in HDL-C level in patients receiving allogenic FMT versus control group (receiving placebo or autologous FMT). This suggests that gut microbiota from lean, healthy donors may display a beneficial effect on modulating lipid metabolism in patients with MetS. In subgroup analyses, allogenic FMT led to a significantly decreased TG as well as increased HDL-C during 4 to 6 weeks after the first FMT, indicating a potential optimal timeframe for FMT efficacy manifestation and offering insights into FMT interval considerations. Notably, moderate AEs (diarrhea, bloating, abdominal discomfort) were transient and resolved within a week, and no FMT-related severe AEs were reported, reinforcing its favorable safety profile for metabolic applications.

The intricate relationship between gut microbiota, lipid metabolism and obesity has been vigorously debated in the past decade. Extensive research efforts have been dedicated to exploring this relationship, unveiling mechanisms targeting various facets of lipid metabolism and elucidating pathways involving diverse microbial metabolites [22, 23]. Methods proposed to foster a healthier gut microbiota environment include the use of prebiotics, probiotics, FMT and herbal medicine, however, comprehensive studies assessing the efficacy and safety of FMT remained scarce. While animal studies investigating the therapeutic potential of FMT in mice with metabolic disorders have reported promising modulatory effects [24–29], the safety and efficacy of FMT on patients with MetS remains unclear.

Previous meta-analyses exploring the potential therapeutic effect of FMT on MetS focused mainly on glycemic parameters, and the most recent relevant meta-analyses that took lipid parameters into consideration revealed conflicting results [30, 31]. A meta-analysis conducted by Li et al. [30] explored the effect of FMT on multiple metabolic parameters in patients with MetS, however, only 6 publications were included in the analysis of cholesterol and 7 included for TG analysis, and the result turned out that all parameters (TG, TCHO, HDL-C and LDL-C) displayed significant reductions in allogenic FMT group compared with placebo group. Contrary to the conclusion drawn by Li et al., Qiu et al. reported a significant increase in both HDL-C and LDL-C levels in a recent meta-analysis comparing FMT with placebo group with follow-up less than 6 weeks [31]. Earlier systematic reviews and meta-analyses were to a large extent limited by the small number of publications included [32–34]. The discrepancies may stem from differences in study inclusion criteria, variabilities in control groups (placebo vs. autologous FMT) etc. Moreover, we contributed new insights by conducting a more focused analysis on lipid parameters in MetS patients, identifying 4–6 weeks as a critical efficacy window.

Our study compensated for the shortcomings of previous meta-analyses in several aspects. This meta-analysis included recent studies related to FMT and MetS and focused on the impact of FMT on lipid parameters, which have largely been overlooked by researchers. Moreover, since FMT is a relatively novel treatment modality that can be performed through various procedures, we included only RCTs in this meta-analysis to better eliminate the bias between studies, thus leading to a more solid and convincing conclusion. Further, a detailed subgroup analysis explored the different therapeutic effects obtained on lipid parameters at different follow-up periods. Additionally, a more rigorous inclusion and exclusion criteria was adopted in this study, as compared with Li et al. and Qiu et al., the study conducted by Witjes et al. [35], Allegretti et al. [36], Su et al. [37] and Leong et al. [38] were excluded because of the target population did not include patients with MetS. Mocanu et al. [39] conducted a double-blind, randomized trial on FMT and fiber supplementation in severe obesity and MetS, assessing metabolic benefits via insulin sensitivity. Due to a lack of raw data concerning lipid parameters, the study was excluded from our analysis, though its overall findings concerning metabolic improvements may align with our study.

The composition of gut microbiota is associated with both genetic and environmental factors, leading to different dominant strains and balance of microbiota for patients with MetS or obesity versus lean, healthy individuals. Correspondingly, allogenic FMT from lean, healthy donors had a significantly better performance on modulating lipid parameters compared to autologous FMT, apparently by inducing significantly more beneficial changes in intestinal microbiota composition and structure [13, 14, 18, 19, 37].

The relationship between gut microbiota and MetS has been linked to multiple aspects of lipid handling (absorption, oxidation, storage), adipose tissue distribution, adipogenesis, and gut–brain signaling. Several convergent pathways may explain the lipid changes observed after allogenic FMT. First, donor microbiota may shift microbial metabolites that influence host lipid metabolism, particularly bile acid signaling and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production [39]. SCFAs can act through G-protein–coupled receptors (e.g., GPR41/FFAR3 and GPR43/FFAR2) expressed in intestinal epithelial and immune cells and adipocytes, thereby affecting host energy and lipid homeostasis [40]. In parallel, microbiota–bile acid crosstalk may modulate diet-related dyslipidemia via bile acid signaling, including farnesoid X receptor (FXR) pathways in the liver and intestine [41]. Consistent with these mechanisms, enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa (including butyrate producers) has been reported following allogenic FMT in MetS-related trials [12, 18, 20]. Second, improved gut barrier function and reduced endotoxin-driven inflammation may indirectly improve insulin sensitivity and hepatic VLDL production, contributing to lower TG; donor-derived strains associated with immune regulation or reduced lipopolysaccharide (LPS) production (e.g., Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum and Bacteroides dorei) have been described after allogenic FMT [37, 42, 43]. Third, microbiota changes may also influence adipose distribution and gut–brain signaling [44, 45]. Trials have reported reductions in microbiota patterns linked to triglyceride production/de novo lipogenesis and improvements in visceral fat distribution metrics after allogenic FMT [13, 38], as well as appetite-related hormonal and neuroactive-microbe signals (e.g., decreased ghrelin at 3 months, and changes in Lactobacillus brevis as a γ-aminobutyric acid producer) [13, 19, 46]. Importantly, mechanistic inference remains limited because bowel preparation and delivery routes may influence donor microbiota engraftment, and microbiome/metabolomic endpoints were not standardized across trials [13].

From a clinical perspective, the time-dependent pattern has two practical implications. First, 4–6 weeks may be a pragmatic time point for lipid reassessment in future trials and in protocolized monitoring, particularly in MetS patients with atherogenic dyslipidemia (elevated TG or low HDL-C). Second, translation of FMT into metabolic practice (if supported by future evidence) would require rigorous donor screening and standardized delivery protocols, as heterogeneity in donor factors, formulation, bowel preparation, and administration routes may influence engraftment, efficacy, and safety.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity remains a major concern, arising from variability in donor selection and microbiota profiles, delivery methods (capsules, duodenal tube, colonoscopy), dosing frequency, bowel preparation, control groups, and follow-up duration (2–24 weeks), as well as differences in participant characteristics (age, sex, BMI, diabetes severity, baseline lipids, and comorbidities). Patient-level data were unavailable, preventing stratified analyses by baseline lipid phenotype or obesity distribution. In addition, most trials had small sample sizes and limited follow-up (≤ 24 weeks), leaving the durability of lipid effects uncertain. Finally, lipid parameters were secondary outcomes in most included trials, underscoring the need for adequately powered RCTs that prespecify lipid endpoints and incorporate harmonized microbiome/metabolomic measurements to identify responders and optimize dosing intervals. Ongoing registered studies may provide further evidence and help refine future trial design, including a completed-recruitment randomized controlled study in obese women with MetS (RBR-2fm8j9), a double-blind placebo-controlled protocol with 12-month follow-up in severe obesity (NCT03273855), and a trial evaluating whether lean-donor FMT combined with lifestyle changes improves insulin resistance beyond lifestyle changes alone (NCT02050607), which together highlight the need for adequately powered, long-term RCTs with prespecified lipid endpoints.

Conclusions

In conclusion, an overall lowered TG level and increased HDL-C level was observed in patients with MetS receiving allogenic FMT, and a potential optimal timeframe for FMT efficacy manifestation might be 4 to 6 weeks after first administration. The results indicated that gut microbiota from lean, healthy donors may display a beneficial effect on modulating lipid metabolism in patients with MetS thus could serve as a viable intervention. Future large-scale RCTs are required for further validation.
