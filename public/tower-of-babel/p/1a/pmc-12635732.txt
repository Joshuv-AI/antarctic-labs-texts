What is innovation?
Authors: Hamid Ghanbari
Published: 2025

In 1878, Thomas Edison stood in his Menlo Park laboratory, holding a thin carbonized bamboo filament between his fingers. For months, he had tested thousands of materials—platinum, cotton thread, and even human hair—searching for the perfect conductor for his electric light. The bamboo came from a fan he had absently dismantled during one of his marathon work sessions. This was not just an invention; this was innovation in action—the relentless process of turning an idea into something people would actually use.1

Edison’s light bulb highlights a key distinction. It shows us the difference between invention and innovation, a distinction that often gets lost in our modern rush to celebrate every new gadget as revolutionary. Invention is the spark—the creation of something new. Innovation is the long, grinding work of making that creation useful, affordable, and widely adopted. Edison did not just invent a light bulb; he built an entire electrical system to make it practical.1

Ideas live in the mind until invention gives them physical form. Every leap in human progress—from the first stone ax to the latest quantum computer—falls into 1 of 4 categories: tools, which extend human capability; machines, which transform 1 type of motion or energy into another; materials, which provide new substances with novel properties; and methods, which systematize how we make things. Each invention reshapes not just our physical world but how we live within it. The stone ax changed not only what early humans could cut but also how they hunted, built, and organized their societies. Today’s smartphones—simultaneously a tool, machine, and method—have similarly rewritten our social patterns, work habits, and daily routines. What begins as an invention becomes, through use and adaptation, the infrastructure of human experience.2

Innovation, at its heart, is less about sudden breakthroughs and more about persistent observation and experimentation. It is the process of turning new ideas into widely used and adopted practices. It is not only Edison testing thousands of filaments but also the countless others who refined his work, developed new applications, and eventually transformed artificial light from a luxury into something so ubiquitous we only notice it when it is absent. That is the true mark of successful innovation—not just creating something new but making it indispensable.

Innovation can be thought of as applied energy—the force that turns improbable ideas into everyday realities. It is the process that transformed Edison’s carbonized bamboo into the light bulb in your ceiling and then into the light-emitting diode screen you might be reading these words on now. Each step along that path required not just technical insight but the practical work of making new technologies affordable, reliable, and worth adopting.

The hardest part is not having ideas—it is recognizing which ones have potential and knowing how to nurture them into reality. This requires a systematic approach to spotting change and opportunity. The most successful innovations often come not from random inspiration, but from methodical observation of where systems are straining, where needs are unmet, and where small improvements could cascade into larger transformations.

Innovation is better described as a process to “create value from ideas.” In the broadest terms, innovation is the process of turning new ideas into widely used and adopted practices. Innovation follows a deceptively simple formula: innovation = idea × value. The multiplication sign matters. Without either idea or value, there is no innovation. A brilliant idea that creates no value remains just that—an idea. Value without originality is mere efficiency.

The resources that drive innovation are surprisingly constant across history. Like a chef working with basic ingredients—heat, protein, fat, and acid—innovators work with fundamental economic elements: capital (the resources we save rather than consume), physical materials (from copper wires to computer chips), labor, management, and, perhaps most crucially, time. What changes is how we combine these elements and to what end.3

This way of thinking about innovation makes it clear that, regardless of the nature of the desired change (radical technical innovation or incremental process improvement), bringing about change is at the heart of innovation. The entrepreneur stands at this intersection, moving resources from areas of lower productivity to higher ones.

However, innovation is not limited to commerce or technology. A clinician developing a new way to teach surgical skills, a nurse pioneering a more effective patient communication method, and a social worker creating a more dignified food distribution system—they are all innovators. They search for change, respond to it, and transform it into opportunity. The most successful among them do not wait for lightning strikes of inspiration. Instead, they develop systems to consistently search for areas of change, knowing that is where opportunities often hide.
