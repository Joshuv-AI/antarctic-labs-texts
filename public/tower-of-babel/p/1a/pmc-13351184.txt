A metabolic amplification strategy for spherical nucleic acid immunogenicity driven by simvastatin–CpG codelivery
Authors: Lulu Zhang, Lin Yu, Jiaxi Zhu, Yuan Ding, Zheng Wang, Ruihong Yu, Lina Jiao, Zhimin Zhang, Yan Ma, Jinli Shi, Zhenguang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) are a promising platform for immunotherapy, although their limited innate immune activation may constrain therapeutic performance. Here, we report a simvastatin–CpG SNAs (S-SNAs) system that integrates a metabolic modulator with CpG to enhance immune activation. Mechanistically, simvastatin is found to potentiate CpG-induced innate immune signaling, likely through modulation of the mevalonate pathway, resulting in regulated macrophage activation in vitro. Integration into the SNAs architecture is associated with improved cellular uptake and enhanced RBD-specific humoral immune responses in vivo. In a murine melanoma model, the engineered S-SNAs exhibit antitumor activity and are associated with prolonged survival under the experimental conditions, without evident systemic toxicity. Overall, this work suggests that metabolic modulation may represent a useful strategy for improving the immunological performance of nucleic acid-based nanostructures.

Introduction

Spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) represent a structurally distinct class of nucleic acid nanostructures characterized by a dense, radially oriented shell of oligonucleotides assembled around a nanoscale core. This architecture confers biological properties that differ from linear nucleic acids, including improved cellular uptake, increased resistance to nuclease degradation, and more efficient engagement of intracellular nucleic acid–sensing pathways [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. These features have enabled the development of SNAs as platforms for immunomodulation, vaccine delivery, and cancer immunotherapy [[4], [5], [6]]. In particular, CpG-functionalized SNAs, which activate Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), have been widely explored for their ability to stimulate innate immune responses and shape downstream adaptive immunity [5,7].

However, the immunostimulatory potential of SNAs remains constrained by intrinsic regulatory mechanisms that control nucleic acid–sensing pathways in immune cells [6,8]. These pathways are tightly regulated by signaling thresholds and metabolic checkpoints that preserve immune homeostasis [9,10]. As a result, although SNAs improve delivery and stability of nucleic acid ligands, they do not fully overcome intracellular regulatory constraints that limit the magnitude of innate immune activation. This represents an important challenge in further improving nucleic acid–based immunotherapeutic platforms [11,12].

Recent studies have highlighted cellular metabolism as an important modulator of innate immune signaling [[13], [14], [15]]. Among these pathways, the mevalonate pathway has been implicated in regulating inflammatory responses and immune activation states [16,17]. Pharmacological intervention targeting this pathway has been shown to influence immune signaling outputs, suggesting a potential opportunity to modulate nucleic acid–driven immune responses through metabolic regulation [17,18]. Nevertheless, whether metabolic modulators can be effectively integrated into SNAs platforms to enhance their immunological activity remains to be fully explored.

In this study, we investigate a strategy to enhance the immunostimulatory performance of SNAs through incorporation of a metabolic modulator. Based on these considerations, we investigated whether metabolic modulation of the mevalonate pathway could be leveraged to enhance CpG-mediated immune activation. Simvastatin was selected as a candidate metabolic regulator, and its integration into a CpG-based SNAs platform was evaluated both in vitro and in vivo. We then construct a simvastatin–CpG SNAs platform to evaluate whether coordinated delivery can further improve immune activation compared with free components. The results provide evidence that metabolic modulation can augment nucleic acid–mediated immune responses and improve therapeutic outcomes in preclinical models.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

1,2-Dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC, Macklin Biochemical, Shanghai, China), Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) ligand CpG-ODN1826 (synthesized by Shanghai Sheng gong Bioengineering Co., Ltd., sequence: 5′-tccatgacgttcctgacgtt/iSp18//iSp18/-3′), RBD (OKAYBIO), simvastatin, mevastatin, atorvastatin, eliglustat, namilastat, and HS-173 (Macklin Biochemical, Shanghai, China).

Preparation of S-SNAs

The liposomal spherical nucleic acids were prepared as follows. Briefly, 4 mL of DOPC chloroform solution (25 mg/mL) was transferred into a glass vial, and simvastatin (final concentration of 2 mM in the organic phase) was co-dissolved with DOPC. The solvent was gradually removed under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to form a uniform lipid film on the wall of the vial. The resulting lipid film was hydrated with 10 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), vortexed, and bath-sonicated for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous liposomal dispersion. The dispersion was subjected to five freeze–thaw cycles and sequentially extruded through 200 nm, 120 nm, and 100 nm polycarbonate membranes to obtain uniformly sized liposomes. For oligonucleotide functionalization, 100 μM 3′-cholesterol-modified CpG-ODN1826 was incubated with the liposomes at 37 °C and 500 rpm overnight. The molar ratio of CpG to DOPC was 75:1. The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of simvastatin was determined by HPLC and reached 96.5%. The drug loading content (DL%) of simvastatin was calculated based on the total mass of lipid carrier and encapsulated drug, yielding a value of 3.1% (Table S2). Agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 3F) confirmed efficient incorporation of CpG into the SNAs structure, as evidenced by the absence of free CpG bands.

Characterization of S-SNAs

A 1.5% agarose gel (Sigma) containing SYBR Safe DNA dye was prepared and electrophoresed in 0.5× TBE buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 118 V for 30 min to evaluate the incorporation of CpG-ODN1826 into S-SNAs. Particle size distribution and surface charge were determined by dynamic light scattering and electrophoretic mobility analysis using a Zetasizer Nano system (Malvern Instruments).

Cell culture and treatment

MH-S cells (mouse alveolar macrophages, ATCC) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). Cells were pretreated with 2 μM simvastatin for 12 h, followed by incubation with 0.1 μM CpG-ODN1826 for an additional 12 h. For liposomal SNAs experiments, MH-S cells were treated with S-SNAs for 24 h.B16-OVA melanoma cells (ATCC) were cultured under identical conditions, and logarithmically growing cells were used for tumor inoculation.

In vivo immunization in mice

Six-week-old BALB/c mice were obtained from Yangzhou University. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Agricultural University (Approval No. NJAU.No20250228037). Mice were housed under controlled conditions (21 °C, 50–60% relative humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle). At day 0, mice were intranasally immunized with 200 μL of the designated formulations. Combination treatment groups included: Control, RBD (10 μg), RBD + simvastatin (10 μg RBD + 5 μg simvastatin), RBD + CpG (10 μg RBD + 5 μg CpG), and RBD + simvastatin + CpG (10 μg RBD + 5 μg simvastatin +5 μg CpG). SNAs treatment groups included: Control, RBD, RBD + liposome, RBD + Sim-liposome, RBD + CpG-functionalized liposome (CpG-SNA scaffold), and RBD + Sim/CpG co-assembled SNA (5 μg simvastatin and 5 μg CpG co-assembled within the SNA platform). Antibody responses were analyzed 14 days after the first and booster immunizations. Lung tissues were harvested for cellular immune analysis.

Subcutaneous melanoma model in mice

Six-to eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice weighing 18–22 g were purchased from Yangzhou University and maintained under conditions approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). B16-OVA cells were resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/mL, and 0.1 mL of the suspension (1 × 106 cells) was injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse. When tumors reached 50–100 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to: Control (PBS), OVA, Sim + CpG + OVA, and SNA + OVA groups. Intratumoral injections (50 μL) were administered every 3 days for three doses. Tumor volume was measured, TGI was calculated, survival was monitored, and Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed. At endpoint, mice were euthanized, and major organs were collected for H&E staining.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells or lung tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen™). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized with the Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcription Kit (Accurate Biology, AG11728) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was carried out using SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS II (Accurate Biology, AG11702), with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the internal reference. Relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Yesen, China) to extract total protein. Protein samples were separated via SDS–PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against phosphorylated AKT (Ser473), phosphorylated GSK3β (Ser9), NF-κB p65, β-actin, and GAPDH. After washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies, and protein bands were detected using standard visualization methods.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA from MH-S cells was sent to Meiji Biotech (Shanghai, China) for RNA sequencing analysis.

Blood and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collection

Blood was collected from anesthetized mice via eyeball puncture. After euthanasia, the trachea was exposed and an 18G catheter was inserted. Lungs were lavaged with 1 mL cold PBS, repeated twice to collect BALF. Lung tissues were mechanically homogenized and passed through a cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were lysed, and the remaining viable cells were collected for subsequent flow cytometric analysis.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum and BALF samples were analyzed by indirect ELISA for antibody levels, using HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, IgG2a, IgG2b, and IgA secondary antibodies.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were rinsed three times with ice-cold PBS and subsequently incubated with a panel of fluorophore-conjugated antibodies, including CD45-PE, CD8-PE-Cy7, CD11c-PE-Cy7, CD4-APC, MHCII-APC, and CD103-FITC. For macrophage polarization analysis, CD86-APC and CD206-PE antibodies were applied. Flow cytometric acquisition and analysis were performed using a CytoFLEX cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

Major organs were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E to evaluate tissue safety.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using the Student's t-test, while multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA). Survival curves were evaluated using the Log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Differences with a P-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Simvastatin potentiates CpG-induced innate immune activation through mevalonate pathway–associated PI3K–AKT–NF-κB signaling modulation

Given that CpG oligodeoxynucleotides constitute the core immunostimulatory shell of SNAs, we first investigated whether modulation of metabolic pathways could amplify CpG-mediated innate immune signaling, thereby enhancing the overall functional performance of CpG-based SNAs. Specifically, this study aimed to evaluate whether inhibition of the mevalonate pathway could serve as a translatable immune-boosting strategy to potentiate the intrinsic immunostimulatory activity of CpG.

To assess the scope and adaptability of this approach, murine alveolar macrophages (MH-S) were stimulated with representative Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists—poly(I:C) (TLR3), CpG (TLR9), R848 (TLR7/8), and Pam3CSK4 (TLR2/1)—either alone or in combination with simvastatin (Fig. 1A). Simvastatin enhanced IL-6 transcription across MyD88-dependent TLR pathways to varying degrees, however, the most pronounced amplification was observed in the CpG-treated group (2.4-fold relative to CpG alone) (Fig. 1B). Notably, TNF transcription was selectively increased in the CpG–simvastatin condition (2.4-fold vs. CpG alone) (Fig. 1C), whereas TLR3-driven responses were minimally affected, consistent with its MyD88-independent signaling mechanism [[19], [20], [21], [22]].Fig. 1Simvastatin Potentiates CpG-Induced Innate Immune Activation Through Mevalonate Pathway–Associated PI3K–AKT–NF-κB Signaling Modulation.(A) Schematic diagrams of in vitro and in vivo experiments.(B–C) Relative mRNA expression of IL-6 (B) and TNF-α (C) in MH-S cells after co-treatment with 2 μM simvastatin and various TLR agonists.(D) IL-6 protein secretion in MH-S cells treated with 2 μM simvastatin in combination with different concentrations of CpG.(E) Heatmap showing transcriptional levels of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., Ccl3, Ccl5) in mouse lungs 12 h after stimulation under optimal conditions.(F) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showing significant overrepresentation of genes in the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway.(G) Western blot analysis of phosphorylation levels of AKT, GSK3β, and NF-κB p65.(H–I) Comparison of the effects of PI3K inhibitors and simvastatin on Ccl5 (H) and IL-6 (I) mRNA expression in MH-S cells.(J–K) Effects of GGPP supplementation on Ccl5 (J) and IL-6 (K) transcription in MH-S cells treated with simvastatin–CpG.Data are presented as mean ± SD. These findings indicate that although inhibition of the mevalonate pathway exerts broader immunomodulatory effects, its impact appears to be more pronounced in the presence of CpG-mediated TLR9 signaling. Given that CpG constitutes the structural and functional core of CpG-SNAs, this finding can be directly leveraged to enhance the design and performance of CpG-based nanotherapeutics.

To further evaluate the robustness of metabolic adjuvantation, simvastatin was compared with other mevalonate pathway inhibitors. At equivalent concentrations, simvastatin demonstrated a significantly greater capacity to enhance CpG-induced IL-6 secretion than mevastatin or alovastatin (Fig. S1B–C). Dose optimization identified 2 μM simvastatin combined with 0.1 μM CpG as achieving maximal cytokine enhancement without compromising cell viability (Fig. 1D).

Importantly, this metabolic amplification extended beyond a single cytokine axis. Intratracheal administration of the optimized combination to BALB/c mice for 12 h resulted in coordinated upregulation of CCL3, CCL5, CXCL10, GM-CSF, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF in lung tissue (Fig. 1A and E, Fig. S1D–K), indicating reinforcement of the broader CpG-driven innate immune transcriptional network in vivo [23,24].

Mechanistically, transcriptomic profiling followed by KEGG enrichment analysis highlighted significant modulation of PI3K–AKT signaling upon CpG–simvastatin co-treatment (Fig. 1F). Consistently, Western blot analysis demonstrated reduced phosphorylation of AKT (Ser473) and GSK3β (Ser9), together with increased NF-κB p65 expression (Fig. 1G), supporting the involvement of PI3K–AKT–NF-κB signaling in the immunopotentiating effects of simvastatin [22,23]. These signaling changes are consistent with previous reports linking mevalonate pathway inhibition to regulation of the PI3K–AKT–NF-κB signaling axis through protein prenylation-dependent small GTPase activity [16,25].

To assess whether this effect resulted solely from PI3K inhibition, three structurally distinct PI3K inhibitors—Eganelisib, Nemiralisib, and HS-173—were evaluated. The effects of these inhibitors on CCL5 and IL-6 expression were variable, whereas the combination of simvastatin and CpG exhibited the highest overall expression level among all treatment groups (Fig. 1H and I). Importantly, supplementation with geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP), a downstream mevalonate metabolite, reversed the transcriptional enhancement induced by CpG–simvastatin co-treatment (Fig. 1J and K), strongly supporting the involvement of mevalonate pathway inhibition in this immunopotentiating effect. This observation is also consistent with previous studies indicating that restoration of protein prenylation by GGPP can influence downstream PI3K–AKT and NF-κB signaling during mevalonate pathway–dependent immune regulation [16,25].

Collectively, these findings establish mevalonate pathway modulation as an effective strategy for amplifying CpG-driven innate immune responses. Simvastatin-mediated metabolic intervention selectively enhances CpG-induced responses and is associated with modulation of the PI3K–AKT–GSK3β–NF-κB signaling axis. This pharmacologically tractable synergy provides the conceptual basis for subsequent structural integration of simvastatin with CpG to construct metabolically amplified spherical nucleic acids.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay revealed that, compared with either CpG or simvastatin alone, co-administration markedly increased IgA and IgG levels in BALF by 1.6- and 1.8-fold, respectively. Serum IgG2a was elevated by 1.4-fold, while total IgG showed a modest, non-significant upward trend (Fig. 2B). Following booster immunization, BALF IgA and IgG further increased, accompanied by sustained serum IgG and IgG2a responses (Fig. 2C), demonstrating that simvastatin-CpG co-administration rapidly induces robust humoral immunity and establishes durable antigen-specific responses.

Immunofluorescence analysis of cervical lymph nodes (CLNs) revealed that GL7+ germinal center B cells increased by 1.4-fold in the combination group compared with CpG alone, indicating enhanced germinal center reactions in upper respiratory tract–draining lymphoid tissues (Fig. 2D). While CD4+ tissue-resident memory T cells (CD4+ TRM) showed a non-significant upward trend (Fig. 2E), CD8+ TRM cells were significantly enriched in the lung following co-administration (Fig. 2F), indicating preferential promotion of cytotoxic tissue-resident memory.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that intranasal co-administration of simvastatin and CpG establishes a pulmonary immune microenvironment characterized by enhanced germinal center reactions and preferential enrichment of CD8+ tissue-resident memory T cells. These findings support the use of simvastatin–CpG as a metabolically programmed adjuvant to potentiate vaccine-induced RBD-induced humoral immune responses.

Fabrication and physicochemical characterization of simvastatin–CpG spherical nucleic acids

Building on the identification of simvastatin and CpG as a synergistic pair capable of amplifying immune activation in vitro and in vivo, we next sought to structurally integrate this metabolic–immunologic cooperation into a defined nanoconstruct. Rather than functioning merely as a delivery vehicle, the spherical nucleic acid (SNA) framework was employed to spatially coordinate simvastatin-mediated metabolic modulation with CpG-triggered innate immune signaling. By co-assembling simvastatin and CpG into a core–shell SNA architecture, we aimed to generate a structurally unified platform capable of enhancing the intrinsic immunostimulatory potential of the construct (Fig. 3A).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the resulting S-SNAs had an average hydrodynamic diameter of ∼100 nm, with a narrow size distribution and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.198, reflecting good particle uniformity and colloidal stability (Fig. 3B). Long-term storage at 4 °C over 30 days revealed minor, transient increases in particle size and zeta potential at early time points, followed by gradual return to baseline values (Fig. 3C–D). This behavior likely arises from initial interfacial adsorption–induced aggregation, followed by reorganization of the electric double layer and stabilizing components, without compromising the overall storage stability of the nanocarriers [[26], [27], [28]].

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed that the S-SNAs exhibited well-defined spherical morphology with a clearly discernible core–shell structure (Fig. 3E). Agarose gel electrophoresis demonstrated that CpG migration was markedly retarded within the S-SNAs compared with free CpG, indicating successful anchoring of CpG ODNs on the nanoparticle surface and formation of a stable nucleic acid shell (Fig. 3F).

To further evaluate the stability of the S-SNAs under physiologically relevant conditions, particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were assessed in PBS, Tris buffer, and RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The S-SNAs maintained a relatively stable hydrodynamic diameter ranging from 106 to 121 nm over 24 h in all three media, with no significant changes in PDI observed during incubation (Fig. S2A–C). In addition, the zeta potentials measured at 0 h were comparable among the different media (Fig. S2D). These results indicate that the engineered S-SNAs possess favorable stability and structural integrity under biologically relevant buffer conditions.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the engineered simvastatin–CpG SNAs possess the essential physicochemical properties for effective delivery and bioavailability, including defined particle size, structural integrity, long-term storage stability, stability under physiologically relevant buffer conditions, and efficient CpG incorporation. This well-characterized nanoplatform establishes a robust foundation for subsequent in vitro and in vivo evaluation of immunomodulatory performance and functional synergy.

Flow cytometric analysis of macrophage polarization revealed that the M1/M2 ratio in the S-SNAs group was higher than that in the Lip + CpG group but lower than that in the Lip (Sim) group (Fig. 4D). Rather than reflecting an overactivated inflammatory state, these results suggest that SNA-mediated co-delivery induces a distinct and balanced macrophage activation state compared with either metabolic modulation alone (Lip (Sim)) or CpG stimulation in non-assembled form (Lip + CpG).

To further investigate signaling changes associated with SNA-mediated immune activation, we examined the mevalonate pathway–associated PI3K–AKT–GSK3β signaling axis. Immunoblotting showed reduced GSK3β Ser9 phosphorylation following S-SNAs treatment (Fig. 4E), consistent with modulation of signaling pathways implicated in mevalonate pathway–dependent immune regulation [29]. These findings suggest that metabolic modulation contributes to the enhanced innate immune activation induced by S-SNAs delivery.

Together, these data demonstrate that simvastatin–CpG SNAs enhance innate immune activation in alveolar macrophages while promoting a balanced activation profile across multiple comparison conditions. These results support the synergistic contribution of metabolic modulation and nucleic acid–mediated signaling in the SNAs platform.

Simvastatin–CpG spherical nucleic acids enhance RBD-induced humoral immune responses in vivo

Previous studies have demonstrated that metabolic inhibition of the mevalonate pathway can synergize with CpG signaling, and the incorporation of both agents into an SNA further amplifies innate immune activation in alveolar macrophages while maintaining controlled inflammatory polarization. Given that effective priming of innate immunity is a prerequisite for the establishment of adaptive immune responses, we next investigated whether this metabolically reinforced nanostructure could enhance RBD-induced humoral immune responses in vivo. Specifically, we assessed whether simvastatin–CpG SNAs could enhance the magnitude and quality of humoral immune responses. Following primary immunization, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay revealed that antigen-specific IgA levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were significantly higher in the S-SNAs group than in all free-drug groups (Fig. 5B). In contrast, total serum IgG levels did not differ significantly among groups at this stage (Fig. 5B), indicating that SNA-mediated co-delivery preferentially enhances mucosal humoral responses without broadly increasing systemic antibody titers after the first immunization.

After booster immunization, BALF IgA levels in the S-SNAs group increased further and remained consistently elevated relative to controls (Fig. 5C), demonstrating sustained amplification of antigen-specific mucosal antibody production. In parallel, serum total IgG titers were significantly increased in SNA-treated mice compared with all control groups (Fig. 5C), indicating enhanced systemic humoral immunity. Subclass analysis further revealed marked elevations of IgG2a and IgG2b (Fig. 5C), suggesting a shift toward Th1-associated antibody isotypes [30,31]. Together, these findings indicate that simvastatin–CpG SNAs enhance both the magnitude and quality of antigen-specific humoral immune responses.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that co-delivery of simvastatin and CpG via spherical nucleic acids substantially amplifies antigen-specific humoral immune responses in vivo. This enhanced immunogenicity validates the simvastatin–CpG synergistic strategy as an effective approach to potentiate the immune performance of spherical nucleic acid platforms. These results further suggest that metabolic modulation can improve the quality of vaccine-induced antibody responses.

Collectively, our preceding data demonstrate that simvastatin–CpG SNAs enhance immune activation and antigen-specific humoral responses. We therefore sought to determine whether this immune enhancement could be translated into therapeutic benefit in a solid tumor setting.

To address this question, a subcutaneous melanoma model was established by inoculating B16-OVA cells into C57BL/6 mice, followed by intratumoral administration of S-SNAs or control formulations once tumors reached a volume of 50–100 mm3 (Fig. 6A). These findings suggest that immune activation induced by the nanoplatform may contribute to tumor growth inhibition and support its potential application in cancer immunotherapy.

Discussion

SNAs have emerged as an important platform in nanomedicine, offering precise spatial organization of nucleic acid ligands, high-density surface presentation, and efficient cellular uptake [4,32]. These structural features endow SNAs with immunomodulatory capabilities, making them attractive for vaccination, cancer immunotherapy, and mucosal immune applications. However, translating in vitro activity into robust and reproducible in vivo immune responses remains challenging. The intrinsic activity of nucleic acid agonists such as CpG is moderate, and endogenous regulatory mechanisms can limit immune activation [[33], [34], [35]]. In addition, conventional SNA designs primarily rely on nucleic acid–driven stimulation, which may restrict further enhancement of functional outcomes.

In this work, we propose a strategy that integrates metabolic modulation into the SNA platform to enhance immunostimulatory performance. By incorporating simvastatin, a clinically used HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor, into CpG-based SNAs, we constructed a nanostructure capable of coupling metabolic regulation with TLR9-mediated immune signaling [36]. This approach provides an additional layer of immune enhancement beyond conventional nucleic acid–only activation. Compared with traditional designs, metabolic modulation offers an orthogonal means to increase cellular responsiveness without relying on stronger inflammatory stimulation [37,38].

Mechanistically, simvastatin suppresses the mevalonate pathway, which is associated with modulation of intracellular signaling networks including PI3K–AKT–GSK3β (Fig. 1G). This modulation is accompanied by enhanced CpG-induced innate immune responses, including increased cytokine transcription and altered macrophage activation profiles (Figs. 1E and 4D). Importantly, this metabolic intervention does not lead to excessive inflammatory activation, suggesting a balanced immune enhancement profile [39,40]. The co-localization of simvastatin and CpG within S-SNAs may facilitate coordinated metabolic and immune signaling at the cellular level.

At the systemic level, the metabolically enhanced S-SNAs promoted strong RBD-induced humoral immune responses, including increased mucosal IgA and Th1-associated IgG subclasses in serum (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that metabolic modulation can enhance the quality and magnitude of antibody-mediated immunity induced by nucleic acid nanostructures. While modest cellular immune features may be suggested by antibody subclass distribution, detailed cellular immune profiling requires further investigation.

From a translational standpoint, integrating metabolic modulation into the SNA platform offers several advantages. First, it provides a generalizable strategy to enhance nucleic acid–based immunotherapies without relying on extensive chemical modification or additional synthetic adjuvants. Second, the engineered simvastatin CpG SNA platform demonstrates clear clinical potential: simvastatin is an FDA-approved statin with well-characterized safety, pharmacokinetics, and low cost, avoiding the high development burden of novel metabolic modulators; the liposomal SNA exhibits a narrow size distribution (∼100 nm) and maintains colloidal stability for at least 30 days at 4 °C, which is favorable for in vivo delivery and scalable clinical formulation; and intratumoral administration localizes drug exposure, reducing systemic off-target effects and minimizing the risk of inflammatory toxicity. Finally, this dual-functional design—combining structural nucleic acid integrity with metabolic amplification—establishes a modular framework that can be adapted to other nucleic acid agonists or combinatorial immunotherapy strategies.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the antitumor efficacy of the system was evaluated only in the B16-OVA melanoma model, and validation in additional tumor models would further strengthen the generalizability of the findings. Second, pharmacokinetics and biodistribution were not systematically characterized, which may be important for future translational evaluation. Third, although physicochemical stability was assessed under selected buffer and serum conditions, the long-term behavior of the system in more complex and dynamic biological environments remains to be further investigated. Fourth, immune characterization in this study primarily focused on macrophage-mediated innate responses and humoral immunity, and a more comprehensive analysis of additional immune cell subsets would further enhance mechanistic understanding. Finally, while metabolic modulation was shown to significantly enhance immune activation, the detailed intracellular and tumor microenvironmental interactions underlying this effect warrant further investigation in future studies.

In summary, this study demonstrates that integrating metabolic pathway modulation into spherical nucleic acid platforms represents an effective strategy in the present preclinical setting to enhance nucleic acid–mediated immune activation. By leveraging metabolic regulation, the structural advantages of S-SNAs can be translated into improved functional immune responses. This work provides insights for the rational design of next-generation nucleic acid nanomedicines with improved immunomodulatory potential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a metabolically guided approach to improve the immunostimulatory activity of spherical nucleic acids (SNAs) through the incorporation of a synergistic metabolic modulator. We identify simvastatin as an effective enhancer of CpG-induced innate immune activation via modulation of the mevalonate pathway, and demonstrate that its integration into the SNA architecture can further potentiate immune responses. The engineered simvastatin–CpG SNAs promote enhanced innate immune activation and support RBD-induced humoral immune responses in vivo, while also showing measurable antitumor effects in a melanoma model. No obvious systemic toxicity was observed under the experimental conditions evaluated. Overall, this work suggests that rational combination of metabolic regulation with nucleic acid nanostructures may offer a feasible strategy to modulate immune responses, providing a potential framework for the further development of nucleic acid–based immunotherapeutic systems.
