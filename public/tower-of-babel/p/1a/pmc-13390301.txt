Clarifying the added value of SOFA-2 in pneumonia-associated sepsis
Authors: Yang Li, Qiujiang Liu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the disease-specific evaluation of SOFA-2 in pneumonia-associated sepsis by Wei et al. [1]. The authors found broadly similar time-dependent discrimination for SOFA-2 and SOFA, while a composite model combining SOFA-2 with additional clinical predictors performed better than SOFA-2 alone. These findings are clinically relevant, particularly because SOFA-2 incorporates clinically important updates to the assessment of organ dysfunction, including a more granular consideration of respiratory support such as high-flow oxygen and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, which may be especially relevant in pneumonia-associated sepsis [2]. At the same time, their interpretation depends on the question being asked. Replacing SOFA as a descriptive measure of organ dysfunction and improving individualized mortality prediction are related but distinct aims. SOFA-2 was developed primarily to update the assessment of organ dysfunction in contemporary intensive care, rather than solely as a stand-alone mortality prediction model [2].

The authors appropriately describe the discrimination of SOFA-2 and SOFA as similar and do not claim formal equivalence [1]. Nevertheless, a non-significant comparison may not fully indicate how different their performance could plausibly be. Because both scores were calculated in the same patients, the paired difference in time-dependent area under the curve (ΔAUC), with its 95% confidence interval, may provide an informative estimate of comparative performance. Formal equivalence would require a prespecified, clinically defensible margin [3], but such a claim is not necessary here. Reporting the paired estimate and its precision could help show whether clinically relevant advantages or disadvantages remain compatible with the data.

A related issue concerns attribution of the composite model’s performance. Its improvement over SOFA-2 alone suggests that age, lactate, vital signs, and laboratory measurements provide additional prognostic information. However, this comparison alone may not fully separate the contribution of SOFA-2 from that of the additional predictors. One useful complementary comparison would therefore be between otherwise identical models containing either SOFA or SOFA-2. Paired differences in discrimination, calibration, and overall prediction error could then help quantify the incremental contribution attributable to the revised score.

The choice of the common predictor set also warrants consideration. Some variables in the composite model, including creatinine and oxygenation or respiratory-support measures, may overlap with information already represented in the organ-specific components of SOFA and SOFA-2 [1, 2]. Conditioning on such variables could absorb part of the information carried by either score and alter the interpretation of its incremental contribution. Two complementary comparisons may therefore be useful: a pragmatic comparison of full clinical models containing the same predictors and either score, and an incremental-information analysis based on a prespecified model that avoids duplicating raw score components. The former could compare total predictive performance in a realistic model; the latter could better isolate information contributed by the scores themselves.

The current study supports the prognostic relevance of SOFA-2 in pneumonia-associated sepsis, while further matched comparisons could help clarify whether SOFA-2 adds information beyond SOFA and how much of the composite model’s improvement is attributable to the additional clinical predictors. Defining the intended use, specifying the corresponding estimand, and comparing matched models may make that distinction clearer.
