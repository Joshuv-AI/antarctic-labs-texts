Establishing a National Deep Vein Thrombosis Network in Ghana: findings from a prospective observational study embedded in a capacity-building initiative

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVenous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. However, epidemiological data and access to diagnostics and management of VTE remain limited in sub-Saharan Africa. This multicentre initiative aimed at strengthening capacity, enhancing management and establishing epidemiological data on VTE—focusing on DVT—in Ghana.MethodsThe DVT network was established in nine hospitals across Ghana from 2018 to 2022. The initiative involved capacity building, technical infrastructure, including a train-the-trainers approach, mentorship, awareness workshops and campaigns. Epidemiological data were collected from patients with suspected DVT in two study phases.ResultsAmong 1422 patients with suspected DVT, diagnosis was confirmed by ultrasound in 44% (624/1422). Before the onset of symptoms suggestive of DVT, 25% of inpatients with active cancer (37/146) and 27% of inpatients with a history of DVT (8/30) were receiving anticoagulation. Following VTE confirmation, 96% (370/384) of patients who were assessed in the second phase of the project received anticoagulation. The all-cause in-hospital mortality rate evaluated in the second phase was found to be significantly higher among inpatients with confirmed VTE than in patients without (19%, 57/295 vs 4%, 15/415, p<0.001). Fifty-nine (19%) patients with DVT received a 3-month follow-up; 34% (20/59) experienced complete resolution; at 6 months, complete resolution increased to 71% (32/45).ConclusionThe high rate of confirmed DVT in suspected cases in Ghana highlights the need for targeted screening. Standardised capacity-building initiatives can be implemented across multiple hospitals, enhancing access to high-quality healthcare. While establishing specialised ‘DVT centres’ provides qualified management for referred patients, underdiagnosis of DVT and limited prophylaxis remain major challenges, underscoring the need to enhance nationwide thromboprophylaxis and healthcare access for patients with suspected DVT.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), represents a major global burden of disease with 10 million cases per year.1 It is a leading cause of death worldwide2 and considered the most common preventable cause of death in the hospital.3

Although Black Americans have a 30%–100% higher incidence of VTE compared with White Americans,4 5 data on VTE in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) are scarce. Many patients in SSA do not receive diagnosis and treatment for VTE due to under-recognition of the symptoms, low health-workforce competency, lack of diagnostic capacities at public healthcare facilities and high cost to patients or providers.6 Improving the diagnosis and treatment of DVT in resource-constrained settings requires addressing these challenges.

This multicentre initiative aimed to improve diagnostic capacity and standardise management of DVT in Ghana. A national capacity-building project was launched, and dedicated ‘DVT teams’ were established, trained and equipped with technical infrastructure in nine public hospitals across Ghana. ‘DVT teams’ were further enabled to train additional providers within the established DVT network. The initiative was flanked by hospital-wide awareness campaigns of DVT.

A secondary aim was to describe data collected within the initiative to assess the impact of our capacity-building project and increase our understanding of DVT in the Ghanaian population. These data include risk factors, clinical presentation, diagnosis, treatment and outcomes of patients with DVT in Ghana. To our knowledge, this is the first multicentre initiative on DVT in West Africa, addressing a critical gap in clinical management by providing epidemiological data on VTE in Ghana.

Methods

The intervention

Structure and phases of the DVT network

This multicentre initiative was carried out across nine public hospitals in Ghana. The locations for these hospitals are shown in figure 1. In each hospital, a ‘DVT team’ consisting of at least one physician and at least one nurse responsible for evaluating patients with suspected DVT cases was set up.

DVT network hospitals in Ghana. DVT, deep vein thrombosis.

In 2018, phase 1 of the initiative involved establishing a ‘Centre of Excellence’ for DVT within the Cardiology unit of the Directorate of Medicine at Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH) in Kumasi. KATH is the second-largest hospital in Ghana with a total of 1200 beds. According to the 2017 annual report of the KATH, the Specialist Outpatient Department recorded 273 927 cases with 34 552 ward admissions, while 16 993 emergency cases were also recorded.7 The total number of outpatients, hospital admissions and emergency visits across the network hospitals is provided, where available, in online supplemental table 1.

The initiative was expanded to Agogo Presbyterian Hospital (AGPH) in 2018 and Kumasi South Hospital (KUSH) in 2019.

In the second phase (2020–2022), the initiative was adopted by the Ghana Heart Initiative, which was operated by the German Agency for International Cooperation (GIZ). A second DVT training centre was established at Korle Bu Teaching Hospital (KBTH), the largest hospital in West Africa. The Ministry of Health and the Ghana Health Service designated five additional network hospitals: Ho Teaching Hospital (HTH), Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH), Cape Coast Teaching Hospital (CCTH), Bono Regional Hospital (BRRH; Sunyani) and Effia-Nkwanta Regional Hospital (ENRH; Takoradi). The project implementation timeline across the nine DVT network sites is shown in figure 2. The ‘DVT teams’ in all hospitals were available 24 h/day by phone.

Training content

In phase 1, KATH was developed into a national centre of excellence for the diagnosis and treatment of DVT. Physicians from KATH selected for the ‘DVT team’ received extensive hands-on training on venous compression ultrasound for the diagnosis of DVT as part of a train-the-trainers programme.

The 2-day training course included four voluntary participants with DVT and was led by experts from Germany and Austria with several years of practical experience in ultrasound. The training covered the diagnostic techniques of both proximal and distal lower extremity DVT (LEDVT) and upper extremity DVT (UEDVT) as well as jugular and vena cava thrombosis. A 2-day refresher ultrasound course was conducted after 6 months. Additionally, the DVT team from KATH participated in workshops on the prevalence, risk factors, signs and symptoms, diagnosis, treatment and prophylaxis of DVT. In a subsequent step, trained physicians from KATH offered training for nurses, clinicians and other healthcare workers across Ghana. KATH provided ultrasound training and workshops for the ‘DVT teams’ of five network hospitals. This included KBTH, which became the second training centre, providing training and mentorship to three hospitals. The training content was standardised across all DVT network hospitals. In the second phase, a series of three educational workshops were conducted, covering theoretical and practical knowledge of DVT, including hands-on diagnostic training across all sites. Detailed information on the number, timing and content of training courses for the ‘DVT teams’, and the mentoring structure is provided in online supplemental table 2.

Diagnostic criteria

Proximal LEDVT was defined as DVT of iliac, femoral and/or popliteal veins. Distal LEDVT was defined as DVT of peroneal, posterior, anterior tibial and/or calf muscular veins.8 Proximal UEDVT was defined as DVT of subclavian, internal jugular and/or axillary veins. Distal UEDVT was defined as DVT of brachial, radial and/or ulnar veins.9

Patients classified as having VTE included all participants with at least one of the following conditions: DVT or PE. The ‘DVT teams’ of all sites were advised to use an algorithm for diagnosing LEDVT based on Wells et al.10 The diagnostic algorithm for outpatients with suspected LEDVT was adapted from Wells et al (online supplemental figure 1).10 If the Wells score was used to guide further diagnostic decisions in outpatients, the dichotomised version was recommended.10 However, direct ultrasound was also permitted in outpatients and generally recommended in inpatients; in these groups, the Wells score was assessed for research purposes only. ‘DVT teams’ at all network hospitals were instructed to consider UEDVT based on typical symptoms and perform ultrasound directly in case of suspicion.11 In line with recommendations from the European Society of Cardiology and the European Respiratory Society, all network hospitals were advised to assess pretest probability of suspected PE using clinical judgement or validated prediction rules (revised Geneva or Wells).12 In patients with clinical suspicion of PE, proximal DVT on ultrasound combined with compatible clinical symptoms was deemed diagnostic of PE. In the absence of proximal DVT, PE was confirmed by chest CT. Cases meeting either criterion were classified as PE, consistent with the ESC guidelines.13 14

Technical equipment and cost coverage for diagnostics

All sites were equipped with a portable ultrasound scanner with a linear probe (LOGIQ e R7 by GE Healthcare, Chicago) and point-of-care devices for measurement of D-dimers (Cobas h232 by Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland) and International Normalised Ratio (CoaguCheck by Roche, Basel, Switzerland). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, D-dimer levels above 0.5 µg/mL were considered pathologically elevated. These diagnostic assessments were provided free of charge to all patients with suspected DVT. Each of the network hospitals had already been equipped with CT imaging capabilities outside of the present initiative, with CT scans performed as part of routine hospital care at the discretion of the responsible physician; these services were provided by the radiology units and were not free of charge.

Number of personnel trained

'DVT teams’ varied by hospital, comprising one to four physicians and one to two nurses. Detailed information is provided in online supplemental table 2.

Awareness strategies

Detailed information on awareness strategies and workshops is in online supplemental table 3. Posters displaying typical DVT symptoms and the DVT team’s phone number were placed in all hospital departments (online supplemental figure 2).

The evaluation

Study design

The evaluation phase was designed primarily as a descriptive observational study based on clinical data collected within the initiative; in addition, data were collected on healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude and practices.

Assessment of knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP) among healthcare personnel

Before the first DVT awareness initiative, an online KAP survey on DVT was conducted at the six hospitals included during the network’s roll-out phase. Participants included had not been trained on DVT before the survey within our project. The survey enabled healthcare professionals to self-assess their baseline knowledge of DVT to guide future campaigns.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All adult patients of African descent (over 18 years old) screened for suspected DVT (including asymmetry of limb pain, swelling, skin veins visibility, cyanosis, erythema and/or warmth) by the ‘DVT teams’ were included in the analysis after written informed consent.

Patients with missing data regarding suspected and confirmed DVT were not considered for this analysis.

Ethical approval

The study was approved by the respective institutional ethics committees of all participating clinics. Detailed information is provided in online supplemental table 4.

Data collection from suspected DVT to follow-up

Epidemiological data, risk factors, symptoms of VTE, site of suspected DVT, suspicion of additional PE, as well as other main and additional diagnoses, were assessed using a standardised questionnaire (online supplemental figure 3). Furthermore, anticoagulation before symptoms of VTE and confirmation of suspected VTE were surveyed.

In the second study phase (2020–2022), the initiation of anticoagulation, mortality rates and a 6-month follow-up were also evaluated. As part of the follow-up, patients received ultrasound scans at 3 and 6 months. An expanded case report form (CRF) was introduced at the beginning of study phase 2 (online supplemental figure 4).

The CRFs were designed based on medically relevant issues aligned with the study aims. Before the implementation of the CRF for phases 1 and 2, all members of the DVT team at KATH and, respectively, the ‘DVT teams’ at the participating hospitals reviewed the instrument and deemed it clear, feasible and appropriate for their respective settings.

Data analysis

Prespecified outcomes included suspected thrombotic events (LEDVT, UEDVT, additional PE) and their confirmation, diagnostic pathway parameters (Wells score, D-dimer levels), predefined symptoms and risk factors, anticoagulation status at presentation, treatment, mortality and follow-up outcomes.

All analyses were performed on available cases, and missing data were not imputed. The number of patients per participating site is reported, and missing data are described for variables with more than 1% missing values. Data from all sites were pooled for descriptive analysis, and no statistical adjustment for potential site-level differences was performed. Since the primary objective of the initiative was implementation rather than hypothesis testing, the analytical approach was largely descriptive and hypothesis-agnostic and should be considered as exploratory.

The map of Ghana showing the locations of the nine hospitals was created in R using raster and vector map data from naturalearthdata.com. All statistical analyses were carried out with R, V.3.6.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Data were assessed for normality by visual (histograms) and analytical means (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test). Variables were summarised as the median and IQR or in counts and percentages, respectively. χ2 test with Yates’ correction or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyse variable differences by group, as appropriate.

Role of the funding source

The funders of the study had no role in the study design, the recommendations on diagnosis or treatment, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation or writing of the report.

Patient and public involvement

It was not possible to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Confirmed cases of VTE

The number of screened patients with confirmed LEDVT, UEDVT and/or PE is depicted in figure 3. Among 1422 patients with suspected DVT, 646 (45%) had confirmed VTE, including 624 (44%) with confirmed DVT. PE, in addition to DVT, was confirmed in 130 patients, while 22 patients had confirmed PE without DVT on ultrasound.

The rate of confirmed VTE cases among screened patients was similar in inpatients (46%, 503/1100) and outpatients (44%, 143/322). Overall, inpatients accounted for most patients with confirmed VTE (78%, 503/646).

Among the 624 cases with confirmed DVT, 362 of 481 inpatients (75%) and 50 of 143 outpatients (35%) were considered to have provoked DVT due to at least one transient or persistent risk factor: active cancer; major surgery within the preceding 12 weeks; stroke; lower limb immobilisation (bed rest >3 days, paralysis/paresis or recent plaster immobilisation); pneumonia; sepsis/septic shock; oral contraceptive, pregnancy and/or puerperium.

Confirmed cases of LEDVT

Suspected LEDVT was confirmed in 44% of patients (619/1405). Among all LEDVTs, 99% were localised proximally (613/619), including four LEDVTs with proximal and distal thrombus. Only 1% of LEDVTs were localised distal-only (6/619). LEDVTs affected the left leg in 59% (364/619) and thus significantly more often than the right leg (49%, 302/619, p<0.001). Both legs were affected in 8% of cases (47/619).

Confirmed cases of PE

Among patients with suspected PE, the diagnosis was confirmed in 64% (152/237) (figure 3). In total, 21% of patients with confirmed DVT had additional PE (130/624). Confirmation was established clinically based on the presence of proximal DVT detected by ultrasound in 85% (129/152) and by chest CT in the absence of proximal DVT in 15% (23/152) of patients.

Pretest probability and D-dimer levels in suspected LEDVT

The median pretest probability assessed by the Wells score was significantly higher in patients with confirmed LEDVT than in those with a negative ultrasound scan, both among outpatients (median 4 vs 2, p<0.001) and in inpatients, in whom the score was recorded for research purposes only (median 4 vs 3, p<0.001) (table 2).

Of the 1405 patients with suspected LEDVT, 1275 (91%) had a Wells score >1, indicating that DVT is likely according to the two-level Wells score. This included 1010 out of 1087 inpatients (93%) and 265 out of 318 outpatients (83%). The negative predictive value (NPV) for a Wells score ≤1 was 89% (47/53) among outpatients, indicating the absence of LEDVT on ultrasound. Among inpatients, the NPV for a score ≤1 was only 77% (59/77). The positive predictive value for a Wells score >1, corresponding to ultrasound-confirmed LEDVT, was higher in outpatients (52%, 137/265) than in inpatients (45%, 458/1010). Among 53 outpatients with a Wells score ≤1, indicating that DVT is unlikely, exact D-dimer values were documented in only three individuals, preventing meaningful statistical analysis. One outpatient with a Wells score <1 showed elevated D-dimers, and LEDVT was confirmed by ultrasound.

Potential risk factors for VTE assessed beyond the Wells score

Differences in the frequency of potential risk factors aside from those included in the Wells score were assessed in all patients with confirmed VTE (DVT and/or PE) and without VTE (online supplemental table 5). However, the potential risk factors were not significantly more common among patients with confirmed VTE.

Anticoagulation prior to symptoms suggestive of VTE

Anticoagulants approved for prophylaxis and/or treatment were received by 16% of patients (223/1422) with suspected DVT before the development of symptoms suggestive of DVT. This included 16% of patients (101/646) in whom either DVT or PE was later confirmed.

Anticoagulants prior to suspicious symptoms were received by 20% of inpatients (216/1100) and 2% of outpatients (7/322). Anticoagulation prior to symptoms of DVT was received by only 25% (37/146) of inpatients with active cancer and 27% (8/30) of inpatients with history of DVT.

Among the 223 patients receiving anticoagulation before developing symptoms of DVT, the most common anticoagulant was enoxaparin with 61% (n=135). The median time of anticoagulation before the onset of symptoms was 5 days (IQR, 3–10) among 185 patients with available information.

Initiation of anticoagulation in patients with VTE

In the second study phase (2020–2022), the CRF was expanded, and the initiation or continuation of anticoagulants approved for the treatment of VTE was also assessed. Anticoagulation was initiated or continued in 96% of patients with VTE (370/384); among these, nearly 50% were treated with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) (184/370). Rivaroxaban was the anticoagulant most frequently applied, with 48% (179/370); five patients received apixaban. Heparins were administered to 28% of patients (103/370), and warfarin was administered to 21% of patients (77/370); one patient received dalteparin, and in six patients, the type of anticoagulant was not documented.

Among the 14 patients who did not receive anticoagulation, contraindication due to symptomatic bleeding was documented in six cases.

All-cause in-hospital mortality rate and frequency of main diagnoses in deceased patients

During the second study phase, the all-cause in-hospital mortality rate among patients with suspected VTE was 10% (72 out of 710 inpatients). The all-cause in-hospital mortality rate was 19% among patients with confirmed VTE (57/295) and thus significantly higher than among patients without VTE (4%, 15/415; p<0.001). Main diagnoses in deceased patients with and without VTE are shown in online supplemental table 6. In summary, nearly all inpatients with VTE who died during hospitalisation had at least one potentially life-threatening disease other than VTE, such as cancer (32%, 18/57), kidney failure (23%, 13/57), stroke (21%, 12/57), heart failure (14%, 8/57) and/or sepsis (9%, 5/57). Two deceased patients with confirmed VTE suffered from gastrointestinal bleeding, one from uterine bleeding and one from subdural hematoma; among the 12 patients with stroke, 2 had haemorrhagic strokes. None of the 15 deceased patients without VTE had symptomatic bleeding. Only one patient with VTE with fatal outcome did not have additional comorbidities apart from VTE. Out of the 57 patients with VTE and fatal outcome, eight (14%) had PE.

Ultrasound follow-up investigations in patients with DVT

In the second study phase, 3-month follow-up with ultrasound was documented in only 19% (59/313) of patients with DVT assumed alive at this time point. Only five out of the nine hospitals documented 3-month follow-up. In KATH, the follow-up rate was highest at 28% (34/122). At 3-month follow-up, complete wall-to-wall compressibility without residuals was observed in 34% of patients (20/59), indicating resolved DVT. In 66% of patients (39/59), a residual thrombus was observed.

Follow-up information 6 months after diagnosis was available for 15% of patients (45/308), not known to be deceased. In 71% of these patients (32/45), DVT was completely resolved, while residual DVT was visible in 29% (13/45) by ultrasound. Among the 32 patients with completely resolved DVT after 6 months, 94% (30/32) had received anticoagulation until the day of 6-month follow-up.

DVT knowledge among healthcare workers not being part of the ‘DVT teams’

The KAP survey demonstrated deficiency in DVT knowledge, with only 63% of 1027 healthcare workers reporting familiarity with DVT. While 94% of healthcare workers were aware that DVT is a life-threatening condition, only 73% knew that anticoagulation is the standard treatment. Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first multicentre capacity development programme to improve the diagnosis and management of DVT in West Africa. The prevalence of DVT among Ghanaians with clinical suspicion was high, with 44%, and a total of 624 patients were diagnosed with DVT during the project period. PE was detected in 130 patients in addition to DVT. Inpatients were more often diagnosed with DVT, which might be partly explained by the increased risk profile inherent to hospitalised patients as well as by the regular ward rounds performed by the ‘DVT teams’ to identify patients with signs or symptoms of DVT. Our findings suggest that DVT is common in Ghana but remains underdiagnosed, highlighting the need to improve nationwide access to diagnosis.

The reported prevalence of DVT or PE among patients with clinically suspected disease ranges from 4% to 44%, depending on the country and clinical setting.1 In the present initiative, the rate was comparatively high, with a DVT prevalence of 44%.

According to an observational study from the Global Anticoagulation Registry in the Field on Venous Thromboembolism (GARFIELD-VTE), which includes more than 10 000 patients with VTE from 28 countries (outside of West Africa), isolated distal DVT represents about 36% of all DVTs.8 Nevertheless, in the present initiative, only 1% of patients with LEDVT had isolated distal DVT. This is an alarming finding, since proximal DVT is an independent risk factor of PE, the most serious and potentially life-threatening complication of DVT.15 However, the number of patients with distal DVT might have been underestimated in the present initiative, since ultrasound has a lower sensitivity for the diagnosis of distal compared with proximal DVT.16 In a recent study from Ethiopia, isolated distal DVT was also relatively rare, with 8%.17

In the present initiative, the all-cause in-hospital mortality rate was high among patients with VTE, with 19% compared with 4% in patients without. An elevated risk of all-cause mortality following diagnosis of DVT was also reported in a recent study from Sweden, with an observed all-cause mortality of 21% in patients with DVT compared with 7% in the reference group during a mean follow-up of 2 years.18 In the present initiative, nearly all patients with VTE and in-hospital mortality had at least one potentially life-threatening comorbidity, such as cancer, stroke and/ or sepsis.

The annual incidence of DVT in Africa is estimated to be substantially lower19 than in high-income countries, where it is reported as 1–2 per 1000 population.20 Given the reported DVT diagnostic yields of approximately 1%–10% among inpatients in Africa,2123 the identification of 624 DVT cases across nine hospitals in our study suggests underinvestigation and underdiagnosis. This likely explains the observed predominance of highly symptomatic patients and the high proportion of proximal LEDVTs and may contribute to the high mortality rate in patients with DVT.

Active cancer, lower extremity immobilisation and previous VTE are among the main risk factors of VTE1 24 and were frequent among inpatients with DVT in the present initiative. For medical inpatients with acceptable bleeding risk and an International Medical Prevention Registry on Venous Thromboembolism (IMPROVE) score ≥2 (given in case of active cancer and/or previous VTE), VTE prophylaxis is considered indicated.24 25 However, among inpatients with active cancer and/or previous VTE, only about 26% received anticoagulation before the onset of suspicious symptoms in the present initiative.

A low rate of appropriate VTE prophylaxis despite a large proportion of hospitalised patients at risk of VTE is a global challenge, as demonstrated in a multicentre study that included data from 358 hospitals across 32 countries.26 Although prevention practices were outside the scope of the implementation activities and not a primary focus of our initiative, the limited use of prophylactic anticoagulation in the patient population emerged as an important unmet need identified through the DVT network.

Given the fact that VTE is considered the most preventable hospital death,3 26 improving the underutilisation of prophylactic anticoagulation in SSA is essential to enhance overall clinical outcomes. Systematic VTE prevention strategies should be prioritised in future research and clinical practices to reduce preventable morbidity and mortality.

VTEs are not only under-recognised but, once diagnosis is established, are also often undertreated in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), including SSA.27 However, most inpatients have a low bleeding risk,25 and in our capacity-building project, anticoagulation was initiated or continued in 96% of patients with VTE treated by physicians of the ‘DVT teams’.

Vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin were reported to be the most widely used oral anticoagulants for VTE in SSA.6 28 Unfortunately, most patients on warfarin in SSA were reported to have suboptimal levels, which increases the risk of complications such as VTE and bleeding.29 It has been shown that anticoagulation clinics in SSA that follow a standardised approach achieve better control of coagulation levels.6 However, convenient and affordable monitoring and management of patients on anticoagulation therapy are scarce in SSA, particularly in the public sector.6 In contrast to previous studies from SSA,6 28 DOACs instead of warfarin were most often prescribed for VTE treatment in the present initiative. Several previous studies suggested that DOACs are largely inaccessible in many countries in SSA and often unaffordable for the population due to their higher cost.6 28 In Ghana, preventive and curative treatments, including warfarin and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), are covered under the National Health Insurance Scheme, whereas DOACs are not.30 Warfarin and LMWH are readily available in public pharmacies. DOACs are available in most public and private pharmacies, especially in cities and bigger towns.

Ultrasound follow-up investigation at the end of anticoagulation is not recommended as standard for all patients with DVT according to the American Society of Hematology Guidelines, while it might be useful for individual patients.31 Nevertheless, to increase understanding of DVT in Ghanaian patients, ultrasound follow-up after 3 and 6 months following initial diagnosis was planned within our initiative. However, only 19% and 15% of patients with DVT had documented 3-month and 6-month ultrasound follow-up, respectively, likely reflecting the absence of incentives to compensate for the time commitment, travel burden and potential financial loss, resulting from absence from work associated with study-related follow-up visits; however, additional survivor bias cannot be excluded.

In patients with documented follow-up, ultrasound suggested resolved DVT in 34% and 71% of patients at 3-month and 6-month follow-up, respectively. Our results after 3 months of follow-up are comparable to a previous study,32 and 50% of patients with proximal DVT were reported to have residual thrombus after 1 year of follow-up previously.33

Anticoagulation treatment for 3–6 months in patients with VTE provoked by transient risk factors, followed by a decision to stop or continue anticoagulation, as recommended by the American Society of Hematology,31 was the recommended standard in our capacity-building project and seems reasonable for Ghanaian patients.

Prior research in SSA has indicated a lack of awareness of VTE and anticoagulation among both healthcare professionals and patients.3436 A recent meta-analysis found that better patient knowledge improves anticoagulation quality,37 underscoring the importance of well-informed healthcare providers. Consistent with this, our KAP survey of healthcare professionals outside the ‘DVT teams’ showed that knowledge of DVT still needed to be improved in Ghanaian healthcare workers before the awareness campaigns. Subsequently, targeted campaigns were implemented to improve staff education and referral of patients with suspected DVT to specialised ‘DVT teams’.

Our capacity-building initiative has been sustainable beyond the project phases and could serve as a blueprint for future efforts focused on DVT or other non-communicable diseases in LMICs. ‘DVT teams’ are operational at all network sites to date. Since physicians are no longer compensated after the end of the project, diagnostic services are now offered for a small fee. This fee is intended to cover the time required by the ‘DVT team’ as well as ongoing material costs, such as ultrasound gel. Nevertheless, because the ultrasound devices were provided through the capacity-building project, the fee remains comparatively low.

We observed that DVT teams preferred to perform ultrasound examinations directly on outpatients with a Wells score <1 rather than obtaining D-dimer measurements first. Although the underlying reasons for this practice were not systematically assessed, the primary contributing factor may have been physicians’ preference to perform ultrasound examinations to maintain proficiency and ensure continued practice of their ultrasound skills. For newly established DVT centres, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings, adopting an ultrasound-only approach could be a practical and sustainable initial strategy for diagnosis and clinical training.

There are notable limitations to our initiative. Although the initiative incorporated quality-improvement principles, it was neither designed nor implemented as a formal Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle. This limits the ability to attribute observed changes to a structured quality improvement process. While our CRFs provided a comprehensive overview of many aspects of DVT in West Africa, especially LEDVT, not all variables of interest in DVT research were assessed. While we assessed main and additional diagnoses from medical records, the exact causes of death and the criteria for major bleeding (beyond symptomatic bleeding) from the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis were beyond the scope of our study and were not assessed. Furthermore, treatment preferences of patients and responsible physicians, including the potentially discussed influence of financial burden, were not evaluated. The doses of anticoagulants taken prior to symptom onset were not assessed. Furthermore, the low follow-up rates at 3-month and 6-month postdiagnosis substantially limit the interpretability of the thrombus resolution rate. Potential contributors to poor study-related follow-up—such as geographic barriers, financial constraints and logistical challenges—should be systematically evaluated in future studies. As the study aimed to build capacity, it predominantly included tertiary hospitals, leading to their overrepresentation in this observational study. This may limit the generalisability of our results due to differences in patient profiles and case complexity. No follow-up was performed for the self-assessment score of healthcare personnel regarding their knowledge of DVT.

Conclusion

The implementation of a targeted screening and treatment programme through the National DVT network led to the detection of a high rate of DVT in clinically suspected cases in Ghana. The results demonstrate an unmet medical need, highlighting DVT as an important cause of morbidity and mortality. Importantly, for almost all eligible patients with a diagnosis of DVT, adequate anticoagulation was initiated by the highly specialised ‘DVT teams’, leading to a high rate of complete thrombus resolution in patients available for their follow-up assessments. However, this data should be interpreted cautiously, given the low follow-up rate. Underdiagnosis and limited prophylaxis for DVT remain major challenges, and future efforts are needed to enhance nationwide thromboprophylaxis and healthcare access for patients with suspected DVT. While our capacity-building project has been sustainable to date, its replicability in other LMICs has not yet been established. Nevertheless, we are convinced that our programme, which sustainably improved medical care in Ghana, can serve as a blueprint approach for future capacity-building projects. Standardised initiatives as described can be implemented across multiple hospitals, improving access to high-quality healthcare and could be implemented in different countries and even adapted for other diseases.
