A rapid tectonic plate reorganization event driven by changes at subduction locations in a mantle convection model
Authors: Joshua M. Guerrero, Chad W. Fairservice, Pejvak Javaheri, Julian P. Lowman, Paul J. Tackley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The occurrence of tectonic plate reorganization events is evident throughout the geologic record and appears to be associated with the cessation of mature and/or initiation of new subduction. Subduction initiation that produced the bend in the Hawaii-Emperor seamount chain resulted in the most recent upheaval of plate motion and engendered dramatic changes in plate velocities. Here, applying a method for identifying plate boundaries in a numerical global mantle convection model, we calculate Euler vector time series of self-consistently generated plates over a period of approximately 144 Myr. During this period, global evolution is characterized by two dominant convergent boundaries and the number of major plates identified varies between 9 and 14. Using a 2 Myr sampling rate to track the histories of three enduring plates that share a common triple junction, we observe a major plate reorganization event occurring over approximately 6-8 Myr. The catalyst for the event is identified as the development of a deep thermal feature intrinsic to internally heated convection. A buildup of heat enveloping a mature mantle downwelling accumulates a buoyancy that resists ongoing plate motion and eventually forces the over-riding plates on a new trajectory.

Introduction

Tectonic plate motion is the surface expression of convection in the Earth’s mantle. Subducting slabs form the downwelling limbs of global scale convection cells that overturn via creep-enabled1,2 crystalline movement and descend from an upper thermal boundary layer defined by a rapid increase in temperature with depth. Traversing the layer conversely and adopting a fluid dynamic framework for analyzing the processes introduces a dynamic viscosity to the description of the physics. The viscosity is characterized by an exponential increase with the inverse of temperature3 and results in obtaining near rigidity at depths less than 100 km4–7, generating formation of the solid Earth’s outermost layer, its lithosphere. The rigid rotation of neighbouring lithospheric tracts about a common axis (an Euler pole) is perhaps the most fundamental characteristic available for identifying the components of each distinct tectonic plate. Quantitatively, the rigid motion of each plate can be both described and assessed by defining the plate’s absolute Euler vector8–10 (hereafter simply referred to as the Euler vector), which utilizes the requirement that each candidate plate’s motion should emulate solid-body rotation about an axis (its Euler pole) passing through the global centre.

Seafloor paleomagnetism and the linearity of fracture zones demonstrate the longevity of relatively steady terrestrial plate motion over the past few tens of millions of years11,12. However, the geologic record also holds evidence that periods of nearly steady plate motion can be interrupted by comparatively rapid reorganization events, unfolding over 10 Myr periods, or less13,14. The bend in the Hawaiian-Emperor seamount chain is at least partially attributed to a recent major episode of plate motion reorganization15–19. A major reorganization event featuring inferred changes in plate motion is also evident in the mid-Cretaceous13,20.

Proposals for the driving mechanism for plate reorganization events have often been divided into two categories, those which posit that force changes at plate boundaries are the source of plate motion adjustment21,22 and those that make a case for deeper mantle derived forces instigating surface motion change23. However, the latter mechanism may be better described as an extension rather than an alternative to the first because it incorporates positive feedback between the slowing of plate motion and slab break-off. Convection models that manifest mantle driven changes in plate velocities exhibit rapid surpassing of a phenomenon analogous to a tipping point, where subduction pull towards a mature plate boundary is superseded by pull towards an adjacent nascent boundary. Deep sources of mantle buoyancy disrupt the ongoing motion and pull of established slabs, but subduction relocation remains the determining factor that changes plate motion direction24.

Improving our understanding of the generation of plate-like surface motion and the features peculiar to plate tectonics (e.g., transform faults) has motivated a substantial amount of the research activity made possible by the numerical modelling of mantle convection. Previous recognition that focused linear weak zones, analogous to plate boundaries, can be obtained with the inclusion of a strain-rate weakening mechanism to lower viscosity, so that surface motion can be obtained in a fluid with an exponentially sensitive dependence of viscosity on temperature24–29, has produced increasingly more sophisticated formulations for model viscosities that result in at least first-order plate generation30,31. For example, as a minimum, plate generation first requires producing networks of interconnected focused weak zones, marked by high deformation, (i.e., potential plate boundaries) that enclose comparatively broad and stiff intervening rigid caps (i.e., the candidate plates). However, obtaining a surface characterized by narrow weak zones contrasting with broad relatively strong lid-fragments does not guarantee that the latter regions are evolving like Earth’s plates.

Successful plate generation is demonstrated in a mantle convection model presented in this study, by identifying all potential plate boundaries and inverting the intervening intra-plate velocities to determine their agreement on a common rotation axis (i.e., the plate’s Euler pole32–35). Given the absence of geophysical data like a record of seismic epicentre locations or paleomagnetic surface data, the identification of plate boundaries and the history of their movement in convection model output is challenging. Here, we employ an iterative method to implement a previously described tool36,37 for identifying potential plate boundaries in the output of a 3D numerical model of mantle convection. Through identifying a consistent set of plate boundaries by iteration, for the first time, we present time series of Euler vector magnitudes and locations calculated from a global mantle convection model that self-consistently generates model plates. Post-processing model output for a period simulating over 140 Myr of evolution, we track the history of three neighbouring plates and find that they maintain rigidity well demonstrated by Euler vector fitting of the intra-plate velocities. Generally, as their sizes and positions change, the plates exhibit motion that changes direction and magnitude slowly. However, we also find that steady evolution can be punctuated by major but relatively short duration reorganization events, that we identify in an example analyzed below as being driven by the impact of mantle internal heating on the loss of slab-pull at a mature convergent plate boundary.

Euler vector histories and plate reorganization

At any point in time, the motion of a rigid plate over the surface of a sphere can be described in terms of rotation of the plate around a pole passing through the sphere’s centre (an Euler pole). The rotation rate of the plate provides a magnitude, and a rotation orientation consistent with right-handedness provides direction, so that plate motion can be described by an Euler vector parallel to the Euler pole33–35. In Fig. 2, we present histories of the Euler vectors by plotting the latitude and longitude locations of the intersection of the Euler poles of Plates A, B and C with the spherical surface. Explicit coordinates as well as the Euler vector magnitude time series are graphed in Fig. S4.

For 110 Myr, the rotation axis for Plate A very gradually drifts northwest. For the following 20 Myr decreased stability of the pole position begins and the rotation rate simultaneously decreases. From 130-134 Myr a more rapid and dramatic change in Euler vector direction occurs (Fig. S4) with a further drop in magnitude. Forcing of the plate reorganization event

Figure 5 shows three fields at five times; prior to and during the reorganization event described in the previous section. (The reverse angle rendition of Fig. 5 is shown in Fig. S8.) The entire sequence spans roughly one-third of a mantle transit time, and with a temporal resolution of 2 Myr between each frame, is animated in Supplementary Animation S1. The left, centre and right columns present the surface velocity field, interior temperature between depths of 170 and 1200 km and the radial velocity component, in the same depth range. Arrows on the neutral background surface in the left column indicate the direction of surface velocity and are coloured by the surface velocity magnitude at the location of their tails. Plate boundaries, as detected by the watershed-algorithm software, are outlined in black. Downwelling sheets in the model interior are produced at subduction zones EW and NS (both labelled in the top row) and trend left-to-right and through the south pole of the panels, respectively. As the model evolves through this period, the velocities shown on Plate C can be seen to change direction substantially, first gradually between times 108 and 124 Myr and then rapidly. During the same period, Plate A also changes direction, with most of the change in its direction coming between 108 and 124 Myr. In contrast with Plate A and Plate C, Plate B’s motion changes very little during the entire sequence, as indicated by Fig. 2.

The temperature and radial velocity fields shown in Figs. 5 and S8 reveal the source of the mantle-derived forcing that drives the plate reorganization event. Downwelling flow fed from the mature convergent boundaries results in the cold curtains that sink into the deep interior, emulating subducted slabs in the terrestrial mantle. At time 108 Myr and earlier the supply of cold material at subduction zone EW is steady and a contiguous downwelling extends deep into the mantle. Subsequently, convergence between Plates B and C weakens at this boundary and, correspondingly, disintegration of the downwelling begins. By the time that the reorganization event begins large sections of the downwelling are absent. Positive feedback between diminished convergence and weakening of the downwelling produces a “tear” in the slab that propagates from west to east during the reorganization sequence, resulting in a drastic reduction in the northward pull on Plate C. The reduction in convergence that leads to disintegration of the slab appears to result from a phenomenon that has been described previously23,39,40.

Sourced from the ongoing internal heating of the mantle, upward flowing warmth, pooled beneath the plates, is entrained by plate motion towards the convergent boundaries where the buoyancy of this hot mantle component resists downward pull41. During periods of steady subduction, the entrained heat gradually envelops the downwelling sheets, eventually balancing and then overcoming slab pull23,39,40. The hot envelope is observable in the centre column of Fig. 5 where a light coloured halo surrounds the downwellings (where they pass through the spherical surface at 1200 km depth). The corresponding renderings of the radial velocity components in the right-most column of the figure show that during this sequence, at locations most distant from the downwellings, the hot envelope adjacent to the descending flow has enough buoyancy to rise, rather than being pulled downward by entrainment, thus exerting resistance and shear on the mature downwelling. Ultimately, the build-up of heat around downwellings, owing to continual subduction, produces a source of mantle buoyancy great enough to shutdown subduction (see Animation S1). Fig. S9 illustrates the entire evolution of the downwelling sheets produced in EW and NS subduction zones in a five frame sequence at 32 Myr regular intervals. The sequence presented indicates that the heat buildup occurs over a period of order 100 Myr. Combined with the coincident appearance of an emerging subducting slab or extension of a second existing subduction zone (i.e., zone NS in this calculation) into a region close enough to influence plates pulled towards the mature downwelling, a rapid change in plate motion may occur as the newer slab’s influence becomes dominant. While multiple factors could impact the termination of convergence at earlier times, our model’s behaviour suggests the potential for the NS trending subduction zone to face a similar fate to the EW trending feature once its age becomes of order 100 Myr.

Observation of the accumulation of heat in parcels enveloping subducting slabs and its potential for impacting plate velocities has been identified previously. In plane-layer geometry systems featuring static plate boundaries but dynamically determined plate velocities, episodic plate reversals were described in two-dimensional geometry calculations41 and plate reorganization events were observed in three-dimensional systems23,39. As described here, in these earlier studies the impact of the buoyancy of the hot parcels was identified as a source of resistance to the ongoing downwelling flow and plate motion responsible for forming the parcels. In addition, the role of internal heating was proposed as critical to the reorganization process. However, plane-layer geometry systems become much hotter than spherical shell analogues, potentially amplifying the temperature anomalies adjacent to the downwellings in the former case42. Moreover, it could be argued that by continually drawing heat entrained by plate motion to a stationary location, the modelling of fixed plate boundaries leads to temperatures building to improbable magnitudes relative to what might be expected when plate boundary movement is possible. Nevertheless, the findings described here show that mantle driven plate reorganization events, produced by instabilities associated with buoyancy accumulation adjacent to subducting slabs, persist in spherical geometry convection and when dynamic plate boundary motion is present. Discussion

Model behaviour and properties

Multiple plate reorganization events are identifiable in the geologic record. During the opening of the Atlantic, fluctuation in mean plate velocity was accompanied by changes in plate direction that occurred both regionally and on a global scale. In general, the tectonic processes attributed to driving different reorganization events incorporate some role for subduction initiation and/or cessation20,43. This study identifies an aspect of convection, originating predominantly from heat input delivered by internal sources, that interacts with downwellings though a feedback loop with plate velocity and initiates the halting of subduction at a mature boundary23,39. Expressed in terms of plate evolution, this feedback is characterized by plate motion deceleration20 and convergent boundary relocation.

Although we have not modelled secular cooling of the mantle, the impact of secular cooling is incorporated in the internal heating rate that we prescribe and acts similarly to specification of a uniform internal heating rate in the model44. Accordingly, the impact of the radiogenic heat sources will be bolstered by modelling a cooling mantle. Prior to analyzing the sequence described in Fig. 1, the model was integrated forward in time until it had reached a statistically steady-state (i.e., showing neither long term heating nor cooling trends). The total heat loss from the core represents approximately 20% of the surface heat loss for the period analyzed here, with total variation in that value of just 2%.

Like secular cooling, continents are also absent from our model. Continental collisions may play a key role in initiating some plate reorganization events, particularly because continents can impact the location and onset of subduction. Our findings agree with the conclusions of previous authors who have described the impact of subduction initiation and cessation24 in global mantle convection models and may be applicable for explaining plate reorganization precipitated along a mature ocean-continent convergent margin as well as where changes in oceanic subduction settings have led to reorganization. However, it doesn’t present an alternative mechanism to reorganization driven by continent-continent convergence in the absence of the appearance of a new subduction zone.

Terrestrial plate reorganization events

A major reorganization event is recorded in continental geology and seafloor fabric during the mid-Cretaceous, at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}105-100 Ma13,20. The event is manifested in Antarctica-South America, India-Australia and India-Antarctica fracture zone relative motions that recorded sharp changes in seafloor spreading directions following the initiation of subduction along southern continental Eurasia45. Perhaps, most relevant to the model evolution described in this study is that this change is temporally coincident with a three-fold reduction in Australia’s motion associated with cessation of long-occurring East Gondwana subduction13. The full global nature of the event is recorded in synchronous reorientation of Pacific hot-spot tracks at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}95 Ma46.

The bend in the Hawaii-Emperor seamount chain, dated at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document}47 Ma15,18, is widely cited as the mark of a major event in the Pacific that initiated approximately 50 Ma19,20,47. This reorganization was one aspect of a wider global plate motion change that impacted the Australian47, South American48,49, Caribbean50 and Indian plates51,52. The Pacific plate’s change in absolute motion was likely a result of evolving north-west Pacific subduction settings along its modern western boundaries, including the Tonga-Kermadec arc15, the Izu-Bonin-Marianas Trench16,53,54 and Aleutian and Kuril-Kamchatka Trenches55. For example, early Eocene collision of the Olutorsky arc and Tokoro arc with Asia resulted in subduction termination, that was subsequently reinstated with a polarity reversal55 while subduction beneath the Kronotsky arc ceased during the Eocene, (transferring the arc to the Pacific Plate and setting the conditions necessary for its Miocene collision with Kamchatka55). The cessation of established intraoceanic subduction55 and onset of slab pull acting on the Pacific plate following the inception of subduction of its western periphery along the East-Asia margin is the primary candidate for the main tectonic driving force that instigated the plate’s change in motion56.

Despite the details describing the sequence of specific events not having reached a consensus, the mid-Cretaceous and Eocene global plate reorganizations following Pangea break-up are widely recognized, as are pre-cursors such as the plate decelerations20 also observed in this study. Moreover, changes occurring at convergent plate boundaries have been identified as key factors in instigating both regional and global changes in plate velocity magnitude and direction.

Summary

The successful identification of plate boundaries by the utilization of deformity fields, following application of a watershed algorithm, can be implemented to determine the Euler vector data for the plates identified in a global mantle convection model. We apply this method to a convection model featuring the system parameter values previously described by Langemeyer et al.29 and analyze an extended period of evolution. The model is characterized by the intermittent appearance of transform-like offsets along divergent boundaries as well as the generation of roughly 12-18 distinct regions that move mostly rigidly and distinctly, relative to one another, like tectonic plates. We tracked the evolution of three particularly durable and consistently identifiable plates sharing a common triple junction for more than 140 Myr. Employing the second-invariant of the strain-rate tensor from the fully dynamic mantle convection model, we assembled a record of the Euler vector data for the three plates at a 2 Myr sampling rate. Our findings showed that plate velocity can remain almost steady for tens of millions of years for one plate while changing gradually but consistently for a neighbouring plate. However, we also find that changes in the presence of a long-enduring convergent boundary results in a rapid plate reorganization event that unfolds in less than 8 Myr. On investigation, we attribute the reorganization event to the resistance of pull towards the mature convergent plate boundary as heat associated with entrainment of the hot upper mantle, towards the downwelling, builds around the descending curtain of cold flow so that buoyancy associated with heat buildup eventually overwhelms the downward pull, pushing the plate off its original course.

Plate reorganization events have been identified in the geologic record and their occurrence has been suggested to coincide with either changes at or initiation of convergent plate boundaries19,20. We show that limited duration for net pull forces at mature convergent boundaries is an aspect of bi-modally heated convection that necessitates periodic surface velocity reorganization. In contrast, the pull from a nascent downwelling is unimpeded by adjacent relatively hot mantle resisting the downwelling’s attraction and when initiated new subduction becomes the dominant force on adjacent surface motion. Forming deep in the Earth’s mantle, the growth of unstable hot parcels adjacent to subducting slabs play a key role in instigating plate reorganization events.

Methods

Mantle convection model

The model analyzed here derives from one of the cases presented by Langemeyer et al.29. Complete details of the equations solved and parameter values specified can be found therein. Here, we summarize the primary features of the model.

Mantle convection is modelled in a spherical shell geometry using the finite volume code STAGYY57. The grid resolution of each half of the yin-yang grid is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$96\times 768\times 256$$\end{document}, giving an approximate 40 km dimensional azimuthal resolution for a 2900 km deep mantle. Thermal convection is modelled by solving the non-dimensional governing equations for an infinite Prandtl number Boussinesq fluid. Heating is derived from an isothermal bottom boundary (with non-dimensional temperature of 1.0) and uniform internal heating of non-dimensional value 30. The surface condition is also isothermal (with a non-dimensional temperature of zero) and both boundaries are free-slip. Upper and lower thermal boundary layers are established by the high convective vigour of the model, which is determined by specifying a Rayleigh number (Ra) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^9$$\end{document}, corresponding to a reference viscosity of non-dimensional value 1.0. Mantle rheology is determined by a combined temperature-dependent and depth-dependent viscosity featuring viscoplastic yielding. The model analyzed here is the case described by Langemeyer et al.29 with a constant valued yield stress of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2 \times 10^7$$\end{document} (110 MPa). The temperature dependence of the Arrhenius-like viscosity specified results in a viscosity contrast of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3.2 \times 10^6$$\end{document} for material at the surface versus material at the core-mantle-boundary temperature. Accordingly, a viscous upper thermal boundary layer (and comparatively rigid surface) can form within the regions intervening the weak bands formed by the strain-rate dependent yielding.

Temporal dimensionalization

The period of evolution described here is from a time that follows the model having reached a state of thermal equilibrium as described by Langemeyer et al.29. Although the model parameter values specified result in vigorous convection, the upper thermal boundary layer obtained is thicker than the Earth’s thermal lithosphere by approximately 50%. For the findings presented here, we dimensionalize time by utilizing the time-averaged non-dimensional root-mean-square of the thermally equilibrated model velocity. The non-dimensional mantle transit time (the time required to traverse the mantle depth of non-dimensional value 1.0) is equal to the inverse of the system root-mean-square velocity. This non-dimensional transit time is equated with a terrestrial transit time estimate of 100 Myr (giving a mean transit velocity of roughly 2.9 cm/yr). All non-dimensional times, strain-rates and velocities are converted to dimensional equivalents using this conversion factor and a mantle depth of 2900 km.

Plate boundary detection and plate rigidity

We utilize the Automatic Detection of Plate Tectonics (ADOPT) software described by Mallard et al.36 for identifying possible plate boundaries and the implied candidate plates. The implementation of ADOPT described in this study utilizes the approach presented by Guerrero et al.37 and the complete details can be found therein. The calculation of plate Euler vectors is described in the next section. Here, we summarize the most important details of the method.

At each time-step analyzed, a watershed algorithm is used to identify a set of distinct candidate plate interiors from a pseudo elevation map derived from the logarithm of the second invariant of the strain-rate tensor. Distinct plate interiors are analogous to catchment basins on a terrain map. A threshold value, corresponding to a percentage of the maximum basin depth, controls the amount of basin filling, which covers lower-lying elevation features (low strain-rate regions) and highlights high elevation features (high strain-rate regions). Guerrero et al.37 showed that using a single global threshold value to detect plate boundaries may be insufficient to detect all significant plate boundaries. Accordingly, we systematically search through diminishing threshold values in the regions found with the first (highest) threshold until each plate detected satisfies a minimum tolerance for rigidity. Plates not satisfying this tolerance are searched for new internal boundaries with a reduced threshold until eventually all of the surface becomes identified with some plate satisfying a specified requirement for rigidity. During this iterative process, the network of candidate plate boundaries (and total number of candidate plates) increases.

For each surface segmentation detected by ADOPT, we evaluate the rigidity of the candidate plate based on the surface velocity’s agreement with the rigid velocity field inferred from the candidate plate’s rotation about its Euler pole. Allowing for regions with unfocused, or diffuse, boundaries (Fig. S5) could allow for boundary identification that would preserve identification of the rigid core of more plates for longer times. Future work should focus on accounting for diffuse boundaries to increase the potential for tracking a larger number of plates over longer periods.

Definition of mantle downwelling structures

Mantle downwelling structures are indicated in Figs. 5, S8, S9, and Animation S1. For Fig. 5 and S8, in the panels showing the temperature field, the downwellings are rendered as isosurfaces of the temperature field. In the panels that depict radial velocity magnitude, we adapt the definition of thermal structures based on the residual temperature formalism described by Labrosse59. In Fig. S9 and Animation S1, the residual temperature formalism is employed. The downwelling is defined as regions where the temperature is less than the horizontally averaged temperature, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar{T}(z)$$\end{document}, by a fraction of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{downwelling}$$\end{document} of the minimum temperature anomaly. That is, regions where temperature is given by6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_{downwelling}(z) \le \bar{T}(z) + c_{downwelling}[T_{min}(z) - \bar{T}(z)], \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{min}(z)$$\end{document} is the minimum temperature. In the figures presented here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{downwelling}$$\end{document} is set to 0.5 so that the regions are defined by half the magnitude of the difference between the temperature anomaly and the horizontal average. The downwelling residual temperature is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T - T_{downwelling}$$\end{document} so that the downwelling residual temperature isosurface is evaluated at a value of zero. The isosurface is coloured by the radial velocity (Figs. 5 and S8) or temperature (Fig. S9 and Animation S1) at the corresponding location.
