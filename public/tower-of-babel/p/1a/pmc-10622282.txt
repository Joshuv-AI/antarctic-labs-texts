A Case of TKA Failure in Patient with Primary Hyperparathyroidism
Authors: Ziyang Dong, Yang Li, Xinguang Wang, Hua Tian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is an endocrine disorder with high serum calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels. Excessive secretion of PTH can cause bone loss and may have potential damage to implant fixation after total knee arthroplasty (TKA). However, there is currently no research or literature reporting prosthesis loosening after TKA associated with PHPT.Case presentationThis case report describes a TKA failure of tibial prothesis loosening associated with PHPT. The patient faced aggravated pain and difficulty in walking 6 months after the primary TKA. Further examinations showed high serum calcium and PTH levels indicating the presence of PHPT. After the surgery of parathyroidectomy, the patient received revision TKA with 3D‐printed metaphyseal cone. Knee symptoms and function were finally improved.ConclusionPHPT is a risk factor for prosthesis loosening after TKA. Preoperative screening test of serum calcium and PTH levels is important to diagnose PHPT. For patients with PHPT undergoing TKA, preoperative endocrinotherapy and parathyroidectomy are recommended to improve the survival of prosthesis.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is considered one of the best treatments for end‐stage knee osteoarthritis. 1 However, some of the patients face a deterioration of knee symptoms and function sometime after TKA and are finally in need of revision surgery. The risk of TKA failure requiring revision is reported to be up to 5%, 2 which makes it a severe threat that cannot be ignored. Aseptic loosening is considered to be the most common reason of TKA failure. 2 Wear particles and other causes leading to bone absorption are thought to be responsible to the occurrence of aseptic loosening. 3

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is an endocrine disorder with high serum calcium as well as high serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels. 4 , 5 The incidence of PHPT is reported to be 52 and 28 per 100,000 for females and males in Asians. 6 Solitary parathyroid adenoma is considered to be the most frequent cause of PHPT. 7 Other causes include parathyroid hyperplasia and parathyroid carcinoma. 7 Due to the excessive secretion of PTH, bone loss may be stimulated and thus increase the level of serum calcium. Recurrent urinary stones and digestive tract symptoms such as constipation are also common clinical manifestations of PHPT. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Bone loss of PHPT may have potential damage to the fixation of prosthesis but the effect of PHPT on patients after TKA is still unreported. There is currently no research or literature reporting prosthesis loosening after TKA associated with PHPT. This case report describes a TKA failure of prothesis loosening in a patient with PHPT. After a parathyroidectomy surgery, the patient received revision TKA with 3D‐printed metaphyseal cone.

Case Report

Basic Information

A 66‐year‐old woman suffered with aggravated pain and valgus deformity of left knee for approximately 40 years. She was diagnosed with severe left knee osteoarthritis and ready for primary TKA. Preoperative physical examinations showed that the range of motion (ROM) of her left knee was limited to 5–60°. Radiological examinations showed severe knee osteoarthritis and valgus deformity of 16.5° in left knee. (Figure 1).

Preoperative x‐rays showing left knee osteoarthritis.

Primary TKA

The patient successfully received left primary TKA with bone cement (Legion PS, Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA). There was no occurrence of perioperative adverse events or complications. Cefuroxime sodium was regularly used for 2 days after surgery (1.5 g bid ivgtt). Active and passive rehabilitation training was guided by a physical therapist 36–48 h after surgery. ROM of left knee was recovered to 0–95° on the third day after surgery. Valgus deformity of left knee was improved from 16.5° to 5.4°. The patient was able to walk without the help of a crutch at 7 weeks after surgery and regained a walking distance of at least 300 meters without discomfort at 3 months after surgery. (Figure 2A).

Follow‐up x‐rays after primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA). (A) 3 months after primary TKA showing good alignment and prosthesis position (yellow lines show an acceptable hip‐knee‐ankle angle (HKA) of 5.4°). (B) 6 months after primary TKA showing prosthesis loosening and tibial bone defect (yellow arrows show prosthesis loosening and bone loss around the prosthesis; yellow lines show an HKA of 15.1° with increased varus deformity).

Prosthesis Loosening

In the follow‐up of 6 months after surgery, the patient complained about worsened pain and difficulty in walking. Physical examination showed pressing pain, varus deformity, positive patella tap test, and ROM of 0°–100° in left knee. Radiological examination showed bone mass loss and bone defect in the tibial area and prosthesis loosening with a radiolucent line around the tibial prosthesis.(Figure 2B).

Further examinations were done during her second hospitalization to exclude periprosthetic joint infection. Serologic tests showed normal erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) (17 mm/h) and C‐reactive protein (CRP) (0.2 mg/dL). Joint aspiration and synovial fluid analysis showed normal white blood cell count (1000 cells/uL), differential (13% multinucleated cells) and negative culture. Bone mineral density test showed no significant evidence of severe osteoporosis (L total = −0.6, Hip neck = −2.0, Hip total = −2.2).

Additional blood tests found a high level of serum calcium (3.02 mmol/L) and parathyroid hormone (PTH) (122 pg/mL), a low level of serum phosphate (0.81 mmol/L) and 25‐Hydroxyvitamin D3 (4.4 ng/ml) which indicated hyperparathyroidism. Ultrasonography and nuclide imaging of parathyroid confirmed the existence of parathyroid adenoma. We asked the patient for supplemented medical history. She admitted that she had suffered from constipation, renal stones and ostealgia in multiple parts of her body before the primary TKA, which were considered to be manifestations of hyperparathyroidism.

The patient was diagnosed with PHPT by consultation from endocrinology department and received a parathyroidectomy in the department of general surgery. After the parathyroidectomy, serum levels of calcium (2.29 mmol/L), phosphate (1.15 mmol/L) and PTH (24.1 pg/mL) were restored to normal range. 25‐Hydroxyvitamin D3 was raised from 4.4 to 14.1 ng/mL.

Revision TKA

The patient finally received one‐stage revision TKA with condylar constrained prosthesis (ACCK, AK Medical, Beijing, China) and 3D‐printed tibial metaphyseal cone by the same surgeon 15 months after the primary TKA. Based on the preoperative modeling from CT scanning, the patient‐customized highly porous metaphyseal cone was manufactured by 3D‐printing technique with Ti6Al4V powders. (Figure 3).

Design of 3D‐printed metaphyseal tibial cone. (A) preoperative modeling of tibia based on computed tomography (CT) scanning. (B) Metaphyseal cone manufactured by 3D‐printing.

Bone defect in the medial tibia is confirmed in preoperative modeling and intraoperative image, as it is shown in Figures 3A and 4A. After removal of the loosening prosthesis and the necrotizing soft tissues, the customized cone was inserted into the metaphyseal bone defect. (Figure 4).

Revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with constrained prosthesis and 3D‐printed tibial metaphyseal cone. (A) intraoperative image showing tibial bone defect with cone trial. (B) intraoperative image after implantation.

Intraoperative culture was also tested negative. Cefuroxime sodium was used for 2 days after surgery (1.5 g bid ivgtt). Postoperative examinations on the fourth day after revision surgery showed satisfying alignment and ROM (0–100°). At the 9‐month follow‐up after revision surgery, the patient had no significant knee pain and regained a walking distance of 2 km. (Figure 5).

Follow up x‐rays after revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) showing good alignment and prosthesis position. (A) 4 days after revision TKA. (B) 9 months after revision TKA.

Discussion and Conclusion

It is known that PHPT can cause bone loss and in turn lead to the potential for impaired implant fixation after TKA. 12 However, as far as we know, there is currently no research or literature reporting prosthesis loosening associated with PHPT. This report describes a case of TKA failure associated with PHPT. After parathyroidectomy surgery, revision TKA was operated with constrained prosthesis and 3D‐printed tibial metaphyseal cone to realize the improvement of patient's symptoms and function.

The patient in this case was diagnosed with parathyroid adenoma, which is the most common form of PHPT. 7 The excessive and inappropriate PTH secretion from parathyroid adenoma can stimulate osteoclasts at the corticomedullary junction and weaken the cortical bone. Though bone involvement is less common in tibia compared to proximal radius, distal clavicle and skull, 4 we are inclined to relate this early prosthetic loosening to bone loss caused by PHPT after excluding the possibility of periprosthetic joint infection and severe osteoporosis. This reminds us that PHPT could be a risk factor of TKA failure.

Unfortunately, the diagnosis of PHPT is still challenging. Considering that the majority of PHPT patients are asymptomatic, laboratory test of serum calcium level before TKA is of great importance. In this case, the increased serum calcium level was ignored before the first operation. It was taken into account only after the occurrence of early prosthesis loosening. This reminds us that preoperative screening test of serum calcium level is necessary for patients undergoing TKA. If the patient has an abnormal serum calcium level, further tests including serum level of phosphate, PTH and 25‐Hydroxyvitamin D3 and cervical ultrasonography are effective to exclude the diagnosis of PHPT.

For patients diagnosed with PHPT undergoing TKA, preoperative treatment is critical to the survival of prosthesis. Parathyroidectomy is considered the gold standard treatment for PHPT, 13 which can effectively improve bone mineral density and reduce fracture risk. 14 Nonoperative treatment of PHPT consists of inspecting for the development of end‐organ damage and regulation of serum calcium levels, which should be provided to asymptomatic patients not meeting surgical criteria. 5 In this case, parathyroidectomy was adopted to cure PHPT and prevent the failure of revision TKA.

Bone defect is a great challenge in revision TKA, which makes it difficult to reach component stability and fixation. A 3D‐printed metaphyseal cone was developed to solve this problem by combining the cone and the tibial component repairing bone defect. It is considered to be an effective tool with satisfying survivorship. 15 , 16 , 17 The patient in this case encountered the bone defect of the medial tibial plateau (AORI type IIA). We applied a constrained prosthesis with a 3D‐printed metaphyseal cone to reconstruct the proximal tibia.

As far as we know, this is the first case report describing prosthetic loosening associated with PHPT. It underlines the importance of pre‐TKA screening test of PHPT. However, there is also limitation in this case report. The relationship between PHPT and prosthesis loosening has not been clarified. Considering the abnormal secretion of PTH which may lead to bone loss and the exclusion of periprosthetic joint infection or severe osteoporosis, we tend to associate this prosthesis loosening with PHPT, while there is no direct evidence to verify the causal relationship. Therefore, further high‐quality research or more cases are needed to study the effect of PHPT on TKA patients.
