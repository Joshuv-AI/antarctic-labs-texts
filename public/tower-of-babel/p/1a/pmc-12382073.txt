Impact of ovarian cystectomy on oncological and reproductive outcomes in borderline ovarian tumors: a retrospective study from a tertiary center
Authors: Chujun Jiang, Yuyin Liu, Jianzhong Yu, Liying Huang, Xiaojun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFertility-sparing surgery (FSS) is commonly performed in patients with borderline ovarian tumors (BOT) due to their favorable prognosis. However, the long-term prognosis and pregnancy outcomes for these patients remain uncertain. This study aims to assess the feasibility of ovarian cystectomy (OC) and evaluate both oncologic and reproductive outcomes in BOT patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis of oncologic and reproductive outcomes was conducted on BOT patients who underwent OC at our department between July 1, 2007, and April 30, 2024, using univariate and multivariate analyses and survival curves.ResultsAmong 85 patients in the study, 12 experienced recurrence post-ovarian cystectomy. High-risk indicators included ultrasound findings, CA-125 levels, extent of surgery, and histological type. Of the 45 who attempted pregnancy, 38 conceived successfully, though no specific factors significantly influenced pregnancy outcomes. Among patients with micropapillary serous borderline ovarian tumors (MP-sBOT), 8 did not receive chemotherapy, while 6 did. Recurrence (2/8 vs. 1/6, p = 0.707) and fertility outcomes (1/8 vs. 2/6, p = 0.347) were not significantly different between those who received adjuvant chemotherapy and those who did not.ConclusionsOC is the preferred fertility-preserving treatment for BOT patients, with laparoscopic ovarian cystectomy recommended for most young, reproductive-aged patients. Early conception and close monitoring are advised for those with bilateral or high-risk tumors, though more research is needed on managing pregnancy-associated BOT.

Background

Borderline ovarian tumors (BOT) were first introduced by Taylor in 1929 and referred to as semi-malignant or borderline tumors [1]. These tumors account for 10–20% of all epithelial ovarian neoplasms [2]. Their biological behavior and histopathological characteristics fall between those of benign and malignant ovarian tumors. BOT exhibit nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, and cell proliferation but lack invasive basement membrane destruction, classifying them as low-grade malignant tumors with limited invasive and metastatic potential [3]. In 1961, the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) included BOT in the ovarian tumor classification system [4]. The global incidence of epithelial BOT is approximately 4.8 per 100,000, while the incidence in Asians is 2.2 per 100,000. Additionally, there is a trend of BOT patients being diagnosed at increasingly younger ages [5].

Similar to invasive ovarian cancer, comprehensive staging for BOTs, has historically included peritoneal washing cytology, hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and resection of visible peritoneal lesions [6]. However, recent evidence suggests that lymph node dissection, once a routine component, is not universally required, as it offers limited survival benefit in early-stage BOTs without nodal involvement, rendering it an optional procedure [7]. For mucinous BOTs (mBOTs), appendectomy was previously recommended as standard; current practice now advises it only when intraoperative findings indicate appendiceal pathology, given the lack of prognostic advantage in removing a grossly normal appendix [8]. These refinements aim to optimize oncologic outcomes while reducing unnecessary surgical interventions.

However, BOT frequently occur in young women, with an average age of 38 years. Clinically, most patients are diagnosed at an early stage (FIGO stage I) [9], and even those with advanced-stage disease have an acceptable risk of recurrence and a favorable prognosis [10]. Given the reproductive age of these patients and their overall good prognosis, fertility preservation becomes an important consideration when planning treatment option for young BOT patients [11].

Fertility-sparing surgery (FSS) is the recommended approach for young BOT patients or those desiring future fertility [12]. Regardless of staging, FSS aims to preserve the uterus and part of the ovaries, which may include unilateral or bilateral cystectomy (UC or BC), unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy with contralateral cystectomy (USO + CC), or unilateral cystectomy alone. However, few studies have specifically investigated prognosis and pregnancy outcomes following ovarian cystectomy (OC) in BOT patients. This study, therefore, aims to evaluate the impact of OC on recurrence and pregnancy outcomes in reproductive-aged BOT patients. The findings may assist BOT patients of childbearing age in making informed decisions about the optimal treatment approach.

Methods

Patients enrollment and information retrieval

This retrospective study included patients with histologically diagnosed with any type of primary BOT admitted to the Department of Gynecology at The Tenth Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (Dongguan People’s Hospital) from July 1, 2007, to April 30, 2024, who underwent OC as part of their initial surgery.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 45 years, (2) concurrent ovarian cancer or other malignancies, (3) concurrent pregnancy, (4) surgery performed outside our hospital, (5) hysterectomy or bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, (6) unilateral adnexectomy of the affected side without contralateral cystectomy, and (7) loss to follow-up. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital (KYKT2023-008-A1). Data, including age, tumor markers, imaging findings, surgical details, histology, recurrence and pregnancy outcomes, were retrieved from hospital records, supplemented by structured telephone interviews, or outpatient follow-up. The cutoff date for follow-up was July 2024.

Ovarian cystectomy and pathology

The surgical approach was determined based on the patient's age, tumor characteristics, fertility preferences and intraoperative findings, as guided by clinical expertise and contemporary guidelines [12]. Fertility-sparing OC was performed, including UC, BC, or USO + CC, to preserve the uterus and at least part of the ovary. In laparoscopic cases, cystectomy was performed within an endoscopic specimen retrieval bag when feasible to minimize spillage, though larger tumors (> 10 cm) were often decompressed intraoperatively before extraction through a mini-laparotomy incision, reflecting our institutional practice.

In addition, cytoreductive surgery (CRS) in Table 4 includes the following: omentectomy, appendectomy, and lymph node dissection: was reserved for patients with suspected advanced disease (e.g., positive ascites cytology, visible peritoneal implants, or FIGO stage > I), while OC alone was prioritized for early-stage cases (FIGO stage I) or those lacking extraovarian spread, consistent with fertility-preserving strategies [5, 11].

The tumor histological type was determined according to the 2003 WHO classification system. Histopathological information was obtained from pathology specimens evaluated by pathologists experienced in gynecological pathology. Patients were classified into four histological types: serous, mucinous, seromucinous, and micropapillary serous. The micropapillary type in serous borderline ovarian tumors (sBOT) was identified by complex microstructures with a filamentous appearance. Microinvasion was defined as stromal invasion confined to an area not exceeding 10 mm.

Oncologic and reproductive outcomes

Postoperative recurrence was defined as the detection of new local or distant metastatic lesions during follow-up based on imaging (ultrasound, CT, or MRI), with histological confirmation of the same tumor type through local biopsy or secondary surgery. The study endpoints included disease-free survival (DFS), recurrence rate, and pregnancy rate (including miscarriage and live birth). High-risk factors influencing recurrence in BOT patients who underwent OC were analyzed based on DFS. The DFS interval was defined as the time from the day of surgery to the detection of disease recurrence; if no recurrence occurred, it was defined as the time from surgery to the last follow-up. Pregnancy was defined as visualization of a gestational sac with positive serum β-HCG levels.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 27.0). Measurement data following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). Differences in normally distributed data were tested using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression models were used to assess correlations between clinical and pathological factors, surgical variables, and DFS. Prognostic factors with p < 0.15 identified in univariate analysis, along with clinically significant factors, were included in the multivariate analysis, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs). Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to evaluate associations between clinical, pathological factors, surgical variables, and pregnancy outcomes. Factors with p < 0.15 identified in univariate analysis, along with clinically significant factors, were included in the multivariate analysis, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs). Additionally, the Kaplan–Meier method was used to assess recurrence-free intervals and survival curves, with the log-rank test applied to determine statistical differences. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics of BOT patients

A total of 85 women of reproductive age with BOT were analyzed in this study. Their clinical and pathological characteristics, along with surgical information, are shown in Tables 1 and 2. The average age at diagnosis was 27.65 ± 5.11 years (range: 16–42 years). The mean preoperative tumor size on ultrasound was 12.64 ± 6.78 cm (range: 3–38 cm). Average levels of tumor markers were as follows: CA125, 274.55 ± 1268.43 U/mL (range: 8–10,932 U/mL); CA199, 676.02 ± 4668.75 U/mL (range: < 0.8–40,308 U/mL); and HE4, 50.18 ± 42.13 U/mL (range: 6–274 U/mL). Notably, 56.5% of HE4 values were unavailable due to inconsistent testing (Tables 1 and 2). While MP-sBOT patients showed significantly elevated HE4 levels compared to other subtypes (p= 0.013), its clinical utility in BOTs appears limited, given its lower specificity and lack of established prognostic value in non-pregnant patients, unlike CA-125. The mean intraoperative cyst diameter was 12.76 ± 7.55 cm (range: 4–38 cm), with an intraoperative tumor rupture rate of 42.3% (n = 36). The average follow-up period was 60.39 ± 48.76 months (range: 3–199 months). Among the patients, 45 (52.9%) initially underwent laparoscopy, 73 (85.9%) underwent OC, 11 (12.9%) underwent USO + OC, and 1 (1.2%) underwent bilateral ovarian biopsy.

6 patients underwent omentectomy, 1 patient had an appendectomy, and 1 patient underwent lymph node dissection. Among the six patients who underwent cytoreductive surgery, one experienced recurrence. In contrast, of the 79 patients who did not undergo cytoreductive surgery, 11 experienced recurrence (16.67% [1/6] vs. 13.92% [11/79], p = 0.852).

Histologically, 24 (28.2%) had sBOT, 46 (54.1%) had mBOT, 1 (1.2%) had seromucinous BOT, and 14 (16.5%) had micropapillary serous borderline ovarian tumors (MP-sBOT). Spontaneous cyst rupture during surgery and the presence of cancer cells in ascitic fluid were both observed in MP-sBOT patients (p < 0.05). Although the average age among the three groups is similar, mBOT patients have the widest age range (range: 16–42 years), while MP-sBOT patients have a more concentrated age distribution (range: 21–33 years). Similar age-related trends have been rarely reported in the literature. mBOT tumors and cysts have significantly largest diameters compared to the other types, but their CA-125 and CA-199 levels are lowest. MP-sBOT exhibits highest levels of CA-125 and HE4, suggesting more significant biomarker elevations and potential tumor invasiveness. SBOT shows a notably elevated CA-199 level, which may reflect its unique pathological characteristics (Table 3). Risk factors contributing to oncologic outcomes

Cox regression analysis of DFS was performed using both univariate and multivariate methods. During the follow-up period, 12 patients experienced recurrence. Among them, 11 had recurrences in either the same ovary or the contralateral ovary, while 1 patient had recurrences involving the ovary, uterine surface, and abdominal wall. The mean age of 12 patients with recurrence was 27.75 ± 4.99 years (range: 16–35 years), and the median DFS was 28.5 months (95% CI: 17–66 months). Histologically, 6 patients (50%) had sBOT, 3 (25%) had mBOT, and 3 (25%) had MP-sBOT. Surgical interventions for these cases included 8 unilateral ovarian cystectomies (9.41%), 3 unilateral adnexectomies combined with contralateral ovarian cystectomy (3.53%), and 1 bilateral ovarian biopsy (1.18%) (Table 4). Discussion

Conservative surgery is the preferred treatment for patients with BOT seeking fertility preservation, demonstrating favorable oncological and reproductive outcomes. However, limited data exist on the prognosis and outcomes of OC following BOT, particularly in younger women. This case series focuses on OC for BOT, highlighting key findings and identifying unresolved questions for future research, despite the inherent limitations of retrospective studies.

Predictive factors for diagnosis and recurrence

BOT are slow-growing tumors with nonspecific clinical symptoms. Approximately 30% of patients are asymptomatic, while 10% may experience abnormal vaginal bleeding. As the tumor enlarges, symptoms such as abdominal distension, pain, urinary frequency, and constipation can develop. Diagnosis relies on imaging studies like ultrasound and tumor markers such as CA125 and CA-199. CA125 levels correlate positively with tumor size and stage in sBOT patients [13], while CA-199 levels are associated with mBOT [9, 14]. In our study, the differences in CA125 and CA-199 levels between serous and mucinous BOT patients were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Elevated preoperative levels of CA125 and CA-199 or persistently high levels post-surgery indicate a higher risk of tumor recurrence (Table 5).

HE4 was included in our analysis as an exploratory marker; however, its utility in BOTs is less practical compared to CA125, with evidence suggesting greater relevance in epithelial ovarian cancer or pregnancy-related contexts [15]. Although HE4 levels were elevated in MP-sBOT (p = 0.013), its high rate of missing data (56.5%) and limited specificity in non-pregnant BOT patients restrict its clinical applicability in this setting.

According to the IOTA simple rules, solid cystic tumors with specific features are classified as malignant [16]. Ultrasound characteristics, such as cyst size and certain features, can predict BOT recurrence [17]. In our study, the mean preoperative cyst diameter on ultrasound was 12.64 ± 6.78 cm, with positive findings for solid cysts and cyst wall nodularity. The cystic-solid echo pattern of preoperative cysts is considered a potential high-risk factor for recurrence after OC (p < 0.15). Interestingly, sBOT had smaller tumor diameters compared to mBOT (p < 0.001), which contrasts with previous literature [9] (Koensgen D, Weiss M, Assmann K, et al. Characterization and Management of Borderline Ovarian Tumors—Results of a Retrospective, Single-center Study of Patients Treated at the Department of Gynecology and Obstetrics of the University Medicine GreifswaldJ. Anticancer Res, 2018,38(3):1539–1545.). Combining ultrasound imaging with CA125 and CA-199 levels can improve the diagnostic accuracy of borderline tumors. Papillary structures on preoperative ultrasound and elevated CA-125 levels emerged as adverse predictive factors for recurrence, aligning with previous research [18]. Similarly, Birge et al. [19] (2021) reported a recurrence rate of 16.7% (8/48) in a 15-year study of 48 BOT patients, identifying incomplete staging surgery as an independent predictor in multivariate analysis, alongside univariate associations with FIGO stage, CA-125, and micropapillary patterns. Our recurrence rate of 14.1% (12/85) aligns closely, with intraoperative cyst rupture (p = 0.014) as a significant risk factor. While our fertility-sparing cohort emphasizes rupture, Birge et al. highlight staging completeness, suggesting complementary surgical impacts on recurrence risk.

Oncologic prognosis of ovarian cystectomy for BOT

OC is a safe treatment option for BOT, commonly diagnosed in young women with low malignant potential, and favorable outcomes. FSS is recommended for patients desiring to preserve fertility [20, 21], as well as for those undergoing reoperations due to recurrence [22]. Unilateral adnexectomy is frequent for patients with unilateral BOT. In this study, recurrence occurred in 14.12% (12/85), of patients while 44.71% (38/85) achieved pregnancy. Table 5 details the 12 recurrence cases, with a median postoperative DFS of 28.5 months (95% CI: 17–66 months), longer than the reported range of 49 to 63 months for BOT recurrence [23]. Overall, this supports OC’s use in young patients with unilateral BOT.

Our study reports an intraoperative cyst rupture rate of 32.9% (Table 2), higher than the 15–25% range in prior studies [7], likely due to large tumors (61.2% ≥ 10 cm). Literature links intraoperative rupture to increased recurrence risk via tumor cell dissemination [24], consistent with our finding (p = 0.014). Ruptures occurred more frequently during laparoscopic procedures (20/28, 71.4%) than laparotomy (8/28, 28.6%), possibly due to technical challenges with larger masses. Despite this, recurrence rates showed no significant difference between laparoscopy (7/45, 15.6%) and laparotomy (5/40, 12.5%) (χ2 = 0.18, p = 0.671). This aligns with studies reporting comparable oncologic outcomes across approaches in fertility-sparing BOT cohorts [25], yet contrasts with findings of higher recurrence with laparoscopy due to rupture risk [26]. Our low recurrence rate and OC focus may explain this lack of difference, suggesting rupture risk, not approach, drives recurrence. In laparoscopic cases, cystectomy used an endoscopic specimen retrieval bag when feasible to minimize spillage; for tumors > 10 cm, decompression and mini-laparotomy extraction were employed. This supports OC’s safety for ovarian reserve and pregnancy rates [27]. However, retrospective design and limited long-term fertility data warrant prospective studies to refine BOT management.”

Identifying high-risk patients with BOT is crucial for preventing recurrence. In this study, bilateral tumors have a higher recurrence rate compared to unilateral tumors (58.33% vs. 41.67%, p = 0.069), especially in mBOT. Spontaneous cyst rupture during surgery increases the risk of recurrence (p = 0.014), which is often observed in MP-sBOT. Similarly, patients with sBOT exhibit a higher recurrence rate compared to those with mBOT [28]. The choice of surgical approach is significantly influenced by the histological type, with serous and mucinous tumors being the most prevalent. However, close postoperative follow-up is essential for patients with sBOT to improve clinical outcomes.

Comprehensive staging surgery for BOT remains controversial, with some studies suggesting it may reduce recurrence but not improve prognosis and potentially impact pregnancy [2, 29]. The 2017 NCCN guidelines removed the recommendation for comprehensive staging, while expert consensus in China still recommends it for certain patients. In this study, among the six patients who underwent cytoreductive surgery, one experienced recurrence. In comparison, 11 of the 79 patients who did not undergo cytoreductive surgery experienced recurrence (p = 0.852). Therefore, no significant relationship was found between tumor recurrence and these extensive procedures, indicating that not all cases require comprehensive staging surgery.

Pregnancy outcomes of ovarian cystectomy for BOT

A meta-analysis found that OC for BOT is associated with a higher recurrence rate but does not affect postoperative pregnancy rates [27]. In our study, 44.7% (38/85) of patients achieved successful pregnancies post-surgery (Table 2), exceeding the 15–25% reported in some literature but aligning with Darai E and Ouldamer L’s broader range of 32–65% [25, 30]. This higher rate likely reflects our cohort’s young age (mean age of pregnant patients: 27.65 ± 5.11 years) and exclusive focus on fertility-sparing OC, as shown in our flowchart (Fig. 1). Among pregnant patients, laparoscopy was used in 20/38 (52.6%) and laparotomy in 18/38 (47.4%), consistent with the overall cohort (52.9% laparoscopy). We did not systematically collect data on postoperative ovulation induction or stimulant treatments; however, 2/38 pregnancies (5.3%) resulted from assisted reproductive technology (ART, specifically IVF-ET), which typically involves ovulation induction, while the remaining 36 (94.7%) occurred spontaneously. The 44.7% represents the total pregnancy rate, with 33/38 (86.8%, or 38.8% of the cohort) resulting in live births, alongside 2 ectopic pregnancies, 2 missed abortions, and 1 elective termination (Fig. 3). We did not collect gestational age data to distinguish term versus preterm births, and no neonatal deaths were reported among live births. Of the 38 pregnant patients, 2 (5.3%) had received adjuvant chemotherapy post-surgery prior to pregnancy, with no impact on pregnancy success (p = 0.645), while 36 had not; no chemotherapy was administered during pregnancy.

To explore predictors of pregnancy, we conducted logistic regression analyses (Tables 6 and 7). Univariate analyses identified significant negative associations with pregnancy for preoperative cyst wall papillae (OR = 0.375, 95% CI: 0.155–0.907, p = 0.030), elevated CA-19–9 (> 37 U/mL, OR = 0.239, 95% CI: 0.062–0.926, p = 0.038), intraoperative cyst diameter ≥ 10 cm (OR = 0.344, 95% CI: 0.140–0.848, p = 0.020), multiple cystic lesions (OR = 0.377, 95% CI: 0.149–0.957, p = 0.040), and intraoperative papillae (OR = 0.412, 95% CI: 0.171–0.990, p = 0.048). However, these factors were not significant in multivariate analyses adjusting for confounders like age and tumor characteristics (all p > 0.05). This lack of multivariate significance, despite our sample size (n = 85), may reflect our focus on young patients (mean age of pregnant patients: 27.65 ± 5.11 years) and a moderate pregnancy event rate (38/85), contrasting with smaller studies reporting significant effects of micropapillary structure, microinvasive lesions, and size increase [24]. Such studies may overestimate associations due to limited adjustment or selection bias.

Beyond clinical metrics, the life experiences of BOT survivors, including fertility success and its psychosocial impact, warrant attention. A recent survey in Italy highlighted the diverse experiences of gynecologic cancer survivors, emphasizing the importance of long-term follow-up to address both reproductive and quality-of-life outcomesThis underscores the need for holistic management strategies in BOT patients post-OC [31].

Ouldamer et al. [32] suggested higher live birth rates in sBOT than mBOT patients, yet we identified no high-risk factors affecting postoperative pregnancy. Among the 12 patients with recurrence, none recurred during pregnancy. Six patients with concurrent pregnancy were excluded (data not reported). While limited data address pregnancy-related BOT management, no evidence suggests pregnancy accelerates BOT progression. Further research with larger cohorts and standardized multivariate models is needed to clarify pregnancy outcome determinants in BOT patients.

Treatment and prognosis of MP-sBOT with ovarian cystectomy

MP-sBOT, a subtype of sBOT, were first described in 1996 and account for about 25% of sBOT cases. These tumors exhibit non-layered branching structures with distinctive micropapillary and/or sawtooth patterns [33]. MP-sBOT patients frequently present with bilateral tumors, ovarian surface involvement, late-stage diagnoses, microinvasive lesions, infiltrative implants, and a higher likelihood of lymph node involvement, resulting in a poorer prognosis compared to typical sBOT. This study found that CA125 and HE4 levels were significantly elevated in MP-sBOT patients compared to mBOT patients (p < 0.05), suggesting that the combination of CA125 and HE4 may serve as strong predictive factors for identifying MP-sBOT.

The debate over whether microinvasive cases carry an increased risk of aggressive recurrence remains unresolved. Chen et al. [34] identified micropapillary patterns as being associated with poorer survival outcomes in patients with BOT. Some literature suggests that microinvasive structures negatively impact prognosis [35], while Du Bois A et al. [26] found no such correlation. Vasconcelos et al. [24]reported that the mortality rate for patients with the micropapillary variant of sBOT is significantly higher than that of patients with advanced sBOT. Nevertheless, in this study, no significant difference in recurrence rates were found among patients with sBOT, mBOT, and MP-sBOT patients (p = 0. 79).

Currently, limited data specifically addresses prognosis, the feasibility of fertility preservation, and pregnancy outcomes after chemotherapy for serous borderline ovarian tumor-micropapillary variant patients. No evidence suggests that adjuvant treatments, such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy, improve survival rates for BOT patients. Chemotherapy is primarily offered to patients with infiltrative implants, especially in sBOT cases [36]. Some researchers have proposed that MP-sBOT is not significantly associated with DFS [37]. In this study, all MP-sBOT patients had non-invasive implants, which did not affect the DFS in this group of cases (p = 0.576), consistent with previous [38].

Compared to other histological types of BOT, MP-sBOT exhibits the highest rates of spontaneous rupture (p < 0.05) and positive ascitic cytology (p < 0.05). The presence of malignant cells in ascites fluid is considered to be the strongest predictor for the diagnosis of patients with MP-sBOT. Cytological examination of ascitic fluid is a simple and effective method for diagnosing peritoneal dissemination in BOT [39, 40]. Positive cytology in peritoneal washings is significantly associated with prognosis, and patients with positive cytology after BOT surgery may be recommended for adjuvant chemotherapy. In this study, 8 out of 14 MP-sBOT patients did not receive postoperative chemotherapy, resulting in varied oncological and fertility outcomes compared to the 6 patients who did receive chemotherapy. Recurrence (2/8 vs. 1/6, p = 0.707) and fertility outcomes (1/8 vs. 2/6, p = 0.347) were not significantly different between those who received adjuvant chemotherapy and those who did not.

It remains unclear whether subsequent serous carcinoma in women who have previously undergone treatment for sBOT represents malignant progression/recurrence or an independent primary tumor. Some women with sBOT may later develop serous carcinoma, with most cases being low-grade [41]. Further research is needed to explore optimal postoperative management of MP-sBOT.

Advantages and limitations

The limitations of this study include its single-center, retrospective design, which may introduce bias and limit generalizability. Additionally, key indicators such as staging were not evaluated, and detailed pregnancy outcomes were incomplete: data on postoperative ovulation induction or stimulant treatments were not systematically collected, limiting assessment of their contribution to pregnancy outcomes beyond the 2 ART cases, and gestational age at delivery was not recorded, preventing distinction between term and preterm births. However, the study's strengths include a large cohort of BOT patients, standardized protocols, and consistent reporting, which enhance the reliability of future comparative analyses. Validation through prospective, multicenter studies is necessary to confirm the findings on factors affecting postoperative pregnancy outcomes.

Conclusion

In this 17-year retrospective study (2007–2024) of 85 BOT patients undergoing OC, 38 (44.7%) achieved successful pregnancies, with 33 (38.8%) resulting in live births. The overall recurrence rate was 14.1% (12/85). Univariate analysis identified factors such as preoperative cyst wall papillae and larger intraoperative cyst diameter as associated with pregnancy outcomes (p < 0.05), but none were significant in multivariate analysis (p > 0.05). These findings support OC as an effective fertility-sparing treatment for young BOT patients, offering a favorable pregnancy rate with an acceptable recurrence risk. Prospective studies are needed to identify reliable predictors of postoperative pregnancy outcomes and confirm these results.
