From emerging viral pathogens to pan-resistant bacteria: toward a post-antibiotic era in immunocompromised patients
Authors: Ayesha Yousaf, Iqra Shahid, Maguie Jaber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging viral infections and accelerating antimicrobial resistance (AMR) have converged into a profound global health crisis, particularly endangering immunocompromised patients. One in six laboratory-confirmed bacterial infections is now resistant to common antibiotics, with resistance rising annually worldwide. AMR was associated with an estimated 4.95 million deaths in 2019 and directly caused 1.27 million deaths, with projections of up to 10 million deaths annually by 2050. Concurrent arboviral outbreaks, such as Oropouche virus, continue to expand geographically. The spread of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance and pan-resistant bacteria threatens modern medical practice, demanding urgent, coordinated global intervention.

To the Editor,

The global infectious disease landscape is being reshaped by the convergence of emerging viral pathogens and accelerating antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Recent outbreaks of Oropouche virus in Latin America illustrate the expanding geographic range of arboviral diseases and the urgent need for improved surveillance and clinical preparedness[1]. The evolving epidemiology, clinical manifestations, and diagnostic challenges associated with Oropouche virus further underscore its growing public health importance[1]. Critically, this convergence is not merely parallel but synergistic: arboviral infections in immunocompromised hosts frequently precipitate secondary bacterial infections, while empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic use during febrile arboviral episodes accelerates the selection pressure that drives AMR. Immunocompromised patients are thus doubly imperiled, being vulnerable to the primary viral insult and to the subsequent emergence of resistant secondary pathogens.

Parallel to these viral threats, AMR continues to undermine modern medicine. A comprehensive global analysis published in The Lancet estimated that bacterial AMR was associated with 4.95 million deaths in 2019, including 1.27 million deaths directly attributable to resistance[2]. Projections from the Review on Antimicrobial Resistance warn that, without decisive intervention, annual deaths could reach 10 million by 2050[3]. The World Health Organization has similarly emphasized the accelerating rise in resistance across critical pathogen-drug combinations worldwide[4].

Immunocompromised individuals, including patients undergoing chemotherapy, organ transplantation, or intensive care, are disproportionately affected. In these populations, multidrug-resistant infections are associated with higher morbidity, mortality, and prolonged hospitalization[5]. Among gram-negative pathogens, the rapid global dissemination of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance represents a particularly alarming development. The evolution and structural diversification of the mcr gene family, from mcr-1 to emerging variants, have significantly compromised colistin efficacy, historically considered a last-resort antibiotic[6].

Compounding this crisis is the continued global spread of multidrug-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii, a critical-priority pathogen frequently implicated in severe infections among immunocompromised hosts[7]. This organism exhibits remarkable genetic adaptability, environmental persistence, and resistance to carbapenems and other broad-spectrum agents, severely limiting therapeutic options[7,8].

The intersection of expanding arboviral threats and pan-resistant bacterial pathogens signals a looming post-antibiotic era. Two specific policy priorities are especially urgent. First, national public health agencies should establish integrated One Health surveillance networks that co-monitor emerging arboviruses and AMR trends simultaneously, enabling early detection of the secondary bacterial resistance patterns that follow arboviral outbreaks in immunocompromised populations. Second, WHO member states and the Global Antibiotic Research and Development Partnership should expand pull-incentive mechanisms, including market entry rewards and priority review vouchers targeted at novel agents active against pan-resistant ESKAPE pathogens such as A. baumannii. Without such targeted interventions, routine medical procedures, including cancer chemotherapy, organ transplantation, and complex surgeries, may become increasingly perilous.
