Inclusion Complexation of Oxymetazoline Hydrochloride and Xylometazoline Hydrochloride with Cucurbit[n]urils (N = 7, 8): Experimental and Theoretical Study
Authors: Alia H. ALBattashi, FakhrEldin O. Suliman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper reports the preparation and analysis of inclusion complexes of oxymetazoline (OXY) and xylometazoline (XYL) with two cucurbit­[n]­uril (n = 7, 8) (CB­[n]) hosts. Using electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), the formation of 1:1 guest:host complexes was confirmed. The solid complexes were characterized for their thermal stability using thermogravimetry/differential scanning calorimetry (TGA/DSC) analysis and their crystallinity by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis. 1H NMR spectroscopy and diffusion-ordered spectroscopy (DOSY) analysis were used to study and compare the behavior of the guests, hosts, and inclusion complexes in aqueous media. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations employing the ωB97X-D/6–31G­(d) method were performed to elucidate the interaction mechanism between the guests and hosts and to evaluate the factors that influence complex formation. The results indicate that OXY and XYL form stable complexes with CB[7] and CB[8]. The formation of these complexes is spontaneous and enthalpy-driven, as suggested by the calculated thermodynamic properties. van der Waals forces mainly stabilize these complexes, along with weak to moderate hydrogen bonding interactions and ion-dipole interactions.

Introduction

Supramolecular chemistry has been a major focus of research over the past few decades. , At its core is host–guest chemistry, which drives the formation of various distinct structural complexes between two or more molecules or ions through noncovalent interactions. These interactions, including hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, electrostatic forces, van der Waals forces, and hydrophobic/hydrophilic attractions, have sparked a resurgence of interest in supramolecular assemblies. , These structures are characterized by straightforward and cost-effective preparation methods, avoiding complex synthetic and purification steps. Additionally, supramolecular assemblies can be produced under ambient conditions, making them environmentally friendly.

Additionally, they provide reversible pathways due to their weak and dynamic interactions, which enable easy dissociation and reassembly. The host–guest interactions involving macrocyclic molecules are among the many noncovalent interactions that have been thoroughly studied. Over the last three decades, several macrocyclic molecules and their derivatives have been developed and extensively used. This includes cyclodextrins (CD), pillar­[n]­arenes, calixarenes, cucurbit­[n]­urils (CBn) and, crown ethers. − Cucurbit­[n]­urils (n = 5–8, 10), are characterized by having a hydrophobic cavity accessible through two portals containing carbonyl groups, which allow the formation of inclusion or exclusion complexes via a combination of ion-dipole, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions. The straightforward synthesis from inexpensive starting materials, combined with high chemical and thermal stability, as well as low toxicity, makes them attractive as receptors for molecular recognition and building blocks for the construction of supramolecular materials. − The investigation of interactions between cucurbiturils and various biomolecules is crucial for the development of new functional materials, selective sensors, and therapeutics, as well as controlled release of agrochemicals and efficient drug delivery systems based on these supramolecular host molecules. −

The imidazoline pharmaceuticals are sympathomimetic vasoconstrictors of ocular or nasal arterioles. Oxymetazoline (OXY) and xylometazoline (XYL) (Figure ) are members of this family. These drugs are characterized by an aromatic nucleus and an imidazoline side group and are commonly used for nasal and ophthalmic decongestion and vasoconstriction. The mechanism of the operation of these compounds is that they act on α1 and α2 adrenergic receptors, resulting in the predominance of α1 adrenergic stimulation. These compounds are not recommended for oral ingestion because of their toxicity. Because of their lipophilicity, they penetrate the nasal mucus to exert their effect. On the other hand, in solution they exist as cationic imidazolium ions, with a pK a value in the range 8.5–10.5. , These structural characteristics qualify these compounds as suitable guests for inclusion into the cucurbit­[n]­urils with a bigger cavity, such as CB[7] and CB[8].

Chemical structures of the guests, OXY and XYL, and the hosts.

The inclusion complexes of some imidazoline drugs with β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) were investigated using fluorescence spectroscopy and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). The formation of a 1:1 complex between the guest and the host was confirmed using fluorescence titration. These complexes were found to be highly stable to photodegradation compared to the free drugs, resulting in an extension of their shelf life. The formation of inclusion complexes with chemical compounds is an effective molecular encapsulation process that protects light-sensitive agents from photodegradation. The antibiotic drug ciprofloxacin has been reported to form a stable complex with the macrocyclic host CB[7], as studied by fluorescence and 1H NMR spectroscopy. The supramolecular interactions of ciprofloxacin with the CB[7] macrocycle have been suggested to enhance the drug’s photostability in formulations and drug delivery systems. The encapsulation of many guests has led to significant changes in the molecular properties of these compounds, enhancing their biological efficacy and increasing their shelf life. Additionally, cucurbit­[n]­uril hosts can influence the properties of drugs; for example, inclusion complexes can become more soluble, bioavailable, and more resistant to metabolic conditions compared to individual noncomplexed molecules. , Generally, cytotoxicity and biocompatibility are essential criteria that promote a given host for promising medicinal applications. Complexation of piroxicam (PX) with CB[7] has significantly increased its bioavailability compared to its complex with β-CD and also exhibited milder side effects, indicating that CB[7] can be used as a favorable, safer excipient. Supramolecular nanoparticles comprising CB[8] and β-CD, which carry doxorubicin hydrochloride, exhibit significantly superior anticancer activity after irradiation with near-infrared light, acting as a favorable platform for disease diagnosis and treatment.

Inspired by these reports, we present an investigation into the inclusion complexation of two imidazoline drugs, OXY and XYL (Figure ), with cucurbit­[n]­urils (n = 7, 8). In this work, we will demonstrate the preparation and characterization of inclusion complexes of these two structurally similar guests, OXY and XYL, with CB[7] and CB[8]. Our goal is to establish fundamental results that will serve as a foundation for future research into formulation strategies for these drugs, ultimately leading to medications with improved bioavailability and longer shelf life. Additionally, we report the use of molecular dynamics (MD) simulation and DFT calculations to study the interaction mechanisms between these guests and hosts and to evaluate the structural differences in the stability of the complexes.

Experimental Section

General

All chemicals used in this study were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Ultrapure water was used to prepare all solutions. CB­[n] were synthesized using the same method reported in the literature, with some modifications. A mixture of CB­[n] was produced, which was then separated through fractional crystallization based on differences in solubility in various solvents. Further purification was achieved by recrystallization. The prepared compounds were analyzed using spectroscopic techniques. However, CB[7] and CB[8] still contain traces of the other CB­[n].

Preparation of Solid Inclusion Complexes and Physical Mixtures

The solid-state inclusion complexes were prepared by freeze-drying a 1:1 molar ratio mixture of hosts and guests. An appropriate amount of each of CB[7] and CB[8], along with 25 mg of guest (OXY and XYL), was dissolved in a suitable amount of deionized water. The mixtures were placed in a shaker incubator (IKA KS 4000i Control Incubating Shaker, Cole-Parmer) and incubated for 48 h at 40 °C. The inclusion complex solutions were frozen overnight in an ultralow temperature freezer (Panasonic, Japan) at −70 °C and then dried in a freeze-dryer (Lyo Quest, Telstar) for 48 h. The resulting solid inclusion complexes were kept in a desiccator for further analysis.

The physical mixtures were prepared by grinding the solid guest and host at a 1:1 mol ratio in a pestle and mortar. The resultant mixtures were kept in a desiccator for further analysis. For ESI-MS analysis, a 1:1 molar ratio of hosts and guests’ solutions was prepared in deionized water. The NMR analysis of the solutions of pure hosts and guests, as well as their 1:1 molar mixtures, was carried out in D2O or DMSO-d6.

Fluorescence Spectra

Fluorescence spectra were recorded using a PerkinElmer fluorometer with excitation wavelength fixed at 285 nm, and the emission spectra were scanned between 300 and 470 nm. The stock solutions of the drugs were prepared at a 1.0 mM concentration in deionized water. CB[7] solution of 1.0 mM was prepared in deionized water. On the other hand, CB[8], due to its poor solubility in water, was prepared in an 80 mM NaCl solution. A series of solutions containing a fixed concentration of OXY or XYL (10 μM) and different concentrations of the individual hosts (0–0.6 mM) was used for the fluorescence measurements.

Instrumentation

The solid samples of guests, hosts, their inclusion complexes (IC), and physical mixtures (PM) were characterized using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). Solution samples of the free forms and ICs were analyzed using MALDI-TOF, ESI-MS (1:1 molar ratio), and 1D/2D NMR (1H NMR, COSY, DOSY).

PXRD was performed using a Panalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer (Cu source, 45 kV, 40 mA) over a 2θ range of 5–70°, with a sample spinner and X-pert software. TGA/DSC analyses were conducted on an SDT Q600 under nitrogen (100 mL/min) with a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 20 to 600 °C in sealed aluminum pans. MALDI-TOF was carried out on a Bruker UltraFlextreme in the m/z range 100–4000 Da, utilizing α-Cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (HCCA) as the matrix. A 2.00 μL matrix solution in a 30:70 ACN/TFA (0.1%) mixture was combined with 2.00 μL of the sample, with 0.50 μL of the mixture spotted and dried on a steel target. Data collection was performed with FlexControl software (v3.3). ESI-MS spectra were acquired using an Agilent 6460 LC/MS system, with a capillary voltage of 3.5 kV, a gas temperature of 300 °C, and a flow rate of 5.00 L/min. The injection volume was 5.00 μL, and data were processed with MassHunter software. NMR spectroscopy was performed on a Bruker 700 MHz instrument using zg30 for 1H-NMR, along with additional COSY and DOSY 2D experiments.

Molecular Modeling

Figure shows the structure of the hosts and guests included in this study. The density functional theory (DFT) method, using the ωB97X-D functional with a 6–31G­(d) basis set, was employed to optimize the geometry of the guests, hosts, and their inclusion complexes. This is a long-range corrected hybrid density functional that includes empirical dispersion corrections and has been shown to perform well for noncovalent interactions. The initial structures of CB[7] and CB[8] were obtained from the crystallographic parameters provided by the Structural Data Base System of the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Center. They were initially optimized by minimizing their energy using the PM6 semiempirical method, followed by the DFT method in the gas phase.

The Desmond molecular simulations package, as included in the Schrödinger 2018 suite of programs, was used for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with the optimized structures of the guests, hosts, and their complexes. , In this study, the OPLS-2005 all-atom force field with explicit solvent (TIP3P water model) was used throughout the calculations. Simulations were performed with periodic boundary conditions in an orthorhombic box, with the solute positioned in the center, 10 Å away from each box edge. The SHAKE algorithm was applied to constrain covalent bonds between hydrogen and heavy atoms. Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled using the Ewald smooth particle mesh (PME) method.

The solvated molecules underwent sequential restraint solvent–solute minimizations and short restraint and unrestraint MD simulations in NVT and NPT ensembles (each for 12 ps) followed by a 24 ps equilibration step (as implemented in the default relaxation protocol in Desmond), coupled with the Berendsen thermostat. Finally, a production run of 50–70 ns was performed in NPT at 300 K and 1 bar.

Quantum Mechanics Calculation

In this study, we employed the Gaussian 09 package to perform all calculations [35]. GaussView 5.0 was used to generate molecular graphics and set up the calculations [36]. The geometries of the guests, hosts, and complexes were initially optimized using the PM6 semiempirical method, followed by DFT computations employing the ωB97X-D dispersion-corrected density functional with the 6–31G­(d) basis set. The energies evaluated here, using the basis set, are basis set superposition error (BSSE)-corrected. At the same time, we conducted vibrational analysis to verify the presence of true minima in all cases by checking for the absence of imaginary frequencies. Additionally, the vibrational calculations allowed us to determine the zero-point energy (ZPE), as well as the enthalpic (H) and free energy (G) corrections to the electronic energy, based on statistical thermodynamics. The variations in thermodynamic functions of the complexes (ΔH, ΔS, and ΔG) have been calculated at a temperature of 298.15 K and a pressure of 1 atm for the gas phase. The complexation energy ΔE complex was obtained as the energy difference between the guest–host complex (E complex) and the respective individual hosts (E CB[n]) and guests (E guest) molecules. ΔEcomplex=Ecomplex−(ECB[n]+Eguest)

The deformation energy E deform is calculated as the difference between the single point energy of the component in the optimized complex (E in‑complex) and its energy in free form (E free). ΔEdeform=Ein‐complex−Efree

The nature of bonding in the inclusion complexes of OXY and XYL with the two hosts was examined using topological analysis based on the quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM). These analyses were conducted using the scripts developed by Liu et al. as part of the Multiwfn package. The bond critical points (BCPs) were determined based on their electron density (ρ­(r)), Laplacian (∇2ρ­(r)), total electron energy density (H(r)), potential electron energy density (V(r)), and Lagrangian kinetic energy (G(r)).

The noncovalent interaction (NCI) and reduced density gradient (RDG) analyses were carried out using the Multiwfn program. Visualization of interactions between guest and host molecules was performed using VMD and Gnuplot software. , In the NCI isosurfaces, blue, green, and red colors represent stabilizing H-bonding, van der Waals, and destabilizing steric interactions, respectively.

Similar results were observed in the ESI-MS spectrum of XYL with CB[7] (Figure S1a). The ion peaks at m/z 1408.10 and 848.50, represents [XYL @CB[7]+H]+ and [2XYL @CB[7]+2Na]2+, respectively. Conversely, the ESI-MS spectrum of the inclusion complex of XYL with CB[8] shows an ion peak at 1574.10 m/z, indicating the presence of [XYL @CB[8]+H]+ (Figure S1b). The guest molecule exhibits a singly charged ion [XYL+H]+ at 245.30 m/z. These results suggest the formation of association complexes between the two guests and the two hosts. Interestingly, all the experimental m/z values of the molecular ions match the calculated m/z values (Table ). The cavity of CB[7] is too small to accommodate two bulky molecules like OXY and XYL; therefore, the peaks in the ESI-mass spectrum corresponding to [2OXY @CB[7]+2H]2+ and [2OXY @CB[7]+2H]2+ indicate that the formation of external complexes cannot be ruled out. The outer surface of the convex side of CB­[n] is electrostatically positive as determined by theoretical calculations. In addition, CB­[n] compounds are capable of outer-surface interactions through their methine or methylene groups on the outer surface, forming a special type of hydrogen bonding. The outer surface of CB­[n] can undergo π···π stacking, C–H···π interactions, and electrostatic interactions. The two guest molecules contain aromatic rings as well as a positively charged imidazole moiety, which can allow for strong interactions with the CB­[n] hosts. This is more likely to lead to the formation of stable adducts in the mass spectrometer. −

We also analyzed mixtures of guests and hosts using MALDI-TOF spectrometry. The results shown in Figure S2 and summarized in Table clearly demonstrate that both guests form complexes with all CB­[n] (n = 6–8). For example, the interaction of OXY with CB­[n] produces signals at m/z 1257.752, 1423.892, and 1590.093, indicating the formation of stable complexes with CB[6], CB[7], and CB[8], respectively. Similar results are observed for XYL interacting with a mixture of CB­[n]. The signals at m/z 1242.095, 1408.175, and 1574.312 correspond to ions of complexes with CB[6], CB[7], and CB[8], respectively. When using pure CB[7], only signals related to OXY and XYL complexes with CB[7] are observed. The observation of signals for the two hosts with CB[6], which possesses a narrow cavity to accommodate the guests, indicates that the formation of exclusion complexes is also possible, as explained above. These results corroborate the findings by ESI-MS, indicating the formation of stable adducts between CB­[n] and the two guests. It has been previously reported that the presence of stable complexes in the gas phase indicates their existence in the aqueous phase. −

1H NMR

The 1H NMR spectra provide valuable insights into the interaction between guests and hosts. Unlike cyclodextrins, cucurbiturils lack protons in their cavity; therefore, the proton resonances of the guest molecule offer essential information about the potential position of the guest within the host cavity. It is known that guest protons located in the shielding region of the cavity experience upfield shifts (Δδ < 0; Δδ= δbound – δfree), whereas the deshielded guest protons at the carbonyl oxygens of the portals undergo downfield shifts (Δδ > 0). The 1H NMR spectra of OXY and OXY@CB[7] are shown in Figure S3. The spectrum displays only a slight upfield shift of the signals for the t-butyl group (H1) and the aromatic proton (H5). Conversely, a slight downfield shift is observed for the methyl groups of the phenol (H2) and the methylene group connecting the two rings. No shift is seen for the protons of the imidazoline ring. These results suggest that OXY forms a weak complex with CB[7] by inserting the t-butyl groups and part of the aromatic ring into the cavity while leaving the rest of the molecule outside.

An upfield shift was observed in the signals of all the protons of OXY@CB[8] compared to the free guest (Figure ). The maximum shift was observed for the aromatic proton (Δδ H5, −0.024 ppm) followed by the methyl (Δδ H2, −0.022 ppm) and the methylene (Δδ H3, 0.019 ppm).

1H NMR (700 MHz, D2O) spectra of OXY (lower trace) and OXY@CB[8] (upper trace).

Similar results were observed for XYL complexes with CB[7] and CB[8] (Figure S4). The maximum upfield shift occurs for the t-butyl (Δδ H1, −0.014 ppm) and the aromatic proton (Δδ H5, −0.012 ppm). Conversely, for the XYL@CB[8] complex, the guest protons experience upfield shifts across all protons, with the aromatic proton showing the most significant shift (H5, 0.041 ppm). It is worth noting that the 1H NMR signals of the guest in the presence of both hosts are notably broadened, and their intensity is significantly decreased. From these results, we infer that the guests insert only the t-butyl group into the cavity of the two hosts, while CB[8] additionally includes H5 into its cavity. Simultaneously, the rest of the molecule remains outside the host due to the steric hindrance caused by the two methyl groups on the meta-position. The large size of CB[8] allows for the insertion of bulky guests into its cavity. It is worth mentioning here that small changes in the chemical shifts of the guests in these complexes may indicate the formation of exclusion complexes.

Interestingly, the 1H NMR spectra of both guests with CB[8] show an additional peak downfield assigned to the imidazolium NH protons. This peak was not observed in the spectra of pure guests or their complexes with CB[7] due to the fast H/D exchange rate in D2O. The appearance of this signal in the presence of CB[8] is interpreted as being due to the lower H/D exchange rate resulting from the involvement of this group in complexation with CB[8]. Similar results have been reported in the literature for the inclusion of imidazolium compounds with CB[7] and CB[8]. , We further ran a 1H NMR of oxymetazoline in DMSO-d6, and the results clearly show a signal at 8.1 ppm (Figure S5). These results support the formation of inclusion complexes between OXY and XYL with CB[8].

Furthermore, using 2D-DOSY, we determined the diffusion coefficients of the two guests both with and without the hosts (Figure S6), and the results are summarized in Table S1. It is evident from this table that the diffusion coefficients of OXY and XYL decrease in the presence of the hosts. A greater reduction in diffusion coefficients occurs for both guest molecules when in the presence of the heavier host CB[8]. This indicates an increase in the hydrodynamic radius of OXY and XYL upon complexation with the two hosts, suggesting the formation of a supramolecular complex.

Fluorescence Analysis

Figures and S7 demonstrate that OXY and XYL display weak fluorescence spectra in water, with a peak at 365 nm when excited at 285 nm. As CB[7] is added gradually, the fluorescence intensity of this peak increases, and a new peak appears at 420 nm, which also significantly grows with higher CB[7] concentrations. Encapsulating the drug within the hydrophobic cavity of CB­[n] enhances fluorescence by restricting the movement of guest molecules in the excited state. For CB[7] complexes, the ion-dipole interaction between the carbonyl groups at the portal and the imidazolium group is essential in stabilizing the excited state, causing a bathochromic shift of the fluorescence band. This effect is not seen in CB[8] because of the larger distance between the imidazolium group and the carbonyl groups. Using nonlinear least-squares methods, the fluorescence intensity was found to fit the 1:1 guest:host equation: I=I0+IcK[CB[n]]01+K[CB[n]]0 where I 0 and I c represent the fluorescence intensity in pure water and in the complex, respectively, and I is the fluorescence intensity at a specific CB­[n] concentration. The results summarized in Table indicate that reasonably stable complexes are formed. Interestingly, the complexes display comparable stabilities. Generally, XYL complexes, compared to OXY complexes of the two hosts, show higher binding constants.

PXRD Analysis

The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) technique was used to analyze the crystallinity of the hosts, imidazoline guests, and their complexes. The PXRD patterns of the OXY, and XYL (Figure S8) are compared to those simulated from single-crystal X-ray data. , The experimental patterns for the two guests show excellent similarities with the calculated ones.

The diffraction patterns of OXY and XYL (Figure S9) reflect the crystalline nature of the two guest compounds. Interestingly, OXY and XYL display similar PXRD patterns, with the most intense peaks at 2θ values of 6.1° and 6.4°, respectively. The other strong peaks for OXY are at 2θ ∼12.5°, 18.9°, 23.8°, and 32.0°. XYL exhibits strong peaks at 11.9°, 14.8°, 15.7°, and 19.0°. The PXRD results of the guests, hosts, their physical mixtures (PM), and the lyophilized complexes (IC) are shown in Figures and S10. As expected, the diffraction pattern of the as-produced CB[7] exhibits a halo structure reflecting its amorphous nature. As reported in the literature, it is not easy to crystallize CB[7] and only a few crystals with CB[7] inclusion complexes have been reported. On the other hand, the diffraction reflections of CB[8] show crystalline structures with peaks at 2θ ∼10.0–25.0° similar to those reported by Bardelang et al. The PXRD patterns of the physical mixtures and the inclusion complexes are compared to those of the pure guest and host in Figures and S10 for OXY and XYL, respectively. The physical mixtures of the guests with CB[7] and CB[8] show overlapped patterns of the guest and the host reflections. This is well illustrated in the case of the crystalline host, CB[8], where the reflections of both the host and the guest are observed in the PM patterns. For both guests, the reflections of the inclusion complexes with CB[7] exhibit patterns similar to those of the host. However, a strong peak of XYL at 6.4 is observed protruding from the halo structure pattern of the complex, indicating the presence of traces of the pure guest within the complex. However, for the complexes of both guests with CB[8], some broad peaks are also observed on top of the amorphous structures. For example, an intense broad peak at 2θ ∼5.6° is observed in the diffraction patterns of OXY@CB[7], which is absent from the patterns of the guest and the host. Moreover, the patterns of ICs of both guests with CB[8] show broad beaks mixed with halo structures. These results suggest that an inclusion complex is obtained only upon mixing the hosts and the guests in the aqueous phase, followed by lyophilization, and not by merely mixing the guests and the hosts.

Thermal Analysis

Using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), the thermal behavior of OXY, XYL, CB­[n], and their complexes was examined. The results shown in Figure indicate that the TGA traces of CB[7] and CB[8] starting materials are similar. The weight losses of 17% for CB[7] and 30% for CB[8] from ambient temperature up to 200 °C are in agreement with those reported in the literature. These changes are attributed to the loss of water and the solvent used for drying the samples, as well as to residual HCl molecules. Brandelang et al. suggested the presence of 15 water molecules and 4 HCl molecules absorbed by a molecule of CB[8] using single X-ray crystallography. The thermal decomposition of CB[7] and CB[8] is characterized by an abrupt weight loss above 300–350 °C with a DTG max value of ∼400 °C.

Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) traces of OXY, CB[7], and OXY@CB[7] complex (left panel) and OXY@CB[8] (right panel).

The pure guest compounds also exhibit comparable TGA traces, with no weight loss from room temperature to 200 °C, indicating that both compounds are anhydrous (Figures and S11). The weight loss for the two guest molecules was observed to start at 200 °C, signaling their decomposition. The TGA traces of OXY and XYL lyophilized complexes with CB[7] show an initial weight loss of 16% from room temperature up to 180 °C, corresponding to the dehydration of the complex. This is followed by an endothermic phase change, accompanied by a 10.5% weight loss, and subsequently by a significant 49.3% weight loss, indicating the decomposition of these compounds. Similar results were observed for OXY and XYL complexes with CB[8], where the decomposition of the complex starts at temperatures close to the decomposition temperature of the host compound.

More information about the formation of complexes between guest and host molecules is also obtained from DSC studies. The complexation of the guest with the host may result in a change or shift in the intensity of the peaks and the disappearance of melting, boiling, or sublimation points observed in the DSC thermogram of the pure substances. , The DSC curves for OXY, XYL, CB[7], CB[8], and their complexes exhibit different features (Figures and S11). The DSC curve of OXY shows a sharp endothermic peak at around 310 °C, suggesting its melting point (301.5 °C) and crystalline nature. As expected, CB[7] exhibits an endothermic peak at 87 °C corresponding to the removal of water from the cavitand and an endothermic-exothermic peak at 382 and 420 °C attributed to the melting followed by decomposition of the compound. However, for the OXY@CB[7] complex, the sharp endothermic peak that appeared at around ∼310 °C for OXY disappeared, and a broad exothermic peak at 397 °C, indicative of the decomposition of the complex, is observed. The dehydration peak of the pure CB[8] host is at 110 °C and extends up to 220 °C. The OXY complex with CB[8] shows slightly different DSC behavior where a broad endothermic peak at 89 °C that expands from room temperature to 180 °C is connected to the dehydration of the complex. The complex shows a phase change at 300 °C, followed by an exothermic peak at 418 °C, similar to that shown in the CB[8] trace, corresponding to the decomposition of the host molecule.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) traces of OXY, CB[7], and OXY@CB[7] complex (left panel) and OXY@CB[8] (right panel).

The DSC results of XYL, the two hosts, and their complexes (Figure S11) show a sharp endothermic peak (black line) at 334 °C, indicating its melting point (332 °C). Like OXY, XYL does not show any peak between room temperature and its melting point, signifying its anhydrous nature. The XYL@CB[7] complex trace exhibits phase change at 275 °C, followed by an exothermic peak at 411 °C, which indicates the decomposition of the complex. Similar results were observed for XYL@CB[8] complex. These results confirm that encapsulation of these imidazoline drugs into the cavity of the cucurbituril hosts imparts greater thermal stability of the complexed drug compared to the pure form. Therefore, encapsulation into nanocavities of such cavitands would allow heating the complexed forms to temperatures of up to 250 °C for drying during the preparation of formulations of these compounds, and this would increase their stability during storage.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

To further examine the stability of these complexes, we conducted molecular dynamics (MD) simulations on OXY and XYL binary complexes with both hosts in aqueous media. The complexes were immersed in an orthorhombic box of TIP3P water, and calculations were performed for 50–70 ns at a pressure of 1 atm and a temperature of 298 K. The evolution of the root-mean-square deviations (RMSDs) with time for all complexes is shown in Figure S12. Clearly, all complexes show stable trajectories throughout the simulation time. Moreover, we also calculated the radius of gyration (r gyr) of the guests, hosts, and their corresponding inclusion complexes from the generated trajectories over the equilibrium time. Table summarizes the results of the average RMSDs and r gyr. These results show that the values of the r gyr for each of the complexes are close to those of the corresponding host. Additionally, lower values of r gyr were obtained for the inclusion complexes compared to the sum of the individual host and guest components. These results indicate the compactness of the complexes, suggesting the formation of stable compounds between OXY and XYL with CB[7] and CB[8].

DFT Analysis

The minimum energy complexes obtained by PM6 semiempirical calculations for both guests with CB[7] and CB[8] are associated with the encapsulation of the guests within the host’s cavity. These geometries were further refined using DFT/ωB97X-D/6–31G­(d) with empirical dispersion corrections, which are known to perform better for systems with noncovalent interactions. In the most stable complexes, the t-butyl group is positioned inside the cavity, with the guests more deeply inserted into the CB[8] cavity compared to CB[7].

The complexation energy ΔE complex values are calculated as the energy difference between the complex and the sum of the energies of the separate free guest and free host molecules. The value and sign of the energy reflect the stability of the system. The complexation energies computed for the inclusion complexes of OXY and XYL with both hosts in the gas phase are shown in Table . These energies were corrected for BSSE using the method proposed by Boys and Bernardi. The negative values of the complexation energies reported in Table confirm the formation of stable complexes. The complexes of both guests with CB[8] exhibit higher complexation energies compared to those with CB[7]. The stronger interaction of both guests with CB[8] is due to the better fit of OXY and XYL into the wider cavity of the CB[8] host, resulting in higher stabilization energies compared to their inclusion within the CB[7] cavity. Therefore, the encapsulation of both guests into CB[8] occurs smoothly by inserting the guest molecules deeply inside the cavity, positioning them to maximize interactions with the host (ion-dipole interaction, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals interaction). This observation resonates reasonably with the 1H NMR and MD results discussed above.

To gain further insight into the interactions between OXY and XYL with the two hosts, we used the quantum theory of atoms in molecules (QTAIM) to examine the topological properties of these complexes [57]. This theory has gained increasing attention recently and has been widely used to understand weak to strong noncovalent interactions in inclusion complexes [58–61]. The molecular topological graphs are shown in Figure S20. A careful look at these figures reveals that the bond paths are mostly straight lines, but some are slightly curved between the CB­[n] and the two guests.

In QTAIM, the topological analysis of the electronic charge density (ρ­(r)) and its Laplacian (∇2 ρ (r)) at bond critical points (BCPs) is used to characterize bonds or interactions. A BCP is a point on the bond path where the gradient vector of ρ vanishes (∇ρ­(r)=0); it defines the existence of a chemical bond or interaction between two nuclei. The value of the electron density usually determines the strength of the bond. On the other hand, the Laplacian of ρ­(r) (∇2ρ) at the BCP helps characterize the nature of the bond or interaction based on information about the charge concentration or depletion at the BCP. In covalent bonds, the electronic charge is accumulated between the nuclei; therefore, ρ­(r) is large (>0.2 a.u) and ∇2ρ­(r) < 0 and large. On the other hand, in noncovalent interactions such as van der Waals interactions (VdW) and hydrogen bonds, ρ­(r) is small (<0.1 a.u) and ∇2ρ > 0. In this work, we will primarily focus on the hydrogen bonding interactions of the types CH–O, OH–O, and NH–O. The results of the topological descriptors are presented in Tables S2–S5, and their cumulative values are summarized in Table for the inclusion complexes. These parameters include, in addition to ρ and ∇2ρ, the local kinetic energy density G(r), local potential energy density V­(r), local electron energy density H­(r) at the BCP, and the ellipticity ε.

The electronic charge density (ρ­(r)) and its Laplacian (∇2ρ­(r)) at the bond critical points (BCPs) are positive in all the complexes, confirming the noncovalent bonding for the intramolecular interactions between the guests and the hosts. For the OXY@CB[7] complex, the highest values of ρ­(r) and its ∇2ρ­(r) were obtained for the NH–OC hydrogen bonding interaction. For the OXY@CB[8] complex, ρ­(r) values range between 0.002 and 0.0165, indicating weaker hydrogen bonding interactions compared to the complex with CB[7]. Moreover, the NH–OC interaction exhibits the highest ρ­(r) value, followed by the OH–OC interaction. A similar trend was observed for the values of ∇2ρ­(r) for the XYL@CB[8] complex.

It is worth noting that the total number of hydrogen bonding interactions of all types is higher for OXY@CB[8], contributing 26.0 kcal mol–1 to its stabilization energy compared to the OXY@CB[7] complex, where they contribute 18.3 kcal mol–1 to the stabilization energy (Table ). These energies were calculated using the methods proposed by Emamian et al. On the other hand, the XYL@CB[7] complex has values of ρ­(r) and its Laplacian ranging from 0.0035 to 0.0241 au and from 0.0129 to 0.0709 au, respectively. Similar behavior was observed for XYL@CB[8] with values of ρ­(r) and ∇2ρ of 0.032–0.213 and 0.0122–0.0656 au. Moreover, the ratio of |G(r)|/V(r) is greater than or equal to 1, ranging from 0.984 to 1.672, which indicates the dispersive nature of these interactions. According to Nakanishi et al. interactions with 0 < ρ­(r) < 0.01 and 0 < ∇2ρ­(r) < 0.04 au are considered van der Waals in nature, while those with 0.01 < ρ­(r) < 0.04, and 0.04 < ∇2ρ­(r) < 0.12 au are considered hydrogen bonding. Using these criteria, it is found that the NH–OC and the OH–OC bonds exhibit hydrogen bonding interactions, whereas most of the CH–OC interactions between guests and hosts are van der Waals interactions. The collective energy contributions of all these weak interactions lead to the formation of stable complexes.

The noncovalent interactions-reduced density gradient analysis (NCI-RDG) provides a platform for better visualization of hydrogen bonds, van der Waals interactions, and repulsive steric effects using graphical representation. The reduced density gradient is related to the electron density (ρ­(r)) and its Laplacian (∇2 ρ­(r)). The Laplacian (∇2ρ­(r)) can be decomposed into three eigenvalues (λ1 ≤ λ2≤ λ3) in its Hessian. Plots of RDG versus (sign λ2) ρ, where (sign λ2)­ρ is the electron density multiplied by the sign of the second Hessian eigenvalue (λ2) provide helpful information about the type of interactions. For instance, repulsive interactions are characterized by (sign λ2) ρ > 0, and for attractive interactions (sign λ2) ρ > 0, such as the presence of noncovalent bonding. The NCI-RDG plots for OXY and XYL complexes with the two hosts are shown in Figures and S21, respectively. The troughs near density values, at (sign λ2) ρ −0.01–0.01 au Indicates van der Waals interactions, whereas the spikes between (sign λ2) ρ −0.01 and −0.02 au corresponds to the hydrogen bond interactions. The spike at (sign λ2) ρ = 0.04 au, which exists in all complexes at the same position, originates from the steric repulsion observed in the glycoluril group.

Conclusion

In this work, OXY and XYL complexes with CB[7] and CB[8] hosts were prepared and characterized using both experimental and theoretical techniques. Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS) and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry results indicate that the two guests form 1:1 complexes with the two hosts. The 1H NMR spectroscopy confirmed the formation of stable inclusion complexes, where the guests are partially inserted into the cavity of CB[7] compared to CB[8]. Additionally, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and thermal gravimetric/differential scanning calorimetry (TGA/DSC) studies suggest the formation of stable complexes between OXY/XYL and the two hosts. Molecular dynamic simulations of the inclusion complexes in aqueous media show that the t-butyl group and part of the aromatic ring are inserted into the cavity of CB­[7], while the imidazoline group remains outside the cavity. The simulation results indicate that the two guests insert deeper into the cavity of CB[8], supporting the 1H NMR findings. By analyzing the hydrogen bond interactions between hosts and guests throughout the simulation, it has been inferred that, in addition to van der Waals and ion-dipole interactions, hydrogen bonding also contributes to the stability of these complexes. The binding energies calculated via DFT/B97X-D/6–31G­(d) on the complexes in the gas phase confirm their stability. The high enthalpy change (H) values for all complexes suggest an enthalpy-driven process dominated by van der Waals interactions. The negative and significant Gibbs free energy values indicate that the complexation process is spontaneous. The formation of inclusion complexes between OXY and XYL with CB[7] and CB[8] provides foundational information for future drug formulation development and suggests that these complexes could effectively protect these compounds from environmental factors such as temperature, thereby increasing their shelf life.
