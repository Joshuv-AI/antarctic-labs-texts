Ancestral genomic functional differences in oligodendroglia: implications for Alzheimer's disease

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a devastating neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive decline and memory loss. Approximately 11% of people aged ≥65 years have AD, and this percentage increases with age, reaching 13% of people aged 75 to 84 years and 33% of people aged 85 years or older. Although age is the strongest risk factor for AD development, genetics plays a critical role in driving AD risk. To date, more than 100 genetic loci have been recognized as predisposing factors for AD through genome‐wide association studies (GWASs). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Most of these studies focused on data acquired from non‐Hispanic White individuals, with African (AF), African American, and Hispanic/Latino populations being largely underrepresented. 9 Recently, expanding the representation of these populations in genetic studies has been a growing area of focus, driven in large part by large multicenter collaborations, such as the Alzheimer's Disease Sequencing Project (ADSP). 10 Both Hispanic/Latino and African American populations are admixed populations. Hispanic/Latino individuals have differing combinations of European (EU), Amerindian (AI), and AF ancestry, while African Americans have primarily AF and EU ancestry. As such, expanding the representation of other genomic compositions in genomic studies is imperative, as there is clear evidence of differences in risk loci and effect sizes of risk alleles shared across populations. For example, the ε4 allele of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene represents the most prominent genetic susceptibility factor for late‐onset AD, exerting the most pronounced effect on East Asian (EA) populations (Japanese), followed by non‐Hispanic White (EU) populations, with a comparatively smaller effect in populations of AF descent. 11 , 12 , 13 The mechanisms underlying these differences have been shown to be due to ancestry‐specific variations in the genomic regulatory architecture (GRA) of the local ancestry surrounding APOE. 14 , 15 , 16 Notably, GRA is not only ancestry biased but also cell type specific.

While extensive research has focused on the role of neurons in AD pathogenesis, recent evidence suggests that glial cells, including oligodendrocytes, play crucial roles in the etiology of AD. Oligodendrocytes (OL), which are traditionally known for their role in myelinating axons in the central nervous system (CNS), 17 have emerged as key players in AD due to their involvement in various AD‐associated pathological processes, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and synaptic dysfunction. 18 For example, many AD risk genes, such as APP, ANK3, BACE1, BIN1, PICALM, PSEN1, and SORT1, are highly expressed in oligodendrocytes. Additionally, phosphorylated tau in gray matter has been associated with white matter abnormalities and demyelination in AD patients, with these abnormalities appearing prior to the onset of clinical AD symptoms, 19 supporting the importance of oligodendroglia in AD pathogenesis. For these reasons, we examined and compared the GRA of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)‐derived oligodendroglia from individuals with significant AF, AI, or EU global ancestry. Taken together, our results provide a resource for the AD research community to gain knowledge of genetic factors that may be specific to certain populations, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of this disease.

METHODS

Sample collection

Samples were obtained through genetic studies of AD in African Americans (R01AG072547 and U01‐AG052410), the Puerto Rico Alzheimer's Disease Initiative (PRADI), 20 , 21 and an ongoing study of AD in Peru (R01‐AG070864). 12 The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) of the University of Miami and the Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia (Lima, Peru). All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the University of Miami. Cognitive assessments (including the Stick Design test, UDS3 battery, IDEAS battery, Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR], Tanner telephone screen, and review of medical history) were conducted for all participants. These data were evaluated by a board‐certified neurologist (J.M.V.) and neuropsychologists (M.C., K.M., A.C‐H.) to determine clinical diagnoses. Family history was obtained, and all individuals underwent genotyping and whole‐genome sequencing (WGS). WGS data were analyzed to identify related individuals, and any such individuals were excluded from the study. To this end, we estimated pairwise genetic relatedness among samples using PLINK 2.0 22 by calculating identity‐by‐descent proportions, which quantify the degree of relatedness between individuals. Additionally, WGS was screened for known risk variants in AD‐associated genes, including APOE, ABCA7 deletion (Arg578Alafs*16), APP, PSEN1 and 2, SORL1, and TREM2. Individuals carrying pathogenic variants in these genes were excluded from the study.

Induced pluripotent stem cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted from whole blood using SepMate‐50 tubes with Lymphoprep media by density gradient centrifugation according to the manufacturer's protocol (STEMCELL Technologies). PBMCs from AD patients or age‐matched non‐demented controls were selected based on their global ancestry (>90% EU, AF, or AI) (Table S1) to be reprogrammed using the CTS™ CytoTune™‐iPS 2.1 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (CTS) CytoTune‐iPS 2.1 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (Invitrogen) to generate iPSCs in the Hussman Institute for Human Genomics iPSC Core Facility.

One iPSC clone per individual was selected based on colony morphology and expanded for downstream differentiation. All iPSC lines were fully characterized prior to use. Each line exhibited morphology consistent with undifferentiated human embryonic stem cells, including a high nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli, and compact colonies with well‐defined borders. Pluripotency was confirmed for all lines by immunocytochemical staining for the canonical markers nanog, oct4, and sox2 (Figure S1A).

To verify that pluripotency was maintained endogenously, the absence of Sendai virus (SeV)‐derived reprogramming vectors was confirmed in all lines by RT‐PCR. G‐banding cytogenetic analysis confirmed normal karyotypes with no chromosomal abnormalities (Figure S1B), and PCR‐based testing verified that all lines were free of mycoplasma contamination.

Global ancestry estimation

The admixture proportion was calculated using a model‐based clustering algorithm within ADMIXTURE software. 23 A supervised ADMIXTURE analysis was conducted at K = 4, incorporating the four reference populations (AI, EU, AF, and EA) from combined reference panels of the Human Genome Diversity Project (HGDP) 24 , 25 and 1000 Genomes Phase 3. 26 , 27

Derivation of oligodendrocyte‐containing spheroids

The iPSC‐derived oligodendrocytes underwent differentiation following a previously established protocol 28 with some minor modifications (Figure S2). Briefly, iPSC lines were cultured on vitronectin‐coated plates and maintained in Stemflex medium until they reached 70% to 90% confluency. These cultures were then dissociated into single cells using ACCUTASE. Once dissociated, the cells were plated on Matrigel‐coated six‐well plates at a density ranging from 5×105 to 1×106 cells per well in StemFlex medium supplemented with RevitaCell. The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 1 to 2 days until colonies with a diameter of approximately 100 to 250 µm were formed. Upon reaching the desired size, colonies were induced using Neural Induction Medium (NIM) supplemented with SB431542 (10 µM), LDN193189 (250 nM), or retinoic acid (100 nM) for 8 days, with daily complete media changes.

On day 8, the cells were transitioned to N2 medium (DMEM/F12 with L‐glutamine, N2 supplement [1×], non‐essential amino acids [NEAA], penicillin‐streptomycin [PenStrep], and 2‐mercaptoethanol) supplemented with retinoic acid (100 nM) and a smoothened agonist (1 µM). The cells were maintained in N2 medium for 4 days, with daily medium changes with the addition of fresh supplements. On day 12, overconfluent monolayers exhibiting three‐dimensional (3D) structures were analyzed by immunocytochemical staining for olig2 (ab109186, abcam) to assess the capacity for each line to produce oligodendroglia. The confluent monolayer was mechanically disrupted in a lattice‐like pattern using a scalpel to generate aggregates. The newly formed aggregates were detached from the wells using a cell lifter and plated on ultralow attachment plates at a 1:2 ratio with N2B27 medium (N2 medium with B27 supplement lacking vitamin A), supplemented with retinoic acid (100 nM) and a smoothened agonist (1 µM). The cells were cultured in N2B27 medium for 8 days, with 2/3 medium replacement every other day.

The cells were subsequently switched to PDGF medium (DMEM/F12 with L‐glutamine, N2 supplement (1×), NEAA, PenStrep, 2‐mercaptoethanol, PDGFaa (10 ng/mL), IGF‐1 (10 ng/mL), HGF (5 ng/mL), NT3 (10 ng/mL), T3 (60 ng/mL), biotin (100 ng/mL), cAMP (1 µM), and insulin 25 µg/mL) with 2/3 medium replacement every other day for 10 days. Round aggregates with a diameter ranging from 300 to 800 µM and a golden or brown appearance were selectively harvested and plated onto PLO/Laminin‐coated plates at a density of 20 aggregates per well of a six‐well plate, using Glial medium (DMEM/F12 with L‐glutamine, N2 supplement, NEAA, PenStrep, 2‐mercaptoethanol, HEPES, T3, biotin, cAMP, insulin, ascorbic acid). The aggregates were maintained in the PLO/Laminin plates for 45 days with 2/3 medium replacement every other day. On day 55, live staining for O4 (MAB1326, R&D Systems) was performed (Figure S1C), and O4‐positive cells were quantified to confirm similar differentiation rates into oligodendroglia for all lines.

Nuclei isolation for 10× chromium single‐cell multiome ATAC + gene expression (RNA‐seq)

On day 76 after the induction of differentiation, the cultures were harvested, and nuclear isolation was accomplished following a 10× protocol: “Nucleus isolation from complex tissues for single‐cell multiome ATAC + gene expression sequencing” with minor adjustments. Briefly, the cultures were detached from the plates using ACCUTASE at 37°C for 5 min and then washed with PBS + 0.04% BSA. The cell pellet was then resuspended in RDD buffer with DNAse I (0.1 U/µL) and incubated on ice for 5 min. After DNAse I treatment, the cells were washed twice with PBS + 0.04% BSA. The pellets were subsequently resuspended in NP40 lysis buffer, incubated on ice for 5 min, and homogenized using a pellet pestle with a cordless motor. The homogenate was then filtered through a 40‐µm strainer and pelleted. Nucleus resuspension buffer was added to the pellet, followed by a 5‐min incubation on ice. The pellet was then resuspended and washed twice with nucleus resuspension buffer. Lysis efficiency was assessed by staining with trypan blue and nuclei counted using the Countess II FL Automated Cell Counter. After verification that lysis had reached >95%, the nuclei were pelleted, resuspended in 0.1X lysis buffer, incubated on ice for 2 min, washed once with wash buffer, and resuspended in cold diluted nuclear buffer (1×). The number of nuclei was determined using a Cellometer (Nexcelom). The nuclei were diluted to a concentration of 5500 nuclei/µL for a target cell recovery of 10,000 nuclei. The diluted nuclei suspensions were used for 10× Chromium Single‐cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression library preparation according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Data analysis

To ensure data quality and reproducibility, we performed standard quality control (QC) assessments for both RNA‐sequencing and ATAC‐sequencing data across the neural spheroid cultures across. For the RNA modality, metrics such as the number of detected genes per nucleus, total Unique Molecular Identifier (UMI) counts, and the percentage of mitochondrial reads was evaluated. For the ATAC modality, we assessed transcription start site (TSS) enrichment scores, library complexity, and the fraction of reads in peaks. All QC metrics were calculated using Cell Ranger ARC (10x Genomics) and are summarized in Table S2, which includes per‐sample statistics for both modalities. These metrics were used to guide downstream filtering and ensure that only high‐quality nuclei were included in the final analyses.

RNA‐seq analysis

The raw RNA‐seq data were preprocessed to remove noise, correct for batch effects, and perform QC checks. Clustering analysis was performed using Seurat's graph‐based clustering algorithm to identify distinct cell populations. After clustering, similar nuclei with a final resolution of 0.2 in Seurat were clustered, leading to the identification of 11 distinct clusters. Cluster identity was assigned based on the expression of lineage‐specific marker genes as follows: 0: Astrocytes; 1: Astrocytes; 2: Excitatory neurons; 3: oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs); 4: Inhibitory neurons; 5: Endothelial; 6: Astrocytes; 7: Cycling progenitors; 8: Oligodendrocytes; 9: Inhibitory neurons; and 10: Excitatory neurons (Figure S3,S4). The differential expression of genes between ancestral groups within each cluster was determined using the MAST test, which employs a generalized linear model framework incorporating cell detection rates within replicates and across groups as a covariate. 29 DEGs were defined as meeting two criteria: p value adjusted < 0.05 and a fold change greater than 1 or less than −1. Compared with other single‐nucleus differential expression methods, the MAST test is known for its low error and false discovery rates. 30

Pathway analysis

The R library gprofiler 31 was used for functional enrichment analysis. We first selected the gene symbols of down‐ and upregulated genes using an adjusted p value of < 0.05 and log2FC > 0.32 or < −0.32. The “gost” function was used to perform the gene set enrichment analysis for each comparison using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathways database. Multiple comparison correction of enrichment scores was done with the gSCS method, and pathways with p‐adj < 0.05 were considered significant.

ATAC‐seq

Analysis of the snATACseq data was performed using the ArchR software package version 1.0.1, 32 as previously described. 14 Briefly, clustering of cells was performed with five iterations of latent semantic indexing (LSI) followed by batch correction with Harmony. 33 We created pseudobulk replicates combining all EU, AF, and AI samples separately and called regions for each ancestry within each cluster via the addReproducibleRegionSet function in ArchR via the default parameters of the MACS2 callregion command version 2.2.7.1. 34 Differential accessibility for each region within each cluster was calculated via ArchR getMarkerFeatures.

ATAC region annotation

The “annotateRegion” function from Chipseeker R library 35 was used to annotate regions with the nearest gene and genomic region. The annotation was done at the transcript level using the GENCODE V44 database, with the following priority: “Promoter,” “5UTR,” “3UTR,” “Exon,” “Intron,” “Downstream,” “Intergenic,” and a distance of ± 3 kb from the transcriptional start site (TSS) was used to assign a region to a gene promoter TSS.

Hi‐C

The in situ Hi‐C libraries were constructed following the protocol outlined by Rao et al. 36 In summary, each library yielded 450 to 550 million paired‐end 150 base pairs reads. Over 270 million non‐redundant, uniquely mapped, paired end reads were subsequently utilized for further analysis of each library. Contact matrices were created at resolutions of 500, 50, and 5 kb. The Directionality Index (DI) value of 40 kb bins was employed to identify the topologically associated domains (TAD). To accurately map enhancer‒promoter interactions, the HiCorr pipeline was employed to correct for Hi‐C bias at the sub‐TAD level and identify Hi‐C loops. 36

RESULTS

Generation of ancestry‐specific iPSCs and iPSC‐derived oligodendroglia

We generated a total of 12 iPSC lines (four AF, four AI, and four EU) in this study (Table S1), of which six were from AD patients and six were from non‐cognitively impaired individuals. In terms of APOE genotype, these 12 lines included four APOE ε4/ ε4 carriers and eight APOE ε3/ ε3 carriers. We differentiated these 12 lines into neural spheroids producing oligodendrocyte lineage cells at different maturation stages ranging from cycling progenitors through to mature myelinating oligodendrocytes. Each line was considered one independent biological replicate for all the experiments and analyses. We did not observe any significant variability in the capacity to differentiate into oligodendroglia between the lines as assessed by OLIG2 staining on day 12 and live O4 staining on day 55. In addition to oligodendrocyte‐related cells, the spheroids contained neurons and astrocytes based on immunocytochemistry (Figure 1A) and snRNA‐seq (Figure 1B,C) analyses.

Single‐nucleus transcriptional profiling of iPSC‐derived oligodendroglia containing neural spheroids across diverse ancestries

Attempts to isolate individual cell types from the tightly clustered spheroids via FACS or pull‐down procedures resulted in a low yield of viable cells. To overcome these challenges and minimize cellular perturbation, we employed a single‐nucleus RNA‐sequencing approach using the 10x Genomics Multiome platform, which simultaneously captures both gene expression and chromatin accessibility from the same nuclei. For the purposes of transcriptomic analysis, we focused exclusively on the RNA modality. These analyses were performed across the 12 iPSC lines from the three ancestral populations. We characterized a total of 47,898 cells, with an average of 3991 ± 1608 per sample. Seurat‐based pattern recognition clustering identified seven cell clusters shared by all the individual samples (Figures 1B and S5). We subsequently detected genes that were enriched in each cluster and utilized these for the assignment of cell types to each individual cluster. We identified one iPSC derived OPC (iOPC) cluster (enriched for the expression of CSPG4. PDGFRA, COL20A1, OLIG1, OLIG2, and PTPRZ1) (Figures 1B,C, S3, and S4) and one iPSC derived oligodendrocyte iOL cluster (MAG, MOG, CLDN11, ENPP6, MYRF, and MBP) (Figures 1B,C and S3) among other cell types, including astrocytes, excitatory and inhibitory neurons, endothelial cells, and cycling progenitors. We did not find any significant differences in the proportion of cells in each cluster across ancestries, suggesting the differentiation efficiency was not affected by this variable (Figure S5).

DEGs between ancestries

To investigate ancestry‐related changes in the transcriptome of the oligodendroglia found in our cultures, we performed differential gene expression analyses using pairwise comparisons of the OPC (Tables S3 to S8) and oligodendrocyte (Tables S9 to S14) clusters independently (Figure S6). In the iOPC cluster, we found that the number of DEGs was greatest in the EU versus AI comparisons, with a total of 1750 DEGs (Figure S6A, Table S4), and approximately 90% (1573) were increased in the EU samples. On the other hand, in the iOL cluster, the greatest number of DEGs was found between the AF and AI lines, with 492 DEGs, of which ∼87% (427) were increased in the AF lines (Figure S6D, Table S9). To identify DEGs common to the oligodendroglia lineage, we analyzed the data to identify overlapping DEGs in both clusters for each comparison (shown in Table S15).

Ancestry‐dependent changes in AD

We compared the DEGs obtained from the different comparisons to genes currently implicated as AD GWAS loci (Figure S7). 1 , 3 , 8 , 37 , 38 We found 11 AD GWAS genes (ALCAM, APP, COX7C, EGFR, GPC6, MYO15A, PLCG2, RTN1, SEC61G, SORL1, and TMEM106B) to be differentially expressed between the ancestries in the iOPC cluster (Table 1, Figure S7A). On the other hand, in the iOL cluster, only five AD GWAS hits were differentially expressed: ADAM17, CLU, EPDR1, FGF12, MAF, and WWOX (Table 1, Figure S7B,). One additional interesting DEG was OPCML, a gene that has recently been linked to AD 39 , 40 , 41 ; in this case, we found it to be increased in EUs compared with AFs.

Male versus female expression

Most of our samples were female, except for two males in the AI lines. While this is a small number of male individuals, they could have introduced a sex‐driven influence into the total ancestry analysis of the single‐cell RNAseq. Therefore, we performed an analysis excluding the two male individuals. Specifically, we compared the two AI females with the four AF females (Tables S6 and S12, Figure S8) and the four EU females (Tables S7 and S13, Figure S8). In these all‐female analyses of iOPCs, we identified two additional significant AD GWAS genes, ANK3 and WWOX, as differentially expressed between AI and AF, and one gene, MGMT, as differentially expressed between AI and EU (Figure S8A). In both comparisons (EU versus AI and AF versus AI), seven out of the nine AD GWAS genes identified when the males were included in the analysis failed to reach statistical significance (Figure S8A). For the iOL all‐female analysis, no additional differentially expressed GWAS loci were observed in the iOLs in any of the comparisons (Figure S8B). On the contrary, MAF was no longer identified as a DEG between AF and AI, and the same occurred to ADAM10 and EDPR1 in the EU versus AI comparison (Figure S8B).

Additionally, we performed a male versus female comparison within the AI samples (Tables S8 and S14). While limited in the number of individuals, in this within‐ancestral analysis, none of the genes identified as differentially expressed in the female‐only comparison between ancestral groups in iOL or iOPCs were found to be DEGs in the AI female versus AI male comparison, except for SORL1. Interestingly, two studies found a sex effect in the overall expression of SORL1. 42 , 43 In addition to SORL1, we identified two AD genes – ALCAM and GPC6 – as differentially expressed between males and females in the iOPC cluster (Tables 1 and S8). On the other hand, none of the AD GWAS genes that were differentially expressed between ancestries were found to be differentially expressed between males and females in the iOL cluster (Tables 1 and S14). These findings suggest that sex did not influence our ancestry‐based findings.

It is worth noting that we observed erosion of X chromosome inactivation (XCI), characterized by ablated expression of XIST, in approximately 70% of our female samples. This phenomenon is not uncommon in iPSC‐derived models. 44 , 45 The expected effect of XCI erosion is increased global biallelic expression of X chromosome genes. 44 , 45 Our snRNA‐seq data suggest that iPSCs with no XIST expression exhibit higher levels of X chromosome gene expression compared to those with high XIST expression, with an average increase of 1.4‐fold. However, we observed no differences in this effect based on ancestry. In male‐derived lines, Y chromosome genes were expressed as expected (e.g., UTY and USP9Y were expressed in male‐derived spheroids but not in female‐derived ones).

Transcriptome comparisons between APOE ε3/ ε3 versus APOE ε4/ ε4

To investigate the transcriptional changes that occur in oligodendroglia dependent on the APOE genotype, we compared the APOE ε3/ ε3 lines with the APOE ε4/ ε4 lines irrespective of their ancestry. For the larger iOPC cluster, there were 5578 DEGs (Figure S9A, Table S16), 30 of which are known AD GWAS loci; interestingly, most of them were upregulated in the 4/4 lines compared with the 3/3 lines (Figure 2A,B), with the exceptions of IGF1R, IDUA, SORT1, ALCAM, MGMT, FGF12, and ARAP1. In the iOL cluster, we identified 1653 DEGs, including nine AD GWAS genes, namely, RTN1, VSNL1, SORL1, COX7C, ANK3, ALCAM, ADAM10, IGF1R, and MGMT (Figure 2C,D, Table S17). A total of 1075 DEGs were shared by both clusters, including the AD GWAS genes ALCAM, ANK3, COX7C, IGF1R, MGMT, RTN1, SORL1, and VSNL1 (Tables S16 and S17). To account for potential sex‐related effects, we excluded the two male samples from the analysis, as both were APOE 3/3 carriers. By focusing exclusively on the female samples, we identified four additional differentially expressed GWAS genes in iOPCs (AGRN, CHRNE, HAVCR2, and RIN3) and one in iOLs (HS3ST5) (Figure S9A,B, Tables S18 and S19).

To determine whether DEGs in oligodendroglia when analyzed by APOE genotype or AD status (Figure 2, Tables S20–S23) were involved in specific processes, we performed pathway analyses on these DEGs. These analyses showed that a wide range of pathways, especially those associated with neurodegenerative diseases, are significantly upregulated in APOEε4/ε4 carrier iOPCs compared to APOE ε3/ ε3 carriers (Figure S10). Many of these pathways are involved in neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, Parkinson's disease, and Huntington's disease (Figure S10A, Table S24). Interestingly, fewer disease‐related and more general pathways were identified in iOLs (Figure S10B, Table S25). The largest number of genes in iOPCs were involved in metabolic pathways, which led us to test for those involved in cholesterol biosynthesis. Indeed, a large number of DEGS were specifically increased in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway in APOEε4/ ε4 compared to APOEε3/ ε3 iOligodendroglia, particularly iOPCs (Figure 3). iOLs followed the same trend but did not reach statistical significance likely due to the reduced number of cells in this cluster. Further, we observed reduced expression of MBP, PDGFRA, and MYRF, key genes in myelin formation in the APOE ε4/ ε4 iOPC lines (Figure 3). Finally, Figure S10C shows pathway differences observed between NCI and AD iOPCs (Table S26). These pathways were similar to those seen in the APOE ε3/ ε3 versus APOE ε4/ ε4 comparison likely due to the fact that the majority of AD samples had the APOE ε4/ ε4 genotype.

Differential expression of genes implicated in cholesterol biosynthesis and myelination between induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived oligodendrocyte progenitor cells with different APOE genotypes. Dot plot illustrates pseudo‐bulk expression of differentially expressed genes (p value adj < 0.05 and fold change > 1 or ← 1).

iOPC and iOLs correlated well with brain transcriptomes

To further validate the identity of our oligodendroglia, we ran Spearman correlation tests to compare our iOPC and iOLs with gene expression patterns from the same cell types in adult and fetal human brain samples. 15 The correlation coefficient between iOPCs and brain OPCs was 0.89 and that between iOLs and adult brain OLs was 0.85, while the comparison against fetal OLs was 0.87, suggesting that our iOligodendroglia are transcriptionally similar to human brain oligodendroglia and fetal OLs.

Pathway analysis between ancestries

Pathway analysis of DEGs between ancestries is presented in Figure S11. Notably, pathways associated with neurodegeneration, including AD, Parkinson's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), were enriched among genes upregulated in AI iOPCs and iOLs compared to those from EU and AF ancestries (Figure S11A–D; Tables S27–S30). In contrast, DEGs upregulated in EU oligodendroglia relative to AI samples did not converge on any significantly enriched pathways. In the EU versus AF iOPC comparison, upregulated DEGs in EU samples were enriched in several neurodegenerative disease pathways, such as Huntington's disease and ALS, as well as neuronal signaling processes (Figure S11E; Table S31). Although all pairwise ancestry comparisons were performed, the EU versus AF comparison in iOLs yielded very few DEGs, and no pathways met the criteria for significant enrichment. This accounts for the absence of a corresponding pathway enrichment plot in Figure S11.

Single‐nucleus chromatin accessibility profiling of iPSC‐derived oligodendroglia‐enriched neural spheroids across diverse ancestries

To characterize chromatin accessibility profiles associated with different ancestries, we performed single‐nucleus multiomic profiling (10x Genomics Multiome), which simultaneously captures gene expression and chromatin accessibility from the same nucleus, on oligodendroglia‐enriched neural spheroids derived from 12 iPSC lines. For the chromatin accessibility analysis, we focused on the ATAC‐seq modality and processed the data independently using ArchR, due to limitations and reduced robustness observed with currently available multiomic integration methods. We characterized a total of 76,409 nuclei, with an average of 6367 ± 3994 per line. ArchR‐based pattern recognition clustering identified eight cell type clusters shared by all the individual samples (Figure 4A). Based on marker genes, we identified one OPC (C6) cluster and one oligodendrocyte cluster (C7) (Figure 4B).

Visualization of single‐nucleus clusters from 76,409 nuclei integrated across 12 samples of induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived neural three‐dimensional cultures including four samples from each ancestry (AF, AI, EU). (A) Clustering of single nuclei from integrated datasets representing identified cellular clusters based on Assay for Transposase‐Accessible Chromatin Sequencing (ATAC‐seq) data. (B) Visualization of oligodendroglia markers expression in the different cell clusters shown in panel (A). Accessibility depicted from purple (low) to yellow (high).

To investigate the ancestry‐related changes in chromatin accessibility in the oligodendroglia clusters, we performed pairwise comparisons of the iOPC and iOL clusters independently (Figure S12). For the AF versus AI comparison in the iOPC cluster, we found 412 differentially accessible regions (DARs) distributed across 357 genes, including RBFOX1, PRDM7, NCK2, EPDR1, and IQCK (AD GWAS hits) (Figure S12A, Table S32). The number of DARs between EU and AI was 413, belonging to 351 different genes, including the AD GWAS genes ADAM17, BIN1, CTSB, ECHDC3, EGFR, IGF1R, MAPT, MGMT, and RBFOX1 (Figure S12B, Table S33). We also found DARs associated with FGF12 and PRDM7, but these same regions were also found when we compared the two female AI lines to the two male AI lines (Table S34), limiting our ability to distinguish ancestry‐related or sex‐related differences in these genes. When comparing the EU to the AF, we found a total of 457 DARs (Figure S12C, Table S35). These regions were distributed across 373 genes, including four AD GWAS hits: RBFOX1, PRDM7, FGF12, JAZF1, PRDM7, PRDM7, and IQCK. On the other hand, for the iOL cluster, the greatest number of DARs was found between AI and EU, with a total of 162 DARs across 139 genes, including PDRM7, ADAMTS1, and REXO1 (Figure S12E, Table S36). However, these DARs were also found in the male versus female comparison (Table S37), suggesting that the differences seen in the accessibility of these genes could be due to sex differences rather than ancestry. Between AFs and AIs, we identified 73 DARs across 63 genes, including a region in PDRM7 that was also present in the male versus female analysis in the comparison (Figure S12E, Tables S38–39). Finally, we found 49 DARs across 39 genes between AFs and EUs, including the AD GWAS risk gene FGF12 (Figures S12, 3F, Tables S40).

Genomic regulatory architecture

To examine the relationship between DEGs and DARs, we compared the genes associated with the DARs with those that showed differential expression for each pairwise comparison in both oligodendroglia clusters (Table 2). In the iOPC cluster, we identified 60 genes that were differentially accessible and differentially expressed between AIs and AFs (Table 2). Only 19 of the 60 were not found to be differentially expressed/accessible in the comparisons between males and females (Table 2); for all of them, increased accessibility was correlated with increased expression. For the AI versus EU comparison, we found 49 genes that were differentially accessible and differentially expressed. Of these, two were previously reported AD GWAS genes (CTSB and EGFR) 1 , 4 , 46 (Table 2). Among these 49 genes, 23 were not affected by sex, and 87% of this accessibility was correlated with expression. The exceptions were MAP2, PPP2R2A, and PDXDC1; however, the last gene had two regions, one in the promoter that correlated with expression and one in an exon that showed an inverse correlation (Table S4 and S32). Finally, in the AF versus EU comparison, we found 22 DAGs that overlapped with the DEGs we identified (Table 2); in all cases, accessibility correlated with expression, but for one of the genes harboring two DARs, only one located in the promoter region correlated, whereas the other, located at an intron, showed the opposite trend (Table S5 and S35). In the iOL cluster, we found five DARs coinciding with DEGs in the AI versus AF comparison (Tables 2, S12 and S38), two of which were not affected by sex and had direct correlation between accessibility and expression. For the AI versus EU comparison, we found 10 genes that were differentially expressed and had DARs; of these, seven were not found to be influenced by sex (Table 2), and the accessibility of the regions was in accordance with their expression. Lastly, for the EU versus AF comparison in iOLs, we only found FGF12 to be differentially expressed and also harbor an associated DAR (Table 2). To investigate whether the genes with differential GRA (expression and accessibility differences) were involved in any specific cell process, we tried pathway analysis on these sets of genes. However, none of the comparisons showed significant enrichment of any specific pathways.

Ancestry‐specific differences in FGF12 chromatin openness and expression

When we looked at the DAR linked to FGF12 (Figure 5, yellow highlight, Table S18), we observed that accessibility was correlated with FGF12 expression (Figure S7B), as shown by the absence of an open chromatin region in the EU lines and corresponding reduction in FGF12 expression in EU compared with the other two ancestries. Interestingly, within this region, there is a site registered on ENCODE as a candidate cis‐regulatory element with a proximal enhancer‐like signature–histone modification H3K4me3 (ENCODE Accession: EH38E2269814) (Figure 5). Additionally, according to the ORegAnno data available on the University of California, Santa Clara browser, this region also contains a transcription factor binding site for CTCF (ORegAnno ID: OREG1365260) (Figure 5). Further, Hi‐C analyses showed chromatin loops occurring between this ATAC region and the transcriptional start site of the non‐coding RNA ENSG00000289165, antisense to FGF12, in spheroids and brain samples (Figure 5). This finding suggests that transcription of FGF12 could be modulated in an ancestry‐dependent manner by this non‐coding RNA.

Chromatin accessibility and Hi‐C loops overview for a subregion of FGF12 in oligodendroglia of different ancestries. Highlighted in yellow is a differentially accessible region between ancestries with a profile that correlates with FGF12 expression.

APOE genotype‐based comparisons

When comparing APOE ε3/ ε3 iOPCs to their APOE ε4/ ε4 counterparts, we identified a total of 67 DEGs that had corresponding DARs. Of these, three were AD GWAS genes – EGFR, EPDR1, and FGF12 (Figure 6A,B, Tables S16 and S40). Additionally, we identified a DAR associated to ADAM17 (Figure 6A,B), a gene previously reported as an AD GWAS gene thought to be involved in TREM2 processing and regulating APP expression and, therefore, amyloid beta formation. 47 On the other hand, in the iOL cluster, only 16 DEGs with corresponding DARs were identified. None of these iOligodendroglia‐specific DARs have, as of yet, been linked to AD (Figure 6C,D, Tables S17 and S41).

Disease status‐based comparisons

When comparing the NCI lines to AD iOPCs, we identified 38 DEGs that had corresponding DARs, including two AD GWAS genes – FGF12 and MGMT (Figure 6E,F, Tables S20 and S42). Additionally, we identified DARs associated with EPDR1 and JAZF1, but these genes were not differentially expressed in this comparison (Figure 6E,F). DISCUSSION

In this study, we compared the chromatin accessibility and transcriptomes of three different ancestries that contribute to African American and Hispanic/Latino admixed populations. Most previous studies focused on EUs, making this study valuable for providing new information and iPSC resources on underrepresented ancestries. While focused on AD, the data can be broadly applied to other disorders involving OPCs and OLs.

Our studies show that chromatin accessibility and transcriptomes are primarily similar across the three ancestries, though several key AD genes showed ancestry‐specific patterns of expression and/or chromatin openness that may reflect differences in risk. Our data support the idea that DEGs and DARs for corresponding genes at AD GWAS loci are not a major factor in most comparisons. While this does not mean AD GWAS loci will have similar causal mechanisms, chromatin conformation analysis may identify unique differences. Though single‐nucleus approaches have significant power to identify individual differences, population representation is limited by iPSC study size. However, the similarity between ancestries in both accessibility and expression in iOPCs and iOLs across most genes is reassuring for future AD therapeutic efforts.

Our 3D neural spheroids produce brain‐like oligodendroglia with transcriptomes showing high correlation (> 85%) with human brain oligodendroglia from adult and fetal datasets, including OPCs and early myelinating oligodendrocytes characterized by high BCAS1 expression. 48 Our ancestry‐based data replicate human brain study findings, such as CLU being differentially expressed between AF and EU iOLs, as previously reported in AD human brains. 15 These observations suggest iOPCs and iOLs effectively model oligodendroglia phenomena occurring in human brains.

While iPSC reprogramming typically resets cellular age, some aging characteristics can persist during differentiation. 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 However, similar transcriptome correlations between both fetal and adult oligodendrocytes with our iOPCs and iOLs suggest aging signatures aren't a major concern in these cells. This consistency likely reflects core oligodendroglial gene expression programs maintained throughout the development and recapitulation of key developmental pathways guiding oligodendrocyte differentiation. Since myelination continues into late adolescence and adulthood, 53 , 54 this similarity could reflect this unique timeline.

We included both APOE ε4/ ε4 and APOE ε3/ ε3 carriers across all ancestral groups in our study. In iOligodendroglia, particularly in iOPCs, APOE4/4 carriers showed upregulated expression of cholesterol biosynthesis genes. This pattern mirrors findings from iPSC‐derived oligodendrocytes and post mortem brain tissue, 55 where APOE ε4 was linked to disrupted cholesterol homeostasis and impaired myelination. Given cholesterol's essential role in myelin formation, 56 , 57 this upregulation may reflect a compensatory response to deficits in myelin production. Supporting this interpretation, we also observed reduced expression of critical myelination genes such as MBP and MYRF in APOE ε4/ ε4 iOPCs, pointing to compromised myelination capacity.

These findings are consistent with broader evidence of APOE ε4‐associated lipid dysregulation in glial cells. For instance, Tcw et al., using human iPSCs, post mortem brain and transgenic mice with APOE targeted replacement, similarly identified APOE ε4‐driven alterations in cholesterol metabolism, including enhanced de novo cholesterol synthesis and lysosomal cholesterol sequestration in astrocytes. 58 This convergence across glial subtypes suggests that lipid dysregulation may represent a common, APOE ε4‐mediated vulnerability mechanism in AD, with cell‐type‐specific consequences for oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and microglia alike. These findings together underscore the importance of APOE 𝜀4‐mediated lipid dysregulation in brain cells and its potential role in disease mechanisms across multiple cell lineages.

We observed maintained PDGFRA expression in APOE ε4/ ε4 iOPCs, indicating retained precursor identity, though transition to functional oligodendrocytes appeared to be hindered. OPCs remain the most proliferative CNS cell type, generating new oligodendrocytes throughout life, while mature oligodendrocytes focus on myelination. 17 , 59 Our findings suggest APOE ε4/ ε4 iOPCs maintain proliferative capacity but struggle with effective differentiation and maturation.

Cholesterol dysregulation appears to occur early in oligodendrocyte development, leading to impaired cholesterol and lipid biosynthesis. Even mild differences in oligodendroglia metabolism over a lifetime could produce pathogenic results. White matter abnormalities are considered early AD neuropathological events, with white matter hyperintensities observed before symptom development. 19 Clinical studies report early AD‐associated changes in infants and children, with APOE ε4‐carrying infants showing lower white matter myelin water fractions and slower myelination rates compared to non‐carriers. 60

The differential role of apoE isoforms in oligodendrocytes and ApoE4‐associated cholesterol dysregulation requires further investigation. Our findings support a complex interplay between APOE genotype, cholesterol biosynthesis, and oligodendrocyte function, appearing universal across tested ancestries. The upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis genes coupled with reduced myelin formation gene expression suggests a compensatory mechanism that ultimately falls short in supporting proper myelination.

OPCs, as CNS resident stem cells, maintain oligodendrocyte populations and participate in myelin repair. 61 In AD, brain microenvironment changes impact OPC functionality, showing reduced differentiation capacity and elevated senescence. 62 This study is the first to focus on human iPSC‐derived oligodendroglia including multiple ancestries. While AD outcomes may be similar across ancestral populations, development paths may differ between genetic ancestries and environments, making varied population studies valuable windows into AD pathology.

This study represents a significant advancement in understanding the ancestry‐specific differences in iPSC‐derived oligodendroglia and their implications for AD. By generating and analyzing iPSC lines from AF, AI, and EU ancestries, we have identified unique transcriptional and chromatin accessibility profiles in genes that may contribute to AD risk.

Our findings emphasize the importance of including various ancestries in iPSC studies to uncover subtle yet crucial differences that may impact disease susceptibility and progression. The differential expression and accessibility of genes associated with AD across ancestries, such as APOE, highlight potential pathways that could be targeted for therapeutic interventions.

This study also underscores the role of APOE genotype in modulating gene expression related to lipid metabolism, providing insights into early intervention strategies for APOE ε4 carriers. The correlation between our iPSC‐derived models and human brain oligodendroglia strengthens the validity of using these models to study neurodegenerative diseases, offering a promising avenue for future research in AD biology and therapeutic development.

Finally, this work not only sheds light on the molecular underpinnings of AD across different genetic backgrounds but also underscores the necessity to understand the distinct genetic and environmental pathways leading to AD in various populations to enhance our ability to develop effective, personalized prevention and treatment strategies.
