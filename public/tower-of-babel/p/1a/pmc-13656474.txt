Levels, trends, and determinants of fertility intentions in Tehran, Iran: the role of educational aspirations, life-course factors, and contextual influences
Authors: Amir Erfani, Marzieh Nojomi, Neda SoleimanvandiAzar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLife-course factors, including parity, are well linked to fertility. However, the moderating role of educational aspirations, controlling for confounders, remains unclear. This study examines how educational aspirations, alongside life-course, normative, and contextual factors, shape fertility intentions in urban Tehran.MethodsGuided by an integrated conceptual framework and using data from the 2025 Tehran Survey of Fertility Intentions (n = 1,999 married women < 45), this study employs descriptive and bivariate analyses to examine fertility intention patterns and multinomial logistic regression to identify key determinants. Trends are assessed using age-matched data from a 2012 survey of women < 36.ResultsOverall, 78% of respondents either intended to stop childbearing or were uncertain. Short-term intentions were concentrated among younger and childless women, while older and higher-parity women mainly intended to stop. Between 2012 and 2025, short-term intentions rose slightly, stopping declined, and uncertainty increased. Multivariate results show that age and parity increase the odds of stopping or uncertainty (vs. intending another child), while later age at marriage reduces both. Educational aspirations have no direct effect but attenuate parity effects on stopping (OR = 0.53; CI: 0.38–0.75) and uncertainty (OR = 0.51; CI: 0.32–0.79). Higher religiosity is associated with lower stopping intentions. Living in middle- and high-status districts increases stopping, while middle-status districts are associated with greater uncertainty.ConclusionsFertility intentions in Tehran are polarized, with considerable uncertainty at early parities shaped by life-course dynamics and future aspirations. Policies should prioritize early childbearing transitions and better align education, employment, and family formation rather than relying solely on pronatalist incentives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28470-2.Keywords: Fertility intentions, Aspirations, Low fertility, Uncertainty, Iran

Introduction

Fertility is strongly shaped by life-course factors, particularly age, parity, and timing of marriage, which structure reproductive decision-making and transitions in childbearing behavior [1–4]. As individuals progress through stages of family formation, intentions typically shift toward stopping childbearing, reflecting accumulated experience and changing constraints [5, 6]. In addition to these life-course dynamics, fertility intentions, defined as individuals’ plans regarding whether and when to have a (another) child, serve as key predictors of subsequent behavior, especially when measured as time-specific intentions that reflect current conditions and opportunities [7, 8]. This is particularly important in low-fertility contexts, where even modest behavioral changes can have long-term demographic consequences [9, 10].

Alongside life-course influences, fertility intentions are shaped by future-oriented aspirations and competing life goals. Educational and career plans affect perceived opportunity costs, expectations, and alternative pathways, thereby influencing how reproductive intentions are formed [11, 12]. In urban and low-fertility settings, reproductive decisions are increasingly embedded within broader life projects related to education, employment, and personal development [13–16]. However, the extent to which educational aspirations interact with life-course position, particularly parity, in shaping fertility intentions remains insufficiently understood, especially when accounting for other normative and contextual influences.

Recent research further highlights that fertility intentions are not always clearly defined. A growing proportion of individuals report uncertainty about future childbearing, reflecting competing aspirations, unstable socioeconomic conditions, and unclear future prospects [10, 17–19]. This uncertainty is systematically patterned across life-course stages and social contexts and is linked to differences in individuals’ ability to translate resources and aspirations into actionable plans [20, 21]. Despite this, uncertainty is often treated descriptively rather than as a structured outcome reflecting variation in individuals’ capacity to form clear and consistent intentions.

Iran provides a particularly relevant setting to examine these issues. The country has experienced a rapid fertility transition, with the total fertility rate declining from over seven children per woman in the mid-1980s to below replacement since the early 2000s [14, 22]. This shift has been accompanied by delayed marriage, prolonged education, and increasing socioeconomic uncertainty, particularly in urban areas such as Tehran [6, 23]. Yet empirical evidence on fertility intentions in Iran remains limited, with most studies focusing on ideal family size rather than time-specific intentions [24]. Moreover, little is known about how educational aspirations, alongside life-course, normative, and contextual factors, jointly shape fertility intentions and the distribution of uncertainty in contemporary urban contexts.

This study addresses these gaps by examining fertility intentions among married women under age 45 in urban Tehran. It focuses on how educational aspirations, in interaction with life-course characteristics such as parity, as well as broader normative and contextual factors, shape intentions to have a child, stop childbearing, or remain uncertain. In addition, the study documents the levels and recent trends of fertility intentions in Tehran, providing a descriptive foundation for interpreting these patterns within a rapidly changing low-fertility context. By incorporating uncertainty as a substantive outcome and controlling for key confounders, the study provides a more integrated understanding of reproductive decision-making in an urban setting undergoing demographic transition.

Conceptual framework

Fertility intentions in this study are conceptualized as the outcome of a multidimensional process shaped by the interaction of life-course position, future-oriented aspirations, contextual conditions, and individuals’ capacity to translate resources into coherent reproductive plans. Drawing on life-course theory, the Theory of Planned Behavior, the Second Demographic Transition, uncertainty frameworks, and a capability-based perspective, the model integrates structural, behavioral, and agency-centered explanations of fertility decision-making.

At its core, the model emphasizes that fertility intentions are dynamic and stage dependent. From a life-course perspective, they evolve as individuals move through key stages such as marriage and childbearing. Age, parity, and timing of marriage structure individuals’ positions within the reproductive life course and strongly influence whether they intend to continue or stop childbearing. Intentions typically decline at later ages, vary across parity levels, and are closely linked to partnership timing and stability [25–31]. Higher parity and advancing age are generally associated with stronger intentions to stop, while later age at first marriage is linked to continued orientation toward childbearing [5, 6, 25]. Thus, fertility intentions reflect both preferences and accumulated life-course experiences.

However, life-course position alone does not fully explain variation. The model incorporates insights from the Theory of Planned Behavior, which highlights the role of attitudes, perceived opportunities, and constraints arising from competing life goals [32]. Educational and career aspirations are particularly important, as they can increase opportunity costs and shape future expectations, often reducing fertility intentions among individuals with strong aspirations in these domains [11, 33]. In this study, educational intentions are treated as forward-looking aspirations that shape how individuals interpret their life-course position and reproductive choices.

The Second Demographic Transition perspective further underscores the growing importance of individual autonomy, self-realization, and non-family aspirations, reflecting a shift toward value orientations that prioritize personal fulfillment over traditional family roles [13, 34]. In Iran’s low-fertility context, reproductive decisions are increasingly embedded within broader life projects, such as education, employment, and personal development [14]. Consequently, fertility intentions are more diverse and contingent on individual priorities.

A central feature of the model is the explicit incorporation of uncertainty. Rather than treating it as residual, uncertainty is understood as arising from competing life projects, unclear future prospects, and unstable socioeconomic conditions [19, 35]. It reflects both indecision and the difficulty of forming stable reproductive plans. Evidence shows that uncertainty varies across life-course stages and social contexts and is closely linked to individuals’ ability to align present conditions with future expectations [19, 36].

To explain variation in both intentions and uncertainty, the model adopts a capability-based perspective of fertility agency [21]. From this viewpoint, fertility intentions emerge through a conversion process in which individuals transform available resources, such as education, employment, and income, into plans and behaviors via context-specific “conversion factors,” including life-course position, future orientations, and social environments. In this study, these processes are not observed directly but are proxied by socioeconomic characteristics, particularly education, employment, and household expenditure, which indicate individuals’ capacity to translate preferences into actionable reproductive plans. This capacity is also reflected in patterns of intention consistency and uncertainty, with clearer future orientations associated with more stable intentions.

The model also incorporates contextual and normative influences, including religiosity and residential socioeconomic environment. Religiosity shapes fertility intentions through value systems and social expectations, while residential context captures variation in perceived opportunities and constraints for childbearing [37–39]. These factors interact with both individual characteristics and life-course dynamics.

Bringing these elements together, the model proposes that fertility intentions are shaped by four interrelated domains: (1) life-course factors (age, parity, age at first marriage), (2) future-oriented aspirations (educational aspirations), (3) contextual and normative conditions (religiosity and residential environment), and (4) socioeconomic resources as proxies for capability processes.

In summary, fertility intentions are not static preferences but context-dependent, life-course-structured, and agency-mediated outcomes that reflect how individuals align their demographic position, aspirations, and social conditions into coherent reproductive plans.

Data and methods

Data

This study uses data from the 2025 Tehran Survey of Fertility Intention (2025TSFI), a representative survey of 1,999 married women under age 45 residing across the 22 districts of Tehran, Iran. As the capital and home to nearly one-fifth of the national population, Tehran is characterized by substantial social and ethnic diversity and one of the lowest fertility rates in the country (approximately 1.2 births per woman [40]), making it a suitable context for examining fertility intentions. Data collection was conducted in April–May 2025 using Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI) by trained female interviewers. Eligibility criteria, including married status, age under 45, co-residence with spouse, and residence in Tehran, were verified at the beginning of each call.

A dual-frame sampling design combining landline and mobile numbers was employed to improve coverage and reduce bias. Landline numbers were sampled proportionally across districts using random digit dialing (RDD) and systematic selection. The mobile sample was generated using RDD based on prefixes from the three main operators (MCI, MTN Irancell, and RighTel), allocated according to market share (56%, 38%, and 6%, respectively). All selections were probability-based, with up to three callbacks to minimize nonresponse.

The survey response rate was 76.02%, calculated using the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) RR3 response rate definition, which adjusts for cases of unknown eligibility by estimating the proportion of eligible respondents among them [41]. To enhance participation, the survey was introduced as an academic study affiliated with a public health research center, interviews were limited to 10–15 min, and multiple callback attempts were scheduled at different times. Standardized procedures, trained interviewers, and assurances of confidentiality were maintained. Despite these measures, limitations inherent to telephone surveys remain, including potential coverage and nonresponse bias, as well as mode effects such as social desirability bias [42, 43].

The 2025TSFI questionnaire includes detailed items on respondents’ and their spouses’ socio-economic characteristics, fertility behaviors, preferences, and intentions. It also incorporates selected questions from the 2012 Tehran Survey of Fertility Intentions (2012 TSFI), allowing for the examination of trends in fertility intentions over time. The 2012TSFI was similarly conducted among a representative sample of 2,267 married women under age 36 in the 22 residential districts of Tehran [44]. To analyze trends in fertility intentions from 2012 to 2025, the 2025 sample will be restricted to women under age 36 to allow for a direct comparison across years. The validity and reliability of the questionnaire were assessed through a pretest conducted prior to the full implementation of the 2025TSFI.

Participants in the 2025TSFI were anonymous to the researchers and oral informed consent was obtained from all participants before conducting the telephone interview. Participants voluntarily participated in the study and did not receive any compensations. They had the right to withdraw from the survey at any time if they wished to do so. They were assured that their answers would remain anonymous. All interviews were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines, regulations and the ethical standards.

Measures

Fertility intention was the dependent variable, measured using two survey questions: whether respondents intend to have a (another) child (“yes,” “no,” or “unsure”), and, if yes, when they intend to do so. Based on these responses, intentions were initially classified into four categories: (1) want a child soon (within 1–3 years), (2) want a child later (in 4 + years), (3) unsure, and (4) do not want more children.

Preliminary analyses indicated that respondents who intended to have a child soon and those intending to have a child later exhibited similar associations with key covariates. This finding was further supported by supplementary sensitivity analyses, including a multinomial model using the four-category specification and a binary model comparing short-term and longer-term intentions, both of which showed comparable effect sizes and no statistically meaningful differences across predictors (see Additional file 1: Tables S1–S2).

Given these results and considering the relatively smaller sample size in the longer-term category, the “want soon” and “want later” groups were combined into a single category (“intend to have a (another) child”) for the main bivariate and multivariable analyses to enhance model stability and parsimony without loss of substantive information. For pregnant women, intentions referred to the period after birth, treating the pregnancy as having a child.

The independent variables for multivariate analyses were grouped into four broad categories reflecting the conceptual framework: life-course, future-oriented, normative and contextual, and socioeconomic background variables. Life-course variables included age, age at marriage, and number of children (parity). Future-oriented variables comprised women’s educational intentions and their interaction with parity. Normative and contextual factors included the importance of religion and residential district status, with districts categorized into high-, medium-, and low-status areas based on cluster analysis. Socioeconomic background variables included education level, employment status, and monthly household expenditure. Household expenditure was log-transformed to reduce skewness and limit the influence of extreme values, improving the stability of the multinomial logistic regression estimates. The log transformation also facilitates interpretation by capturing proportional rather than absolute differences in spending.

Methods of analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics and illustrate patterns of fertility intentions. Differences in fertility intentions across covariates were assessed using chi-square tests of independence. To examine adjusted associations between independent variables and fertility intentions, multinomial logistic regression models were estimated, given the nominal nature of the three-category dependent variable. Adjusted predicted probabilities for each fertility intention category were subsequently estimated for selected covariates using the margins command in Stata and graphically displayed using marginsplot. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF); all VIF values were below 2 and tolerance values exceeded 0.58, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity.

As part of sensitivity analyses, additional parity-specific models were estimated using binary logistic regression, with fertility intention coded as 1 = do not want/unsure and 0 = want a (another) child (see Additional file 1: Table S3). Given the relatively small sample sizes within parity subgroups, particularly among women with no children (n = 319) and those with one child (n = 612), this binary specification was adopted to ensure adequate statistical power and model stability. This approach also maintained consistency with the main analytical framework while allowing assessment of the robustness of findings across parity groups.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 shows socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of the study sample. Age (mean = 37.5 years; SD = 5.2) is heavily concentrated in the 30–44 range, with very few respondents under 25 and only about 9.0% under age 30. This age composition situates the sample primarily in the later reproductive ages, where childbearing intentions are closely intertwined with parity, employment, and economic considerations. Consistent with the age profile, age at first marriage (mean of 22.7 years and SD = 5.2) is skewed toward later adulthood, where the largest group married at ages 20–24 (37.0%), followed by 25–44 (31.9%) and 12–19 (31.1%). Parity shows a modal pattern at two children (41.6%), with one child (30.6%) and no children (16.0%) also sizable. Higher parity (3 + children) represents about 11.8%. Altogether, nearly three-quarters (about 72.2%) have one or two children, underscoring that most respondents are either in the midst of family building or have reached commonly observed parity targets.

Levels and timing of fertility intentions by parity

Table 2 presents the distribution of fertility intentions and their timing by number of living children in the sample. Fertility intentions show a sharp decline with increasing parity. Overall, two-thirds of respondents (66.8%) reported that they want no more children, while only 8.9% intend to have a child within the next year and 5.3% within two years. Near-term fertility intentions (within the next year) are highest among childless women (21.6%), compared with 10.6% among women with one child and under 5% among those with two or more children. Longer-term intentions (3 + years) and unspecified timing are rare, together accounting for less than 8% of all women. About 11.3% of respondents were unsure about their future childbearing plans. These findings indicate that the intention for additional children is largely limited to women with zero or one child, while women with two or more children overwhelmingly consider their families complete. At parity two, more than four in five women (83.5%) want no more children, rising to 88.1% among those with three or more children.

Trends of fertility intentions

Table 3 shows that the share of women who want a (another) child soon (within 1–3 years) has increased modestly, from 28.1% in 2012 to 31.1% in 2025 (+ 3.0% points). In contrast, the share wanting a child later (4 + years) or at an undecided time has edged down from 14.8% to 12.8% (− 2.0 points). Taken together, the proportion who want any additional child (soon or later) is slightly higher in 2025 (+ 1.0 point, from 42.9% to 43.9%,), suggesting a small shift toward near-term childbearing rather than postponement. Over the same period, the intention to stop childbearing has declined from 44.8% in 2012 to 39.1% in 2025 (− 5.7 points), indicating greater openness to having another child among women under 36. At the same time, uncertainty has grown, with the “unsure” category rising from 12.3% to 17.0% (+ 4.7 points). In sum, the 2025 profile, when matched on age with 2012, shows nearer-term intention for a child, less stated stopping, and more indecision about future childbearing. These statistically significant movements point to a reallocation from clear stopping and longer-term plans toward either near-term intentions or uncertainty.

Age shows the strongest pattern, where it is negatively related with fertility intentions.

The intention to have another child is highest among women under 30 (58.1%) but declines steeply with age, falling to 9.7% among women aged 40–44. Conversely, the intention to stop childbearing increases from 26.8% in the youngest group to 83.4% in the oldest. The share who are unsure is also highest among younger women (15.1% under age 30), peaks slightly at ages 30–34 (19.4%) and then declines to 6.9% among women aged 40–44.

Age at first marriage also shows significant differences. Women who married at age 25 or older are more likely to want another child (33.6%) and less likely to stop childbearing (52.3%) compared with those who married before age 20 (12.2% want; 79.9% stop). The proportion unsure is lowest among those married before age 20 (7.9%) and highest among those married at 25 or later (14.1%), suggesting greater uncertainty among women with later marriages.

Parity shows a clear pattern, with the intention to have another child declining sharply as the number of living children increases, stopping rising correspondingly, and uncertainty decreasing from its highest levels among childless women. Wanting another child drops from 61.4% among childless women to 1.8% among those with three or more. Uncertainty falls from 15.0% at parity zero and 16.3% at parity one to 3.8% among women with three or more children, while stopping rises from 23.5% to 88.1% across the same groups.

Educational attainment shows a consistent and significant pattern. Both the intention to have another child and the level of uncertainty rise as education increases, while the intention to stop declines. The share wanting another child grows from 15.7% among women with ≤ 11 years of schooling to 31.9% among those with a master’s degree or higher, and uncertainty increases from 6.9% to 15.6%. Over the same range, stopping falls from 77.4% to 52.5%.

Married women who plan to continue their education in the next five years report higher intention for another child (32.2% vs. 18.6%) and lower intention to stop (57.8% vs. 69.8%) than those without such plans, with only a slight difference in uncertainty (10.0% vs. 11.6%). Further analyses (not shown here) indicate that women with no or one child (35% and 24%), those employed (37.2%), women under age 30 (37%), those with 17 + years of schooling (34%) or 13–14 years (29%), and residents of high-status districts (31%) are also more likely to plan to continue their education. Employed women are more likely to want another child (27.3%) and less likely to stop (59.9%) than unemployed women (18.8% want; 70.9% stop).

Residential district is significantly related to fertility intentions. Women in high-tier districts show the highest intention to have another child (28.6%) and the lowest uncertainty (8.4%), while those in middle-tier districts report lower intention (19.0%) and the highest uncertainty (13.2%). Women in low-tier districts fall in between. Differences by ethnicity, religiosity, and household expenditure are small and not statistically significant.

Among life-course variables, age, parity, and age at first marriage emerge as the strongest predictors. Each additional year of age increases the likelihood of intending to stop childbearing by 22% (OR = 1.22, p = 0.001) and slightly increases the likelihood of uncertainty (OR = 1.06, p = 0.005). Parity shows a particularly strong effect, with each additional child increasing the likelihood of intending to stop by more than threefold (OR = 3.45, p = 0.001) and raising uncertainty (OR = 1.91, p = 0.001). In contrast, age at first marriage is negatively associated with stopping and uncertainty (OR = 0.90, p = 0.001; OR = 0.96, p = 0.033), indicating that women who marry later remain more oriented toward continued childbearing.

Years of schooling is not significantly associated with fertility intentions. However, future-oriented educational intentions play an important moderating role. Although education continuation plans show no significant direct effect, the interaction between parity and educational intentions is negative and statistically significant for both outcomes (OR = 0.53, p = 0.001 for stopping; OR = 0.51, p = 0.003 for uncertainty). Substantively, this reduces the effect of parity on stopping from about 3.4 times to roughly 1.8 times (3.45 × 0.53 = 1.83), while for uncertainty the effect declines from about 1.9 times to approximately unity (1.91 × 0.51 = 0.97), effectively eliminating the association between parity and uncertainty among women with educational intentions. These multivariate results are consistent with descriptive patterns showing that women with educational intentions are more likely to want another child and less likely to intend stopping. Descriptive evidence further indicates that these women are disproportionately younger, of lower parity, more likely to be employed, reside in higher-status districts, and have higher levels of schooling, suggesting a more advantaged socioeconomic profile.

Figure 1 illustrates this moderating relationship. Among women without educational intentions, the probability of intending to stop childbearing increases sharply with parity. Among those with educational plans, the increase is more gradual, and the likelihood of wanting another child remains consistently higher across parity levels. Differences in uncertainty are modest, indicating that the moderating effect operates mainly through the transition from childbearing to stopping.

Figure 2 reinforces the central role of life-course factors. The age profile shows a clear shift from wanting a child and being unsure toward stopping and lower uncertainty as age increases. A similar pattern is observed for parity, with a strong increase in stopping and a decline in both the desire for additional children and uncertainty as family size grows. Notably, the probability of intending to stop childbearing is relatively high even among women with no children, whereas it is very low at younger ages. This reflects the heterogeneity within parity groups, which include women at different life stages, including older women for whom childbearing may no longer be preferred. The panel for age at first marriage shows that women who marry later maintain stronger intentions for continued childbearing and higher levels of uncertainty, whereas those who marry earlier are more likely to intend stopping childbearing.

Among contextual and normative factors, religiosity shows a strong independent relationship with fertility intentions. Women who report religion as very important are substantially less likely to intend stopping childbearing (OR = 0.26, p = 0.001), and those reporting it as important also show reduced likelihood (OR = 0.54, p = 0.001), compared with women reporting low importance. Residential context is also significant. Living in middle- and high-status districts increases stopping intentions (OR = 1.76, p = 0.001; OR = 1.58, p = 0.024), and middle-status residence increases uncertainty by about 70% (OR = 1.70, p = 0.008). Other socioeconomic background variables, including household expenditure and employment status, are not statistically significant.

Overall model fit is strong. McFadden’s pseudo-R² is 0.23, corresponding to a Nagelkerke R² of approximately 0.40, indicating substantial improvement over the null model.

In sum, fertility intentions in Tehran are strongly shaped by life-course dynamics, particularly age and parity, while also being conditioned by future-oriented educational aspirations and contextual factors. The transition toward stopping childbearing is therefore not uniform but depends on women’s anticipated life trajectories.

Sensitivity analyses using alternative specifications of fertility intentions yielded consistent results. Models distinguishing short- and longer-term intentions showed similar effect sizes, with no meaningful differences across covariates, and parity-specific analyses indicated comparable patterns across subgroups (Additional file 1: Tables S1–S3).

Discussion

Drawing on an integrated conceptual framework and recent data from the 2025 Survey of Fertility Intentions in Tehran, this study examines the levels, trends, and determinants of fertility intentions among married women under age 45 in the urban center of Tehran. Descriptive evidence shows that nearly two-thirds of women (67%) intend to have no (more) children, while about 11% remain uncertain. Intentions for additional children are concentrated among women at earlier stages of family formation, whereas most women with two or more children consider their families complete, indicating that fertility limitation occurs at relatively low parities. Trend analysis shows a rise in uncertainty alongside a modest increase in short-term fertility intentions between 2012 and 2025. Multivariate results reinforce these patterns, showing that fertility intentions are strongly structured by life-course factors, while also varying systematically with future-oriented, normative and contextual factors. Together, the findings point to a multidimensional process in which reproductive intentions are shaped by the interaction of life-course position, forward-looking plans, and social context.

The multivariate findings show that fertility intentions in Tehran are strongly structured by life-course factors. Consistent with life-course theory outlined in the conceptual framework [1–3], parity emerges as the most powerful determinant, with each additional child associated with a more than threefold increase in the likelihood of intending to stop childbearing. Age shows a similar, though more gradual, association, while later age at first marriage is linked to a continued orientation toward childbearing. These patterns are consistent with prior research demonstrating that fertility intentions evolve systematically as individuals progress through stages of family formation and accumulated reproductive experience [4, 5, 25].

At the same time, these associations are not uniform across groups. The interaction between parity and educational intentions indicates that future-oriented aspirations shape variation in how life-course factors translate into fertility intentions. While parity is strongly linked to stopping among women without educational plans, this association is substantially weaker among those with such plans and nearly disappears for uncertainty. These findings are consistent with the Theory of Planned Behavior, which emphasizes that intentions reflect competing goals and perceived opportunities [32], and with Second Demographic Transition perspectives highlighting the growing importance of diverse life aspirations in shaping reproductive behavior [13, 34]. In this context, educational plans are associated with a distinct orientation toward family formation, suggesting that fertility decisions are embedded within broader life trajectories and future expectations [11, 16].

These findings further indicate that differences in clarity and consistency of fertility intentions are linked to variation in future orientation. The lower uncertainty observed among women with educational intentions, and the decline in uncertainty across the life course, suggest that more structured trajectories are associated with more stable reproductive plans. Similar patterns have been reported in recent studies, which show that clearer future orientations and stronger goal alignment are linked to more consistent fertility intentions and lower ambiguity in planning [17, 20, 21]. The moderating effect of educational intentions, particularly the weakening of parity effects among women with such plans, further indicates that future-oriented goals can reshape how life-course constraints translate into reproductive decisions. This aligns with evidence that competing aspirations, especially related to education and career, alter both the timing and certainty of fertility intentions [11, 16].

The role of uncertainty is particularly notable in relation to both life-course stage and temporal change. Consistent with prior research, uncertainty is concentrated at earlier life-course stages and declines with age and parity, reflecting increasing clarity as individuals progress through family formation [4, 17]. At the same time, the rise in uncertainty among women under age 36 between 2012 and 2025 suggests a broader shift toward less certain fertility planning. Similar increases in uncertainty have been documented in other settings and are often linked to changing economic conditions and more complex life transitions [18–20]. The coexistence of rising short-term intentions and declining stopping further suggests a reallocation of intentions, with some women advancing childbearing while others postpone decisions altogether.

These patterns are consistent with a growing body of research showing that fertility intentions are shaped by uncertainty about the future and competing life priorities [18, 36]. Studies across different contexts indicate that uncertainty is higher when individuals face unstable socioeconomic conditions or unclear life trajectories and tends to decline as pathways become more structured [20, 35]. Although the present analysis does not directly measure all relevant contextual conditions, the observed increase in uncertainty over time aligns with broader evidence linking changes in economic and institutional environments to more fluid and less predictable fertility planning [10, 36].

Normative and contextual factors also play a role. The negative association between religiosity and stopping intentions is consistent with prior research showing that stronger religious commitment is linked to more pronatalist attitudes and higher desired fertility [37, 38]. Differences by residential context further indicate that fertility intentions vary across social environments. The higher likelihood of stopping in high- and middle-status districts, and the greater uncertainty in middle-status districts, align with studies highlighting the importance of local context, such as urban conditions and socioeconomic environments, in shaping reproductive behavior [39]. These findings suggest that fertility intentions are embedded not only in individual characteristics but also in the broader social settings in which decisions are made.

Overall, the findings show that fertility intentions in Tehran reflect the joint influence of life-course position, future-oriented aspirations, and contextual conditions. While life-course factors provide the primary structure, variation in aspirations and social context modifies how individuals respond to that structure, producing differences in both intended behavior and uncertainty. Similar multidimensional patterns have been documented in other low-fertility settings, where reproductive decision-making increasingly reflects the coordination of competing goals under conditions of uncertainty [10, 28].

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fertility intentions among married women in Tehran are shaped by the joint influence of life-course dynamics, future-oriented aspirations, and contextual conditions. Age and parity remain the primary factors structuring movement toward stopping childbearing, while educational intentions modify these patterns, highlighting the importance of broader life trajectories. The findings also show that uncertainty is an integral component of fertility intentions. Although it declines across the life course, its increase over time indicates growing difficulty in forming clear reproductive plans in a changing urban context. At the same time, modest increases in short-term intentions alongside declining stopping point to a reconfiguration of fertility preferences rather than a simple shift toward higher fertility.

Overall, by documenting levels and trends alongside their determinants, this study contributes to a more integrated understanding of reproductive decision-making in a low-fertility urban setting. Future longitudinal research is needed to examine how fertility intentions evolve over time and how changing social and economic conditions shape these dynamics.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and interpretations should therefore be limited to observed associations. Second, trend comparisons rely on repeated cross-sectional data rather than longitudinal evidence, limiting the ability to assess individual-level changes in fertility intentions over time. Third, the absence of direct measures of key contextual and relational factors, such as partnership quality, housing conditions, and employment insecurity, limits the ability to test mechanisms frequently cited in explanations of low fertility. Finally, the focus on Tehran may restrict the generalizability of findings to other urban and rural contexts in Iran.

Despite these limitations, the study provides recent, context-specific evidence on fertility intentions in Iran’s largest and lowest-fertility city, while distinguishing analytically between intending another child, stopping childbearing, and uncertainty. Future longitudinal or mixed-methods research would be well suited to examine transitions between these intention states and to assess how changing economic, relational, and residential conditions shape these dynamics over time.

Policy implications

The findings highlight important limitations of current pronatalist policies in Iran, which primarily rely on financial incentives and restrictive measures [14, 45]. Such approaches are unlikely to substantially influence fertility behavior because they do not adequately account for how fertility intentions are shaped by life-course stage, future aspirations, and contextual conditions. First, the strong effect of parity indicates that fertility limitation occurs early, with most women stopping at relatively low parities. This suggests that policies targeting higher-order births are unlikely to be effective. Greater impact may be achieved by supporting earlier transitions, particularly first and second births. Second, the role of educational intentions underscores that fertility decisions are embedded in longer-term life planning. Policies that ignore educational and career trajectories, especially among urban and educated women, risk limited effectiveness. Supporting the compatibility of education, employment, and childbearing through flexible education systems, childcare provision, and stable early-career opportunities is therefore critical. Third, the findings on uncertainty point to the importance of stability in shaping fertility intentions. Rising uncertainty suggests that individuals face increasing difficulty in making clear reproductive plans. Policies that improve predictability in employment, housing, and family formation pathways are likely to have stronger effects than direct pronatalist incentives alone. Finally, variation by residential context indicates that fertility intentions are shaped by local conditions. This highlights the need for place-based, context-sensitive policies rather than uniform national approaches. Overall, a more effective policy approach would move beyond narrow pronatalist strategies toward supporting individuals’ broader life opportunities, reducing uncertainty, and enabling the alignment of family formation with education and employment trajectories.
