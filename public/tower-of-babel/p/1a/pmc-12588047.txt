Interpreting discordant results in mixed-method research: data triangulation, participant voices, and epistemic issues in health research
Authors: Janet Page-Reeves, Megan Rivera, Evelyn Lozano, Lidia Regino, Maria Tellez, Pachely Mendivil-Aguayo, Marina Moore, Daniel Perez Rodriguez, Andrea Kalvesmaki, Rebecca Crocker, Alejandro Aragon, Camille Vasquez, Jackie Perez, Reuben J. Thomas, Elaine L. Bearer, Cristina Murray-Krezan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this article, we discuss unexpected methodological challenges revealed during interim analysis of data from a mixed-methods study with female Mexican immigrants. ‘Tertulias’ was a randomized controlled trial of an innovative peer support group model for reducing social isolation and depression among women immigrants from Mexico using a Community-Driven, Community-Engaged Research (CD-CEnR) approach. We describe discordant data obtained using different methods and how we went about triangulating and integrating analyses. In the process, we identified cultural, contextual, and methodological factors that influenced the nature and content of the data, and issues of power and epistemic justice in the broader research enterprise. Based on this experience, we offer insights for data integration to improve both scientific rigor and epistemic justice in health disparities research.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, there has been an increasing recognition of the need for using mixed methods to investigate complex health issues (Creswell et al., 2011; Jeffries et al., 2019; Stewart & Nápoles-Springer, 2003; Weishaar et al., 2015). While a large body of literature discusses the advantages of a mixed methods approach and how to use mixed methods, researchers commonly utilize quantitative and qualitative methods in parallel rather than in an integrated way. Moreover, distinct methods can produce discrepant or contradictory data, and there is a dearth of literature to guide researchers how to interpret this discordance and how to attribute value to disparate results.

Method ‘mixology’?

Mixed methods are a hallmark of social science research in fields such as anthropology and sociology, but the health sciences have been slower to adopt this approach. Although mixed-method studies have become popular in health research in the past 20 years, the actual mixing of methods continues to be underutilized, undertheorized, and under-reported (Doyle et al., 2016; Harris & Brown, 2019). When following the accepted orthodoxy in health research, the primary outcomes of mixed-methods studies tend to remain predominantly defined by statistical analyses of quantitative data, with other methods often treated as secondary (e.g., Cheek, 2021). In studies designed to test the effectiveness of an intervention, qualitative data may be considered incidental and of little account, with more emphasis placed on the use of ‘validated instruments’ producing quantitative results – what Cheek (2021) identifies as the ‘[merely] part of a larger study’ phenomenon. This subordination of qualitative data in mixed methods studies is considered so entrenched and problematic that leading qualitative health journals often refuse to publish qualitative data drawn from mixed methods studies under the assumption that it is generally not designed to produce robust, stand-alone results. In addition, most mixed-method health research employs multiple methods that are functionally parallel but not intentionally integrated (Wasti et al., 2022). The actual mixing of methods and data sets is under-utilized, under-theorized, and under-reported (Doyle et al., 2016; Harris & Brown, 2019; Moseholm & Fetters, 2017; Wasti et al., 2022). Reviews of studies using mixed-method design found that few sufficiently described the rationale for and implementation of mixed methods (Matović & Ovesni, 2023), and that only one-third provided concrete details about mixing and integration (Irvine et al., 2020).

Dealing with discordant data

Mixed-methods approaches are optimized when the various data sets are ‘triangulated’ to detect areas for further exploration. Yet within health science research the default is to report results from validated measures over patient self-reported qualitative data, when it stands in contradiction to the former (O’Cathain et al., 2008; Zohrabi, 2013). When this occurs, a lack of meaningful study integration becomes particularly problematic as it eliminates the potential for further scientific inquiry to examine differences between participant’s responses to validated measures and their lived experience as documented via qualitative inquiry. To date, there are no clear guidelines for how to obtain ‘rigour’ while addressing conflicting results in the health sciences (Brown et al., 2015; Guetterman et al., 2023). The lack of integration between methods and results in mixed methods research impairs the ability of investigators to recognize, assess, or explain contradictory data derived from different research methods. The silencing of this discord within the data is a loss for science and for improving health outcomes, because discord is a vital platform from which to identify and interrogate complexity.

Although there is a small body of literature addressing discordant data in health research (e.g., Deacon et al., 1998; Perlesz & Lindsay, 2003; Vikström, 2010), the under-reporting of discordant data can muffle the impact of patient perspectives, which is a stated goal of health sciences/services research to improve patient outcomes (Austin et al., 2020; Davies & Cleary, 2005; LeBlanc & Abernethy, 2017). This reflects the overwhelming priority given to quantitative and statistical outcomes that tends to relegate other sources of data and analyses as ‘pilot’ and ‘exploratory’ only (Cheek, 2021). Such a stance forgoes an opportunity for enhanced methodological coherence and scientific rigor across any mixed methods study, but it is an especially egregious gap in research conducted with minoritized and underrepresented populations whose ‘voices’ have been marginalized or excluded (Botha, 2011; Hughes & Cohen, 2010; Wilson et al., 2022). When researchers fail to address or consciously ignore discord among data sets that may call into question responses given to validated survey questions and opt to report quantitative survey results as stand-alone, voices contained in self-narrative or self-report data from minoritized participants may be silenced. As such, it is important to be aware of how study design and the methodological choices we make can create epistemological inequity and unequal power dynamics in how we know what we know and what is accepted as ‘evidence.’

Analytic integration

DiLoreto and Gaines (2016) present a detailed review of approaches for dealing with discordant data in mixed-methods research, some of which they apply to unravel data from their study of faculty and student perceptions of educational assessments. Thinking about data discord began in the 1950s when Campbell and Fiske (1959) set the stage for using data from different methods as a way to validate findings via ‘convergence’ or comparing data from different sources to develop an interpretation that makes sense of all the results (Younas & Sundus, 2022). Campbell and Fiske proposed the use of a matrix, sometimes called a ‘joint display’ (Fetters et al., 2013; Guetterman et al., 2015; Haynes-Brown & Fetters, 2021) as a valuable tool for this triangulation. By arranging data from different data sets in a way that visually and conceptually juxtaposes them, Haynes-Brown and Fetters (2021) demonstrated that using a process of iterative joint displays, they were able to identify inconsistencies in the findings that would likely not otherwise have been revealed. Foreshadowing more recent concerns, Campbell and Fiske noted that ‘divergent’ results should not be seen as a mistake or failure, but as an opportunity to deepen understanding of the issue under study. Nearly 30 years later, Mathison (1988) re-emphasized the need for triangulation but also reinforced the value of a broad, holistic approach incorporating the context in which the study occurs as a critical point for reflection. She articulated three potential outcomes – convergence, inconsistency, or contradiction.

Interrogating the data

Moffatt et al. (2006) identify that the distinctiveness of the paradigms underpinning qualitative and quantitative methods poses a foundational challenge to data integration. Nonetheless, they argue that the complexity of seemingly intransigent public health problems and health inequities requires that we develop equally complex and sophisticated research methods to overcome those challenges and produce actionable results. They propose a multi-part framework for dealing with discordant data that involves a deeper dive into understanding the context and content of each data set but also investigates the dynamics of the method(s) used to gather those data. This framework introduces the idea of adding new methods to test results or gather supplementary or confirmatory data when discord cannot be resolved. Like Campbell and Fiske (1959), they see discordant data as an opportunity that requires proper time and attention be allocated to dissect the inconsistencies. They argue that, . . . discrepancies can be harnessed to interrogate each dataset more fully. Not only does this enhance the robustness of the study, it may lead to different conclusions from those that would have been drawn through relying on one method alone and demonstrates the value of collecting both types of data within a single study. More widespread use of mixed methods in trials of complex interventions is likely to enhance the overall quality of the evidence base.(Moffatt et al., 2006, emphasis added, p. 1)

This theme is further operationalized in Lee and Rowlands (2015) proposal that the process of triangulation is, in itself, a method to create a ‘higher level of synthesis’ that allows for a different way of conceptualizing the existing data and what we know about it.

A case of discordance

In this article, we discuss methodological challenges encountered when an interim analysis of data we conducted to comply with a request made by the study sponsor revealed unexpected discordance between different data sets. ‘Tertulias’ (Spanish for ‘conversational gatherings’) was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of an innovative peer support group model for reducing social isolation and depression among women immigrants from Mexico using a Community-Driven, Community-Engaged Research (CD-CEnR) approach (Page-Reeves et al., 2019, 2021). We were interested in publishing these interim results because participants in the first two cohorts were reporting dramatic impact from participation (Mendivil Aguayo et al., 2024; Mendez Huerta et al., 2023; Page-Reeves et al., 2024; Rivera et al., n.d.; Lozano et al., n.d.; Page-Reeves, et al. n.d.). In addition, we were ‘strongly encouraged’ to publish interim results to be considered for follow-on funding, despite the fact that the quantitative study outcomes were powered on the full sample.

Below, we describe discordant datasets and how we triangulated and integrated analyses to better understand the meaning of the discord, and what the discord revealed about our data-gathering instruments and approaches. In the process, we identified cultural, contextual, and methodological factors that influenced the nature and content of the data, and issues of power and epistemic justice in the research enterprise. From this experience, we offer methodological insights to improve both scientific rigor and research equity. However, it is important to note that the purpose of the case study presented here is not to critique quantitative approaches or psychometric science, nor to revisit the debate over qualitative versus quantitative methods, which has been aptly and extensively covered in the literature. Rather, our purpose is to discuss the on-the-ground reality of how to approach and interpret data discordance in the context of health sciences research.

Study disposition and design

In Tertulias, we enrolled 252 adult, female Mexican immigrants from low-income households in four cohorts between July 2020 and July 2024. All participants provided informed consent, and all participant-facing activities and components were conducted in Spanish. The study had two ‘arms,’ with half assigned to the intervention and half to the control. Intervention participants attended a weekly two-hour Zoom-based peer support group for 10 months. Controls did not receive an intervention, but we used a Modified Attention Control Placebo design (Popp & Schneider, 2015) in which they were called bi-monthly to confirm their contact information to reduce attrition between baseline and follow-up at 12 months.

Participants in both arms completed pre/post surveys using instruments validated with Spanish-speaking populations: Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) (Orme et al., 1986), Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-14) (Cohen et al., 1994), Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) (Connor & Davidson, 2003), Medical Outcomes Study Social Support Survey (MOS-SSS) (Zanini & Peixoto, 2016), and project-specific empowerment using the Trauma-Informed Practice (TIP) Scale (Goodman et al., 2017), and evaluation questions. Participants also answered project-specific questions for Social Network Analysis (SNA). Extensive qualitative data from participant observation, meeting notes, WhatsApp chat streams (Mendivil Aguayo et al., 2024), interviews, Shared Reading activities (Rivera, et al. n.d.), Arts-Based Research including the submission of a self-selected creative work (Rivera et al., n.d.), group evaluation discussions, and Ripple Effects Mapping (Chazdon et al., 2017) were gathered from the intervention group only.

Moreover, these results were consistent and repeated across both the Y1 and Y2 cohorts, anecdotally for the Y3 cohort that was in process, and directly mirrored in data from the Tertulias pilot study (Page-Reeves et al. 2021). In our retrospective evaluation of Y1 and Y2, we were struck by the extent to which participant quotes from the pilot and the Tertulias intervention were absolutely redundant and interchangeable – and without documentation, it would be impossible to say which study they came from. These results also aligned with the theoretical frameworks guiding our study design and with our hypotheses about Tertulias as an innovative model for peer support (Page-Reeves et al., 2021). Given the clear and comprehensive alignment of findings from qualitative and other data sources demonstrating that the intervention was addressing domains of interest, we anticipated the intervention’s impact on participants would be visible in the survey data in the form of a positive advantage of intervention participants as compared with controls.

However, when we statistically analysed the survey data (as described in our protocol paper Page-Reeves et al., 2021), the overall differences on social support, depression, resilience, and stress between intervention and control groups were either smaller than expected or not statistically different. For example, we hypothesized that depression scores would decrease by at least 6.5 points more in the intervention group than in the control group (corresponding to a Cohen’s effect size d=0.47), but at interim analysis, the effect size was d = 0.29. Although in favor of the intervention, the results were not statistically meaningful. We saw a similar effect with the social support measure where we hypothesized an effect size of d = 0.50, and we saw d = 0.29. Resilience showed an effect larger than hypothesized but had high variability in responses resulting in no statistical difference.

From an analytic perspective, it was as if participants in both arms indicated a high level of mental well-being that remained static across time points, a phenomenon our study team dubbed as ‘I’m fine, everything is fine.’ For example, the median depression scores in both groups at both time points were below the standard cutpoint for depression (score of 16, higher scores = higher level of depression) (Vilagut et al., 2016) and both groups decreased their scores but not as expected (median decrease: −4 pts in Control and −5.5 pts Tertulias, not statistically different). Additionally, the two cohorts coincided with the global pandemic which we hypothesized should have been reflected in our psychosocial survey data as increased stress and anxiety across both arms. Given that the intervention was intensive and that the qualitative data revealed that it had a deeply positive and even transformative impact on participants, we were perplexed by the overall low scores, and the lack of difference between results from the different timepoints or between intervention and control participants.

In terms of the lack of change across time, the pre/post survey scores of intervention participants were neutral in a way that directly contradicted the powerful qualitative results and the existence of the pandemic. Moreover, the research team’s interactions with intervention participants provided detailed information about their lives that confirmed a deep level of personal and familial upheaval and tragedy that was also not reflected in the neutral survey results. For instance, one participant experienced the death of her husband from COVID; another, a daughter’s diagnosis of cancer. Multiple intervention participants reported domestic violence and depression, accompanied by requests for assistance or referral to therapy. The fact that survey responses did not seem to reflect these factors was not only surprising but concerning. If the quantitative instruments failed to capture change across time, differences between study arms, the potential impact of life hardship, or the context of the pandemic, how were we to interpret the results of the statistical analysis?

Discrepant data are threatening and scary

The strong qualitative evidence of the substantial impact of the intervention was compelling and indicated an opportunity to advance Tertulias as an evidence-based model for reducing social isolation and depression among female Mexican immigrants. But the lack of resonance of these data with the quantitative results would compromise our ability to argue the model’s success. When we conducted the preliminary analysis and saw that the quantitative and qualitative results were not aligning, we decided to take a step back and assess how to interpret what we were learning.

Triangulation

In the context of our discrepant data conundrum, we relied on our intent to triangulate as described in our published study protocol (Page-Reeves et al., 2021). Our project outcomes were defined by statistical analysis of change in pre/post survey responses from baseline to 10 months, but we designed the study as a triangulated QUAL↔QUAN approach with simultaneous and recursive data collection and analysis per guidance for mixed methods research design (Creswell, n.d.; Doyle et al., 2016; Moseholm & Fetters, 2017; O’Cathain et al., 2008; Palinkas et al., 2011). This study design poised us to integrate, converge, and elaborate our discordant findings in a way that would not have been feasible using only one method. Following advice from Moffatt et al. (2006) and Lee and Rowlands (2015), and paraphrasing from Campbell and Fiske (1959), we sought to ‘harness the discrepancy and interrogate each dataset.’

Preliminary hypotheses to explain discordance

After reviewing the literature, consulting with experts in research methods and our six-member Advisory Board of participants from our pilot study, and much discussion among team members, we hypothesized several possible explanations for discrepant results:

Pandemic impacts

The COVID-19 pandemic created unique and unprecedented social isolation and risks to mental health. Data collection occurred at distinct and coincidentally key timepoints punctuating the pandemic. This pandemic backdrop could have had impacts that universally affected participants from both arms and served as a confounder in interpreting study results.

Latinos and mental health disclosure

There is a large body of literature describing how Mexican culture discourages the disclosure of information about mental and behavioral health (e.g., Eghaneyan & Murphy, 2020). Moreover, it has been demonstrated that a ‘social desirability bias (SDB)’ in research in which participants of any ethnic background may want to present themselves in a positive light can also introduce bias in data collection (e.g., Krumpal, 2013). Bergen and Labonté’s (2020) article on SDB from research in Ethiopia, ‘Everything is perfect and we have no problems,’ was particularly helpful in capturing dynamics that seemed apropos for thinking about our results. For example, survey responses could have been influenced by: participants not wanting to ‘wash their dirty clothes in public’ (a quote from one of our Advisory Board members), not wanting to portray themselves as victims, lacking trust in what will be done with the data, feeling shame and cultural taboos about mental health issues, or not wanting to share personal information with strangers. Although both Data Collectors for the survey were female Mexican immigrants, none of the participants knew them personally.

Response-shift bias and understanding of depression as a construct

There may also have been a ‘response-shift bias (RSB)’ occurring in our survey responses (e.g., Howard & Dailey, 1979; Kaushal, 2016). RSB is a well-documented phenomenon in which participants change their understanding of the constructs of interests as a result of participation in a study, especially if they change understanding of specific concepts that they were not aware of or understood differently (e.g., depression, stress). We also hypothesized that our results could reflect construct-related problems in the data collection process or the design of the questions. Validated instruments measuring depression and other mental health concerns are often written in English and validated with English-speaking populations. For use with other groups, measures are translated to ensure the meaning of each item within a survey remains intact and then retested with Spanish speaking participants. However, constructs of interest may still be framed in language that does not originate with the population of study. We used instruments in Spanish that had been demonstrated to be valid and reliable, but perhaps the wording of the questions was problematic for these particular participants, or maybe the questions the instruments asked were not the right questions to get at the constructs of interest in the context of the Tertulias study.

In addition, epistemological challenges have been identified by mental health researchers working with immigrant and indigenous populations in the U.S. and Canada. Cultural interpretations of constructs like depression or stress vary, are influenced by social networks and contexts, and may be understood differently within and between groups (Kirmayer et al., 2011). There is a large literature on cultural perceptions of mental health issues among Hispanics (e.g., Cabassa et al., 2007) that suggests it is incorrect to assume that Hispanic participants do not ‘understand’ what depression and stress are, and that it is critical to recognize that this population often has a culturally based way of thinking about them that differs from normative research approaches and constructs. Hispanic participants may not associate the constructs in standard survey questions about mental health issues with their own lives or experience, making it challenging for participants to answer in ways that accurately reflect their emotions or lived experience. The constructs of interest may not actually apply to or properly capture how the participants experience the world – the constructs themselves may be problematic. Interpreted this way, the fact that our pre/post survey results showed no/little change could indicate that despite ‘validation,’ the survey questions were not measuring what they were intended to measure.

Methods added to examine discordant results

Considering the need to investigate discordant results further, we added two additional data collection strategies and employed two new analytic strategies.

Evaluate qualitative results using retrospective pre-test design

First, we summarized the qualitative findings that were discordant with the quantitative results. The major themes in the qualitative data were ‘resource knowledge and connectedness,’ ‘social connectedness,’ ‘health (including depression),’ ‘resilience,’ and ‘empowerment.’ We also identified sub-items from within each theme such as ‘social isolation’ and ‘sense of belonging’ (for social connectedness). We used themes and subthemes to develop evaluation questions using a Retrospective Pre-Test (RPT) design (Pratt et al., 2000). RPT questions are administered after the completion of an intervention to evaluate issues of interest or identified findings. While RPT questions have limitations, specifically in recall bias (Hill, 2020), the purpose of the RPT approach is to test a participant’s self-perceptions of change (Drennan, 2012). Sample RPT questions are presented in Table 1 with the qualitative finding bolded in red. Question structure follows a template of ‘before X intervention, how . . . ‘ followed by ‘when you finished X intervention, how . . . ‘ Based on constructs identified from the qualitative analysis, we added 17 pairs of questions plus 11 independent questions that were designed not to be a definitive, stand-alone finding of causal claims, but to test the validity of our qualitative results and to identify more specific details regarding the discrepancies between the quantitative, validated measures and participant perceptions of the change that was experienced.

We obtained responses from 95 of 120 Y1 and Y2 participants. We compared pre and post scores between arms by fitting linear regression models to perceptions of critical intervention constructs before and after the intervention and assessing least squares mean differences between arms. Women in the intervention arm reported more improvement than those in the control arm in relation to social connectedness (p < 0.05), resilience (p < 0.01), empowerment (p < 0.05), knowledge of and access to resources (p < 0.01), and self-reported health (including depression) (p < 0.01) Results of the RPT supported findings from qualitative and other methods. While RPT can demonstrate bias in retrospective reflection and self-report, especially with respect to social desirability effects or response-shift-bias (Hill 2020), retrospective reflection and self-report are also strengths in that they tell us what the participant wants to convey to us about how the intervention impacted their lives – which really is the point of the intervention, not whether it moves the scale on a validated instrument that may or may not be measuring an objective psychosocial construct. This dimension of participant intentionality of RPT is a strength in this context.

Add focus groups with controls

Our original study design did not include qualitative data collection from participants in the control arm. We did not think such inquiry would produce meaningful results because they were not receiving an intervention. However, in the context of our discrepant data, we added two focus groups with control participants to explore their experiences in the control arm of the study and their perspectives on the data collection process.

The focus groups shed light on discrepancy issues and added new insights. Consensus in the focus groups was that participants joined the study because they believed in the purpose of the research to improve the health of women immigrants, and they felt that it was essential to answer the questions truthfully. Participants indicated that the Data Collectors were very professional, and they were confident that the Data Collectors would not be judgemental or gossip. Therefore, our hypotheses about the impact of SDB or stigma did not stand out as primary explanations for data discrepancies.

However, we did identify problems with the administration of the survey and the survey questions. The survey was administered orally by a Data Collector entering answers on an iPad. Participants were divided over whether or not they liked this method and whether or not it was a good way to document their answers. About half indicated that they could have understood the questions better reading them and would have preferred entering their own responses into the iPad. This finding questions our study design that purposely uses uniform data collection methods across all participants to limit potential inconsistencies that could be introduced by variable data collection strategies. Our participants suggested the opposite – that more accurate results could be achieved if participants are given an option about how they provide data to suit their individual cognitive learning style and assets. Focus group participants also identified the length of the survey as a barrier, describing what is known as ‘survey fatigue’ (e.g., Field, 2020) that led to superficial responses to get through the questions more quickly.

Experience of the control as an intervention

Perhaps the biggest insight from information gathered through the focus groups was the way that participants in the control arm experienced the study. We used a Modified Attention Placebo Control design to reduce the attrition that is common in an RCT, especially with data collection appointments 12 months apart. Our approach involved brief bi-monthly phone calls where control participants were asked to confirm their contact information. The calls were geared to encourage study retention rather than to instil change in participant experience. Knowing that many of the participants had been very disappointed to be assigned to the control arm, at the time of randomization, we emphasized how important both arms of the study are to research science. But participant descriptions of perceived benefits of being in the control arm exceeded the knowledge that they were contributing to science to support the wellbeing of other women (Nichols et al., 2012). This, in fact, is not unusual, as others have found that high-need participants benefit from the time, attention, and perceived resources provided through participation in research studies, even in the control arm.

Participants came to the study information meeting and joined the study because they wanted and needed the peer support intervention. Thus, when participants ended up in the control arm, many espoused an assets mind frame, believing that ‘anything is better than nothing’ and ‘at least I got something.’ They described that being in the study made them feel that they were ‘part of something’ or connected to something, and that they looked forward to the brief check-in calls from the study Coordinator designed only to confirm contact information because they experienced these calls as someone caring enough about them to call. Although they also indicated that they felt that the calls were in no way sufficient and that they wanted more, participants experienced these calls in a therapeutic way. Our findings from the control arm mimic those in other studies where enrollment in a clinical trial is a reflection of the need for support in the area of study in general, and any support, even if a control process, is better than none (Nichols et al., 2012). This reflects the great need among the participant population (and ironically supports the hypothesis of the study about social isolation and peer support) but was not the intention of the design of the control arm.

Data collection had unintended impacts on participants

Considering the therapeutic effect described by control participants, our Team conducted further focused analytic discussions to explore what we were learning and to rethink anything that may have been previously overlooked. Through this process, we identified additional unintended impacts of data collection that may have been at play in our discordant results.

The ‘Hawthorne Effect’ is a well-documented situation where participant behavior is changed by being watched or studied (e.g., McCambridge et al., 2014). This effect has been associated with the desire on the part of participants to live up to expectations of the researcher(s). In our data, we did not discover an example of the Hawthorne Effect per se, but we found that the experience of being asked questions about themselves at the data collection appointments had an effect on participants which was neither an intended study outcome nor something that we could adjust for in our analysis but that may have introduced response bias.

We heard from participants that the survey questions were surprising to them in a way that made them reflect upon the questions and the issues raised, which in turn, shifted how they viewed their own lives. We were familiar with this happening following ethnographic interviews where the researcher asks about things in new ways and really engages the participant in dialogue about themselves, their lives, and their experiences. But control participants described the standard survey data collection appointment as a very deep and meaningful experience – something which was not anticipated or intended in relation to the nature and structure of the data collection. Similarly, Data Collectors recounted that participants often answered a question using the standard response options, but then started talking about related aspects of their lives. For example, following a question asking the participant to rate their feelings of sadness on a scale of 1 to 5, participants would sometimes describe, in great detail, an aspect of their lives that they were feeling very sad about. The Data Collectors reported that they tried to stay neutral in these situations, but that it often felt as if the participants would ‘really unload’ their hopes, fears, and experiences in this way during the data collection appointment. Thus, our participant population’s deep need for peer support impacted participant experience of being in the control arm and, in light of descriptions of the data collection appointment from both participants and Data Collectors, we discovered that many participants experienced taking the survey as therapeutic.

Response-shift bias (RSB) & bereavement at losing the group

Another discrepancy that we investigated was that some participants in the intervention arm increased in their depression score. This could be based on a few factors, including a better understanding of the measures we were collecting based on their participation in a study about behavioral health issues or experiencing traumatic events during the time of the study (e.g., a death in the family, a divorce, diagnosis of a severe illness).

We also identified other dynamics of the intervention that may have played a role in these increases in depression scores. First, participants experienced increased emotional and mental health awareness as a result of participation in the Tertulias group session discussions. Many participants reported that they were more aware of their own emotions and understood themselves better, and some indicated that they now realized that they had been depressed for years and never knew it. This RSB was very likely impacting scores on the surveys. So, instead of reporting less depression at the end of the study that might have reflected the positive impacts of the intervention, participants could be reporting more depression because their understanding of their own lives and situations had changed. In this way, their increased depression scores made sense but were not a reflection of the intervention causing them harm or failing to address existing depression.

More novel was the importance of something that we had previously noted but had failed to fully appreciate its potential impact on the study and study results. Midway through each cohort (at ~5 months), participants in the intervention arm began asking when the groups would end and expressed feeling upset and sad that something helpful in their lives (the group support) was going to be ‘taken away.’ This anticipatory sadness around the upcoming loss of support may have been expressed on the survey as a sense of ‘bereavement.’ But in the context of trying to measure the impact of the intervention, this outcome was problematic – the bereavement was not caused by the intervention, rather, it was caused by anticipatory grief over its conclusion, in essence further confirming its beneficial impact in participants’ lives.

These outcomes were not something that we anticipated, and they counter-intuitively mean that some intervention participants could experience and/or report more depression at the end of the intervention than in the beginning. But this is not something that would be interpretable or able to be distinguished from an intended impact of the intervention through independent statistical analysis of the survey data. These are analytic outcomes that cannot stand alone and that require qualitative interpretation in order to be meaningful. In this context, the results of the Retrospective Pre-Test Questions (RPT) paired questions that support the qualitative findings became even more important.

Triangulation and analytic integration

As indicated, triangulation was a structured part of our original study design, and it became crucial for our analysis of discrepant data. We conducted a side-by-side comparison of data derived from different methods/data sources. This included that we created a series of user-friendly matrices or ‘joint displays’ to identify and illustrate issues of convergence, inconsistency, or contradiction. We examined areas of concordance or discordance using an innovation of the joint display approach for data from individual participants. Since we gathered many types of data, we wanted to see both the congruence/discordance between the overall results comparing data sets, but also within participant responses. The matrix made it possible to determine the degree to which results were consistent across instrument and methods and provide the same answer to the same research questions. The matrix also revealed areas of divergence, prompting us to delve into the reasons for discrepancies. These comparisons have yielded insight for our interpretation.

We identified several examples of participants with low baseline depression scores who subsequently, and in some cases, immediately after the intervention began, reached out to group facilitators for help with their depressive symptoms or reported that they have been depressed for a long time, even years. Conclusions and implications for mixed-method design

Our purpose in documenting our study experience was not to critique quantitative or psychometric approaches but to share how we responded when we encountered unanticipated discordance in our data and the steps we used to address this issue in a meaningful way. Disentangling the many factors at play in our data and interpreting how to move forward was challenging, but our triangulated analysis provided a clear path. The problems we uncovered in our survey data, together with the fact that data from all of the other methods used in the original study design were well-aligned with the new RPT response outcomes, across time between the pilot and the current study, and between groups in each cohort and across cohorts – provides strong evidence supporting our interpretation of the data to demonstrate the effectiveness of the intervention. As indicated by Bazeley (2018), the separation of methods is artificial and counterproductive to good research. Strong research design, especially of complex health issues, necessarily requires not only multidimensional strategies but thoughtful integration of those strategies. Bazeley argues ‘it is better to focus on what methods will best answer the research questions than on attempting to show that the research design cleverly combines qualitative and quantitative approaches.’ This is a lesson that we believe should be more widely applied in health sciences and health services research.

Our story with discrepant data has implications for designing mixed-methods studies, but also for thinking about interpretation of data. The high variability in responses across the groups, and smaller changes in scores over time warrant a thorough investigation if we are to produce data that move us toward achieving goals of health equity for populations such as in this study, but it is important to keep in mind that this interim analysis only includes half of the study data and results may change upon analysis of the full sample. Without our full sample size, the interpretation of null results are ambiguous – we cannot definitively conclude that the intervention is ineffective or that we are underpowered to detect our hypothesized effects. This ‘politics’ of publishing in academic contexts is something that is rarely discussed or acknowledged but that we would like to call attention to here given that it may lead to interpretation ambiguity. Pressure to publish unplanned interim results from funders who want researchers to get results into print, or the pressure that researchers feel to publish to qualify for follow-on funding or for promotion and tenure can be detrimental to the scientific process.

Moreover, we want to be clear that we were not concerned that our statistical analysis of the data was flawed. We are confident in the robustness of our statistical design and the skills and expertise of our analytic team. However, the discord between data sets called our attention to weaknesses introduced by the context of the study and the lived experience of the participants. The acts of involved in conducting the research including gathering the data, data analysis, and interpretation of results required that we attend to a level of complexity that is often minimized or ignored. It is clear that there is a need to counter ‘orthodoxy’ (Dean, 2004; Thorne, 1991) of the dominant paradigm in health research that prioritizes quantitative survey methods and statistical analyses that are generally inadequate for ‘capturing complexity’ (e.g., Weishaar et al., 2015), particularly in research with participants from minoritized, excluded, and oppressed populations. Florczak (2014) urges for ‘pragmatism,’ suggesting that neither qualitative or quantitative paradigms should be considered superior, that ‘the use of qualitative data should be recognized and stand on its own (not as transformed numerical values mixed into a quantitative database),’ and that ‘the researchers should engage in self-reflection and scrutinize their basic beliefs about the world and how they understand it to avoid purism in either paradigm.’

Our experience highlights how ‘method mixology’ to create concrete mechanisms for ‘interrogating the data’ in mixed-method research is essential. Methods and strategies for triangulation and convergence need to be consciously incorporated into the design of the research in a way that dovetails appropriately with the broader design of a study. But the ‘danger’ that discrepant data pose to the accuracy and scientific rigor of the research enterprise also makes it imperative for study designs to clearly articulate how these data will be analysed to address discrepancies. Based on our experience, in future grant proposals, our team will be more explicit about our inclusion of an integrated analysis that does not prioritize one data set or method over another with the statement to this effect: ‘Final interpretations for will be based on integrated, braided analysis rather than prioritizing one data set or method (i.e., quantitative or qualitative).’

Women’s voices and epistemic justice

Denzin (2017a) argued that the purpose of triangulation is not validation, but rather contextualizing and explaining discrepancies which potentially become not only interesting but important in their own right. But the dilemma we discuss here also highlights the ways that methods we use and analytic decisions we make impact what we know and the way we know it with social justice implications. And problematically, standard survey questions are unable to capture the co-constructed, complex nature of how and why participants respond the way they do. Survey data collection is a more co-constructed and complex process than is typically acknowledged in quantitative research (Westmarland, 2001). This aligns with literature revealing often ignored or underappreciated subjective aspects of the research enterprise (Denzin, 2017b; Rojas-Lizana, 2017)

In the process of conducting research with Mexican immigrant participants, we have identified cultural, contextual, and methodological factors that shaped survey responses, as well as issues of power and epistemic justice in the way we conduct research. These problems are recurrent over time and across a variety of different studies and sites (e.g., personal communication with Rebecca Crocker, Julia Hess, Benjamín Aceves, Sally Moyce, Theresa Cruz, Scott Carvajal). As is illustrated in the Tertulias study by the difference between our pre-/post-survey results and the results from the rest of our data, if we had relied on surveys alone, the responses would have suggested that the intervention does not work and may have in fact elevated depression scores in some cases, erasing the generalized consensus to the contrary as corroborated across multidimensional qualitative data, Social Network Analysis (SNA) involving ego network surveys, and Retrospective Pre-Test (RPT) data.

Through their participation in a study designed to show something important about immigrant women’s health, participants placed their trust in us. Intervention participants told us in strong and powerful terms that the intervention not only worked but in some cases transformed their lives. Controls told us, sometimes with tears, that they desperately wanted and needed the intervention. Seeing the discrepancies in our data compelled us to write this article. Challenging normative research methods and standards that contradict or devalue the voices of our participants has become essential to us. Our data triangulation process incorporated equity throughout not only the process of data collection but also in the triangulation and analysis. Through this experience with data discordance, we clearly see why and how it is important to incorporate equity and participatory practices in health research. The stories we tell have an impact, including the stories we tell in our research.
