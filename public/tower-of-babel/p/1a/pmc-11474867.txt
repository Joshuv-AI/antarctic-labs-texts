Decline in and recovery of fertility rates after COVID-19-related state of emergency in Japan
Authors: Tomohiro Mitoma, Jota Maki, Hikaru Ooba, Hisashi Masuyama
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic led to a decline in fertility rates worldwide. Although many regions have experienced a temporary drop in fertility rates with the spread of the infection, subsequent recovery has varied across countries. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of COVID-19 infection rates and regional sociodemographic factors on the recovery of fertility rates in Japan following the state of emergency.MethodsThis study examined prefectural fertility data from before the COVID-19 pandemic to forecast fertility rates up to 2022 using a seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average model. A regression analysis was conducted on fertility rates during the first state of emergency and the subsequent recovery rate with respect to the number of new COVID-19 cases and sociodemographic factors specific to each prefecture.ResultsDuring the first state of emergency, the monthly fertility rate decreased by an average of −13.8% (SD: 6.26, min: −28.78, max: 0.15) compared with the previous year. Over the following 22 months, the average fertility recovery rate was +2.31% (SD: 3.57; min: −8.55, max: 19.54). Multivariate analysis of the impact of the pandemic on fertility changes during the first emergency indicated a negative correlation between new COVID-19 cases per capita and the proportion of nuclear households. No significant correlation was found between fertility recovery rate and new COVID-19 cases or emergency duration. When classifying fertility rate fluctuation patterns before and after the emergency into four clusters, variations were noted in the proportion of the elderly population, marriage divorce rate and the number of internet searches related to pregnancy intentions across the clusters.ConclusionsNo association was found between pregnancy intentions related to the spread of infection, such as the number of new cases and the fertility recovery rate following the first state of emergency. Differences in the patterns of decline and recovery during the pandemic were observed based on population composition and internet searches for infection and pregnancy across different prefectures.Keywords: COVID-19, public health, internet, social medicine, health economics

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, which first appeared in early 2020, has had a worldwide impact on lifestyles and social interactions, resulting in altered fertility rates.1 2 Factors such as economic, racial, political and social factors have contributed to state-specific declines in North America.1,3 In Europe, a significant reduction of −14.1% in fertility rates was observed as of January 2021, with lockdown periods being a contributing factor.4 Regarding this temporary decline in fertility rates, surveys conducted in the early stages of the pandemic have revealed that many women postponed or abandoned their childbirth plans.5 6 Additionally, uncertainty about the future caused by the pandemic has been found to affect reproductive behaviour in various ways.

Consider the decrease in Japan’s fertility rate, along with the government’s responses to the pandemic and the declining fertility rate. Japan is a developed country facing an ageing population and a declining birth rate, and the total fertility rate (TFR), which showed recovery beginning in 2005, has been gradually decreasing since 2015.7 8 In 2016, the annual number of births fell below one million for the first time, and the TFR has been declining every year.9 10 In Japan, a series of states of emergency and priority measures have been enacted from April 2020 to March 2022. These measures have been implemented for varying durations and with different content depending on the prefectures, with stricter restrictions being imposed on urban areas. For instance, the first state of emergency was declared from 7 April 2020 to 25 May 2020, initially targeting seven prefectures, including Tokyo and Osaka, and was later extended to cover the entire nation.11 12 The state of emergency led to the government-initiated ‘Stay home’ campaign, where people were strongly encouraged to refrain from going out, a suggestion also evidenced through the utilisation of big data from mobile carriers, analysing population movement patterns.13,15 The decline in fertility rates during this period was similar to that in other developed countries, with a significant drop in fertility rates reported between December 2020 and February 2021, approximately 9 months after the state of emergency was declared.16 Japanese authorities declared a second state of emergency from 7 January 2021 to 21 March, 2021, followed by another from 25 April 2021 to 20 June 2021, targeting the Tokyo metropolitan and Kansai regions. Subsequently, the government intermittently enforced policies to curb infections, including reduced restaurant hours and event attendance limits, from February 2021 to March 2022.11 12 Restrictions varied by prefecture, with different durations and measures. The declaration and lifting of the state of emergency were based on daily infection rates and healthcare system pressures, with national broadcasts reporting the number of infections by prefecture.

While the recovery of fertility rates following the pandemic has been observed on a global scale, it is essential to recognise that this recovery varies across countries, as indicated in recent reports.17 As demonstrated by Sobotka et al, using the short-term fertility fluctuations data series, the decline in births worldwide due to the pandemic and the subsequent recovery of fertility rates have been documented.18 The fertility rates in several European countries demonstrated a swift recovery, which was subsequently succeeded by a gradual drop. In Japan, a sharp drop in the number of births occurred in January 2021 during the early stages of the pandemic, followed by a return to the pre-COVID trend of a gradual decline in birth numbers throughout 2021, with a further decline observed in 2022.18

The recovery of fertility rates may be influenced by various factors, such as the anxiety generated by the COVID-19 pandemic and the uncertainty regarding the future. Additionally, the changes in sociodemographic circumstances caused by the pandemic may have played a role in the recovery of fertility rates,19 20 rather than the virus itself. Therefore, it is essential to conduct further research to understand the underlying factors that contribute to the recovery of fertility rates. The recovery of fertility rates will have a significant impact on the policies that a given country will implement in the future because the decline in fertility rates affects subsequent economic growth and welfare systems.21 22

In light of the various infection control measures enacted across prefectures, this study aimed to analyse whether the temporary decline in fertility rates during the first state of emergency and the subsequent recovery rate were influenced by the spread of the infection, in addition to factors related to pregnancy intentions. Furthermore, we identify the patterns of fertility rate decline and subsequent recovery and analyse common factors to determine whether the characteristics of each prefecture influenced these patterns. This study focused on examining the influence of policies implemented by Japan, a country severely affected by population ageing, on the recovery of fertility rates.

Methods

Study design

The present ecological study aimed to investigate fertility rates at the prefectural level in Japan by employing national demographic data. The primary objective of this research was to elucidate the characteristics of the decline in fertility rates 9 months after the initial state of emergency due to COVID-19 and the subsequent fertility recovery rates at the prefectural level. To achieve this objective, this study hypothesises that anxiety related to the spread of COVID-19 influenced the decline in fertility rates and their subsequent recovery.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Data acquisition

The dataset for this study was obtained from the Japanese government’s statistical portal site e-stat.23 The monthly birth counts for each prefecture from 2017 to 2022 were captured from the monthly reports of the vital statistics survey.24 The population estimates provide yearly reproductive-aged populations from 15 to 49 years of age. Additionally, data on the explanatory variables for each prefecture were extracted from the social life statistics surveys conducted in 2020 or 2021.

Definition of fertility rate

This study defined monthly fertility rate as the number of births per month divided by the population of women of reproductive age (15–49 years) multiplied by 1000. Any population bias was corrected by adjusting the number of births for each prefecture with the reproductive-age population in that area. The fertility rate for the ninth month following the emergency declaration period was considered the fertility rate during the first state of emergency. The state of emergency of the government was in response to surging infections and healthcare strains. Given the rapid spread of infection and the number of new cases before this declaration, the study analysed fertility rates from 1 month before to 9 months after the declaration of the emergency. Thus, the fertility rates from December 2020 to February 2021 were classified as occurring during the first state of emergency (online supplemental figure 1). The annual change in average monthly fertility rate during the first state of emergency in each prefecture was compared with that from December 2019 to February 2020. Furthermore, all recorded births were live births, and intrauterine fetal deaths and stillbirths were excluded.

Seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average model and birth trends in Japan

The seasonal autoregressive integrated moving average (SARIMA) model is a statistical technique for analysing and forecasting time series data with seasonal patterns.25 26 Japan’s monthly fertility rate demonstrated a consistent pattern of annual fluctuations.27 Fertility rates in Japan were forecasted using the SARIMA model to account for temporal variations, seasonal patterns and trends in the monthly birth data. Data from 2017 to 2019 were used as pre-COVID-19 data to forecast fertility rates from 2020 to 2022. Differencing was applied to confirm data stationarity and understand trends and seasonality. The appropriate orders for the autoregressive and moving average components were estimated using autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation plots.28 Akaike information criterion and model fitness were used to compare multiple combinations of the non-seasonal and seasonal parameters to identify the optimal model.25 26 Through this process, the parameters of the SARIMA model that best fit the data were estimated. The observed-forecast difference ratio was calculated for each month from 2020 to 2022 by dividing the difference between the observed fertility rate and SARIMA-forecasted value by the observed value.

Fertility recovery rate

In this study, considering that the significant decline in fertility rates observed after the first state of emergency did not persist, we defined the period from March 2020 to December 2022 as the recovery period for fertility rates. To examine the long-term recovery of fertility rates after the first state of emergency, we defined the fertility recovery rate as the observed-forecast difference rate for each prefecture per month obtained using the SARIMA model; this approach considers temporal dynamics from past data and has the capability to detect unforeseen events, such as pandemics, demonstrating adaptability to disturbances.29

This metric is used to demonstrate the extent to which the observed fertility rate has returned in comparison to the forecasted fertility rate. The fertility recovery rate following the first state of emergency was defined as the average of the monthly observed forecast difference rates from March 2021 to December 2022.

Exposure

To assess anxiety about COVID-19 and uncertainty about the future, we used the number of new infections as a proxy variable. The declaration and lifting of the state of emergency were determined based on the number of new COVID-19 infections per capita and the strain on the medical institutions in each prefecture.30 31 Specifically, we used the daily number of new infections per capita available for each prefecture. The monthly number of infections was derived from the daily new infections per capita, and the monthly average was used. Additionally, considering that the number of new infections had already increased prior to the declaration of the state of emergency, we factored into the average number of new infections from the month preceding the declaration of each state of emergency. For the initial state of emergency, the new infection numbers were from March to May 2020, whereas for the subsequent recovery period, the new infection numbers were from April 2020 to March 2022.

Covariates related to pregnancy intention

In addition to the exposure variable of new infections per capita, we considered the impact of pregnancy intention, which can be influenced by sociodemographic factors, on the temporary decline in fertility rates and the fertility recovery rate. Therefore, we examined the relevant covariates. The factors influencing fertility rates included economic and social factors and motivation for pregnancy.32,34 Assuming their interrelated complexity, we conceptualised them in a flowing pattern (online supplemental figure 1) and referenced each variable based on prefecture-level data.

In categorising the various influences of reproductive age on pregnancy intention during the COVID-19 pandemic, we identified four principal categories. The first category pertains to population composition and family structure. Given Japan’s cultural emphasis on family structure when raising children,35 36 we included the following items in our analysis: Labour force participation rate of women aged 20–49, elderly dependency ratio for age >65, and nuclear family household percentage. The second category comprised marital status and gender equality, as Japan has one of the lowest marriage rates worldwide and a minimal participation rate of men in childcare.37,39 We posit that these factors contributed to the decrease in marriage rates and the intention to become pregnant during the pandemic. The number of marriages and divorces per capita, marriage/divorce rate, daily male parenting time, daily male housework time, and male commuting time were considered as the second category. The third category is the impact of economic factors.32 40 41 We selected the following economic variables: per capita education expenditure, consumer price index yearly change and average saving rate. The fourth category pertained to the fear of pregnancy during the COVID-19 pandemic. We reasoned that the fear of contracting the virus during pregnancy could have a negative impact on pregnancy intentions, and thus, we used Google, the most extensively used internet search engine in Japan,42 43 for our analysis. Google Trends allows users to quantify frequently searched words by digitising Google searches.44 45 We analysed the frequency of two search queries, ‘corona, pregnancy’ and ‘corona, vaccine pregnancy’ to track variations in interest and concerns related to pregnancy, reflecting shifts in trends and interests in specific regions and periods. In addition to examining the relationship between ‘Corona and pregnancy’, we also sought to explore concerns about the potential impact of COVID-19 vaccines on the developing fetus. To conduct this analysis, we employed two primary keywords and used the relative search volume metric to quantify the search volumes, with a maximum value of 100 assigned to each prefecture.

All of the above covariates were analysed using national data. Additionally, online supplemental table 1 provides a description of all variables, along with their mean values, standard deviations (SD), medians, years of data collection and data sources used.

Statistical analysis

Fertility rate predictions were obtained by fitting the fertility rates of each prefecture from the pre-COVID-19 period (2017–2019) to a SARIMA model. To examine the relationship between covariates that could potentially influence the monthly fertility rate and both the average monthly fertility rate change during the first state of emergency and the average fertility recovery rate after the state of emergency, we performed a simple regression analysis for each dependent variable. A robust regression analysis model was used to detect the coefficient value, 95% confidence interval (CI), coefficient of determination (R2) value and p value. Additionally, variance analysis of the main exposure and potential covariates was conducted using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. For the multivariate analysis, covariates with high correlation coefficients were excluded, and multivariate regression analysis was performed after selecting five factors related to pregnancy intention. We conducted a cluster analysis to examine the relationship between the average monthly fertility rate change during the first state of emergency and the average fertility recovery rate after the state of emergency across different prefectures. The cluster analysis was performed using the KMeans algorithm, which partitions a dataset into four predefined clusters based on the elbow method.46 47 The Euclidean distance was used as a measure of dissimilarity, and the results were validated using silhouette scores to assess the degree of separation between clusters.48 The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to test for significant differences in the mean values of covariates affecting fertility rates among the four clusters, as the data followed a non-normal distribution. The Kruskal-Wallis test statistics (H) and p values were determined. SARIMA-based fertility rate forecasting and cluster analysis were performed using Python, V.3.8.16, whereas other analyses were conducted using two-tailed significance tests (ɑ=0.05) with SAS V.9.4 TS1M8 (V.9.4 M8).

Results

Changes in fertility rates before and during COVID-19 pandemic

Online supplemental figure 2 depicts the changes in fertility rates from 2017 to 2019, revealing a national average annual fertility rate change decrease of −3.18% (SD: 0.84, Min −5.25, Max −1.37). A comparison of the average monthly fertility rates 9 months after each state of emergency declaration to the rates in the same months in 2019 (figure 1A) showed a −13.82% decrease (SD: 6.26, Min: −28.78, Max: 0.15) nationwide.

Fertility rate change and sociodemographic factors

We analysed the correlation between the annual change in the average monthly fertility rate during the first state of emergency and the sociodemographic variables associated with pregnancy intention using univariate regression analysis (table 1). The characteristics of prefectures experiencing a more significant decline in average monthly fertility rates during the initial state of emergency included a higher number of new COVID-19 cases per capita, a lower percentage of elderly individuals aged 65 and above, a higher marriage-to-divorce ratio, a lower divorce rate, a lower unemployment rate, reduced educational expenditures, longer commuting times for men and fewer internet searches related to pregnancy intentions. Examination of the average monthly fertility recovery rate and related variables did not reveal any correlation with the emergence of new COVID-19 cases or the length of the state of emergency. Among the various pregnancy-related factors, only the pre-COVID fertility rate change was negatively correlated with the average fertility recovery rate.

Multivariate analysis of new COVID-19 cases and fertility rate changes

The correlation coefficients between the covariates and outcomes were examined (online supplemental figure 4). Considering multicollinearity, the following five factors that may potentially influence changes in the fertility rate were selected: nuclear family household percentage, marriage-to-divorce ratio, unemployment rate, internet search volume, and per capita new COVID-19 cases. These factors were subsequently used in the regression analysis of the fertility rates. The average monthly change in the fertility rate during the first state of emergency showed a negative relationship with the number of new COVID-19 cases per capita (e=−0.78, 95% CI −1.50 to –0.07) (table 1). However, after multivariate analysis with sociodemographic covariates (table 3), the effect size increased (e=−0.95, 95% CI −1.75, −0.16). The proportion of nuclear families and internet search volume in each prefecture showed a significant negative relationship with the change in fertility rate.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that the annual change in the average monthly fertility rate during the first state of emergency showed a 13% decrease compared with the previous year, although disparities were observed at the prefecture level. Furthermore, during the subsequent 22 months, when states of emergency were intermittently declared and activity restrictions were imposed, the actual fertility rates per reproductive age population were greater than those predicted by the prediction model, with a nationwide average difference of 2.31%. Previous research has documented the short-term resurgence of fertility rates following the pandemic, and it is well established that unforeseen occurrences and shifts in living standards can influence subsequent fertility recovery.49 50 In Japan, a prolonged period of social restrictions and anxiety related to the spread of COVID-19 could have led to changes in pregnancy behaviour. Nevertheless, the long-term recovery in fertility rates in Japan was unexpectedly higher than forecasted. Moreover, the total number of days under the state of emergency did not affect the fertility recovery rate, suggesting that anxiety regarding the spread of infection may not have played a significant role in the long-term recovery of fertility rates. The observed fertility rate was almost equivalent to the forecasted fertility rate, indicating a return to the pre-COVID-19 declining trend from 2017 to 2019. The exact reasons for the lack of a significant decline in the fertility recovery rate are uncertain, but Japan’s economic support during the COVID-19 pandemic, such as household support, increased child allowances and wage compensation, likely alleviated household anxiety and reduced factors delaying childbirth. Further research is required to gain a more thorough understanding of this matter.

Our study used internet search volume as a variable to assess the impact of information gathering on the desire to conceive. The results of cluster analysis indicated that prefectures with high search volumes related to pregnancy during the pandemic experienced a small decline in fertility rates under the state of emergency and showed high recovery rates. Therefore, online searches for pregnancy-related information during the pandemic may have played a role in the changes in fertility rates. The millennial generation primarily relies on the internet for making important life decisions,51 52 and this trend has become even more pronounced during the pandemic.53 54 News about severe disease in pregnant individuals and the spread of false information about vaccines55,57 may have influenced couples’ decisions to postpone pregnancy. Pregnant women infected with COVID-19 during the pandemic reportedly experienced mild symptoms, with rare cases of intensive care unit admissions or death.58,60 However, concerns about droplet transmission during childbirth in Japan led to caesarean sections being performed in almost all cases at the start of the pandemic, potentially affecting the country’s fertility rate.61 62 Moving forward, couples considering pregnancy must be provided accurate information and address any fears and anxieties that may arise during the pandemic.

The COVID-19 pandemic has had varying effects on fertility rates worldwide. Contrary to the belief that lockdowns would result in more sexual activity and subsequently higher fertility rates,63 the stress stemming from social, health and economic crises prompted many couples to delay pregnancy.64 65 This finding underscores the influence of socioeconomic disparities and societal circumstances on the decision to procreate in contemporary society. Japan has a strong cultural tradition of valuing family composition and multigenerational households. The ‘satogaeri childbirth’ culture, where childbirth occurs in the parents’ hometown, is also prevalent in nuclear family households.66 During the pandemic, movement restrictions and calls for self-restraint that prevented people from receiving support from their families may have further hindered the childbirth rates.62 Japan’s support system for childbirth and child-rearing, including support from the elderly, was crucial during the pandemic. The decrease in the number of marriages was pronounced during the state of emergency, and regions with high marriage/divorce rates experienced a pronounced decrease in fertility rates. Despite being married, couples hesitated to have children and tended to postpone pregnancy due to pandemic-related uncertainties. In Japan, where extramarital births are rare, a decrease in the number of marriages also affects birth numbers. Large-scale data showed that 20% of those who hesitated to conceive among reproductive age groups.67 Overall, our findings suggest that Japan’s support system for childbirth and child-rearing was critical during the pandemic, and a decrease in the number of marriages had a significant impact on fertility rates.

The fertility recovery rate after state of emergency was greater than the forecasted values. Nevertheless, the overall downward trend in fertility rates, which began prior to the pandemic, has not been reversed. Factors such as ‘partner support’, ‘sense of hopelessness’ and ‘concerns about health status’ have been reported to affect the well-being of pregnant women.68 Inadequate postpartum care can affect future pregnancy intention. Providing interconception care is essential for maintaining or increasing future fertility rates.69 70 Postpartum care needs to be urgently improved by addressing concerns about pregnancy and familial support.

The strengths and limitations of this paper are as follows: First, the findings are based on data collected at the prefectural level, enabling a comprehensive examination of fertility rates and related variables. Although the concentration of Japan’s population in urban areas could have provided an effective means for reflecting the social situation, this aspect was not covered by the scope of the study. By employing the SARIMA model to forecast fertility rates, this study determined the fertility recovery rate, accounting for the impact of COVID-19, which could not be adjusted by year-on-year changes. The study identified prefectural differences in recovery rates; however, the limited number of regions constrained the number of covariates. Despite adjusting for several factors, we could not establish a statistically significant relationship between the number of infections and decline in fertility rates. The fertility rate is influenced by various factors, and while the study incorporated internet search to understand the desire for pregnancy, other factors such as the rate of reduction in assisted reproductive technology and financial support by region also impact fertility rates, warranting further investigation.

Conclusion

The temporary decrease in fertility rates in Japan following the initial state of emergency due to the COVID-19 pandemic had an impact on almost the entire country. This decrease has been linked to various factors, such as the number of new COVID-19 cases, family structure and the frequency of internet searches related to pregnancy intentions. However, the subsequent recovery of fertility rates was not influenced by the number of new cases during the recovery period or the duration of the state of emergency. Nevertheless, differences in the patterns of recovery were associated with demographic composition and Internet searches related to infection and pregnancy in different prefectures.
