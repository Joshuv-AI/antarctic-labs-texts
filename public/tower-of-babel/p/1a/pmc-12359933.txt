Clinical study on precision repair of orbital fractures using 3D-printed orbital models assisting with absorbable plates
Authors: Yiyi Chen, Wei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the surgical precision and postoperative safety of 3D-printed patient-specific orbital model-assisted absorbable plate reconstruction for orbital wall fractures.MethodsThe study included 94 orbital fracture patients divided into two groups: (1) 3D-assisted group (n = 44): treated with absorbable plate reconstruction assisted by 3D-printed orbital models.(2) Conventional group (n = 50): underwent standard absorbable plate reconstruction via traditional surgical approach. Postoperative evaluations at 6–11 months included standardized assessment of diplopia severity, ocular motility impairment, enophthalmos magnitude (Hertel exophthalmometry), and orbital volumetric restoration (CT-based segmentation analysis).ResultsAll patients achieved successful surgical outcomes with radiographic confirmation of anatomical reduction via postoperative CT. No cases exhibited postoperative complications including infection, implant displacement, or vision deterioration. Comparative analysis revealed both cohorts demonstrated statistically significant improvements in enophthalmos correction and orbital volume restoration compared to preoperative measurements (P < 0.001). The 3D-assisted group showed superior enophthalmos correction relative to conventional treatment (P = 0.014 < 0.05), along with greater orbital volume restoration (P = 0.008 < 0.05). Diplopia severity and ocular motility limitations were significantly ameliorated in both groups postoperatively (P < 0.001), with the 3D-assisted group achieving better diplopia resolution (P = 0.023 < 0.05) and ocular mobility restoration (P = 0.049 < 0.05) compared to the conventional group. Notably, the 3D-assisted procedure required significantly shorter operative duration than conventional methods (P < 0.001).ConclusionThe 3D-printed patient-specific orbital model-assisted absorbable plate reconstruction demonstrates superior clinical efficacy to conventional orbital fracture repair, providing precise anatomical restoration with enhanced enophthalmos correction and orbital volumetric accuracy. This technique significantly reduces operative time while achieving better functional outcomes in diplopia resolution and ocular motility recovery. The absence of implant-related complications confirms its procedural safety, establishing this modality as a valuable adjunct in orbital reconstruction surgery.

Introduction

The escalating incidence of orbital fractures parallels advancements in industrial and transportation sectors, predominantly attributed to occupational trauma and road traffic collisions [1]. Surgical intervention remains the cornerstone for orbital fracture management, yet challenges persist due to the region’s intricate osteo-anatomical configuration. Conventional reconstruction techniques, heavily reliant on surgeon experience for defect approximation, carry inherent technical challenges and operative risks, often yielding inconsistent anatomical restoration outcomes [2]. These limitations underscore the critical need for innovative approaches to optimize surgical precision while ensuring procedural safety.

As an additive manufacturing modality integrating computer-aided design (CAD), computer-aided manufacturing (CAM), and biocompatible polymers, 3D printing enables fabrication of anatomically precise 1:1 orbital replicas derived from high-resolution CT datasets. Preoperative plate contouring using these biomodels allows for accurate osteosynthesis device customization, thereby optimizing intraoperative efficiency through reduced manipulation time while enhancing anatomical fidelity. This prospective study therefore investigates the clinical application of 3D-printed patient-specific orbital model-assisted absorbable plate reconstruction, with dual endpoints assessing topographic accuracy and procedural safety, aiming to establish an evidence-based protocol for precision orbital restoration.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This retrospective study analyzed 94 consecutive orbital fracture patients (94 eyes) treated at Yangzhou University Affiliated Hospital and Suqian First People’s Hospital between April 2017 and May 2024. Preoperative CT imaging confirmed orbital wall defects with herniated orbital tissues. Inclusion criteria required CT-documented fractures with ≥ 2 mm enophthalmos, restricted ocular motility or diplopia, positive forced duction testing, voluntary surgical participation with informed consent, and ethics committee approval (2020-VKL04-Y005). Exclusion criteria encompassed severe comorbidities involving vital organs (cardiovascular, cerebral, hepatic, or renal), pregnancy/lactation status, active ocular inflammation, optic nerve injuries, bilateral fractures, and other contraindications to study protocols.

Methods

(1) Preoperative modeling: Preoperative CT scans were performed in all patients. 3D printing production process: ① Export CT data in DICOM format and perform 3D reconstruction using Mimics software. ② Model processing. ③ Plan confirmation. ④ Add printing supports. ⑤ Develop the printing program. ⑥ Print on the machine. ⑦ Printing completed. ⑧ Support removal and cleaning. ⑨ Re-curing. ⑩ Burr grinding. The printing is finally completed. Each step in the above process is completed by the corresponding same operator. The printer model is: YL CD3540, independently developed by Shanghai Yilun 3D Co., LTD. Printing accuracy: 0.05 mm, forming principle: LCD light curing forming. Printing material: DB-07 photosensitive resin (special for surgical guides), in compliance with biocompatibility (Test report number: CSTBB2022120191). Clinical outcomes (diplopia, ocular motility limitation, periocular numbness) were recorded before surgery and 6–11 months after surgery, along with intraoperative/postoperative complications. Procedure

Prophylactic antibiotics were administered preoperatively to all patients undergoing general anesthesia, with procedures performed by the same senior oculoplastic surgeon via a transconjunctival approach. For medial orbital wall fractures (isolated or combined), medial conjunctival incisions were extended to access the fracture site. After periosteal incision and subperiosteal dissection, herniated soft tissues were meticulously reduced, followed by removal of displaced bone fragments. In the conventional fracture repair group, the absorbable plate was trimmed and implanted based on the surgeon’s experience (it may need to be adjusted multiple times according to the intraoperative situation). After satisfaction, the implant was fixed with absorbable screws after checking that the eyeball was not incarcerated. In the 3D group, the absorbable plate was cut on the 3D head model to make the size and shape highly fit the orbital fracture area of the patient. Then, the fracture area was implanted. It was checked that there was no incarceration of the eyeball, and finally the absorbable screw was used to fix the implant. During the operation, it is necessary to pay attention to protecting the infraorbital nerve, inferior oblique muscle, etc. Closure involved layered suturing of periosteal and conjunctival layers, with postoperative care comprising application of tobramycin-dexamethasone ointment, pressure dressing, and structured ocular motility training (200 repetitions daily for two months).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.5. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality distribution was verified through Shapiro-Wilk testing. Parametric comparisons (independent/paired t-tests) were applied to normally distributed data, while nonparametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney U/Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) were used for non-normal distributions. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages), analyzed using χ² test for independent groups and McNemar’s test for paired comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed P < 0.05. The analytical framework followed small-sample methodology guidelines (n < 2000) to ensure methodological validity of inferences.

Basic information of the patients

The baseline data of 94 patients with orbital fractures were balanced (Table 1). Among the causes of injury, there were 52 cases of traffic accident injuries, 18 cases of boxing injuries, 15 cases of injuries caused by hard object strikes, 6 cases of injuries from self-falls, and 3 cases of injuries from falls from heights (Table 2). Traffic accident injuries were the primary injury factor causing orbital fractures. All 94 patients had simple orbital blowout fractures, and the fracture sites included the medial wall, inferior wall, medial wall and inferior wall (Fig. 2). The median time from injury to surgery for patients in the 3D group was M = 9.00 days, and the quartiles were Q1 = 6.00 days and Q3 = 12.00 days respectively. The median time from injury to surgery for patients in the conventional group was M = 10.00 days, and the quartiles were Q1 = 8.00 days and Q3 = 13.25 days respectively.

Discussions

The orbital complex comprises seven bones forming superior, inferior, medial, and lateral walls, with the thin lamina papyracea (medial wall) and orbital floor being particularly susceptible to fracture due to their reduced osseous thickness [4]. These walls contain critical neurovascular foramina transmitting ophthalmic vessels and cranial nerves essential for visual function. Clinically significant fractures often manifest with diplopia, ocular motility impairment, and enophthalmos secondary to volumetric displacement of orbital contents [5]. In the early stage of a fracture, conservative treatment with drugs and functional exercises can be feasible. However, if diplopia persists and imaging indicates significant incarceration or herniation of the extraocular muscles and/or soft tissues into the paranasal sinuses, surgical treatment is required [6].

Conventional reconstruction techniques rely on intraoperative implant contouring guided by two-dimensional CT interpretation, requiring iterative adjustments through repeated surgical site access [7]. This approach introduces three principal limitations: (1) Subjective intraoperative decision-making increases soft tissue trauma from repeated instrument manipulation [8]; (2) The intricate orbital geometry challenges accurate three-dimensional implant shaping without anatomical references; (3) Prolonged tissue manipulation elevates risks of postoperative fibrosis and neurovascular injury. These technical constraints underscore the critical need for innovative solutions that simultaneously achieve precise anatomical restoration, minimize iatrogenic trauma, and optimize functional rehabilitation-enduring challenges in oculoplastic surgery.

In recent years, a variety of materials have been applied for the reconstruction of orbital blowout fractures, with preformed titanium mesh and resorbable plates being among the most commonly used. Each material type possesses distinct advantages and disadvantages [9]. Resorbable materials can gradually degrade and be absorbed by the body, thereby eliminating the need for secondary removal surgery and reducing patient morbidity as well as healthcare burden [10]. Furthermore, the inherent malleability of resorbable materials allows surgeons to readily tailor their shape and size intraoperatively to accommodate the specific fracture pattern encountered in each patient [11]. Postoperative infection rates are low, and these materials do not interfere with skeletal growth and development in pediatric patients [12]. These advantages render resorbable materials an ideal choice for orbital fracture reconstruction. Regardless of the material selected, the clinical emphasis is increasingly placed on utilizing computer-assisted techniques to compare anatomical positions, refine implant shape and positioning, and achieve precise anatomical reconstruction of orbital fractures.

Three-dimensional (3D) printing technology, also recognized as additive manufacturing, has emerged as a transformative tool in craniofacial reconstruction, with particularly profound applications in orbital wall defect rehabilitation [13]. For patients with orbital fractures, 3D printing technology enables reconstruction using anatomical models of the fractured orbit or mirrored images of the unaffected contralateral orbit [6]. These 3D-printed biomodels, characterized by anatomical precision, facilitate preoperative contouring and customization of implants to achieve optimal adaptation to individual orbital anatomy and defect dimensions, thereby theoretically improving postoperative functional and clinical outcomes [14]. Furthermore, the 1:1 orbital models provide patients with three-dimensional visualization of fracture patterns, enhancing preoperative communication and informed consent. Quantitative analysis of orbital volume discrepancies between the affected and healthy orbits guides implant design and volume restoration, significantly streamlining preoperative planning [5]. This technique demonstrates versatility across fracture complexities, from simple defects to comminuted fractures. Although associated with increased procedural costs, most of patients prioritize surgical precision over financial considerations [15], particularly those with complex fractures involving large defect areas, deep orbital apex involvement, or significant enophthalmos, where the technical advantages prove most pronounced [16].

The findings of this investigation substantiate the clinical superiority of 3D printing-assisted absorbable plate reconstruction in orbital fracture management. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that preoperatively customized implants in the 3D cohort achieved near-anatomical congruence with defect margins, resulting in significantly enhanced restoration of orbital geometry compared to conventional freehand techniques. Specifically, the 3D-assisted group exhibited superior enophthalmos correction (P < 0.001) and orbital volumetric restoration (P < 0.001), attributable to the precision of defect-specific implant contouring enabled by patient-specific biomodels. Functional outcomes paralleled anatomical improvements, with both cohorts showing significant postoperative resolution of diplopia (P < 0.001) and ocular motility limitations (P < 0.001). However, the 3D-assisted approach yielded superior functional recovery, evidenced by higher rates of complete diplopia resolution (P < 0.05) and ocular motility restoration (P < 0.05). Operative efficiency metrics further underscored the technical advantages of this methodology. The 3D cohort required substantially shorter surgical duration (P < 0.001), reflecting reduced intraoperative adjustments through preoperative implant optimization. Existing evidence confirms that preoperative 3D printing-assisted customization of orbital implants improves surgical outcomes through enhanced precision [17]. By creating patient-specific biomodels, this technology achieves optimal adaptation of absorbable plates to fracture defects, resulting in improved orbital volume restoration and higher defect coverage accuracy. These advancements correlate with superior correction rates for enophthalmos and diplosis, alongside reduced operative time and complication rates—findings consistent with our study results. Building upon conventional surgical protocols, this study innovatively integrates 3D printing technology to fabricate patient-specific anatomical models of orbital injuries. These high-fidelity biomodels enable surgeons to perform meticulous preoperative contouring of absorbable plates. Such technical refinement ensures complete anatomical restoration of fractured orbital walls, particularly critical for reconstructing the complex curvature of the medial wall and orbital floor junction. This paradigm shift from experience-based surgery to computational precision not only enhances immediate postoperative outcomes but also establishes a standardized framework for orbital trauma management. The integration of 3D-printed patient-specific orbital models with absorbable plate technology demonstrates significant potential to become the preferred surgical approach for orbital fracture reconstruction. As a retrospective study, material selection may be limited by selection bias due to surgeon experience and individual patient needs. To enhance reliability, future prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs) could assign implants via randomization, controlling confounders and elevating evidence levels. However, full randomization may be impractical due to ethical constraints and patient compliance. Therefore, multicenter studies are recommended, expanding sample sizes, standardizing inclusion criteria, and harmonizing surgical procedures to validate generalizability and provide higher-quality evidence.

In conclusion, the integration of 3D-printed anatomical orbital models as surgical templates for absorbable plate reconstruction offers a clinically viable approach to orbital wall fracture repair. This methodology enables preoperative precision planning, significantly optimizing surgical efficiency while improving functional outcomes such as diplopia resolution and ocular motility restoration. By achieving enhanced anatomical congruence between implants and defect topography, the technique refines orbital volumetric symmetry, ultimately advancing the paradigm of personalized, precision-targeted treatment in oculofacial trauma management.
