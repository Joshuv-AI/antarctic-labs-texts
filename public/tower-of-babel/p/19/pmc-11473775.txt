Developmentally controlled subcellular remodeling and VND-initiated vacuole-executed PCD module shape xylem-like cells in peat moss
Authors: Ningjing Liu, Qiuqi Guo, Fangming Shi, Lei Gao, Yongqi Liu, Yiwen Wang, Zhiwei Gong, Haoran Liu, Yue Sun, Bosheng Li, Bing Ni, Rui-Liang Zhu, Qiong Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeat moss (Sphagnum) is a non-vascular higher plant with unique xylem-like hyaline (H) cells that are accompanied by photosynthetic chlorophyllous cells. These cellular structures play crucial roles in water storage and carbon sequestration. However, it is largely unknown how peat moss develops the H cells. This study systematically explored the Sphagnum Developmental Cell Atlas and Lineage and classified leaf cell development into two lineages with six stages (S0-S5) based on changes in key cellular traits, including the formation of spiral secondary cell walls (S4) and the presence of water pores (S5). Cell lineage-specific subcellular remodeling was transcriptionally regulated during leaf development, and vacuole-mediated clearance of organelles and cell death led to mature dead H cells. Interestingly, expression of land plant conserved Vascular-related NAC Domain (VND) genes correlated with H cell formation. Overall, these results suggest that the origination of xylem-like H cells is related to VND, likely through the neofunctionalization of vacuole-mediated cell death to attempt xylem formation in peat moss, suggesting potential uncoupling of xylem and phloem cell origins. This study positions peat moss as a potential model organism for studying integrative evolutionary cell biology.Subject terms: Protein trafficking in plants, Cell fate

Introduction

Sphagnum moss, also known as peat moss belonging to Bryophyta, plays an important ecological role1–3. It creates specific conditions in peatlands that limit the growth of other species and enable carbon sequestration1–3. The Sphagnum genus consists of about 300 species and has a high water absorption capacity3. The uppermost region of the plant, known as the capitulum with several sub-capitulums, consists of the youngest leaves surrounding the center apical cell and shows high physiological activity1,4,5. The growth of Sphagnum is characterized by apical infinite growth, and the leaves of Sphagnum consist of a single layer of interconnected two types of cells: chlorophyllous (C) cells, which carry out photosynthesis, and xylem-like hyaline (H) cells, which are large and dead, having thickened spiral secondary cell wall and water pores to provide support and store water6. The unique formation of hyaline cells in Sphagnum distinguishes it from other bryophytes and allows Sphagnum to absorb 25 times its dry weight in water6,7. Microbes inhabit the water-filled hyaline cells and play a crucial role in the plant’s ecological functions8. The unique cellular traits of Sphagnum contribute to its roles in regulating water flow, storing carbon, and cycling nutrients in peatland ecosystems1,7,9. Interestingly, as a bryophyte, Sphagnum lacks true vascular tissue but has xylem-like hyaline cells, yet the formation of the cell pattern is still not fully understood.

Previous studies have shown that hyaline cell formation in Sphagnum involves key processes like cell expansion, thickening of the secondary cell wall, and programmed cell death (PCD) with water pores10. Over the years, several regulators of PCD have been identified in plants, and many of these regulators are associated with the endomembrane system (ES) that is highly conserved in eukaryotes, highlighting the fundamental role of the ES, especially the vacuoles, in cell death processes11. The vacuole is the largest essential multifunctional endomembrane compartment and often occupies up to 90% of the mature plant cell volume12. It primarily functions as a fluid-filled sac for storing water, nutrients, and waste products13,14. Recent studies suggest that vacuole-mediated subcellular remodeling plays a crucial role in determining cell fate, regulating cell size, and shaping cell structure15–22. The molecular machinery responsible for vacuole formation is conserved in land plants, including bryophytes15.

Plants use vacuoles for PCD through collapse and constructive mechanisms23. Collapse happens when the vacuolar membrane collapses, releasing hydrolytic enzymes into the cytosol, causing rapid cell collapse. On the other hand, the constructive pathway resembles developmental cell death and creates structurally functional dead cells like tracheary elements and Sphagnum hyaline cells. However, it is unclear how vacuole-related pathways contribute to peat moss hyaline cell development.

Functional dead cells that result from constructive PCD are commonly found in land plants, including xylem cells in angiosperms, cotton fiber cells, tracheids in gymnosperms, hydroids in bryophytes, peristomes in moss, and elaters in liverworts24–26. These cells shared key cytological features of highly modified cell walls, such as thickened secondary cell walls (SCWs) and programmed cell death (PCD) during cell maturation24–26. The structural and mechanical features of these cells, including hollow structures with a rigid outer shell, make them well-suited for water and force conductivity 27. Understanding the mechanisms that govern the differentiation of these cells remains a challenging task and is still under intensive investigation. The main mechanistic concept is based on establishing a laboratory-based system that induces the differentiation of xylem vessel cells, which allowed for the identification of the VND (Vascular-related NAC Domain)-based transcription network as the central regulatory mechanism responsible for xylem differential in vascular plants28. The VND genes are plant-specific NAC transcription factors that belong to the VNS (VND, NST/SND, SMB-related) subfamily (Ohtani et al.29). The VND transcriptional network is highly conserved across all land plants and is believed to play a crucial role in determining the differentiation of these cells30. However, there is still a lack of evidence to support whether and how VND may regulate hyaline cell differentiation of peat moss.

The programmed development of ES-related subcellular remodeling likely plays a major role in forming Sphagnum’s dimorphic cells. To investigate this, we explored Sphagnum Developmental Cell Atlas and Lineage (SDCAL) using integrative approaches. The process of Sphagnum leaf cell development can be summarized into four types of leaves, two types of cells, and three types of cell activities. The developmental unit is formed in the early S3 stage, consisting of two C cells and one H cell, which sets the foundation for the unique Sphagnum leaf cell pattern. Subsequent significant cell expansion of both cell types is a prerequisite for further differentiation. Distinct modes of remodeling subcellular structures play an important role in shaping and determining the function of different cell types. Further transcriptomic analysis revealed that the developmental transcriptional control of key endomembrane pathways, specifically the vacuole-related pathway, coordinated the development of Sphagnum’s dimorphic cells, supporting the importance of vacuoles in cell maturation, particularly in H cells. Interestingly, the expression of the VND genes was correlated with the formation of peat moss hyaline cells. Overall, our findings suggest that the formation of dimorphic cells in Sphagnum involves multiple processes, including programmed transcriptional regulation and precisely controlled ES-mediated subcellular remodeling. Our results also indicate that the origin of xylem-like hyaline cells in peat moss is likely related to VND and may involve the neofunctionalization of the eukaryotic conserved ES-mediated cell death pathway. This study positions peat moss as a potential model organism for the study of integrative evolutionary cell biology.

Results

The Sphagnum Developmental Cell Atlas and Lineage (SDCAL) of the leaf cells

To characterize Sphagnum cell development, we established the Sphagnum Developmental Cell Atlas and Lineage (SDCAL) focusing on leaf cells and summarized the whole developmental process into four types of leaves, two types of cells, and three types of cell activities. Four representative Sphagnum species had typical sub-capitula with characteristic interconnected C and H cells in the leaves (Supplementary Fig. 1a–p, Fig. 1a). Next, focusing on S. palustre as a reference species, from outside inward based on leaf maturity, the sub-capitulum was divided into four parts: mature leaves (ML), young leaves (YL), bud leaves (BL) and bud core (BC) (Fig. 1). Cell age aligned well with leaf age, with the core center, which exhibited multi-cell-layered structures surrounding a single apical cell, serving as the developmental starting point (Fig. 1m–q).

Based on the spatial trajectory of the Sphagnum sub-capitulum growing from the center outward, the entire process of Sphagnum leaf cell development was summarized into six stages (S0 to S5) within three types of cell activities: cell division, cell growth, and dimorphic cell formation (maturation) (Figs. 1, 2). The cell fates (differentiation) of the C and H cells were determined at S2 after two rounds of asymmetric cell divisions (Figs. 1, 2; Supplementary Fig. 2). The formation of the developmental unit, which consists of two C cells and one H cell at the early S3 stage, sets the basis for the unique Sphagnum leaf cell pattern, and the subsequent significant cell expansion of both cell types is a prerequisite for further growth (Fig. 2). Subsequently in S3-S5, the C cells expanded and increased the number of chloroplasts while the expanded H cell underwent xylem-like programmed cell death, signifying distinct developmental trajectories (Figs. 1, 2; Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings provide a spatial cell lineage map of Sphagnum leaf development and demonstrate the temporal sequences of each stage.

Cell lineage-specific remodeling of subcellular structures shape and determine the two cell types in Sphagnum

To investigate our inquiry as to how subcellular remodeling contributes to Sphagnum dimorphic cell development, subcellular changes were observed in S. palustre cells using combined imaging techniques (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 2). We discovered cell lineage-specific changes in subcellular structures, including the nucleus, vacuole, chloroplast, and mitochondria. Distinct changes in the size of the nucleus were observed during the development process from S0 to S4. Small equiaxed nuclei were generated through symmetrical division in C0-I cells. Elongated elliptical nuclei were observed in expanded C0-II cells. Two daughter cells with varying sizes and potentials were produced from C0-II cells. The size of C cell nuclei remained consistent from stages S3 to S5, while the size of H cell nuclei increased at S4 and eventually disappeared at S5 (Fig. 3a, c). Distinct changes in the size, numbers, and actions of vacuoles were more noticeable. To both lineages of cells, multiple small vacuoles were observed in the division stages (S0-S2), while few large vacuoles were observed during differentiation stages (S3-S4), indicating that vacuoles gradually fused during cell development. The H cell seemed to have more activated vacuole fusion, as a larger occupied vacuole volume was observed in S3-S4 (Fig. 3b, e; Supplementary Fig. 4). In S5, C cell vacuoles remained intact, while H cell vacuoles ruptured and disappeared (Fig. 3b, e). The number of chloroplasts in C cells gradually increased, while in H cells, they became deformed and gradually disappeared in S4. For mitochondria, cells in the division stage had a higher density of mitochondria compared to other stages (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 5), and a dense area appeared in the mitochondrial matrix in S3-S4 H cells, indicating mitochondrial dysfunction and impending cellular demise (Fig. 3f; Supplementary Fig. 5). In summary, distinct modes of subcellular remodeling were crucial for the formation of two cell types during the development of dimorphic cells in Sphagnum.

Comparative analysis of Sphagnum dimorphic cell development transcriptomes revealed important transcriptional regulation

To understand the molecular mechanisms of Sphagnum dimorphic cell development, we analyzed the gene expression profiles in buds, young leaves (YL), and mature leaves (ML) (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Data 1). Comparing buds, YL, and ML, we found distinct genetic profiles associated with different stages of growth and development. Buds had genes related to cell division, YL had genes related to cell growth (elongation/expansion), and ML had genes related to photosynthesis (Fig. 4b, c; Supplementary Figs. 7, 8). To further understand the characteristics of different cell stages, we compared (Bud plus ML) vs YL, (YL plus ML) vs Bud, and (Bud plus YL) vs ML (Fig. 4d; Supplementary Figs. 7, 8). Heatmaps of gene expression confirmed these findings, with specific genes upregulated in each stage (Fig. 4e). Upregulated genes in the bud (S0-S2) included kinetochore proteins, kinesin-like proteins, synaptosomal-associated proteins (SNAPs), mitotic checkpoint proteins, and cell division proteins (Fig. 4e). Interestingly, in YL S3-S4 cells, highly expressed genes included those encoding tracheary element (TE) differentiation transcriptome factors (such as VNS transcription factor and C2H2-type zinc finger), cytoskeleton-related filament-like plant proteins, and proteins involved in glucuronoxylan biosynthetic processes (Fig. 4e), indicating a robust cellular growth in this particular phase. These genes participating in important redox reactions (thioredoxin, peroxiredoxin, glutaredoxin), photosystem I/II, and carbon assimilation (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylases) were broadly expressed in the ML S5-C cells (refer to Fig. 4e). In brief, these results showed gene expression profiles align well with cell activities in buds, YL, and ML: active cell division in buds (S0-S2), specialized functions and maturation processes in YL (S3-S4), and the important role of ML (S5) in photosynthesis. Additionally, specific genes involved in cell division, growth, differentiation, redox reactions, and carbon assimilation were identified, offering valuable insights into the genetic processes governing Sphagnum growth and development.

Vacuole-executed organelle degradation contributes to H cell maturation

Vacuoles may play similar digestive roles in hyaline cell PCD process. To verify this, a 3D reconstruction method was used to visualize subcellular structures at S3 stage, when notable differences between the two cell types became evident (Fig. 5a–d). Approximately half of the entire cell volume was reconstructed at the ultrastructural level for the S3-C-cell and S3-H-cell, respectively (Fig. 5) with the mini-3D models for the S3-C cell with 20 slices and the S3-H cell with 30 slices demonstrated the relative size and location of the organelles in detail (Fig. 5a–d; Supplementary Fig. 9). In contrast to the S3-C cell, the S3-H cell had a higher volume of large vacuoles, a nucleus with multiple small irregular pores on its surface, and deformed chloroplasts and mitochondria. These observations indicate that the S3-H cell displays typical degradative features with enlarged vacuoles (Fig. 5a–d). Compared with normal chloroplasts, the damaged chloroplasts were engulfed by surrounding vacuoles and gradually digested in the vacuoles (Fig. 5e–j; Supplementary Videos 1–3). These observations support the idea that vacuoles play a direct role in digesting damaged chloroplasts during Sphagnum H cell development.

At the transcription level, enzymes that break down carbohydrates (glycosidases) and proteins involved in protein degradation, found in the vacuoles, were predominantly expressed during vacuole development (S3-S5) (Fig. 5k, l). Furthermore, the majority of genes associated with vacuole formation showed high levels of expression during leaf development, which perfectly aligned with vacuole development during Sphagnum cell maturation (S0-S4) (Supplementary Fig. 10). Disturbing the vacuole (marked with FM4-64 staining) development via abscisic acid (ABA)16 and E-64d31,32 led to abnormal plant growth (Supplementary Fig. 11). These results indicate that the coordinated transcriptional and structural regulation of vacuole size, morphology, and functions facilitated the development of hyaline cells.

Possible neofunctionalization of conserved vacuole-mediated programmed cell death (PCD) to develop hyaline cells in Sphagnum

Functional dead cells resulting from programmed cell death (PCD) are commonly found in land plants, such as xylem cells in angiosperms, cotton fiber cells, moss peristomes, liverwort elaters, and Sphagnum hyaline cells (Fig. 6a). These cells maintain their intact cell wall structures, allowing efficient water and force conductivity for physiological purposes. However, the presence of spiral secondary cell walls, water pores, high water retention capacity, and long-term preservation ability suggest that the hyaline cells are more similar to tracheophyte xylem cells. This indicates a likely shared cellular and molecular mechanism in the formation of hyaline cells and xylem cells. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed gene presence or absence variations (PAVs) related to xylem development in 27 representative plant species (Fig. 6b). These species include 9 algae, 11 bryophytes, and 7 tracheophytes (Fig. 6b). The gene list includes proteins involved in vacuole biogenesis, transcriptional regulation, and lignin biosynthesis (Fig. 6b). Results showed that proteins related to vacuole biogenesis were conserved across 27 species. Some proteins exhibited slight expansions in bryophytes and tracheophytes, indicating a potential neofunctionalization of conserved vacuole-mediated PCD to develop hyaline cells in Sphagnum (Fig. 6c). The presence of key VNS genes was more specific to land plants, including the accepted ancestors of the land plant Zygnemaophyceae (Spirogloea muscicola gen. nov. and Mesotaenium endlicherianum). This suggests an early-landing-associated origination of VND-based transcriptional regulation. In addition, bryophytes contained even more increased copy numbers of VND compared to tracheophytes, indicating their potentially important hidden roles, possibly related to the functional cell death process (Fig. 6c). Kremer et al.10 (2004) observed similar SCW formation between Sphagnum H cells and xylem tracheary elements, but Sphagnum lacks lignin10. Our analysis of key enzymes suggests that bryophytes and tracheophytes have lignin biosynthesis genes. This suggests that Sphagnum may have a different pathway for building its secondary cell walls, but it’s unclear if this involves the same genes (Fig. 6c).

Discussion

Previous research has shown that hyaline cells in peat moss have an important ecological role in peatlands. They create conditions that inhibit the growth of other species and promote carbon sequestration. This allows peatlands dominated by Sphagnum moss to store about 30% of the soil’s organic carbon33–36. In this study, we found that the developmental program regulates the neofunctionalization of vacuole-executed programmed cell death (PCD), which leads to the formation of hyaline cells resembling xylem cells (Fig. 6c). This is an early attempt to form xylem-like cells in peat moss for carbon sequestration.

We presented the Sphagnum Developmental Cell Atlas and Lineage (SDCAL) by classifying the development of Sphagnum leaf cells into four leaf types, three cell activities, two cell lineages with six stages based on their shapes, sizes, subcellular structure, and organelle dynamics (Figs. 1–3). From the analysis of the SDCAL, we found that the formation of the tri-cellular developmental unit, comprising two C cells and one H cell during the early S3 stage, establishes the foundation for the distinctive Sphagnum leaf cell pattern. Subsequent cell growth and vacuole-mediated subcellular remodeling contribute to Sphagnum dimorphic cellular differentiations. We propose that the developmental program regulates distinct sets of cell lineage-specific subcellular remodeling, which drives Sphagnum dimorphic cell patterning. Future single-cell resolution transcriptomic profiles will help dissect the molecular mechanism of this developmental program in response to external factors.

Vacuoles in plants have key roles in cell division, expansion, and differentiation12. They interact with other components of the endomembrane system to regulate the trafficking and sorting of proteins and lipids12. This regulation is crucial for determining the fate of plant cells12,14. In Sphagnum, vacuoles likely contributed to cell expansion and cell death (Fig. 3). Vacuoles break down cells by engulfing the target into the vacuolar lumen, where degradative enzymes are present23. For plant cells undergoing programmed cell death (PCD), the vacuole-related pathway aids in achieving vacuolar-executed cell suicide23. Extensive studies have shown that plants use conserved molecular pathways for vacuole biogenesis12,14,23. The formation of hyaline cells likely involved a neofunctionalization of the conserved vacuole-related pathway, and the details require further investigation.

The VNS genes are conserved in land plants, including bryophytes and the accepted ancestors of the land plant Zygnemataceae (Fig. 6b). This suggests that VNS-based transcriptional regulation originated early in land plants and may be relevant to changes in the water environment. Overexpression of VNS genes in angiosperms leads to cell death and thickened secondary cell walls (SCWs) with spiral patterns28. In S. palustre, 19 VNS homologs were found, 11 of which were upregulated during hyaline development (Figs. 4, 6b; Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Data 1). Eight of the VNDs were verified via qRT-PCR (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. 12). Representative VNS genes in Sphagnum palustre encode nuclear proteins and induce ectopic secondary wall deposition in leaf cells of Nicotiana benthamiana37. This indicates that the transcriptional activity of VNSs is conserved in peat moss. It is likely that VNS homologs in peat moss initiate hyaline cell differentiation, and the strong correlation between VNS expression and hyaline cell development supports this concept (Fig. 4). Efficient transformation techniques for Sphagnum or overexpression of peat moss VND genes in Arabidopsis or complementation of Arabidopsis mutants will help in understanding the role of VNSs and the gene regulatory network in hyaline cell differentiation.

Besides, plant hormones, such as auxin, are essentially involved in the regulation of VND and xylem formation in vascular plants38,39. We examined the transcriptome data, focusing on hormone-related genes (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Fig. 13). The results revealed that a major of genes implicated in the auxin signaling pathway exhibited upregulated expression patterns in both the bud and YL regions, indicating the pivotal role of auxin signals in facilitating cell division and growth processes (Supplementary Data 1; Fig. 6d). We also analyzed the other hormone-related genes and found that more than half of the genes in cytokinin (CK), gibberellin acid (GA) and brassinosteroid signaling pathway showed upregulated during cell developmental stage (S0-S4 stage), however, also part of them expressed highly in the ML (Supplementary Data 1; Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). These hormones participate in Sphagnum leaf growth, as their role in cell proliferation at the meristem and cell elongation in vascular plants has been well-established38–41. About 1/3 to half of the genes in jasmonic acid and ethylene related pathway displayed higher expression level along leaf cell growth (Supplementary Fig. 13e, g), while most of the genes in abscisic acid- and salicylic acid signaling pathway did not show regular expression pattern during leaf growth (Supplementary Fig. 13d, f).

The lignification of the secondary cell wall is crucial for carbon assimilation. Only the Sphagnum class of bryophytes can store carbon by depositing polysaccharides in the thickened cell wall of its H-cell structure42. Sphagnum contains most of the main components found in vascular plant cell walls, except for lignin (Fig. 6b). Instead, the cell wall surface of Sphagnum is covered with lignin-like phenolics38. These phenolics, including sphagnum acid, have a similar structure and function to lignin42–44. Bryophytes have enzymes like CCR and CAD orthologs (Fig. 6) that can produce hydroxycinnamyl alcohols, but this only results in the production of flavonoids and lignans. It is possible that Sphagnum lacks the specific lignification micro-environments, including proper pH or other factors for polymerization, which need further investigation.

In summary, our work focused on understanding the cellular and molecular mechanisms of hyaline cell formation in Sphagnum moss. We discovered that the development of xylem-like cells in the Sphagnum lineage is an independent effort towards xylem formation. However, Sphagnum moss only provides insights into the origin of xylem, not phloem, raising the possibility that the emergence of xylem and phloem could be independent in vascular plants. The similarities between Sphagnum moss and vascular plants make it a valuable reference for studying vascular origination. The single-cell-layered leaf structure of Sphagnum moss, along with the shared common cell behaviors including cell division, expansion, secondary wall thickening, and programmed cell death, and its two types of distinct cells, make Sphagnum an ideal model for studying integrative evolutionary cell biology in multicellular plants.

Methods

Plant materials

The S. palustre and S. cuspidatum were collected from Guizhou and Jiangxi Province, S. girgensohnii and S. squarrosum were collected from Sichuan Province in China. These plants were generously gifted by the research groups of Professor Rui-Liang Zhu and Professor Sun Yue from the School of Life Sciences at East China Normal University. Aseptic cultivation of S. palustre was carried out according to the following method. Briefly, the upper part of S. palustre with an approximate height of 1.0–1.5 cm was cut and inserted into Hoagland solid culture medium (1.04 g/L powder, 1% (w/v) sucrose, 0.8% (w/v) agar, pH 5.7, sterilized at 121 °C for 20 min) in culture bottles/dishes. The bottles/dishes were then placed in a plant light incubator for cultivation and observation. The incubator was set at a relative humidity of 65 ± 5%, a temperature of 22 °C, LED cool white light as the light source, and a light/dark cycle of 16/8 h.

The ABA treatment was conducted at a final concentration of 10 μM or 50 μM in liquid Hoagland culture medium. The E-64d treatment was conducted at a final concentration of 10 μM in a liquid Hoagland culture medium. Samples were placed in the same incubator as previously described.

Microscope observation, DAPI staining and FM4-64 staining

The sub-capitulum of S. palustre was excised and gradually peeled from the outer layer inward. DAPI was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (10 mM Na2HPO4, 10 mM NaH2PO4, 147 mM NaCl, pH 7.4, PBS) to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. Plant samples were placed onto a microscope slide and stained with DAPI stain solution for 10–15 min. The excess stain was removed with PBS. Mounted the slide onto a fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS) and visualized the stained nuclei under UV excitation. FM4-64 stock (dissolved in DMSO) was diluted into PBS at a final concentration of 2 μM. Gently transferred the plant samples to the staining solution and incubated for precisely 6 h in dark. Samples were washed using PBS to remove free FM4-64 and observed via fluorescence microscopy (OLYMPUS) under 400–500 nm wavelength.

Cryogenic scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM) observation

The fresh samples were fixed onto the sample platform and embedded into liquid nitrogen for freezing. The frozen samples were then transferred to the cryo-SEM chamber and held for a few minutes. To avoid ice sublimation and sample damage, images of the samples were taken at low temperatures (below −130 °C). The obtained images were analyzed and the area of each cell was measured using Image J/Fiji software.

SBF-SEM sample preparation and 3D reconstruction

For SBF-SEM imaging, a solution of 2.5% (w/v) glutaraldehyde and 1% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in PBS was used to fix the entire sub-capitulum overnight at 4 °C. Then, these tissues were stained with a 2% (w/v) osmium tetroxide solution, treated with thiocarbohydrazide (TCH) solution, and restained with 1% aqueous uranyl acetate/Walton’s lead aspartate solution. After staining, samples were washed and dehydrated through an ice-cold acetone series (30%, 50%, 70%, 95%, 95%, 100%, 100%, and 100% acetone). The dehydrated tissues were then embedded in epoxy resin at room temperature. Blocks were modified into thin strips and mounted onto a stereomicroscope holder. A new double-edge razor blade was used to make the thin sections parallel with the block face until the tissue was visible. The exposed surface of the block was then imaged and recorded using SEM (3VIEW-SEM, Zeiss). To obtain a three-dimensional image of the sample, a series of images (40 nm per slice) were obtained by removing each slice and imaging the next surface. The series of images were then aligned, manually segmented and reconstructed to a 3D model using OSR viewer software. We obtained 343 and 396 slices (about 1/2 volume of the whole cell) with a cutting increment of 40 nm for the S3-C-cell and S3-H-cell, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 9). After manual segmentation, surface rendering was performed to display the PM (brown), Nuc (nuclear, yellow), Chl (chloroplast, green), Vac (vacuole, blue), and Mito (mitochondrion, purple).

Plant RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing, analysis and qPCR

The sub-capitulum of Sphagnum was isolated into three portions: the bud, the young leaf, and the mature leaf. About 20 mg plant tissue per sample was collected and preserved in RNA later solution. High-quality RNA was extracted using the TRIzol® protocol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Darmstadt, Germany) and RNeasy® Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The extracted RNA was used for constructing cDNA libraries, which were sent for Illumina sequencing by Biomarker Technologies, Beijing, China. For transcriptome sequencing library preparation, 1 μg of RNA per sample was used as input material. The cDNA library was constructed according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen Biotechnology, China). The process involved enriching mRNA, fragmenting it into approximately 200nt RNA inserts, synthesizing first-strand and second cDNA, performing end-repair/dA-tail, and adapter ligation. Suitable fragments were isolated using Hieff NGS DNA selection Beads (Yeasen Biotechnology, China) and enriched by PCR amplification. Finally, the constructed cDNA libraries of the peat moss were sequenced on a flow cell using an Illumina Novaseq6000. Each sample yielded approximately 6G of clean data from the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform. The obtained reads were assembled using Trinity. Three individual biological repeats were conducted for RNA sequencing. Gene expression profiles were normalized according to Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million fragments mapped (FPKM). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 software based on three independent repeats for each group. Differential gene screening criteria were established using Fold Change ≥2 and FDR <0.01. Annotations were conducted based on NR, Swiss-Prot, COG, KOG and KEGG databases. Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes was conducted using Gene Ontology databases.

For qPCR, cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using reverse transcriptase (Transgene). The housekeeping gene S.p_tubulin was used as the internal control. Three individual biological repeats were conducted. The primers used for qPCR are shown in Supplementary Data 2.

Species tree building and gene analysis

The protein databases of all species were acquired from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and Phytozome, except for the S.pa species. The protein database was obtained from transcriptome data. The species tree was constructed using OrthoFinder (version 2.5.4). Genes involved in the endomembrane system and secondary cell wall development were selected based on published Arabidopsis known genes (identity > 50%; coverage > 60%). Details were listed in Supplementary Data 3.

Statistics and reproducibility

To ensure the validity and reliability of our findings, we implemented rigorous sampling and statistical protocols. For the analysis of individual chloroplast numbers, we selected 30 single cells at random, providing a representative sample of the population. In our cell size analysis, we collected data from over 20 individual cells per sample, enhancing the statistical power and generalizability of our results. We clearly specified all sample sizes in the figure legends, offering transparent and accessible information. Our statistical analyses involved a minimum of three independent replications to reinforce data reproducibility and validity. For specific analyses, such as chloroplast number quantification, we employed at least 30 replicated samples to ensure high precision and reliability. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied in the statistical analysis of Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 12b.

In our analysis using DESeq2, we employed the False Discovery Rate (FDR) control method to address multiple testing concerns. Specifically, we implemented the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure, a widely accepted approach in genomic research, to adjust p-values and control the false discovery rate.
