A platform for analyzing translational control by RBPs at single-mRNA resolution in cells
Authors: Hotaka Kobayashi, Robert H Singer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranslation—the process by which mRNAs are decoded into nascent peptides—is fundamental to life. This process is regulated by various RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), which interact with their target mRNAs. While traditional biochemical approaches have provided valuable insights into translational control, they rely on ensemble measurements of bulk mRNAs in test tubes. Consequently, the behavior of individual mRNAs and their spatiotemporal dynamics in cells during translational control remain largely unexplored. Offering a way to address such limitations, we developed a method for imaging translational control by Argonaute (AGO) proteins, a class of RBPs, at single-mRNA resolution in cells. This method, which employs three-color fluorescence microscopy to detect mRNAs, nascent peptides, and AGO, can also serve as a versatile platform for analyzing translational control by other RBPs. In this protocol, we provide a step-by-step guide for implementing this method to facilitate spatiotemporal studies of translational control at the single-cell and single-mRNA levels.Keywords: mRNA, translation, RNA-binding protein, Argonaute, single-molecule imaging

Significance

This protocol offers a platform for simultaneous detection of mRNAs, translation, and RBPs in cells, enabling in situ imaging of translational control by RBPs at single-mRNA resolution and facilitating spatiotemporal analyses at both the single-cell and single-mRNA levels.

Introduction

Translation is a fundamental biological process in which mRNAs are decoded into nascent peptides by ribosomes. These peptides are further processed into functional proteins that are essential for virtually all cellular activities, making the proper regulation of translation crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and responding to environmental changes. A key mechanism of translational control involves RNA-binding proteins (RBPs). By interacting with specific mRNAs, RBPs can promote or repress translation, thereby shaping protein-expression patterns in a highly dynamic and context-dependent manner. Although translational control has been extensively studied, most knowledge has come from biochemical approaches, which analyze mRNAs and proteins in test tubes. These ensemble methods make it difficult to determine the timing and location of translational control in cells—for example, when RBPs bind to mRNAs and where in the cell they exert their regulatory effects. Moreover, since biochemical approaches examine populations of cells and mRNAs collectively, they often overlook the heterogeneity of translational control at the single-cell and single-mRNA levels.

Offering a way to address such limitations, we developed a method for imaging translational control by Argonaute (AGO) proteins, a class of RBPs, at single-mRNA resolution in cells [1]. While originally developed for AGO, this method can also serve as a versatile platform for imaging translational control by other RBPs. To image translational control by RBPs at single-mRNA resolution, it is necessary to detect three molecular components: mRNAs, nascent peptides, and RBPs. To this end, we designed an artificial reporter mRNA with three key features (Figure 1). First, it contains a sequence that can be detected using single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH). This technique uses 30–40 fluorescently labeled probes that hybridize to the same mRNA species, enabling the mRNA to be imaged with single-molecule sensitivity [2,3]. Second, the open reading frame (ORF) encodes a peptide called SunTag, consisting of 24 repeats of an epitope derived from the yeast GCN4 protein [4]. Nascent peptides, emerging from translating ribosomes, can be detected with single-molecule sensitivity by immunofluorescence (IF) using anti-GCN4 antibodies [5–8]. Third, the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) contains multiple binding sites for the RBP of interest—in our case, AGO. RBPs bound to the reporter mRNA can be detected by IF using specific antibodies, enabling imaging of when and where they interact with each mRNA in cells. These features make it possible to simultaneously detect mRNAs, translation, and RBPs, enabling imaging of translational control by RBPs at single-mRNA resolution in cells. In this protocol, we provide a step-by-step guide for implementing this method using AGO as a model RBP to facilitate spatiotemporal studies of translational control at the single-cell and single-mRNA levels.

Materials and methods

Plasmids

First, design and construct a plasmid encoding a reporter mRNA for the RBP of interest. In this protocol, we describe the reporter mRNA used to image AGO, which repress translation [9]. While various plasmid backbones can be used, we employed a construct with a Ponasterone A (PonA)-inducible promoter, which enables tight control of reporter mRNA transcription [10–12]. To image translation, the SunTag sequence was inserted into the ORF. To image AGO, which binds to target mRNAs through microRNAs (miRNAs) [13], eight tandem miRNA sites were inserted into the 3′ UTR. Specifically, we used sequences complementary to miR-21, the most abundant miRNA in human U2OS cells [14], a cell line routinely used in our laboratories and frequently employed in RNA imaging studies. As a negative control, we also prepared a plasmid encoding a reporter mRNA with mutated miR-21-binding sites [1].

Cells

Choose cells according to your experimental objectives. We used a U2OS cell line stably expressing VgEcR and RXR, which activate transcription from the PonA-inducible promoter in response to PonA [12]. Cells were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 in DMEM (Corning, 10-013-CV) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (R&D Systems, S11150H) and 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Thermo Fisher, 15240-062).

Deliver the plasmids into cells

To initiate the experiment, deliver the plasmids into cells using any method of choice. We used nucleofection rather than lipofection, as the latter occasionally results in fluorescent aggregates that interfere with single-molecule imaging and subsequent image analysis. Nucleofection was performed using the Nucleofector II device (Lonza). U2OS cells were briefly rinsed with DPBS (Corning, 21-031-CV), then treated with Trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher, 25300-054) to detach them from the culture dish. After neutralizing trypsin with culture medium, the cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 300 g for 1 min. The resulting cell pellets (~1×106 cells) were resuspended in 100 μL of Ingenio Electroporation Solution (Mirus, MIR50115) containing 2 μg of plasmid DNA. Nucleofection was then carried out in an electroporation cuvette (Mirus, MIR50115) using the Nucleofector II. Following nucleofection, cells were plated on coverslips (Thermo Fisher, 12-545-81) coated with collagen (Cell Applications, 125-50) in culture medium.

Induce the expression of reporter mRNAs

If your plasmids use a constitutive promoter, skip this step. For plasmids containing an inducible promoter, induce transcription of reporter mRNAs. In our case, one day after nucleofection, U2OS cells were treated with 20 μM PonA (Santa Cruz, sc-202768A) for 30 min. After washing the cells with culture medium, they were incubated for 1 hr. The incubation time can be adjusted depending on the experimental objective.

Label RBPs by IF

Fix the cells with 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 15714) in 1× PBS (MilliporeSigma, 11666789001) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by washing with 1× PBS. We recommend labeling RBPs first, prior to the subsequent immunofluorescence-fluorescence in situ hybridization (IF-FISH). Although it is possible to label RBPs concurrently with mRNAs and nascent peptides by adding primary antibodies to the hybridization buffer during IF-FISH, this occasionally results in reduced IF efficiency (see Results and discussion). To label AGO, fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (MilliporeSigma, T9284) in 1× PBS for 10 min and washed with 1× PBS. Cells were then incubated for 30 min in blocking buffer consisting of 1× PBS, 0.02% Triton X-100, 0.5% UltraPure BSA (Thermo Fisher, AM2618), and 40 U/ml SUPERase In RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher, AM2696). Subsequently, cells were incubated for 1 hr in blocking buffer supplemented with 24 μg/ml mouse anti-AGO2 antibody (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, 015-22031, clone 4G8). Although humans express four AGO proteins (AGO1–4) [15], AGO2 is predominantly expressed in U2OS cells [16], and was therefore the focus of this protocol. After washing with blocking buffer, cells were incubated for 30 min in blocking buffer supplemented with 2 μg/ml Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-11029), followed by washes with 1× PBS.

Label mRNAs and nascent peptides by IF-FISH

Next, label mRNAs and nascent peptides by IF-FISH. Cells were first incubated for 30 min in pre-hybridization buffer containing 10% deionized formamide (Thermo Fisher, AC205821000), 2× SSC (MilliporeSigma, 11666681001), 0.5% UltraPure BSA, and 40 U/ml SUPERase In RNase Inhibitor. Cells were then incubated for 3 hr at 37°C in hybridization buffer containing 10% deionized formamide, 2× SSC, 10% dextran sulfate (MilliporeSigma, D8906), 1 mg/ml competitor tRNA (MilliporeSigma, 10109541001), 0.05% UltraPure BSA, 40 U/ml SUPERase In RNase Inhibitor, 50 nM Quasar 570-conjugated smFISH probes targeting the reporter mRNAs, and 10 μg/ml rabbit anti-GCN4 antibody (Absolute Antibody, AB00436-23.0). After washing with 10% deionized formamide in 2× SSC, cells were further incubated for 30 min at 37°C in the same buffer supplemented with 2 μg/ml Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher, A-21245). Cells were washed again with 2× SSC, and coverslips were mounted onto glass slides (Thermo Fisher, 3051-002) using ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Thermo Fisher, P36962), and incubated overnight protected from light. smFISH probes targeting reporter mRNAs were designed using Stellaris Probe Designer version 4.2 (Biosearch Technologies) and synthesized by Biosearch Technologies [1]. Acquire images

After the mounting medium has solidified, acquire images using a fluorescence microscope (Figure 2). We used a BX63 automated wide-field fluorescence microscope (Olympus) equipped with a SOLA FISH light engines (Lumencor), an ORCA-R2 cooled digital CCD camera (Hamamatsu Photonics), a 60×1.35 NA super apochromat oil immersion objective (Olympus, UPLSAPO60XO), and zero pixel shift filter sets: DAPI-5060C-Zero, FITC-5050A-Zero, Cy3-4040C-Zero, and Cy5-4040C-Zero (Semrock). Multicolor 3D imaging was performed using MetaMorph software (Molecular Devices), in Multi Dimensional Acquisition mode. Exposure times were 200 ms for CY5 (gain: 2), 200 ms for CY3 (gain: 2), 200 ms for FITC (gain: 2), and 10 ms for DAPI (gain: 0). Z-stacks spanning the entire cell volume were acquired at 200 nm intervals along the z-axis. The resulting image resolution was 107.5 nm in the XY plane and 200 nm along Z. To prepare figure panels, raw images were processed using ImageJ software (Version: 2.16.0/1.54p).

Images of a U2OS cell expressing the reporter mRNA with miRNA sites. mRNAs (A), nascent peptides (B), and AGO (C) were labeled and imaged. The mRNA molecules indicated as “1” and “2” represent an AGO-free translated mRNA and an AGO-bound non-translated mRNA, respectively. The nucleus was stained with DAPI. Scale bar, 5 μm.

Detect outlines and fluorescent spots

To analyze the images, first, detect cellular outlines (Figure 3A). To distinguish nuclear and cytoplasmic regions, their outlines were detected using the CellProfiler algorithm [17]. Nuclear boundaries were identified based on DAPI signals, and cell boundaries were segmented using the nonspecific background fluorescence of the smFISH probes. Next, fluorescent spots were detected using FISH-quant (Figure 3B), a MATLAB-based algorithm [18]. FISH-quant identifies fluorescent spots within the defined outlines and localizes them in 3D at sub-pixel resolution by fitting a 3D Gaussian function. This analysis yields the number, intensity, and 3D position of individual spots. Based on nuclear and cellular outlines, all detected spots were classified as either “nuclear” or “cytoplasmic”.

Workflow of image analysis. First, outlines of cells (orange solid lines) and nuclei (orange dashed line) were detected using CellProfiler (A). Fluorescent spots (magenta circles) were then detected using FISH-quant, which localizes them in 3D by fitting 3D Gaussians (B). Based on the 3D distances between individual spots, colocalization analysis was performed (C).

Perform colocalization analysis

As a final step, perform colocalization analysis using 3D spot positions (Figure 3C). We used FISH-quant again for this analysis [18]. First, the average shift between different fluorescence channels was calculated and corrected. Then, pairwise 3D distances between spots of different channels were computed. If two spots were located within a predefined threshold distance (500 nm for mRNA–SunTag; 250 nm for mRNA–AGO), they were considered colocalized. Because AGO yields a much higher number of fluorescent spots than SunTag (Figure 2), random spatial coincidence can occur more frequently between mRNAs and AGO spots. To reduce false-positive colocalization, a shorter distance threshold (250 nm) was used for mRNA–AGO pairing. Based on colocalization with SunTag and AGO, mRNAs were classified as “translated”, “non-translated”, “AGO-positive”, or “AGO-negative”. Likewise, SunTag and AGO spots were classified as “on mRNAs” or “free”, depending on their colocalization status with mRNAs.

Results and discussion

As described in the Materials and methods section, mRNAs, nascent peptides, and AGO were labeled and imaged (Figure 2). Fluorescent spots were then detected and analyzed for colocalization (Figure 3). In line with our previous findings [1], classification of all mRNA molecules into AGO-positive or AGO-negative confirmed that the reporter mRNA containing miR-21 sites exhibited a higher fraction of AGO-positive mRNAs than the negative control mRNA (Figure 4A). Single-cell analysis, in which the fraction of AGO-positive mRNAs was calculated for each cell using Equation 1, showed the same trend (Figure 4B). In addition, quantification of AGO signal intensity on AGO-positive mRNAs revealed higher AGO intensity for the miR-21 reporter than for the negative control (Figure 4C). We performed essentially the same analysis for translation. Classification of mRNAs into translated or non-translated revealed that the miR-21 reporter exhibited a lower fraction of translated mRNAs than the control (Figure 4D). Single-cell analysis based on Equation 2 yielded consistent results (Figure 4E). Furthermore, SunTag intensity on translated mRNAs was lower in the miR-21 reporter than in the control (Figure 4F). Together, these data indicate that our method enables analysis of translational repression by AGO at single-mRNA and single-cell resolution.

To successfully image translational control by RBPs, the balance between the expression levels of reporter mRNAs and RBPs should be considered. If reporter mRNAs are expressed at levels 100-fold higher than those of the RBP of interest, most mRNAs would remain RBP-free, making it difficult to detect proper translational control. To address this issue, we employed an inducible promoter system, which allows flexible control of reporter mRNA expression. For the experiment shown in Figure 4, given that U2OS cells contain ~15,000 AGO molecules and that miR-21 accounts for ~25% of total miRNAs [1,14,16], we limited reporter mRNA expression to ~100 molecules per cell, ensuring that most mRNAs could be efficiently recognized by AGO. A previous study has shown that in mammalian cells, mRNA copy numbers per gene typically range between 10 and 100 molecules per cell [19], placing our reporter expression level within the physiological range.

Another key factor for successfully imaging translational control by RBPs is the order in which mRNAs, nascent peptides, and RBPs are labeled. We found that labeling order matters, at least in the case of AGO. In this protocol, we labeled AGO first by IF, followed by labeling mRNAs and nascent peptides using IF-FISH. Although it is technically possible to label all components simultaneously by including anti-AGO antibodies in the hybridization buffer, this approach substantially reduced AGO labeling efficiency (Figure 5). We speculate that this is due to the presence of formamide in the hybridization buffer, which may weaken antibody binding to target epitopes [20]. Thus, we recommend labeling RBPs first by IF, followed by mRNA and nascent peptide labeling with IF-FISH.

Conclusion

Translational control by RBPs is a crucial layer of gene expression. While biochemical ensemble methods have provided valuable mechanistic insights, they inherently average over populations of mRNAs and cells, thus failing to capture the behavior and spatiotemporal dynamics of individual mRNAs in cells. As such, our method—serving as a platform to image translational control by RBPs at single-mRNA and single-cell resolution—would offer a valuable framework for future studies of translational control. With this platform, for example, it is possible to analyze when and where the RBP of interest binds to target mRNAs, as well as when and where it modulates translation, including the heterogeneity of translational control across individual mRNAs and cells.

We would like to note that the Tim Stasevich laboratory has developed a conceptually similar method for imaging RBP-mediated translational control in cells, likewise using AGO as a model RBP [21]. In their system, AGO is fused to a fluorescent protein and forcibly tethered to reporter mRNAs, enabling imaging of translational repression by AGO at single-mRNA resolution. While this artificial tethering may not fully recapitulate the behavior of endogenous AGO, their approach, in which AGO is labeled by a fluorescent protein, enables real-time imaging of translational control in living cells, which is a clear advantage over our fixed-cell method. By contrast, although our method requires fixation and thus cannot be applied to live-cell imaging, it does not require genetic modification of RBPs. This enables the observation of their natural binding timing, localization, and affinities to target mRNAs. Rather than one method being superior to the other, we consider these approaches to be complementary. Applied individually or in combination, these two approaches can together advance our understanding of translational control by RBPs.
