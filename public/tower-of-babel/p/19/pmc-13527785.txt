Post-Coital Intracranial Haemorrhage: A Nigerian Regional Experience
Authors: Linda U Iroegbu-Emeruem, Chibuike U Akaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Post-coital intracranial haemorrhage (PC-ICH) is a non-traumatic intracranial bleeding occurring during or within 30 minutes of sexual intercourse. In sub-Saharan Africa, poorly controlled hypertension, delayed presentation, and limited access to neurosurgical care compound its lethality. No prospective series from the region exists.Methodology:Prospective observational cohort study at a tertiary referral centre in a metropolitan city in the south-south region of Nigeria, from January 2021 to December 2025. Consecutive adults presenting with CT-confirmed, non-traumatic ICH where coitus was the immediately preceding activity were enrolled. The primary outcome was the six-month Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) score; secondary outcomes were in-hospital mortality and in-hospital complications.Results:Fifty-two patients were enrolled over five years (mean age 49.6 ± 9.8 years; 57.7% male). Hypertensive ICH was the most common aetiology (53.8%; n=28), followed by aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage (aSAH; 36.5%; n=19) and other/undetermined causes (9.6%; n=5). Median time from ictus to hospital arrival was 41.2 hours; only 11.5% presented within six hours. Overall six-month mortality was 19.2% (n=10); a favourable functional outcome (GOS 4–5, i.e. moderate disability or good recovery) was achieved in 59.6% (n=31). Low GCS on admission (OR 9.4; 95% CI 2.6–34.0; p < 0.001), haematoma expansion or rebleeding and presentation delay >24 hours (OR 4.3; p = 0.012) were the strongest independent predictors of poor outcome.Conclusion:PC-ICH in the region is predominantly a complication of uncontrolled hypertension in middle-aged men. Delayed presentation, driven by sociocultural stigma, misdiagnosis, and financial barriers, is the most actionable determinant of poor outcome. Intensification of hypertension control, structured emergency triage for post-coital thunderclap headache, and removal of financial barriers to neurosurgical care are urgently required.

Introduction

Sexual intercourse generates one of the most demanding acute haemodynamic challenges the cerebrovascular system encounters. During the orgasmic phase, systolic blood pressure may surge to 130 - 175 mmHg and heart rate reaches 150 - 180 beats per minute, accompanied by a Valsalva-like manoeuvre that transiently raises intracranial pressure[1]. In a brain harbouring hypertensive small-vessel disease, an arteriovenous malformation, or a saccular intracranial aneurysm, this sudden haemodynamic insult may be sufficient to precipitate catastrophic intracranial haemorrhage.

Post-coital intracranial haemorrhage (PC-ICH) encompasses any non-traumatic intracranial bleed, intraparenchymal, subarachnoid, or combined, that occurs during or within 30 minutes of sexual activity. Published series from high-income countries predominantly feature aneurysmal SAH as the leading aetiology[1,2]. However, in sub-Saharan Africa, where hypertension is the most prevalent modifiable vascular risk factor and is undertreated in the vast majority of those affected[3], hypertensive intraparenchymal haemorrhage (ICH) likely constitutes a proportionally larger share of PC-ICH than has been recognised.

The cardinal presenting feature of PC-ICH, sudden, maximal-intensity “thunderclap” headache at orgasm, demands immediate neuroimaging and neurosurgical evaluation[4]. Failure to present promptly or misdiagnosis at the primary care level leads to haematoma expansion, aneurysm rebleeding, and preventable death. In Nigeria, case fatality rates after spontaneous ICH and aneurysmal SAH range from 17 - 45%[5,6], and the absence of endovascular neurovascular capabilities at most centres means that surgical clipping remains the sole treatment modality for ruptured aneurysms.

Despite the recognised burden of hypertensive stroke in the South-South region[7,8], no prospective series specifically characterising the aetiology, presentation, and outcomes of PC-ICH has been reported from this centre or region. This study addresses that gap.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a prospective, single-centre observational cohort study conducted in a metropolitan South-South city in Nigeria, from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2025 (60 months). The centre is the principal referral hospital for neurosurgical and neurovascular emergencies in its catchment area, serving about ten states in the region, and receives both self-presenting patients and inter-facility transfers from primary and secondary health facilities across the subregion.

Ethical Approval and Consent

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Rivers State University Teaching Hospital (Approval No.RSUTH/REC/2021/913), dated 13th January 2021, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; where a patient lacked decision-making capacity owing to a reduced level of consciousness, consent was obtained from the next of kin or a legally authorised representative, with assent sought from the patient once capacity was regained.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion: Adults (≥ 18 years) with brain CT-confirmed, non-traumatic ICH where coitus was the immediately preceding activity within 30 minutes of symptom onset. The post-coital temporal relationship was ascertained using a standardised, structured interview administered privately by a trained clinician and was required to be corroborated by at least one of the following: direct patient report, a credible witness (most commonly the sexual partner), or contemporaneous documentation in the referral record. Cases in which the post-coital context could not be confirmed by the patient or a witness were not enrolled.

Exclusion:

Traumatic cause; haemorrhagic transformation of ischaemic stroke; haematological malignancy or coagulopathy; anticoagulant/antiplatelet use without confirmed vascular lesion; refusal or inability to consent.

Case Ascertainment

During the study period, over 700 adults were admitted with CT-confirmed spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracranial haemorrhage, by both the Neurosurgery and Neurology units of the hospital. Of these, 52 met the criteria for PC-ICH and were enrolled. Patients were identified prospectively at the point of admission through the neurosurgical and emergency services, and the post-coital context was sought systematically in every adult presenting with non-traumatic ICH or thunderclap headache. Because the sexual context of onset was actively and routinely elicited in all such patients using a private, non-judgemental structured interview rather than relying on spontaneous patient disclosure, the proportion identified is higher than in series where the context is captured only incidentally. The relatively high case number for a single centre reflects both this systematic ascertainment and the hospital's role as a major referral centre for neurosurgical emergencies across a large catchment population.

Data Collection and Outcomes

Standardised data collection forms captured demographics, cardiovascular risk factor profile, clinical presentation (headache characterisation, GCS, Hunt-Hess grade, blood pressure), radiological findings (CT pattern, haematoma volume, Fisher grade, CTA findings, mass effect), and management details. Haematoma volume was calculated on the admission non-contrast CT using the validated ABC/2 ellipsoid method, where A is the greatest haemorrhage diameter on the slice with the largest area of haemorrhage, B is the diameter perpendicular to A on the same slice, and C is the number of slices with haemorrhage multiplied by the slice thickness.

Imaging and Vascular Work-Up

All patients underwent non-contrast brain CT on arrival. CT angiography (CTA) was the primary modality used to identify or exclude an underlying vascular lesion and was performed in 43 patients (82.7%). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA), the reference standard for cerebral vascular imaging, was not available at the study centre during the study period, and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) was not routinely used in the acute setting. In the 9 patients (17.3%) in whom CTA was not obtained, this was due to early death before imaging could be completed, haemodynamic instability, renal contraindication to contrast, or financial inability to fund the study. A diagnosis of hypertensive ICH was assigned only when a patient had a haemorrhage in a location typical of hypertensive vasculopathy together with documented hypertension and either a CTA negative for a causative vascular lesion or, where CTA was not feasible, a clinico-radiological picture wholly consistent with hypertensive haemorrhage. The constraints imposed by the absence of DSA and by incomplete CTA coverage, and the resulting potential for misclassification, are acknowledged explicitly in the Limitations section.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was functional status at six months, measured by the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) and assessed at in-person review or, where attendance was not possible, by structured telephone follow-up. Secondary outcomes were in-hospital (all-cause) mortality and the occurrence of in-hospital complications (including haematoma expansion, aneurysm rebleeding, hydrocephalus, vasospasm or delayed cerebral ischaemia, hyponatraemia, pneumonia, and seizures). For regression analysis, functional outcome was dichotomised a priori into good (GOS 4-5) and poor (GOS 1-3). Follow-up for six months was completed in 43 of the 52 patients and patients with missing six-month status were excluded only from the corresponding outcome analyses.

Definitions

PC-ICH: Any non-traumatic intracranial haemorrhage temporally linked to sexual intercourse (within 30 minutes). Hypertensive ICH: Spontaneous intraparenchymal haemorrhage in a patient with documented hypertension, in a location consistent with hypertensive vasculopathy (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum, or lobar) and without an identified causative vascular lesion on the available vascular work-up (CTA where feasible; see Imaging and Vascular Work-Up). Aneurysmal SAH (aSAH): subarachnoid haemorrhage with a saccular aneurysm confirmed on CTA. Uncontrolled (or poorly controlled) hypertension: a documented history of hypertension with either no current antihypertensive treatment, or a most recent recorded blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg despite treatment, or no record of blood pressure control in the preceding 12 months. Delayed presentation: an interval from symptom onset (ictus) to first contact with the study facility exceeding 24 hours; the 24-hour threshold was selected a priori because it approximates the upper bound of the window within which acute interventions to limit haematoma expansion and to secure a ruptured aneurysm and prevent early rebleeding are most effective, and it is a widely used cut-off in stroke and aneurysmal SAH outcome literature[1,2,7]. Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS): GOS 1 = death; GOS 2 = persistent vegetative state; GOS 3 = severe disability (conscious but dependent); GOS 4 = moderate disability (independent but disabled); GOS 5 = good recovery (resumption of normal life, with or without minor deficits).

Statistical Analysis

As post-coital ICH is a rare condition with no prior prospective regional incidence data on which to base a formal power calculation, no a priori sample size estimation was performed; the study was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating consecutive case series, and all eligible patients presenting during the fixed 60-month enrolment window were included. Data were analysed with IBM SPSS v26. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages; continuous variables as mean ± SD or median (IQR) based on normality testing. Group comparisons used the Chi-square or Fisher's exact test (categorical) and the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test (continuous). Predictors of poor outcome (GOS 1-3) were first examined on univariate analysis. Because the cohort contained only 21 poor-outcome events, a full multivariable model entering all univariately significant variables would have violated the events-per-variable (EPV) rule and risked overfitting. We therefore deliberately constrained the multivariable binary logistic regression to a small number of pre-specified, clinically prioritised variables, keeping the EPV ratio close to the conventional minimum of approximately 5-10 events per variable, and we report the number of events alongside the model. Model fit was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Given the limited number of events, the adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, and the Hunt-Hess subgroup estimate (aSAH only) in particular is based on a small number of events and is presented with appropriate caution. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Enrolment and Aetiology

Fifty-two patients meeting inclusion criteria were enrolled over the five-year study period (Figure 1). Annual enrolment ranged from 8 (2021) to 13 (2023), with no statistically significant year-on-year trend. Hypertensive ICH was the most common aetiology (n = 28; 53.8%), followed by aneurysmal SAH (n = 19; 36.5%). Five patients (9.6%) had other or undetermined causes after the available vascular work-up. A further 11 potential cases were identified retrospectively but excluded, principally for failure to document the post-coital context contemporaneously (n = 7) or patient refusal (n = 4).

Clinical Presentation

All 52 patients (100%) reported sudden-onset, severe thunderclap headache, the cardinal feature of PC-ICH, with 90.4% describing it as the worst headache of their life. The full symptom spectrum at admission is depicted in Figure 3. Focal neurological deficit was present in 25 patients (48.1%), reflecting the high proportion of parenchymal haematomas with mass effect. Meningism was present in 28 (53.8%); it was near-universal in the aSAH subgroup but less consistent in hypertensive ICH. Median time from ictus to hospital arrival was 41.2 hours (IQR 10.4–86.8 h); only 6 patients (11.5%) presented within six hours (Figure 4). Delays were significantly longer for the aSAH subgroup (median 46.8 h) than hypertensive ICH (38.6 h; p = 0.041), likely due to the greater initial clinical impairment in the latter facilitating earlier family-initiated transfer.

Management

All patients received intravenous antihypertensives (labetalol) to achieve a target SBP of 130 - 160 mmHg in hypertensive ICH, in keeping with current evidence-based targets[10,11]. All aSAH patients received nimodipine. Surgical haematoma evacuation was performed in 9 hypertensive ICH patients (32.1%) with mass effect and clinical deterioration. Among the aSAH subgroup, 15 of 19 patients (78.9%) underwent craniotomy and aneurysm clipping; median time from admission to surgery was 5 days (IQR 3-9). Endovascular coiling was not available in the study area during the study period. Of the 4 aSAH patients

who did not undergo clipping, 2 declined due to financial constraints, 1 had rebleed and met criteria for brain death before surgery, and 1 was deemed too high-risk on neurological grounds (HH grade V). Hydrocephalus requiring external ventricular drain (EVD) insertion occurred in 8 patients (15.4%).

Complications

In-hospital complications are summarised in Table 4. Haematoma expansion in the hypertensive ICH subgroup (32.1%) and rebleeding prior to surgery in the aSAH subgroup (31.6%) were the most clinically consequential complications, carrying in-group mortalities of 55.6% and 66.7% respectively. Both were strongly associated with the duration of delayed presentation and with inadequate blood pressure control, although the observational design precludes a definitive causal inference. Hyponatraemia occurred in 26.9% of patients overall, most commonly as cerebral salt-wasting in the SAH subgroup.

Discussion

This prospective series represents, to our knowledge and based on a structured search of the published literature, one of the first dedicated prospective efforts to characterise post-coital ICH from South-South Nigeria. Its most important departure from Western literature is the aetiology: hypertensive ICH, not aneurysmal SAH, was the dominant cause, accounting for 53.8% of cases. This distinction has profound implications for both clinical management and public health strategy.

Hypertension as the Dominant Substrate

The prevalence and severity of hypertension in our cohort are striking. Eighty-eight percent of patients were hypertensive, and 82.6% of those had uncontrolled or untreated disease. This reflects the documented crisis of hypertension management in sub-Saharan Africa, where treatment and control rates remain critically low despite rising prevalence[3,12,13,14]. At the haemodynamic extreme of orgasm, uncontrolled hypertension creates a permissive environment for vessel rupture, either through established hypertensive small-vessel disease with lacunar necrosis and Charcot-Bouchard microaneurysm formation, or through the acute blood pressure surge on an already hypertensive vascular bed[15]. The male predominance (57.7%) in our cohort, stronger in the hypertensive ICH subgroup (64.3%), is consistent with the well-established sex differential in hypertensive stroke risk in Nigerian men, who carry higher rates of hypertension and lower rates of treatment compared to women in the same age bracket[16].

Aneurysmal SAH - Second in Frequency, Not Severity

Aneurysmal SAH accounted for 36.5% of our cohort, a numerically smaller but clinically severe subset. Rebleeding occurred in 31.6% of aSAH patients before definitive surgery, with a 66.7% in-group case fatality, a rate substantially higher than the 15 - 20% rebleeding mortality reported in high-income settings, a difference that is most plausibly explained, at least in part, by the extended median 46.8-hour pre-operative delay in our cohort.[5] The MCA bifurcation (36.8%) was the most common aneurysm site, followed by the ACoA (26.3%), consistent with the anterior circulation preponderance reported in other parts of the country[6,17]. The absence of endovascular coiling capability in the study environment remains a significant treatment gap; all aneurysm management was by open surgical clipping.

The Presentation Delay Crisis

A median presentation delay of 41.2 hours is the defining modifiable hazard in this cohort. Three interacting drivers were identified. First, over half of patients withheld the sexual context of symptom onset from clinicians, leading to misdiagnosis as tension headache or hypertensive urgency. Second, faith healing remains a first resort for acute neurological events in many communities, with 21.2% consulting a traditional practitioner before hospital attendance. Third, financial barriers, the inability to pay for emergency imaging or neurosurgical fees in a predominantly out-of-pocket healthcare system, delayed admission after diagnosis in 32.7% of cases. Each of these barriers is addressable at a policy and community level.

Comparison with Existing Literature

Our overall six-month mortality of 19.2% is broadly consistent with the 17.2% operative mortality reported from Lagos, Nigeria, and the 20.8% reported in a South-South series[5,6], though our cohort captures all PC-ICH aetiologies rather than aneurysmal cases alone. Good functional recovery in 59.6% of cases is comparable to other resource-limited neurosurgical series, with the hypertensive ICH subgroup achieving slightly better six-month GOS than the aSAH subgroup (64.3% vs 57.9%), a reversal of the pattern typically seen in Western high-income series, where haematoma evacuation and excellent ICU support drive superior outcomes[10].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The single-region design limits external generalisability and may introduce selection bias; patients who died before reaching the tertiary facility, or who did not disclose the post-coital context, are not captured, and the true incidence is likely underestimated. As a single-centre study conducted at the sole tertiary neurosurgical referral hospital for the subregion, the cohort is subject to referral bias: the case mix is weighted towards patients who survived long enough to be transferred and who could access tertiary care, and local referral pathways and practice patterns may differ from those elsewhere, so the aetiological proportions and outcomes reported here may not be generalisable to other regions or to community-level populations. Vascular imaging was limited to CTA, which was completed in 82.7% of patients and was not always achievable within 48 hours of admission; digital subtraction angiography was unavailable. This raises the possibility that a small number of underlying vascular lesions (for example, small or thrombosed aneurysms or arteriovenous malformations) were missed, leading to potential misclassification of some cases as hypertensive ICH. The multivariable model was constrained by a limited number of outcome events (21 poor-outcome events), so the regression estimates are exploratory and at risk of residual confounding and instability despite our efforts to limit the number of predictors. Six-month follow-up was obtained by telephone rather than in person in 14 patients (26.9%), which may introduce ascertainment bias in GOS scoring, and 9 patients were lost to follow-up. Finally, patient-reported outcomes in the psychological and sexual domains were not systematically captured. These limitations should be borne in mind when interpreting the findings, and they reinforce the need for the multicentre registry proposed below.

Conclusion

Post-coital ICH in South-South Nigeria is predominantly a complication of severely uncontrolled hypertension in middle-aged men, with aneurysmal SAH as the second most frequent aetiology. Both subtypes present with thunderclap headache as the universal warning presentation, a sign that must be treated as a neurosurgical emergency at every level of the health system.

Delayed presentation driven by sociocultural shame, misdiagnosis, and financial barriers remains the modifiable factor most strongly associated with death in this cohort and was closely linked to the lethal complications of haematoma expansion and aneurysm rebleeding, although a causal relationship cannot be established from observational data.

On the basis of these observations, and recognising that several of the measures below extend beyond what this single observational cohort can itself prove, we offer the following as policy considerations and priorities for future evaluation rather than as firmly evidence-based directives:

1.Hypertension control: Intensify population-level hypertension screening, treatment, and follow-up across the Niger Delta, targeting working-age men as a high-risk demographic.
