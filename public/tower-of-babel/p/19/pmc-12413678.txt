Perspectives on Accessing Mental Health Supports for Youth Living With Post‐Traumatic Stress Symptoms After a Solid Organ Transplant
Authors: Mallorie T. Tam, Anna Riminchan, Julia M. Wu, Candice Wiedman, Monica Ho, Linda Li, Tom Blydt‐Hansen, Julie M. Robillard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMany children and adolescents who undergo solid organ transplants (SOT) develop post‐traumatic stress (PTS) symptoms. Despite its prevalence and strong association with long‐term impairments in quality of life, PTS is often overlooked as a major co‐morbidity in many transplant programs. To address this unmet need, the purpose of this study was to explore the factors that impede or facilitate awareness of PTS, access to resources, and readiness to engage with mental health services.MethodsSeparate semi‐structured interviews (N = 17) were conducted with pediatric SOT recipients between the ages of 12 and 18, and a parent/caregiver. Descriptive content analysis was used to uncover emerging themes from the interviews that explored: (1) awareness and management of PTS symptoms; (2) timelines surrounding PTS symptom awareness and resource‐seeking; (3) facilitators to PTS symptom awareness; (4) barriers to PTS symptom awareness; (5) information seeking; and (6) areas for improvement in the current content and availability of resources.ResultsEmotional and physical impacts of SOT were identified for pediatric SOT recipients and their parents/caregivers. The majority of parent/caregiver and youth participants preferred to learn about the risk of PTS symptoms and available support resources before the transplant. Participants recommended several improvements and additional resources to support PTS, which centered around increasing access to mental health care, counseling and therapy, patient‐centered support, and advocacy.ConclusionsBy exploring the personal experiences and perspectives of pediatric SOT recipients and their parents/caregivers, this work can be used to improve the accessibility and quality of mental health supports for PTS.

Introduction

Solid organ transplantation (SOT) is a life‐saving procedure for children and adolescents with end‐stage organ failure. After the restoration of organ function by SOT, children and adolescents enter a phase of rehabilitation, which includes adapting to the transition from hospital to home, returning to physical activity and school, and adapting to changes in restrictions that result from the successful transplant [1]. Although many pediatric SOT recipients experience full recovery of organ function after transplantation, they have been exposed to physical and psychological trauma throughout their transplant journey, including life‐threatening illnesses, invasive medical treatments, surgical procedures, medical interventions, pain, and intensive care unit stays [2, 3]. Previous studies have identified depression and anxiety as common mental health issues in organ transplant recipients. In turn, these conditions may increase the risk of medication non‐adherence, which can contribute to organ loss and mortality among patients who survive kidney, heart, and liver transplants [2, 4]. Other symptoms of post‐traumatic stress (PTS) in this population can also include avoidance, hyperarousal, and re‐experiencing of traumatic events [5], which substantially impair quality of life [3].

Previous studies have investigated factors that are associated with greater PTS symptoms in pediatric transplant recipients and identified medical complications in the previous year leading up to transplant such as hospitalization, re‐transplantation, greater medication, and hospital admission days as key predictors [3, 6]. A more recent retrospective analysis found that pediatric SOT recipients continued to experience PTS symptoms post‐transplant, with higher scores on the Child Trauma Screening Questionnaire (CTSQ) associated with hospitalization in the last 12 months, medication complexity, involvement with foster care, and lower family functioning [7]. A history of pre‐transplant psychiatric illness and prior exposure to traumatic events are also associated with an increased risk of PTS symptoms [2]. PTS symptoms are amenable to therapy and while psychological therapies and resources are available to treat PTS symptoms in children and adolescents, families often do not engage in these services due to lack of awareness, low readiness to engage in treatment, and challenges in accessing mental health resources in their communities. These barriers are further compounded by the mental, physical, and practice demands of ongoing medical care throughout the transplantation journey [8].

In addition to lack of family engagement, there is less emphasis on discussion about PTS symptoms at the time of pediatric transplant referral, given the priority of medical and surgical considerations. Previous studies have reported that between 9% and 34% of pediatric SOT recipients exhibit some degree of PTS symptoms [6, 9, 10, 11]. Despite its prevalence and strong correlation with long‐term impairments in quality of life in pediatric SOT recipients, PTS symptoms and PTS disorder are not widely acknowledged as a major co‐morbidity after transplantation. In the current care ecosystem, children are not proactively and systematically screened for trauma symptoms, and high‐quality resources are not consistently accessible to families [3]. To address this gap, and to inform improvements in transplant care, this study aimed to explore experiences that impede and facilitate awareness of PTS, as well as facilitators and barriers to families' access to available resources.

Methods

Study Population

Between August 2022 and September 2023, recipients of a kidney, liver, or heart transplant at a tertiary pediatric transplant center between ages 12 and 18 and their parents or primary caregivers were invited to participate in an interview study. A total of 34 pediatric SOT recipients and 35 parents/caregivers were introduced to the study or given a study brochure. A formal diagnosis of post‐traumatic stress disorder was not required. The CTSQ, which identifies children with PTS symptoms who are at risk of developing post‐traumatic stress disorder [12], was administered annually as part of clinical care. The questionnaire is highly correlated with the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL), has been validated in children admitted to the hospital after a variety of injuries, and is accurate at predicting PTS disorder in children at 6 months post‐trauma [12, 13, 14]. The CTSQ score was used to screen for eligibility by identifying pediatric SOT recipients in the top quartile of scores within the clinic (scores of 3–10/10), indicating higher levels of PTS symptoms. Based on this threshold, some participants were included even if they did not complete the full CTSQ. For pediatric SOT recipients who completed multiple CTSQs, the data nearest to the time of transplantation was used (e.g., the first CTSQ completed after transplantation). Patients and parents/caregivers were required to understand and speak English to participate. The final sample size was determined based on data saturation, where recurring themes and insights emerged across participant narratives. The study was approved by the institutional research ethics board (H21‐02891), with participants providing informed consent/assent.

Interviews and Coding

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted by MTT and AR with pediatric SOT recipients and parents/caregivers separately. The interview guide was developed and piloted by the research team, with questions focused on (1) awareness and management of PTS; (2) timelines surrounding PTS awareness and resource‐seeking; (3) facilitators to PTS awareness; (4) barriers to PTS awareness; (5) information seeking and the impact of existing resources and services; and (6) areas for improvement. Examples of interview questions are found in Table 1. A definition of PTS for this study was developed by the research team and given to each participant at the start of the interview. The interviews utilized the term “PTS” with parents/caregivers to facilitate a broader exploration of the participants' experiences and perspectives. The term “mental health” was used for pediatric SOT recipient participants instead of ‘PTS’ to make questions easier to understand.

The interviews were recorded using the Zoom platform and transcribed by a professional transcription service, Transcripts were analyzed qualitatively using the NVivo software. A descriptive content analysis of the transcripts was used to uncover emerging themes around PTS experiences and understanding. This form of analysis was chosen to allow for systematic organization and summarization the data, as well as to produce a transparent and reliable representation of the data while minimizing interpretive bias. A preliminary coding guide was developed during an initial analysis of a set of interview transcripts and applied to a randomized set of interviews (n = 2) by two independent coders (MTT and AR). The coding guide was refined through an iterative process by discussing and resolving discrepancies until an overall, unweighted, kappa coefficient 0.75 was reached. The final coding guide was applied by a primary coder (MTT) to the remaining sample of interview transcripts.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Interviews were conducted with 11 parents/caregivers (all female) and 6 pediatric SOT recipients (2 females, 4 males), with an average duration of 47 minutes. The sample included five parent/caregiver–child pairs who were related and participated in the study.

Recipients of a kidney (n = 4) and heart (n = 2) transplant who participated in the interviews had a mean age of 10.83 years (range: 6–14 years) at the time of their transplant and 15.33 years (range: 12–19 years) at the time of the interview. The mean time since transplant was 4 years (range: 1–8 years). The mean CTSQ score was 4.4, although one participant's score was omitted due to an incomplete CTSQ.

Parents/caregivers who participated had children who received a kidney (n = 10) and heart (n = 1) transplant and had a mean age of 9.18 years (range: 2–14 years) at the time of transplant and 13 years (range: 7–19 years) at the time of the interview. The mean time since their child's transplant was 3.8 years (range: 0–8 years). Their child's mean CTSQ score was 3.86, although scores of four children were omitted due to an incomplete CTSQ.

Familiarity and Understanding of PTS

Parents/caregivers were asked about their level of familiarity with PTS and their child's PTS symptoms and were provided a scale shown in Figure 1. This scale was developed for this study to prompt participants to reflect on their experiences, based on similar instruments used in prior studies assessing mental health awareness and understanding [15, 16]. Two parents/caregivers indicated they had little knowledge, five parents/caregivers had some knowledge, and three parents/caregivers reported having strong knowledge of PTS and understanding of their child's PTS symptoms.

Scale presented to parents/caregivers during the interview around understanding of PTS and child's PTS symptoms.

Pediatric SOT recipients were asked a similar question about their understanding of mental health and PTS, with two children reporting they had no understanding, four children reporting some understanding, and no children reporting a strong understanding. Children who had some understanding defined mental health as keeping the brain “happy,” how they were “feeling,” and was related to their “thoughts.”

Having their child communicate about PTS symptoms also enabled parents/caregivers to better understand what their child was going through.She really makes it known; she doesn't hide when she's scared, she's always just been very vocal about it, and I've just had to try and reassure her. Discussion

This qualitative study explored the impacts of PTS on the SOT journey of both pediatric patients and parents/caregivers who underwent kidney or heart transplantation. We uncovered the experiences of transplant on pediatric SOT recipients and their families and how PTS symptoms are manifested during the transplant journey. We identified barriers and facilitators to their awareness and understanding of PTS, as well as the benefits and limitations of existing resources to support PTS. Patients and parents/caregivers offered practical recommendations on how to improve accessibility and support for children who will need or have had a solid organ transplant.

The SOT journey can be traumatic for pediatric patients, as it involves emotionally and physically stressful events such as being hospitalized, surgery, post‐transplant care, and lifestyle changes. Our findings offer detailed insights about the way PTS symptoms are manifested and persist in SOT recipients. In line with these findings, previous quantitative studies investigated the presence of PTS symptoms in patients who had organ transplantation and other health‐related complications [3, 4, 6, 17, 18, 19, 20]. In studies exploring post‐traumatic stress in young adults who survived childhood cancer, approximately 20% of the sample met the criteria for a PTS disorder diagnosis [18, 21]. Similar reports were found for adolescent liver, heart, and kidney transplant recipients, where more than 16% of the sample met all the symptom criteria for PTS disorder [6]. Taken together, the narratives presented in this study emphasize the need to draw attention to the presence of PTS experienced by pediatric SOT recipients throughout their transplant journey.

This study also uncovered narratives around the impact of the transplant on the family members of SOT recipients, with parents/caregivers experiencing trauma related to their child's transplant. Results revealed emotional impacts with feelings of anxiety, guilt, fear, depression, and uncontrollable thoughts. Participants shared about constantly worrying that something may go wrong, being unable to relax, and experiencing new or increased feelings of anxiety. It has been found that parents commonly experience trauma associated with the pediatric SOT journey. Previous studies discovered that transplant and other child health variables significantly predicted PTS symptom severity scores in parents [19, 22]. Parents of children who had complex health conditions, underwent general surgery or transplantation, and met the DSM‐IV criteria for PTS disorder [20, 22, 23]. Trauma is not an isolated experience of those undergoing transplant. While the present study did not have the opportunity to speak directly to siblings of pediatric SOT recipients, future work should incorporate interviews with this group to uncover a complete understanding of how the SOT journey affects the immediate family.

Majority of both parents/caregivers and SOT recipients expressed that they preferred to receive PTS information and resources early on before the transplant and from a trusted source. The preferred timing and source of information is supported by previous literature related to other medical events. Children awaiting a medical procedure preferred to receive relevant information in preparation for their procedure [24]. A study investigating what perioperative information children wanted to receive discovered that children with heightened levels of anxiety sought out information to help manage their symptoms [25]. Moreover, family caregivers of head and neck cancer patients needed information at the time of diagnosis related to managing the patient's pain or distress [26]. The same study reported that caregivers would first seek information from formal health care professionals [26]. Similarly, pediatric cancer patients preferred hearing information about their prognosis directly from their physician over receiving information from the Internet [27]. As resources may come from different health care professionals throughout a pediatric patient's medical journey, it is important that the information provided is consistent across the health care team [28]. To support patients' and families' understanding, information should be presented in clear and easy‐to‐understand language, tailored to the patient's needs, with activation strategies used to ensure both comprehension and preparedness [28, 29]. A recent case study utilized the Stanford Integrated Psychosocial Assessment for Transplantation (SIPAT) to assess an adolescent's readiness and understanding about their transplant [30]. This evaluation led to a formulated care plan that involved the provision of psychoeducation and psychological support by a clinical psychologist [30]. Results demonstrated that early identification of psychosocial challenges enabled targeted interventions and the establishment of psychological supports, contributing to the successful management of the transplant waiting period and favorable outcomes [30]. Similarly, a brief mindfulness intervention delivered to patients with advanced heart failure awaiting transplant resulted in significant reductions in stress and anxiety and an increase in resilience [31]. Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of providing early information and psychoeducation to patients and families, ensuring that they are met with adequate support throughout the transplant journey.

Participants in this study also emphasized that the age of the pediatric SOT recipient played an important role in the timing of PTS information and resources shared. This finding overlaps with insights provided in a review around educational needs of adolescent transplant recipients. The authors stressed that every patient does not benefit from the same education as they exhibit a range of developmental experiences, values, and family environments that can affect their understanding and learning [32]. Similar to this, families of youth with diabetes complications expressed that the amount, depth, and timing of information about complications depend on the child's level of maturity [33]. Information needs may also change over the course of the patient's medical journey. A study investigating the information needs of cancer patients at different stages of their journey found a relationship between the time since diagnosis and level of information needed [34]. Another study reported that kidney recipients and their caregivers were overwhelmed to retain information at hospital discharge, posing a need for ongoing and repeated information throughout the post‐transplant period [35]. With this, future work should develop a framework to determine the optimal time and forms of PTS information to provide pediatric SOT recipients and families to better support them long‐term.

Both pediatric SOT recipients and parents/caregivers in this study provided improvements to their current care and additional resources or services that could better support them and the impact of PTS. Existing interventions such as family camps and online programs have shown to impact the well‐being of transplant recipients and their families. Pediatric heart transplant recipients and their families benefited from a family camp by gaining knowledge about their situation, coping strategies, and improved well‐being [36]. An internet‐based intervention for heart transplant recipients and families found improvements in depressive and anxiety symptoms and social functioning [37]. Psychoeducational interventions during hospital stays have also been suggested to help parents develop coping and stress‐reduction skills in order to adjust to life after the hospital and reduce the risk for PTS symptoms and PTS disorder [23]. Although participants in this study did not raise the need for specific mental health interventions, they emphasized the need to improve the health care process to gain access to mental health support. This highlights the strong desire for embedding mental health care directly into the overall care journey, rather than placing the responsibility on patients and families to seek out resources independently. Mobile apps [38] and social media can play a supportive role in this process by raising awareness of mental health issues experienced by patients and families. A recent systematic review demonstrated that digital media campaigns can increase knowledge and increase help‐seeking among young people [39]. However, while social media can foster connection and support, it can also cause harm such as spreading misinformation or limiting access to resources due to overly restrictive moderation practices [40]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of integrated and accessible mental health support as a fundamental component of care for pediatric transplant recipients and their families.

While this study yields novel qualitative insights from interviews with both parents/caregivers and SOT recipients about their experiences of PTS, there are limitations in the representation in our sample. As pediatric SOT recipients who participated were selected based on a CTSQ score in the top quartile due to resource constraints, we were unable to include some voices from those with lesser PTS symptoms that may have been nonetheless important. Conversely, because families were approached based on elevated PTS symptoms in the SOT recipients, it is possible that the emotional difficulty of discussing their experiences contributed to some declining to participate in the interview. Future efforts should consider implementing strategies to engage with children and families at both ends of the symptom spectrum to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the experiences of PTS for pediatric SOT recipients. To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative study exploring experiences of PTS in pediatric SOT recipients, providing critical insights that are valuable for improving the care and support for patients and their families.

Conclusion

This qualitative study emphasizes the importance of early, targeted, and developmentally appropriate mental health supports throughout the transplant journey. Several themes emerged relating to how personal experiences and systemic factors impede or facilitate the awareness and understanding of PTS for both pediatric SOT recipients and their parents/caregivers, with each theme providing actionable insights for care program development and implementation. Development of support programs should be guided by patient voices, and substantial weight should be given to the recommendations provided here by patients and families about what should be prioritized. These findings can inform improvements to the accessibility and effectiveness of PTS supports for pediatric SOT recipients and their families.
