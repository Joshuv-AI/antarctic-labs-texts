A multimodal dataset for emotional transition analysis in virtual reality
Authors: Namazbai Ishmakhametov, Mohammad Y. M. Naser, Selam B. Kelil, Charles McClary, Jason S. Metcalfe, Sylvia Bhattacharya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emotion recognition from physiological signals typically treats emotions as discrete, static states rather than dynamic processes, limiting real-world affective computing applications. This dataset contains multimodal physiological recordings from 28 participants experiencing systematically designed emotional transitions in virtual reality. Participants viewed emotion-eliciting video stimuli across three emotional quadrants with transition periods between stimuli. Four physiological modalities were recorded: EEG (7 channels, 300 Hz), ECG (4 leads, 512 Hz), EMG (2 channels, 512 Hz), and GSR (3 channels, 10 Hz). The protocol employed a balanced incomplete block design across six emotional sequences. Statistical validation shows quadrant differentiation with 70% physiologically validated and 85% self-reported emotion induction success rates on average. Individual journey analysis indicates that participants traversed between 8.84% and 58.39% of the theoretical maximum cumulative distance on the valence–arousal plane, reflecting substantial individual differences in emotional responsivity. The dataset comprises 1.84 GB of original XDF recordings, 238 video-aligned physiological segments, and self-assessment ratings. This resource enables research in dynamic emotion recognition, and individual differences in responsivity during controlled emotional transitions.

Background & Summary

Emotion recognition plays an increasingly important role in affective computing applications, from human-computer interaction to mental health monitoring1–3. Among various emotion recognition tasks, understanding dynamic emotional processes represents a key challenge for achieving naturalistic emotional intelligence4,5. Emotion recognition traditionally aims to enable machines to classify discrete emotional states from physiological and behavioral signals. Similar to other machine learning problems, high-quality datasets are prerequisite for advancing this field. Consequently, several datasets have been proposed to promote emotion recognition research from multimodal signals.

For example, the DEAP dataset6 collected EEG, GSR, blood volume pressure, and other physiological signals from 32 participants viewing music videos. MAHNOB-HCI7 constructed a multimodal database consisting of facial videos, audio signals, eye gaze data, and physiological signals including EEG, ECG, and GSR. Recently, K-EmoCon8 created a dataset for emotion recognition in naturalistic conversations, while AMIGOS9, DREAMER10, and other datasets have contributed additional resources focusing on discrete emotion classification in laboratory settings.

Although these existing datasets greatly promote emotion recognition research, they share a fundamental limitation of discrete treatment of emotions. They treat emotional experiences as discrete, independent events rather than components of continuous emotional flow. Effectively, their emotional elicitation paradigm is static in the sense that it is defined as isolated clips followed by single post-stimulus rating or continuous external annotation. Although current datasets focus on static emotion classification from isolated stimulus-response pairs, the real-world emotional experiences involve dynamic transitions between different emotional states. While recent advances in affective computing have achieved substantial performance on static emotion classification tasks, with multimodal fusion approaches reaching 75–95% accuracy on established benchmarks11–13, this success masks a critical limitation. In natural settings, emotional states evolve over time, transitions between emotions create unique physiological signatures, and the temporal context of previous emotional states influences current responses14,15. This static emotion paradigm overlooks the temporal nature of emotional processes and creates a significant gap between laboratory performance and real-world emotion recognition applications. Based on this limitation, we adapt the concept of emotional journey from affective gaming research, defined as a temporally ordered sequence of distinct emotional states experienced by an individual16. While previous emotional journey implementations focused on adaptive gaming narratives responding to real-time emotional states, our approach systematically guides participants through predetermined emotional transitions for scientific analysis. We define our concept of dynamic emotional journey as a temporally ordered sequence of emotional blocks – each block consisting of pre-stimulus emotional rating, video stimulus, and post-stimulus emotional rating, interspersed with structured inter-stimulus intervals (comprising pre-stimulus rating, neutral washout video, and post-stimulus rating) to ensure complete autonomic recovery between emotional states. This controlled paradigm enables systematic study of emotional transitions and their physiological signatures, distinct from the adaptive entertainment applications of previous emotional journey concepts. Thus, the concept of emotional journeys necessitates need for affective datasets that explicitly invoke emotions in a chronologically successive manner. Most datasets employ variable stimulus durations that complicate standardized analysis—DREAMER uses clips ranging from 65–393 seconds but analyzes only the final 60 seconds, while other datasets show similar temporal inconsistency. Existing datasets employ varying reset periods between emotional stimuli. For instance, DEAP provides almost no reset period between stimuli, creating inevitable emotional carryover effects14,15, while ASCERTAIN’s 4-second fixation cross presentation intervals are physiologically insufficient for full autonomic recovery, particularly for slower-responding signals like GSR which requires approximately 1–5 seconds for response latency17.

The SEED dataset and its final version SEED-VII, on the other hand, explicitly treat neutral stimuli as a target emotion class for classification, and also include varying intervals between stimuli. For instance, SEED-VII provided 3 second interval between stimuli and participants assessed their emotional responses only after having watched all 20 videos within single session, while SEED provided 15 seconds of rest and 45 seconds for self-assessment after each of stimuli.

In addition to video-based datasets, several influential datasets use static pictures to elicit emotions. For instance, the I-DARE dataset introduced multimodal physiological recordings (EEG, skin conductance, PPG, EMG, and eye tracking) collected from 63 participants viewing 32 images that uniformly cover all four quadrants of the valence–arousal space. These pictorial datasets are well suited for studying responses to brief, non-narrative stimuli such as advertisements, and single frames. However, the present work focuses on dynamic, narrative film clips presented in immersive VR, where temporal unfolding and emotional journeys over several minutes are central to the design; for this reason, our primary comparisons were against video-based datasets. Perhaps most critically, no existing dataset explicitly designs for studying emotional transitions in a controlled manner.

The Dynamic Emotional Journey Analysis in Virtual Universe (DEJA-VU) dataset addresses these limitations through a transition-focused experimental design using virtual reality emotion induction. The use of virtual reality creates substantially greater immersion than traditional 2D screen presentations18–20 while maintaining experimental control. While participants experienced emotional stimuli designed to elicit specific transitions between emotional quadrants, we collected synchronized EEG, ECG, EMG, and GSR signals using portable devices. The dataset employs video stimuli standardized to be on average 3 minutes long (ranging from 1:41 to 4:26) with structured 133-second inter-stimulus intervals incorporating neutral washout video and self-assessment periods (see Experimental Protocol), enabling systematic study of emotional transitions that has been impossible with existing resources. To the best of our knowledge, DEJA-VU is currently the only available dataset specifically designed for emotional transition analysis, and it may help advance research in dynamic emotion recognition and temporal affective computing by allowing study of carry-over, recovery and sequence effects during emotional journeys.

Methods

Stimuli selection

To elicit emotional responses across different valence-arousal quadrants, we selected video clips from two validated emotion elicitation databases: FilmStim21 (70 clips validated on 364 students, https://sites.uclouvain.be/ipsp/FilmStim/) and EmoStim22 (99 clips validated on 638 students, https://yareta.unige.ch/archives/3476077e-5a1c-4572-a99b-1dada060384f). We did not conduct independent inter-rater reliability assessments for the clip extensions, relying instead on the original database validations and post-hoc empirical confirmation of induction success (see Technical Validation). This is a limitation we acknowledge. Since both databases catalog scenes from commercially available films, the corresponding high-definition source material was readily accessible. HD versions were used to ensure visual fidelity within the VR headset’s display resolution.

Consequently, the durations reported in Table 2 may differ from the original database entries. To optimize emotional induction within the immersive VR environment, we prioritized narrative coherence and tension development over strict adherence to the original clip boundaries. Specifically, in instances where the original cut was deemed abrupt, we extended the clip start time (variable Y) relative to the reference duration (X) to provide adequate narrative context (Total time = X + Y). To adhere to copyright regulations, the video content itself is not included in the distributed dataset; only the physiological responses to these stimuli are shared.

We targeted three emotional quadrants: high arousal positive valence (Quadrant A), high arousal negative valence (Quadrant B), and low arousal negative valence (Quadrant C), plus low arousal, positive valence (Quadrant D) which served as a neutral baseline condition. Quadrant D was included in every session as the initial baseline condition rather than as a fourth target emotional state. States of calm contentment are physiologically difficult to distinguish from neutral baseline, as both are characterized by low autonomic arousal and positive affect; using Quadrant D as a standardized starting point instead enables consistent measurement of transitions from baseline to each target quadrant (D → A, D → B, D → C), providing a common reference for cross-participant comparison

Ethics statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kennesaw State University (IRB # IRB-FY25-69). All participants provided written informed consent and were informed about experimental procedures, potential risks, and the unrestricted sharing of anonymized physiological data for future research without requiring additional consent.

Participants

Twenty-eight healthy participants (age range: 20–65 years, mean age = 33.1, 11 females, 17 males) were recruited from Kennesaw State University through campus flyers and email announcements. Inclusion criteria required participants to be ≥18 years old, be comfortable with VR technology, and have no psychiatric or neurological conditions.

Sample size determination was based on pre-registered power analysis in IRB targeting detection of moderate-to-large effects across four emotional quadrants (Cohen’s f ≥0.25, α = 0.05, power = 0.80), with SAM valence ratings as the primary outcome measure. The original pre-registration specified Cohen’s d ≥0.5 for pairwise comparisons and we report the equivalent Cohen’s f for omnibus four-group testing.

The initial target of 40–50 participants was reduced to 28 participants (34 sessions) due to recruitment constraints. Post-hoc sensitivity analysis indicates that with N = 136 ratings (34 sessions ×4 quadrants) and α = 0.05, the study achieves 80% power to detect effects of Cohen’s f ≥0.29 (small-to-medium).

Physiological signals and video presentation were synchronized through Lab Streaming Layer (LSL), which provides unified timestamps across all recording devices. All data streams (EEG, ECG, EMG, GSR) were recorded on a single PC configuration that remained constant across all 34 sessions.

The experimenter initiated video playback on the VR headset and LSL recording simultaneously using a verbal countdown protocol. The session anchor timestamp (onset of the first SAM video, recorded as video_start_24 in the dataset’s table videos) was documented manually based on system time, and all subsequent video timestamps were calculated deterministically from this anchor using known video durations from the video_mappings table in the database.

This procedure yields an estimated synchronization precision of approximately ± 0.5 seconds, which is acceptable for the temporal resolution of our analysis (10-second windows with 8-second stride). Experimental protocol

All stimuli were presented through Meta Quest II VR headsets to ensure immersive, controlled visual experiences. Each session followed a standardized structure:baseline recording using Quadrant D videosequence of three emotional stimuli from different quadrantsstructured inter-stimulus intervals between emotional stimuli, each comprising a post-stimulus self-assessment (32 s), a neutral washout video (69 s), and a pre-stimulus self-assessment (32 s), yielding a total non-stimulus duration of 133 seconds.

The washout and self-assessment durations (69 and 32 seconds, respectively) differ slightly from the originally planned 60- and 30-second intervals due to video rendering precision. These longer durations were retained as they provided conservative recovery margins exceeding minimum requirements for all modalities as explained below.

EEG oscillatory activity changes rapidly with cognitive demands and typically shows phasic, time-locked responses that return toward baseline soon after stimulus offset, whereas autonomic indices show slower recovery28. EMG-based emotion protocols commonly include multi-second pre-trial baselines and tens-of-seconds inter-trial intervals to capture these phasic changes and allow partial recovery between stimuli29, while ECG-derived heart-rate variability measures may require considerably longer periods to return fully to resting levels30. In contrast, GSR-based stress paradigms often incorporate recovery phases on the order of several minutes (e.g., approximately 3 minutes) to permit autonomic responses to subside before the next induction31. Hence, our inter-stimulus intervals were designed to be long enough to accommodate relatively slow autonomic recovery while remaining practical in total session length.

The neutral washout video component was a waterfall segment from the wordless documentary ‘Baraka’32 (segment start: 27:29), selected for its absence of dialogue, narrative structure, and human faces. Nature scenes have been empirically demonstrated to facilitate physiological recovery and stress reduction33,34, and among available segments this contained the least emotionally activating content.

Attention and engagement verification

While this study utilized the Meta Quest II which lacks integrated eye-tracking, the experimental paradigm was specifically designed to minimize the need for gaze verification. Unlike naturalistic VR scenarios with multiple competing targets, this protocol presented a single, immersive visual stimulus occupying the participant’s complete visual field (110° FOV), leaving no alternative targets for visual attention. In this constrained context, the validity of engagement is best evidenced by the depth of emotional processing rather than gaze direction. As detailed in the Technical Validation section, the robust physiological differentiation across autonomic signals (GSR, ECG) and cortical patterns (EEG) provides strong objective evidence that participants were not merely looking at the stimuli but were physiologically engaged with the emotional content.

Participants were assigned to one of six possible emotional sequences (ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA) to control order effects, ensuring balanced representation across all transition types. The full randomized schedule of videos for all participants is provided in the session_metadata sheet of data.xlsx in the dataset repository. Total VR exposure time was up to 22.5 minutes per session.

Emotional self-assessments were collected before and after each video clip using an adapted version of the Self-Assessment Manikin35 (SAM) with inverted Valence and Arousal 1–9 point scales and original direction of Dominance, presented as a static video. Participants provided verbal ratings during 32-second assessment periods, recorded by the principal investigator.

Each session consisted of four blocks: an initial baseline block (Quadrant D) followed by three emotional blocks from Quadrants A, B, and C in randomized order. Each block comprised a pre-stimulus SAM assessment, presentation of the emotional video, and a post-stimulus SAM assessment. Between emotional blocks, the structured inter-stimulus interval (post-SAM + neutral washout video + pre-SAM, totaling 133 seconds) provided physiological recovery as described above.

After the end of session, participants took custom designed Ekman’s six basic emotions36 survey with 9-point scales for happiness, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise for each of three emotional videos. Before and after the full session participants took PANAS37 test to assess overall change of emotional state, and they also The Big Five38 personality inventory was also completed by all participants in the first session to characterize individual differences in emotional responsivity. The Big Five measures five major personality dimensions (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism) that have been extensively demonstrated to influence emotional response patterns and affective processing. Meta-analytic and theoretical evidence shows that primary emotional systems including FEAR, SADNESS, and ANGER consistently relate to high Neuroticism, while positive emotional systems link to Extraversion and other Big Five traits39,40. Research demonstrates that personality traits predict physiological responses to emotional stimuli, with neuroticism showing significant correlations with EEG asymmetry patterns during emotional face processing41.

Technical Validation

Self-assessment analysis

To validate that the emotional stimuli successfully elicited the intended emotional states, we conducted comprehensive statistical analysis of Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM) ratings collected after each emotional video. The analysis included 816 valence, arousal and dominance ratings with the following breakdown: 28 participants participated in 28 first sessions and 6 subjects returned for second session, and within each session subjects gave numeric triplet Valence-Arousal-Dominance before and after each emotional video from quadrants A, B, C and D, thus yielding 24 numbers per session.

Individual journey pattern analysis

We analyzed complete emotional trajectories for 34 participant-sessions to characterize individual differences and sequence effects in emotional transitions.

To minimize habituation and induction fatigue, participants were limited to a maximum of two experimental sessions. Physiological validation

To demonstrate that the elicited emotional states produced distinct and detectable physiological signatures suitable for machine learning applications, we conducted a two-tiered statistical validation analysis focusing on both static state differentiation and dynamic transition sensitivity.

Static state differentiation for window-level separability

We analyzed the distribution of physiological features across emotional quadrants to assess signal separability. To assess signal stability and granularity, continuous recordings corresponding specifically to the four emotional stimulus blocks (Quadrants A, B, C, and D) were extracted from all 34 experimental sessions. These targeted segments were partitioned into 10-second sliding windows with an 8-second stride. Due to the varying duration of the video stimuli (ranging from 1:41 to 4:26 per clip), the number of windows differs per quadrant. Windows with insufficient valid data, such as fewer than 3 detected R-peaks for ECG features, were excluded via automated quality criteria documented in the preprocessing code. The exclusion yielded a final dataset of N = 2,892 analysis windows for this validation with the following counts of windows per quadrants: A – 644, B – 796, C – 704, and D – 748.

To reproduce this windowing strategy, the precise start and end timestamps for every video stimulus are provided in the videos table of the database file. Researchers can utilize these timestamps to replicate our segmentation or extract custom epochs from the continuous Level 1 files if different windowing parameters are required.

Since the preprocessing pipeline includes ICA-based artifact removal, which redistributes noise energy rather than eliminating it, direct frequency-domain SNR comparison between raw and cleaned signals is non-trivial. We therefore performed downstream physiological validation to assess quadrant differentiation. Of the 83 computed features, 80 were testable (3 ECG frequency-domain features were excluded due to insufficient data in 10-second windows). A Kruskal-Wallis H-test was performed on N = 2,892 analysis windows to determine if feature distributions differed significantly across the four emotional quadrants (A, B, C, D). Of these, 34 features showed significant differentiation at p < 0.001 and 51 at p < 0.05. Dynamic transition sensitivity analysis

To validate the dataset’s temporal sensitivity, we analyzed physiological change scores (Δ) associated with specific emotional transitions. To ensure statistical independence and avoid pseudo-replication inherent in windowed analyses, we aggregated data at the session level. For each experimental session and each specific transition, we calculated the median value of each physiological feature across all valid windows within the starting quadrant (M_start) and the ending quadrant (M_end). The change score was defined as Δ = M_end – M_start, yielding a single independent delta value per session for each transition (n = 9–15 depending on sequence permutation). We then applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test against a null hypothesis of zero median change.

Signal quality analysis

To ensure technical rigor and transparency, we conducted a comprehensive signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) analysis across all physiological modalities. In contrast to many prior affective computing datasets that focus on preprocessing methods but omit quantitative signal quality, DEJA-VU provides systematic SNR evaluation to enable informed utilization and direct comparison with future studies.

SNR estimation methodology

For each modality, we employed Welch’s method for power spectral density (PSD) estimation, using window sizes tailored to the sampling rate and typical frequency content of each signal. Specifically, we set window length to 2048 samples for EEG (≈6.8 s at 300 Hz), 2048 samples for ECG and EMG (≈4 s at 512 Hz), and 512 samples for GSR (≈51.2 s at 10 Hz). This modality-specific approach maximizes the reliability of PSD and SNR estimates across the wide range of temporal resolutions inherent to multimodal physiological data, and follows best practices reported in recent open datasets and wearable sensor studies. All windows used 50% overlaps and a Hann window; window size never exceeded 1/4 of the analyzed segment.

SNR calculation

For each channel, SNR was computed in the frequency domain as the log-ratio of the total spectral power within a physiologically relevant “signal” band versus a “noise” band:EEG: Signal 1–60 Hz; Noise >60 Hz, following standard cortical EEG frequency ranges45.ECG: Signal 0.5–40 Hz; Noise >40 Hz, to capture the full QRS complex while excluding high-frequency noise46,47.EMG: Signal ≥ 20 Hz; Noise 0.5–20 Hz, consistent with surface EMG bandwidth recommendations48.GSR: Signal 0.01–1 Hz; Noise >1 Hz, encompassing the main electrodermal spectral band17.

Channel SNR was computed for the full experimental session per participant. We report SNR relative to both classic laboratory (‘lab’) thresholds and more permissive ‘field’ thresholds representative of mobile, VR, or wearable signal quality. This dual reporting provides transparency and context, as mobile/VR studies routinely yield lower SNR than stationary, wet-electrode laboratory setups. For EEG and ECG, we set a field threshold of 10 dB, reflecting evidence that wearable ECG49,50 and mobile EEG51 applications can still support reliable QRS detection and meaningful spectral responses at single-digit to low-double-digit SNRs. For EMG, we adopted a 5 dB field threshold, consistent with reports that wearable EMG systems typically operate within a 5–20 dB SNR range52,53. For GSR, we used a 25 dB field threshold, chosen to ensure a clearly dominant low-frequency electrodermal component relative to high-frequency noise, in line with published EDA spectral analyses17,54. These values are therefore pragmatic, literature-informed criteria rather than strict physiological limits.

We have also compared the change in SNR analysis after the data has been preprocessed as shown in Table 11a. All modalities with applicable frequency-domain comparison showed consistent gains, with the largest improvement in GSR (+14.2 dB) and ECG (+8.7 to +11.6 dB).

Across all 34 sessions, preprocessed ECG channels achieved 97–100% compliance with both laboratory and field thresholds, reflecting the substantial improvement from bandpass filtering. GSR maintained high quality with 94.1% of sessions exceeding both thresholds. EEG channels showed moderate SNR consistent with mobile, dry-electrode paradigms, meeting field quality standards in most of sessions after preprocessing. Raw EMG SNR values are negative, which is expected for surface facial EMG where muscle activation energy is distributed across a broad frequency range and low-amplitude signals are recorded from small muscle groups; these values are consistent with wearable facial EMG literature. By reporting both sets of thresholds and the preprocessing improvement, we provide full transparency regarding signal quality and enable users to select modalities and channels appropriate for their research context and analytic goals.

Signal preprocessing

All preprocessing was implemented in Python using MNE-Python55 and SciPy.

For EEG, we applied FIR bandpass filtering (1–100 Hz, firwin design) and notch filtering (60 Hz) to all EEG and EOG channels. ICA decomposition was performed using FastICA with 4 components (random_state = 97, max_iter = 1000). Artifactual components were identified by computing the absolute Pearson correlation between each ICA component time course and each EOG channel (EOG1, EOG2); the component with the highest absolute correlation was excluded if |r| > 0.3, which is a conservative threshold informed by correlation-based EOG-ICA criteria56, with a maximum of one component per session. This polarity-invariant approach was necessary because EOG signal polarity varied across participants due to VR headset positioning (observed correlations ranged from −0.95 to + 0.86 across 34 sessions).

ECG signals were bandpass filtered (0.5–45 Hz, Butterworth order 4, zero-phase); the 45 Hz cutoff inherently attenuates 60 Hz line noise, making a separate notch filter unnecessary.

EMG signals were high-pass filtered (20 Hz, Butterworth order 4, zero-phase), notch filtered (60 Hz, Q = 30), full-wave rectified, and smoothed with a 200 ms moving average to extract the amplitude envelope.

GSR signals were linearly detrended and low-pass filtered (1.0 Hz, Butterworth order 2, zero-phase); tonic/phasic decomposition was performed during feature extraction via 5-second moving average subtraction. Feature extraction

To assess signal quality and validate the physiological responses reported in the Technical Validation section, we implemented a comprehensive feature extraction pipeline producing 83 features across all four modalities, covering temporal statistics, frequency-domain power, asymmetry, coherence, heart rate variability, muscle activation, and electrodermal measures. These comprise 50 EEG features (28 temporal statistics, 10 band powers, 10 asymmetry indices, 2 coherence measures), 15 ECG features (7 time-domain, 5 frequency-domain, 3 nonlinear HRV indices), 12 EMG features (6 per channel for zygomaticus and trapezius), and 6 GSR features (3 tonic SCL and 3 phasic SCR measures). For ECG, RR-peak detection used a simplified Pan-Tompkins46 implementation with adaptive thresholding; RR intervals were constrained to 300–2000 ms with a minimum of 3 valid peaks per analysis window. To identify the most discriminative features for the technical validation, we ranked all 83 features by Kruskal-Wallis H-statistic across four emotional quadrants and selected the top 3 per modality. Usage Notes

The DEJA-VU dataset addresses several key application areas for emotion recognition research described below.

VR and real-world emotion research

Although collected using virtual reality environments, the emotional and physiological responses recorded are not limited to VR-specific contexts. VR induction amplifies core affective processes by more closely mirroring the multisensory, spatial, and interactive nature of real-world emotional experiences. Compared to traditional 2D video protocols, VR generates more intense, sustained, and naturalistic emotional responses, which in turn elicit richer and more robust physiological signals across EEG, ECG, EMG, and GSR modalities43,57,58. Results derived from DEJA-VU should therefore be at least as generalizable to daily-life emotion recognition as those obtained from conventional laboratory paradigms.

Emerging technology applications

The dataset’s focus on high-quality, multimodal physiological recordings in immersive environments positions it well for training and benchmarking AI systems designed for wearable and mobile deployment scenarios. As consumer AR/VR and AI-powered wearable devices become more prevalent, datasets that capture emotional responses in embodied, interactive contexts become increasingly valuable for developing adaptive human-computer interaction systems and digital health applications.

Research applications

DEJA-VU enables multiple research directions including dynamic emotion recognition, temporal affective computing, individual differences in emotional responsivity, multimodal fusion algorithm development, and transition-based emotion analysis that has been impossible with existing static emotion datasets.
