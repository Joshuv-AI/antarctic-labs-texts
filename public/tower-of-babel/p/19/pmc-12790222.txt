Intrapericardial Chronic Expanding Hematoma Causing Myocardial Ischemia by Compressing the Left Anterior Descending Artery
Authors: Motoki Taniguchi, Yasutsugu Shiono, Kanako Sagan, Shingo Ota, Kentaro Honda, Kazushi Takemoto, Hironori Kitabata, Shinichi Murata, Yoshiharu Nishimura, Atsushi Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntrapericardial chronic expanding hematoma (CEH) is rare, but the diagnosis is challenging because it should be differentiated from cardiac tumors and can cause unique consequences.Case SummaryAn 80-year-old woman with a history of chest trauma presented with exertional dyspnea. Computed tomography revealed an intrapericardial mass compressing the left ventricle, pulmonary artery, and left anterior descending artery (LAD). Cardiac magnetic resonance and positron emission tomography suggested CEH without malignancy. Coronary angiography showed 99% stenosis of the LAD, but right heart catheterization revealed no hemodynamic compromise in the pulmonary artery and left ventricle. Surgical resection was performed to relieve myocardial ischemia. Histology confirmed the diagnosis of CEH.DiscussionMultimodal testing helped exclude the malignancy and identified myocardial ischemia due to CEH as the primary cause of symptoms.Take-Home MessagesCEH should be considered in patients with prior chest trauma. Multimodality testing is essential for accurate diagnosis and management.

History of Presentation

An 80-year-old woman was referred to our cardiology department because of shortness of breath and for the evaluation of a large anterior mediastinal mass detected on computed tomography (CT). Past Medical History

The patient had a history of breast cancer treated with chemoradiation therapy 5 years ago and chest trauma resulting from a scooter accident approximately 8 years ago.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included mediastinal tumors (eg, teratoma, ectopic thyroid tumor, and malignant lymphoma), cardiac tumors (eg, myxoma, papillary fibroelastoma, lipoma, and primary or metastatic malignancies), and hematomas. It was also important to investigate how the mass contributed to her symptoms, potentially through pulmonary artery stenosis or left ventricular diastolic impairment. A multidisciplinary imaging approach and invasive diagnostics were pursued.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified the mass adjacent to the basal anterior wall of the left ventricle and the pulmonary artery, which showed no flow acceleration in the artery (Video 1). Contrast-enhanced CT showed no contrast enhancement within the mass, though calcified tissue was observed. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed a core with a mosaic pattern of high and low signal intensities on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, encapsulated within a thick low- or iso-intensity rim (Figures 2A and 2B, Video 2). There was no enhancement observed in the myocardium and the core of the mass, but the rim was enhanced on late gadolinium enhancement imaging (Figures 2C and 2D). Positron emission tomography (PET) using 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose showed no uptake in the mass (Figure 3). Cardiac CT demonstrated the mass located between the left ventricle and the left anterior descending artery (LAD), which appeared occluded, being displaced upward and stretched by the lesion (Figure 4). To investigate the coronary arteries and the hemodynamics, this patient underwent invasive cardiac catheterization. Coronary angiography revealed 99% stenosis with flow delay at the proximal LAD (Figures 5A and 5B, Videos 3 and 4), with the distal LAD perfused by collateral flow from the right coronary artery. Right heart catheterization revealed a mean right atrial pressure of 4 mm Hg, a right ventricular systolic pressure of 24 mm Hg, a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 17 mm Hg, no pressure gradient across pulmonary artery stenosis, and a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 10 mm Hg, indicating no significant hemodynamic compromise. Serum tumor markers (ie, squamous cell carcinoma antigen, carcinoembryonic antigen, human chorionic gonadotropin, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor) were within normal limits. Management

The patient underwent open chest surgery for mass removal. The mass was easily excised with minimal adhesion; however, the coronary flow in the LAD was not sufficiently restored during the surgery, so bypass grafting to the LAD was additionally performed. Gross examination showed an organized hematoma encapsulated by calcified fibrous tissue (Figures 6A and 6B). Histological analysis confirmed a chronic hematoma with no evidence of neoplasm (Figure 6C).Figure 6Gross and Pathological Findings(A) Resected calcified capsule, (B) internal hematoma, and (C) microscopic image. Discussion

CEH is defined as a slowly growing hematoma over months or years after trauma or surgery.1 Although CEH can arise in various body regions, intrapericardial CEH is rare.2,3 Since 1995, only 6 cases have been reported.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 In those cases, the interval from trauma to symptom onset ranged from 1.7 to 50 years. Given the nature of slow progression, CEH must be distinguished from neoplasms.10 In this context, cardiac imaging plays an important role in the diagnosis. Cardiac magnetic resonance is useful for characterizing mass morphology and tissue properties. The mosaic signal pattern observed on T1- and T2-weighted images in CEH is reflective of varying hematoma age. However, malignant tumors such as angiosarcomas or metastases can also present such patterns. In such instances, combining PET with magnetic resonance imaging helps distinguish CEH from malignancies.

Even when malignancy is excluded, CEH can have serious clinical consequences. All previously reported cases involved heart failure and required surgery.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Typically, intrapericardial CEH compresses the heart, restricting diastolic filling and causing a condition similar to constrictive pericarditis. In our patient, however, no constrictive physiology was seen. Instead, myocardial ischemia due to LAD compression was the main issue. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of intrapericardial CEH causing myocardial ischemia via coronary artery compression.

An additional noteworthy finding was pulmonary artery stenosis. Although no pressure gradient was detected, the potential for future hemodynamic compromise reinforces the need for early intervention. This case highlights how CEH within the pericardium may lead to varied pathophysiologic consequences depending on which structures are involved. Therefore, a multimodal, multidisciplinary diagnostic strategy is critical in such cases. For example, in this case, TTE identified the mass and evaluated its hemodynamic impact on the heart. CT provided more detailed information regarding the location of the mass, its relationship to adjacent structures including coronary arteries, and the degree of calcification. Magnetic resonance imaging was valuable for tissue characterization alongside location assessment. PET/CT provided definitive evidence for excluding malignancy, which was later confirmed through histopathologic examination. Invasive catheterization verified the hemodynamic effects of the mass that TTE identified. Coronary angiography provided clear visualization of the mass compressing the LAD and the collateral circulation from the contralateral coronary artery. These findings established the histological diagnosis, clarified the pathologic features of the mass, and informed the decision regarding surgical intervention.

Follow-Up

The patient had an uneventful clinical course for 10 months after the surgery. Long-term clinical and imaging follow-ups are planned to monitor for recurrence of the mass and its potential consequences.

Conclusions

Intrapericardial CEH is rare but should be considered in patients with a history of chest trauma, even when it happened remotely. When the coronary arteries are involved, myocardial ischemia can occur. Multimodality testing is essential for accurate diagnosis of CEH and for guiding management.
