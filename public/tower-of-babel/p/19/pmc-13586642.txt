Bark‐associated yeasts and their potential role as tree carbon sink at the treeline of the southern Andes
Authors: F. I. Piper, C. Oporto, C. Reyes‐Bahamonde, P. Moreno‐Meynard, R. F. Nespolo, L. Saona, F. A. Cubillos, A. Fajardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract The upper elevation of the tree life form (treeline) is explained by temperature limitations in the carbon (C) investment in biomass (the growth limitation hypothesis, GLH). The GLH predicts that tissue concentrations of non‐structural carbohydrates (NSC) increase with elevation. This prediction has received mixed support in deciduous species. In addition, other potentially relevant C sources (e.g., inner bark) and sinks (e.g., bark microbiome) have not been considered.We assessed the year‐round NSC concentrations in the inner bark, roots and branches of Nothofagus pumilio at the treeline and 200 m below it in the southern Andes of Chile. We furthermore quantified the abundance of bark‐associated yeasts and evaluated the relative importance of inner bark's starch and soluble sugars (SS), tree size and seasonality as predictors of the yeast abundance.NSC, starch and SS concentrations decreased in springtime in both elevations. In late spring, NSC, starch and SS concentrations were significantly lower at the treeline than below the treeline for all organs and most so for the inner bark. Bark's starch and SS concentrations were the best predictors of yeast abundance. At the treeline, yeast abundance was highly predicted by the month, peaking in spring.We found support for C limitation in the treeline formation of N. pumilio in late spring, when C reserves were at their minimum concentrations and yeasts' abundance was at their maximum. Given that yeast abundance was mostly predicted by starch and SS concentrations, our results suggest that yeasts act as a C sink, particularly at the treeline.

INTRODUCTION

The upper altitudinal boundary of alpine forests, that is, alpine treelines, marks the most conspicuous transition between vegetation types in montane regions. The treeline elevation occurs globally at the isotherm of ca. 6.6 °C, indicating that treeline formation and maintenance are set by low temperature (Körner & Paulsen 2004; Hoch & Körner 2012; Körner 2012a). The dependence of treeline elevation on environmental temperature posits this vegetation boundary as a natural indicator of the effect of global climate change on high‐elevation forests (Körner 2003a), with an upward migration predicted under warmer conditions (Harsch et al. 2009). Current consensus establishes that the treeline is formed by temperature‐driven impediments to allocating photoassimilates to biomass, that is, the growth limitation hypothesis (GLH) (Körner 1998). These impediments are thought to arise because apical meristems in the tree life form are coupled to air temperatures. In contrast, by having a prostrate growth habit, short‐stature vegetation should benefit from the radiative warming from soil and be able to grow further upslope (Körner 1998). According to the GLH, low temperature exerts stronger limitations on growth (i.e., cell division, differentiation and elongation) than on carbon (C) assimilation (Körner 2012b, 2013), predicting an elevational increase in the tissue concentration of non‐structural carbohydrates (NSC) (Piper et al. 2006; Fajardo et al. 2012; Hoch & Körner 2012). Nonetheless, most evidence supporting the GLH has come from evergreen, coniferous treelines in the Northern Hemisphere (Hoch et al. 2002; Hoch & Körner 2003, 2012; Fajardo et al. 2012). Evidence has been less consistent in deciduous treeline species: the GLH was supported in a deciduous shrub species (Dolezal et al. 2019) but it was generally not supported in deciduous tree species (Handa et al. 2005; Dawes et al. 2011; Hoch & Körner 2012; Fajardo & Piper 2014, 2017; Wang et al. 2018; Han et al. 2020).

The main approach used thus far to test the GLH has been assessing NSC concentrations in the sapwood of branches, trunks and roots at a single point in the growing season, usually at the end. This approach might fail to adequately capture the elevational trends in C balance for deciduous species, as growth reactivation starts generally earlier in deciduous than in evergreen species, and hence, the period of highest reliance on C reserves to support growth occurs earlier (Fajardo & Siefert 2016; Löiez & Piper 2021). Many treelines formed by deciduous trees also occur in Mediterranean biomes (Piper et al. 2016), with summer drought advancing the end of the growing season and increasing plant metabolic dependence on NSC pools (Reyes‐Bahamonde et al. 2022). A proper assessment of the GLH in deciduous species with a wide geographical distribution should therefore evaluate NSC concentrations in springtime and account for contrasting climates. Likewise, while previous assessments have considered the sapwood as the main storage tissue of NSC, the parenchyma of the inner bark can account for even higher NSC concentrations than the sapwood (Wiley et al. 2019; Rosell et al. 2021), and its contribution to radial growth could be higher than the sapwood given its proximity to the cambium (Landhausser & Lieffers 2012). In fact, it has been shown that inner bark's C reserves make the largest contribution to the regrowth of aspen (Populus tremuloides), a deciduous species (Wiley et al. 2019). Despite the potential role of inner bark as an NSC reservoir, it has been rarely studied along elevation gradients.

Although the debate on the physiological mechanisms causing treeline formation has centred on the role of growth as the main C sink (Körner & Hoch 2023), other potentially relevant sinks have been less explored. Many plant‐associated microbes, such as mycorrhizal fungi, depend on tree C supply and can compete with plants for C under conditions of limited C availability (Sapes et al. 2021). It has been found that the relative abundance of mycorrhizal fungi in the treeline of Nothofagus pumilio is higher than below the treeline (Saona et al. 2025) and that trees of Pinus mugo and Larix decidua at the treeline allocate up to 18% of photoassimilates to mycorrhizal fungi (Hilman et al. 2024). According to these findings, symbiotic interactions might further help explain the previously reported decrease in NSC concentrations with elevation in N. pumilio (Hoch & Körner 2012; Fajardo & Piper 2017). While the influence of mycorrhizal and other rhizospheric microorganisms in tree source–sink relations is recognized in models of tree C balance (Körner 2003b; Martínez‐Vilalta et al. 2016), the influence of microorganisms inhabiting the tree phyllosphere on the tree C balance remains unknown. Yeasts are one of the most abundant microorganisms in temperate and cold forests (Starmer & Lachance 2011; França et al. 2016; Kowallik & Greig 2016). Trees of the order Fagales, represented by some iconic treeline species (e.g., Nothofagus pumilio, N. antarctica and Betula pubescens), have been found to be highly colonized by cold‐tolerant yeasts in their bark (Boynton & Greig 2014; de Garcia et al. 2014; Mozzachiodi et al. 2022; Villarreal et al. 2022). It has been proposed that the bark offers refuge to yeasts and/or acts as a source of sugars (Sampaio & Gonçalves 2008; Boynton & Greig 2014; de Garcia et al. 2014). Many cold‐tolerant yeasts, including Saccharomyces, produce ethanol in the presence of oxygen and high external glucose concentrations. Accordingly, these ethanol‐tolerant yeasts proliferate at high sugar concentrations and, therefore, are closely related to the phenology and metabolism of trees (Sampaio & Gonçalves 2008; Boynton & Greig 2014). As such, they have the potential to act as C sinks.

Nothofagus pumilio is a deciduous tree species that forms the elevational treeline along c. 20 degrees of latitude in the southern Andes (Veblen et al. 1996; Fajardo et al. 2019). The treeline of N. pumilio has been found to exhibit decreasing growth rates and decreasing NSC concentrations with elevation, indicative of C limitation (e.g., Fajardo & Piper 2017). Forests of Nothofagus pumilio host high biodiversity of cold‐tolerant yeasts across different ecosystem components and tree organs and tissues, including bark (de Garcia et al. 2014). One of these yeasts, Saccharomyces eubayanus, is a species endemic to the southern Andes exclusively colonizing N. pumilio (Libkind et al. 2011; de Garcia et al. 2014; Villarreal et al. 2022). The ecology of S. eubayanus, especially its potential function as a C sink at the elevational treeline of N. pumilio, has not yet been investigated.

In this study, our general objective was to assess the year‐round NSC concentrations and the abundance of bark‐associated yeasts in the treeline species N. pumilio in the southern Andes of Chile. Nothofagus pumilio is the single dominant treeline species of the southern Andes, covering 20 degrees of latitude, which constitutes an ideal system to test treeline formation hypotheses. Previous studies examining temperature effects on C reserves on the N. pumilio treeline have clearly documented decreasing growth rates with elevation, but have been inconclusive about the elevational trends in NSC concentrations (Fajardo et al. 2011; Hoch & Körner 2012; Fajardo & Piper 2014, 2017; Piper et al. 2016). Our specific objectives were to (i) test the GLH for treeline formation by considering seasonality and (ii) determine the relationship between C reserves and the abundance of bark‐associated yeasts, particularly Saccharomyces eubayanus. We hypothesized that the abundance of bark‐associated yeasts depends on the inner bark NSC concentration (starch and sugars). An alternative is that the bark offers refuge from abiotic stress to yeasts. If so, yeasts will be more associated with tree size and elevation (Fig. 1). We also characterized the potential of the inner bark as a C store by comparing the seasonality and magnitude of their NSC (starch and sugars) concentrations with those of roots and branches. Finally, using DNA sequencing, barcoding and PCR amplification, we identified S. eubayanus.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study sites and sample collection

Nothofagus pumilio (Poepp. et Endl.) Krasser (Nothofagaceae) is a deciduous, broadleaf, light‐demanding tree species whose distribution extends through a wide latitudinal and elevational range in the southern Andes of Chile and Argentina. This study was conducted in southern Chile, where N. pumilio forms the treeline from c. 36 to 54° SL (Veblen et al. 1996; Fajardo et al. 2019). We selected four geographical locations (sites) across disparate climate treelines spanning 10 degrees of latitude from a Mediterranean‐like treeline in central Chile (36° SL) to a sub‐Antarctic treeline in Patagonia (46° SL). At each site, we conducted year‐round sampling of tree tissues and yeasts (Fig. 1, Table 1, Table S1) at two elevations: one at treeline elevation (TL) and the other 200 meters below the treeline (BTL). We defined the treeline as the uppermost limit of a group of individual trees with an upright growth form of at least 3 m (Körner 2003a).

At each elevation, 5 mature trees, separated by at least ca. 5 m, were identified and tagged with a metal plate. Sampling for NSC and yeast determination was performed from each tree. Although we did not measure growth, we have previously found decreasing elevational trends in the basal area increment of N. pumilio at our study locations and elsewhere (Fajardo et al. 2011, 2013; Fajardo & Piper 2017). Every tree's diameter was measured at breast height (DBH, 1.35 m) using a diameter tape. For yeast isolation, we aseptically collected one small bark tissue sample (ca. 2 cm2) at 1.5 m above ground using a chisel and a rubber mallet, which was transferred immediately into a labelled sterile empty tube (15 mL) and placed in a cooler with ice packs for transportation. We also collected another small piece of bark tissue, a branch segment and a root segment for NSC determination. We identified and cut one terminal 2‐year‐old branch segment with pruning shears. We dug and identified the root of the target tree and selected and cut a root piece of ~1 cm diameter (Fig. 1). In the field, we removed the phloem and bark (periderm) from branches and roots with a knife. These samples were placed in labelled paper bags that were then stored in the cooler. On the same day, NSC samples were subjected to 3 30 s cycles of microwave to stop enzymatic activity, according to a standardized protocol (Popp et al. 1996). Finally, we stored all samples at 4 °C in a laboratory refrigerator until analysis. Sampling was carried out across the four seasons of the year (Table S2), including two collections in spring to increase the temporal resolution during the period of the most drastic changes in NSC concentrations in deciduous tree species (Landhäusser 2011; Löiez & Piper 2021).

Yeasts determination

We isolated the ethanol‐tolerant yeasts, extracted and sequenced their DNA and identified S. eubayanus following a previously described procedure documented in Nespolo et al. (2020). Briefly, ethanol‐tolerant yeasts were isolated using 5 mL of enrichment medium (0.67% yeast nitrogen base, YNB, 1% glucose, 8% ethanol) and incubated for 20 min at 20 °C with agitation (Sampaio & Gonçalves 2008). Aliquots (200 μL) of undiluted and diluted samples (1:2, 1:4) were plated on YNB 2% glucose media supplemented with 8% ethanol and chloramphenicol (1 ng/mL). In addition, we included ethanol‐free control plates, which were incubated for 10 days at 12 °C. Colony‐forming units (CFUs) were counted per gram of bark, and five morphologically representative colonies per sample were selected for DNA extraction and sequencing. Isolates were stored at −80 °C in 20% glycerol. For metabarcoding identification of the isolated colonies, pools were formed by site, altitude and season (40 in total), and DNA was extracted using the Quick‐DNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research), quantified via Qubit™ 4 Fluorometer (cat. no. Q33226) and quality‐checked by gel electrophoresis. The ITS2 region was amplified using primers ITS3/ITS4 (White et al. 1990) with Illumina adapters, and libraries were indexed, pooled equimolarly, quantified, analysed by TapeStation 2200 and sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq (300 bp paired‐end reads). Data were processed in Qiime2 (Bolyen et al. 2019) and the R package (v4.2.3) using DADA2 (Callahan et al. 2016), trimming sequences (Q > 30) and identifying ASVs with the otu_table function (phyloseq). Taxonomy was assigned via BLASTn v2.16.0 (Camacho et al. 2009) against the ITS_eukaryote_sequences database (NCBI Resource Coordinators 2018) using megablast. For visualizations, we used ggplot2 (Wickham 2016). For seasons when Saccharomyces colonies were identified (autumn, early spring, summer), 1,428 isolates were analysed by ITS1/ITS4 PCR and compared to a S. eubayanus control strain (CBS12357; Libkind et al. 2011). Species‐specific PCRs (Pengelly & Wheals 2013) confirmed species using controls for S. cerevisiae, S. uvarum (Nespolo et al. 2020) and S. eubayanus. The relative abundance of S. eubayanus was calculated and applied to CFU data (Table S3).

NSC analyses

Tissue samples for NSC analysis were first dried in an air‐forced stove (Memmert GmbH, Schwabach, Germany) at 70 °C for 72 h. Then, samples were ground into a fine powder in a ball mill (MM 200, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) and stored under cool, dry and dark conditions until chemical analyses were conducted. We determined NSC concentrations for each tissue separately as the sum of the quantitatively low‐molecular‐weight carbohydrates (i.e., glucose, fructose and sucrose; hereafter SS) and starch (Hoch et al. 2002). SS were extracted with distilled water at 100 °C for 60 min and analysed photometrically at 340 nm on a 96‐well multiplate reader after enzymatic conversion (invertase and phosphoglucose isomerase from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Sigma‐Aldrich I4504 and P5381, respectively, St Louis, MO, USA) of sucrose and fructose to glucose. We photometrically determined the glucose concentration after enzymatic conversion of glucose to gluconate‐6‐phosphate (Glucose Assay Reagent, G3293, Sigma‐Aldrich). Following the degradation of starch to glucose, using a purified fungal amylase (‘amyloglucosidase’ from Aspergillus niger, Sigma‐Aldrich 10115) at 45 °C overnight, starch was determined in a separate analysis by the same procedure. We finally calculated starch concentration as NSC minus SS and expressed it as glucose equivalents. We present concentrations on a percent‐of‐dry‐matter basis (% d.m.). NSC analyses were conducted at the Institute of Biological Sciences in Universidad de Talca (Talca, Chile).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025). A P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. First, to evaluate the effect of seasonality on NSC, starch and SS concentrations in inner bark, branch and root of N. pumilio at the two elevations (treeline and below treeline), we fit generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) using the gamm() function from the RMGCV package (Wood 2010). Response variables were transformed (square root or log) when necessary to approximate normality of the residuals. In each model, seasonality was described by a cyclical spline of month (s(Month, bs = ‘cr’, k = 6)), a random intercept per site was included, and correlation between observations from the same site was modelled with a compound symmetry structure (corCompSymm). The optimal specification of each GAMM was selected by comparing AICc values, and the validity of the assumptions was verified using basis dimension checks with the mgcv's::gam.check() function in the RMUMIN package (Bartoń 2025).

Second, we fitted generalized linear mixed‐effects models (GLMMs) using the R package nlme (Pinheiro et al. 2021) to test whether elevation has a significant effect on NSC (starch and SS) concentration on each date. In the model, elevation was the fixed effect, and location (site) and tree were the random effects. We fitted a separate model for each tissue (inner bark, branch and root sapwood). We fitted GLMMs with a Gaussian family and an identity link function.

We used boosting regression trees (BRT) to predict the abundance of etanol‐tolerant yeasts and of S. eubayanus. We preferred to work with BRT for two reasons: (1) Instead of GLMM, which delivers good correlations about the phenomenon (GLMM searches for a linear response to the biological phenomenon), machine‐learning techniques, in particular BRT, have proved to be an excellent supervised approach for ecological process predictions (e.g., Elith et al. 2008; James et al. 2013; Fajardo et al. 2022) and (2) BRT is a predicting method that delivers variable correlations that may be threshold dependent.

The BRTs included the following explanatory variables: seasonality (sampling month), DBH, elevation (treeline, below treeline), location (site) and starch and SS concentrations. In the BRT models, the shrinkage parameter was 0.001 to reach at least 1000 trees with a slower learning rate; the tree complexity was 4, and we used 75% of the data for fitting and 25% for prediction. We used a Gaussian family of relationships, with 10‐fold cross‐validation. The interaction between predictors was identified following the methodology of Elith et al. (2008). We evaluated the model efficiency by calculating the percentage of explained deviance (ED)—defined as (mean total deviance–mean residual deviance)/mean total deviance—and by using cross‐validation correlation coefficients (Kärnä et al. 2019). We also ran BRT models separately for each elevation (treeline and below treeline) to examine whether the relative importance of starch and SS as predictors of yeast abundance differed between elevations.

RESULTS

Seasonal variation of C reserves

Seasonal variation in NSC, starch and SS concentrations of inner bark, branch and root was highly significant in both elevations, treeline (TL) and below treeline (BTL). In general, seasonal variation of NSC (starch and SS) in all tissues showed a pronounced decrease in spring and a subsequent increase in summer, which remained high until winter (Table 2, Fig. 2). The seasonal effect, though, was more variable at BTL than at TL for NSC concentrations in the branch (Table 2, Fig. 2) and at TL than at BTL for NSC in the root and for starch concentrations in the bark (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Elevation variation of C reserves

In late spring, the inner bark's NSC, starch and SS concentrations were significantly lower at the TL than at the BTL (Table 3, Fig. 3). Also, inner bark's NSC concentrations were significantly lower at TL than at BTL in autumn. Inner bark's SS concentrations were significantly lower at TL than at BTL in summer 1, autumn and winter. Conversely, starch concentrations in this tissue were significantly higher at TL than BTL in summer (1 & 2) and winter. For the branch and the root, we did not observe any significant difference between elevations for any of the sampling dates, except for the branch's SS concentrations in early spring, where concentrations were significantly higher at the TL than at the BTL (Table 3, Fig. 3).

Abundance of ethanol‐tolerant yeasts and C reserves

In the BRT model, we found that starch and SS concentrations were the most important predictors of yeast abundance (ED = 34.5%; 26.3% and 24% of relative influence for starch and SS, respectively, Fig. 4A). The association between yeast abundance and starch and SS was non‐linear. Yeast abundance increased from ca. 0.25% up to 1% of starch concentrations and from ca. 0.75% up to 1.25% of SS concentrations; it decreased between 1% and 1.8% of starch concentrations and between 1.25% and 1.5% of SS concentrations, and then increased slightly and stabilized from 2% of starch and SS concentrations onwards (Fig. 4A). These trends were different between elevations; BRT models were more robust at treeline (ED = 67.4%) than below treeline (ED = 10.3%), with SS having a higher relative importance at treeline (28.8%) and starch having a higher relative importance below treeline (26%) (Fig. S1). Also, the month had 22% of relative influence in the yeast abundance at treeline (with a peak in spring) but had a very minor relative importance below treeline (c. 4%, Fig. S1). Saccharomyces eubayanus was a major component of the isolated and cultivable yeast community, particularly at the treeline (Fig. S2). We found that S. eubayanus's abundance was mostly predicted by the bark concentrations of SS and the DBH (31.5% and 21.8% of the relative influence, respectively) (ED = 65.8%) (Fig. 4B). S. eubayanus's abundance increased from ca. 1% up to 1.35% of SS concentration, decreased steeply afterward and stabilized at 1.5% (Fig. 4B).

DISCUSSION

Partial support for the GLH in Nothofagus pumilio

In this study, we found mixed support for the growth limitation hypothesis (GLH). In general, NSC concentrations were not higher at the treeline (TL) than at the lower elevation (BTL), except for a significantly higher branch sugar concentration at treeline in early spring and significantly higher inner bark starch concentrations at treeline in summer and winter. In agreement with the GLH, starch concentrations in the inner bark and sugar concentrations in the branch were significantly higher at the TL than at the BTL (Körner 1998; Hoch & Körner 2003). However, SS concentrations in the inner bark were lower at the TL than at the BTL elevation for most sampling dates, including the autumn and the late spring, when inner bark's NSC concentrations were also lower at TL. Given that the tree growth of N. pumilio decreases with elevation across the southern Andes (Fajardo et al. 2013, 2019; Fajardo & Piper 2017), these results are consistent with C limitation increasing with elevation (Stevens & Fox 1991). Our results show that, in contrast to most treelines formed by evergreen conifers (Hoch & Körner 2012), reduced growth at the treeline of N. pumilio is associated with an NSC and sugar shortage in the inner bark. This result is consistent with previous findings in N. pumilio (Fajardo & Piper 2017) and in another winter deciduous species of the order Fagales: Betula ermanii (Wang et al. 2018; Han et al. 2020).

The organ‐dependent disparity in elevational trends of NSC, starch and sugar concentrations indicates that distinct C balances operate across organs. In plants, the root is a major storage organ, particularly in angiosperms (Piper et al. 2019; Wiley et al. 2019; Landhäusser & Adams 2024). Due to their distance from the autotrophic tree fraction (i.e., leaves), the root has been hypothesized as the first organ to reflect long‐term C shortage or surplus (Landhausser & Lieffers 2012). However, in the root, we found similar NSC, starch and sugar concentrations between elevations. Higher C efflux to mycorrhizal symbionts at the treeline could rule out elevational trends expected from the GLH. The abundance of ectomycorrhizal fungi relative to the whole soil fungal microbiota is higher at the treeline of N. pumilio than below it (Saona et al. 2025). Ectomycorrhizas increase the nitrogen (N) access for trees at low total soil N (Hua et al. 2025), which characterizes treelines, including the treeline of N. pumilio (Fajardo & Piper 2017; Mayor et al. 2017). In Larix decidua, the dependence on mycorrhizal for N supply was higher at the elevation where temperatures were lowest (Hilman et al. 2024), which in our case corresponds to treeline elevation. Deciduous treeline species could be particularly more dependent on mycorrhizas when compared to evergreen conifer treelines due to their higher nutrient demands (Fajardo & Piper 2017). On the other hand, the rather higher concentrations of sugars in the branch at treeline could reflect a local effect of temperature decoupling between the air and the apical meristems. Although decoupling has been posited as the main cause of sink limitations at treeline, it occurs locally, affecting meristems that are most exposed to the air above c. 2–3 m from the ground (Grace et al. 2002). By contrast, the root and the lower part of the trunk, where we sampled inner bark, are relatively more coupled with soil temperatures than with air temperatures and therefore their meristematic activities can be presumed to be less limited by temperature (than that of branches). The fact that the elevational increase in the branch's sugar concentrations was not accompanied by an increase in NSC suggests a role in osmoprotection (Koster & Lynch 1992), supporting the idea that low temperatures have a greater impact on the branch than on other organs and tissues that are closer to the ground. Overall, our findings call for a re‐evaluation of which organs we should consider the best indicators of C balance, suggesting that the trunk is a more informative organ of the whole‐tree. However, as modular organisms, trees probably compartmentalize their C reserves as much as their meristematic activity.

Yeasts at the treeline, a potential new C sink

Our results suggest that bark‐associated yeasts may represent a tree C carbon sink. We show that yeasts inhabiting the bark of N. pumilio, which proliferate at high sugar concentrations (Boynton & Greig 2014), were strongly associated with the tree's carbohydrates, particularly at the treeline elevation. BRT models indicated that the inner bark's sugar and starch concentrations significantly explained the abundance of these yeasts and more so at the treeline (i.e., BRT for the treeline was more robust than the BRT for below treeline). Whether yeasts, particularly at the treeline, use carbohydrates that otherwise could be used for tree growth remains uncertain. Sugars are fundamental inputs of xylogenesis that confer the turgor required to promote cell division and expansion, while starch is a source of C during cell wall thickening (Deslauriers et al. 2014; Buttò et al. 2025). Notably, concentrations of NSC (and starch and sugars) reached their minimums at the treeline in spring, when the yeast abundance peaked, and when xylogenesis of temperate trees is known to rise (Buttò et al. 2025). Potentially, the competition between xylogenesis and yeast proliferation for C could account for the lack of support for the GLH found in this study and others in treeline deciduous species of the order Fagales (Fajardo & Piper 2017; Wang et al. 2018), which are known to be highly colonized by cold‐tolerant yeasts in their bark (Boynton & Greig 2014; Mozzachiodi et al. 2022). Although our results are consistent with the possibility that bark‐associated yeasts may act as a C sink, further quantitative and experimental approaches are required to confirm such a role and assess whether this represents a meaningful limitation to tree growth. Among the limitations that impede us from drawing definitive conclusions, the estimation of yeast abundance is an important one. This is because the fraction of bark‐associated yeasts that can be effectively isolated by methods based on CFUs is uncertain (Boynton & Greig 2014). In the same line, our results are potentially biased toward ethanol‐tolerant taxa and, therefore, the contribution of the total microbial community remains unclear. It is also unknown whether the yeasts quantified in our assessment directly feed from sugars of the tree, for example, as parasitic or symbiotic, or if they rather inhabit the bark as saprophytic organisms (Starmer & Lachance 2011; Christita et al. 2022). In the first case, it would be critical to determine how much C of the trees is consumed by the yeasts. The cost of maintaining ectomycorrhizas has been estimated at 15%–28% of net carbon fixation (Finlay 2008). Given the faster and more versatile carbon metabolism of yeasts when compared to mycorrhizas (Li et al. 2021; Verhagen et al. 2025), their C demand could be higher.

The effects of SS concentration on S. eubayanus and the whole yeast assemblage detected by the CFUs method were not linear. The abundance of S. eubayanus increased significantly when SS concentrations increased from 1% to 1.35% and decreased at higher SS concentrations, when the abundance of other yeast species increased. This suggests that the abundance of S. eubayanus is sugar‐limited at low SS concentrations but becomes limited by other factors at higher sugar concentrations. One plausible explanation is that other yeast species are disproportionately favoured at higher sugar concentrations, eventually outcompeting S. eubayanus (Postma et al. 1989). The narrow range of sugar concentrations where S. eubayanus exhibits the highest abundance, along with the high significance of sugar concentrations as a predictor of the S. eubayanus abundance, suggests a subtle tuning between the tree C balance and the yeast proliferation, where small variations in sugar concentrations strongly affect the yeast community composition. As the tree's sugar and starch concentrations depend on environmental factors such as temperature and soil nutrient availability (Körner 2003b), changes in these conditions may critically affect the tree microbiome.

CONCLUSION

Along the elevational and latitudinal gradients in the southern Andes of Chile, over a wide temporal range, we show mixed support for the GLH in N. pumilio. While elevational increases in NSC concentration were not found, the inner bark, a major C source during cambial activity (Deslauriers et al. 2014; Buttò et al. 2025), undergoes NSC and sugar shortage with increasing elevation. The association between yeast abundance and inner bark carbohydrate concentrations suggests that yeast proliferation is linked to host C availability and that these organisms might act as a C sink in the treeline of N. pumilio. If so, the elevational decreases in NSC concentrations in the inner bark could be driven by competition for C between tree growth and bark‐associated yeast proliferation. A precise quantification of the C consumed by these microorganisms is required to determine the potential role of yeasts as C sinks.
