Factors associated with family caregiver stress related to behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia: A stress process model-based path analysis
Authors: Eunkyo Kim, Minhee Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFamily caregivers of people living with dementia (PLWD) may experience mental and physical health issues. The purpose of this study was to examine factors associated with family caregiver outcomes and to describe their interrelationships.MethodsGrounded in the stress process model, path analysis was conducted using secondary data from 178 dyads of community-dwelling PLWD and family caregivers in Korea. Caregiver outcome was assessed through caregivers’ health status and stress due to behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD). Agitation and depression were specified as primary stressors, and PLWD factors were included as background factors associated with BPSD, with separate symptom models.ResultsAgitation and depression showed the strongest associations with family caregiver stress related to BPSD. Agitation explained 45.1% of caregiver stress related to BPSD variance (β=.66, p<.001), while depression explained 41.5% (β=.63, p<.001). Agitation and depression were related to caregiver health status (β=.17, p=.006; β=.18, p=.017). In both agitation and depression models, female caregivers (β=.19, p=.008, in both models), older age (β=.21, p=.007, in both models), and lower economic status (β=.32, p<.001, in both models) were associated with poorer caregiver health status. Dementia severity and patient age showed indirect associations with caregiver stress related to BPSD through agitation and depression.ConclusionAgitation and depression were associated with caregiver stress related to BPSD, while caregiver characteristics were associated with health status. These findings highlight the need for tailored interventions to address BPSD and support caregivers. Family-centered and system-level approaches are essential for improving outcomes for PLWD and their families.Keywords: Dementia, Caregivers, Behavioral symptoms, Psychomotor agitation, Depression

INTRODUCTION

Globally, 57 million people were diagnosed with dementia in 2021 [1], and the number is estimated to increase to 152.8 million by 2050 [2]. Dementia is a progressive disease that leads to significant cognitive decline, behavioral disturbances, social dysfunction, and a gradual loss of independent function and increased reliance on caregivers [1]. Its increasing prevalence has made the resulting stress and pressure on individuals, families, and society a significant concern [1,3].

Due to the nature of the disease, caregivers need to provide comprehensive support to people living with dementia (PLWD), encompassing physical care, emotional support, management of behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), and supervision to ensure safety and well-being [4]. BPSD, including delusion, hallucination, agitation, anxiety, depression, and disruptive behavior, comprise broad symptoms observed in approximately 90.0% of PLWD [5]. Of these, agitation and depression are prevalent [6-8]. As dementia progresses, these symptoms require increasing levels of supervision and support.

Informal caregivers of PLWD, such as family members, experience significantly higher levels of stress, depression, and anxiety and poorer physical health compared to those of individuals with other chronic illnesses or cancer [9,10]. Caregiving for PLWD is often time-intensive, requiring long hours of daily supervision and support. However, the complexity and unpredictability of BPSD management are notably associated with caregiver stress [7]. Unlike caregivers of individuals with other chronic conditions, those of PLWD must navigate unique behavioral and psychological challenges that demand specialized attention and targeted support.

Caregivers experience physical, psychological, emotional, social, and economic consequences of their caregiving role [11]. The accumulation of caregiving stress is associated with negative caregiver outcomes, including poorer physical and mental health [12]. Sörensen et al. [13] proposed a modified stress process model that reflects the specific characteristics of dementia caregiving and incorporates both PLWD- and caregiver-related factors.

Prior research has identified several PLWD-related factors of caregiver outcomes, including the presence of BPSD, functional decline, disease progression, total duration of dementia, depression, activities of daily living (ADL), instrumental ADL, and use of psychotropic and antidepressant medications [14-17]. Caregiver-related factors include caregiving time, sex, education level, income, age, employment status, relationship to the PLWD, individual personality, perceived stress level, self-efficacy, and coping abilities [15,18-20].

Nevertheless, examining these factors individually may not fully capture the complexity of caregiver outcomes. Therefore, the present study applied a stress process model–informed path analysis to examine associations among PLWD-related characteristics, agitation and depression, and caregiver outcomes. Using cross-sectional secondary data from Korean community-dwelling dementia dyads, we estimated separate symptom-specific models (agitation and depression). These models examined direct associations between BPSD symptoms and caregiver outcomes, as well as statistical indirect associations linking PLWD characteristics to caregiver outcomes through agitation or depression. In addition, associations between caregiver sociodemographic characteristics and caregiver outcomes were examined.

METHODS

Ethic statement: This study was conducted using secondary data; therefore, it was exempted from review by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the principal investigator’s affiliated institution (IRB no. 4-2024-0451).

1. Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This was a descriptive correlational study that utilized secondary data to construct a hypothetical path model, investigating factors associated with family caregiver outcomes based on PLWD and caregiver characteristics. This study was described in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies (https://www.strobe-statement.org).

The participants were 186 dyads of PLWD and their family caregivers in community settings. The data were collected from 2018 to 2020 for the project “Development and Evaluation of a Predictive Model for Behavioral and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia using Machine Learning.” After excluding eight pairs with missing Korean version of the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (K-NPI) data, 178 pairs were included. Among the remaining cases, no missing data were observed in the other key variables (Korean version of the Mini-Mental State Examination [K-MMSE], Clinical Dementia Rating [CDR], Korean version of the ADL [K-ADL], Korean version of the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory [CMAI], Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia [CSDD], caregiver characteristics, and outcome variables); consequently, analyses were conducted using the 178 completed cases. The sample size should be at least five times the number of estimated parameters in the path analysis [21]. Given a total sample size of 178, applying the recommended 5:1 sample-to-parameter ratio allows estimation of up to 35 parameters in the present analysis.

2. Theoretical Framework

According to Sörensen’s model [13], primary stressors (e.g., PLWD-related characteristics and caregiving situations) affect secondary stressors, which, in turn, influence caregiver outcomes through an appraisal process. Additionally, moderating, background, and contextual factors of caregivers influence each stage of the process.

A hypothetical path model was constructed to examine factors associated with caregiver outcomes, including primary stressors and background and contextual factors. Secondary stressors, appraisals, and moderating factors were excluded due to limitations in the original dataset. Sörensen’s model treats all PLWD characteristics (i.e., cognitive impairment, ADL, and BPSD) as primary stressors. However, empirical evidence suggests that disease-related and background factors in PLWD, such as cognitive function, ADL, age, and sex, are associated with BPSD [22,23]. Therefore, the present study repositioned BPSD (specifically agitation and depression) as the primary stressor in caregiver outcomes. Subsequently, cognitive function, disease severity, ADL, and general characteristics of PLWD were considered as background factors associated with BPSD. BPSD was hypothesized to be associated with family caregivers’ health and stress. The exogenous variables representing caregiver characteristics included caregiving years and hours, and caregiver age, sex, and economic status that were modeled with direct paths to caregiver outcomes (Figure 1).

While the original Stress Process Model proposed by Pearlin et al. [12] conceptualized caregiver background characteristics as influencing outcomes primarily through stressors, subsequent theoretical extensions have suggested that these background factors may also exert direct effects on caregiver stress and health outcomes. Sörensen and colleagues [13], in particular, emphasized that caregiver sociodemographic characteristics function not only as distal contextual factors but also as independent correlates of caregiver well-being. Empirical evidence has consistently demonstrated that caregiver characteristics such as sex, age, and socioeconomic status are directly associated with stress and health outcomes, independent of care-recipient stressors [11,15,17]. Consistent with these theoretical extensions and empirical findings, the present model specified direct paths from caregiver background factors to caregiver outcomes.

3. Variables and Measurement

Trained research assistants with graduate-level nursing education collected data. General characteristics, ADL, and variables related to BPSD were self-reported by the primary family caregiver who provided most of the care.

1) Cognitive Function

Cognitive function was assessed by research assistants using the K-MMSE. All assessors followed standardized administration procedures as outlined in the official K-MMSE manual [24]. The K-MMSE was purchased with an appropriate license for the original study. The scale comprises 30 items, scored from 0 to 30, with lower scores indicating poorer cognitive function. A score below 24 suggests possible cognitive impairment.

2) Dementia Severity

Dementia severity was assessed using the Expanded CDR. For participants recruited from neurology outpatient clinics, CDR scores were extracted from electronic medical records with institutional approval and informed consent. For participants recruited from community centers, CDR assessments were conducted by trained research assistants following standardized procedures. The CDR evaluates six domains (memory, orientation, judgment/problem-solving, community affairs, home/hobbies, and personal care). Total scores are interpreted as follows: 0.5=questionable; 1=mild; 2=moderate; 3=severe; 4=profound; and 5=terminal. Inter-rater reliability (Kappa) was .90~.96 [25].

3) Activities of Daily Living

ADL was assessed using the K-ADL, which comprises seven items rated on a 3-point scale (1=independence, 3=dependence). Total scores range from 7 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater functional impairment. The Korean version had a Cronbach’s α of .94 [26].

4) Depression

Depression was assessed using the Korean version of the CSDD, comprising 19 items across five categories [27]. Caregivers rated the PLWD’s depressive behaviors over the past week on a 0~2 scale (0=absent, 1=mild, 2=severe), yielding a total possible score of 0~38, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptom severity. The Korean version demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=.78) [27].

5) Agitation

Agitation was evaluated using the CMAI-K, comprising 29 items across four sub-domains (physically aggressive, physically non-aggressive, verbally agitated, and hiding objects). Each item is rated on a 7-point scale, yielding a total score of 29~203, with higher scores indicating more severe agitation. The Korean version had a Cronbach’s α of .88 [28].

6) Caregiving Years and Daily Hours

Caregiving years refer to the total period during which the caregiver has lived with and provided care for the PLWD. Daily caregiving hours refer to the time the caregiver spends caring in 24 hours. Both variables were assessed via a self-report questionnaire.

7) Caregivers’ Economic Status

Caregivers’ economic status was assessed using a self-reported, single-item, 5-point scale (1=very good, 5=very poor), with higher scores indicating worse economic status.

8) Caregiver Outcomes

Caregiver outcomes were assessed using two tools. First, subjective health status was measured using a single item on a 5-point scale (1=very healthy, 5=very unhealthy), with higher scores indicating poorer health. Second, caregiver stress related to BPSD was evaluated using the K-NPI, which rates stress for each symptom from 0 (no stress) to 5 (very severe stress), yielding a total score of 0~60; higher scores indicate greater stress. The Korean version had a Cronbach’s α of .85 [29].

4. Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using JASP 0.19.3.0 (JASP Team). The general characteristics of the dyads were analyzed through descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations. Given the sample size (n=178) and the number of parameters to be estimated, separate path models were conducted to maintain the recommended sample-to-parameter ratio of 5:1; Model 1 examined pathways through agitation and Model 2 through depression [21].

Based on the hypothetical model, path coefficients were estimated using the weighted least squares means and variance adjusted (WLSMV) method, given the presence of ordered categorical variables. CDR, caregivers’ subjective health status, and economic status were treated as ordered categorical variables. Statistical significance of direct and indirect effects was determined using robust standard errors with 95% confidence intervals in the WLSMV estimator. The model fit was assessed using the following criteria: χ2/degree of freedom (df) ratio (<3); comparative fit index (CFI, ≥.90); goodness-of-fit index (GFI, ≥.90); Tucker-Lewis index (TLI, ≥.95); root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA, ≤.08); and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR, ≤.10) [30,31].

5. Measurement Considerations

Although the K-NPI comprehensively assesses BPSD, separate instruments were used to measure agitation (CMAI) and depression (CSDD) to minimize measurement dependency. This methodological decision was intended to reduce measurement dependency between predictor and outcome variables in the path models. Moreover, because K-NPI caregiver stress scores are rated only when corresponding symptoms are present, including both BPSD and stress scores in the same model could have introduced structural correlations and biased the estimation of associations.

RESULTS

1. General and Disease-Related Characteristics

The mean age of the PLWD was 84.34±5.67 years, with the majority being male (60.1%). The mean age of their family caregivers was 66.30±14.08 years, with the majority being female (77.5%), primarily spouses (42.1%) and daughters (36.0%).

The PLWD had moderate cognitive impairment with mean cognitive function (MMSE) scores of 17.43±5.09 and dementia severity scores of 1.56±0.91. Depression (CSDD) averaged 9.02±5.86, and agitation (CMAI), 41.70±14.39. Family caregivers provided care for an average of 4.18±6.94 years, spending 13.67±8.86 hours per day on caregiving activities. Caregivers’ subjective health status score was 2.79±0.99, and their stress related to BPSD was 14.85±12.01; the majority (70.8%) identified their economic status as middle income (Table 1).

The agitation model demonstrated good fit with χ2/df=1.34, GFI=.96, SRMR=.01, RMSEA=.04, TLI=.99, and CFI=.91. The depression model showed χ2/df=1.64, GFI=.95, SRMR=.02, RMSEA=.06, TLI=.97, and CFI=.83. While most indices in this model indicated acceptable fit, the CFI of the depression model was below the recommended threshold. Supplementary Table 1 and 2 present the direct and indirect effects for each path analysis model. To further examine potential sources of model misfit, modification indices were inspected. The maximum modification index was 5.26, with a standardized expected parameter change of 0.11 for the corresponding path, suggesting that freeing additional parameters would not substantially improve model fit.

(1) Direct effects

The path from dementia severity to agitation was significant and positive (β=.26, p=.005), while the path from PLWD age to agitation was significant and negative (β=-.28, p=.001).

Of the caregiver characteristics, age (β=.21, p=.007), sex (β=.19, p=.008), and economic status (β=.32, p<.001) were significantly associated with caregiver health. (2) Indirect effects

Dementia severity and PLWD age were indirectly associated with caregiver stress related to BPSD (β=.17, p<.01 and β=-.18, p<.001, respectively) via agitation.

(3) Model explanation

The agitation model explained 14.5% of the variance in agitation (R2=.15), 26.4% in caregiver health (R2=.26), and 45.1% in caregiver stress related to BPSD (R2=.45).

3) Depression Model

(1) Direct effects

Cognitive function (β=.20, p=.010) and dementia severity (β=.28, p=.002) were positively associated with depression, while PLWD age was negatively associated with depression (β=-.17, p=.031).

Regarding caregiver outcomes, depression was significantly associated with health status (β=.18, p=.017). Of the caregiver characteristics, age (β=.21, p=.007), sex (β=.19, p=.008), and economic status (β=.32, p<.001) were significantly associated with health. Depression was significantly and positively associated with caregiver stress related to BPSD (β=.63, p<.001) (Table 3).

(2) Indirect effects

Significant indirect associations were identified through the depression pathway. Cognitive function, dementia severity, and age of the PLWD were indirectly associated with caregiver stress related to BPSD (β=.13, p<.05; β=.18, p<.01; and β=-.11, p<.05, respectively) via depression.

(3) Model explanation

The depression model explained 10.7% of the variance in depression (R2=.11); 26.5%, caregiver health (R2=.27); and 41.5%, caregiver stress related to BPSD (R2=.42).

DISCUSSION

Previous studies have identified factors associated with caregiver outcomes; however, they have not sufficiently examined how these factors interact to shape those outcomes. The present study examined statistical associations among PLWD characteristics, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and caregiver outcomes using path analysis grounded in the stress process model. The findings suggest that dementia severity and PLWD age were associated with caregiver outcomes, with agitation and depression incorporated within the path structure. In addition, caregivers’ sociodemographic characteristics were associated with subjective health status. The directionality specified in the path models was theory-driven; therefore, all findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal relationships, and indirect effects should be understood as statistical indirect associations rather than causal mediation.

Most BPSD are recognized as factors associated with caregiver stress [5]. Among these, depression and agitation in PLWD have consistently been identified as primary correlates of caregiver stress [7,32,33]. The distinct clinical characteristics of these symptoms may explain their differential associations with caregiver stress related to BPSD. Physically aggressive agitation has been associated with greater feelings of embarrassment and frustration among caregivers, whereas non-aggressive physical agitation has been linked to an elevated sense of guilt and uncertainty in caregiving [34]. In addition to these emotional responses, family caregivers managing agitated behavior in PLWD have reported higher levels of psychological stress, as well as difficulties in occupational functioning and daily activity performance [6].

In contrast, depression in PLWD may manifest physically or verbally, potentially creating barriers to effective emotional communication and being associated with emotional burden among caregivers [35]. Depressive symptoms in PLWD have been linked to elevated caregiver stress, particularly in the mild stage of dementia, when cognitive function is relatively preserved and affective symptoms may be more readily expressed and observed [7]. This may explain the positive association between MMSE scores and depression observed in our path model, as higher cognitive function may facilitate the recognition and reporting of depressive behaviors. Furthermore, depression in PLWD has been associated with greater functional impairment, which may relate to increased complexity and demands of caregiving and correspondingly higher caregiver stress related to BPSD [36]. Given its affective and functional clinical characteristics, depression was associated with caregiver outcomes, underscoring the importance of targeting specific BPSD in interventions for both PLWD and caregivers.

Regarding model fit, the agitation model demonstrated good fit across all indices. However, the depression model showed a CFI of .83, which fell below the conventional threshold of .90 adopted in this study. The CFI is an incremental fit index that evaluates the relative improvement of the proposed model over a baseline (independence) model in which all observed variables are assumed to be uncorrelated. When correlations among focal constructs and antecedent variables are modest, the chi-square value of the baseline model may be insufficiently large, thereby limiting the magnitude of improvement and attenuating the CFI [37].

In the present study, depressive symptoms were strongly associated with caregiver stress but exhibited comparatively weaker or non-significant associations with several other variables. This pattern may have constrained the incremental improvement over the baseline model and contributed to the relatively lower CFI. Additional diagnostic analyses—including examination of modification indices, inspection of potential nonlinear relationships, and assessment of measurement reliability—did not indicate substantial structural misspecification. Taken together, although the depression model is unlikely to reflect major structural misspecification, its findings should be interpreted with caution, given the reduced incremental fit, and conclusions related to depression outcomes should not be overgeneralized.

The stress process model considers caregiving hours and years as primary stressors. In the present study, the mean caregiving hours were relatively higher than those reported in previous studies [38]. However, neither caregiving duration nor daily caregiving time was associated with family caregivers’ subjective health status or stress related to BPSD. This finding is consistent with Lindt et al. [39], who reported that caregiver stress cannot be fully explained by caregiving hours or years but shows stronger associations with the nature and intensity of caregiving responsibilities. This underscores the need for comprehensive, individualized interventions.

The subtype composition of the present sample warrants consideration when interpreting these findings. Lewy body dementia accounted for 55.1% of the sample and is often characterized by prominent neuropsychiatric symptoms and sleep-related problems, which may be associated with higher caregiver stress [40]. As suggested by Huang et al. [40], trajectories of caregiver stress may vary by dementia subtype. Therefore, the high proportion of Lewy body dementia in this sample may have strengthened the observed associations between BPSD and caregiver outcomes, and these findings should be interpreted with caution.

As highlighted by Hughes et al. [41], family caregivers of PLWD often report multiple unmet needs, including limited access to community resources and insufficient dementia-related education, emotional and respite support, and counseling and specialty care. In particular, a lack of knowledge about dementia may leave caregivers underprepared, highlighting the need for training that strengthens both disease knowledge and practical caregiving skills [42]. Such training may improve caregivers’ attitudes toward dementia care and enhance self-efficacy and confidence in managing daily care [43,44]. Additionally, psychological interventions—such as stress management training, support groups, and cognitive-behavioral therapy—may help reduce stress and support resilience [45,46].

Caregivers’ sex, age, and economic status were significantly associated with their subjective health. Specifically, female sex, older age, and lower economic status were associated with poorer subjective health among caregivers. These findings suggest that caregiver support interventions should extend beyond emotional support to include structural and financial assistance. Targeted interventions, such as gender-sensitive support programs and financial assistance schemes, may be particularly beneficial for high-risk groups. From a nursing perspective, family caregivers of PLWD should be regarded as care recipients in their own right. This caregiver-as-care-recipient perspective highlights the need for family-focused and system-level approaches, as well as the development of structural and policy frameworks to support both PLWD and their caregiving families.

This study has several limitations. First, the use of cross-sectional secondary data limited the range of variables available for analysis, thereby preventing the full operationalization of Sörensen’s stress process model [13]. Variables that could not be incorporated include dementia subtype, medication use, pain, comorbidities, and caregiver contextual factors such as social support and coping strategies. As a result, residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Future studies should incorporate these variables to more comprehensively test the stress process model in the context of dementia caregiving.

Second, all constructs were based on caregiver reports, introducing the risk of common method variance and shared-rater bias. Caregiving years and daily caregiving hours were also self-reported and may not accurately reflect actual caregiving intensity, as these measures are susceptible to recall and social desirability biases. Additionally, there is inherent overlap between the predictor and outcome variables, as the K-NPI measures caregiver distress in direct response to each BPSD symptom. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting BPSD-linked caregiver distress rather than general caregiver stress or burden. Importantly, the depression model demonstrated suboptimal model fit (CFI=.83), falling below the conventional threshold of ≥.90. This indicates that the model does not adequately capture the underlying data structure, and therefore, the results derived from this model should be interpreted with caution. In particular, conclusions related to depression outcomes should be considered tentative and not overgeneralized, as the reduced model fit may limit the reliability and validity of these findings.

Third, the cross-sectional design limits understanding of changes in caregiver outcomes over time. Given the progressive nature of dementia, caregiver stress may evolve and increase over time, in association with worsening symptoms and increasing care demands [32]. Longitudinal studies are needed to capture these dynamic changes and inform targeted interventions throughout disease progression.

CONCLUSION

The study examined factors associated with outcomes among family caregivers of PLWD using the stress process model. The findings can serve as a foundation for exploring ways to reduce caregiver stress related to BPSD and enhance caregiving approaches. This study can contribute to enhancing the quality of dementia care services and improving health outcomes for both PLWD and their family caregivers. Future longitudinal research is needed to examine the temporal dynamics of caregiver stress related to BPSD as dementia progresses. Additionally, extended models that incorporate secondary stressors and moderating factors should be developed and validated. Intervention research should focus on evidence-based programs targeting BPSD management, providing gender-specific and financial support, and adopting family-focused, system-level approaches to comprehensively support caregiving families.
