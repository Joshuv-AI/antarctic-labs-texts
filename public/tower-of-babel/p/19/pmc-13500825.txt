Mitochondrial drivers of stem cell aging and inflammaging
Authors: Jhommara Bautista, Andrés López-Cortés
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondria are increasingly recognized as master regulators of aging, integrating bioenergetics, redox control, stem cell fate, and innate immune signaling. This review synthesizes evidence that mitochondrial dysfunction is not only a hallmark but also an upstream driver of stem cell exhaustion and inflammaging. We discuss how age-associated mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations and clonal mosaicism impair respiration and reshape metabolite availability, thereby reprogramming long-lived epigenetic states that govern quiescence, lineage commitment, and regenerative output. In parallel, erosion of mitochondrial quality control (MQC), including fission–fusion balance, mitophagy, and the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), permits the persistence of reactive oxygen species (ROS)–producing organelles and lowers containment of mitochondrial danger signals. A central advance is that mitochondrial damage can be decoded as inflammation: cytosolic mtDNA and other mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) activate cGAS–STING and NF-κB pathways, reinforcing senescence-linked cytokine circuits and chronic inflammatory tone. We further highlight nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) depletion as a metabolic bottleneck that compromises sirtuin-dependent resilience and can enforce mitochondrial dysfunction–associated senescence (MiDAS), linking redox collapse to altered senescence phenotypes and regenerative decline. Finally, we evaluate emerging mitochondria-targeted rejuvenation strategies, NAD⁺ repletion, mitophagy enhancers, mitochondrial transplantation/engineering, and precision elimination of mutant mtDNA using mitochondria-targeted transcription activator–like effector nucleases (mitoTALENs) or zinc-finger nucleases (mitoZFNs), emphasizing tissue-specific thresholds and context dependence for effective healthspan extension.

Introduction

Aging is an orchestrated biological process defined by the gradual decline of physiological function and regenerative capacity across tissues. Among the central hallmarks of aging, mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a pivotal factor that intersects with metabolic reprogramming, stem cell exhaustion, and systemic inflammation. Mitochondria, long recognized for their bioenergetic roles, are now appreciated as dynamic organelles at the nexus of cellular signaling, redox balance, metabolite availability, and inter-organelle communication—functions that progressively deteriorate with age1,2. Accordingly, the field has shifted from viewing mitochondrial alterations as downstream correlates of aging to interrogating how mitochondrial defects can actively drive cellular senescence, tissue degeneration, and systemic decline, and how restoring mitochondrial integrity might mitigate, or potentially reverse, age-related dysfunction3,4.

Recent studies underscore that mitochondrial decline in aging is not merely a consequence of bioenergetic failure, but a causal driver of age-associated pathologies through multiple interconnected pathways5–7. Dysfunctional mitochondria amplify reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, weaken antioxidant buffering, and engage cell-death and stress-response programs, thereby contributing to degenerative trajectories across organ systems, from neurodegeneration to cardiovascular disease1,3,8. A major conceptual advance is the close linkage between mitochondrial damage and inflammaging, the chronic, low-grade inflammatory state that accompanies aging9. In this context, mitochondria-derived danger signals, including oxidized mitochondrial DNA and other mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs), activate innate immune sensors such as cGAS–STING and reinforce cytokine networks that propagate tissue-wide inflammatory tone10,11.

Historically, the conceptual role of mitochondria in aging has evolved markedly over the past seven decades. Harman’s free radical theory of aging framed oxidative reactions as cumulative drivers of functional decline, and subsequent advances crystallized a mitochondrial variant that emphasized mitochondria as both major sources and targets of ROS12. Miquel et al. further proposed that mitochondrial injury and mitochondrial genome instability could act as primary aging drivers, offering a basis for tissue-selective vulnerability13. A decisive shift occurred in the 2000s with genetic tests of causality: POLG mutator models demonstrated that accelerating mtDNA mutation accumulation can be sufficient to elicit premature aging-like phenotypes, while also sharpening the nuance that aging-linked mitochondrial dysfunction cannot be reduced to ROS-mediated damage alone5,6. Over the last decade, this trajectory has culminated in a network-centric view in which mitochondria function as signaling organelles that govern stress adaptation, stem cell fate, and inflammatory tone via integrated quality-control circuits (fission–fusion–mitophagy–mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt)) and innate immune engagement when mitochondrial components mislocalize—most notably through mtDNA release–driven activation of cGAS–STING programs that directly connect mitochondrial dysfunction to inflammaging14. Importantly, because mitochondrial roles and aging kinetics vary substantially between tissues, the phenotypic readout of mitochondrial damage is context-dependent. Differences in baseline energetic/Ca²⁺-handling demand, mitochondrial damage containment capacity (MQC/lysosomal competence), and inflammatory gain (propensity to convert mtDNA/mtDAMP escape into cGAS–STING/NF-κB programs) help explain why similar mitochondrial lesions can drive regenerative collapse in stem-cell–dependent tissues yet manifest as mosaic respiratory deficits and chronic inflammatory priming in long-lived post-mitotic compartments15–17.

Concurrently, emerging evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction plays a decisive role in stem cell aging, impairing quiescence, differentiation, and self-renewal capacity. Studies using adipose-derived stromal/stem cells (ASCs) and other stem cell systems show that age-dependent remodeling of mitochondrial respiration and proteome composition diminishes regenerative potential, underscoring mitochondrial control over stemness and lineage commitment18,19. Mitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum (ER) crosstalk, mitophagy, and the UPRmt are integral to maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, and their dysregulation is implicated in both stem cell exhaustion and impaired tissue repair with age1,20. Importantly, failure of mitochondrial containment, via compromised quality control and altered organelle contacts, can lower the threshold for mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)/mtDAMP escape and innate immune engagement, thereby mechanistically coupling stem cell decline to systemic inflammaging.

Understanding these mechanisms has catalyzed the development of mitochondria-targeted anti-aging strategies. Interventions such as NAD⁺ precursors, mitochondrial transplantation, and pharmacological agents that enhance mitochondrial biogenesis and turnover are being actively explored for their ability to restore mitochondrial resilience, rejuvenate stem cell function, and extend healthspan3,8,10. Together, these advances mark a paradigm shift in aging research, from viewing mitochondria as mere energy factories to recognizing them as master regulators of aging trajectories and therapeutic opportunity. Here, we synthesize evidence across mtDNA instability, mitochondrial quality-control circuits, NAD⁺-dependent resilience, and mtDAMP-driven immune sensing to explain how mitochondrial failure converges on stem cell exhaustion and inflammaging, and to highlight emerging mitochondria-directed rejuvenation strategies9,21.

Mitochondrial DNA mutations and the rewiring of stem cell aging

mtDNA mutations accumulate progressively with age and are considered key contributors to stem cell dysfunction, tissue degeneration, and systemic aging. These mutations arise due to the high replication frequency of mtDNA, its proximity to ROS generated by oxidative phosphorylation, and relatively inefficient DNA repair compared to nuclear DNA22,23. As a result, somatic mutations expand clonally, particularly in metabolically active and post-mitotic tissues such as the brain, muscle, and intestine24,25 (Fig. 1). Somatic mtDNA mutations accumulate with age and include both clonally expanded variants and low-frequency cryptic variants that are largely invisible in bulk tissue measurements until they expand within individual cells or microclones. As these variants rise in heteroplasmy, they can impair respiratory capacity and engage stress programs linked to ER stress, proteostasis disruption, and inflammation24,25,28. While some mtDNA point mutations may be merely stochastic byproducts of aging, large-scale deletions have been increasingly recognized as causal in tissue dysfunction and cellular aging. The question of whether mtDNA mutations are causes or consequences of aging remains debated. Evidence from single-cell analyses and simulation models supports a view where mutations originate early in life and clonally expand over time, especially in postmitotic tissues like the brain and muscle, leading to regional energy deficits29,30. These findings align with the mitochondrial theory of aging, while also underscoring the complexity of distinguishing causality from correlation.

POLG mutator mice, carrying a proofreading-deficient mtDNA polymerase γ, are a foundational model demonstrating that mtDNA mutations can induce premature aging phenotypes5. These mice display hematopoietic anomalies, anemia, lymphopenia, and skewed differentiation toward the myeloid lineage, all features of stem cell aging. However, while hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) retain their numbers, their functional differentiation capacity is impaired, indicating that mitochondrial integrity is essential for lineage specification rather than stemness maintenance27. In intestinal stem cells (ISCs), a high burden of mtDNA mutations reduces LGR5⁺ cell function and organoid-forming capacity. This phenotype is mediated by ATF5-driven activation of the UPRmt, and is reversible with NAD⁺ precursor supplementation (e.g., NMN), which restores ISC regenerative potential15.

A study has revealed that mtDNA mutations form a dynamic mosaic within tissues, comprising both inherited heteroplasmies and postzygotic mutations that expand over time26. The accumulation and selection of these mutations differ across tissues: rapidly dividing tissues accumulate random mutations that expand stochastically, while non-dividing tissues exhibit mutation hotspots under purifying or compensatory selection24,25. In some cases, somatic reversions emerge to restore mito-nuclear genome compatibility, demonstrating evolutionary pressure even within aging individuals24. Aging-related single-cell analyses also highlight that cryptic mtDNA mutations correlate with disrupted expression of genes linked to inflammation, ER stress, and neurodegeneration28, further reinforcing their relevance in the functional decline of aged stem cells.

mtDNA mutations not only impair energy production but can also reshape stem cell fate by remodeling metabolic–epigenetic coupling31. Because mtDNA lesions compromise electron transport and thereby alter TCA-cycle flux, they are expected to shift the availability of epigenetically active metabolites, particularly α-ketoglutarate, succinate, fumarate, and acetyl-CoA, that gate the activity of chromatin-modifying enzymes. Thus, mutation-linked respiratory constraints provide a direct route by which mtDNA instability can be written into chromatin state via metabolite availability and long-lived epigenetic programs. Separately, evidence from ISC models indicates that mitochondrial age itself, rather than mtDNA mutation load, can define functionally distinct stem cell subpopulations: selective retention/inheritance of older mitochondria can elevate α-ketoglutarate and promote TET-dependent epigenetic remodeling and niche regeneration via Paneth cell formation32. Together, these data support two complementary mechanisms of mitochondrial control over stem cell aging: (i) mtDNA mutation–driven respiratory insufficiency that reshapes metabolite–epigenome coupling, and (ii) organelle-age–driven mitochondrial heterogeneity that partitions stem cell states and regenerative trajectories. Framing these mechanisms explicitly helps prevent over-attribution of aged mitochondria phenotypes to mtDNA mutations when the causal driver is mitochondrial segregation/quality rather than genome lesions.

In contrast, evidence that mtDNA mutations promote de novo serine synthesis in intestinal tumors likely reflects a context-dependent, pro-growth metabolic adaptation; accordingly, we discuss such tumor-linked rewiring separately to maintain the focus here on aging-associated metabolic–epigenetic coupling in stem cells33.

Mitochondrial quality control: fission, fusion, mitophagy, and the unfolded protein response in aging

Mitochondrial quality control (MQC) is a critical homeostatic system that maintains mitochondrial integrity by coordinating dynamic processes such as fission, fusion, mitophagy, and the UPRmt34 As organisms age, the efficiency of these mechanisms declines, leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria that contribute to cellular senescence and age-associated diseases35–37 (Fig. 1). Importantly, MQC failure is therefore both a hallmark of aging (damaged mitochondria persist and accumulate) and a driver that can actively propagate aging phenotypes by amplifying ROS stress, increasing the likelihood of mitochondrial danger signaling (mtDAMP/mtDNA release), and reinforcing innate immune activation that contributes to stem cell dysfunction and inflammaging38,39.

Mitochondria constantly undergo fission and fusion to adapt to metabolic demands and segregate damaged components. Fusion enables complementation between mitochondria by mixing contents, thus diluting damaged mtDNA or defective proteins. In contrast, fission facilitates the isolation of dysfunctional mitochondrial fragments for degradation via mitophagy35,40. In aging cells, the balance between these processes is disrupted, often favoring fission, which is associated with increased mitochondrial fragmentation, bioenergetic failure, and apoptosis34,41. This imbalance is evident in aged tissues where the expression of fusion mediators such as MFN1/2 and OPA1 is reduced, while fission proteins like DRP1 are upregulated42. This shift contributes to mitochondrial network disorganization and loss of membrane potential, triggering mitophagy or apoptosis depending on the severity of damage40,41. In stem and progenitor compartments, sustained fragmentation is not merely a marker of aging; it can also maintain stress signaling programs that erode durable quiescence, bias lineage decisions, and increase vulnerability to niche-derived inflammatory cues—thereby linking mitochondrial dynamics to regenerative decline43,44. Crucially, mitochondrial dynamics also determine whether age-associated mtDNA lesions are converted into inflammatory triggers: fusion can buffer the functional impact of heteroplasmic mtDNA mutations through complementation, whereas excessive fission and cristae disorganization can destabilize nucleoids and increase the probability of mtDNA leakage. In this framework, mtDNA mutations and dynamics converge mechanistically, mutations increase respiratory stress/ROS, and altered dynamics lower the threshold for mtDNA/mtDAMP release, thereby coupling organelle genome instability to innate immune activation45,46.

Mitophagy is essential for eliminating irreversibly damaged mitochondria and preventing their persistence as sources of cellular stress47. The PINK1–Parkin pathway orchestrates one major mitophagy axis: PINK1 accumulates on depolarized mitochondria, recruits Parkin, and drives ubiquitination of outer mitochondrial membrane proteins to initiate autophagic engulfment and lysosomal degradation48. Aging compromises this pathway at multiple steps, including inefficient PINK1 stabilization, defective autophagosome formation, and reduced lysosomal clearance capacity34,49. Defective mitophagy therefore promotes the retention of dysfunctional mitochondria with pathologically elevated ROS output, which can trigger inflammatory signaling and tissue dysfunction50. Importantly, in stem cell compartments, impaired mitophagy has been linked to loss of quiescence, reduced self-renewal, and premature functional exhaustion, underscoring MQC as a determinant of stem cell fitness during aging51,52. Mechanistically, failed “fission–mitophagy coupling” creates a permissive state in which mutant/ROS-producing mitochondria persist long enough to engage mitochondrial danger pathways. Under these conditions, mtDNA can accumulate to higher heteroplasmy within damaged fragments and, if not cleared, can translocate to the cytosol where it activates cGAS–STING/type I IFN and NF-κB programs—linking an organelle-quality defect directly to inflammaging and stem cell niche remodeling53,54.

The UPRmt is an evolutionarily conserved transcriptional program activated in response to mitochondrial proteotoxic stress. It is triggered by the accumulation of misfolded or unassembled proteins, often due to impaired protein import or mitochondrial translation errors55,56. In mammals, transcription factors such as ATF4, ATF5, and CHOP mediate UPRmt signaling by inducing genes that encode mitochondrial chaperones (e.g., HSP60), proteases (e.g., LONP1), and antioxidants37,48. Upon mitochondrial dysfunction, ATF5 is retained in the cytosol and translocated to the nucleus, where it promotes recovery programs aimed at restoring proteostasis, detoxifying ROS, and enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis48. However, aging is associated with reduced sensitivity and/or effectiveness of UPRmt signaling, potentially reflecting epigenetic remodeling, altered chromatin accessibility, or persistent mitonuclear stress that blunts adaptive capacity. Notably, moderate, properly timed UPRmt activation has been linked to improved stress resilience and lifespan extension in model organisms, consistent with a hormetic framework in which transient mitochondrial stress engages FOXO-linked antioxidant and repair pathways37,57. By contrast, attenuation or dysregulation of UPRmt during aging may convert an initially protective response into a chronic stress state, increasing the probability that unresolved proteotoxicity and redox imbalance escalate into inflammatory signaling and functional erosion in stem cell compartments35,58. In the context of mtDNA mutation burden, insufficient UPRmt may further impair mito-nuclear coordination, thereby amplifying respiratory stress and increasing the likelihood that mitochondrial genome instability is read out as inflammatory danger signaling rather than resolved by repair/turnover59,60.

All components of MQC, fission, fusion, mitophagy, and UPRmt, are interlinked. Excessive fission can promote mitophagy initiation, whereas sustained fusion can delay clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria; UPRmt activation can precede mitophagy to determine whether damage is reversible or requires elimination37,48. Dysregulation of this balance is a hallmark of aging, contributing to tissue degeneration, immune dysfunction, and loss of stem cell potential36,41,61. Moreover, restoration of NAD⁺ levels has been shown to enhance UPRmt activity and improve mitochondrial function in aging models; interventions such as NAD⁺ precursors or PARP inhibition can promote mitonuclear communication and UPRmt activation, delaying functional decline57. Together, these data support a unified mechanism in which mtDNA mutations increase mitochondrial stress, while altered fusion/fission and impaired turnover lower the containment threshold—permitting mtDNA/mtDAMP escape and chronic innate immune signaling that drives inflammaging and accelerates stem cell exhaustion9.

NAD⁺ metabolism and mitochondrial bioenergetic collapse in aging

The progressive decline in NAD⁺ levels during aging has emerged as a central factor in mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired energy production, and stem cell exhaustion62. NAD⁺ is a critical coenzyme that mediates redox reactions in glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), while also serving as a substrate for sirtuins, PARPs, and CD38, enzymes regulating DNA repair, stress responses, and metabolic homeostasis57,63.

A hallmark of aging is the bioenergetic collapse driven by NAD⁺ depletion64. This loss arises from both a reduction in biosynthetic capacity (e.g., impaired salvage pathways for precursors such as nicotinamide riboside (NR) and nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN)) and increased degradation via enzymes such as CD38, PARP1, and SARM157,65,66. CD38, in particular, becomes hyperactive with age, hydrolyzing NAD⁺ into ADPR and contributing to calcium imbalance, mitochondrial fragmentation, and oxidative stress63,67. The combined effects lead to impaired mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ATP production, and elevated ROS levels68.

NAD⁺ deficiency has been shown to directly impair mitochondrial biogenesis and respiratory capacity. Reduced NAD⁺ availability compromises the activity of mitochondrial sirtuins, especially SIRT1 and SIRT3, which regulate key transcriptional programs such as those governed by PGC-1α, FOXO, and NRF169,70. These transcription factors promote antioxidant responses, mitochondrial protein turnover, and mitonuclear coordination, all essential for healthy aging57,71. Importantly, mitochondrial dysfunction can enforce senescence through NAD⁺/redox collapse: a reduced NAD⁺/NADH ratio is sufficient to trigger mitochondrial dysfunction–associated senescence (MiDAS; a senescence program driven by mitochondrial metabolic/redox failure and NAD⁺ imbalance) via AMPK–p53 signaling and is coupled to a distinct secretory phenotype with attenuation of the IL-1–dependent inflammatory arm of the SASP, highlighting that NAD⁺ status can shape not only bioenergetic fitness but also the quality of senescence-linked inflammatory output72.

Stem cells are especially vulnerable to NAD⁺ loss. In aged muscle stem cells (MuSCs), neural stem cells, and melanocyte stem cells, NAD⁺ depletion leads to senescence, loss of quiescence, and diminished regenerative capacity68,73. Zhang et al. demonstrated that restoring NAD⁺ levels in aged mice using NR reactivated mitochondrial metabolism, upregulated prohibitin proteins, and induced the UPRmt, a protective transcriptional program that mitigates proteotoxic stress. This intervention delayed stem cell senescence and extended lifespan, highlighting the rejuvenating potential of NAD⁺ repletion73.

Additionally, NAD⁺ repletion can also rescue age-associated mitochondrial defects in stem cell systems, reinforcing a direct link between NAD⁺ availability and regenerative bioenergetic fitness. For instance, aged stem cell populations exhibit reduced mitochondrial respiration and fragmented mitochondrial networks, phenotypes that are partially restored by NAD⁺ supplementation, consistent with a causal role for NAD⁺ decline in bioenergetic failure during aging73,74. Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction in aging is linked to disrupted NAD⁺/NADH ratios, impaired mitophagy, and failure to maintain mitochondrial dynamics. Loss of NAD⁺ impairs the PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway and dampens UPRmt signaling, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria. In aged tissues, this contributes to inflammaging, metabolic inflexibility, and cellular senescence57,75.

Beyond NAD⁺ depletion, aging-associated changes in amino-acid availability and redox-regulatory circuits can further constrain mitochondrial resilience and stem/progenitor function. Amino acids can act as mitochondrial cofactors by supporting redox buffering, proteostasis, and translation, thereby modulating the probability that mitochondrial stress progresses to senescence rather than adaptive recovery76,77. Taurine, which declines with age, has been linked to improved systemic aging phenotypes upon supplementation and is mechanistically tied to mitochondrial fitness through roles in mitochondrial translation via tRNA modification, providing a direct route by which amino-acid status can stabilize OXPHOS capacity78,79. Proline represents a complementary lever: recent work indicates that proline metabolism can restore mitochondrial function and improve differentiation capacity in senescent/aged progenitor contexts, consistent with engagement of mitochondrial stress-resolution and quality-control programs80. In parallel, amino-acid supply that sustains glutathione synthesis (notably glycine and cysteine availability) can relieve redox limitation, reducing ROS-driven mitochondrial damage and lowering the likelihood of chronic inflammatory signaling downstream of mitochondrial dysfunction.

In summary, NAD⁺ depletion represents a central metabolic axis of mitochondrial failure in aging, with direct consequences for energy homeostasis, stem cell function, and longevity. Therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving NAD⁺, such as supplementation with NR or NMN, inhibition of NAD⁺-consuming enzymes, and activation of sirtuin signaling, are being actively explored to reverse age-associated decline65.

Mitochondrial control of stem cell fate: from quiescence to senescence

Mitochondria play a decisive role in stem cell fate determination by acting as metabolic rheostats that integrate bioenergetic, redox, and epigenetic cues. Their influence extends across the spectrum of stem cell states, from deep quiescence to active proliferation and eventual senescence81. One of the fundamental mechanisms by which mitochondria exert control is through metabolic programming. Quiescent stem cells typically rely on glycolysis, whereas the transition to activation involves a metabolic shift towards OXPHOS, a process tightly regulated by mitochondrial function and quality82.

Mitochondria play a central role in regulating stem cell fate through asymmetric inheritance, where organelles are unequally distributed to daughter cells to drive distinct functional outcomes. In iISCs, the selective inheritance of aged mitochondria gives rise to a subpopulation (ISCmito-O) with enhanced α-ketoglutarate production, promoting epigenetic remodeling and tissue regeneration via TET enzyme activation32. Similarly, in HSCs, recent imaging advances have revealed that asymmetric segregation of mitochondria predicts daughter cell differentiation and long-term function. These findings underscore mitochondria not only as metabolic hubs but also as determinants of stem cell identity, linking mitochondrial age and activity to regenerative capacity across tissue types32,83.

The metabolic identity of stem cells is also coupled to mitochondrial morphology and dynamics. Activated HSCs exhibit increased mitochondrial biogenesis and network complexity, features associated with their entry into the cell cycle84. Notably, mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm/MMP) can stratify chronologically old HSCs into functionally distinct states, and in vivo pharmacological potentiation of MMP in aged HSCs has been shown to improve engraftment and reverse age-associated myeloid-biased output, indicating that mitochondrial fitness can be experimentally tuned to rejuvenate stem cell function85. Mitochondrial dynamics are increasingly understood as functionally coupled to cell-cycle control itself: core cell-cycle regulators can modulate fission–fusion machinery (e.g., DRP1/MFN/OPA1-linked states) to tune mitochondrial hyperfusion versus hyperfission, thereby influencing cell-cycle progression, exit into quiescence, and senescence86. Importantly, increased mitochondrial mass in aged HSCs is not necessarily a dysfunction marker: a mitochondria-high subset can retain strong self-renewal and regenerative capacity, consistent with mitochondrial abundance coupled to preserved quality control (e.g., autophagy/turnover) and without a proportional ROS penalty87. Beyond physiological aging, Dnmt3a R878H/+ mutant HSPCs in clonal hematopoiesis exhibit a mitochondria-driven fitness state, increased OXPHOS/respiration, and a hyperpolarized mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) that enhances competitiveness in the aged bone marrow. This high-Δψm state is also an Achilles’ heel: alkyl-TPP compounds (e.g., MitoQ, d-TPP) preferentially accumulate in hyperpolarized mitochondria, suppress respiration, induce mitochondria-dependent apoptosis, and selectively eliminate mutant HSPCs in vitro and in aged in vivo settings, with similar effects observed in human DNMT3A-knockdown HSPCs88. Conversely, impaired mitochondrial clearance due to defective mitophagy leads to accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria, resulting in increased ROS, DNA damage, and premature senescence89.

Furthermore, mitochondria directly interface with nuclear transcriptional programs. For instance, aged or metabolically damaged mitochondria produce ROS that activate the p53 and p16INK4a pathways, key regulators of senescence in neural and mesenchymal stem cells28,90. Simultaneously, mitochondrial-derived metabolites such as succinate, fumarate, and acetyl-CoA influence the activity of epigenetic modifiers, including histone deacetylases and DNA methyltransferases, thereby altering chromatin accessibility and gene expression patterns relevant to differentiation and senescence24,82.

mtDNA integrity also contributes to stem cell function. Somatic accumulation of mtDNA mutations, particularly truncating variants, can drive clonal expansion and disrupt respiratory efficiency, a hallmark of aging stem cell niches23,26. Interestingly, not all mtDNA mutations are deleterious. Some appear to be positively selected in aging tissues due to metabolic advantages conferred to the host cell, potentially via alterations in one-carbon metabolism or redox homeostasis23,24. However, when mutation burden surpasses a threshold, particularly in stem cell compartments, it can destabilize mitochondrial-nuclear coordination and lead to functional exhaustion26,90.

The interaction between mitochondrial dynamics and environmental cues also determines whether a stem cell enters senescence or maintains regenerative potential. For example, nutrient deprivation or mitochondrial stress can induce a transient pause in stem cell proliferation through activation of the integrated stress response and UPRmt, both of which serve as adaptive checkpoints28,89. If the stress is chronic or mitochondrial repair fails, this checkpoint may become a terminal senescence program28,90. In summary, mitochondria govern stem cell fate through a multidimensional framework involving energy production, ROS signaling, mtDNA integrity, metabolite-mediated epigenetic regulation, and dynamic segregation during cell division. The emerging concept of mitochondrial age and functional heterogeneity introduces a novel layer of control in tissue renewal and aging, underscoring mitochondria not merely as powerhouses, but as fate-determining organelles within the stem cell niche32,82 (Fig. 1).

Oxidative stress, inflammation, and the mitochondrial-inflammaging axis

Mitochondria are major producers and targets of ROS, and their dysfunction is a central driver of the chronic, low-grade inflammation observed during aging, commonly termed inflammaging91. With age, mitochondrial efficiency declines, increasing electron leakage from the electron transport chain and pathologically elevating ROS output. This oxidative stress damages mitochondrial proteins, lipids, and mtDNA, further impairing bioenergetics and promoting a pro-inflammatory state. Mitochondrial dysfunction also activates redox-sensitive pathways, such as NF-κB, enhancing the production of cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α, which reinforce systemic inflammation92,93. A concurrent reduction in sirtuin activity, particularly SIRT3 due to NAD⁺ depletion, weakens antioxidant defenses and mitochondrial detoxification mechanisms, amplifying ROS burden94 (Fig. 1). In this context, mitochondrial ROS is not only a hallmark of aged tissues but a causal driver that feeds forward into inflammaging by licensing mtDNA/mtDAMP release and sustaining NF-κB– and SASP-linked cytokine circuits17,95.

A major contributor to inflammaging is the release of oxidized or fragmented mtDNA into the cytosol. This occurs in response to mitochondrial membrane damage, particularly during sublethal mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (minority MOMP), which is a partial permeabilization affecting a subset of mitochondria without full apoptotic execution96. Cytosolic mtDNA acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), engaging the cGAS–STING axis and inducing type I interferon responses and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)11,95,97. The SASP involves the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and proteases, which propagate local and systemic inflammation. Inhibition of miMOMP in aged animal models reduces SASP factors and inflammatory signatures, suggesting a direct role for mitochondrial stress in promoting senescence-linked inflammation95.

In parallel, aging-associated dysregulation of mitochondrial Ca²⁺ handling provides a direct mechanistic bridge between stem cell dysfunction and inflammaging. Remodeling of ER–mitochondria Ca²⁺ transfer at MERCs (IP3R–VDAC1–MCU uptake versus NCLX efflux) can drive either insufficient matrix Ca²⁺ (blunting TCA/OXPHOS support and promoting energetic stress) or Ca²⁺ overload (triggering mPTP opening and sublethal membrane injury). Both states amplify ROS and destabilize membrane integrity, increasing the probability of mtDNA/mtDAMP escape and reinforcing cGAS–STING and NF-κB signaling, thereby sustaining SASP-linked cytokine circuits95,98,99. In stem cell compartments, impaired mitochondrial Ca²⁺ buffering erodes quiescence and biases premature differentiation/senescence, while the resulting inflammatory milieu remodels the niche and entrenches chronic low-grade inflammation—positioning Ca²⁺ homeostasis as a mitochondria-centered gain control for regenerative capacity and inflammatory tone in aging tissues95,100.

Beyond redox imbalance and mtDNA sensing, aging also perturbs mitochondrial calcium handling101,102. In stem cell compartments, disrupted ER–mitochondria communication and impaired mitochondrial Ca²⁺ buffering can erode quiescence and promote premature differentiation or senescence, thereby sensitizing stem cells and their niches to stress-responsive inflammatory programs (including NF-κB–linked transcription). This provides a mechanistic route by which mitochondrial calcium dysregulation can converge with ROS and mtDNA-driven innate immune signaling to shape inflammatory tone during aging in tissues sustained by adult stem cells103–105. In parallel, immune-aging–associated diseases such as idiopathic inflammatory myopathies illustrate how chronic interferon-γ exposure rewires mitochondrial metabolism and elevates ROS in muscle; importantly, mitochondria-targeted antioxidants partially reverse these defects, reinforcing a causal connection between mitochondrial oxidative stress and inflammaging106,107.

At the cellular level, damaged mitochondria accumulate in senescent cells due to defective mitophagy. These mitochondria remain metabolically active yet dysfunctional, excessively generating ROS that helps sustain SASP signaling97,108. In addition, mitochondrial lipid peroxidation byproducts, such as 4-HNE and oxidized cardiolipin, further stimulate inflammatory pathways via toll-like receptors and inflammasomes11. Thus, mitochondrial dysfunction not only initiates but sustains a sterile inflammatory environment across tissues during aging. Therapeutic strategies targeting ROS, restoring calcium homeostasis, enhancing mitophagy, or blocking mtDNA-mediated innate immune activation represent promising avenues to interrupt the mitochondrial–inflammaging feedback loop and preserve tissue homeostasis92,109.

Mitochondria-ER crosstalk and organelle network remodeling in aging

Mitochondria and the ER are physically and functionally coupled through mitochondria–ER contact sites (MERCs), which act as regulatory hubs for Ca²⁺ signaling, lipid exchange, mitochondrial dynamics, and stress adaptation110. In aging, MERC architecture and function are remodeled, weakening inter-organelle communication and shifting cellular homeostasis toward energetic fragility and stress hypersensitivity. A central consequence is impaired ER-to-mitochondria Ca²⁺ transfer through the IP3R–GRP75–VDAC1 axis, which normally sustains matrix Ca²⁺ levels needed for TCA-cycle dehydrogenase activity and respiratory output. With age, altered Ca²⁺ coupling can produce either insufficient mitochondrial Ca²⁺ (blunting ATP production and limiting stress-responsive bioenergetic flexibility) or pathological Ca²⁺ overload (sensitizing mitochondria to permeability transition and sublethal membrane injury), thereby increasing vulnerability to apoptosis and inflammatory danger signaling111–113.

MERC disruption also amplifies proteostatic and redox failure by coupling ER stress to mitochondrial decay. In aged cells, MERC spacing is frequently altered, and key tethering/bridging components, including mitofusin 2 (MFN2) and GRP75, become dysregulated, weakening contact-dependent signaling and metabolite exchange11. This promotes chronic activation of the ER unfolded protein response (UPRER), which, with age, can shift from adaptive remodeling toward pro-apoptotic outputs, while simultaneously impairing autophagy-dependent clearance pathways. Importantly, MERCs are not passive wiring but operational platforms where mitochondrial fission and mitophagy are coordinated; thus, defective MERC function can uncouple fission from turnover, allowing damaged mitochondria to persist as chronic sources of ROS and proteotoxic stress. This creates a feed-forward loop in which ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction reinforce one another, accelerating organelle network collapse and inflammation-permissive states114–116.

Critically, MERC remodeling is especially consequential in stem cell compartments because organelle coupling helps set the threshold between durable quiescence, regenerative activation, and senescence. In young stem cells, MERCs support mitochondrial remodeling during fate transitions and asymmetric division, enabling selective retention or inheritance of functionally competent mitochondria that preserve self-renewal potential. With age, stem cells often exhibit fragmented networks, impaired ER–mitochondria Ca²⁺ coordination, and reduced mitochondrial membrane potential—features associated with loss of quiescence, premature differentiation, and functional exhaustion that erode tissue regeneration18,113,115. Conversely, excessive or persistent MERC activity in stressed or aged contexts can drive mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload, promoting permeability transition pore opening, mitochondrial membrane injury, and cell death, thereby directly constraining stem/progenitor pool maintenance and repair capacity11,111.

These insights position MERCs as actionable targets for rejuvenation strategies aimed at restoring organelle network function rather than treating isolated pathways. Approaches that stabilize ER–mitochondria coupling, such as MFN2 reinforcement or pharmacologic tuning of tethering complexes, have been reported to improve mitochondrial performance and attenuate age-associated inflammatory signatures117,118. Likewise, interventions that modulate MERC-linked Ca²⁺ flux (e.g., targeting IP3R signaling) or rebalance mitochondrial dynamics may restore bioenergetic flexibility and reduce ROS amplification in aging models112,113. Collectively, mitochondria–ER crosstalk represents a core control node in aging biology whose remodeling can convert transient stress into persistent organelle dysfunction, stem cell decline, and inflammation-prone tissue states111,116.

Administration of NAD⁺ precursors like NR and NMN has been shown to enhance mitochondrial respiration, stimulate the mitochondrial UPRmt, and improve stem cell function. Therapeutic interventions targeting mitochondrial biology in stem cell aging and inflammaging

Pharmacological strategies targeting mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidant defenses, and mitophagy are also advancing. Agents that activate AMPK or PGC-1α signaling promote mitochondrial turnover and metabolic homeostasis in aging tissues. Moreover, compounds like urolithin A induce mitophagy, selectively removing damaged mitochondria and improving muscle health in aged models. These approaches aim not only to prevent mitochondrial decay but also to restore dynamic remodeling of the mitochondrial network, essential for adaptation to energetic and redox stress during aging121,122.

One of the most innovative therapeutic modalities is mitochondrial transplantation. This strategy involves the isolation of functional mitochondria from healthy donor cells and their introduction into damaged or aged recipient tissues123. Mitochondrial transplantation has been shown to restore bioenergetics, reduce oxidative stress, and improve cell viability in models of cardiac ischemia, liver injury, and neurodegeneration. A particularly promising advancement is the development of bioengineered mitochondria, such as nMITO, which fuse mitochondria with neutrophil membranes to enhance targeted delivery to inflamed tissues. These hybrid constructs exhibited superior retention, anti-inflammatory activity, and regeneration in multiple organ systems compared to native mitochondria124.

Beyond cellular therapies, gene editing strategies provide a route to mitigate deleterious mtDNA mutations that accrue with age. Although mitochondrial targeting remains technically demanding, owing to the double-membrane barrier and distinct replication/repair biology, mitochondria-targeted zinc-finger nucleases (mitoZFNs; zinc-finger DNA-binding arrays fused to a nuclease and directed to mitochondria via a targeting sequence) and mitochondria-targeted transcription activator-like effector nucleases (mitoTALENs; TALE DNA-binding modules fused to a nuclease and similarly targeted) have shown promise in selectively eliminating mutant mtDNA molecules and improving mitochondrial function. In parallel, droplet-based microfluidic platforms enable scalable, high-throughput mitochondrial transfer with tighter control over payload and dosing, enhancing regenerative performance in muscle injury models120,123. Collectively, mitochondria-directed interventions, from NAD⁺ augmentation to precision organelle engineering, are reshaping the anti-aging therapeutic landscape and expanding opportunities to treat age-associated disease73.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Mitochondria are not passive bystanders in the aging process but active regulators of cellular fate, metabolic plasticity, and tissue regeneration. Their dysfunction, arising from mtDNA mutations, impaired quality control systems, and redox imbalance, lies at the heart of multiple aging hallmarks, including stem cell exhaustion, inflammaging, and bioenergetic collapse53,62. This review integrates the converging lines of evidence that mitochondrial perturbations act as both upstream triggers and downstream amplifiers of age-associated decline across tissues.

As demonstrated by studies on POLG mutator mice and single-cell mtDNA analyses, the accumulation of somatic mitochondrial mutations undermines bioenergetics and cellular resilience, leading to tissue-specific degeneration and premature aging phenotypes5,27,28. Yet, these mutations exert distinct effects across cell types and differentiation states, suggesting that mitochondrial quality must be understood within the context of lineage identity, proliferation dynamics, and microenvironmental cues27,35. Notably, while mitochondrial mutations may not uniformly drive stem cell depletion, they compromise lineage fidelity and regenerative output, especially in hematopoietic and intestinal compartments90.

The role of mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, fission, fusion, mitophagy, and the UPRmt emerges as a central pillar of cellular longevity. Aging is marked by an imbalance in these processes, leading to the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria that escape clearance and perpetuate stress signaling. While moderate activation of the UPRmt has been linked to lifespan extension and stress resilience, chronic dysregulation contributes to proteostasis collapse, altered metabolite flux, and impaired inter-organelle communication35,40,120. Restoration of these protective pathways through NAD⁺ supplementation or genetic modulation holds promise in rejuvenating aged mitochondria and restoring cellular function66,68.

The progressive depletion of NAD⁺ represents a key metabolic bottleneck in aging, with broad consequences on mitochondrial function, redox balance, and stem cell homeostasis. As highlighted in recent studies, NAD⁺ boosters such as NMN and NR enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, activate sirtuin signaling, and reinstate mitonuclear coordination, thereby delaying senescence and improving regenerative capacity in aged tissues11,66,68. These interventions are now being tested in human trials and represent a paradigm shift from disease treatment to healthspan extension.

Emerging strategies such as mitochondrial transplantation, mitotherapy, and targeted editing of mtDNA mutations further expand the therapeutic landscape. The success of mitochondrial transfer in preclinical models of cardiac, neural, and hepatic injury underscores the feasibility of directly reconstituting bioenergetic function in aged or damaged tissues. Moreover, novel delivery platforms, including bioengineered mitochondria and droplet-based systems, enhance the precision and retention of these therapies, while tools like mitoZFNs and mitoTALENs offer avenues for correcting pathogenic mtDNA mutations11,120.

Looking forward, several challenges and opportunities must be addressed. First, deciphering tissue-specific thresholds of mitochondrial dysfunction is critical for timing interventions appropriately. Second, understanding the bidirectional crosstalk between mitochondria and other aging regulators, such as epigenetic clocks, senescence-associated secretory phenotypes, and systemic inflammation, will be essential for combinatorial therapies11,35. Third, the heterogeneity of mitochondrial populations within cells, including their spatial distribution and replicative age, requires deeper exploration using high-resolution imaging and single-organelle omics90,118. Finally, the long-term safety and systemic effects of mitochondrial interventions in humans remain to be rigorously evaluated.

In conclusion, mitochondria have transcended their canonical role as energy generators to become central architects of aging and cellular plasticity. Therapeutically targeting mitochondrial networks, through metabolic repletion, genetic editing, or organelle replacement, holds the potential to reshape our approach to aging, not merely by slowing decline, but by enabling regeneration, resilience, and rejuvenation.
