Longitudinal changes in systemic right ventricular remodeling in adult patients with transposition of the great vessels as assessed by cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Jonathan Kochav, Michael P DiLorenzo, Matthew J Lewis, Maarten Groenink, Malou van den Boogaard, Barbara Mulder, Marlon Rosenbaum
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic right ventricular (sRV) physiology occurs in patients with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (ccTGA) and D-TGA post atrial switch repair, and the natural history is of progressive sRV dysfunction. No study has assessed longitudinal changes in sRV remodeling by serial cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR).MethodsPatients evaluated at two adult congenital heart disease centers and who underwent ≥2 CMR exams were studied. Indexed sRV end-diastolic volume (sRVEDVi), end-systolic volume (sRVESVi), and ejection fraction (sRVEF) were determined by a core laboratory. Concurrent echocardiograms were assessed for degree of systemic tricuspid regurgitation (sTR). Tricuspid valve events were defined as ≥moderate sTR, or interval tricuspid valve replacement (TVR). Generally, the earliest and most recent studies were compared. A subset of patients were followed with ≥moderate sTR, and then subsequently underwent interval TVR. For these patients, two study time intervals were defined to analyze the impact of each event independently.ResultsSixty-seven patients were studied (33 ± 11 years, 48% [32/67] male, 33% [22/67] ccTGA), with 72 total time intervals studied (median interval 9.0 years [interquartile range 4.6–13.3]). There was a small increase in sRVEDVi over time (ΔsRVEDVi 5.5 ± 15.8 mL/m2, p < 0.001), but mean change in sRVEF was not significant (ΔsRVEF 0.1 ± 6.9%, p = 0.86); notably, confidence intervals (CI) were wide for both. ccTGA patients had a trend toward greater decrement in sRVEF (ΔsRVEF −1.7 ± 6.8 vs 1.3 ± 6.7%, p = 0.06). For each 25 mL/m2 increase in baseline sRVEDVi, there was a 1.8% decrease in sRVEF (95% CI −3.2% to −0.5%, p = 0.01). Patients without significant sTR had lesser deterioration in sRVEF compared to those with ≥moderate sTR or with interval TR intervention (ΔsRVEF 1.8 ± 6.9% vs −2.1 ± 6.6% and −2.6 ± 4.5, p < 0.05). Interval sRV conduction delay was associated with a trend toward greater decrements in sRVEF (ΔsRVEF −3.9 ± 6.3 vs 0.9 ± 6.8%, p = 0.07). Overall, underlying congenital anatomy, baseline sRVEDVi, advanced sTR or interval TVR, and sRV conduction delay explained only 16% of the variability in ΔsRVEF over time.ConclusionLongitudinal changes in sRV remodeling were small, with great heterogeneity. Apparent risk factors in our study, namely underlying congenital anatomy, baseline sRVEDVi, TR events, and sRV conduction disease accounted for only 16% of the variability seen in the longitudinal change of sRVEF.Keywords: Systemic right ventricle, Transposition of the great arteries, Congenital heart disease, Adult congenital heart disease, Tricuspid regurgitation, Cardiovascular magnetic resonance

Introduction

In congenital heart disease, a systemic right ventricle (sRV) occurs in patients with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (ccTGA) and in patients post atrial switch operation for D-TGA. The physiology is defined by a morphologic right ventricle (RV) supporting the systemic circulation, with resultant hypertrophy and dilatation that is initially adaptive [1]. Although the natural history is of sRV myocardial dysfunction progressing to end-stage heart failure, arrhythmia, or death [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], the rate of disease progression is highly variable, with some patients showing remarkable stability into their fifth–sixth decades.

Risk factors for early sRV failure are poorly understood. Strategies to mitigate sRV deterioration have targeted suspected risk factors, including systemic tricuspid valve replacement (TVR) for significant regurgitation [8], [9], resynchronization therapy for sRV conduction disease [10], [11], and use of guideline-directed medical therapies typically applied for the failing systemic left ventricle (LV) [12], [13], [14], [15], each with mixed results. A greater understanding of typical longitudinal remodeling in sRV patients is needed to provide guidance on clinical prognosis and to guide optimal timing for interventions in patients undergoing close clinical monitoring.

Prior studies relating serial assessments of sRV function to clinical outcomes have focused on echocardiography [2], [3]. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) offers distinct advantages over echo, as it provides a highly reproducible assessment of sRV structure and function unconstrained by complex RV geometry or imaging windows [16], [17], [18], [19]. To date, no study has assessed changes in sRV remodeling as by CMR. We sought to assess longitudinal sRV remodeling on CMR in relation to suspected sRV risk factors at two large adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) centers.

Methods

Study population

The population comprised adults (≥18 years old) with sRV in the context of ccTGA or following atrial switch repair of D-TGA, followed at Columbia University Irving Medical Center between December 1999 and April 2023, and the Amsterdam University Medical Center between December 1999 and November 2020, with serial CMR imaging. Only patients with complete CMR data were included. Demographic data were obtained from the clinical record. Electrocardiograms were reviewed for assessment of sRV conduction disease; defined as a QRS duration >120 ms with right bundle branch block morphology in D-TGA, or a “left bundle branch block” morphology in ccTGA patients with normal situs. Patients also underwent routine echocardiography, used to assess changes in systemic tricuspid regurgitation (sTR) over time.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of both institutions prior to data acquisition (IRB AAB2732, approved April 18, 2024). There is no funding to declare.

Image acquisition

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance

CMR studies were performed with breath holding and electrocardiogram gating using General Electric (Milwaukee, Wisconsin) or Siemens (Erlangen, Germany) 1.5T scanners. Short-axis cine images were acquired using a balanced steady-state free precession pulse sequence in nearly all cases, with the following typical parameters: TR 3.6 ms, echo time 11.5 ms, flip angle of 45°, 24 views-per-segment, field of view 35 cm, acquisition matrix 192 × 160, slice thickness 8 mm with no gap, and receiver bandwidth 125 kHz. Spoiled gradient echo cine imaging was used in most baseline scans performed before 2004 (n = 10).

Echocardiography

Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiograms were performed using commercial equipment. Exams included evaluation of the sRV and sTR in parasternal and apical views according to the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography [20]. Tricuspid regurgitation severity was estimated by an ACHD cardiologist based on color Doppler based on the width and length of the regurgitant jet and the Doppler flow pattern in the pulmonary veins [5].

Image analysis

Cardiac MRI

Image analysis was performed using clinically available software (CVi42, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Calgary, Canada). Image analysis was performed by a single reader (J.K.), for whom reproducibility has previously been validated [18]. Image analyses were blinded to clinical history, but the prior study within serial study pairs could be referenced to facilitate consistency in contouring approach.

Cine loops were used to select images at end-diastole and end-systole. Endocardial segmentation was performed by manual tracing of each end-diastolic and end-systolic short-axis slice and used to calculate RV and LV volumes. Measurements were indexed to body surface area to yield indexed sRV end-diastolic (sRVEDVi) and end-systolic volumes (sRVESVi). sRV ejection fraction (sRVEF) was calculated from end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes. Epicardial segmentation was performed by manual tracing of each end-diastolic short-axis frame of the RV and used to calculate sRV mass using a myocardial specific gravity of 1.05 g/cm3. Major trabeculations and papillary muscles were excluded from the blood pool using semi-automatic pixel-intensity–based software with manual correction, as has previously been shown to be reproducible (Fig. 1B) [21]. In accord with prior studies, the septum was considered part of RV mass [17], [18].

Statistical analysis

Generally, the earliest and most recent CMR were compared. A subset of patients were followed with significant (≥moderate) sTR, and then subsequently underwent interval TVR. For these patients, two study time intervals were defined as outlined in Fig. 1A, so as to analyze the impact of each event independently.

Continuous variables are summarized as means ± standard deviations when normally distributed, and otherwise as medians [interquartile range (IQR)]. Categorical variables are summarized as frequencies and percentages. Normally distributed continuous indices were compared via Student’s t-tests (two-group comparisons) or analysis of variance (ANOVA) (multiple-group comparisons). Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to test associations between baseline parameters and longitudinal sRV remodeling. Linear regression analysis was used to test associations between sRV risk factors and change in sRVEF at follow-up. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical calculations were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois ).

Results

Population characteristics

Sixty-seven patients were included in the study cohort (33 ± 11 years, 48% male [32/67]); 22/67 patients (33%) were ccTGA and 45/67 (67%) were D-TGA. Baseline imaging characteristics are also shown. ccTGA patients were more likely to have ≥moderate sTR by echocardiographic analysis (46% vs 20%, p = 0.03), and had a trend toward greater sRVEDVi (123.6 ± 23.5 vs 111.4 ± 27.9 mL/m2, p = 0.08).

Longitudinal changes

Overall population

Seventy-two total study time intervals were studied. The median time between CMR was 9.0 years (IQR 4.6–13.3, range 0.9–19.7 years). Mean changes in sRV remodeling were small, with wide variability (Table 2); there was a significant mean increase in sRV size (ΔsRVEDVi 5.5 ± 15.8 mL/m2, p < 0.001 | ΔsRVESVi 3.8 ± 13.8 mL/m2, p < 0.01), but mean change in sRVEF was not significant (ΔsRVEF 0.1 ± 6.9%, p = 0.86). D-TGA and ccTGA patients had similar interstudy time intervals (10.0 ± 5.1 vs 8.2 ± 4.8 years, p = 0.14). When compared to D-TGA patients, ccTGA patients had similar increase in sRV diastolic volumes (ΔsRVEDVi 5.4 ± 18.3 vs 5.6 ± 14.1 mL/m2, p = 0.96), but a trend toward greater decrement in sRVEF (ΔsRVEF −1.7 ± 6.8 vs 1.3 ± 6.7%, p = 0.06).

Age at first CMR did not correlate with change in RV remodeling (p = NS). Time duration between CMR studies correlated with interval change in sRV mass (r = 0.3, p < 0.01), but not with other markers of sRV remodeling (p = NS for all). Thus, sRV remodeling indexes were not indexed to interstudy interval duration for subsequent analyses.

Overall, a significant decrease in sRVEF (≥5%) occurred in 19% of patients (n = 14/72). However, this was countered by a significant increase in sRVEF (≥5%) in 20% (n = 15/72). A significant increase in sRVEDVi (≥25% baseline sRVEDVi) was seen in 12% (n = 9/72).

Systemic tricuspid valve

Patients were stratified by sTR status during the interstudy interval: (1) no significant sTR, (2) ≥moderate sTR present during the interstudy interval, (3) TVR performed during the interstudy interval. As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 3, sTR status was associated with ΔsRVEF but not ΔsRV volumes; with lesser deterioration in sRVEF occurring for patients without sTR (ΔsRVEF 1.8 ± 6.9%) compared to those with ≥moderate sTR (ΔsRVEF −2.1 ± 6.6%) or with interval TVR (ΔsRVEF −2.6 ± 4.5, ANOVA p < 0.05). Among patients with >5% reduction in sRVEF, 8/14 (57%) had interval ≥moderate sTR or interval TVR, compared to 2/15 (13%) of pateints with >5% improvement in sRVEF (p = 0.01). Mean RVEF at time of TVR was 40 ± 6.8% (range 32%–50%), with 4/7 surgeries performed in patients with sRVEF <40%.

Systemic right ventricular conduction delay

As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 4, interval sRV conduction delay was associated with greater increases in sRV size compared to patients with no or prior conduction delay (ΔsRVEDVi 17.7 ± 19.6 vs 4.1 ± 15.3 mL/m2, p = 0.03), with a trend toward greater decrements in sRV ejection (ΔsRVEF −3.9 ± 6.3 vs 0.9 ± 6.8%, p = 0.07). Among patients with >5% reduction in sRVEF, 4/14 (29%) had interval sRV conduction delay, compared to 0/15 (0%) of pateints with >5% improvement in sRVEF (p = 0.04). While prior sRV conduction delay was not associated with an interval change in sRV size or EF (p = NS for both), prior sRV conduction delay was associated with trend toward greater sRV volumes (sRVEDVi 133.5 ± 37.7 vs 114.5 ± 26.5 mL/m2, p = 0.08) and lesser sRVEF (sRVEF 37.4 ± 3.7 vs 41.3 ± 8.3%, p = 0.06) at the baseline scan. Of note, no patients in the study had pacemakers placed before or during the interstudy interval.

Additional risk factors and medications

As shown in Table 3, interval pregnancy was not associated with differential changes in sRV size or ejection (p = NS). Findings were similar when males were included from the analysis such that females with interval pregnancy were compared to females without (p = NS). Mean baseline sRVEF for women undergoing interval pregnancy was 42.7 ± 7.1% (range 33.0–55.6%), with 50% (5/10) having a pre-pregnancy sRVEF <40%. Likewise, anatomic complexity, as defined by the congenital ventricular septal defect or pulmonary stenosis, did not modify change in sRV size or ejection (p = NS). With respect to interval medication initiation (Table 4), initiation of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibition was not associated with change in sRV size or ejection (p = NS), whereas initiation of beta blockade was associated with relative improvement in sRVEF (ΔsRVEF 3.0 ± 7.7 vs −0.8 ± 6.3%, p = 0.04).

Multivariable modeling

A multivariable model was developed with ccTGA anatomy, baseline sRVEDVi, significant sTR or interval TVR, and interval sRV conduction delay as independent variables, and ΔsRVEF as the dependent variable (Table 5). The overall model was predictive of ΔsRVEF (p = 0.02), with an R2 of 0.16, indicating that variance in underlying anatomy, baseline sRVEDVi, sTR status, and sRV conduction disease explained only 16% of the variance observed in ΔsRVEF.

Discussion

This study yields novel insights regarding longitudinal RV remodeling in relation to various suspected risk factors for sRV deterioration in adult patients with ccTGA or D-TGA following atrial switch repair. Key findings are as follows: (1) Over a median of 9.0 years of follow-up, there was a small statistical increase in sRV volumes with no change in sRVEF, although a subset of patients experienced a significant decrease of ≥5% in sRVEF (19% of patients) and a significant increase in sRVEDVi of ≥25% from baseline (12% of patients). (2) Baseline RV size correlated with change in sRVEF with a 1.8% decrease in sRVEF for each 25 mL/m2 increase in baseline sRVEDVi. (3) Patients without significant sTR had lesser deterioration in sRVEF compared to those with ≥moderate sTR or with interval TVR (4). Interval sRV conduction delay was associated with a trend toward greater decrements in sRV ejection fraction. (5) In multivariable modeling, underlying congenital anatomy, baseline sRVEDVi, sTR, and sRV conduction delay explained only 16% of the variability in change in sRVEF over time.

Many prior studies have highlighted the utility of sRV geometry and function for risk prognostication. Recently, two large multicenter cohorts have investigated clinical outcomes among patients with ccTGA [2] and D-TGA post atrial switch repair [3], with each implicating severe RV dysfunction by echocardiography as independent risk factors for adverse outcomes. However, echocardiographic assessments of sRV function are challenged by limited reproducibility [19], which constrains echo as a tool for following sRV remodeling both clinically and for investigating risk factors for sRV deterioration. CMR provides a reproducible, imaging-window independent, and geometric-assumption free assessment of sRV structure [16], [17], [18], [19] and has been increasingly used for longitudinal monitoring of sRV remodeling. Accordingly, prior CMR data from our group [18] and others [2], [3] have demonstrated CMR-based sRV volumes and EF to be associated with death, heart transplant, or ventricular assist device. Our study is the first to define CMR-derived changes over long-term follow-up in an adult cohort of patients with sRV physiology.

Our finding that sRVEF did not progressively decline over 9.0 years of follow-up is seemingly in contradiction with long-held notions of the natural history of sRV, and with prior data derived from echocardiography [2], [3]. Other longitudinal studies in patients with ccTGA and D-TGA have suggested that deterioration in sRV function occurs in a subset of patients over the first three decades [22], [23], with subsequent stabilization of cohort prevalence of sRV dysfunction until the sixth decade [22]. Therefore, the older age of patients in our adult sRV cohort may impact observed longitudinal trends. Our finding of wide heterogeneity in sRV remodeling over time, with deterioration in some patients but improvement in others, is also consistent with prior assessments using nuclear-multigated acquisition (MUGA) scans; a cohort of 48 ccTGA and 76 D-TGA patients studied with serial MUGA [24] showed no significant change in sRVEF or RVEDVi over 4.9–5.8 years of follow-up, but a sRVEF decline of >5% was noted in 33%–36% and increase in sRVEDVi in 33%–44%, implying improvements in other patients of similar magnitude. Taken together, these findings may indicate fluctuations in sRVEF, perhaps related to loading conditions, which could limit the utility of longitudinal sRV volumetric analysis as a marker of sRV prognosis. Our finding that change in sRVEF related to baseline sRVEDVi further supports the concept of a load-sensitive ventricle. Echocardiographic RV longitudinal strain, a less load-dependent parameter, has recently been shown to demonstrate progressive decline in a cohort of ccTGA patients [25], and may have adjunctive utility.

Systemic tricuspid regurgitation

sTR has long been implicated in sRV deterioration, particularly among patients with ccTGA in whom structural tricuspid valve abnormalities are highly prevalent [5]. While sTR has been associated with sRV dysfunction and poor clinical outcomes [2], [3], [26], whether the regurgitation is causative of, or resultant from the sRV deterioration is incompletely elucidated. For example, prior work from our group studying sRV patients with ccTGA or D-TGA demonstrated no association between sTR severity and CMR-derived sRV volume or EF, suggesting that sTR and sRV remodeling can occur independently [27]. Correspondingly, among a cohort of 186 ccTGA patients, Egbe et al. demonstrated >moderate sTR to be associated with temporal decline of RV longitudinal strain in univariable, but not multivariable analysis [25]. The impact of TVR is similarly controversial. Prior studies focused on ccTGA patients have shown improvement [25] or stabilization [8], [9], [28] of sRV function when TVR was performed before sRV deterioration, with excellent surgical outcomes in this context [9]. However, other studies have not demonstrated a definitive relationship between early TVR with improved survival or sRV function [3]. In our cohort, ≥moderate sTR and TVR were not associated with significant changes in sRV volumes, but were associated with interval decrements in sRVEF. Whether findings would have been different if baseline sRVEF were greater at time of TVR (4/7 cases were in patients with sRVEF <40%) is uncertain.

Systemic right ventricular conduction delay

LV conduction disease is a well-established risk marker for ventricular deterioration among patients with a systemic LV and has been successfully targeted with resynchronization therapy [29], supporting a causative role of LV conduction delay in the pathophysiology. sRV conduction delay has similarly been linked to adverse remodeling in both D-TGA [3], [30] and ccTGA [31] cohorts, and has been associated with sRV fibrosis [32], [33]. Inconsistent results of resynchronization in sRV patients with conduction delay [11], [25], [34], [35] raise the question of a true pathophysiologic link with sRV deterioration, although this interpretation is confounded by challenges in determining and executing optimal sRV lead placement. Our data corroborates prior findings, with trend toward more advanced sRV remodeling at baseline among patients with preexisting sRV conduction delay, and adds to the literature by demonstrating progressive sRV dilatation and dysfunction among patients with interval, but not preexisting conduction delay. Such findings raise questions as to whether conduction disease represents a marker, rather than a driver of sRV remodeling in some patients.

The increased cardiac demands and circulating blood volume during pregnancy may contribute to sRV volume loading and augmented risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes [36], [37], [38], which has been related to impaired sRV function as assessed by CMR [38]. Prior echocardiography data have suggested sRV dilatation and dysfunction after pregnancy in some patients that did not recover [39], though other data show no clear sRV deterioration following pregnancy [36], or similar deterioration compared to matched controls [40]. Our cohort demonstrated no association of interval pregnancy with longitudinal changes in sRV remodeling, despite 50% of patients having a reduced pre-pregnancy sRVEF <40%.

Limitations

Several limitations must be noted. First, sTR severity was assessed by echocardiography, rather than by quantitative CMR-based assessment due to lack of comprehensive phase contrast imaging in all studies. However, this approach is concordant with that used in large, multicenter registry cohorts [2], [3], and prior studies have shown good correlation between echocardiographic and CMR-based assessment of TR [41]. The population size of this multicenter cohort limits statistical power to answer important questions in this heterogeneous population. For example, impact of sTR on sRV remodeling may differ between patients with D-TGA and ccTGA due to distinct mechanisms of TR. However, this is currently the only CMR study to investigate longitudinal changes in sRV remodeling, and we hope that it will inform future investigations with larger sample size and longer follow-up. As this study follows patients longitudinally over an extended follow-up period, advanced CMR techniques such as four-dimensional flow and parametric mapping are not universally available, and late gadolinium enhancement imaging was not routinely performed. Importantly, while CMR in patients with pacemakers and defibrillators has become routine in recent years, this was not common practice during the study timeframe. Thus, important questions regarding the impact of LV pacing and resynchronization therapy on longitudinal remodeling could not be addressed. As a retrospective analysis, the impact of clinical interventions such as TVR and medication initiation on longitudinal remodeling are confounded by indications that may not be apparent from review of the CMR alone—for example suggestion of deterioration in sRV function on an interval echocardiogram. Finally, relating change in sRV remodeling to clinical outcomes is a critically important step in our advancing prognostication and management of patients with sRV. Our study design was optimized to investigate longitudinal changes in sRV remodeling by maximizing the interstudy interval, and thus clinical follow-up time is extremely limited in the majority of our patients. Future prospective trials are warranted.

Conclusion

In conclusion, identifying which patients with sRV are at risk for accelerated adverse remodeling is challenged by great heterogeneity in this patient population. Apparent risk factors in our study, namely underlying congenital anatomy, baseline sRV end-diastolic volume, advanced sTR or interval TVR, and interval sRV conduction disease accounted for only 16% of the variance seen in the longitudinal change sRV ejection fraction over a median 9-year follow-up. While several potential mechanisms implicated in sRV deterioration, such as coronary supply-demand mismatch due to single coronary perfusion of the systemic ventricle [42], sRV fibrosis [43], and sRV afterload [25], [44] are not accounted for in this cohort, other inherent factors such as underlying genetic predisposition to RV structural abnormalities [45] likely play a significant role in determining the morphologic RV response to the systemic circulation and warrant further study.
