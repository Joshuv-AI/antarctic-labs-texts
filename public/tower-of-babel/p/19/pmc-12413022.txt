Preoperative intensity‐modulated radiation therapy in lower extremity soft tissue sarcomas with and without dose avoidance of uninvolved skin/subcutaneous tissue
Authors: Siyer Roohani, Anthony M. Griffin, Zhihui Amy Liu, Charles N. Catton, Colleen I. Dickie, Peter C. Ferguson, David G. Kirsch, Brian O’Sullivan, David B. Shultz, Kim M. Tsoi, Philip Wong, Jay S. Wunder, Peter W. M. Chung
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe objective of this study was to evaluate whether dosimetric sparing of uninvolved normal tissues, including skin/subcutaneous flaps, affects acute and late toxicities in preoperative image‐guided intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IG‐IMRT) for lower extremity soft tissue sarcomas (LE‐STS).MethodsPatients with LE‐STS from a phase 2 preoperative IG‐IMRT trial (flap‐sparing‐IMRT, 2005–2009) and a prospectively maintained institutional database (standard‐IMRT, 2005–2020) were propensity matched by age, sex, tumor size, grade, location, wound closure, and interval from IG‐IMRT to surgery; all received 50 Gy in 25 fractions preoperatively. The primary outcome was major wound complication (MWC). Secondary outcomes were late Radiation Therapy Oncology Group toxicities, functional scores (Toronto Extremity Salvage Score [TESS]; Musculoskeletal Tumor Society scales [MSTS‐87 or MSTS‐93]), and oncologic outcomes. Kaplan–Meier estimates, cumulative incidence functions, and linear, logistic, and Cox regression were used, as appropriate.ResultsFifty‐five patients who received flap‐sparing‐IMRT were 1:5 matched to 275 patients who received standard‐IMRT (median follow‐up, 104 vs. 56 months, respectively). Regression analyses identified no significant association between treatment technique and MWCs (29% vs. 27%; odds ratio, 0.92; p = .77), late grade 2 or greater toxicities: subcutaneous fibrosis (15% vs. 8%), joint stiffness (5% vs. 2%), edema (11% vs. 10%), fracture (2% vs. 4%), or functional outcomes (TESS, 87 vs. 89; MSTS‐87, 33 vs. 33; and MSTS‐93, 93 vs. 97; all p > .1). Five‐year overall survival (83.6% vs. 75.2%), disease‐free survival (65.5% vs. 64.1%), local recurrence (5.2% vs. 7.3%), distant metastasis (29.1% vs. 30.1%) were also comparable (p > .1).ConclusionsFlap‐sparing‐IMRT with specific avoidance of uninvolved skin/subcutaneous tissues demonstrated minimal differences in MWCs, late toxicity, and functional and oncologic outcomes compared with standard‐IMRT in patients with LE‐STS.

INTRODUCTION

Perioperative radiotherapy (RT) improves local tumor control in soft tissue sarcoma of the extremities and trunk wall. 1 , 2 The randomized National Cancer Institute of Canada SR2 (NCIC‐SR2) trial, comparing preoperative versus postoperative RT, reported a two‐fold increase in acute, reversible major wound complications (MWCs) with preoperative RT but a higher incidence of late, irreversible toxicity with postoperative RT. 3 , 4 MWC was defined as the need for a secondary operation under general/regional anesthesia for wound repair or nonoperative wound management, such as invasive procedures, readmission for care, or persistent deep packing ≥120 days, assessed cumulatively within 4 months postsurgery. 4 As oncologic outcomes were equivalent, and given the toxicity profile—greater acute but reversible toxicity with preoperative RT and increased late, irreversible, and functionally impactful toxicity with postoperative RT—preoperative RT is preferred. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 The trial used conventional two‐dimensional RT (2D‐RT) for all patients during accrual from 1994 to 1997. 4 Although intensity‐modulated RT (IMRT) was first described in 1982, its widespread adoption in the United States became evident around 2004. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 As IMRT gained popularity, efforts were made to optimize its use in reducing treatment‐related toxicities. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 This led to a prospective phase 2 study investigating preoperative image‐guided IMRT (IG‐IMRT) to improve toxicity outcomes through normal tissue sparing in patients with curable lower extremity soft tissue sarcomas. 12 The primary outcome was MWCs as defined in the NCIC‐SR2 trial. 4 , 12 In addition, RT avoidance structures, including uninvolved skin and subcutaneous tissue at the future surgical closure site, were defined. This virtual skin flap region, encompassing the incision site and future scar, was delineated and spared during treatment planning (flap‐sparing‐IMRT), when feasible. However, target coverage remained the priority; if the planning target volume (PTV) overlapped with the virtual flap sparing was not prioritized. The preoperative flap‐sparing IG‐IMRT procedure resulted in a 30.5% MWC rate and reduced late toxicities, both of which were numerically lower than in the NCIC‐SR2 trial but did not reach statistical significance. In the current study, we evaluated the effect of the flap‐sparing‐IMRT technique on both acute and late toxicities, as well as functional and oncologic outcomes, in a contemporary cohort of patients who received preoperative IG‐IMRT for lower extremity soft tissue sarcomas.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patient selection

The prospective, single‐center, phase 2 IG‐IMRT trial evaluated the use of preoperative IG‐IMRT with flap‐sparing in patients with histopathologically confirmed lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma between 2005 and 2009. 12 To assess the impact of flap‐sparing‐IMRT, these patients were propensity‐matched to a cohort with lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma who underwent preoperative IG‐IMRT without flap‐sparing (standard‐IMRT) between 2005 and 2020. Their data were obtained from a prospectively collected institutional database at the same center that participated in the prospective trial.

Treatment methods

The flap‐sparing approach and common treatment methods used in both cohorts have been previously described. 12 Both cohorts received RT at 50 Gy in 25 fractions over 5 weeks followed by surgery 4–8 weeks after completion of IG‐IMRT. The dose was prescribed to the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurement reference point with PTV coverage goals of 95% of the dose and a maximum dose limit of 107% according to International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurement Report 50. 13 No bolus was used. IG‐IMRT was delivered using daily cone‐beam computed tomography (CT) with three‐dimensional (3D) alignment and automated bone‐matching. A 0.5‐cm expansion from the clinical target volume (CTV) to the PTV was used. PTVs were intentionally restricted from extending to the skin surface, and a standard 5‐mm pull‐back from the skin surface was applied. As outlined above, PTV coverage was prioritized for patients in whom an overlap with the flap region occurred.

In the flap‐sparing‐IMRT cohort, each patient’s dose‐avoidance structures—including the future surgical skin/subcutaneous flaps—were collaboratively delineated directly by the surgical oncologist with the aid of the radiation oncologist based on radio‐opaque markers on the RT planning CT. In the standard‐IMRT cohort, this collaborative delineation of surgical flaps was not performed. All other treatment parameters were consistent between cohorts.

Propensity score matching

Each patient who received flap‐sparing‐IMRT was matched with five patients who received standard‐IMRT. Nearest‐neighbor matching on the propensity score without replacement was used, and the score was estimated using logistic regression, including age, sex, tumor size, grade, disease location, surgical wound closure, and time from the end of RT to surgery as covariates. R package MatchIt was used for propensity score matching (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). 14

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the MWC rate, as previously defined. 4 Secondary outcomes were late Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) toxicity (subcutaneous fibrosis, joint stiffness, edema, bone fracture), functional outcomes (on the Toronto Extremity Salvage Score [TESS], and the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society scales [MSTS‐87 and MSTS‐93]), and oncologic outcomes. 4 , 15 , 16 , 17 Oncologic time‐to‐event end points of interest were overall survival (OS), disease‐free survival (DFS), the time to local recurrence (LR), and the time to distant metastasis (DM). OS was defined as the period from surgery to death from any cause. DFS was defined as the time from surgery to either disease recurrence at any site or death from any cause. LR was defined as the time from surgery to local recurrence, with death as a competing risk. DM was defined as the period from surgery to distant disease recurrence, with death as a competing risk. Patients were censored at the last available follow‐up if no disease recurrence or death had occurred. Follow‐up was defined as the time from surgery to the last clinical contact. For descriptive statistics, ranges, means, medians, and standard deviations (SDs) were reported for continuous variables. OS and DFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and LR and DM were estimated using the cumulative incidence function.

Linear regression was used to compare continuous outcomes (TESS, MSTS‐87, and MSTS‐93 scores), logistic regression was used for binary outcomes (MWC, yes vs. no; RTOG subcutaneous fibrosis grade ≥2; joint stiffness grade ≥2; edema grade ≥2; and bone fracture, yes vs. no), and Cox regression analysis was used for time‐to‐event outcomes (OS, DFS, LR, and DM). All regression analyses were conducted with robust variance estimation. All statistical analyses and figure designs were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1). 18 A p value ≤ .05 was considered statistically significant. The study received Institutional Review Board approval (no. 18‐5689).

RESULTS

Patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics

After excluding four patients because of missing data, the flap‐sparing‐IMRT group consisted of 55 patients. These patients were matched in a 1:5 ratio with 275 patients from the standard‐IMRT cohort. The patient demographics and treatment characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The covariates age, sex, tumor size, grade, surgical wound closure, and time from the end of RT to surgery were balanced between the matched cohorts. The median age at surgery in the flap‐sparing‐IMRT and the standard‐IMRT group was 53 and 57 years, respectively. All tumors were located in the lower extremities and were treated with preoperative RT at a dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions without postoperative boost irradiation or perioperative chemotherapy.

Functional outcomes

The latest median ± SD TESS score was 87 ± 19 (range, 2–100) in the flap‐sparing‐IMRT cohort and 89 ± 17 (range, 12–100) in the standard‐IMRT cohort, with missing data in nine (16.4%) and 91 (33%) patients, respectively. The latest median ± SD MSTS‐87 score was 33 ± 4 (range, 21–35) and 33 ± 4 (range, 12–35) in the flap‐sparing‐ and standard‐IMRT cohorts, respectively, with missing data in nine (16.4%) and 91 (33%) patients, respectively. The latest median ± SD MSTS‐93 score was 93 ± 14 (range, 43–100) and 97 ± 15 (range, 21–100), in the flap‐sparing‐ and standard‐IMRT cohorts, respectively, with missing data in 12 (21.8%) and 97 (35.3%) patients, respectively. Standard‐IMRT was not significantly associated with differences in the latest TESS score (estimate, 1.79; p = .55), MSTS‐87 score (estimate, −0.47; p = .46), or MSTS‐93 score (estimate, −0.64; p = .79).

Oncologic outcomes

The median OS was not reached in either cohort, with 5‐year OS rates of 83.6% and 75.2% in the flap‐sparing‐IMRT and standard‐IMRT cohorts, respectively (Figure S1). The median DFS was 12.8 years in the flap‐sparing‐IMRT cohort and was not reached in the standard‐IMRT cohort, with 5‐year DFS rates of 65.5% and 64.1%, respectively (Figure S2). The median time to LR was not reached in either group, with 5‐year rates of 5.2% and 7.3% in the flap‐sparing‐ and standard‐IMRT cohorts, respectively (Figure S3). The median time to DM was 12.8 years in the flap‐sparing‐IMRT cohort and was not reached in the standard‐IMRT cohort, with 5‐year rates of 29.1% and 30.1%, respectively (Figure S4). Standard‐IMRT was not significantly associated with worse OS (hazard ratio [HR], 1.25; p = .40), DFS (HR, 1.02; p = .91), LR (HR, 0.86; p = .80), or DM (HR, 1.05; p = .83).

DISCUSSION

In this study of patients with lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma undergoing preoperative IG‐IMRT, we compared patients who underwent dosimetric sparing of uninvolved normal tissues, including the skin/subcutaneous flaps (flap‐sparing‐IMRT) from a previously published phase 2 trial to matched patients who received standard‐IMRT with data prospectively collected from the same sarcoma center. 12 No significant differences were observed in MWCs rates, late toxicities, or functional outcomes. Furthermore, oncologic endpoints were also comparable.

Acute toxicity

The similar MWC rates using IG‐IMRT with and without flap‐sparing presented herein yield important conclusions. First, flap‐sparing‐IMRT is unlikely to be the sole or primary factor driving MWCs. Importantly, after matching patients who had similar clinical characteristics associated with MWCs (tumor size, time to surgery, RT dosing and sequence, tumor location, surgical wound closure), no significant association between flap‐sparing‐IMRT and MWC rates was observed. 12 However, a more detailed dosimetric comparison could reveal subtle differences between the plans that have yet to translate into clinical outcomes, warranting further investigation in future studies. Second, IG‐IMRT does appear to reduce MWC rates compared with the 42.9% rate with preoperative 2D‐RT for lower extremity soft tissue sarcomas. 4 , 12 This finding is supported by the prospective phase 2 IMRiS trial (Intensity‐Modulated Radiotherapy in Sarcoma), which reported a 29.2% MWC rate in a cohort with 81.8% lower extremity tumors. Although the only other prospective study to examine this issue (the RTOG 0630 trial) reported a 41.9% rate in lower extremity tumors, one quarter of those patients received 3D conformal RT (3D‐CRT). 19 , 20 The avoidance of dose to subcutaneous tissues, whether directly through a flap‐sparing process or indirectly through higher conformality and dose restriction to superficial tissues, used in current IMRT techniques may well reduce the incidence of MWCs. This should include reducing the use of bolus except where clinically indicated. Late toxicity and functional outcomes

Although the flap‐sparing‐IMRT cohort had numerically lower rates of late toxicities (subcutaneous fibrosis, joint stiffness, and edema), these differences are likely caused by the rarity of these events, with the small number of occurrences making it difficult to draw meaningful conclusions. Regression analyses indicated no significant association between flap‐sparing‐IMRT and these outcomes. Despite the large 1:5 propensity‐matched sample size, an even larger cohort could potentially reveal differences. However, the high‐quality, well matched, and prospectively collected data from cohorts at the same institution as the prospective trial strongly suggest that flap‐sparing‐IMRT is unlikely to have a clinically significant effect on these toxicities. Moreover, functional outcomes, as measured by TESS and MSTS scores, were similar between both cohorts, further supporting the absence of a clinically meaningful difference.

As discussed above, subtle dosimetric differences may exist between plans, but these have yet to translate into clinical outcomes, warranting further investigation in future studies. Importantly, the flap‐sparing techniques investigated in the previous phase 2 trial may have influenced how IMRT is currently planned and delivered. Modern treatment planning often involves CTVs and PTVs that are intentionally restricted from the skin surface, effectively replicating flap‐sparing principles without explicitly delineating surgical flaps. This evolution in technique—which was absent at the time of the original phase 2 flap‐sparing study—may partly explain the observed similarities in outcomes because current IMRT practice inherently limits the dose to subcutaneous tissues.

Although flap‐sparing‐IMRT did not significantly affect late toxicity, the use of IG‐IMRT in general has brought considerable improvements compared with previous techniques. In this study, preoperative IG‐IMRT demonstrated a more favorable late toxicity profile (grade 2 or greater [≥G2] subcutaneous fibrosis, 8% and 15%; ≥G2 joint stiffness, 2% and 5%; ≥G2 edema, 10% and 11%) compared with significantly higher rates observed with data from patients who received 2D‐RT (≥G2 fibrosis, 31.5%; ≥G2 joint stiffness, 17.8%; ≥G2 edema, 15.1%). 3 Although flap‐sparing‐IMRT did not significantly improve wound complications or late toxicity, the benefits of IG‐IMRT in reducing these adverse effects are evident. This is further supported by the IMRiS trial and RTOG 0630, which reported 11% and 5.3% ≥G2 fibrosis, respectively; 9% and 3.5% ≥G2 joint stiffness, respectively; and 5% and 5.3% ≥G2 edema, respectively. 19 , 20 An additional factor contributing to these improvements, compared with the NCIC‐SR2 trial, is the use of daily image guidance during RT in all subsequent trials. Furthermore, the reduction in CTV margins in the RTOG 0630 trial may have contributed to the low late toxicity rates compared with all other studies. 20

Oncologic outcomes

Our results demonstrate no differences from flap‐sparing‐IMRT in terms of oncologic outcomes. The LR rates with flap‐sparing‐IMRT and standard‐IMRT were consistent with previous studies. 4 , 19 , 20 Similarly, the OS, DFS, and DM rates observed in this study align with previous research using perioperative IG‐IMRT for localized soft tissue sarcoma and were not anticipated to be affected by flap‐sparing‐IMRT. 19 , 20

Future perspectives

As we strive to further improve local outcomes in soft tissue sarcomas, it is essential to explore potential strategies that could enhance tumor control while minimizing toxicity.

With modern perioperative IG‐IMRT and surgical excision, local tumor control has reached 90%–95%, and oncologic outcomes are comparable between preoperative and postoperative RT, suggesting limited potential for further improvement in this regard. 4 , 19 , 20 However, efforts to reduce local toxicity are ongoing. One approach under investigation is lowering the postoperative RT dose to 50 Gy for patients who have clear surgical margins. 21 Furthermore, reducing CTV margins without compromising local control may be another strategy to reduce toxicity, as was done in RTOG 0630. 20 These findings suggest that further research into margin optimization could enhance treatment outcomes. Adaptive RT techniques have demonstrated both tumor coverage and toxicity benefits in other disease sites and may emerge as an area of interest in soft tissue sarcomas. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 In addition, advances in surgical care, such as the implementation of multimodal perioperative care pathway (enhanced recovery after surgery) protocols, appear to demonstrate significant improvements in MWC in patients who have extremity soft tissue sarcoma and receive preoperative RT. 28

Limitations

One of the major limitations of our study is its retrospective nature, specifically, propensity matching; although it may substantially reduce bias, there undoubtedly remains an imbalance between the cohorts that cannot be eliminated except by well conducted, randomized, controlled trials. In our current study, one potential source of such bias may have been the difference between rates of closure technique. In addition, a thorough dosimetric comparison focusing on tumor coverage and dose distribution in the flap region is necessary to fully assess the effect of flap‐sparing‐IMRT, this was not feasible to complete because of the large number of patients in the standard‐IMRT cohort, nor was it within the scope of the current study. Differences in the years during which the flap‐sparing‐ and standard‐IMRT cohorts were treated may introduce bias because advancements in surgical techniques, perioperative care, and RT could have influenced outcomes over time.

Conclusions

The current results suggest that, in lower extremity soft tissue sarcoma, there was no difference in MWCs, late toxicity, or functional outcomes between cohorts who received IMRT with avoidance of skin/subcutaneous flaps compared with a cohort that received modern preoperative IG‐IMRT. The reasons for this are not entirely clear but may relate to the evolution of IMRT planning, in which current practices often inherently spare superficial tissues even without explicit flap delineation. A detailed dosimetric comparison may uncover subtle differences between the plans that have yet to translate into clinical outcomes. In addition, our analysis strongly supports that the use of IG‐IMRT in the preoperative setting significantly improves late toxicity profiles, aligning with findings from other prospective studies and surpassing outcomes observed with 2D‐RT treatments.
