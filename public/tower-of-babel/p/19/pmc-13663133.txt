Effectiveness of cefazolin versus anti-staphylococcal β-lactam for treating methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus pneumonia in critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Sufyan M Alomair, Khalid Al Sulaiman, Aisha Alharbi, Rahaf Alqahtani, Hatun M Labban, Nader Damfu, Sultan Alotaibi, Huda J Aljedaani, Shahad A Alshamrani, Rahaf R Altalhi, Rana F Albadrani, Khalid M Alkharfy, Deem A Alkaoud, Nada F Alshehri, Enas Munshi, Nadine Alanazi, Shahad S Almutairi, Maha A Alanizi, Sarah A Almugbil, Lujain M Almatrafi, Asmaa A Almehmadi, Maram A Alzahrani, Marah Almutairi, Munirah F Alhmoud, Hadeel M Alharbi, Ohoud Aljuhani

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCritically ill patients carry a high risk of infections and related complications, which significantly increase mortality. For patients with confirmed methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) pneumonia, the optimal definitive β-lactam therapy remains controversial. To date, clinical evidence comparing the effectiveness of cefazolin against other antistaphylococcal β-lactam antibiotics in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of cefazolin versus non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics for treating MSSA pneumonia in critically ill adults.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter, retrospective cohort study from January 2016 to July 2021 at three tertiary hospitals in Saudi Arabia. The study included adult intensive care unit (ICU) patients with confirmed MSSA respiratory infections who received definitive, targeted anti-MSSA β-lactam therapy. Eligible patients were categorized into two groups based on the specific antibiotic received (cefazolin vs. non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics). The primary outcome was treatment failure, while all other endpoints were considered secondary. Logistic, linear, and negative binomial regression analyses were utilized to analyze the study’s endpoints, as appropriate.ResultsA total of 54 patients were included in the study, evenly distributed with 27 patients in the cefazolin group and 27 in the alternative anti-MSSA β-lactam group. The treatment failure rate was significantly higher in the cefazolin group compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group (OR 5.73; 95% CI (1.303–25.191), p = 0.02). Moreover, the rate of re-escalation to other antibiotics was higher in the cefazolin group (OR 17.98; 95% CI (2.731–118.425), p = 0.002). No significant difference was found in time to defervescence, or MSSA recurrence infection rate within three months. In addition, in-hospital and 30-day mortality rates, ICU re-admission, length of stay, and ventilator-free days were not statistically significant different between the two groups.ConclusionIn critically ill patients with MSSA pneumonia, cefazolin was associated with higher rates of treatment failure and re-escalation compared to non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics. Further studies with a larger sample size are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: Cefazolin, Anti-staphylococcal β-lactam, Methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), Pneumonia, Critically ill patients

Introduction

Pneumonia is one of the leading cause of admissions to intensive care units (ICU) and remains the most common secondary infection acquired in these settings [1, 2]. In addition, developing pneumonia complications are associated with increased mortality rates [3]. Recent data indicated that approximately 37% of cases are caused by Gram-positive bacteria in critically ill patients [4]. Among Gram-positive pathogens, methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) is a prominent cause of morbidity and mortality in the ICU settings. It accounts for approximately 20–30% of Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) pneumonia cases, and studies reported that S. aureus both MSSA and Methicillin-Resistant S. aureus (MRSA) represents around 20% of all hospital-acquired pneumonia in ICU settings [5–7]. A meta-analysis highlighted that a delays in antibiotic administration and inappropriate antibiotic selection are critical risk factors, contributing to increased mortality in ICU patients [8]. These findings emphasize the urgent need to identify the optimal antibiotic regimen for managing MSSA pneumonia infections to improve clinical outcomes and reduce mortality rates.

Several studies have assessed the effectiveness and safety of cefazolin versus non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics (e.g., nafcillin, oxacillin) for treating MSSA infections. Meta-analyses and large observational studies have consistently shown that cefazolin is associated with comparable or lower all-cause mortality, reduced treatment failures, and fewer adverse events compared to other antistaphylococcal β-lactams [9–11]. These findings underscore cefazolin as a safer and more tolerable alternative with less nephrotoxicity risk, particularly in critically ill populations.

Studies by Rao et al. and Lee et al. demonstrated no significant differences in mortality or treatment failures between cefazolin and non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics, even in critically ill patients with deep-seated MSSA infections [9, 10]. Furthermore, a multicenter retrospective study reported that patients treated with cefazolin for MSSA bacteremia experienced fewer adverse events, including rash and acute kidney injury, compared to nafcillin or oxacillin protocols [11].

Although the evidence strongly supports the use of cefazolin in MSSA bacteremia and other invasive infections, the literature is limited regarding MSSA pneumonia, particularly in ICU settings. Most studies focused on bloodstream infections or surgical site infections, leaving a gap in understanding its effectiveness in managing MSSA pneumonia in critically ill patients. To address this gap, our study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of cefazolin in comparison with non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics in treating MSSA pneumonia in critically ill patients.

Methods

Study design

This is a multicenter, retrospective cohort study of adult critically ill patients with confirmed MSSA pneumonia admitted to intensive care units (ICUs) at three tertiary hospitals in Saudi Arabia from January 2016 to July 2021. This study is part of the Saudi Critical Care Pharmacy Research (SCAPE) platform, which conducts studies assessing the safety and effectiveness of multiple therapies in critically ill patients [12]. The diagnosis of pneumonia was confirmed by the treating physician alongside the presence of positive MSSA in respiratory cultures. Eligible patients were categorized into two groups based on the targeted antibiotic received for MSSA pneumonia (cefazolin vs. non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics). The non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group included patients who received other antistaphylococcal antibiotics, including intravenous cloxacillin, flucloxacillin, cefuroxime, or ceftriaxone. All patients were followed during their hospital stay until discharge or in-hospital death. The study was approved by King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) (Ref.#0575/24). The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki (adopted in 1964 and amended in 2013), as well as national ethical regulations and local institutional guidelines at the study centers. Due to the retrospective, observational design of the study, informed consent was waived.

Study participants

Critically ill adult patients aged ≥ 18 years who were admitted to the ICUs with diagnosed pneumonia and a proven MSSA respiratory culture, and who received targeted anti-MSSA β-lactam therapy as definitive antibiotics during the study period, were screened for eligibility. Patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), polymicrobial respiratory infections, concurrent bacteremia, or an immunocompromised status were excluded. In addition, we excluded patients who received anti-MSSA definitive antibiotics other than cefazolin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, cloxacillin, or flucloxacillin, as well as patients who were co-administered other antibiotics with anti-MSSA activity (Fig. 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was the rate of treatment failure. Other outcomes, such as 30-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, ICU re-admission within 3 months, antibiotic re-escalation rate, recurrence of MSSA infection within 3 months, ICU and hospital length of stays, and ventilator-free days, were considered secondary outcomes.

Study setting

The study took place at three tertiary hospitals in Saudi Arabia. The centers were chosen based on their advanced ICU settings with a total of more than 180 ICU beds, ability to handle critically ill patients, geographic variation, accessibility of electronic medical records, and willingness to contribute. The included centers were King Abdulaziz Medical City in Riyadh, King Abdulaziz Medical City in Jeddah, and King Abdulaziz Hospital in Al Ahsa . The primary site for this multicenter study was King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC-RD), a tertiary care center in Riyadh. King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) was the approving Institutional Review Board (IRB) across all three study sites, and study data were extracted electronically following IRB approval . Furthermore, study data were available, accessible, anonymized, and managed by the assigned data collectors at each site.

Data collection

Study data were obtained from patients’ electronic medical records and collected using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap®) system hosted by the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC). Information collected included patient demographics, comorbidities, vital signs, and severity scores, such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Additionally, data on acute kidney injury (AKI), mechanical ventilation (MV) status, and MV parameters (e.g., FiO2 requirement and PaO2/FiO2 ratio) were assessed within the first 24 hours of ICU admission. Laboratory tests, including renal profiles, liver function tests (LFTs), albumin, and coagulation profiles (e.g., INR, aPTT), were also collected. The de-escalated and re-escalated antibiotics used, duration of de-escalated antibiotics, time to defervescence, recurrence of MSSA infection within three months, and rate of treatment failure were obtained from the patients’ electronic medical records. Surrogate biomarkers of infection—such as procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP)—alongside microbiological cultures and their susceptibilities, ICU/hospital mortality, and length of stay were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and outcome variables were compared between the two study groups. Quantitative variables were summarized as mean (standard deviation) or median with interquartile range [lower quartile (Q1), upper quartile (Q3)] , as appropriate, depending on data normality . Qualitative variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical or binomial data, while the student's t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was utilized to compare continuous parametric and nonparametric variables, respectively.

. Regression analyses were conducted for the study endpoints based on clinically and statistically relevant covariates, including age, gender, Multiple Organ Dysfunction (MOD) score, and mechanical ventilation use within 24 hours of ICU admission. These variables were selected a priori based on their clinical relevance and potential association with the study outcomes in critically ill patients. Multivariable logistic regression was used for binary outcomes, including mortality, treatment failure, antibiotic re-escalation, and MSSA recurrence. Multiple linear regression was utilized for the time to microbiological sterilization, while negative binomial regression was used for the time to defervescence. Odds ratios (ORs) or estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported as appropriate. A p-value < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance for all comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 7,719 Patients screened for eligibility, 54 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1). Of the included patients, cefazolin was given to 27 patients, and 27 received non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics. In the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group, ceftriaxone was the most commonly used de-escalated antibiotic, accounting for 59.25% of cases. This was followed by flucloxacillin at 22.22%, cloxacillin at 14.81%, and cefuroxime at 3.70% (Table 1). The time from culture to the administration of the de-escalated antibiotics and the duration of the de-escalated antibiotics themselves were comparable between both groups, with p-values of 0.1727 and 0.1222, respectively (Table 2).

Demographic and baseline characteristics

In the entire cohort, the median age for both groups was 42.0 (28.00, 66.00), with a predominance of male patients (57.4%) and body mass index (BMI) of 27.2 (± 6.25) as shown in Table 2, Diabetes mellitus (38.9%) and hypertension (38.9%) were the most common comorbidities reported in the whole cohort followed by chronic kidney disease (13%) and dyslipidemia (13%). Chronic kidney disease was statistically significantly higher in individuals who did not receive cefazolin compared to those who were exposed to it (25% vs. 0%; p-value = 0.0046). Other comorbidities were comparable between the two groups as shown in Table 2.

Patients who received cefazolin as a deescalated antibiotic were younger with a median age of 39 years (27.00, 55.00) compared with 58 years (30.00, 70.00) in the non-cefazolin group (p-value = 0.08). Male gender was more predominant in the cefazolin group than patients who received other than cefazolin a deescalated antibiotics (74.1 vs. 40.7; p-value = 0.01). The mean APACHE II and multiple organ dysfunction scores were statistically lower in the cefazolin group compared with patients who did not receive it (15.3 ± 7.53 vs. 20.2 ± 7.94; p-value = 0.03) and (5.4 ± 2.72 vs. 7.6 ± 2.75; p-value = 0.0043), respectively.

Regarding the ICU parameters, the use of mechanical ventilation within the first 24 h was slightly higher in the cefazolin group compared to non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group, although this difference was not statistically significant (92.6% vs. 76.0%; p-value = 0.09). Other clinical and laboratory variables assessed within 24 h of ICU admission, such as the need for inotropes or vasopressors, and the maximum temperature, were comparable between the two groups (see Table 2).

Outcomes

Treatment failure and time to defervescence

The rate of treatment failure was significantly higher in patients who were de-escalated to cefazolin compared to those in the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group (74.2% vs. 37.0%; p-value = 0.0062). Furthermore, after adjusting for relevant covariates using logistic regression, the odds of treatment failure remained consistently high in the cefazolin group (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 5.73, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.30–25.91; p-value = 0.02).

There was no statistically significant difference in defervescence time between the cefazolin de-escalation group and the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group (9% vs. 2%; p = 0.0506). Additionally, regression analysis showed that cefazolin use was not significantly associated with increased defervescence time after adjusting for relevant covariates (beta coefficient: -1.06; 95% CI: -2.79 to 0.66; p = 0.227).

30-Day and in-hospital mortality

The in-hospital mortality rate was higher in non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics 22.2% vs. 7.4% in cefazolin with no statistical significance difference before and after adjustment (P = 0.1255) and (aOR: 0.22; 95% CI: 0.019–2.533; p-value = 0.2233) respectively) (Table 3). Furthermore, 30-day mortality was higher in non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group 14.8% compared to cefazolin group 7.4% with no statistical significance prior to and after adjustment (p-value = 0.3865) and (aOR: 0.28; 95% CI: 0.026–3.106; p-value = 0.3017) respectively) (Table 3).

Length of stay and re-admission

The median hospital length of stay was higher in the cefazolin group [40.0 days (IQR: 17.0, 97.0)] than in the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group [19.0 days (IQR: 9.0, 72.0)]; however, this difference was not statistically significant before or after adjustment (p = 0.056 and beta coefficient: 0.36; 95% CI: −0.39 to 1.10; p = 0.346, respectively). Similarly, the cefazolin group had a longer ICU length of stay [15.0 days (IQR: 8.0, 24.0)] compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group [11.0 days (IQR: 6.0, 33.0)], with no significant difference observed in either the crude analysis (p = 0.544) or the adjusted model (beta coefficient: −0.06; 95% CI: −0.56 to 0.44; p = 0.812). The ICU readmission rate was higher in the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group than in the cefazolin group (11.1% vs. 3.7%), but the difference was not statistically significant before or after adjustment (p = 0.578 and aOR: 0.53; 95% CI: 0.031–9.127; p = 0.662, respectively). (Table 3).

Antibiotics escalation and infection recurrence

The rate of escalation to another antibiotic was higher in the crude analysis for patients who were deescalated to cefazolin compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics group (70.4% vs. 22.2%; p-value = 0.0004). This trend was also observed in the regression analysis, showing a significantly higher likelihood in the cefazolin group (aOR: 17.98, 95% CI: 2.73–118.43; p-value = 0.003) (Table 3**). On the other hand, the recurrence rate of MSSA infections within 3 months of completing the treatment was comparable in crude analysis (11.1% vs. 7.4%; p-value = 0.6387) and in multivariate analysis between the two groups (aOR: 1.28, 95% CI: 0.07–23.19; p-value = 0.87) as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

Our study contributes to the limited evidence evaluating cefazolin for the treatment of methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) pneumonia in critically ill patients by comparing its effectiveness with that of other antistaphylococcal β-lactam agents. To address this important evidence gap, treatment failure was selected as the primary outcome. Patients who received cefazolin experienced a significantly higher rate of treatment failure than those treated with non-cefazolin β-lactam antibiotics, a finding that remained consistent in both crude and adjusted analyses. In addition, the rate of re-escalation to another antibiotic was higher in patients who were deescalated to cefazolin compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group. No differences were observed between the groups in all other outcomes including mortality, time to defervescence, recurrence of MSSA infection, readmission within 3 months, and length of stay.

In our study, treatment failure was significantly higher in the cefazolin group compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics in the crude analysis. Additionally, after adjusting for possible confounders, cefazolin use was associated with more than five-fold increased odds of treatment failure compared to alternative B lactam. A similar trend was observed in a retrospective study comparing cefazolin and nafcillin for MSSA bacteremia, where treatment failure occurred in 12.8% of patients at 4 weeks, increasing to 15.8% at 12 weeks [16]. It is important to note that pneumonia was identified as a site of infection in 4/49 (8%) cefazolin group compared to 11/84 (13%) patients in the nafcillin group [16]. Notably, pneumonia was a strong predictor of treatment failure, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 6.0 (95% CI, 1.5–23.7; P = 0.02) [1]. However, our findings differ from a large meta-analysis evaluating cefazolin versus antistaphylococcal penicillins (ASPs) in MSSA bacteremia, which included seven clinical trials. Among the five studies that specifically assessed clinical treatment failure with different definitions, rates were comparable between cefazolin and ASPs (4.8% vs. 5.5%) (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.41–1.76, P = 0.66) [17]. These results suggest that the efficacy of cefazolin may be influenced by the infection source, with evidence supporting its use in MSSA bacteremia not necessarily extending to pulmonary infections. Moreover, ceftriaxone accounted for the majority of antibiotic exposure within the non-cefazolin β-lactam cohort (59.2%), which should be considered when interpreting the comparative findings.

Furthermore, we found that antibiotic re-escalation, which might be a marker for clinical failure, was significantly more frequent in the cefazolin group. Our significant findings introduce concerns regarding cefazolin’s effectiveness in MSSA pneumonia and require further studies to confirm that. One possible explanation for the higher treatment failure rates observed in our cefazolin group could be suboptimal dosing in critically ill patients with pneumonia. A multicenter retrospective study specifically evaluated the impact of high-dose cefazolin (≥ 2 g every 6 h) in ICU patients with MSSA pneumonia and found a clinically significant reduction in clinical failure compared to standard dosing (27.2% vs. 47.3%). Additionally, high-dose cefazolin was associated with lower in-hospital mortality and reduced MSSA recurrence without prolonging ICU stay [18]. These findings emphasize the importance of individualized dosing in critically ill patients with MSSA pneumonia to optimize cefazolin efficacy.

Despite the higher treatment failure rate observed with cefazolin, our study found no significant difference in in-hospital or 30-day mortality between the cefazolin and non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics groups. Both the In-hospital mortality and 30-days mortality were numerically higher in the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group, however, the difference was not statistically significant. Our findings are consistent with previous studies on MSSA bacteremia, where cefazolin and antistaphylococcal penicillins (nafcillin/oxacillin) demonstrated comparable mortality outcomes [19]. Beganovic et al. conducted a retrospective cohort study across multiple medical centers, including hospitalized patients with MSSA bacteremia [19]. After propensity score matching, < 11.4% of the included patients in both arms had a respiratory source of MSSA bacteremia [19]. The authors directly compared cefazolin to nafcillin/oxacillin, finding no significant difference in 30-day mortality (HR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.11–4.00), suggesting comparable effectiveness [19]. In contrast to our findings, McDanel et al., conducted a multicenter study of 3,167 patients, in which 1,163 (37%) received cefazolin, and 2,004 (63%) received nafcillin/oxacillin [20]. Of the included patients, 46% required ICU admission [20]. The study reported that cefazolin was associated with a 37% reduction in 30-day mortality (HR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.51–0.78) and a 23% reduction in 90-day mortality (HR, 0.77; 95% CI, 0.66–0.90) compared to nafcillin/oxacillin. 30-day mortality rates were 10% in the cefazolin group vs. 15% in the nafcillin/oxacillin group, suggesting a potential survival benefit with cefazolin, however, pneumonia as a source of bacteremia was not specifically reported [20].

One potential explanation for the observed discrepancy between treatment failure and mortality in our study is our broad definition of treatment failure, which included treatment discontinuation due to side effects and MSSA recurrence within three months as these factors may not directly impact mortality. Moreover, baseline characteristics may have influenced the observed outcomes as patients in the cefazolin group were younger and had lower severity scores (APACHE II and MODS), which might have allowed recovery despite higher treatment failure rate. Conversely, the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group had a significantly higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease and other comorbidities at baseline, which may have contributed to increased mortality risk independent of antibiotic choice. These findings emphasize the importance of antibiotic selection in MSSA pneumonia, particularly in critically ill patients. While cefazolin remains a valuable option for MSSA bacteremia, its mortality benefit in pneumonia requires further investigation.

In addition, we observed a longer median time to defervescence in the cefazolin group (2 days) compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group (0 days), however, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.0506). For instance, one retrospective cohort study analyzing outcome in complicated MSSA bacteremia found no significant difference in time to defervescence between cefazolin and oxacillin groups (median of 2 days in both groups; P = 0.33) [14]. Similarly, a retrospective propensity-score-matched case-control study demonstrated comparable times to defervescence between cefazolin and nafcillin for the treatment of MSSA bacteremia, with both groups showing a median of 3 days and no significant difference reported [16]. The numerical difference observed in our study could indicate a potential delay in the clinical response to cefazolin compared to non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group highlighting the need to further investigate the utility of standard dose cefazolin in treating MSSA pneumonia in critically ill patients.

Moreover, our study observed a longer ICU length of stay in the cefazolin group (15 days) compared to the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group (11 days), although this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.5443). Currently, there is a lack of specific data addressing ICU length of stay in patients with MSSA infections treated with cefazolin versus non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics. However, large multicenter study by McDanel et al., compared definitive therapy with cefazolin to treatment with nafcillin or oxacillin among patients with MSSA bacteremia have shown that cefazolin was associated with a slightly shorter overall hospital stay compared to nafcillin/oxacillin (12 days vs. 13 days, respectively; P < 0.001) [20]. Furthermore, we found that ICU readmission within three months was higher in the non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics group (11.1%) compared to the cefazolin group (3.7%); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.5783). Studies evaluating ICU readmission for MSSA bacteremia are limited. However, McDanel et al., found no significant difference in 30-day readmission rates between patients treated with cefazolin and those treated with nafcillin or oxacillin (HR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.26–2.16) [20].

This study also demonstrated a comparable recurrence rate of MSSA infection within 3 months in cefazolin compared to non-cefazolin groups (11.1% vs. 7.4%, respectively, p = 0.6387). A retrospective cohort study by Corsini Campioli et al., analyzing outcomes in patients with MSSA spinal epidural abscesses, found no significant difference in 90-day recurrence between the cefazolin group (11.4%) and non-cefazolin β-lactams antibiotics groups (9.4%), p-value = 1 [21]. Additionally, another quasi-experimental study of 73 patients with MSSA endocarditis also found no statistically significant difference in relapse rates between ASPs (0%) and cefazolin (5.3%), p-value = 0.494 [22].

Our study is among the first to compare the effectiveness of cefazolin with that of other antistaphylococcal β-lactam antibiotics for the treatment of MSSA pneumonia in critically ill patients. However, several notable limitations must be acknowledged. The study’s relatively small sample size limits its statistical power and raises the risk of type II errors, which is reflected in the wide confidence intervals observed for some outcomes. As a result, caution should be exercised when interpreting the findings. Additionally, the retrospective design of the study relied solely on microbiological confirmation for diagnosing pneumonia. Moreover, the absence of cefazolin dosing data limits the assessment of the effective dose. Also, the relatively broad criteria for defining treatment failure may have influenced the findings. Lastly, by assessing overall readmission rates within three months without differentiating between infection-related and unrelated causes, we may have inadvertently introduced confounding variables that impacted the interpretation of the results.

Conclusion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort study of critically ill patients with MSSA pneumonia, cefazolin use was associated with higher rates of treatment failure and antibiotic re-escalation compared with other antistaphylococcal β-lactam antibiotics.Our study found no significant differences in all other outcomes including mortality, time for microbiological sterilization, or infection recurrence between the two groups. Further studies with larger sample size are needed to confirm our findings.
