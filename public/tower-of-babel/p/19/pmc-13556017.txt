Fragile cooperation in high-casualty traffic incidents: a grounded theory study of inter-organizational emergency response in Iran
Authors: Babak Farzinnia, Davoud Khorasani-Zavareh, Javad Shojafard, Morteza Mortazavi, Vahid Delshad, Hamidreza Khankeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRoad traffic injuries remain one of Iran's top four causes of death, posing a continuing public health and governance concern. Inter-organizational cooperation is frequently fragmented, particularly in mass-casualty traffic incidents, despite the existence of numerous emergency response organisations. In addition to developing a grounded conceptual framework that explains patterns of coordination failure, this study aims to theorise and model inter-organizational cooperation in such occurrences in Iran.MethodsFrom 2020 to 2024, this qualitative grounded theory study was carried out. Through semi-structured interviews and expert panel discussions, twenty-seven managers, experts, and emergency response professionals from EMS, police, fire departments, the Iranian Red Crescent Society, and academic institutions took part. The Corbin and Strauss constant comparative approach, which uses open, axial, and selective coding, was used to analyse the data.Results"Fragile Cooperation" emerged as the main category from the investigation. Adaptive and maladaptive tendencies alternated in interorganisational collaboration. Paternalistic leadership in chaotic conditions, advocacy and volunteer participation, personalised crisis encounters, increased interaction after significant incidents, and selfless risk-taking were examples of adaptive tactics. Vague responsibility boundaries, cooperation-averse communication structures, interorganisational bias and competitiveness, conflicts of interest, and disorganised event scene management were examples of maladaptive tactics. The main tactic for enhancing collaboration, according to participants, is the creation of a unified emergency response system with a single national emergency number and integrated dispatch structure.ConclusionsThe interorganisational collaboration of Iranian emergency response systems is highly dependent on relational and contextual factors and is structurally fragile. Sustainable improvement requires institutional reforms, unified emergency dispatch procedures, integrated operating protocols, and interoperable communication technology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28137-y.Keywords: Disaster governance, Emergency response systems, Unified emergency dispatch, Road traffic injuries, And inter-organizational cooperation

Background

Every year, the quiet echo of a car door closing, the laugh shared on a bus, or the routine commute home is shattered for millions. Road traffic injuries constitute a major global public health challenge, claiming approximately 1.3 million lives annually and leaving an additional 20–50 million individuals with non-fatal, often disabling, injuries [1]. The burden of these injuries falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the loss of economically productive individuals and the costs of long-term care generate profound social and economic consequences. Projections indicate that by 2030, road traffic injuries will rank among the leading causes of years of healthy life lost in these settings [2], Prehospital mortality disparities further underscore systemic inequities: in many LMICs, the probability of dying before reaching definitive medical care is approximately twice that observed in high-income countries [3]. These disparities reflect structural deficiencies in emergency response capacity, coordination, and governance rather than unavoidable epidemiological variation.

In Iran, road traffic accidents remain one of the principal causes of mortality and morbidity. In 2024 (1403 S.H.), 19,435 fatalities and 381,484 injuries were recorded nationwide [4]. Beyond their epidemiological significance, these figures signal sustained strain on healthcare infrastructure, emergency response systems, and social welfare structures.

Survival and functional outcomes following traffic accidents are strongly influenced by the timeliness and effectiveness of emergency response, victim characteristics, and injury severity [5]. Among these determinants, coordinated prehospital response plays a pivotal role. Effective management of crash scenes requires seamless inter-organizational collaboration among Emergency Medical Services (EMS), the Iranian Red Crescent Society, municipal fire departments, and law enforcement agencies [6, 7]. Despite the dedication and operational experience of these actors, coordination challenges persist. Organizational diversity, while potentially advantageous, often translates into fragmented command structures, inconsistent operational protocols, overlapping jurisdictional mandates, and multiple emergency access numbers [8–10]. Communication bottlenecks, unclear role delineation, and incompatible information systems further impede real-time coordination [11–13].

The 2013 bus accident on the Qom-Tehran highway, with approximately 90 casualties, laid bare a hard truth: the level of cooperation between Iran's rescue organizations is fragile and unpredictable, swayed by factors that should not decide a life [7]. When communication breaks down, responsibility becomes a shadow, and agencies operate in silos, the human cost is inevitably higher [14–16].

The structural barriers to effective cooperation are multifaceted. Persistent communication failures, fragmented regulatory frameworks, absence of validated inter-organizational coordination models, technological incompatibilities, and cultural or institutional resistance to data sharing collectively undermine system integration [17–20]. These challenges are amplified during major disasters, where uncertainty and scale intensify the demand for synchronized action [21–23].

Addressing these systemic inadequacies necessitates a shift from descriptive research to theory-informed model building. In order to explain the dynamics influencing inter-organizational interaction among Iranian emergency response organisations, this study aims to develop a grounded conceptual model. Additionally, it suggests institutionally and culturally grounded measures to improve emergency response efficacy and collaborative capacity in Iran's high-casualty traffic situations.

Methodology

Study design

This study employed a grounded theory approach as articulated by Juliet Corbin and Anselm Strauss (2014) [24]. The methodology was selected to enable the systematic development of a substantive conceptual model grounded in participants’ experiences by using the constant comparative method [25]. The researchers iteratively analyzed the data to identify key challenges, underlying processes, and context-specific strategies shaping inter-organizational cooperation in emergency response settings. Content analysis of the opinions expressed by experts in a expert panel was employed to identify strategies for improving inter-sectoral coordination [25]. This approach facilitated theory generation through continuous comparison across incidents, codes, and emerging categories.

Research context and participants

In-depth interviews were conducted with staff from four national emergency response organizations in Iran police, Prehospital EMS, the Iranian Red Crescent Society, and fire departments each operating independently with distinct protocols and dedicated emergency numbers. Participants were recruited from Tehran, Alborz, and Qom provinces, selected for their dense road networks, high population, and elevated traffic accident rates.

The study also included university professors with expertise in emergency management. Demographic data (age, education, and work experience) were collected, and semi-structured interviews explored participants’ experiences managing fatal traffic accidents, focusing on coordination, communication, and operational challenges. Interviews progressed from general to detailed questions, with probing used to clarify and deepen emerging insights. Data were collected between 2020 and 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible if they had experience in high-casualty traffic incidents involving multiple emergency organizations and at least five years of managerial or operational experience. Individuals were excluded if they were unwilling or unable to participate due to fear of repercussions or insufficient verbal communication skills.

Purposeful sampling was initially employed to recruit participants with relevant expertise, followed by theoretical sampling to select individuals who could further refine emerging concepts. Consistent with qualitative research practices, the sample size was determined iteratively, and interviews continued until theoretical saturation was achieved.

Data synthesis

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, which allow flexibility and depth in qualitative research. Interviews began with broad, open-ended questions, such as participants’ personal experiences responding to traffic incidents and the provision of services, and progressively moved to more specific inquiries. Each interview lasted approximately 45–60 min and was conducted at a location of the participant’s choice.

In addition, a structured expert panel discussion was conducted with 27 selected participants. This forum facilitated the sharing of insights, collaborative debate, and the co-development of practical solutions and recommendations related to inter-organizational cooperation [26].

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection to allow emerging insights to guide subsequent interviews. The constant comparative method, as outlined by Juliet Corbin and Anselm Strauss (2014) [24], was employed using a three-stage coding process: open, axial, and selective coding.

Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim. During open coding, transcripts were read repeatedly to identify discrete concepts, which were then assigned initial codes and grouped according to similarity. In axial coding, these codes were organized into categories and subcategories, and relationships among them were examined to provide a more comprehensive explanation of the phenomenon. During this stage, similar categories were integrated around central themes. Finally, in selective coding, categories were further refined and integrated, and a core category was identified to develop a grounded theory explaining the phenomenon under investigation. Thematic analysis was performed manually, with iterative comparison and memoing to enhance conceptual clarity and rigor [24, 25].

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to Iran’s ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects. Prior to each interview, participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, and their right to withdraw at any time. Written informed consent was obtained, and participants were assured that their data would remain confidential and anonymous.

This research is part of the project entitled “Feasibility Study of Setting Up a Joint Dispatch Center and Emergency Unit Number (SOS) in Tehran” (IR Code of Ethics: USWR. REC.1402.211). The study purpose was explained both orally and in writing, and participants provided explicit consent for both participation and audio recording of interviews.

Trustworthiness

To ensure rigor, the study applied Lincoln and Guba’s criteria for qualitative research: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [25]. Credibility was enhanced by engaging participants in reviewing and validating codes and findings derived from their interviews. Dependability was ensured through independent coding and categorization by the first and second authors, followed by verification by a third researcher. Confirmability was strengthened via constant comparative analysis and continuous monitoring of transcripts, codes, and categories by two experienced team members throughout the study. Transferability was supported through maximum variation sampling, capturing participants with diverse ages, genders, work experiences, and professional roles to enhance the applicability of findings to similar contexts [25].

Demographic characteristics and cooperation methods of participants

Based on participants’ experiences and perceptions, the central concept of this study “Fragile Cooperation” along with related sub-concepts, was synthesized into a conceptual model (Fig. 1). According to this model, cooperation among Iran’s national emergency response organizations is shaped by adaptive strategies, including risk-sharing in critical situations, intensified inter-agency interaction during major incidents, paternalistic leadership in chaotic contexts, advocacy-driven engagement, and contingency-based management. These strategies promote participatory, integrative management and facilitate maximal inter-organizational cooperation.

Conceptual model to enhance inter-organizational cooperation in high-casualty traffic incidents

Conversely, the adoption of maladaptive strategies such as ambiguous responsibility boundaries, inter-organizational prejudice and artificial competition, cooperation-averse communication structures, conflicts of interest, and operational disorder at the incident sceneleads to isolated or “island” management, characterized by minimal cooperation.

These findings are elaborated in detail in the subsequent sections.

Sacrificial risk

This concept refers to the risky actions taken by rescuers to save lives, particularly during major incidents, which may involve exceeding their formal organizational responsibilities or intervening in situations where they are not officially authorized to act. Such behaviors are strongly influenced by local cultural norms; for example, in some Iranian tribal communities, this type of risk-taking is more socially and institutionally accepted.

One participant (P27) highlighted the tension between legal restrictions and practical necessity: “Another problem is the legal prohibition and limitation on transporting more than one injured person per vehicle and the lack of insurance coverage, which we often have to violate to save lives.”

More interaction after major incidents

In Iran, large-scale traffic accidents often trigger increased inter-organizational cooperation due to public outrage, media scrutiny, and pressure from authorities. This heightened interaction typically persists for several months and may lead to temporary reforms in coordination and operational procedures.

One participant (P12) noted: “After the horrific accident on the Qom–Tehran highway, which resulted in nearly 90 deaths and injuries and received extensive media coverage, strong cooperation developed among the province’s rescue organizations, although it gradually declined over time.”

Paternalistic management in chaos

In some Iranian rescue organizations, unwritten norms and a strong sense of personal leadership among managers can facilitate more effective inter-organizational cooperation during peak chaos, such as severe traffic incidents. Paternalistic management refers to a leadership style in which managers draw on their personal experiences and judgment, often guided by emotions and situational awareness, to resolve conflicts and coordinate actions across agencies in critical moments.

One participant (P14) explained: “Currently, we are not formally obligated to assist each other, but a shared understanding of cooperation has been established.”

Advocacy and volunteer services

Participants highlighted the critical role of volunteers in emergency response, public education, and cultural promotion. In parallel, advocacy efforts aimed at fostering inter-organizational cooperation and strengthening a culture of safety are expanding at the community level.

In this study, advocacy and volunteer services are conceptualized as two interrelated factors that enhance emergency operations and contribute to broader social development. Participants emphasized practical strategies for leveraging volunteer capacities, noting their significant contributions across multiple domains, including emergency response, education, and community engagement.

One participant (P22) stated: “Overall, the presence of organized volunteers is lifesavingfor example, rescuing victims trapped inside vehicles on the outskirts of the city.”

Integrating volunteer services with advocacy efforts is seen as a mechanism to promote a culture of cooperation, improve community safety, and enhance overall quality of life. These factors should be carefully considered in social planning and policymaking.

Personalized crisis interactions

Some emergency personnel are able to establish personal, trust-based relationships that facilitate individualized, community-level interactions. These personalized connections enhance collaboration in specific situations, particularly at the local level, and help overcome structural or procedural barriers to cooperation.

One participant (P13) noted: “Perhaps if I were not here, cooperation with other organizations would face difficulties. The system is person-dependent.”

Vague responsibility boundaries

Ambiguities in laws and differing interpretations of organizational mandatesfor instance, regarding corpse transport, road firefighting, and hospital admission procedureslimit the willingness of Iranian emergency organizations to engage beyond their formal duties. As a result, under normal conditions, inter-organizational collaboration is often minimal.

One participant (P1) explained: “There is ambiguity in the division of responsibilities between organizations, which may seem unclear to the public but is well understood by officials. Currently, we face challenges in corpse transport and firefighting, especially outside urban areas.”

Prejudices and false inter-organizational competition

Participants indicated that authoritarian structures and organizational biases hinder information sharing and collaboration among emergency organizations. These biases are also reflected in the use of multiple emergency numbers, which organizations sometimes treat as symbols to showcase their capabilities rather than as tools for coordinated response.

One participant (P25) noted: “These multiple emergency numbers have somehow become a way to assert organizational identity and a source of competition.”

Cooperation-averse communication structures

Participants reported that Iranian emergency organizations often operate independently due to the absence of standardized legal communication pathways between emergency operation centers, multiple emergency numbers, and deficiencies in communication systems. These include unreliable telephone lines, radios, and hotlines, outdated internal and inter-organizational platforms, diverse software for recording contacts, and a lack of unified databases or statistical resources to support coordination.

One participant (P20) explained: “The multiplicity of emergency numbers is a major source of frustration. Even in communication, we cannot simply instruct a caller to contact another emergency organization. Call transfers lack a uniform national procedure and are handled subjectively.”

Conflict of interest in cooperation

Participants indicated that weak regulatory frameworks, the absence of formal obligations to collaborate, and the prioritization of organizational self-interest can reduce inter-agency cooperation. At the executive level, resistance to interaction and information sharing is often motivated by the desire to preserve administrative authority and organizational positions. In some cases, emergency organizations withhold information to avoid responsibility and accountability, ultimately undermining collaborative efforts.

One participant (P18) stated: “Emergency organizations strive to lobby parliamentarians and other policymakers to secure more resources and to influence laws in ways that minimize accountability and transparency for their operations.”

Chaos at the scene of the accident

Participants described how uncoordinated arrival of responders, inefficient incident scene management, variable rescuer skill levels, and emotional competition during operations reduce inter-organizational cooperation. Inefficiencies are often linked to resistance to unified command, weak incident leadership, and, in some cases, delayed provision of scene security, which can generate tension between responders and the public.

One participant (P19) reflected: “Looking back, perhaps the harm caused to the injured by volunteer forces acting alone was greater than if we had waited for skilled rescuers. Now, each team tends to manage itself independently.

Unified emergency response (Joint Dispatch Center)

Participants consistently identified the establishment of a unified emergency response system as the primary strategy to enhance inter-agency cooperation. This approach involves consolidating emergency call reception channels-integrating all existing numbers into a single, nationwide access pointand creating a joint emergency dispatch center.

Key prerequisites for successful implementation include gaining institutional support, shifting governance perspectives, reforming laws to facilitate inter-agency interaction, establishing integrated communication infrastructure, developing standardized joint emergency protocols, and ensuring robust inter-organizational oversight through a National Emergency Management Organization.

One participant (P21) emphasized the current inefficiency: “Given the multitude of organizations and emergency numbers, people now have to decide which emergency organization to call and what type of assistance they need, which leads to resource wastage.”

Discussion

This study advances a grounded theoretical explanation of inter-organizational emergency response in fragmented governance systems, conceptualized as “Fragile Cooperation.” Rather than treating coordination failures as purely operational deficiencies, the findings reveal cooperation as a dynamic and oscillatory phenomenon shaped by structural, institutional, cultural, and relational mechanisms.

The proposed theory suggests that inter-organizational cooperation fluctuates between participatory and isolated management, mediated by the dynamic interplay of adaptive and maladaptive strategies (Fig. 1). This conceptualization aligns with global disaster response frameworks, which emphasize that effective crisis management is inherently collaborative and depends on seamless integration across agencies [7, 21]. While international guidelines, such as the WHO’s technical package, advocate for fully integrated post-crash emergency systems [3], this theory highlights the socio-institutional mechanismscultural norms, organizational biases, legal ambiguities, and leadership stylesthat facilitate or hinder such integration in practice. By elucidating these context-specific factors, the model provides a framework for understanding why structural reforms alone may be insufficient without accompanying changes in organizational culture and inter-agency governance.

Adaptive strategies in global perspective

The adaptive strategy of sacrificial risk, in which responders exceed formal mandates to save lives, mirrors a widely documented phenomenon in emergency services worldwide. Larkin and Fowler note that EMS personnel often engage in “heroic” behaviors that transcend standard protocols, driven by a professional commitment to lifesaving [27]. Similarly, Power et.al [21] and farzinnia et.al [28] report that teams operating in extreme disaster contexts frequently act beyond their predefined roles. Our findings suggest that, in certain Iranian contexts, this behavior is culturally institutionalized, indicating that local norms can systematically encourage prosocial rule-breaking. This cultural dimension of risk-taking adds nuance to the literature on emergency ethics, operational performance, and cross-cultural differences in disaster response [29].

The findings indicate that, alongside emergency services, advocacy plays a crucial role in promoting a culture of cooperation and social responsibility within communities. The involvement of non-governmental organizationsas first responders, provided that technical and operational requirements are metcan enhance inter-agency collaboration. For example, participation of Red Crescent volunteers in rescue operations on intercity roads in Iran, coupled with advocacy efforts to strengthen inter-organizational cooperation and government support to equip rescue fleets, can significantly improve emergency response during incidents and disasters. Given the increasing frequency of disasters and humanitarian crises amid declining global resources, disaster management and humanitarian operations must prioritize enhanced cooperation, coordination, and effectiveness in aid delivery [30].

Another key factor in promoting inter-organizational cooperation is Personalized Crisis Interactions, which foster mutual trust. In ambiguous and complex emergencies requiring rapid action, team members who trust one another are more likely to rely on colleagues’ guidance without hesitation. Such trust facilitates faster decision-making in high-risk contexts, as team members are confident that others are committed to shared goals and will provide necessary support [21].

In such situations, personnel are often compelled to make decisions based on the immediate circumstances, frequently acting beyond formal organizational boundaries. Parker and Sundelius [8]. emphasize that, in the absence of established structures and procedures for cooperation, such flexible, boundary-crossing actions have proven necessary and have produced relatively positive outcomes. In other words, when organizational boundaries are de-emphasized in collaborative emergency operations, higher levels of inter-agency cooperation can be achieved.

Paternalistic management and leadership dynamics

The adaptive strategy of “paternalistic management in chaos” offers a culturally contextualized perspective on crisis leadership. While Western models often emphasize standardized incident command systems, our findings show that informal, authority-based leadership can temporarily resolve inter-organizational conflicts during periods of peak chaos. This aligns with Parker and Sundelius [8], who note that collaborative crisis management frequently relies on informal networks and trusted individuals. Similarly, ‘t Hart and Sundelius [31] observe that in complex emergencies, personal credibility can outweigh formal authority. However, our theory emphasizes that such paternalistic management is person-dependent and inherently unsustainable, highlighting the tension between adaptive leadership and the need for institutionalized, coordinated protocols.

While major incidents can temporarily enhance inter-organizational collaboration due to public scrutiny and media pressureas observed in Iran following the Qom–Tehran highway disaster [7] such cooperation often diminishes over time in the absence of a sustained framework. International research highlights that durable interoperability depends on integrated technical infrastructures, shared training programs, and the cultivation of organizational trust, shifting agencies away from isolated “siloed” operations toward a cohesive “network culture” [13, 23].

Maladaptive strategies and structural barriers

Conversely, maladaptive strategies, including vague responsibility boundaries and cooperation-averse communication structures, reflect systemic obstacles that are common in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Iran, previous studies have documented significant structural fragmentation within pre-hospital emergency services [7, 9]. These deficiencies are further exacerbated by fragmented urban management and inadequate urban data transmission infrastructure [32]). These maladaptive strategies should be understood as behavioral manifestations of deeper systemic deficiencies. Specifically, fragmented urban governance and inadequate data transmission infrastructure function as contextual conditions (systemic drivers) within our conceptual model (Fig. 1), rather than as separate categories.

Our theory extends this understanding by showing how structural deficiencies manifest as behavioral barriers, such as prejudices and false inter-organizational competition and conflicts of interest. This aligns with Bharosa et al. [19], who identified poor incentives and limited awareness of operational interdependencies as major obstacles to multi-agency information sharing. The persistence of multiple emergency numbers a clear symptom of such fragmentation directly contrasts with WHO recommendations advocating a single, unified emergency access system [3, 33].

Specifically, the absence of integrated urban management generates siloed administrative domains, which directly hinder coherent crisis response. This fragmentation contributes to the persistence of multiple emergency numbers, in contrast to WHO recommendations advocating a single, unified access system [3, 33]. These organizational shortcomings are further amplified by weak data infrastructure; outdated networks, limited coverage, and a lack of interoperability prevent real-time information sharing, severely constraining coordinated action during emergencies.

Recent evidence identifies fragmented institutional architectures as a key barrier to integrated emergency platforms, resulting in operational delays. In combination, fragmented urban management and deficient data infrastructure create a vicious cycle that entrenches systemic obstacles to effective crisis communication, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [34].

A central finding of this study is that incomplete information exchange remains a fundamental impediment to effective emergency response. This is strongly corroborated by international evidence: Moynihan et al. [16] highlight that “learning under uncertainty” is hindered by inadequate information sharing, while Fischer et al. [20] identify communication barriers as critical points of failure in crisis management. Our model specifies the mechanisms underlying these challenges, including disparate software systems, the absence of shared databases, and organizational reluctance to share information. These findings provide empirical support for Strohmandl et al.’s recommendation for integrated operations centers and align with Nazif-Munoz et al.’s observation that centralized dispatch systems are associated with reduced fatality rates [17, 18].

Uncoordinated arrivals, emotional competition among rescuers, and resistance to unified command critically undermine incident scene management and inter-organizational cooperation, directly impeding effective disaster response in Iran. Similar patterns have been documented both in Iran and internationally; studies by Khankeh et al. and Sushi et al. report comparable challenges in emergency and disaster operations [9, 35].

Unifying emergency communication channels

One key solution to enhance inter-agency cooperation is the consolidation of public-to-emergency communication channels. In Iran, due to organizational attachment to existing emergency numbers and considerations for caller rights, this process should be implemented gradually in two phases: initially, by routing calls to a single number while maintaining existing numbers, and subsequently by establishing a single national emergency aid number [7]. Hardy et al. demonstrated that in major incidents, countries utilizing a single emergency number for EMS, police, and fire services achieved the fastest response timesfrom the occurrence of the incident to the arrival of the first EMS unitand the shortest delays in initial communication among different rescue organizations [29].

In the study by Joshi and his colleagues, the establishment of a national aid number in India was mentioned as an unavoidable necessity for enhancing coordinated emergency efforts [33]. The results of Bazli and his colleagues' research also indicated that selecting a national aid number is an effective step for efficient emergency in incidents, especially traffic accidents in Iran [15]. In the study by Strohmandl and his colleagues in the Czech Republic regarding strategies to reduce road accident casualties, improving the accuracy of information related to the location and severity of incidents by establishing an operations and information center (phone number 112) and the presence of intervening agencies in a unified emergency and rescue system was recommended [17]. Nazif-Munoz et al. found that implementing a single emergency phone number with a centralized dispatch office correlates with a 19–20% decrease in traffic fatality rates, based on negative binomial regression models [18].

Several studies highlight the critical role of a national emergency aid number in improving coordinated response. Joshi et al. [33]. Identified the establishment of a single national number in India as an essential measure to enhance multi-agency emergency efforts. Similarly, Bazli et al. [15] found that selecting a national aid number is an effective step toward efficient emergency response in traffic incidents in Iran.

In the Czech Republic, Strohmandl et al. [17]. Recommended the creation of an operations and information center (e.g., number 112) to improve the accuracy of incident location and severity data, emphasizing the presence of multiple agencies within a unified emergency and rescue system. Furthermore, Nazif-Munoz et al. reported that implementing a single emergency phone number with centralized dispatch correlates with a 19–20% reduction in traffic fatality rates, based on negative binomial regression [18].

Based on participants’ experiences in this study, establishing an integrated framework to enhance multi-agency emergency response coordinationgrounded in technological integration and structural reformis essential. Recent international studies indicate that current approaches are often fragmented and inefficient, underscoring the need for unified systems and standardized protocols.

For instance, research in Saudi Arabia highlights that limited public awareness of the unified emergency number (112) delays incident reporting and service dispatch. Establishing a single national access point, along with improving operator-led triage to accurately identify rescue needs, are critical steps to reduce response delays and enhance public trust in emergency medical services [36]. Similarly, Johnson et al. [22] in the United States emphasize the need for legal reforms to enable joint action teams, integrating fire services, EMS, and police, for more effective management of high-casualty incidents.

However, technological tools alone are insufficient. Theoretical studies indicate that successful institutionalization requires supportive legislation, updated operational guidelines, and clear governance structures that mandate and facilitate inter-agency interaction. Evidence from Nigeria similarly identifies the absence of a unified regulatory and strategic framework as a major barrier to effective cooperation. Consequently, modernizing emergency and rescue laws to enable data sharing, joint training, and coordinated command is a fundamental prerequisite for improving multi-agency response [23].

Limitations and generalizability

As a qualitative grounded theory study, the findings are contextually rich and analytically generalisable to similar situations, but not statistically. Participants were from a particular professional cohort of managers and professionals from three Iranian regions, despite the fact that they represented a wide spectrum of viewpoints. The experiences and opinions of first responders and the broader public might not be adequately represented by this. Furthermore, even if the model and suggested tactics are based on actual data, they are still only conceptual frameworks that need more practical testing and validation. The idea of theoretical transferability is applicable: scholars and practitioners in other low- and middle-income nations or disaster-prone environments with comparable fragmented governance structures can use our model as a sensitising framework.

Conclusion

This study enhances the theory of "Fragile Cooperation" by illustrating how complex socio-institutional dynamics and fragmented municipal governance fundamentally hinder the effectiveness of Iran's integrated emergency response system.

The results show that while locally adaptable tactics like paternalistic leadership and selfless risk-taking can offer temporary, context-specific resilience, they are ultimately insufficient to guarantee dependable, system-wide coordination. The formal institutionalisation of important facilitators, such as interoperable data-sharing platforms, standardised joint operational protocols, and unified communication systems, is necessary to achieve long-term interagency cooperation.

Future research should take two critical approaches in order to translate these findings into meaningful policy and practice. First, comprehensive quantitative research on important performance metrics, like as response times and patient outcomes, is necessary to objectively assess the consequences of some structural actions, such as declaring a single national emergency. Second, comparative cross-national study across varied governance and cultural contexts is crucial. These studies can pinpoint the fundamental ideas of successful multi-agency crisis management and make it clear how these ideas might be modified for regional institutional environments, thereby assisting in the creation of stronger and more resilient emergency response systems.

As a qualitative grounded theory study, the results are contextually rich and analytically generalisable to similar settings, but not statistically generalisable. The participants were selected from a certain professional cohort of managers and professionals in three Iranian regions, despite the fact that they represented a wide variety of viewpoints. The experiences and opinions of first responders and the broader public might not be adequately represented by this. Furthermore, even if the model and suggested tactics are based on actual data, they are still only conceptual frameworks that need more practical testing and validation.

To summarise, optimising responses to catastrophic disasters requires a systemic reform. A unified public interface, interconnected operational systems, and a supporting legal and regulatory environment should all be part of this transition. When combined, these components can transform disjointed emergency services into a coordinated, flexible, and more efficient response ecosystem.
