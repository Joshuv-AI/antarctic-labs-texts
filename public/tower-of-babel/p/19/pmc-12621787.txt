Polyglycolic acid-cyanoacrylate complex for prevention of major intestinal anastomotic leakage in a rat model: an experimental animal study
Authors: Chinock Cheong, Hyun Myung Doo, YeonUk Ju, Jun-Woo Bong, Sang-Hee Kang, Sun-Il Lee, Byung Wook Min, You-Jin Jang, Won Jun Seo, Jong-Han Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVarious materials and techniques have been explored to prevent anastomotic leakage (AL). Polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheets and cyanoacrylate (CA) glue have been investigated for their potential in wound healing and fistula closure. This study aims to assess the feasibility of PGA-CA complex (PCC) to prevent AL by developing a major leakage model in rats.MethodsAfter the creation of the major leakage model in the jejunum of rat, the leakage site was treated with each material individually and in combination. Rats were categorized into the control, CA, PGA, and PCC groups.ResultsA total of 40 rats were included, with one animal from the CA and PGA groups having expired. Gross intra-abdominal conditions showed no significant difference between the groups. Median intraluminal bursting pressure was 120.0 (range, 102.5–138.0) in the CA group and 138.0 (range, 127.0–147.0) in the PCC group (P = 0.286). Pathologic evaluation revealed fibroblast activation was significantly different in the PCC group (P = 0.025).ConclusionNo significant differences were observed in the macroscopic intra-abdominal findings or intraluminal bursting pressure between the groups. The only difference in PCC group was enhanced fibroblast activation at moderate levels compare to the control group. Further research is warranted to optimize material combinations and validate efficacy in larger preclinical and clinical settings.Keywords: Anastomosis leakage, Cyanoacrylates, Gastrointestinal, Polyglycolic acid, Prevention & control

INTRODUCTION

Anastomotic leakage remains one of the most serious complications following gastrointestinal surgery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 2.8% to 30% following colorectal procedures and 1.5% to 1.88% after gastric cancer surgery [1,2,3]. This complication has been demonstrated to increase morbidity and mortality rates (12%–30%) and significantly extend hospital stays, while also increasing healthcare costs [4]. Recent studies have shown that patients with anastomotic leakage incur higher costs for index hospitalization and experience longer hospital stays compared to those without leakage [4,5].

A variety of materials and techniques have been investigated in an attempt to prevent anastomotic leakage. Polyglycolic acid (PGA) sheets have shown promise in wound healing and anastomosis reinforcement, which were found to be effective in reinforcing the anastomosis and reducing the incidence of leakage. Previous studies demonstrated the efficacy of PGA sheets in preventing anastomotic leakage for rectal surgery [6] and endoscopic application of PGA sheets for closing enterocutaneous fistular closure with endoscopic application [7,8]. Cyanoacrylate (CA) glue has also been explored for its potential in wound healing and fistula closure. Several case reports described the successful use of CA adhesive for the closure of an enterocutaneous fistula [9]. This minimally invasive approach with CA and PGA successfully achieved complete healing and closure of refractory enterocutaneous fistular closure [10].

While several case reports have documented successful outcomes using these materials individually or in combination with others, there is a notable lack of systematic research investigating their combined efficacy in preventing major anastomotic leakage. The absence of in-vivo experiments specifically designed to evaluate this combination limits our understanding of its true potential and safety profile in diverse surgical scenarios.

This study aims to evaluate the leakage prevention capabilities of a PGA-CA complex (PCC) by developing a major leakage model in rats. By focusing on a major leakage scenario, this research aims to provide valuable insights into the potential of PCC as a novel approach to reducing the incidence and severity of anastomotic leakage in gastrointestinal surgery.

METHODS

Ethics statement

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Korea University (approval number: KOREA-2023-0087) and conducted in compliance with national animal welfare regulations. Sample sizes were determined based on historical effect sizes from analogous animal models and compliance with ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines to balance scientific rigor with ethical animal use.

Materials

PGA sheets (Neoveil, Gunze Co., Ltd.) and n-butyl 90% + 2-octyl CA 10% (Liquiband, Hanmi Science Co., Ltd.) were used individually or in complex form to confirm the ability to prevent anastomotic leakage.

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 40), weighing 250–300 g, were obtained from a licensed laboratory animal supplier. The rats were housed in a controlled environment (22 ± 2 ℃, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to standard rat chow and water. A potential attrition rate of 10%–15% was factored in, accounting for postoperative mortality or unforeseen complications during the experiment, leading to the inclusion of 10 animals per group. Rats were allocated sequentially to each group. The models were divided into 4 categories: control (n = 10), CA glue (CA group, n = 9), PGA sheet (PGA group, n = 9), and PGA + CA complex (PCC group, n = 10) following the death of 2 subjects to allow for comparison of individual and combined treatments, ensuring that the study design could effectively assess the efficacy and safety of the materials used (Fig. 1).

Major leakage model in rats. (A) Operative field preparation. (B) Control group. (C) Cyanoacrylate glue group. (D) Polyglycolic acid sheet group. (E) PGA + CA complex group.

Surgical procedure

Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane without preoperative fasting period (3% for induction, 1.5%–2% for maintenance). A midline laparotomy was performed, followed by an antimesenteric incision extending halfway around the circumference of the small bowel. Then, major leakage was created by placing a single interrupted 6–0 polypropylene suture at each end of the incision, leaving most of the incision unsutured to simulate a major leakage site. Following the creation of the major leakage model, the animals were assigned to one of 3 treatment groups to evaluate the efficacy and safety of single or combination use of materials in preventing major anastomotic leakage. For CA group, a thin layer of CA adhesive was meticulously applied to the anastomosis, taking care to achieve uniform coverage without excess application. In PGA group, a sheet of PGA was carefully applied to cover the anastomosis site, ensuring complete circumferential coverage of the leakage area. In PCC group, a combination approach was employed where a PGA sheet was first placed over the anastomosis site, followed by an application of CA adhesive on top of the PGA sheet. This combined application was designed to potentially enhance the sealing effect and provide a more robust barrier against leakage. The control group received no additional treatment. The abdominal wall was closed in 2 layers using 4–0 polypropylene sutures.

Postoperative period

Postoperatively, rats were monitored for signs of distress, dehydration, or infection. Ketoprofen (2-(3-benzoylphenyl) propionic acid, 3 mg/kg, subcutaneously) was administered immediately after surgery for pain management and antibiotics were not given. Rats had free access to water 12 hours after surgery and to food 24 hours after surgery. Planned second laparotomy for gross exploration, intraluminal bursting pressure (IBP) measurement, and tissue sampling was performed on postoperative day (POD) 5 for all surviving animals, after which the animals were euthanized. For subjects requiring early euthanasia due to humane endpoint criteria (cumulative score ≥8 or a score of 3 in any individual category), the same protocol (exploration, IBP measurement, and tissue collection) was conducted immediately and after the exploration. During the exploration, the experimental subjects were compared for gross adhesion, soiling, necrosis, and peritonitis to investigate the efficacy of each material, individually or in combination in preventing major anastomotic leakage.

Intraluminal bursting pressure measurement

IBP was measured only in cases where the integrity of the anastomosis major leakage site was maintained. Rats were anesthetized using the same protocol as described for the surgical procedure. A midline laparotomy was performed to expose the anastomotic site. If the anastomosis appeared intact, one end of the anastomotic segment was ligated, and the other was connected to an infusion pump and pressure transducer. The segment was gradually inflated with normal saline (2 mL/min) until a leak was detected or the pressure reached 300 mmHg. The maximum pressure recorded before leakage or reaching 300 mmHg was defined as the bursting pressure. In cases where the anastomosis had already dehisced or showed clear signs of leakage prior to measurement, the bursting pressure was recorded as 0 mmHg.

Histopathological examination

Anastomotic segments were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5-μm thickness. Sections were stained with H&E and Masson’s trichrome. A blinded pathologist evaluated the slides for neutrophil, macrophage, giant cell and mast cell infiltration, fibroblast activity, and collagen deposition using a semiquantitative scoring system (0–4 scale).

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were analyzed using the Student t-test and chi-sqaure test, respectively. Mann-Whitney U-test or Fisher exact test was used in the nonparametric analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software ver. 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Perioperative status

Planned intra-abdominal exploration was performed in 38 animals on POD 4 or 5. Necropsy revealed severe soiling and bowel necrosis in the abdominal cavity of experimental subjects. The intra-abdominal peritonitis status was scored and categorized into groups based on the criteria of soiling, adhesion, necrosis and peritonitis Table 1. Although severe soiling (score ≥3) was observed more frequently in the control group than in the others, it did not differ significantly (P = 0.772). Adhesion and necrosis status were also similar across the groups (P = 0.620 and P = 0.676, respectively). The peritonitis score, the percentage of peritonitis score ≥5, was higher in the control group compared to others, though not with statistical significance (control, 30.0% vs. CA, 11.1% vs. PGA, 11.1% vs. PCC, 20.0%; P = 0.671) (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Intraluminal bursting pressure

IBP was measured during the second laparotomy (Fig. 3). In most subjects in the control and PGA groups, IBP measurement was unsuccessful due to significant loss of anastomotic integrity. IBP is quantified within a group exhibiting robust and sustained integrity at a major leak site and IBP ranged between 102.5 and 138.0 in 4 of the 9 subjects in the CA group. In 6 subjects from PCC group, IBP ranged from 127.0 to 147.0 (P = 0.286) (Table 2).

Histology scoring for inflammation and fibrotic ability

Histopathological evaluation revealed that neutrophil scores were elevated in the CA and PCC groups, though the increase was not statistically significant (neutrophil score ≥3: control, 40.0% vs. CA, 77.7%; PGA, 55.5% vs. PCC, 50.0%; P = 0.147). Macrophage infiltration and necrosis rates were similarly distributed (P = 0.734 and P = 0.853, respectively), as were giant cells and mast cells ratio (P = 0.091 and P = 0.367, respectively). Fibroblast activation scores ≥3 were significantly higher in the control group compared to the others (C, 80.0% vs. CA, 22.2% vs. PGA, 33.3% vs. PCC, 20.0%; P = 0.025) (Fig. 4). Collagen deposition was also not significantly different between groups (P = 0.155) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The objective of this study was to identify the optimal material or complex to prevent major anastomosis leakage. No significant differences were observed between PCC and the other materials in terms of intraperitoneal gross exploration and other pathological findings. While moderate fibroblast activation was noted in the PCC group, this did not translate to functional improvements, underscoring the need for further mechanistic studies.

Various therapeutic nonsurgical or surgical methods for treating anastomosis leakage such as clipping, stent insertion, endoscopic suturing, and endoscopic internal drainage or vacuum application, have been explored. Although endoscopic methods are less invasive, complications occurring such as stent migration and bowel perforation may still occur. Endoscopic clipping has been suggested for smaller leaks (<1 cm), though treatable size was limited [11]. Another researcher reported a successful stent placement rate of 85.98% for leakage after bariatric surgery; according to a systematic review and meta-analysis, the stent migration rate remained 18.65% [12]. Alternative approaches, such as the endoscopic submucosal injection of gel-forming solutions, have shown promise in animal models but may pose toxicity concerns [13]. Intraoperative anastomosis reinforcement suturing and the use of transanal drainage tubes have also been investigated, but their effectiveness in preventing leakage has been limited in certain patients [14,15].

Tissue adhesives such as CA glue and fibrin glue have emerged for use not only in hemostasis but also in wound closure and fistula treatment [16]. Recently, CA glue has been widely utilized for various purposes, including hardware fixation, approximate incision and lacerations, and stabilizing skin graft. CA glue is a liquid that polymerizes in the presence of moisture to form an adhesive layer [17,18]. CA glue has seemed to be safe, causing less toxicity and tissue inflammation while promoting faster wound closure. Previous researchers represented successful use of CA glues for endoscopic applications for bile leakage, pancreatic fistula and gastrointestinal trac fistula [19,20,21]. CA can be applied endoscopically via needle injections and rapidly solidifies upon contact with water and blood. Although Kotzampassi and Eleftheriadis [22] demonstrated a high rate of successful cases (96.7%) using tissue sealant such as CA glue in the upper and lower gastrointestinal, no studies have evaluated the effectiveness of this approach in preventing anastomotic leakage.

Although various tissue adhesives are composed of either n-butyl or 2-octyl CA, those with 100% n-butyl may cause tissue rigidity due to rapid polymerization. To enhance flexibility and reduce tissue irritation, we used CA glue, which contains 90% n-butyl and 10% 2-octyl CA in this research.

PGA sheets are absorbable material that induce inflammatory response. PGA sheets are highly pliable and can be easily attachable to the surface of intestinal anastomosis site or inserted into abdominal wall defects [23]. They have been proven effective in preventing postoperative pancreatic fistula, pulmonary fistula or bile leakage [23,24]. Additionally, PGA sheets have also been used to inhibit anastomosis leakage in gastrointestinal surgery [6,8,25]. The reaction between PGA sheets and host tissue is temporal dynamic because PGA sheets are absorbable through hydrolysis approximately 2 or 3 months after attachment. When PGA sheets are applied to intestinal tissue, they can induce the production of granulation tissue, enhance fibroblast activity and collagen tissue formation [26]. Thus, PGA sheets can serve as scaffolds, facilitating the infiltration of inflammatory cells and fibroblasts [27]. However, if a PGA sheet detaches prematurely, they cannot reduce fistula or major leakage. When leakage occurs after PGA application, detached sheets fail to seal the site, leading to significant anastomosis leakage. In such cases, PGA sheets are limited to preventing only minor anastomotic leakage.

Although PGA sheets have been combined with fibrin glue to reduce gastrointestinal bleeding after endoscopic submucosal dissection and to prevent esophageal stricture [28,29], fibrin glue can cause the formation of blood clots due to its adhesive ability and procoagulant effect. In addition, it is inconvenient for storage and transportation in clinical application. It also has a potential risk for infection, as it is derived from human or animal sources [30]. To overcome those disadvantages of fibrin glue in combination with PGA, Tada et al. reported combination sandwich therapy using PGA and CA for refractory enterocutaneous fistular for advanced rectal cancer [10]. Although these reports were successful, they demonstrated their results in case reports with varying procedural protocols.

Taken together, these findings indicate that while the PCC demonstrated an acceptable safety profile and moderate fibroblast activation, its overall efficacy in preventing major anastomotic leakage remains inconclusive. Although the PCC group exhibited modest enhancements in tissue repair and maintained anastomotic integrity when compared to the control group, these differences did not attain statistical significance.

In the long-term outcomes, the rate of in-vivo degradation of CA as well as tissue reactivity may vary depending on the ratio of these components. N-butyl CA may increase acute or chronic inflammation because it has been associated with increased infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages at the application site. PGA sheets are also useful for reinforcing tissue and promoting healing after surgery; however, they may also cause adverse effects such as chronic inflammation, fibrosis, or tissue adhesion over the long-term. However, since this is the first study to demonstrate the combined use of PGA and CA in intestinal anastomosis, the long-term biocompatibility of PCC should be investigated in further research and may provide valuable foundational data for future.

Consequently, further studies with larger sample sizes and optimized application protocols are warranted to clarify the potential benefits of PCC and to determine its clinical applicability in preventing anastomotic leakage and long-term biocompatibility.

This preclinical study did not demonstrate the efficacy of the PCC group in preventing anastomosis leakage, but it is meaningful for several reasons. First, it is the first time that a major leakage animal model was applied. When anastomosis leakage occurs, minor leakage can often be controlled with conservative management. Therefore, a major leakage model that could endanger the clinical course of patients in actual clinical practice was established and applied in this study. The establishment of this model enabled the confirmation of the functional and pathological characteristics of the leakage prevention materials that are currently employed. Furthermore, 2 materials that have been reported in actual clinical practice were utilized in combination to ascertain whether there was a synergistic effect when compared with the use of each material individually. While the combination of the 2 materials did not demonstrate a significant effect in this study, the major leakage model which used in this research can serve as a platform for the confirmation of the preclinical effects of other materials or their combination.

Notwithstanding the considerable strength of this research, the study is subject to several limitations. First, the preventive effect of anastomosis leakage in the PCC group could not be clearly identified in the gross finding. However, the combination of PGA and CA caused sealed-off anastomotic leakage and also induced moderate or greater adhesion to adjacent tissues. We could not confirm through long-term follow-up whether adhesion at the anastomosis site improved after POD 5. Secondly, the sealing effect may vary depending on the type of CA used. In this study, n-butyl 10% ± 90% was applied, but different results may be observed when using 100% 2-octyl. Future studies are expected to confirm differences based on the composition of CA. Thirdly, this study evaluated macroscopic outcomes; tissue remodeling in anastomotic repair involves intricate cytokine networks. Future research should explore PCC’s molecular effects, including its modulation of matrix metalloproteinases, growth factors (e.g., transforming growth factor beta), and inflammatory mediators (e.g., interleukin 6) to identify biomarkers predictive of healing success. Such mechanistic insights could guide personalized material applications in high-risk patients. Finally, although CA tissue adhesive was used for medical purposes, the toxicity of materials in the intraabdominal organs should be evaluated when combined with PGA sheets.

In conclusion, this study establishes a novel major anastomotic leakage model in rats, providing a platform for evaluating future therapeutic strategies. While the PCC combination did not demonstrate significant leakage prevention in this model, further studies are necessary to investigate anastomotic leakage preventive material in terms of safety and efficacy in both animal models and human trials before clinical implementation.
