A final push strategy for malaria elimination by 2030: Lessons from community health workers in Chhattisgarh
Authors: Senthilkumar Ramasamy, Aarna Galhotra, Suganya Panneerselvam, Sanjana Agrawal
Published: 2024

Malaria poses a most severe life-threatening vector-borne disease and a significant public health problem globally. Its elimination is a global priority due to its high mortality and morbidity rates. The recent World Malaria Report 2023 emphasizes that in 2022, nearly half of the world’s population was at risk of malaria, resulting in approximately 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths worldwide.[1] After Africa, the Southeast Asia region had the second-highest malaria cases globally in 2021, accounting for about 5.4 million patients and 9000 deaths.[1] In Southeast Asia, India has the highest suspected, unconfirmed number of malaria cases. In the year 2021, India accounted for 83% of estimated cases and 82% of all malaria-related deaths in Southeast Asia, with 2 million confirmed cases and 1000 recorded deaths annually. Recent data show an 8% increase in malaria cases in this region.[2] Apart from seasonal outbreaks in India, malaria cases are concentrated primarily in five states: Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, and Maharashtra. Overall, approximately 66% of malaria infections in India are caused by Plasmodium falciparum, and Plasmodium vivax causes 34%.[1] The disease predominantly affects impoverished regions in tropical and subtropical areas, and 80% of malaria cases reported are confined to areas where 20% of the population resides—in tribal, hilly, hard-to-reach, or inaccessible areas. Vulnerable people, including infants, children under 5 years, pregnant women, travelers, and individuals with human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, face higher risks of severe infection. Despite its preventable and curable nature, malaria continues to cause chaos in many parts of the world, especially in Southeast Asia and India. These staggering numbers underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions and comprehensive strategies to combat the disease effectively.

The Global Technical Strategy (GTS) for malaria 2016–2030 sets a target to achieve a minimum 90% reduction in global malaria incidence and mortality rates by 2030.[3] This target is supported by three key pillars: ensuring universal access to malaria prevention, diagnosis, and treatment; accelerating efforts toward elimination and achieving malaria-free status; and transforming malaria surveillance into a fundamental intervention. Aligned with the GTS and the Asia Pacific Leaders Malaria Alliance Malaria Elimination Roadmap, the National Framework for Malaria Elimination in India has established specific targets for 2016–2030.[1] This target includes the complete elimination of malaria, with zero indigenous cases, across the country by 2030 and maintaining a malaria-free status in areas where malaria transmission has been successfully interrupted and preventing the reintroduction of malaria.

Malaria surveillance is an integral part of the primary healthcare system, and it helps prevent and control malaria in the community. Even though malaria is both preventable and curable, it remains a significant public health challenge due to major challenges identified in malaria-endemic areas, which are limited access to healthcare, inadequate resources, lack of knowledge, difficulties in diagnosing asymptomatic cases, and an increase in migrant workers. Continuous exposure to plasmodium parasites can lead to asymptomatic carriers, and they become a fundamental reservoir of parasites. Surveillance plays a vital role in the fight against malaria, and elimination cannot be achieved without quality surveillance systems.

Chhattisgarh, a state in India with seven southern districts, contributes around 50–60% of reported annual malaria cases. These areas are hard-to-reach forest areas and tribal populations with the annual parasite incidence ranging from 10.4 to 53.6. The Malaria Mukt Bastar Abhiyan was launched in 2019–20.[4] A comprehensive approach was followed in this Abhiyan, including active mass screening of door-to-door surveys and malaria tests done using rapid diagnostic kits with the help of community health workers. The primary objective of this Abhiyan was to identify both symptomatic and asymptomatic (afebrile) carriers and ensure 100% coverage of the target population. The malaria-positive individuals were marked on the toe by permanent ink, and a wall sticker was pasted outside the surveyed houses to identify the place and left-out persons. Treatment cards were issued, and Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) ensured regular follow-ups under the supervision of rural health officers. After the completion of treatment, empty blisters of medicines were collected to enhance drug compliance. The severe/complicated cases were referred to higher centers. Pregnant women, lactating mothers, and children under 5 years old were explicitly targeted. ASHAs were whistled to create awareness for using long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) and source reduction activities. Heath education, entomological surveillance, intersectoral coordination, and recording of cases using Google sheet activities were also done. These efforts led to a 65% year-on-year decline in malaria cases and a significant drop in test positivity rates [Figure 1].[5]

Community health workers serve as the backbone of the primary health care system. They are the first contact for people seeking healthcare services. They work primarily in remote areas and play a pivotal role in raising awareness, conducting screening, and ensuring treatment adherence. They are well accepted by the community not only because they are part of them but also due to their accountability to the communities for their health-related activities. These frontline health workers are mostly Community Health Officers, Rural Health Officers, and ASHAs, and apart from their other responsibilities, they are also responsible for early diagnosis and treatment of malaria cases. They play an essential role in conducting surveillance, administering diagnostic tests, conducting door-to-door surveys, providing health education, raising health awareness on LLIN usage, providing treatment support and follow-up, and referring complicated cases when necessary. To eliminate malaria by 2030, the identified challenges should be addressed, including understanding the social and cultural beliefs and local perceptions and addressing the basic demands of health workers. Regular training should be conducted to address the identified gaps and monitored regularly to ensure they are on the right track of elimination. The challenges of sustainable financing, availability of drugs, and the diagnosis of malaria should be provided to improve completion rates. Implementing a digital platform for robust surveillance and geospatial mapping interventions can enhance malaria control efforts. If these lessons are incorporated, malaria could be eliminated before 2030. Community health workers can play a vital role in social behavior change in the community by creating awareness[6] about environmental modifications and personal protection using LLINs. With a renewed focus on empowering community health workers and implementing targeted interventions, we can pave the way toward a malaria-free future, ensuring better health and well-being for vulnerable populations worldwide.
