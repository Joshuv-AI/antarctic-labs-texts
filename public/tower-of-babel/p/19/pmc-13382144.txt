Spatial and single-cell profiling identify NDRG1⁺ macrophages as key hallmark of angiogenic remodeling and platinum resistance in HGSOC

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) remains the most lethal gynecologic malignancy, with the majority of patients eventually developing resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy [1,2]. While genetic alterations in tumor cells contribute to disease progression, increasing evidence suggests that the tumor microenvironment (TME)—particularly tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—plays a decisive role in shaping therapeutic response, immune evasion, metabolic adaptation, and angiogenesis [[3], [4], [5]]. HGSOC is characterized by extensive desmoplasia, hypoxia, and ascites dissemination, conditions that are well-suited to drive macrophage recruitment and functional reprogramming. However, despite the abundance of TAMs in ovarian tumors, their phenotypic diversity, spatial organization, and mechanistic contribution to chemotherapy resistance remain poorly resolved [6].

Macrophages constitute a major immune component in HGSOC lesions and have been linked to immunosuppression, cytokine-driven tumor progression, and matrix remodeling. Yet, traditional M1–M2 classification fails to capture the full spectrum of TAM plasticity, and a comprehensive delineation of macrophage states in HGSOC has been lacking [6,7]. Therefore, rather than assigning macrophages to binary M1/M2 states, we focused on transcriptionally resolved TAM subsets that may capture disease-relevant functional programs, including hypoxia adaptation, matrix remodeling, angiogenic signaling, and therapy-associated macrophage reprogramming. Moreover, how specific macrophage subsets may contribute to angiogenic remodeling—a known hallmark of aggressive disease and poor drug penetration—remains an open question. Decoding macrophage heterogeneity and identifying prognostically relevant TAM phenotypes could therefore uncover critical determinants of tumor behavior and provide opportunities for therapeutic intervention [8].

Recent technological advances in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics have enabled high-resolution mapping of immune landscapes within solid tumors. These approaches are particularly powerful for dissecting macrophage states and their interactions with neighboring cells in situ, allowing precise identification of TAM subsets that occupy hypoxic niches, vascular interfaces, or chemotherapy-resistant regions. When combined with bulk deconvolution, machine-learning–based modeling, and pseudotime inference, these tools offer an integrated strategy to reveal both the phenotypic diversity and functional evolution of macrophages in HGSOC.

Here, we leveraged multi-omics profiling—including immune deconvolution, spatial transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, and bulk transcriptomic modeling—to define the macrophage ecosystem of HGSOC and investigate its prognostic significance. We found that macrophage enrichment is a fundamental and early-established feature of the HGSOC microenvironment, occurring irrespective of chemotherapy response and preferentially localized to resistant tumor regions. Single-cell analysis revealed seven transcriptionally distinct TAM subsets with divergent functional programs, among which we identified a highly conserved, pro-angiogenic macrophage population—Macro4—that displayed strong hypoxia-associated, extracellular matrix–remodeling, and VEGF/SPP1 signaling signatures. This macrophage state was consistently associated with poor overall survival across multiple patient cohorts.

By integrating single-cell–derived signatures with bulk datasets, we developed a Macro4-based Random Survival Forest (RSF) risk model that robustly stratified patient prognosis and outperformed conventional clinical variables. The resulting risk score reflected a dynamic shift from homeostatic to angiogenic macrophage states, suggesting that progressive TAM reprogramming toward the Macro4 phenotype underlies aggressive tumor behavior. Furthermore, ligand–receptor analysis identified Macro4 as a major communication hub engaging endothelial cells through VEGF and SPP1 pathways, implicating this subset as a hallmark of pathological angiogenesis and microenvironmental remodeling.

Pseudotime reconstruction revealed that Macro4 aligns with a trajectory transitioning from CXCL9⁺/CXCL11⁺ inflammatory activation toward hypoxia-driven metabolic adaptation. Within this trajectory, we identified NDRG1 as a key regulatory gene enriched across hypoxia-related GO categories, associated with poor prognosis, and selectively expressed within Macro4 macrophages. Spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunofluorescence confirmed that NDRG1+SPP1+ macrophages formed discrete angiogenic niches and were enriched in platinum-resistant tissue. Clinically, NDRG1 expression correlated with chemotherapy resistance and reduced progression-free survival, linking hypoxia-driven macrophage adaptation to therapeutic failure.

Together, these findings delineate a macrophage-centered axis of hypoxia adaptation, angiogenic signaling, and chemoresistance in HGSOC. By defining Macro4 and identifying NDRG1 as a key hallmark of this state, our study provides mechanistic insight into TAM-driven tumor progression and lays a foundation for the development of macrophage-targeted or hypoxia-directed therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Bulk RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics datasets

Publicly available multi-omics data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) parent series GSE274659 [9]. The pretreatment bulk RNA-seq subset used for immune deconvolution consisted of 5 platinum-sensitive and 5 platinum-resistant HGSOC samples and corresponded to GSE273637, a subset of GSE274659. Four paired spatial transcriptomics datasets were also obtained from the same parent series GSE274659. Immune cell composition in the GSE273637 pretreatment bulk RNA-seq subset was estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm implemented through the IOBR R package [10].

In addition, bulk RNA-seq data with accompanying clinical annotations were obtained from the TCGA-OV cohort (n = 377). To expand validation cohorts, three microarray datasets generated on the Affymetrix GPL570 platform were downloaded from GEO: GSE9899 (n = 167) [11], GSE63885 (n = 70) [12], and GSE26193 (n = 79) [13]. Only patients diagnosed with serous ovarian carcinoma were included in subsequent analyses.

To quantify macrophage subtypes in TCGA-OV cohort, we performed reference-based cell-type deconvolution using BisqueRNA [14] following the standard pipeline. Bulk RNA-seq data for TCGA-OV were applied as raw count matrices and converted into an ExpressionSet object. Single-cell RNA-seq profiles from primary tumor tissue [15] were used as the reference dataset.

Single-cell RNA sequencing dataset

Single-cell transcriptomic data were obtained from a recently published study comprising 7 platinum-sensitive and 3 platinum-resistant HGSOC samples [15]. Raw count matrices and corresponding metadata were downloaded from the authors’ repository and used for downstream preprocessing and cell-type annotation. To quantitatively compare the distributional preference of cell types across samples, we computed the Ro/e (ratio of observed-to-expected frequency) metric for each cell type.

Functional status signatures were generated from previous research [16]. These signatures encompassed key biological processes relevant to tumor-associated macrophage functional modalities.

Gene trajectory analysis

Gene trajectory analysis was performed using the GeneTrajectory [17] R package, following the standard workflow described in the official tutorial (https://klugerlab.github.io/GeneTrajectory/articles/GeneTrajectory.html). GeneTrajectory infers dynamic gene progression patterns by embedding genes into a low-dimensional manifold derived from graph-based Wasserstein distances.

A cell–cell k-nearest neighbor graph was first constructed, and the graph-based distance matrix was computed using a k value of 10. To improve computational stability, coarse graining was applied, during which gene expression and graph distances were aggregated into 1000 meta-cells.

The refined gene–gene distance matrix and gene embeddings were subsequently used to extract gene trajectories. Trajectories were inferred using the default random-walk–based strategy with a neighborhood size k = 10, and the temporal parameters were set to t_list = [2,3] to capture both short- and intermediate-range gene progression patterns.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) detection

Differential expression analysis in the TCGA-OV cohort was performed using the limma package [18]. Raw count matrices were first transformed using the voom method to model the mean–variance relationship, followed by the fitting of linear models with empirical Bayes moderation. Genes with a q-value < 0.05 were considered initially significant.

Gene enrichment analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the enrichGO function implemented in the clusterProfiler package [19], with biological process (BP) terms selected as the primary ontology category. Significant GO terms were identified based on a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using the GSEA function in clusterProfiler, employing the MSigDB HALLMARK, GO Biological Process (BP), and GO Molecular Function (MF) gene sets as reference collections. Genes were ranked based on their differential expression statistics, and enrichment significance was determined according to the default permutation-based procedure.

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis for overall survival (OS) was conducted using the survival and survminer R packages, and the optimal cut-off value was determined using survminer. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate OS-associated prognostic markers. For NDRG1, progression-free survival (PFS) analysis was additionally performed using the KM-plotter website (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) [20]. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Integrated machine learning method

A total of 101 machine learning approaches were implemented, including individual algorithms such as Random Survival Forest (RSF), Elastic-Net (Enet), Stepwise Cox regression (StepCox), CoxBoost, partial least squares regression for Cox models (plsRcox), SuperPC, gradient boosting machine (GBM), survival support vector machine (survival SVM), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), and Ridge regression, as well as their pairwise combinations.

For two-step combination models, the first algorithm was used for feature selection, and the second algorithm was used to construct the predictive survival model based on the filtered variables.

The TCGA-OV cohort was used as the training cohort for model construction, whereas GSE9899, GSE63885, and GSE26193 were used as external validation cohorts for the Macro4-derived risk score. Candidate features were selected from conserved Macro4-associated genes. For the RSF model, the optimal mtry parameter was selected according to out-of-bag prediction error, and variable importance was estimated using the randomForestSRC framework. Model performance was compared with conventional clinical variables, including stage and grade, using multivariable Cox regression, time-dependent AUC analysis, and decision curve analysis.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing and analysis

Processed single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from a recent study [15]. After quality control and integration, the final single-cell atlas contained 45,146 cells. To mitigate sample-specific batch effects and enhance cross-sample comparability, reciprocal PCA (RPCA) integration was performed using the Seurat workflow with the first 30 principal components. Shared nearest-neighbor graphs were constructed using the integrated reduction, and clustering was performed at resolution 0.4 for the global atlas.

Macrophages were subsequently subsetted and reclustered at resolution 0.3, yielding seven macrophage states, termed Macro1–Macro7. Signature scores for predefined gene sets were computed using the AddModuleScore function in the Seurat R package [21]. Differentially expressed genes between groups were identified using the FindMarkers function. For macrophage subset marker analysis, representative markers were selected using min.pct > 0.25 and log2 fold change > 0.25. To validate the biological relevance of our identified macrophage clusters, we compared subgroup-specific signatures with publicly available macrophage atlases. The top marker genes for each cluster from the external dataset were retrieved, and the overlap between our gene signatures and external signatures was quantified using the Jaccard similarity coefficient, defined as the size of the intersection divided by the size of the union of the two gene sets.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication patterns were inferred using the CellChat R package [22]. Default preprocessing steps were applied to construct the CellChat object, followed by the identification of overexpressed ligands, receptors, and ligand–receptor interaction pairs. Communication probabilities were computed using a permutation-based framework, and signaling pathway aggregation was performed to identify dominant communication patterns across macrophage subsets.

Spatial transcriptomics data processing

Spots in the spatial transcriptomics (ST) datasets were first filtered based on standard quality-control thresholds, retaining spots with >300 detected features and a mitochondrial gene percentage <0.3. After quality filtering, data normalization and variance stabilization were performed using the SCTransform function in Seurat. Spatial deconvolution was performed using SpaCET [23] to estimate the relative abundance of tumor and stromal/immune components at each spatial spot. Visium raw matrices were imported using the create.SpaCET.object.10X function, followed by deconvolution with cancerType set to OV. The resulting spot-level estimates included malignant cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophage subpopulations.

For quantitative spatial niche analysis, HALLMARK_HYPOXIA and HALLMARK_ANGIOGENESIS scores were calculated for each spatial spot using MSigDB HALLMARK gene sets. A Macro4-like score was calculated using Macro4-enriched genes derived from the macrophage single-cell analysis, together with representative Macro4-associated genes including SPP1, NDRG1, VEGFA, MMP12, HK2, SLC2A1, and ABCA1. Spot-level Spearman correlations were performed to evaluate associations among NDRG1 expression, Macro4-like score, hypoxia score, angiogenesis score, SpaCET-derived endothelial abundance, and PECAM1/CD31 expression. For enrichment analysis, spots were stratified into high and low groups based on the within-sample median of hypoxia or angiogenesis scores, and differences in NDRG1 expression or Macro4-like score were tested using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests.

Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) assay

CD68, SPP1, ABCA1, and NDRG1 antibodies were used for the mIF assay. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4 % PFA, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100, and cultured with the primary antibody. After incubation with secondary antibodies, cell nuclei were counterstained using DAPI, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (XSP-63B, Shanghai Optical Instrument Factory).

Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analysis, and plotting were performed in R 4.3.1 software. Correlations between two continuous variables were calculated via Spearman’s correlation analysis. The chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables. The univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted with R package survival. The receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was implemented using R package timeROC, and the time-dependent ROC was calculated with R package reportROC with a 1-month interval. All statistical tests performed were two-sided and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used for two-group comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for comparisons among multiple macrophage subsets, and Spearman correlation analysis was used for spot-level spatial associations. All newly generated figures were exported at high resolution for publication.

Results

Essential role and variable phenotypes of macrophages in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC)

To define the immune landscape associated with platinum response in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), we first performed immune deconvolution (CIBERSORT) on the pretreatment bulk RNA-seq subset GSE273637 (n = 10; 5 platinum-sensitive and 5 platinum-resistant samples), which is included in the parent series GSE274659. M2-polarized macrophages emerged as the most abundant immune population across both resistant and sensitive tumors (Fig. 1A), highlighting macrophages as a stable and dominant component of the HGSOC microenvironment. Spatial transcriptomics further validated this observation in situ, revealing densely aggregated M2-like macrophage niches in both patient groups (Fig. 1B–C). These findings indicate that macrophage enrichment is a fundamental property of HGSOC tumors, irrespective of treatment response.

To characterize immune composition at higher resolution, we generated a single-cell transcriptomic atlas comprising ten primary HGSOC tumors (Supplementary Fig. S1A–D). All major epithelial, stromal, and immune lineages were identified (Fig. 1D). Quantitative analyses demonstrated that macrophages represented one of the most abundant immune subsets (Fig. 1E–F), with their infiltration particularly elevated in all three platinum-resistant tumors. Region-of-origin (Ro/e) mapping further showed preferential localization of macrophages to resistant tumor tissues (Fig. 1G), suggesting that macrophage accumulation is an early feature present prior to chemotherapy exposure.

To dissect their functional diversity, we re-clustered macrophages and identified seven transcriptionally distinct TAM states, termed Macro1–Macro7 (Fig. 1H–I). These states showed clear functional specialization, including inflammatory, complement-associated, lipid-associated, hypoxic/angiogenic, proliferative, FOLR2/MRC1-like, and monocyte-like programs. To clarify the marker identity of each subset, we added a marker dotplot showing representative genes across Macro1–Macro7 (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Macro4 exhibited prominent NDRG1, SPP1, HK2, SLC2A1, and MMP12 expression, supporting its identity as a hypoxia-associated and angiogenic TAM state, whereas Macro5 and Macro7 showed stronger proliferative programs.

At the patient level, platinum-resistant tumors showed increased Macro4 abundance, as measured by the absolute number of Macro4 cells among macrophage-reclustered single-cell profiles (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Because Macro4 shared several features with previously reported SPP1+ angiogenic TAMs, we further quantified a representative SPP1+ TAM signature across Macro1–Macro7. Macro4 showed the strongest enrichment of this SPP1+ TAM program and was significantly higher than the remaining macrophage subsets (Supplementary Fig. S3C–D). To further support this comparison at the gene level, we added a dotplot showing the expression of canonical SPP1+ TAM signature genes across Macro1–Macro7, which confirmed that Macro4 was enriched for multiple SPP1+ TAM-associated genes (Supplementary Fig. S3E). These results indicate that Macro4 overlaps with canonical SPP1+ TAM biology while being further distinguished by NDRG1 and hypoxia-associated programs.

This observation motivated us to explore whether TAM subsets could serve as prognostic indicators in ovarian cancer.

Establishment of a prognostic risk model based on the tumor-promoting Macro4 macrophage signature

To quantify macrophage phenotypes in bulk tumors, we constructed macrophage-subtype transcriptional signatures based on the single-cell data and applied BisqueRNA deconvolution to TCGA-OV samples (Fig. 2A). Among the seven macrophage states, only Macro4 demonstrated a robust association with poor overall survival, while low Macro7 abundance was also linked to adverse outcomes (Fig. 2B). Given that Macro4 exhibited a strong pro-angiogenic phenotype, we hypothesized that Macro4-related genes may capture tumor-promoting macrophage programs relevant to patient prognosis.

(C) Establishment of the risk model using integrated machine-learning algorithms. (D)The final risk score distribution across datasets. To identify robust Macro4-specific genes, we extracted those enriched in macrophages and selectively upregulated in Macro4 relative to other macrophage subsets. Cross-cohort filtering across TCGA-OV and multiple GEO datasets yielded a conserved set of 83 Macro4-associated genes. A systematic evaluation of ten machine-learning algorithms identified the Random Survival Forest (RSF) model as the optimal prognostic classifier. The resulting Macro4-derived risk score demonstrated clear stratification across datasets (Fig. 2C).

This prognostic signature exhibited strong predictive performance across four independent cohorts (Fig. 2E–I), establishing its generalizability. Taken together, these results indicate that the angiogenic Macro4 subset encapsulates macrophage programs that strongly influence ovarian cancer prognosis.

Because the Macro4-derived risk score was prognostically powerful, we next asked whether it provides information beyond clinical variables and reflects macrophage dynamics in tumors.

Integrated nomogram enables precise risk classification and reflects macrophage-state dynamics

Multivariable Cox analysis incorporating tumor stage and grade confirmed that the Macro4-derived risk score remained an independent predictor of survival (Fig. 3A). An integrated nomogram combining clinical variables with the risk score displayed high accuracy and calibration across all time points (Fig. 3B–C), while ROC and time-dependent AUC analyses demonstrated stable predictive power over the course of follow-up (Fig. 3D–E). Decision curve analysis further supported its clinical benefit (Fig. 3F). Although subgroup-based KM analyses were constrained by limited sample sizes in certain stage and grade strata, multivariable Cox regression, time-dependent AUC analysis, and decision curve analysis consistently supported the added prognostic value of the Macro4-derived risk score beyond conventional clinical variables.

Given the macrophage origin of the risk model, we examined its relationship with macrophage infiltration patterns. The risk score was positively correlated with Macro4 abundance and negatively correlated with Macro2 and Macro7 (Fig. 3G). Stratifying patients by incremental increases in the risk score revealed a progressive shift from homeostatic macrophage states toward angiogenic Macro4 dominance (Fig. 3H). This suggests that higher-risk tumors undergo macrophage-state remodeling toward pro-angiogenic, tumor-promoting phenotypes.

These findings motivated us to investigate the underlying biology of Macro4 and to determine how it promotes tumor progression.

Macro4-associated subgroups promote tumor progression by enhancing angiogenic signaling

To elucidate mechanisms linking Macro4 to poor prognosis, we compared transcriptomes between high- and low-risk groups. Although overall fold changes were modest, the M2-associated marker SPP1 was significantly upregulated in high-risk patients (Fig. 4A), suggesting selective amplification of tumor-promoting macrophage programs.

(C-D)Macro4 signature, malignant cell proportion and angiogenesis signature in resistant and sensitive patients (signatures were compared within uniform range). GSEA revealed strong enrichment of angiogenesis and hypoxia pathways in the high-risk group, whereas oxidative phosphorylation predominated in the low-risk group (Fig. 4B). These results imply that Macro4-related risk is linked to hypoxic–angiogenic remodeling.

CellChat analysis demonstrated that Macro4 exhibited heightened outgoing signaling, especially through VEGF and SPP1 pathways (Fig. 4E–F). Macro4 showed strong SPP1–ITGA5/ITGB1 communication with endothelial cells, suggesting that Macro4 serves as a key hallmark of endothelial activation. In addition, Macro4 engaged T cells and other immune populations through MIF-mediated signaling, consistent with its regulatory TAM identity.

Because Macro4 was linked to a hypoxic–angiogenic program, we next sought to identify the transcriptional regulators defining this macrophage phenotype.

Gene trajectory, functional enrichment, and survival analyses identify NDRG1 as a key regulator of the Macro4 phenotype

Pseudotime analysis revealed two major macrophage transcriptional trajectories (Fig. 5A–C), each reflecting distinct functional fates. Trajectory 1 progressed toward a proliferative terminus characterized by high expression of MKI67, TOP2A, and other cell-cycle regulators, consistent with the proliferative Macro7 state identified earlier. In contrast, Trajectory 2 began with strong activation of M1-like immune programs—including elevated CXCL11 and CXCL9—and gradually transitioned toward a terminal state enriched for hypoxia-associated metabolic genes (SLC2A1, HK2) and extracellular matrix–remodeling mediators (MMP12, MMP1). This continuum from inflammatory activation to hypoxic and matrix-remodeling phenotypes mirrors the functional features of Macro4 observed in our single-cell analysis.

To further delineate the molecular determinants of this Macro4-aligned trajectory, we performed GO enrichment analysis on Macro4-associated genes relative to other macrophage subsets (Fig. 5D). The most significantly enriched pathways were centered on hypoxia responses and signal transduction processes. Through this analysis, we identified six hub genes—NDRG1, ENO1, BNIP3, MT3, HK2, and LDHA—that participated in at least five enriched biological categories. Except for LDHA, all hub genes were retained in the Macro4-derived prognostic signature, supporting their biological relevance to the Macro4 phenotype.

Subsequent Cox proportional hazards modeling for overall survival highlighted NDRG1, HK2, and ENO1 as adverse OS-associated markers, each demonstrating hazard ratios >1 (Fig. 5E). Among these candidates, NDRG1 emerged as the most compelling regulator, as it was strongly represented in Macro4-enriched GO categories, prognostically adverse, and selectively expressed within the Macro4 macrophage population (Fig. 5F). This convergence across pseudotime dynamics, pathway enrichment, prognostic impact, and cell-type specificity led us to prioritize NDRG1 for downstream mechanistic investigation. Functional validation of NDRG1 in macrophages, including hypoxia-induced expression assays and NDRG1 knockdown or overexpression experiments, is ongoing and will be an important direction for future studies.

NDRG1 defines Macro4 macrophage niches and is associated with angiogenesis, chemoresistance, and poor clinical outcomes

Spatial transcriptomics revealed pronounced NDRG1 enrichment within Macro4-dense regions, coinciding with angiogenesis hotspots (Fig. 6A). Multiplex immunofluorescence validated that CD68+SPP1+ macrophages strongly expressed NDRG1 and frequently co-expressed ABCA1, a key gene in our RSF model, reinforcing their identity as Macro4 cells (Fig. 6B–C). Comparison with an external pan-cancer macrophage atlas (Coulton et al. [24]) revealed that our Macro4 subgroup shares significant transcriptomic similarities with subsets characterized by ICI-associated phenotypes, metabolic reprogramming, and ECM remodeling (Supplementary Fig. S2). To further quantify these spatial relationships, we performed spot-level niche analysis across the four spatial transcriptomic samples. Spatial maps showed the distribution of NDRG1 expression, Macro4-like score, HALLMARK hypoxia score, HALLMARK angiogenesis score, endothelial abundance, and M2 macrophage abundance across P2, P8, P16, and P17 samples (Supplementary Fig. S4A–D). NDRG1 expression and Macro4-like scores showed positive spatial correlations with HALLMARK hypoxia and angiogenesis scores (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Consistently, hypoxia-high and angiogenesis-high spots exhibited higher NDRG1 expression and Macro4-like scores than the corresponding low-score spots (Supplementary Fig. S4F). These findings support the spatial enrichment of NDRG1+ Macro4-like macrophage programs within hypoxic and angiogenic tumor niches.

Analysis of external single-cell datasets showed that NDRG1⁺ macrophages were preferentially localized to primary tumor lesions. Although NDRG1⁺ macrophage frequencies in non-tumor tissues were similar between chemotherapy-sensitive and -resistant patients, lymph nodes from sensitive patients exhibited significantly fewer NDRG1⁺ macrophages (Fig. 6D), implying reduced systemic distribution of pro-angiogenic TAMs.

Endothelial-cell proportions were higher in resistant tumors in single-cell data, consistent with Macro4’s angiogenic signaling, though not statistically significant (Kruskal–Wallis p = 0.21) (Fig. 6E). To further evaluate the relationship between NDRG1+ Macro4-like macrophage programs and endothelial niches, we correlated NDRG1 expression and Macro4-like scores with SpaCET-derived endothelial abundance and PECAM1/CD31 expression at the spatial spot level. Both NDRG1 expression and Macro4-like scores were associated with endothelial features, supporting their proximity to angiogenic endothelial regions (Supplementary Fig. S4G). Bulk transcriptomic correlations further demonstrated strong associations between NDRG1 expression and HALLMARK hypoxia and angiogenesis pathways (Fig. 6F).

Clinically, NDRG1 was significantly upregulated in chemotherapy non-responders (Fig. 6G), and high NDRG1 expression predicted shorter progression-free survival in KM-plotter analysis (Fig. 6H).

Together, these findings position NDRG1 as a defining molecular marker of the Macro4 macrophage state, linking hypoxia-driven macrophage adaptation to angiogenesis, chemoresistance, and adverse clinical outcomes.

Discussion

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) have long been recognized as central mediators of immune suppression and tissue remodeling across multiple cancer types [25], yet their precise contribution to disease progression and therapeutic response in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) has remained incompletely defined. Here, by integrating spatially resolved transcriptomics, single-cell profiling, bulk deconvolution, and pseudotime inference, we delineate a macrophage-centered microenvironmental program associated with angiogenesis, hypoxia adaptation, and chemotherapy resistance. Our findings converge on a distinct macrophage phenotype—Macro4—as a major orchestrator of tumor-promoting signaling and identify the hypoxia-responsive regulator NDRG1 as a defining molecular determinant of this state.

Our analyses reveal that macrophage accumulation is a fundamental and early-established property of HGSOC tumors. Both immune deconvolution and spatial transcriptomics indicated that M2-like macrophages are abundantly enriched across treatment-naïve tumors, independent of clinical response status. This suggests that the HGSOC microenvironment is predisposed toward a macrophage-dominant architecture even before chemotherapy exposure. Given the tight anatomical constraints and hypoxic niches characteristic of ovarian tumors [26], it is plausible that early TAM recruitment represents an adaptive mechanism co-opted by tumor cells to sustain growth under metabolic and immunologic stress. The preferential localization of macrophages within platinum-resistant regions further supports the notion that TAM remodeling is not solely a consequence of therapeutic selection but may precede and facilitate the emergence of resistance.

Single-cell transcriptomic reconstruction provides a higher-resolution view of this immune architecture [15], revealing seven transcriptionally distinct TAM states with clear functional specialization. Among these, Macro4 exhibited a unique pro-angiogenic and hypoxia-associated signature, characterized by elevated SPP1, extracellular matrix–remodeling enzymes, and VEGF pathway components. These features are reminiscent of recently described SPP1⁺ macrophages and angiogenic TAM phenotypes in other tumor types [27,28], suggesting convergent differentiation under hypoxic or metabolically stressed microenvironments. In the revised analysis, Macro4 showed the strongest enrichment of a representative SPP1+ TAM signature among Macro1–Macro7 and was significantly higher than other macrophage states. Thus, Macro4 overlaps with canonical SPP1+ angiogenic TAM biology, but our findings further define it as An spp1+/NDRG1+ macrophage state with hypoxia-associated gene expression and spatial enrichment in hypoxic and angiogenesis-enriched niches. The consistent enrichment of Macro4 across patients and analytical platforms indicates that this phenotype reflects a stable and biologically consequential macrophage state in HGSOC.

The clinical implications of this finding are substantial. By translating single-cell–derived TAM signatures into bulk transcriptomic predictors, we demonstrate that Macro4 abundance is strongly associated with poor overall survival across multiple independent cohorts. Importantly, the Macro4-derived risk score remained an independent predictor of outcome when integrated with conventional clinicopathologic variables, enabling the construction of an accurate and clinically actionable nomogram. Beyond its prognostic utility, the risk score quantitatively captured macrophage state dynamics, revealing a progressive shift from homeostatic TAM populations toward Macro4-dominant programs as risk increased. This indicates that the model not only predicts outcome but also reflects biologically meaningful immune remodeling processes within the tumor microenvironment.

Mechanistically, Macro4-enriched tumors were characterized by strong activation of angiogenesis and hypoxia signatures, accompanied by suppression of oxidative phosphorylation. These findings are consistent with a metabolic reprogramming trajectory that reinforces tumor progression under low-oxygen conditions [29,30]. CellChat analysis further demonstrated that Macro4 macrophages act as major communication hubs, particularly through VEGF and SPP1 signaling to endothelial cells. Such interactions likely promote the development of aberrant vasculature—a hallmark of aggressive ovarian tumors—and may contribute to the reduced drug delivery and metabolic stress that underlie platinum resistance. In addition, Macro4–T cell interactions mediated by MIF signaling suggest that this macrophage state may also participate in shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment that reinforces tumor tolerance.

Trajectory analysis offers further mechanistic insight by resolving macrophage differentiation into two principal lineages: a proliferative path (Trajectory 1) and an inflammatory-to-hypoxic continuum (Trajectory 2). The latter aligns precisely with the Macro4 phenotype and reflects a transition from CXCL9⁺/CXCL11⁺ inflammatory activation toward terminal states enriched for glycolytic enzymes and ECM-remodeling mediators [31]. This trajectory mirrors transcriptional adaptation to hypoxic stress, suggesting that Macro4 may represent the endpoint of a microenvironment-driven reprogramming process rather than a fixed lineage. The enrichment of hypoxia-responsive and signal transduction pathways among Macro4-specific genes further supports this model.

Within this framework, NDRG1 emerged as a central regulatory node. NDRG1 was consistently enriched along the hypoxia-aligned trajectory, represented a multicategory hub in GO enrichment analysis, and was retained in the prognostic model. Spatial mapping and immunofluorescence confirmed that NDRG1+SPP1+ macrophages form distinct niches within angiogenic hotspots, supporting a close association between NDRG1-expressing TAMs and local vascular remodeling. The preferential localization of NDRG1⁺ macrophages to primary tumor lesions—and their reduction in lymph nodes of chemotherapy-sensitive patients—suggests that these cells may facilitate both primary tumor adaptation and metastatic conditioning. Moreover, high NDRG1 expression was associated with poor chemotherapy response and reduced progression-free survival, underscoring its clinical relevance.

NDRG1 has context-dependent functions across cancer types and cellular compartments [32]. In several epithelial tumor contexts, NDRG1 has been reported to suppress epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and metastasis. In contrast, our data highlight a distinct macrophage-associated NDRG1 program in HGSOC [33]. Within hypoxic macrophage niches, NDRG1 expression marked Macro4-like TAMs that were associated with angiogenic remodeling, endothelial proximity, and platinum resistance [34,35]. These findings underscore the importance of interpreting NDRG1 function in a cell-type-specific manner. Whether NDRG1 directly regulates effector genes such A. spp1 or cooperates with HIF-1α in TAMs warrants further investigation.

Collectively, the convergence of pseudotime dynamics, signaling interactions, spatial localization, and clinical correlations supports NDRG1 as a defining feature of the Macro4 state, while its direct regulatory function requires future experimental validation.

These results have important translational implications. Targeting TAMs has historically yielded limited success, partly due to the redundancy and plasticity of myeloid populations. Our data suggest that selectively disrupting Macro4 reprogramming—or its effector pathways such A. spp1–integrin or VEGF signaling—may represent a more effective strategy than indiscriminate macrophage depletion. Moreover, the Macro4-derived risk score may serve as a biomarker to identify patients most likely to benefit from anti-angiogenic therapies, hypoxia-targeting agents, or emerging macrophage-modulating drugs. Given the resemblance of Macro4 to SPP1⁺ TAM populations across multiple cancers, such therapeutic strategies may have applications beyond ovarian cancer.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the current single-cell dataset provides high-resolution insight into TAM heterogeneity, the number of scRNA-seq samples remains modest, and larger cohorts are needed to validate the prevalence and stability of Macro4 across disease stages and treatment settings. Second, the current evidence remains primarily observational and computational. Although spatial transcriptomics, cell–cell communication analysis, and multiplex immunofluorescence support a strong association between NDRG1+ Macro4 macrophages and angiogenic remodeling, direct gain- or loss-of-function experiments are required to determine whether NDRG1 actively regulates macrophage polarization, endothelial remodeling, or platinum resistance. Third, external validation of the full nomogram was constrained by the availability of independent cohorts with complete clinical annotations. Future studies will include hypoxia induction, NDRG1 perturbation in macrophage models, macrophage–endothelial co-culture assays, and validation in larger single-cell and spatial cohorts.

In summary, this study identifies NDRG1+ Macro4 macrophages as a hypoxia-driven angiogenic niche associated with macrophage remodeling, endothelial interaction, and platinum resistance in HGSOC. These findings highlight the importance of macrophage state transitions in shaping tumor behavior and provide a foundation for future studies targeting the Macro4/NDRG1 axis.
