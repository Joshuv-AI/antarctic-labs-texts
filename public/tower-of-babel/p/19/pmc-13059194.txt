Glycogen storage disease type Ia with a 17-year history of renal involvement: a case report
Authors: Minting Chen, Lubin Xu, Xiaoxiao Shi, Yubing Wen, Jie Ma, Rongrong Hu, Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlycogen storage disease type Ia is a rare inherited metabolic disorder often accompanied by renal complications; however, the dynamic progression and its renal pathology remain poorly understood.Case presentationWe report a genetically confirmed Chinese Han female with glycogen storage disease type Ia followed up from childhood to early adulthood (over a 17-year period), documenting the complete natural history of renal involvement. Proteinuria emerged in her childhood (age 6 years), followed by the onset of estimated glomerular filtration rate decreasing and overt Fanconi syndrome in adolescence. Her renal involvement progressed to chronic kidney disease stage 3b in early adulthood, accompanied by a shift to predominantly glomerular-origin proteinuria (84.5%). Key renal biopsy findings revealed abundant glycogen granule deposition in renal tubular epithelial cells, accompanied by extensive tubulointerstitial pathology, while the glomeruli exhibited only secondary focal segmental sclerotic-like changes. Background

Glycogen storage disease type Ia (GSD Ia) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder with an occurrence rate of approximately 1 in 100,000 individuals [1]. It results from a defect in the glucose-6-phosphatase system, leading to impaired glycogen breakdown and characteristic fasting hypoglycemia, along with a range of metabolic disorders such as hyperlactatemia, hyperuricemia, and hyperlipidemia. These abnormalities can lead to complications including gout, osteoporosis, kidney damage and even renal failure, hepatic adenoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, and pulmonary hypertension, which may be life-threatening in severe cases [2, 3]. Renal involvement is a major clinical concern in GSD Ia, manifesting as kidney enlargement, proteinuria, hematuria, renal tubular dysfunction (for example, aminoaciduria and electrolyte losses), nephrolithiasis, and eventual progression to renal failure. Previous renal biopsy reports preliminarily describing renal pathological features in GSD Ia such as tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) [4–6]. On the basis of these findings, glomerular injury has traditionally been regarded as a major contributor to renal progression in GSD Ia. This article reports a case of GSD Ia with a 17-year history of renal involvement, documenting the progression from early metabolic disturbances and renal tubular dysfunction (Fanconi syndrome) to overt glomerular proteinuria and chronic kidney disease. Renal biopsy revealed that the severity of tubulointerstitial injury was significantly greater than that of glomerular lesions. This longitudinal clinical–pathological correlation suggests that kidney damage in GSD Ia is associated with early glycogen accumulation in the renal tubules, with subsequent progression to glomerulosclerosis. The present case provides new insights and supplementary perspectives on the pathophysiology of renal involvement in GSD Ia.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old Chinese Han female patient was admitted to the Department of Nephrology on 12 September 2024 for evaluation of a progressive rise in serum creatinine over the preceding 7 years. Prior to this referral, she had been under long-term follow-up and management by the Department of General Internal Medicine for her underlying GSD Ia and its systemic metabolic complications. The patient was diagnosed with glycogen storage disease type Ia (GSD Ia) at the age of 5 years, on the basis of hepatomegaly and characteristic metabolic abnormalities—including severe mixed hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, and hyperlactatemia, accompanied by persistent normocytic normochromic anemia. Whole-exome sequencing identified a homozygous c.648G>T (p.Leu216=) variant in the G6PC1 gene, confirming the diagnosis. She was prescribed uncooked cornstarch, allopurinol, lipid-lowering agents, and urinary alkalinization. However, her metabolic control remained suboptimal despite therapy.

From the age of 6 years, urinalysis indicated proteinuria without hematuria. Renal ultrasonography revealed increased cortical echogenicity in both kidneys. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor therapy (ACEi, benazepril) was initiated at age 9 years. By age 14 years, serum creatinine (sCr) increased from the normal range to 81–104 μmol/L (reference range: 18–69 μmol/L) (height = 135.5 cm, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] 47.5–61.1 mL/min/1.73 m2; eGFR was calculated using age-appropriate equations: the bedside Schwartz formula for patients < 18 years [7] and the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation for patients ≥ 18 years [8]). The 24-h urine protein (UP) was 0.56 g, and protein electrophoresis indicated mixed proteinuria (glomerular 50.9%, tubular 49.1%). Tubular dysfunction was corroborated by markedly elevated biomarkers, including urinary β2-microglobulin (Uβ2-MG, 31.5 mg/L, reference range: 0–0.227 mg/L), urinary α1-microglobulin (Uα1-MG, 142.0 mg/L, reference range: 0–12 mg/L), urinary transferrin (U-TRF 11.6 mg/L, reference range: 0–2.2 mg/L), and aminoaciduria. When she was 18 years old, the patient developed overt Fanconi syndrome, characterized by hypophosphatemia (phosphate clearance index 0.83 mmol/L, fractional excretion of phosphate 25.0%), decreased corrected serum calcium, hypokalemia, and metabolic acidosis. Complications included symptomatic hypophosphatemic osteomalacia: 25-hydroxyvitamin D 26.2 nmol/L and high alkaline phosphatase 421U/L, clinically presenting as lower limb weakness and a waddling gait. During this period, her sCr fluctuated between 97 and 130 μmol/L (reference range: 45–84 μmol/L). Although bone-related symptoms improved with the supplementation of phosphorus, potassium, and active vitamin D, renal function continued to deteriorate. By April 2024, her sCr increased to 171 μmol/L (reference range: 45–84 μmol/L), and her 24hUP reached 1.9 g. Electrophoresis demonstrated a shift to predominantly glomerular-origin proteinuria (84.5%). Owing to progressive renal dysfunction and worsening proteinuria, a renal biopsy was planned after correction of anemia in September 2024.

Past medical history: The patient had no history of hypertension. In July 2024, the patient was subjected to surgical repair and hepatic artery embolization for traumatic liver rupture. Personal history: The patient was the second child, delivered at full term via spontaneous vaginal delivery, breastfed, and received timely complementary food introduction. Developmental milestones were delayed, and academic performance was poor. Currently, the patient is unmarried and has no children. Family history: The patient’s parents are healthy, and consanguineous marriage was denied. The patient has an elder brother (aged 25 years) who is also diagnosed with GSD type I. His clinical manifestations include hyperuricemia, hyperlactatemia, hyperlipidemia, moderate anemia, and hepatic adenoma, without renal dysfunction. Physical examination: On admission, the patient presented with short stature (height 140 cm, weight 37 kg, body mass index 18.9 kg/m2), and a slow, waddling gait. Other notable sign included hepatomegaly (palpable 9 cm below the costal margin). Auxiliary examinations: Key laboratory parameters at the time of renal biopsy are presented in Table 1. Notably, the hematological workup revealed anemia with an elevated reticulocyte count, consistent with a hyperproliferative bone marrow response (hemoglobin 89 g/L, reference range 110–150 g/L; reticulocyte count 105.20 × 10⁹/L, reference range 35.8–99.6 × 10⁹/L; reticulocyte percentage 3.81%, reference range 0.83–2.24%). Renal function tests confirmed chronic kidney disease (CKD) stage 3b, with significant tubular injury evidenced by markedly elevated urinary β2-microglobulin, α1-microglobulin, and transferrin. The metabolic profile was characterized by severe hypertriglyceridemia, hyperuricemia, and hyperlactatemia. In bone metabolism, parathyroid hormone (PTH) was 2.38 pmol/L (reference range, 1.6–6.0 pmol/L) with an elevated bone resorption marker, type I collagen cross-linked C-telopeptide (0.65 ng/mL; reference range, 0.21–0.44 ng/mL). Serum phosphorus and corrected calcium were within normal limits (1.23 mmol/L and 2.33 mmol/L, respectively), and total 25-hydroxyvitamin D was sufficient (102 nmol/L). For iron metabolism, serum iron was 15.0 μmol/L (reference range 9.0–30.4 μmol/L), with a ferritin level of 84 ng/mL (reference range 14–307 ng/mL); folate and vitamin B12 were within normal range. Liver enzymes, bilirubin, pancreatic function, and thyroid function tests were within normal ranges. No abnormalities were detected in the screening tests for complement, immunoglobulins, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, and antinuclear antibody profiles. Tumor markers, including alpha-fetoprotein, carcinoembryonic antigen, carbohydrate antigen 19–9, carbohydrate antigen 72–4, and cancer antigen 125, were all within normal ranges. In addition, urine immunofixation electrophoresis, serum protein electrophoresis, serum immunofixation electrophoresis with free light chain analysis, Epstein–Barr virus DNA, and cytomegalovirus DNA yielded unremarkable results. Imaging studies: Color Doppler ultrasonography revealed that the major axis of right and left kidney was 9.1, 9.6 cm, respectively. Increased cortical echogenicity was observed in both kidneys, along with diffuse renal lesions. The renal arterial resistance index was mildly elevated bilaterally.

Key laboratory parameters of renal function and metabolism at biopsy

Clinical course: A renal biopsy was performed on 18 September 2024. Light microscopic examination revealed remarkable global glomerulosclerosis (6/8). The remaining nonsclerotic glomeruli showed only occasional segmental mesangial hypercellularity and matrix expansion, with well-patent capillary loops and no significant thickening of the glomerular basement membrane. No definitive lesions of focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) were identified in these nonsclerotic glomeruli. Marked cloudy swelling was observed in the renal tubular epithelial cells. Most tubular basement membranes exhibited varying degrees of thickening, accompanied by multifocal to patchy tubular atrophy. Patchy to extensive interstitial fibrosis was noted, accompanied by sparse inflammatory infiltrates. Intrarenal small arteries generally exhibited wall thickening and luminal narrowing. Immunofluorescence staining was negative. Electron microscopy further revealed mild segmental hypercellularity and matrix expansion in the mesangial regions, with segmental effacement of podocyte foot processes. The lamina densa of the basement membrane exhibited segmental lamellation or a sieve-like appearance. No electron-dense deposits or other abnormal deposits were detected. Two intact proximal tubules and a small number of atrophic tubules were observed throughout the specimen. Glycogen granules were markedly increased in the cytoplasm of proximal tubular epithelial cells, and collagen fiber proliferation was noted in the interstitial areas (Fig. 1). In summary, the findings are consistent with a definitive diagnosis of GSD Ia and subsequent GSD Ia-associated nephropathy. For the comprehensive management of glycogen storage disease, dietary modifications were implemented, including uncooked cornstarch and a low-salt, low-oil diet, low purines, and low protein. Pharmacological interventions were administered to control metabolic disorders, including lipid-lowering and uric acid-lowering agents. Acidosis and vitamin B12 deficiency were corrected. Regarding complications of renal insufficiency, roxadustat was concurrently administered owing to renal anemia.

At the last follow-up in November 2025 (age 22 years), the patient’s fasting blood glucose was maintained at 5.2–6.2 mmol/L with uncooked cornstarch (40 g, four times daily). Her renal parameters demonstrated progression, serum Cr 222 μmol/L (eGFR 26.4 mL/min/1.73 m2), and 24hUP 2.12 g. Her total carbon dioxide was 23.7 mmol/L during treatment with sodium bicarbonate tablets 14–20 g/day. Discussion and conclusion

In this report, we present a genetically confirmed patient with glycogen storage disease type Ia who was followed for 17 years, allowing documentation of the long-term course of renal involvement. This case illustrates the progressive and multisystem nature of GSD Ia and provides longitudinal clinical data in conjunction with renal histopathological findings. Although skeletal manifestations of this patient have been reported previously [9], the current report delineates a complete 17-year natural history of renal involvement, correlating longitudinal clinical data with distinctive histopathological findings.

Glycogen storage disease type Ia is caused by deficiency of the glucose-6-phosphatase catalytic subunit, leading to intracellular accumulation of glucose-6-phosphate and its diversion into alternative metabolic pathways [1]. In the kidney, G6PC is predominantly expressed in proximal tubular epithelial cells, rendering the proximal tubule particularly susceptible to metabolic injury [10, 11]. The exact mechanism of glomerular injury remains unclear but may involve activation of the renin–angiotensin system [12, 13], oxidative stress [13], and metabolic disturbances related to intracellular energy deficiency in tubular epithelial cells [1]. To meet metabolic demands, renal blood flow and intraglomerular hydrostatic pressure typically increase during childhood, manifesting as hyperperfusion and hyperfiltration. Over time, this leads to glomerular fibrosis and sclerosis, with gradual scar formation and the appearance of microalbuminuria. During adolescence to early adulthood (between 12 and 25 years), overt proteinuria and CKD develop (around 21 years of age) [3, 5, 6, 14, 15]. Chronic proteinuria may further exacerbate glomerular injury by inducing chemo-attractants and inflammatory pathways [16]. With disease progression, scarring gradually surrounds the entire glomerulus, resulting in nephron loss and eventual renal failure. Common pathological findings include focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and renal interstitial fibrosis, with a few cases reporting interstitial nephritis. Light microscopy often reveals glomerular hypertrophy, significant mesangial matrix expansion, cystic dilation and vacuolar degeneration of tubular epithelial cells, glycogen deposition, tubular atrophy, lipid deposition in tubular epithelial cells and the interstitium, interstitial fibrosis, and arteriolosclerosis. Electron microscopy may show segmental effacement of podocyte foot processes, thickening, splitting, or “moth-eaten” changes in the glomerular basement membrane, typically without electron-dense deposits. Immunofluorescence is generally negative [17]. Kiyama et al. [18] reported a female patient who underwent two renal biopsies at ages 24 and 27 years, suggesting that glomerular damage may precede tubulointerstitial injury. In addition, some researchers have proposed that glomerular lesions (such as focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [FSGS]) may play a dominant role in disease progression [19–21].

The observed sequence of events—from renal tubular glycogen deposition to functional impairment, interstitial fibrosis, and subsequent glomerulosclerosis—supports a potential role of persistent glycogen accumulation within renal tubular cells in the progression of renal injury. In this patient, severe tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis were observed alongside comparatively mild glomerular alterations, such as focal podocyte foot process effacement. This pathological pattern raises the possibility that tubulointerstitial injury may represent an early feature of renal involvement, whereas glomerular changes may develop later in the disease course. The renal biopsy was performed at a relatively advanced disease stage (with global sclerosis in six out of eight glomeruli). This late timing, along with the limited number of glomeruli available for assessment, constrains direct evaluation of early glomerular lesions and precludes a definitive statistical assessment of their initial severity. Nonetheless, the 17-year longitudinal clinical data provide a dynamic context for inferring the sequence of pathological progression.

Elevations in tubular injury markers (urinary β2-microglobulin, urinary α1-microglobulin, and urinary transferrin), along with a typical Fanconi syndrome, preceded both a significant decline in the estimated glomerular filtration rate and the shift of proteinuria toward a glomerular-dominant pattern. This clinical timeline suggests that the conventional view of glomerulopathy as the initial event may not fully capture the early renal changes in all patients with GSD Ia, and raises the possibility that tubular injury represents an underestimated early component of the disease continuum. Experimental models provide compelling evidence supporting this perspective. Studies on kidney-specific G6pc-knockout mice (K. G6pc−/−) have demonstrated that isolated renal glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC) deficiency is sufficient to induce structural tubular injury—including glycogen accumulation and tubular dilatation—prior to the appearance of glomerular damage markers such as microalbuminuria, even in the absence of systemic metabolic disorders [22]. Although this model does not fully replicate the human disease state accompanied by systemic metabolic stressors (for example, hyperlactatemia and hyperlipidemia), which may accelerate disease progression, it substantiates the possibility that tubular injury can occur as a primary, autonomous process.

The current cornerstone treatments for GSD Ia, such as uncooked cornstarch and various supportive therapies, primarily aim to correct downstream metabolic disorders (for example, hypoglycemia and hyperuricemia) [23]. Although these interventions are essential for maintaining systemic homeostasis, they are inherently incapable of eliminating glycogen already deposited in tissues or correcting the underlying enzymatic defect. In the present case, despite the patient being subjected to standard treatment plans, renal disease continued to progress, objectively untangling the limitations of existing therapeutic approaches. Therefore, future treatment strategies should extend beyond metabolic control and focus on directly reducing the pathological glycogen load in affected cells, such as renal tubular epithelial cells, thereby intervening in the disease process at its origin.

In conclusion, the severe tubulointerstitial pathology and the temporal precedence of tubular dysfunction observed in this long-term case indicate that renal tubular injury may be an early and clinically significant event, potentially heralding the progression of nephropathy in GSD Ia. These observations may warrant further investigation in future studies and consideration in the clinical assessment of renal involvement in GSD Ia.
