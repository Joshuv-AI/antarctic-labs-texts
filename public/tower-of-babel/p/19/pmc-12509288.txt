Posterior ulnar recurrent artery perforator flap for elbow reconstruction: a forearm-based propeller flap
Authors: Jenaleen Law, Isobel Yeap, Bishoy Soliman, Tim Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoft tissue reconstruction around the elbow is challenging due to the need for durable yet pliable coverage that preserves joint mobility and function. We present the case of a 56-year-old gentleman with a chronic elbow wound secondary to osteomyelitis. Following debridement, the defect was reconstructed using a posterior ulnar recurrent artery perforator (PURAP) flap designed in a propeller style. Unlike previous reports that have described PURAP flaps based on arm perforators, this case uniquely utilised perforators arising from the forearm. This forearm-based approach offers several advantages, including reduced donor-site morbidity, and avoidance of dissection near the ulnar nerve. This case highlights the versatility and safety of the forearm-based PURAP flap for elbow reconstruction. Level of evidence: Level 5.

Introduction

Soft tissue reconstruction of the elbow can be challenging, as it requires a pliable yet well-padded reconstruction which preserves range of motion and function. We describe a case utilising a forearm-based propeller flap based on a perforator of the posterior ulnar recurrent artery to reconstruct an elbow defect.

Materials and methods

Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of this case report and accompanying images. In accordance with institutional and national guidelines, ethical approval was not required for the preparation and publication of this case report.

Case report

A 56-year-old right-hand-dominant gentleman suffered right olecranon bursitis, which was debrided multiple times over a one-year course and was complicated by osteomyelitis. After the osteomyelitis was eradicated with surgery and antibiotics, he was referred by the Orthopaedic Surgeon for excision of a chronic sinus over the olecranon and soft tissue reconstruction. The patient had a history of scleroderma with previous digital tip necrosis requiring sympathectomy of common digital arteries. Pre-operative Allen’s test and CT angiogram (CTA) demonstrated a lack of arterial flow in the ulnar artery to the right hand, which precluded a reverse radial forearm flap reconstruction. Liaising with his Rheumatologist, the patient was pre-operatively optimised with calcium channel blockers and sildenafil to improve his upper limb perfusion. He was then considered for a free anterolateral thigh flap (ALT) and we intended to perform an end-to-side arterial anastomosis to the ulnar artery proximal to the vasospastic segments identified on CTA. However, the CTA also revealed a large perforator (diameter 1.79 mm) of the posterior ulnar recurrent artery, with its origin arising 10 cm distal to the elbow joint (see Figure 1A). It was decided that a local propeller flap based on this perforator would adequately reconstruct the elbow defect, with our back-up option being an ALT flap.

It had a diameter of 1.79 mm on CT angiogram (B) PURAP flap isolated on its perforator, located 10 cm distal to the elbow joint, and has an intramuscular course through flexor carpi ulnaris and flexor digitorum superficialis (C) 180-degree rotation of the PURAP flap into the defect.

The sinus cavity was injected with methylene blue dye. Under tourniquet control, the sinus tract was excised down to the olecranon. Using a handheld Doppler, the perforator on the ulnar forearm was identified and a 12x4cm propeller flap was planned in reverse capturing this perforator. The flap raise was suprafascial and the perforator was intramuscular through the flexor carpi ulnaris muscle located 3cm distal to the olecranon (see Figure 1B). The fascia around the perforator was released and the flap was rotated 180 degrees to cover the elbow defect (see Figure 1C). The tourniquet was letdown to confirm flap perfusion. The flap was inset using 3-0 Monocryl. The donor site was closed primarily and two suction drains were used. The entire operative time was under two hours.

The patient was placed in a 120-degree elbow splint. Post-operatively, he was commenced on a supraclavicular block of 0.2% ropivacaine for 72 h to encourage vasodilation, and after this period he was recommenced on sildenafil. In the immediate postoperative period, the flap demonstrated mild congestion, presenting as erythema with a 2-second capillary refill. However, the flap remained soft and consistent with the expected appearance of a propeller flap. These changes gradually resolved over the following days.

The flap had healed well at his two-month follow-up (see Figure 2). At the 3-year follow-up, the flap demonstrated complete healing with durable soft tissue coverage. The patient achieved a Mayo Elbow Performance Score of 95, corresponding to excellent functional outcome [1]. Specifically, he reported no pain (45 points), demonstrated an arc of motion between 50–100° (15 points), had a stable elbow (10 points), and was able to independently perform activities of daily living (25 points) (see Figure 3).

Discussion

The posterior ulnar recurrent artery arises from the ulnar artery in the proximal forearm, passes backward on the flexor digitorum profundus, and ascends between the medial epicondyle and olecranon (see Figure 4). It then lies beneath flexor carpi ulnaris before passing between its two heads to anastomose with the superior and inferior ulnar collateral and interosseous recurrent arteries [2,3]. Mateev et al. (2009) first described the Posterior Ulnar Recurrent Artery Perforator (PURAP) flap in a series of 22 patients undergoing reconstruction of forearm and elbow defects [3]. Nakao et al. (2017) also reported 18 patients who underwent elbow reconstruction with the PURAP flap [4]. Both studies used the PURAP flap to reconstruct burn contractures [3,4], whereas our PURAP flap was used to reconstruct a chronic wound. Mateev et al. (2009) utilised a pedicled island flap design, whereas similar to Nakao et al. (2017) we used a propeller flap design. Mateev et al. (2009) and Nakao et al. (2017) raised their PURAP flaps from the distal upper arm, whereas we raised our flap on the proximal forearm (see Figure 4B) [3,4]. The main advantage of basing our flap on the forearm rather than upper arm perforators was that no dissection of the ulnar nerve was required during flap harvest. Mateev et al. (2009) and Nakao et al. (2017) both reported sensory deficits of the ulnar nerve associated with their dissection around it on the arm [3,4].

Studies suggest that ulnar artery perforators in the forearm tend to be variable, but a reliable landmark to locate them is along a line between the pisiform and volar aspect of the medial epicondyle [2,5]. Most of the perforators are located on or slightly ulnar to this line. The perforators may be musculocutaneous through flexor carpi ulnaris or septocutaneous [2,5,6]. Yu et al. (2012) reported up to three significant ulnar artery perforators that can be located in the mid to distal forearm along the aforementioned line (7 cm, 11 cm and 15.5 cm proximally from the pisiform in an average adult) [5]. Wei et al. (2014) reported a common perforator at the intersection of the proximal and middle thirds of the forearm [7]. Guiotto et al. (2021) demonstrated the consistent presence of at least one ulnar perforator in the proximal third of the forearm, arising either directly from the ulnar artery or the anterior recurrent ulnar artery, forming the basis of the proximal ulnar perforator flap (PUFP) [6]. Sun et al. (2013) found that the ulnar artery had two main clusters of perforators useful for flaps concentrated in the proximal one-third and distal one-fourth of the forearm [2]. Cherubino et al. (2021) examined ten cadaveric specimens and found that the most proximal ulnar perforator in the forearm originated from the posterior recurrent ulnar artery in only 30% of cases. These perforators passed through flexor carpi ulnaris and emerged 5.6 ± 0.3 cm distal to the medial epicondyle. In the rest of the specimens (70%), the most proximal ulnar perforator arose directly from the ulnar artery. The authors reported that ulnar artery perforator flaps based on the posterior ulnar recurrent artery or directly from the ulnar artery were both good reconstruction options [8]. The lack of understanding of the anatomical variants of the PURAP flap may explain the current underutilisation of similar flaps in elbow reconstruction. Further research may clarify the anatomical relationships of ulnar artery perforators for clinical use. Meanwhile, we found handheld Doppler and CTA to be useful in operative planning and perforator localisation.

Other variations of perforator flaps based on the ulnar artery have been described. The first ulnar recurrent artery flap was a pedicled fasciocutaneous flap [9]. Further developments include the distal ulnar artery perforator flap [10], and the superior ulnar collateral artery perforator flap [11]. More recently, the free proximal ulnar perforator flap has been described [6].

Conclusion

Most existing papers describe PURAP flaps based on using the arm as a donor-site, whereas we describe a PURAP flap from the forearm. In our experience, the forearm-based PURAP flap is a good local option for reconstruction of elbow defects. The forearm-based PURAP flap resurfaces the elbow with thin and pliable skin, it is simple to perform, and it does not involve dissection around the ulnar nerve. Unlike the radial and ulnar artery forearm flaps, the PURAP flap does not sacrifice a major artery. Furthermore, the donor site is ideal because it is well-concealed and can be closed primarily. Further studies are needed to guide the continued application of this flap.
