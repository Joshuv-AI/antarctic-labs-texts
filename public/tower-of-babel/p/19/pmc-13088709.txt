Factors motivating attendance at continuing medical education programs: a mixed-methods study from a Middle Eastern Country
Authors: Awad Al Essa, S Ayhan Caliskan, Mohi Eldin Magzoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContinuing medical education enables healthcare professionals to maintain and enhance their knowledge and skills in a rapidly evolving medical field. In many settings, a specified number of continuing medical education hours is required annually for renewal of professional practice licenses. However, the factors that motivate healthcare professionals to attend continuing medical education programs are not fully understood. This mixed-methods study examined the factors influencing attendance at continuing medical education programs in a Middle Eastern country. Four accredited continuing medical education programs delivered by a national university were evaluated. A total of 276 healthcare professionals completed a semi-close-ended questionnaire in which they could select one or more of ten potential motivating factors. Qualitative data was complemented by semi-structured interviews with 14 participants who discussed reasons for attendance and barriers. Overall, 63.6% of participants selected the need to obtain education hours for license renewal and 49.3% selected program content as motivating factors. When only one factor was selected, the need for education hours (49.6%) and scientific content (31.4%) remained the most frequently reported motivators. Nurses were most likely to attend primarily to obtain required education hours, whereas doctors were more likely to attend for scientific content. Location and affordable fees were particularly important for nurses. Interviewees described continuing medical education as an efficient way to obtain updated knowledge but highlighted barriers including limited paid educational leave, high fees, and scheduling constraints. The need to obtain education hours for recertification and the perceived relevance of program content were the main drivers of continuing medical education attendance. Introduction

Professional development can be defined as the process of enhancing and improving the skills and competencies of employees through education and training in the workplace [1]. Healthcare professionals are a critical resource in any health system, and maintaining their competence is essential for patient safety and quality of care [2]. Continuing medical education (CME) is one of the main strategies used to support lifelong learning, providing structured opportunities for healthcare professionals to update their knowledge after completion of formal education [3]. Short, focused CME activities, such as rapid review courses and workshops, enable participants to obtain updated information in a relatively short period of time [3].

Internationally, a growing number of jurisdictions have linked licensure or recertification to documented participation in CME [4, 5]. In many countries, healthcare professionals are required to complete a specified number of CME hours or credits per year [1, 4, 5]. These requirements are intended to promote engagement in lifelong learning and maintain standards of care. However, the relationship between mandatory CME and meaningful learning remains a subject of debate [1, 2, 6]. Some authors argue that mandates can increase participation while not necessarily guaranteeing changes in practice or improved outcomes [1, 2, 6]. Recent work on CME/continuing professional development (CPD) systems has also emphasized the importance of moving beyond simple credit accumulation towards models that support sustained practice improvement and outcomes-based evaluation [3–5, 7].

Multiple factors may influence whether healthcare professionals attend CME activities and how they choose among available programs. Studies from the Middle East and elsewhere have highlighted the role of content relevance, perceived quality of teaching, accessibility of delivery formats, and organizational support [8–13]. Workload, patient volume, time constraints, and financial considerations have been reported as barriers to participation [8, 10–12, 14]. These influences may vary across professional groups and healthcare systems, with recent studies reporting differences in motivations and barriers among physicians, nurses, and pharmacists [10–13].

In a Middle Eastern country with a rapidly expanding healthcare sector, CME is mandatory for renewal of practice licenses for doctors, nurses, pharmacists, and other healthcare professionals [4, 5, 8–12]. Numerous CME activities are conducted each year, including review courses, international conferences, and workshops. Despite the volume of activity, the factors that motivate healthcare professionals in this context to attend CME—and the barriers they face—have not been examined in depth. Previous local work has explored perceptions of CME and organizational support but has not focused specifically on motivators and barriers to attendance [9].

This mixed-methods study examined the factors affecting attendance at CME programs in a Middle Eastern country. The study focused on ten potential motivating factors: need for CME hours, program content, invitation, faculty, location, reputation of the organizer, schedule, fee, date, and other. Motivating factors were analyzed quantitatively, and qualitative interviews were used to explore reasons for program selection and barriers to attendance. The study is part of a larger project evaluating CME activities based on Kirkpatrick’s model of training evaluation, with the present analysis situated at Level 1 (participants’ reactions) [15]. Internationally, CPD systems are increasingly shifting from credit accumulation toward outcomes-oriented, practice-relevant learning that emphasizes demonstrable improvement in clinical performance and patient care [3, 4, 7].

Methods

This mixed-methods study was conducted in the United Arab Emirates, a Middle Eastern country with a multicultural healthcare workforce and mandatory continuing medical education (CME) requirements for license renewal. Four CME programs accredited by a national university were selected: an Acute Coronary Syndrome course, an Infectious Diseases course, a Cardio-rheumatology review, and a Respiratory rapid review course. For each program, information on the date, duration, topic, expected attendance, and actual attendance was recorded. All four CME programs were fee-based, industry-independent, and nationally recognized for license renewal; attendees represented a mix of public and private healthcare sectors.

A self-administered questionnaire was distributed to participants at the start of each program as part of the registration process. Of 334 healthcare professionals who received the questionnaire, 276 completed it (response rate 82.63%). The survey included closed-ended questions on demographic characteristics and a semi-close-ended item listing ten potential motivating factors: need for CME hours, program content, invitation, faculty, location, reputation of the organizer, schedule, fee, date, and other. Participants could select one or more factors that applied to them. For analysis, responses from individuals who selected only one motivating factor were examined separately under the assumption that the single selected factor represented the dominant motivator. The questionnaire was paper-based and distributed to participants at the registration desk before the CME activities started; completion was voluntary and anonymous. This subgroup analysis was conducted to explore participants’ strongest/primary motivator and reduce noise from multifactorial selections; however, we recognize that real-world decisions are often driven by multiple concurrent factors.

Demographic variables collected included age, profession (doctor, nurse, pharmacist, or other healthcare professional), nationality (Arab or non-Arab), level of education, workplace, years of practice, and weekly patient load. Age, profession, and nationality were examined in greater depth because of their potential influence on CME attendance patterns. Nationality was grouped into Arab and non-Arab categories to reflect the diverse composition of the healthcare workforce.

To complement the survey findings, qualitative data were gathered through semi-structured interviews with 14 purposively selected participants representing different professions (eight doctors, four nurses, one laboratory technician, and one research assistant). Purposeful sampling was used to identify participants able to provide information-rich insights. Interviewees were informed about the study’s purpose and their right to withdraw at any time. Interviews explored reasons for selecting specific CME programs, perceived benefits, and barriers to attendance, such as scheduling constraints, financial considerations, and institutional support. Participants were encouraged to elaborate freely to provide richer qualitative data. During registration, participants were asked whether they were interested in participating in an interview; those who volunteered registered their names and were contacted by phone after the event to arrange a face-to-face interview. Interview participation was voluntary.

The questionnaire and the semi-structured interview guide used in this study were specifically developed by the researchers for this project. Their development was guided by an extensive review of the relevant literature and aligned with the local context, cultural considerations, and study objectives. Prior to deployment, the questionnaire and semi-structured interview guide were reviewed by a medical education expert and pilot tested with doctors, nurses, and laboratory technicians to ensure clarity, relevance, and alignment with the study objectives, supporting content validity. Reliability of the quantitative questionnaire components was assessed using internal consistency analysis, suggesting acceptable internal consistency for exploratory use. Formal psychometric validation was beyond the scope of this study. The questionnaire and semi-structured interview guide developed for this study are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethical Committee of the British University in Dubai and from the ethical committee of the national university. Approval letters indicated no objection to conducting the study, accessing accreditation data, or collecting information from participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Confidentiality and anonymity were assured, and data were reported in aggregate form.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS (version not specified). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic characteristics and frequencies of the ten motivating factors. The association between profession and the two most frequently selected single motivating factors—need for CME hours and program content—was examined using Pearson’s chi-square test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

For the chi-square tests, the outcome variable was whether participants selected a given factor as their only motivating factor, and the explanatory variable was profession (doctor, nurse, pharmacist, or other healthcare professional).

Interview transcripts were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach. A single researcher read all transcripts repeatedly, coded the data manually, and identified recurring ideas and themes. Themes were refined iteratively as analysis progressed. Qualitative findings were used to complement and contextualize the quantitative results.

All 276 participants responded to the question on motivating factors. The most frequently selected motivators were the need to obtain CME hours required for license renewal (63.6%) and the scientific content of the program (49.3%). Location was selected by 29.5% of participants, while date, fee, schedule, faculty, and organizer reputation were selected by smaller proportions. When restricted to participants who selected only one motivating factor, the need for CME hours (49.6%) and program content (31.4%) remained the dominant motivators. Fewer respondents selected invitation, faculty, location, or organizer reputation as their sole reason for attendance. These patterns correspond to the distributions illustrated in Fig. 1.

Profession showed a significant association with selecting the need for CME hours as the only motivating factor (χ²(3) = 11.46, p = 0.009). Nurses formed the largest proportion of participants who identified this factor as their sole motivation, followed by doctors, pharmacists, and other healthcare professionals. This suggests that license renewal requirements exerted a strong influence on nurses’ decisions to attend CME programs.

Profession was also significantly associated with selecting program content as the only motivating factor (χ²(3) = 11.90, p = 0.008). Doctors were the most likely to report scientific content as their sole reason for attending CME activities, while nurses tended to consider content as one among several motivating factors.

Location was among the top three motivating factors overall. More than half of the participants who selected location as one of their motivating factors were nurses, followed by doctors, pharmacists, and other healthcare professionals. Nurses also constituted the largest proportion of those who selected affordable fees as a motivating factor, indicating that cost considerations played a particularly important role for this group.

The semi-structured interviews provided additional insight into the participants’ motivations and barriers regarding CME attendance. Interviewees consistently described CME programs as valuable opportunities to efficiently update their knowledge and bridge gaps between current practice and emerging evidence. Many emphasized the advantage of receiving concise, well-structured information through organized courses rather than relying solely on reading books or journals, which they perceived as more time-consuming and less focused [1, 3]. Overall, CME activities were viewed as an accessible and effective mechanism for continuous professional development.

However, participants also highlighted several challenges that limited their ability to attend these programs. A common concern was the lack of paid educational leave, with many reporting that CME participation was expected during personal time, creating conflicts with family responsibilities and reducing opportunities for rest. Others described the financial impact of losing work hours or income when programs were held on weekdays, while weekend sessions further encroached on personal time. High registration fees and limited access to institutional or industry sponsorship were noted as significant barriers, particularly among nurses. Additional logistical issues, including program location, lengthy sessions that often extended to eight hours, and scheduling on weekends—were also reported as deterrents to regular attendance. Discussion

This mixed-methods study identified the main factors influencing attendance at CME programs among healthcare professionals in a Middle Eastern country. The combination of survey and interview data showed that the need to obtain CME hours for license renewal and the scientific content of programs were the dominant motivators, while location, fees, and organizational support also played important roles. These findings are consistent with recent regional and international reports highlighting that license requirements, perceived educational value, accessibility, and institutional support are central drivers of participation in CME/CPD [1, 3–5, 7, 10–12].

The strong influence of license renewal requirements aligns with international experience, where linking CME to recertification has been used as a strategy to promote participation [1, 2, 4–7]. In this study, nurses were particularly likely to attend primarily to accumulate required CME hours. Similar patterns have been described in several Middle Eastern and Gulf settings, where healthcare professionals report fulfilling mandatory credit requirements as a key reason for engaging in CME activities [8, 10–12]. While mandatory systems may be effective in increasing attendance, several authors have cautioned that they do not guarantee meaningful learning or changes in practice and may risk reinforcing a “box-ticking” culture [1–3, 5–7]. These findings highlight the importance of designing CME programs that not only provide credits but also address learners’ needs and promote application in clinical practice [1–3, 5, 7]. We interpret these findings as self-reported motivations and associations rather than evidence of causality; mandatory credit requirements may increase participation without necessarily ensuring meaningful learning or practice change. This tension between mandatory credit requirements and meaningful learning reflects contemporary international debates on the evolution of CME toward outcomes-based CPD systems.

Program content was the second most frequently reported motivator and the main single motivator for doctors. This is consistent with literature emphasizing that CME is most effective when content is relevant to practice and aligned with learners’ perceived gaps [3, 7–12]. Recent work on online and blended CME has similarly shown that healthcare professionals prioritize activities that clearly address their clinical responsibilities and perceived learning needs [3, 7, 10–12]. Interviewees in the present study underscored the value of high-quality content, experienced faculty, and opportunities to discuss cases and share experience. This suggests that program planners should actively engage target audiences in needs assessment and ensure that content is tailored to the professional roles of different groups [3, 7, 10–12].

Location and cost were particularly important for nurses, who were more likely than doctors to report affordable fees and nearby venues as motivating factors. Qualitative data indicated that nurses had fewer opportunities for sponsorship and that relatively few programs were designed specifically for their needs. This is consistent with recent reports from the region and elsewhere, which show that nurses and other non-physician professionals often face greater logistical and financial barriers to participation, including travel distance, shift patterns, and limited employer support [8, 10–13]. Addressing these inequities—through targeted programs, reduced fees, flexible scheduling, and institutional sponsorship—could improve participation and support professional development among nurses and other allied health professionals [1, 3–5, 7, 10–13]. Interviewees described cost barriers including registration fees, opportunity costs related to unpaid leave or loss of income, and travel or accommodation expenses; nurses reported fewer opportunities for external sponsorship compared with doctors.

Barriers related to time and organizational support were common. Healthcare professionals often had to attend CME activities during personal time, and programs scheduled on weekends or weekdays created challenges for work–life balance, workload coverage, and income. Similar barriers have been reported in other contexts, where high patient loads, competing responsibilities, and lack of protected time impede engagement in CME [8, 10–12, 14]. Recent qualitative work has further emphasized that heavy workloads, limited staffing, and insufficient recognition of CME as part of routine clinical duties can reduce motivation and make sustained participation difficult, even when license requirements exist [1, 3, 14]. Implementing policies that grant protected educational time or paid leave for CME, and explicitly recognizing CME within job plans, may reduce these barriers and help organizations signal their commitment to staff development [1, 3–5, 7, 14].

Taken together, the findings support a view of CME as both an educational and organizational issue [1, 3–5, 7]. Ensuring that CME programs are accessible, relevant, and supported by institutions may enhance both participation and impact. In settings where CME is mandatory for license renewal, particular attention should be paid to aligning credit-driven attendance with high-quality educational experiences that support practice improvement [1–3, 5–7]. Strengthening needs assessment, promoting interactive and practice-oriented formats, and embedding CME within broader CPD systems may help shift focus from accumulating hours towards demonstrable improvements in competence and patient care [1–3, 5–7].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it focused on four CME programs delivered by a single academic institution over a short period, which may limit generalizability to other programs, providers, or years. Second, the programs were not promotional courses sponsored by pharmaceutical companies, and findings may not apply to such activities. Third, qualitative data were based on a relatively small number of interviews conducted by a single researcher, introducing the possibility of interviewer bias. Although analysis was conducted by a single researcher, iterative reading, constant comparison, and integration with quantitative findings were used to enhance analytic coherence; nevertheless, interpretive depth may have been limited. Finally, although the response rate to the survey was high, self-reported motivation may be subject to social desirability bias. Industry-supported CME is common in the setting and was outside the scope of this evaluation focused on university-delivered activities; some interviewees nevertheless mentioned promotional CME as an alternative source of education credits.

Selection bias is likely because the study included only individuals who attended the selected CME programs; the perspectives of healthcare professionals who do not attend CME or who face the greatest barriers to participation were not captured.

Despite these limitations, the mixed-methods design, relatively large sample of participants, and inclusion of diverse professional groups provide a useful picture of the factors influencing CME attendance in this setting.

Conclusion

In this mixed-methods study, healthcare professionals reported that the need to obtain CME hours for license renewal and the scientific content of programs were the primary reasons for attending CME activities. Nurses were more likely to attend mainly to meet licensing requirements, whereas doctors prioritized content. Location and fees, along with limited paid educational leave and scheduling constraints, were important barriers, particularly for nurses and other non-physician professionals.

Policies that provide financial support, reduce fees, and ensure protected educational time may improve attendance at CME programs. Program planners should prioritize relevant, practice-oriented content and consider the needs of different professional groups. Additional research examining a broader range of CME providers and program formats over longer periods is warranted to confirm and extend these findings.
