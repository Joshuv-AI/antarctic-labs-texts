Home bias in Chinese science is overestimated
Authors: Lan Jin, Zhesi Shen, Yang Wang
Published: 2026

Main text

The rapid rise of Chinese science has reshaped the global research landscape. China has become one of the largest producers of scientific publications and now contributes a substantial share of the world’s highly cited papers. Yet, this rise has recently generated growing concerns that Chinese science receives a disproportionately large share of citations from China, indicating that Chinese papers exhibit the strongest citation home bias among major countries.1,2 However, existing evidence has largely focused on who cites Chinese papers rather than whom Chinese papers cite. This distinction is critical, as a high domestic share among the citations received by Chinese papers may reflect not an unusually strong preference for domestic work among Chinese scientists, but rather fewer-than-expected citations from papers in other countries.

Using journal articles and conference proceedings from 15 major countries in the Dimensions dataset,3 we revisit the claim by distinguishing citation home bias and reference home bias. We find that Chinese papers exhibit a relatively weaker tendency to cite domestic papers than do papers from other major countries. Notably, we also find that Chinese scientists are not unusually inclined to cite their own prior work compared to their counterparts from other countries. Our results suggest that China’s home bias has likely been overstated by indicators based only on received citations, and such home bias partly reflects asymmetric global knowledge flow.

Recent work measured citation home bias by comparing the share of domestic citations received by a country with that country’s share of global publications.1 Specifically, the citation home bias of country i is defined as its domestic share of citations received minus its publication share (Figure 1A). This measure adjusts for the fact that large scientific countries naturally receive more domestic citations. Following prior research,1 we focus on 1,581,955 articles and conference proceedings published between 2005 and 2020 in venues ranked in the top 10% by the SCImago Journal Rank (i.e., SJR) in the Dimensions dataset, and use fractional counting to assign a paper to countries.3 We find that China exhibits a markedly higher citation home bias than any other country in our sample (Figure 1B), which is consistent with prior research.1 We also use the corresponding author’s country as the paper’s affiliated country, finding consistent results.3 However, this measure captures the geography of received citations, not the citing behavior of Chinese papers. A country may appear highly home biased if its papers receive relatively few citations from other countries compared to expectations. In that case, the domestic share of its received citations will be high even if scientists do not cite domestic work more than scientists from other countries. To examine whether Chinese papers disproportionately cite domestic research, we construct a null model. The idea is that for each country pair and publication year, we quantify whether papers from country j cite papers from country i more often than expected if references were formed randomly, regardless of country.

Following Sun and Latora,5 we quantify the deviation between the observed case and the random case, as follows:ϕj→i=observedprobabilitythatpapersfromjcitepapersfromiexpectedprobabilitythatpapersfromjselectreferencesrandomly.

A value greater than 1 indicates that country j cites country i more often than expected. We define a country’s reference home bias as the value of this ratio when the citing and cited countries are the same (Figure 1A).3 Using a 5-year backward citation window, we find that China’s reference home bias is 3.20, indicating that Chinese papers cite domestic research more often than expected under the random baseline. However, China’s relative position is much more moderate when home bias is assessed from the reference side. Specifically, China’s reference home bias is higher than that of the United States but substantially lower than that of other major science-producing countries. To account for differences in citation propensity across document types and fields, we conduct additional robustness tests by considering only English articles and papers from certain scientific domains, finding consistent results.3 Taken together, the contrast between a very high citation home bias and a moderate reference home bias suggests that China’s apparent citation home bias is potentially amplified by limited international recognition of Chinese papers.

We further examine the paper-level author self-reference ratio. Specifically, we calculate, for each paper, the proportion of references that were written by at least one of its authors. We examine 16,433,300 journal articles and conference proceedings published between 2005 and 2020 in the Dimensions dataset, whose disambiguated authors each had between 5 and 500 career publications. In this analysis, we exclude internationally coauthored papers.3 The results provide additional evidence against the view that Chinese scientists are unusually self-referential. The average self-reference ratio for papers from China is 8.59%, which is substantially lower than that of authors from all other countries (Figure 1C). This result reinforces the reference-based finding, i.e., Chinese scientists are not especially prone to citing their own previous work.

Our results suggest that the home bias of Chinese science has likely been overestimated. Chinese papers do receive a high share of domestic citations, but this pattern should not be interpreted mechanically as evidence that Chinese scientists disproportionately cite Chinese research. Once citation behavior is examined from the reference side, Chinese papers show only a moderate tendency to cite domestic work. Scientist-level self-reference patterns point in the same direction: Chinese scientists are not unusually likely to cite their own prior publications.

This distinction matters for both bibliometric evaluation and science policy. Measures based only on received citations conflate two different processes: domestic citation preference and insufficient international recognition. If Chinese papers are cited less often than expected by scientists from other countries, then domestic citations will account for a larger share of all citations received by Chinese papers, even without excessive domestic referencing. More broadly, our findings call for a more careful interpretation of national citation indicators. Citation-based rankings are often used to evaluate scientific strength, but they can be distorted by asymmetric recognition across countries. For China, the key issue may not be excessive self-citation but insufficient recognition of Chinese science worldwide. Strengthening international visibility, improving cross-national scientific communication, and encouraging deeper intellectual exchange may therefore be more productive than simply discounting domestic citations.
